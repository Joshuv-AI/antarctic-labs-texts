The evolution of reproduction without blood feeding in an invasive vector mosquito Aedes albopictus
Authors: Samantha L Sturiale, Mara Heilig, Matthew L Aardema, Luciano V Cosme, Margaret Corley, Sarah Marzec, Micah Hamilton, Daniel Vizcarra, Luke T Anderson, Christina M Holzapfel, William E Bradshaw, Megan E Meuti, Adalgisa Caccone, Peter A Armbruster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMosquitoes transmit a diverse range of deadly pathogens, including dengue and Plasmodium spp., when they consume the blood of vertebrate hosts in order to nutritionally provision their eggs. However, mosquito species from multiple genera have independently evolved the ability to produce eggs without blood. The molecular and physiological processes underlying the evolution of blood-independent reproduction are largely unresolved, in part because the two most well-studied models of mosquito reproductive physiology and genetics are obligate blood feeders. Herein, we integrate life-history, genomics, and gene expression to identify physiological processes and candidate genes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in a non-model vector mosquito, Aedes albopictus.ResultsRelative to a blood-dependent (control) population, females selected for blood-independent reproduction exhibit a longer larval development period and larger adult size, implying increased nutrient sequestration at the larval stage. Additionally, blood-independent larvae exhibit higher expression of amino acid and lipid storage genes (hex1.1 and lsd-2) relative to blood-dependent larvae. Linkage clusters and FST outliers identify a ca. 40 Mb genomic region that is highly differentiated between blood-independent and blood-dependent lines. Moreover, microRNAs and mRNAs related to reproductive physiology are differentially abundant between adults of the selected vs. control lines.ConclusionsMultiple lines of evidence indicate that larval nutrient sequestration and altered regulation of vitellogenesis have contributed to the rapid evolution of blood-independent reproduction in Ae. albopictus. Overlapping evidence also identifies proposed candidate genes affecting these processes. These results provide a foundation for developing novel paradigms to suppress disease transmission by vector mosquitoes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02670-z.Keywords: Autogeny, Blood-independent reproduction, Vitellogenesis, Reproductive physiology, Arbovirus transmission, Vector genetics, Aedes albopictus

Background

Females of many arthropod species feed on the blood of vertebrate hosts to obtain nutrients that can be used to provision their eggs with nourishing yolk. When mosquitoes blood feed, they can transmit pathogens such as dengue virus, Plasmodium spp., and filarial worms, leading to more than one million deaths annually [1]. However, some mosquitoes can produce mature eggs without blood feeding, a reproductive strategy called autogeny. Autogeny, hereafter referred to as “blood-independent reproduction,” has evolved multiple times independently from blood-dependent ancestors in mosquitoes [2–4], likely in part due to the under-appreciated but substantial physiological costs of blood feeding [5–8]. Determining the molecular and physiological regulation of mosquito reproduction both with and without access to blood is a fundamental and long-standing research goal [3, 9–11]. Additionally, such work has important public health implications because it could reveal novel targets to disrupt egg production or blood-feeding behavior in vector mosquitoes.

In all arthropods, reproduction depends crucially on the process of vitellogenesis. Vitellogenesis is a hormonally regulated process whereby the fat body synthesizes and secretes massive amounts of yolk protein precursors (YPPs) that are taken up by developing oocytes. In the well-studied, blood-dependent yellow fever mosquito (Aedes aegypti), vitellogenesis is initiated when blood feeding stimulates neurosecretory cells in the brain to release insulin-like peptides (ILPs) and ovary ecdysteroidogenic hormone (OEH) [12, 13]. In the blood-independent American rock pool mosquito, Ae. atropalpus, OEH is similarly required to initiate vitellogenesis and egg maturation, but Ae. atropalpus releases OEH shortly after adult emergence independent of a blood meal [14]. Thus, OEH is a central hormonal regulator of egg maturation in blood-dependent and blood-independent mosquito reproduction. However, the mechanisms downstream of OEH that enable blood-independent mosquitoes to mobilize nutrients to support vitellogenesis without a blood meal remain unresolved.

In addition to altered regulation of vitellogenesis, several lines of evidence imply that blood-independent reproduction requires shifting nutrient acquisition from the adult to the larval stage to support egg provisioning [4]. For example, many obligately non-blood-feeding mosquitoes are predatory as larvae (e.g., Toxorhynchites spp.), or consume proteinaceous detritus (e.g., pitcher-plant mosquitoes) [3, 4]. Furthermore, blood-independent reproduction is often associated with prolonged larval development, larger adult body size, and/or greater adult mass-specific nutritional reserves relative to blood-dependent reproduction [4, 11, 15–18]. Despite the presumed importance of larval nutrient acquisition for blood-independent reproduction at the adult stage, relatively few studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms by which larval resources are sequestered and carried over to the adult stage to support reproduction without blood (but see [19, 20]).

Complementing physiological studies, identifying the genetic basis of blood-independent reproduction in mosquitoes is a longstanding goal [21–24]. However, most previous genetic studies have relied primarily on crosses to infer inheritance patterns and lacked the resolution of modern genomics. This limitation is partly because genomic resources for mosquitoes were previously available primarily in two model vector species that are obligate blood feeders, Ae. aegypti and Anopheles coluzii. Furthermore, in non-model species that do exhibit variation in the propensity to reproduce without blood feeding, such as Ae. albopictus, direct selection for blood-independent reproduction is not always successful [25, 26]. However, recent advances in DNA sequencing technologies and additional genomic tools for non-model vector mosquitoes, as well as the recent establishment of a vigorously reproducing blood-independent population of Ae. albopictus [27], offer a valuable opportunity to fill the current knowledge gap regarding the genetic basis of blood-independent reproduction.

Here, we investigate genomic, transcriptional, and life-history differences associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction of the invasive Asian tiger mosquito, Ae. albopictus, a vector species of significant public health concern [28, 29]. While an ideal experimental design would involve replicate selected and control lines, this was not feasible given the challenges of producing a population capable of replacement without access to blood [25, 26]. As a result of using a single selected line, we cannot rule out genetic drift as a potential source of differentiation between the selected and control populations in our study. However, by integrating multiple types of evidence (life-history, genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and gene expression), we nonetheless identify physiological processes and proposed candidate genes associated with blood-independent reproduction that provide a basis for further investigation.

Our study design incorporates several significant advances relative to previous work on this topic (Fig. 1). First, we take advantage of a directly selected line that has rapidly evolved the ability to reproduce without blood [27]. This intraspecific approach expands on previous studies, which have primarily relied upon interspecific comparisons of blood-dependent and blood-independent reproductive physiology [3, 4, 11, 14], because it allows us to identify differences in reproductive physiology that are not confounded by unrelated, long-term evolutionary differences between species. Second, we utilize a recently developed Ae. albopictus SNP genotyping array [30] to perform high-resolution genomic analyses of differences between blood-dependent and blood-independent populations. Third, we conduct female-specific transcriptional profiling of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) at the adult stage, and targeted gene expression studies at the larval stage, to identify genes involved in blood-independent egg maturation and larval nutrient acquisition, respectively. Finally, we combine these analyses with studies of life-history differences at both the larval and adult stages. By integrating these diverse data, we identify physiological processes and proposed candidate genes associated with the evolution of the ability to reproduce without blood in a normally blood-dependent species.

Results

The blood-independent population is genetically distinct from two blood-dependent populations based on genome-wide SNPs

Our initial genomic analysis compared three populations (Fig. 1A): AUTO (blood-independent, collected from New Jersey), NON-AUTO FIELD (field samples from the AUTO collection site in New Jersey), and NON-AUTO (blood-dependent, collected from Virginia). The rationale for these population collections is described in detail in the Methods section (see Collection of NON-AUTO FIELD samples). Briefly, we were not able to maintain a parallel blood-fed control population while the AUTO population was undergoing selection for blood-independent reproduction. Therefore, the NON-AUTO FIELD samples were utilized as a representation of the genetic composition of the AUTO population before selection for blood-independent reproduction. Additionally, the geographically distinct NON-AUTO population was used as a control for life-history and gene expression experiments because previous studies indicate no detectable levels of genetic differentiation among eastern North America populations of Ae. albopictus (FST < 0.05; [31–33]), as corroborated by our results below.

We utilized a recently developed Ae. albopictus SNP genotyping array [30] to genotype samples from all three populations (AUTO n = 28, NON-AUTO FIELD n = 22, NON-AUTO n = 10). All AUTO, NON-AUTO, and NON-AUTO FIELD samples passed the quality control steps from the Axiom Suite software; genotype calling identified 129,381 SNPs. We removed one sample from the AUTO population due to excess heterozygosity. After quality control using Plink [34, 35], we obtained a dataset with 110,353 SNPs for the downstream analysis. The results of population clustering with principal component analysis (PCA) of these 110,353 quality-filtered SNPs are presented in Fig. 2 and show that the AUTO population is genetically distinct from the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations, which are not distinct from each other. Despite the greater variation of the NON-AUTO FIELD samples on PC2 of Fig. 2, which explains ca. 4% of the overall SNP frequency variation, the expected heterozygosity (He) of the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations do not differ (He of both populations = 0.33, bootstrap P = 0.68). However, the expected heterozygosity of the AUTO population is significantly reduced (He = 0.29, bootstrap P < 0.001) relative to the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations. Further results of the SNP-chip analysis are provided below.

Blood-independent females exhibit slower larval growth, larger adult size, and reduced size-specific fecundity relative to blood-dependent females

To confirm that the AUTO population females can complete ovary maturation without access to blood, we measured ovary maturation and wing-length specific fecundity of females from the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations. Additionally, to identify larval life-history traits associated with the ability to produce eggs without access to blood, we measured larval development period and adult size (i.e., wing length) of males and females from the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations (Fig. 1B).

Without access to blood, a higher proportion of females from the AUTO population (58.3%) produced at least one mature follicle relative to females from the NON-AUTO population (3.3%; χ21, 236 = 39.20; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the number of mature follicles produced by AUTO females between 3 and 8 days post-eclosion was higher than the number of mature follicles produced by NON-AUTO females of the same age range (χ21, 234 = 41.70, P < 0.001). Follicle production was not affected by age (χ21, 234 = 0.43; P = 0.51) or age-by-population interaction (χ21, 234 = 2.04; P = 0.15, Additional file 1: Figure S1).

Larval development time differed between populations and sexes (Kruskal–Wallis H = 75.04, P < 0.001; Fig. 3B). Follow-up pairwise comparisons indicate AUTO females took longer to pupate than NON-AUTO females (P < 0.001), and females of both populations took longer than males of either population (P < 0.01). Larval development time did not differ between AUTO males and NON-AUTO males (P = 0.06).

Wing length differed significantly between both populations (AUTO, NON-AUTO) and sexes (Kruskal–Wallis H = 82.557, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). Follow-up pairwise comparisons indicate that AUTO females had longer wings than NON-AUTO females (P < 0.001), and females of both populations had longer wings than their respective males (P < 0.001). Wing length did not differ between AUTO males and NON-AUTO males (P = 0.84).

Fecundity increased with wing length (χ21, 121 = 20.62; P < 0.001) and significantly differed among non-blood-fed AUTO, blood-fed AUTO, and blood-fed NON-AUTO (χ22, 121 = 164.59; P < 0.001; Fig. 3D). There was no interaction between population and wing length (P > 0.2), so the interaction term was removed from the final model. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons indicate that blood-fed NON-AUTO females exhibited greater wing-length specific fecundity than both blood-fed AUTO females (P = 0.006) and non-blood-fed AUTO females (P < 0.001). Additionally, blood-fed AUTO females exhibited greater wing-length specific fecundity than non-blood-fed AUTO females (P < 0.001).

Blood-independent female larvae exhibit higher levels of amino acid and lipidstorage transcripts, but not lipid metabolism transcripts

In order to identify candidate genes relevant to nutrient sequestration in AUTO larvae, we measured the abundance of mRNA transcripts encoding proteins involved in amino acid storage (Hexamerin-1.1 (hex-1.1)), lipid storage (lipid storage droplet-2 (lsd-2)), and lipid metabolism (fatty acid synthase (fas), diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (dgat-1)) in AUTO and NON-AUTO male and female fourth instar larvae. Transcript abundance of hex-1.1 was significantly higher in fourth instar larvae of AUTO females compared to NON-AUTO females (Wilcoxon test, W = 16, n = 8, P = 0.03; Fig. 4A), and in AUTO males compared to NON-AUTO males (Wilcoxon test, W = 16, n = 8, P = 0.03; Fig. 4B). The abundance of lsd-2 transcripts was also significantly higher in fourth instar larvae of AUTO females compared to NON-AUTO females (t = 5.14, df = 6, P = 0.002; Fig. 4C), but did not differ between AUTO vs. NON-AUTO males (Wilcoxon test, W = 8, n = 8, P-value = 1.0; Fig. 4D). Finally, we detected no difference in dgat-1 and fas mRNA abundance between AUTO and NON-AUTO females or males (dgat-1 females: t = 1.49, df = 5, P = 0.20, Fig. 4E; dgat-1 males: W = 8, n = 7, P = 0.63, Fig. 4F; fas females: t = 1.03, df = 5, P = 0.35, Fig. 4G; fas males W = 11, n = 7, P = 0.11, Fig. 4H).

Linkage cluster differences between populations

In order to identify candidate genomic regions associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population, we used the SNP-chip data to perform a linkage network analysis, FST outlier analysis, and a selection scan analysis (Fig. 1C).

The linkage network analysis was performed using the AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD samples with no missing genotype calls in each dataset, resulting in 7,102 SNPs on chromosome 1; 13,289 SNPs on chromosome 2; and 12,344 SNPs on chromosome 3. Clusters larger than 1Mb are listed in Additional file 2. A visual representation of the clusters over 1 MB in size is presented in Fig. 5A (NON-AUTO FIELD) and Fig. 5B (AUTO). As illustrated in Fig. 5, several distinct linkage clusters in the AUTO population are absent in the NON-AUTO FIELD population and vice versa. Notably, linkage cluster #14 on chromosome 2 in the AUTO population is not present in the NON-AUTO FIELD population (Fig. 5B).

A chromosomal region of strong genetic differentiation aligns with linkage cluster #14 in the AUTO population

Pairwise FST estimates indicate low genetic differentiation between the NON-AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD populations (FST = 0.04), but notably higher differentiation between NON-AUTO vs. AUTO (FST = 0.13) and NON-AUTO FIELD vs. AUTO (FST = 0.13) populations. The FST sliding window analysis revealed a notable FST peak on chromosome 2 between AUTO vs. NON-AUTO (Fig. 5C) and AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD populations (Fig. 5D), but not between NON-AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD (Fig. 5E). Importantly, this notable FST peak co-localizes with the previously identified linkage cluster #14 in the AUTO population (Fig. 5B, C, D). Thus, we combined the six scaffolds comprising linkage cluster #14 (Additional file 1: Figure S2) and the two adjacent scaffolds corresponding to the FST peak (2.41, 2.42) to define a candidate region associated with the rapid differentiation of the AUTO population relative to the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations. This candidate region is 40.3Mb in size and contains 735 annotated genes. The set of 735 genes is significantly enriched for genes in the Folate Transport and Metabolism KEGG Pathway (aalb04981, 6 genes, adjusted P < 0.01, Additional file 3: sheet 1). Additional, but less distinct, FST peaks were also present on chromosome 1, but the correspondence of these peaks to linkage clusters in the AUTO population is less clear (Fig. 5).

Selection scans identify candidate SNPs

Selection scans using pcadapt and OutFLANK identified 156 outlier SNPs detected by both methods that differed in either AUTO vs. NON-AUTO or AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD. These 156 SNPs correspond to 140 gene models (Additional file 3: sheet 2), but the gene list is not significantly enriched for any KEGG pathways. Additionally, 17 of these 156 SNPs were detected in both the comparison of AUTO vs. NON-AUTO and AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD. The allele and genotype frequencies for these 17 SNPs were highly divergent in the AUTO population relative to NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations, which were strongly similar (Additional file 1: Figure S3).

Extensive differential expression of mRNAs and miRNAs between blood-independent and blood-dependent populations

mRNA DE analysis

To identify additional candidate genes and physiological processes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction, we performed mRNA sequencing of head tissues from AUTO and NON-AUTO adult females 3 days post-eclosion that were maintained without access to blood. We chose to sample head tissue to identify gene expression differences specifically related to the hormonal control of reproductive physiology, and three days post-eclosion because that is the earliest timepoint at which differences in ovary maturation between the populations were observed (Additional file 1: Figure S1). mRNA sequencing produced between 12,059,252 and 18,457,308 quality-filtered reads per sample that mapped to annotated gene models with at least 10 reads summed across all biological replicate samples (Additional file 1: Table S1). The PCA plot shows a clear separation of mRNA expression in head tissue between AUTO and NON-AUTO biological replicate samples on the first principal component axis, which explains 76% of the variance (Additional file 1: Figure S4A). In total, we found 793 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations (adjusted P-value < 0.05, log2FC >|1|; Fig. 6A). 474 of these DEGs were upregulated in the NON-AUTO population (positive log2FC values) and 319 of these DEGs were down-regulated in the NON-AUTO population (negative log2FC values). The genes with the 10 highest and 10 lowest log2FC values are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2. A list of all significant DEGs is provided in Additional file 3: sheet 3, and the full description of all the mRNA gene expression results can be found in Additional file 4.

Small RNA sequencing of four biological replicate samples of AUTO and three biological replicate samples of NON-AUTO head tissue produced between 848,143 and 5,332,957 quality-filtered and size-sorted reads that mapped to 181 known and novel miRNAs with at least ten reads summed across all biological replicate samples (Additional file 1: Table S4). Discussion

In this study, we integrate genomic, life-history and gene-expression data to identify physiological processes and candidate genes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the invasive Asian tiger mosquito, Ae. albopictus, a vector species of significant public health concern. Aedes albopictus is an ecological generalist and low levels of blood-independent reproduction (autogeny) have been observed in multiple populations from across a wide range of the species distribution [18, 24–26, 36–39]. Thus, a capacity for limited blood-independent reproduction is likely a species-level characteristic that allows Ae. albopictus to colonize and/or persist in habitats with low host availability [18, 24]. As noted in the introduction, an ideal experimental design would include replicate selected and control lines. However, the AUTO population was established after the fortuitous observation of blood-independent reproduction in a laboratory colony, followed by laboratory selection [27]. Several previous attempts to establish self-sustaining (R0 > 1), blood-independent populations have been unsuccessful [25, 26]. Therefore, during laboratory selection, the population was maintained as a single large population rather than being split into replicate lines in order to maximize the possibility of establishing a population with R0 > 1 in the absence of blood feeding. Despite the limitations of working with a single selected line, our approach of focusing on overlapping evidence from multiple approaches (life-history, genome-wide SNPs, gene expression) to identify proposed candidate genes is intended to minimize the possibility of identifying differences between the selected and control lines caused by genetic drift.

The AUTO population is genetically distinct and capable of blood-independent reproduction

Both principal component (Fig. 2) and pairwise FST (Fig. 5E) analyses indicate a lack of differentiation between the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations, consistent with previous studies showing no detectable genetic differentiation among populations of Ae. albopictus from the mid-Atlantic and Northeast regions of the US (FST < 0.05; [31–33]). In contrast, both principal component (Fig. 2) and pairwise FST (Fig. 5C, D) analyses indicate that the selected AUTO population is genetically differentiated from both the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations. Furthermore, the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD samples exhibit equivalent levels of expected heterozygosity (He = 0.33), whereas the AUTO samples show a ca. 12% reduction in expected heterozygosity (He = 0.29), likely reflecting reduced effective population size during selection. Taken together, these results support the use of the unselected NON-AUTO FIELD or NON-AUTO populations as controls for genomic, life-history, and gene expression comparisons with the rapidly evolved AUTO population.

In addition to establishing genomic distinctions between the AUTO and two control populations, our results confirm that AUTO females can complete ovary maturation without nourishment from blood (Fig. 3A). A low percentage of NON-AUTO females also produced a small number of mature follicles without access to a blood meal (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Figure S1), consistent with previous observations of low levels of blood-independent reproduction (autogeny) in natural populations of Ae. albopictus [18, 24–26, 36–39].

Evidence of greater larval nutrient sequestration in the AUTO population

We found that AUTO females exhibited longer larval development times and larger adult wing lengths, a proxy for body size [40], relative to NON-AUTO females (Fig. 3B, C). In multiple mosquito species, longer larval development times are associated with increased acquisition and storage of nutrients, leading to adults with greater size and teneral reserves [10]. Although we did not directly measure teneral reserves, our results are consistent with previous findings that blood-independent adult females have greater energy reserves compared to blood-dependent females in Ae. albopictus [18] and other mosquito species [11, 17, 41]. Non-blood-fed AUTO females had lower size-specific fecundity than blood-fed NON-AUTO females (Fig. 3D), indicating that AUTO females cannot fully compensate for the absence of a blood meal. We also observed that females in the non-blood-fed AUTO group with a wing size below ca. 2.5 mm produced very few or no mature oocytes, suggesting a size-threshold for blood-independent reproduction (Fig. 3D). Several previous studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between adult size and blood-independent egg production [24, 42–44], with one study in Culex pipiens molestus noting an adult-size threshold for blood-independent egg production similar to our results [45]. Taken together, these results emphasize the importance of larval resource acquisition leading to larger adult size as a critical mechanism supporting adult blood-independent reproduction.

Our targeted gene expression results from larvae further support the importance of larval nutrient sequestration for blood-independent reproduction, and identify two candidate genes associated with this process. First, we found that AUTO female larvae exhibit a dramatic 95% increased abundance of hexamerin-1.1 transcripts relative to NON-AUTO females (Fig. 4A). Insect hexamerins are involved in the storage of amino acids and accumulate to high levels in larvae [46]. Thus, hexamerin-mediated amino acid storage during the larval stage may support adult YPP production and could also have a positive regulatory effect on vitellogenesis at the adult stage. Although vitellogenesis is largely a hormonally regulated process [47], amino acids have been shown to stimulate the expression of YPPs through the target of rapamycin (TOR) signaling pathway [48], and the injection of free amino acids into the hemolymph is sufficient to stimulate ovary maturation in five species of blood-dependent mosquitoes [49]. Second, we found that the abundance of lipid storage droplet protein 2 (lsd-2) mRNA is 42% higher in AUTO compared to NON-AUTO female 4th instar larvae (Fig. 4C). LSD2 localizes to the surface of lipid droplets and is associated with increased accumulation of triglycerides (TAGs) [50], implying that AUTO female larvae increase storage of TAGs that persist across metamorphosis to be mobilized during vitellogenesis. In blood-dependent mosquitoes, mobilization of TAGs typically occurs following a blood meal when TAG levels in the fat body drop and yolk deposition in developing oocytes rapidly increases [51]. Therefore, an increase in TAG accumulation before vitellogenesis is likely to support the provisioning of TAGs during vitellogenesis in blood-independent mosquitoes. Experimental manipulation of larval gene expression and direct measurement of teneral reserves are important next steps to test our proposed role for increased larval nutrient sequestration in the evolution of blood-independent reproduction.

Three genomic approaches to identify candidate genes

We utilized three genomic approaches to identify candidate genes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population: selection scans, identification of a highly differentiated chromosomal region, and quantification of gene expression in adults using RNA sequencing (RNAseq). While all three approaches could be impacted by genetic drift, we have attempted to minimize the effect of drift on the interpretation of our results by combining evidence from these three approaches as well as functional annotation information to prioritize candidate genes. We acknowledge that relying on functional annotations related to physiological processes that are a priori presumed to be important in blood-independent reproduction may lead to overlooking functionally important genes with a less obvious role. However, because so little is known about the genetic basis of blood-independent reproduction, identification of these proposed candidate genes represents a significant advance and an important platform for future functional genetics studies.

Selection scans identify candidate genes including a transcriptional activator of yolk proteins

Selection scans with PCadapt and OutFLANK identified 156 outlier SNPs corresponding to 140 gene models that were detected by both approaches between AUTO and at least one of the two control populations (NON-AUTO, NON-AUTO FIELD). A particularly compelling candidate gene from this group is LOC109407640, which contains three of the 156 outlier SNPs. Additionally, two of these three SNPs are among the 17 SNPs identified in both comparisons of AUTO vs. NON-AUTO and AUTO vs. NON-AUTO FIELD (Additional file 1: Figure S3). LOC109407640 is annotated as a Cyclic-AMP response element binding protein A (CrebA). In Drosophila melanogaster, CrebA regulates secretory activity in the salivary gland and epidermis [52], and is also a transcriptional activator that binds to fat-body-specific enhancers of yolk proteins genes [53]. Thus, based on both a strong signal from the selection scan and its annotated physiological function related to vitellogenesis, CrebA is a compelling candidate for a genetic determinant of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population.

A strongly differentiated genomic region is associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction

Separate genomic analyses based on distinct attributes of the genome-wide SNP data (linkage cluster analysis and genome-wide FST scans) both identify the same region of chromosome 2 as being associated with the rapid evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population (Fig. 5). Both linkage disequilibrium (LD) and FST can depend on the details of the demographic history of populations and be caused by multiple evolutionary forces including selection and genetic drift [54, 55]. The observation of both a distinct LD block within the AUTO population and increased FST in the same genomic region between the AUTO and both the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations is consistent with the expected effects of selection. However, because the AUTO population exhibits ~ 12% reduced heterozygosity relative to the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations, we cannot rule out a possible effect of genetic drift. Nevertheless, these results implicate the region comprised of linkage cluster #14 and the two adjacent scaffolds corresponding to the FST peak as a basis for identifying candidate genes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population. Interestingly, the 735 genes located in this candidate region are enriched for genes in the Folate Transport and Metabolism KEGG pathway (6 genes, Additional file 3: sheet 1), but none of the six genes in this pathway were differentially expressed or were identified by the selection scan. Folate (Vitamin B9) cannot be synthesized by mosquitoes, but is likely obtained at the adult stage from the gut microbiome. Furthermore, reducing microbiome-mediated vitamin B levels interferes with several aspects of vitellogenesis and fecundity in Ae. aegypti [56]. Thus, altered folate transport/absorption may contribute to blood-independent fecundity in the AUTO population, although this possibility requires additional experimental validation.

Evolution of gene expression related to blood feeding and vitellogenesis in adult females of AUTO vs. NON-AUTO

We found extensive differential expression of mRNAs (n = 793; Fig. 6A) and modest differential expression of miRNAs (n = 10, Fig. 6B) in the head tissue between AUTO and NON-AUTO females maintained under standardized conditions without access to a blood meal. Although we sampled head tissues in order to identify gene expression differences related to the neuroendocrine control of reproductive physiology, surprisingly, several transcripts related to vitellogenesis and ovary provisioning were four-fold or more abundant in AUTO vs. NON-AUTO head tissue (Table 1A, Additional file 4). These differences are likely due to the presence of metabolically active fat-body cells surrounding the brain, an underappreciated phenomenon that has been clearly demonstrated in Drosophila melanogaster [57]. This interpretation is further supported by high read counts of Vitellogenin and/or Vitellogenic carboxypeptidase (VitCarb) transcripts in our RNAseq data (Additional file 4), similar to previous RNAseq data from head tissues in other mosquitoes [8, 58, 59]. Although we were not able to control for potential difference differences in the volume of fat-body tissue in AUTO vs. NON-AUTO female heads, the dramatic expression differences of these genes are consistent with the unique physiological ability of AUTO females to initiate vitellogenesis shortly after eclosion without having consumed a blood meal [3, 60].

The extensive differences in gene expression we detected are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that direct selection on life-history or physiological performance traits can lead to rapid and widespread transcriptional changes [61–64]. We found that NON-AUTO females upregulate transcripts that support blood digestion (i.e., trypsins, chymotrypsins, aminopeptidases) [65], cuticular remodeling, and immunity (Table 1A). Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of DEGs in the “Lysosome” and “Pentose and glucuronate interconversion” pathways. All six DEGs in the “Pentose and glucuronate interconversion” pathway were upregulated in NON-AUTO females (Additional file 1: Table S3), suggesting that NON-AUTO females are prepared to deaminate or transaminate host amino acids into those necessary for vitellogenin, and convert pentose sugars, such as those found in host DNA, into glucose and lipids to provision their eggs.

We found that AUTO females upregulate mRNAs and miRNAs that degrade vitellin and initiate vitellogenesis relative to their blood-dependent counterparts (Table 1A, B), similar to blood-independent Cx. p. molestus and Wy. smithii [7, 8]. We also found that AUTO females upregulate miR-282-5p, an miRNA that inhibits juvenile hormone (JH) synthesis (Table 1B). In mosquitoes, reduced JH signaling precedes ecdysone-induced vitellogenesis in the fat body (reviewed by [3]). Because the developmental timepoint when these mosquitoes were collected coincides with vitellogenesis in the AUTO females, the upregulation of miR-282-5p in AUTO females is likely a mechanism of inhibiting JH signaling.

Combined evidence identifies candidate genes with functions related to vitellogenesis

In addition to the candidate genes discussed above, we identified two genes as proposed candidates for changes associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population because they were detected by at least two of the three genomic approaches (selection scans, candidate region, or differential expression) and had functional annotations related to adult reproductive physiology (Additional file 3: sheet 4; Fig. 7). The first candidate identified based on combined genomic evidence is VitCarb (LOC109398207). The VitCarb gene is located in the candidate genomic region and VitCarb transcripts were more abundant in head tissue of AUTO relative to NON-AUTO females. The canonical function of VitCarb is to degrade the yolk protein vitellin during embryogenesis [10]. However, our results suggest that the candidate VitCarb we identified may have a novel function in adult AUTO females, such as breaking down stored proteins carried over from the larval stage to release amino acids that support vitellogenesis and/or to stimulate vitellogenesis through the TOR signaling pathway as discussed above. The second candidate is an ecdysone-induced transcription factor (E74, LOC109418949) that is essential for regulation of vitellogenesis [47, 66]. The E74 gene has one SNP identified by the selection scan in the 3′ UTR and is located in the candidate genomic region. We hypothesize that E74 is differentially regulated in the adult female AUTO fat body to modulate vitellogenesis in the absence of blood meal relative to NON-AUTO. The identification of two candidate genes that are important components of vitellogenesis strongly implicates alteration of this crucial physiological process in the evolution of blood-independent reproduction of the AUTO population. Whereas blood-dependent reproduction involves rapid digestion of blood-derived proteins to support the nutritional and energetic requirements of vitellogenesis [3, 67], blood-independent reproduction involves mobilization of teneral reserves, including glycogen and lipids already stored in the fat body, as well as protein likely stored in the form of hexamerins in the hemolymph and fat body [10, 20, 68]. Thus, changing the intensity and/or duration of vitellogenesis in blood-independent individuals likely involves novel regulatory mechanisms to utilize stored nutrients that are typically rapidly acquired through blood feeding.

Conclusions

Understanding the physiological mechanisms and genetic basis of blood-independent reproduction (autogeny) in mosquitoes is a fundamental and long-standing challenge in insect reproductive physiology. However, modern genomic approaches have not yet been applied to this important problem, in part because traditional model mosquito species with the most well-developed genomic resources such as Ae. aegypti and An. coluzzi are obligately blood feeding. Our life-history (Fig. 3) and larval gene expression (Fig. 4) results implicate larval nutrient sequestration as an important physiological process associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction in the AUTO population. Additionally, the genome-wide SNP data identified a strongly differentiated genomic region between blood-dependent and blood-independent populations (Fig. 5). This genomic region, combined with results from both selection scans and the adult gene expression data (Fig. 7), implicates proposed candidate genes involved in vitellogenesis that are associated with the evolution of blood-independent reproduction. The evidence supporting the role of these proposed candidate genes in blood-independent (autogenous) reproduction is currently correlational, and an important future goal is to functionally validate their role using manipulative approaches such as RNA interference (RNAi). Previous work has demonstrated the importance of blood-independent release of ovary ecdysteroidogenic hormone (OEH) to initiating the process of vitellogenesis during blood-independent reproduction [11, 14], but our results are the first to identify candidate genes for blood-independent reproduction that appear to function downstream of OEH during vitellogenesis. Taken together, our results provide a strong foundation for functional genetic analyses of blood-independent reproduction and basic reproductive physiology in mosquitoes. Ultimately, such studies have the potential to establish novel paradigms to suppress disease transmission by vector mosquitoes through chemical or genetic modifications that disrupt reproduction or alter blood feeding behavior.

Methods

Establishment of the blood-dependent (NON-AUTO) colony

The blood-dependent, non-autogenous laboratory population (NON-AUTO) was established with over 200 larvae and pupae collected from at least 10 tires at an auto-salvage yard in Manassas, Virginia in 2018 as described previously [69, 70]. Prior to the initiation of experiments described in the current study, the population was reared under standard laboratory conditions as described previously for eight subsequent generations [31]. Briefly, the mosquitoes were maintained in a temperature-controlled walk-in incubator held at 21 °C, 80% relative humidity (RH), and long-day photoperiod (16:8 light:dark). Larvae were reared at a density of ca. 200 individuals in 5.5-L Sterlite containers with 2-L of deionized (DI) water. Every Monday-Wednesday-Friday (M-W–F), larvae were transferred to a new Sterlite container and fed 2 mL of larval food slurry consisting of 120 g of dog food (Nutro Ultra Small Breed Puppy, Nutro Products Inc., Franklin, TN) and 40 g of frozen brine shrimp (Sally’s Frozen Brine Shrimp, San Francisco Bay Brand, Newark, CA) in 1 L of DI water. Pupae were collected M-W–F from larval bins and transferred to adult cages consisting of 2.5-gallon plastic buckets with mesh covering the tops to let light in. Each adult cage contained approximately 200 adults. Adult cages were provisioned with organic raisins (Newman’s Own, Westport, CT) for ad libitum sugar feeding. One week after the last pupae were collected, all cages were blood-fed to repletion on a human host. The Georgetown University Institutional Review Board (IRB) has determined that mosquito blood feeding is not human research and thus does not require IRB approval; however, the blood feeding protocol has been approved by the Georgetown University Occupational Health and Safety Office. Three to four days after blood feeding, each adult cage was provisioned with a brown plastic oviposition cup half filled with DI water and lined with a strip of unbleached paper towel (i.e., oviposition paper). Oviposition papers were collected M-W–F, left moist for 48–72 h, and then gently dried and stored in Tupperware containers maintained in the walk-in controlled environment room under conditions described above. Eggs were stimulated to hatch in 5.5-L Sterlite containers with 2 L of DI water and 2 mL of larval food slurry to initiate the next generation.

Establishment of the blood-independent (AUTO) colony

The details on the establishment and laboratory maintenance of the blood-independent, autogenous population (AUTO) are described in [27]. Briefly, the population was established with eggs collected from oviposition cups placed throughout the Montclair State University Campus in Passaic County, New Jersey. An initial collection in July of 2019 led to the fortuitous observation of limited egg production before females were provided with a blood meal. As a result, additional collections were performed throughout August and September. The population was maintained with overlapping generations and the adults were never given access to a blood meal. This process led to an eventual establishment of the AUTO population with 451 adult Ae. albopictus that never received a blood meal. Eggs were hatched and larvae reared at 28 °C and a (16L:8D) photoperiod in white plastic deli containers filled with 600 mL dechlorinated water at low larval densities (max. of 30 individuals per container). Developing larvae were fed TetraMin Tropical Flakes (Spectrum Brands Pet LLC, Blacksburg, VA); first instar larvae received 0.025 mg per individual, and larger larvae received ca. 10 mg per individual. Pupae were transferred to a 60 cm3 adult cage with access to 8% sucrose solution maintained at an uncontrolled relative humidity that averaged ca. 60% RH. The adult cage was provisioned with three 0.5-L black plastic cups lined with paper towel (oviposition papers) and filled with 150 mL dechlorinated tap water. Oviposition papers were collected weekly, left moist for 48 h, and then submerged in 300 mL dechlorinated water to stimulate larval hatch. The population was maintained without access to blood and with overlapping generations from the fall of 2019 to the fall of 2021 at Montclair State University. After the fall of 2021, the population was transferred to Georgetown University and maintained as described for the NON-AUTO population above, except that larvae were fed approximately 0.4 g TetraMin Tropical Flakes every M-W–F, and both larvae and adults were maintained at 28 °C. We estimate that the AUTO population had been reared in the laboratory without access to blood (i.e., continuous selection for blood-independent reproduction) for ca. 60–70 generations when the experiments described in this study were performed.

Collection of NON-AUTO FIELD samples

We collected the NON-AUTO FIELD samples as free-living larvae in August 2021 from the Montclair State University campus, the same location in northern New Jersey where the AUTO population was collected (Fig. 1). Larvae were collected from discarded refuse containing small pools of rainwater across the campus. Larvae were returned to the laboratory and maintained at 28 °C with an LD photoperiod and fed TetraMin Tropical Flakes once a week with the amount varying depending on larval density and approximate size. After pupation, adults were anesthetized by cooling in a − 20 °C freezer and then transferred individually to 1.5mL tubes with 70% ethanol for preservation. Only one individual from each of 22 refuse containers was used for SNP-chip genotyping to reduce the probability of utilizing siblings. We utilized the NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD populations as controls in this study because it was not possible to maintain a parallel blood-fed control population while the AUTO population was undergoing selection for blood-independent reproduction. To verify the suitability of these control populations, we genotyped samples from all three populations on the Ae. albopictus SNP genotyping array (see below) to compare genome-wide SNP differentiation: (1) between the AUTO population and the NON-AUTO FIELD samples collected from the same location before selection (i.e., test for genomic differentiation related to selection), and (2) between NON-AUTO FIELD samples and the NON-AUTO population which were used for life-history comparisons to the AUTO population (i.e., test for genetic differentiation due to differences in geography).

Laboratory rearing to control for parental effects

For all of the life-history and gene expression experiments described below, the parents of the experimental animals were reared as larvae and adults under standardized conditions with the same photoperiod and temperature conditions as the experimental animals. NON-AUTO experimental animals were obtained from an F10-F21 laboratory colony, the AUTO experimental animals were obtained from a ca. F60-F70 laboratory colony. Briefly, the parents of the mosquitoes used for these experiments were reared at 28 °C with a 16:8 (light:dark) photoperiod. Larvae were maintained at a density of ca. 100–200 individuals in 5.5-L Sterlite containers with 2 L of DI water and 80–100 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes, with new food and water provided M-W–F. Pupae were collected every M-W–F and females were placed into population-specific mass-swarm cages provisioned with organic raisins (Newman’s Own, Westport, CT, USA) and 8% sucrose solution to allow adults ad libitum sugar feeding. To produce the experimental generation of eggs for each experiment, the NON-AUTO females were blood-fed whereas the AUTO females were not blood-fed, and eggs were collected from each population every M-W–F and gently dried as described above. Egg papers were stored in petri dishes within a closed Tupperware container containing a small cup of water to maintain humidity before initiation of experiments described below.

Blood-independent ovary maturation in AUTO and NON-AUTO

To test for differences in blood-independent reproduction, we compared ovary maturation in NON-AUTO and AUTO females without a blood meal. Eggs of the experimental generation for each population were placed in 5.5-L Sterlite containers with 2 L of DI water and 90 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes to stimulate hatch. Within 2 days of hatching, larvae were added to 5.5-L Sterlite containers containing 2 L of DI water and 90 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes at a maximal larval density of 200 (range, 194–200). New food and water were provided every M-W–F. We collected experimental pupae every M-W–F, sorted them by sex, and placed females into population-specific adult cages consisting of 2.5-quart plastic buckets in groups of 18 on average (range: 7–32). Males of each population were kept in separate large adult cages. To ensure experimental cages contained females of the same age, female pupae that did not eclose within 24 h were removed from the experimental cage. Within 24 h after all females within a cage had eclosed, males were added to experimental cages at a ratio of 1 male:5 females. All experimental adult cages were provisioned with organic raisins and 8% sucrose solution for ad libitum sugar feeding. Females did not receive a blood meal or an oviposition cup. Groups of 5–10 females were aspirated out of cages and frozen at daily intervals between 3 and 8 days post-eclosion. For each population (AUTO, NON-AUTO), 120 females were haphazardly selected across experimental adult cages and ovaries were dissected in order to determine the number of mature ovarian follicles per female. A mature follicle was defined as Stage IV or beyond, meaning the oocyte occupied at least 90% of the follicle, which had grown to a narrow shape typical of an egg [71].

To compare ovary maturation of the AUTO vs. NON-AUTO populations, we assigned each female a score of 0 (no developed follicles were present) or 1 (one or more mature follicles present) and analyzed this binary trait using a binomial generalized linear mixed model with population and age as fixed effects and adult rearing cage as a random effect. Initial models included all interactions but interaction terms were excluded from the final models if found to be non-significant (P > 0.2). Additionally, to compare the number of mature ovarian follicles produced across populations, we used a generalized linear mixed model with a negative binomial distribution to account for overdispersion (glmer.nb function in the lme4 R package, v. 1.1–35.1; [72]). Population and age of females were included as fixed effects, and adult rearing cage was included as a random effect. For all models, we performed a Type II Wald Chi Square test (Anova function in the car R package, v. 3.1–2, [73]) to determine the significance of each fixed effect.

Larval development period and adult size of AUTO and NON-AUTO

To determine if larval development period and adult size (wing length) differ between the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations, we conducted a larval growth assay under carefully standardized conditions. We used the dog food slurry described above as larval food for this experiment in order to control the larval nutrition more precisely than would have been possible with TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Experimental generation eggs were stimulated to hatch in 5.5-L Sterilite bins with 2 L of DI water and 1 mL of fresh dog food slurry. After 24 h, freshly hatched first-instar larvae were moved individually into single wells of 24-well cell culture plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY). Each well was established with a single first-instar larva in 1 mL of a homogenized diluted larval food solution consisting of 2.5 mL dog food slurry mixed with 500 mL of DI water. The 24-well plates were maintained in a Percival I-36VL incubator (Percival Scientific, Perry, IA) at 28 ℃. Four 24-well plates per population, corresponding with 96 larvae per population, were utilized in the experiment. Every M-W–F, 1 mL of the homogenized diluted larval food solution was added to each well of new 24-well plates, and larvae were transferred into new wells (near-optimal food level based on prior larval rearing experience). Starting on day six post-hatch, 2 mL diluted larval food solution was added to the new 24-well plates to account for increased food consumption of older larvae. Wells were checked at the same time daily for pupae, and larval development period was calculated as the number of days from first-instar hatch to pupation. Due to low levels of larval mortality during the assay, n = 91 AUTO and n = 89 NON-AUTO larvae were scored for larval development period. Pupae were removed from the 24-well plates, sexed, and then split into three 2.4-L adult cages per population consisting of 13–15 females and 15–16 males each. Each cage was established according to standard rearing protocol with a small petri dish containing raisins, approximately 100 mL of 8% sucrose, and an oviposition cup (although egg production was not measured in this experiment). Seven days after the last pupae were added to the cages, all individuals were aspirated and frozen at − 20 ℃ to measure wing length as a proxy for adult body size [40]. Frozen adults were haphazardly sampled (NON-AUTO males: n = 23, NON-AUTO females: n = 33, AUTO males: n = 18, AUTO females n = 31) to measure wing length as the distance from the apical notch to wing tip (not including fringe scales) on ImageJ [74]. Due to the non-normality of residuals, we performed a Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test to determine whether the different populations (AUTO and NON-AUTO) and sexes (female and male) differed in larval development period and wing length [75]. We performed pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests with continuity correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate to control for multiple comparisons to determine specific pairwise differences in wing length [75].

Wing length-specific fecundity of non-blood-fed AUTO, blood-fed AUTO, and blood-fed NON-AUTO

We measured the size-specific fecundity of non-blood-fed AUTO (n = 48), blood-fed AUTO (n = 44), and blood-fed NON-AUTO (n = 35) females. Experimental generation eggs from the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations were stimulated to hatch in separate 5.5 L Sterlite bins with 2 L DI water and 90 mg TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Within 2 days of hatching, larvae were counted and added to bins with a maximal density of 200 individuals (range, 163–200). Pupae were removed from bins every M-W–F, and the remaining larvae were moved into fresh DI water with 90 mg TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Pupae were transferred to adult cages which were maintained with adult females of the same age (days post-eclosion) within each cage as described above for the ovary maturation experiment. Within 24 h after all females within a cage had eclosed, males were then added to experimental cages at a ratio of 1 male:5 females. Non-blood-fed AUTO females were placed directly into oviposition vials (described below) 2 days after eclosion. All 6 NON-AUTO adult cages and 7 out of 11 of the AUTO adult cages were blood-fed 2 or 3 days post-eclosion. To improve blood-feeding rates, raisins and sucrose were removed 24–48 h before blood feeding. Females were visually inspected to confirm blood feeding and were placed in individual fly vials (50-mL, Genesee Scientific) approximately half filled with DI water containing a strip of unbleached paper towel for oviposition, and a raisin placed atop the vial’s mesh covering [76]. After 6 days in the oviposition vial, females were frozen, and oviposited eggs were collected and counted. Frozen females were later dissected, and mature follicles were counted to account for non-oviposited (= retained) eggs. Fecundity was calculated as the sum of the oviposited eggs plus dissected mature follicles. One wing from each female was imaged and measured as a proxy for body size [40] as described for the larval growth experiment above.

To compare size-specific fecundity across groups, we performed a linear mixed-effects model (lmer) with treatment (non-blood-fed AUTO, blood-fed AUTO, or blood-fed NON-AUTO) as a fixed effect, wing length as a covariate, and adult experimental cage as a random effect (lmer function in the lme4 R package, version 1.1–35.1; [72]. We then used a Type II Wald Chi Square test to determine the significance of fixed effects (Anova function in car R package, version 3.1–2; [73]. Finally, to determine which treatment groups differed in size-specific fecundity, we used the same model to perform pairwise post hoc multiple comparisons (emmeans function in the emmeans R package, version 1.10.4.900001; [75], with Tukey’s method to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Larval expression of amino acid storage, lipid storage, and lipid metabolism genes

Larval rearing and tissue generation

To determine if the expression of amino acid storage, lipid storage, and lipid metabolism genes differ between male and female fourth instar larvae of the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations, we compared sex-specific transcript abundance of hexamerin-1.1 (hex1.1), lipid storage droplet 2 (lsd-2), fatty acid synthase (fas), and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (dgat-1) using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). To generate tissue for qRT-PCR, experimental generation eggs were stimulated to hatch in a 28 °C incubator (model I-30 VL, Percival Scientific, Perry, IA, USA) in 50 cm diameter petri dishes with 100 mL of DI water and three drops of larval food slurry (described above). All larvae were maintained in the 28 °C incubator for the duration of the experiment. Within 24 h of hatching, larvae were moved into single wells of 24-well cell culture plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) and were provisioned with 1 mL of diluted larval food described above (2.5 mL of larval food slurry mixed with 500 mL of DI H2O). A total of three 24-well plates (72 larvae) per population were established over the course of 3 days. Every M-W–F, 1 mL of diluted larval food was added to each well of new 24-well plates and larvae were transferred into the new wells. Beginning 6 days post-hatch, 2 mL of diluted larval food was added to remaining wells every M-W–F to account for increased feeding of older larvae.

Wells were examined daily between Zeitgeber time 3 and 4 h to check for exuviae and record molting between instars. Twenty-five to 28 h after the molt to fourth instar (Zeitgeber time 4–7 h), larvae were removed from their wells and quickly rinsed with DI water. A small slice of the anal papillae of each larva was excised using a fresh 9-mm hypodermic needle (BD Precision-Glide ® Single Use Hypodermic Needles, Canaan, CT), and the extracted tissue was added to 9.5 µL of Dilution Buffer and DNARelease Additive (ThermoFisher Scientific, Philadelphia, PA) and stored at − 20 °C. Immediately upon excision of the anal papillae, larvae were flash frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored in individual 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes at − 80 ℃ for RNA extractions.

Larval genetic sexing assay

To determine the sex of individual larvae prior to creating male- and female-specific pools for RNA extraction, we PCR amplified an 833 bp region of the male determiner gene (Nix), located in the male-determining M-locus. Primers to amplify Nix were designed based on sequence provided in [77], while primers specific to a 495 bp region of the mitochondrial gene encoding nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide dehydrogenase subunit 5 (nd5) were used as positive controls (Additional file 1: Table S6) [78]. First, the frozen, excised anal papillae tissue in buffer from individual larvae (see above) was thawed on ice. Then, 1 µL of thawed sample was added to two separate PCR reactions (separate reactions for nd5 and Nix primers), where each reaction consisted of 10 µL of 2 × Phire Tissue Direct PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Philadelphia, PA), 5 µL of nuclease-free H2O, 2 µL each of 5 µmolar forward and 5 µmolar reverse primer. Additionally, each time a set of PCR reactions was performed, three additional template-control samples were established: (A) 1µL of a male leg tissue suspended in 20 µL of Dilution Buffer and DNARelease Additive (positive control), (B) 1 µL of the Dilution Buffer and DNA Release additive mixture without sample (negative control), (C) or 1 µL of nuclease-free H2O (negative control). The following cycling parameters were used: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 5 s, annealing at either 58.2 °C (nd5) or 65.2 °C (Nix) for 5 s, extension at 72 °C for 20 s, and a single final extension step at 72 °C for 1 min. Six microliters of amplified PCR product were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis (Additional file 1: Figure S5).

Since Nix is located in the male-determining M-locus, an 833 bp band corresponding to Nix was expected to only be present in male samples. No band was expected in female samples. The 495 bp band corresponding to nd5 was expected to be present in all samples. Only samples for which an nd5 band was present were used to determine sex. To verify the accuracy of the PCR sexing assay, we also allowed 16–20 larvae of each population to pupate in order to confirm sex based on morphology of the anal lobe. The assay was determined to be 100% accurate with 14 males and 22 females scored consistently based on both morphology and PCR amplification.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

In order to compare transcript abundance of fas, hex1.1, lsd-2, and dgat-1 separately in males and females, the sex was determined of individual larvae one day (25–28 h) after the molt to 4th instar using the PCR-based assay described above. Then, four separate male and four separate female biological replicate samples per population (AUTO and NON-AUTO) were established by combining between seven and nine individually frozen larvae per biological replicate sample on dry ice. To avoid confounding effects of outliers, only larvae that fell within the 95% quantile of days to reach 4th instar were included in each pool. RNA was then extracted by homogenizing pooled whole bodies in TRIzol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and performing a phenol–chloroform extraction followed by an isopropanol precipitation according to the manufacturer’s directions. RNA was assessed for purity and concentration using a NanoDrop (Fisher Scientific ®, Waltham, MA) and stored at − 80 ℃.

qRT-PCR reactions were prepared with Agilent’s Brilliant II SYBR® Green QRT-PCR 1-Step Master Mix (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in 96-well plates. Primers to amplify lsd-2 were designed based on sequences described in [79], while primers to amplify fas, dgat-1, and hex1.1 were designed using Primer3. Since there are multiple annotated hexamerins in the AalbF3 genome, the hex1.1 transcript we amplified was identified by searching for the best BLASTp match to the Ae. atropalpus hex1.2 gene used in a study by Zakharkin et al. [20], which had 100% query coverage, 83.65% identity and an E-value = 0. A complete list of primer sequences is provided in Additional file 1: Table S6. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate in a total volume of 12.5 μL with 25 ng RNA and 2.5 pmol primers using the following cycling parameters: 95 °C for 3 min followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Thermal cycling was performed using a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Melt curve analysis of qRT-PCR products verified only one product was amplified in each reaction.

The 2−ΔΔCt method [80] was used to determine the relative mRNA abundance (i.e., fold change) of fas, hex1.1, lsd-2, and dgat-1. First, the average CT of Ae. albopictus ribosomal protein S6 (AalRpS6) [81] was subtracted from the average CT of the target gene for each sample to determine the normalized abundance of the target gene in each sample. Next, the normalized abundance of the target gene for each sample was subtracted from the average normalized abundance of the target gene in AUTO samples. This value was used to exponentiate the base − 2 to calculate the relative fold change in expression of the target genes. Comparison of the average relative expression between female AUTO and NON-AUTO samples and male AUTO and NON-AUTO samples that were normally distributed and met assumptions of homogeneity of variance was analyzed using Student’s t-test; otherwise, data was analyzed using a Wilcoxon-rank-sum test [75].

Genomic differentiation based on SNP-chip analysis

Tissue preservation, DNA extraction, and chip hybridization

Procedures for colony establishment (AUTO, NON-AUTO) and field collection (NON-AUTO FIELD) are described above. Whole adult mosquitoes from the ca. F70 AUTO (n = 29), F0 NON-AUTO (n = 10), and F0 NON-AUTO FIELD (n = 22) collections described above were preserved in individual 1.5mL tubes in either 70% ethanol or at − 80 °C before DNA extraction. The NON-AUTO samples were originally genotyped on the SNP chip as part of a separate study [30], and at the time of the current study, it was not possible to add additional NON-AUTO samples for genotyping. DNA was extracted from whole preserved adults using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen). We followed the manufacturer’s instructions for the purification of total DNA from insects with the following slight modifications: instead of a mortar and pestle or electric homogenizer, we used a bead beater and then lysed samples overnight on a Thermomixer. In the final step, we eluted the samples in 100–200 µL of 1% Tris–EDTA buffer. We also performed an additional step of treating our samples with 4 µL of RNase A (Qiagen). We stored all samples at − 20 °C and then concentrated and purified the DNA using AmiconR Ultra 30 k centrifugal filter devices (Millipore), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We obtained approximately 23 µL of elute. Next, we quantified the genomic DNA concentration using Qubit 1 × dsDNA HS Assay Kits (Invitrogen). DNA samples were normalized to a concentration of 20 ng/µL and 20 µL of each sample was sent to Affymetrix for genotyping on the recently created Aealbo SNP chip [30]. The chip contains probes for 175,296 SNPs in coding and non-coding regions of all three chromosomes. The average density is 102 SNPs per 1 Mb window, with at least one SNP in each of the 17,461 annotated protein-coding genes.

Genotype calls and genotype filtering

The unprocessed SNP chip data is available from a Zenodo repository [82]. The code used for all processing and analyses is available in RMarkdown and HTML formats (Additional file 1: Table S7). We used the Affymetrix “Best Practices Workflow” to perform quality control (QC) analysis. First, we ran the post-processing off-target variants (OTV) caller algorithm to identify miscalled clusters. We also obtained the SSToll from Affymetrix to create population-specific priors.

After genotyping all the samples, we used additional quality control measures to filter the SNP dataset using Plink v 1.9 and v 2.0 [34, 35]. Details are described in Additional file 1: Table S7 and Table S8. Briefly, quality control filtering consisted of removing (1) loci with more than 20% missingness, (2) individuals with more than 20% missing loci, (3) loci that failed Hardy–Weinberg tests with a threshold of 0.00001 for each population, (4) loci with a minor allele frequency less than 1%, (5) samples whose expected heterozygosity values deviated more than ± 4 standard deviations from the mean of all samples (an indication of low DNA quality, contamination, or high inbreeding), and (6) related individuals using a relatedness threshold score of 0.354. For the linkage network analysis (see below), we used a minor allele frequency threshold of 5% for each population and only included loci that were genotyped in all three populations (AUTO, NON-AUTO, NON-AUTO FIELD; i.e., no missing genotype calls).

Population clustering with principal component analysis (PCA)

We performed PCA using Plink with its default parameters [34, 35], which included variance-standardizing the genotype data before the calculation. We visualized the first two principal components by plotting the data with the R package ggplot2.

Chromosomal location of SNPs

For the linkage network and chromosomal genetic differentiation analyses described below, we first compiled a dataset of quality-filtered genotypes for all three populations (AUTO, NON-AUTO, NON-AUTO FIELD) with zero genotype missingness as described above. We then subdivided this dataset per population, applying a minor allele frequency threshold of 5%. Next, we identified the intersection of SNPs across the three populations. We created a dataset for each population containing an equal number of SNPs, all free from genotype missingness. We merged scaffolds into chromosomes to establish a chromosomal scale, following the order outlined in the AalbF3 genome assembly [76]. We then divided the data into chromosomes for each population.

Within population diversity

Expected heterozygosity was calculated using hierfstat in R on 41,620 LD-pruned SNPs (PLINK –indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2). LD pruning was implemented to ensure independent markers for unbiased diversity estimates. Differences between populations were determined based on 95% confidence intervals estimated via bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates).

Linkage network analysis

We used the R package LDna [54] to perform detailed linkage network analyses for each chromosome in the NON-AUTO FIELD and AUTO populations; the NON-AUTO population was excluded from this analysis due to the low sample size (n = 10). We employed Plink to estimate pairwise linkage for all SNPs on each chromosome. Next, we utilized LDna [54] to identify linkage disequilibrium (LD) networks. We used the option “branch.traversal = TRUE” to compare the linkage clusters across our datasets, implementing “branch traversal” for cluster extraction. In this process, clusters are identified either at the base of their branch or further down the branch if the lambda value remains above a specified threshold. We also used the option “edges_min = 20” to specify the minimum number of edges a cluster must have to be displayed as a branch in a tree structure. Clusters with fewer edges than threshold [20] will not appear as distinct branches. These networks visually represent the non-random association of alleles at different loci in a population.

Chromosomal genetic differentiation

We performed pairwise FST estimates across all combinations of population pairs using the R package StAMPP 9 [83] (Additional file 1: Table S7). Next, we utilized vcftools [84] to perform a sliding window FST analysis with a 100 kb window with a 10 kb step in pairwise comparisons between populations.

Selection scans

We used selection scans on SNP data from the AUTO, NON-AUTO, and NON-AUTO FIELD populations to identify the specific SNPs associated with the rapid evolution of the AUTO population. The analyses were performed in the R packages OutFLANK [55] and pcadapt [85, 86] as described in detail in Additional file 1: Table S7. For the OutFLANK analysis, we inferred the distribution of FST values for neutral markers using the intergenic SNPs and used that distribution to identify outlier loci indicative of selection. For the pcadapt analysis, we used Mahalanobis distance to detect outlier loci in a PCA [85]. We first conducted analyses of AUTO, NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD together, and then separately compared the two blood-dependent populations (NON-AUTO and NON-AUTO FIELD) with the blood-independent population (AUTO).

Variant functional annotation

We used SnpEff [87] with the AalbF3 [76] annotation to perform the functional annotation of the SNPs. We provide the step-by-step of analyses in Additional file 1: Table S7.

RNAseq of mRNAs and miRNAs in the AUTO and NON-AUTO populations

Tissue generation

Experimental generation eggs were stimulated to hatch by placement into 2 L DI water with 100 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Within 24 h of hatching, larvae were transferred to 5.5-L Sterlite containers at a density of 200 larvae per container and reared in 2 L of DI water with 100 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Every M-W–F larvae were transferred to fresh water and provisioned with 100 mg of TetraMin Tropical Flakes. Pupae were collected M-W–F and sorted by sex based on genital lobe morphology, and all male pupae were pooled into two 9.5-L adult cages per population. Female pupae were transferred to new 2.4-L adult experimental cages and given 24 h to eclose. Any pupae that had not eclosed were put in a new 2.4-L cage and given an additional 24 h to eclose. This was repeated daily until the final pupae were collected. Thus, the experimental adults in each cage had eclosed within a 24-h period in order to minimize physiological differences related to adult age. After all the females within a cage had eclosed, males were added to the cage to create a ratio of 1 male:5 females.

Because we were interested in identifying gene expression differences related to the neuroendocrine control of blood-independent reproduction, adult female head tissue collections were performed 72 to 96 h after female eclosion without access to a blood meal. This post-eclosion time range was chosen because most blood-independent mosquitoes develop the functional competence to blood feed between 24 and 78 h after eclosion [88–90]. Additionally, 72–96 h represents the earliest time point the AUTO females began to oviposit without a blood meal, and our goal was to identify transcriptional processes initiated in the AUTO population that allow them to complete ovary maturation without access to a blood meal. To perform tissue collections, adult females from the experimental cages were transferred into a clear, plexiglass cage 30 cm (height) × 30 cm (width) × 53 cm (length) at room temperature (20–23 °C). Males were removed from the plexiglass cage before the start of female collections. Fifteen to 20 min after acclimation in the plexiglass cage, females were aspirated into a collection tube and snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen for 30 s. All collections occurred between Zeitgeber time 11 and 13 h. Following flash freezing, heads were isolated by cutting anterior to the thorax with a scalpel on dry ice. Head tissue was then stored at − 80 °C before RNA extraction.

Four AUTO biological replicate, samples of head tissue were produced for RNA extraction and sequencing by pooling tissues from 10 independent adult experimental cages and tissue collections. Four NON-AUTO biological replicate, samples of head tissue were also collected across five independent cohort cages/tissue collections. For both the AUTO and NON-AUTO samples, no cage/tissue collection contributed to more than one biological replicate sample, and each sample consisted of 36–58 heads. The samples were shipped in 500 µL TRIzol (Invitrogen) with dry ice to the University of Oregon Genomics and Cell characterization Core Facility. Tissue homogenization, RNA extraction, integrity assessment, cDNA library preparation, and sequencing were performed as described previously [91]. Briefly, samples were homogenized using silica grinding beads in a Spex Genogrinder 2100 (2 × 1500 RPM for 2 min) and RNA was extracted using a Zymo Direct-Zol kit (Zymo Research). RNA samples were assessed for integrity on an RNA chip (Bioanalyzer 2100), and mRNA was isolated using Oligo dT beads according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These samples were then used for paired-end, barcoded, and stranded library construction with the Universal Plus mRNA-Seq protocol (Tecan Genomics). The eight libraries were combined in equimolar ratios and then sequenced on a single lane of a NovaSeq 6000. Additionally, small-RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using Perkin Elmer’s NextFlex small RNAseq kit v3 (NOVA-5132–05) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Size selection was performed using the gel-free method as described in the manual. All eight libraries were combined in equimolar ratios and single-end, 160-bp reads were sequenced on a single lane of NovaSeq 6000. Raw mRNA and miRNA reads are available in NCBI’s short read archive (SRA) under accession PRJNA1221572.

mRNA DE analysis

The bioinformatics analyses for differential expression of mRNAs follow the procedures described in [92]. A GitHub repository [93] provides a detailed description of the bioinformatics workflow and the relevant coding scripts. Briefly, we first used Trimmomatic (version 0.39; [94]) to quality trim sequences and remove adapters. We then used FastQC (version 0.11.9; [95]) to confirm the quality of the remaining reads for each sample. Read mapping was performed using a two-pass method in STAR (v2.7.1a; [96]) with the AalbF3 (GCA_018104305.1) reference genome sequence [76]. The aligned sam files were converted to bam format and indexed using samtools (v1.9; [97]). We then obtained read counts using HTSeq (version 0.13.5; [98]) and retained only transcripts with at least 10 reads across all eight biological replicates.

Downstream data visualization and differential expression analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2). To visualize the overall transcriptional clustering of biological replicate samples, we performed a PCA (plotPCA function in the DESeq2 package version 1.40.2; [99]) using variance stabilizing transformed read counts (varianceStabilizingTransformation function in R). We then performed a differential expression analysis using the DESeq2 package with fold change shrinkage using the apeglm package [100]. We identified DE genes with a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjusted P-value less than 0.05 and a log2 fold change >|1|.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using a custom script in R. We first obtained a list of Ae. albopictus KEGG pathways IDs and genes in each pathway using the KEGGREST package (version 1.40.0; [101]). For each pathway, we used a Wilcoxon Rank Sum test to determine whether the adjusted P-values of genes in the pathway were significantly lower than the adjusted P-values of genes not in that pathway. We identified enriched pathways as those with a Wilcoxon Rank Sum test P-value less than 0.05 and greater than four DE genes.

miRNA DE analysis

The bioinformatics workflow for the miRNA differential expression analysis follows the procedures described in [91]. A GitHub repository [102] provides additional details and coding scripts. Before quality filtering, one NON-AUTO replicate sample was removed from the miRNA dataset as an outlier since it contained a small number of reads (3,925) compared to the other replicate samples (each of which had > 9 million reads). Next, reads were cleaned with Trimmomatic to remove Illumina small RNA NexFlex adapters and low-quality reads. tRNA and rRNA reads were removed by aligning the reads to the tRNA and rRNA features from the Ae. albopictus reference genome, AalbF3 (GCA_018104305.1) [76] using bowtie2 (v2.4.4; [103]). Additionally, the first four bases of each single-end read were removed, and a custom Python script was used to size-sort reads and retain only those that fell within 18–24 base-pairs, the expected size of mosquito miRNAs [104].

miRDeep2 was used for miRNA identification and mapping (miRDeep2.0.1.3; [105]). Known mature miRNA sequences were obtained for Ae. albopictus from previous studies [106–110], and exact sequence duplicates were removed from the combined list, resulting in 304 unique known mature miRNA sequences (Additional file 6: sheet 1). Forty-two additional novel miRNAs with unique precursor sequences were identified by miRDeep2 from our data set based on threshold criteria of ten reads for both mature and star miRNA sequences, a significant and fold P-value, and a miRDeep score greater than 4 (Additional file 6: sheet 2). Using the 42 novel and 304 known miRNAs, miRDeep2 generated a list of 181 unique matched precursor and mature miRNAs with at least ten reads across all samples (Additional file 6: sheet 3). After obtaining read counts from miRDeep2, all downstream analysis was performed in R (version 4.0.2). Principal component and differential expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 (version 1.30.1) with the same processes as described above for the mRNA data.

Combined evidence from genomic analyses

To identify candidate genes from the genomic analyses, we first constructed lists of gene models associated with each approach (selection scan, candidate region, differential expression; Additional file 3). The list of gene models based on the selection scan included 109 gene models for which one or more SNPs identified by the selection scan were located in the gene model, and 31 gene models that were closest to the remaining intergenic SNPs. As described above, gene models based on the differential expression analysis were those with a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjusted P-value less than 0.05 and a log2 fold change >|1| (n = 793). Gene models based on the candidate region on chromosome 2 included all annotated features in that region (n = 735). Genes detected by more than one approach were identified using the VennDiagram package in R. In addition to the KEGG enrichment test described above for genes identified as differentially expressed by RNAseq, we also performed a KEGG pathway over-representation analysis (ORA) in the R package clusterProfiler [111] for the set of genes identified in the selection scan and those located in the candidate region, as well genes that were identified by more than one genomic approach (selection scan, candidate region, differential expression; see Fig. 7, Additional file 3: sheet 4). For the ORA analyses, we used the full set of Ae. albopictus gene models (n = 21,553) as the universal background set.
