Quality and Readability Assessment of Patient-Centered Online Information on Apical Surgery
Authors: Pablo Álvarez-Nóvoa, Ramón Miguéns-Vila, Benjamín Martín-Biedma, Gustavo De-Deus, María Pilar Pecci-Lloret, Miriam Teulé-Trull, Juan Seoane-Romero, Pablo Castelo-Baz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsNowadays, patients seek medical information online. Patient-oriented content must be easy to read and trustworthy. This study aimed to assess the quality and readability of online information about apical surgery and apicoectomy in English and Spanish.MethodsThe authors performed the following systematic searches on Google in February 2023: “apicoectomy”, “apicectomía”, “apical surgery”, and “cirugía apical”. The first 100 websites of each query were selected. English readability was assessed using Flesch-Kincaid Reading Grade Level, Flesch Reading Ease Score, Gunning Fog Index, Coleman-Liau Index, Automated Readability Index and Simple Measure of Gobbledygook Index. The Fernández-Huerta Index and INFLESZ were used to assess Spanish readability. Quality was measured using the DISCERN tool.ResultsA total of 165 sites were included in total. Readability for English-language sites (“apical surgery” and “apicoectomy”) was categorised as “fairly difficult to read” [FRES (apical surgery) = 54.7; FRES (apicoectomy) = 54.3]. Similarly, Spanish sites (“cirugía apical” and “apicectomía”) were classified as “relatively difficult to read”. Overall, the DISCERN tool showed a low average quality of information for all terms analysed.ConclusionEnglish and Spanish online information about apical surgery is difficult for the average patient to understand and presents significant quality deficiencies.Clinical relevanceThe Internet is a powerful tool for communicating with patients, but available apical surgery information is difficult for laypersons to understand and has a low overall quality. To overcome this issue, endodontists should produce high-quality, patient-relevant materials in plain language.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the percentage of the population regularly using the Internet has been steadily increasing. In 2019 alone, there were 366 million new users, meaning that almost 60% of the global population is now “online” and has access, almost instantly, to more information than ever before in history.1

Since the mid-twentieth century, the relationship between healthcare providers and patients has shifted from traditional paternalistic medicine to a healthcare model emphasising patient autonomy and active participation in treatment decision-making. Information is a cornerstone of this doctor-patient relationship model, as patients have the right to access all available information related to any medical procedure that concerns them. Correspondingly, healthcare professionals must provide understandable and appropriate information that meets the specific needs of the patient.2,3

The development of internet access at the end of the 20th century and its globalisation during the last few decades have only accelerated this change process. With a growing number of patients using the Internet as a tool, there is virtually unlimited access to information on medical issues. It is not surprising that a substantial percentage of the population, such as 60% of Europeans and 80% of North Americans, admit to having searched for medical information on the Internet.4

Dentistry is not excluded from this situation, and certain topics such as aesthetic dentistry treatments, implantology, periodontics, oral pathology, and oral cancer generate significant interest among patients, accounting for the majority of search engine queries.5, 6, 7, 8

Currently, there is a vast amount of web-based information available on different treatment options. Although patients seem to find the information found online reliable and trustworthy,9 the reality may be different. The quality of medical information available on the Internet is often highly heterogeneous and may lack a scientific basis, and is rarely peer-reviewed.4,10 Therefore, access to flawed information may negatively impact patient decision-making.

For information to be useful and accessible to patients, it must meet 2 fundamental criteria: it should be high quality and understandable for the layperson. To evaluate the quality of medical information available online for patients, specific tools such as DISCERN have been developed for qualitative assessment.11 Additionally, it is important to consider the readability of the information, which refers to the ease with which a person can understand a text. A medical text may have a high degree of scientific quality, but if it is not understandable to the public due to complex language, it loses its practical value. For this reason, to assess readability, tools such as the Flesch-Kincaid Reading Grade Level (FKRGL) or the SMOG index (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook)12 are recommended.

One area where this issue is particularly relevant is apical surgery and apicoectomy, which are frequently used synonymously despite referring to distinct procedures. Apicoectomy is the surgical procedure of removing the tooth apex, without considering fundamental aspects of apical surgery, such as root-end sealing. While there has been research on the quality and readability of online information related to endodontics, there is currently no such research on apical surgery.10 This is a significant problem because patients often search for information online, and inaccurate or misleading information can lead to poor treatment decisions.

To address this gap, the current study aims to assess the quality, accessibility, and readability of patient-targeted information on apical surgery and apicoectomy in Spanish and English, the second and third most widely spoken languages in the world, respectively.13 The findings may improve the quality of online information and provide patients with accurate, reliable resources to support informed decision-making.

Methods

Search strategy

The authors conducted an electronic search in February 2023 using the Google® search engine. The following terms were used individually: “apicoectomy”, “apicectomía”, “apical surgery” and “cirugía apical”. For each search term, the results were displayed in sets of 10 websites per page. We considered the first 100 results for each term, resulting in a total of 400 websites being reviewed.

To be included in the study, websites had to provide information specifically aimed at patients regarding apicoectomy or apical surgery, in either English or Spanish.12 We excluded websites that were duplicated, unavailable, primarily commercial, irrelevant to the topic, video-based, or intended for healthcare professionals. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, 235 websites were discarded: 33 related to “apicoectomy”, 61 to “apicectomía”, 69 to “apical surgery”, and 72 to “cirugía apical”.

All websites were independently analysed by 2 reviewers (R.M.-V., P.A.-N.) according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through discussion and consensus with the involvement of a third reviewer (P.C.-B).

Quality assessment

The DISCERN tool11 was used to assess the quality of the information contained in the webpages selected for analysis, both in English and Spanish. This instrument consists of 15 items organised into 2 sections: the first section evaluates the reliability of the information provided (items 1-8), and the second section focuses on the quality of information regarding treatment options (items 9-15). Each item is scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 representing the lowest quality and 5 the highest. A final global score (item 16) is included, following the same scale, to help objectively identify potential shortcomings in the analysed information.

Readability assessment

Readability was assessed using several tools for English-language websites, including the Flesch-Kincaid Reading Grade Level (FKRGL), Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES), Gunning Fog Index (GFI), Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG), Coleman-Liau Index (CLI), and Automated Readability Index (ARI), as shown in Table 1. These tools were accessed via the Readability Test platform (https://www.webfx.com/tools/read-able/),14 which automatically calculates index scores after entering the URL or plain text. The readability scores obtained vary according to the tool used. For example, FRES classifies texts as: “very difficult” (0-30, scientific literature), “difficult” (30-50, university level), “relatively difficult” (50-60, high school level), normal (60-70), “relatively easy” (70-80), “easy” (80-90), and “very easy” (90-100, understandable by an 11-year-old). The FKRGL provides the required educational level for comprehension, with “easy” indicating texts suitable for children under 12, and “difficult” for a reading age above 16.7 Similarly, SMOG, ARI, GFI, and CLI yield a grade-level score reflecting the minimum U.S. education level needed for understanding.

For Spanish-language websites, readability was evaluated using the INFLESZ scale and Fernández-Huerta formula, also shown in Table 1. The INFLESZ scale adapts the FRES method to Spanish reading norms, with 5 interpretative ranges: "very difficult" (0-40), "relatively difficult" (40-55), "normal" (55-65), "relatively easy" (65-80), and "very easy" (80-100).15 The Fernández-Huerta index is a direct adaptation of the FRES method to the Spanish language, using equivalent scoring. Its categories are: “very easy” (90-100), “easy” (80-90), “relatively easy” (70-80), “normal” (60-70), “relatively difficult” (50-60), “difficult” (30-50), and “very difficult” (0-30). Both analyses (Fernández-Huerta and INFLESZ) were conducted using a dedicated platform (http://www.legible.es).

For statistical purposes, results from FRES and Fernández-Huerta were grouped into 3 categories: “difficult” (FRES ≤ 60; FH ≤ 60), “normal” (FRES 60-70; FH 60-70), and “easy” (FRES ≥ 70; FH ≥ 70).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the statistical package IBM® SPSS® Statistics 27.0 (IBM) for Mac. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each variable, including the mean and median as measures of central tendency, and the SD and IQR as measures of dispersion.

To determine whether the data followed a normal distribution, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied. Since the samples did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were applied: the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the DISCERN scores across the 4 search terms and the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare readability and DISCERN values between English and spanish websites. The level of significance was set at P = .05.

We observed that the webpages resulting from the terms “apical surgery” and “apicoectomy” were ‘relatively difficult to read’, equivalent to the level of education of a student in the final stage of secondary education based on the FRES index (FRES (apical surgery) = 54.7, IQR [48.1 - 62.0]; FRES (apicoectomy) = 54.3, IQR [49.3-60.4]). Similarly, based on the Fernández-Huerta index, and in line with the INFLESZ scale, Spanish web pages were classified as 'relatively difficult to read, at the level of a pre-university student (Fernandez-Huerta (cirugía apical) = 54.7, IQR [50.4-60.4]; Fernandez-Huerta (apicectomía)= 59.2, IQR [47.8-57.9]).

No statistically significant differences in FRES and Fernandez-Huerta values were found between the different search terms in both languages (p = .27).

Overall, the evaluation of the quality of information displayed on the web pages using the DISCERN tool showed a low average quality of information for all terms analysed. Discussion

During the last few decades, particularly the last third of the 20th century, the practice of medicine has shifted from a paternalistic model based on the principle of beneficence, which created an unequal relationship between doctors and patients, to a more patient-centric approach, characterised by a horizontal relationship based on a peer-to-peer dynamic.16,17 The medical monopoly on health-related knowledge has been replaced by more universal and accessible sources of information, mainly due to the expansion and development of information and communication technologies, particularly the Internet.18 Although primary care doctors are still considered trustworthy sources of information, it is worth noting that health-related topics are frequently searched for on the Internet, which means that a large number of patients use online resources as their main source of information.19

The Internet is undoubtedly a valuable tool for accessing information globally and instantly, and the amount of medical information available online is increasing daily. However, to be truly useful for patients (and society), this information must be easily accessible, understandable, and highly trustworthy. Patients primarily access health information through search engines.18 In our study, Google® was used as the source of information, which accounts for 91.94% of global search engine traffic (Search Engine Market Share Worldwide, Statcounter Global Stats).20

It is not uncommon for the terms "apicoectomy" and "apical surgery" to be used synonymously in scientific literature, which can confuse patients seeking online information. Although the term “apical surgery” is preferred from an academic standpoint, we deemed it appropriate to compare the quality and readability of information for both terms.

Currently, anyone with Internet access can find a vast amount of information with just a few clicks. For instance, a Google search for "apicoectomy" yields 1,700,000 results, while "apical surgery" generates 15,200,000 results. Similarly, "apicectomía" and "cirugía apical" generate 53,000, and 267,000 results, respectively. With such vast quantities of information readily available, it is safe to say that the first criterion for accessibility has been met, at least for the 4.6 billion people who have Internet access worldwide (Internet world stats: Internet usage statistics).

However, the number of results is only an indicator of topic popularity. When it comes to locating and selecting, the average user rarely goes beyond the first page of search results. Therefore, our study analysed a total of 165 pages extracted from 400 search results, in 2 different languages: English and Spanish. These languages encompass approximately 1.5 billion speakers (983 million English speakers, the second most spoken language, and 527 million Spanish speakers, the third).13 The inclusion of 2 related terms ("apicoectomy" and "apical surgery") also contributes to the relevance of this study.

Nonetheless, the existence of substantial web content on a given topic does not necessarily equate to true accessibility, especially in terms of readability and comprehensibility. Most medical information available online is written at the level of a 16-year old (10th grade) student. This contrasts with the fact that the average adult reads at the level of a 13 to 14-year-old adolescent, with 20% reading at or below the level of an 11-year-old.21 Unsurprisingly, nearly half of the adult population struggles to understand this type of information, which may negatively impact individual and community health.22 Our findings are consistent with these concerns, as the websites in both languages were categorised as “relatively difficult to read”. The comparability of readability scales across languages, such as the FRES and Fernández-Huerta indices used in this study,12 offers an interesting tool for multilingual analysis.

Similar results have been reported in the dental literature. For instance, Alpaydin et al. found that online data regarding clear aligners in English showed poor quality and readability (87% scored poor/very poor with DISCERN; 66.6% classified as fairly or very difficult to read).23 Likewise, Ayala Aguirre observed that only 2.3% of web content on early childhood caries (in English, Spanish, and Portuguese) provided information of fair quality;24 Incerti Parenti et al. reported comparable results for mandibular advancement devices used in obstructive sleep apnea (mean DISCERN = 39.93 [95% CI: 37.90-41.96]; mean FRES = 59.50 [95% CI: 57.58-61.42]).25 Alshehri et al. similarly found that information on prosthodontic treatments was difficult to read (median FRES = 48.06 for educational websites vs. 54.74 for commercial ones) and of low quality (median DISCERN = 2 for educational vs. 1 for commercial sources).26 These studies illustrate that high-quality dental web-based information is far from being a practical reality.

The DISCERN tool was designed with 2 purposes: to assist users in evaluating medical content and to serve as a guide for developing new, higher-quality content. Its use confirmed that, in our sample, there were no significant differences in content quality between languages (Spanish vs. English). However, relevant differences were found when comparing specific terms ("apicoectomy" vs. "apicectomía"; "apical surgery" vs. "apicoectomy"). The significant discrepancy between "apical surgery" and "apicoectomy", is particularly noteworthy, as these terms are often used interchangeably.

The absence of a regulatory framework for health information on the Internet hinders the development of patient-oriented content that meets basic standards for quality and readability. Therefore, using validated tools such as DISCERN and established readability indices is essential when creating new patient educational resources.

Although the Internet is a powerful medium for patient communication, the available information on apical surgery is generally difficult for laypersons and lacks quality. Endodontists should be encouraged to develop accurate and accessible patient education materials using plain language.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the dynamic and ever-changing nature of online content means that the information retrieved during the search period may no longer be representative later. Second, the search was conducted exclusively using Google®, which, while the most widely used search engine, may not reflect results obtained from other platforms or regions with different indexing algorithms. Third, the study was limited to English and Spanish, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings to speakers of other languages. Additionally, although a structured search strategy was applied, there is an inherent risk of selection bias, as only the first 100 results per term were considered. This may exclude relevant content that users could still access beyond the initial pages. Furthermore, although the data collection and assessment were conducted by calibrated reviewers, the subjective nature of some evaluation tools may introduce variability despite standardisation efforts.

Lastly, it is important to note that a significant limitation of our study is that it did not include an analysis of information-seeking tools based on artificial intelligence (AI). The popularity of these technologies has grown in recent years, mainly due to their ability to provide immediate and personalised access to information about symptoms, diseases or treatments. We believe it is important to emphasise that future research on health literacy should include an analysis not only in terms of the quality of the information obtained through such tools, but also regarding users’ understanding and perception of that information.

Conclusions

The information available online in both English and Spanish regarding apical surgery and apicoectomy treatments is difficult for the average patient to understand. Furthermore, the quality of this information is clearly inadequate, as assessed by the DISCERN tool.

Improving the quality and readability of online health content is a shared responsibility between content creators and regulatory bodies. Efforts should focus on providing clear, accurate, and accessible information to support informed decision-making.
