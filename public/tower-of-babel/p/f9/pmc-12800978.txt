Patient-Specific 3-Dimensional Surgical Guide for Anteriorizing Tibial Tubercle Osteotomy to Treat Patellofemoral Osteoarthritis
Authors: Ashton Kai Shun Tan, Shaun Kai Kiat Chua, Don Thong Siang Koh, Junwei Soong, Hamid Rahmatullah Bin Abd Razak, Shing Yi Tan, Kong Hwee Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
There is an emerging role of using patient-specific instruments in orthopaedic surgery, such as high tibial osteotomy, to improve surgical precision and reduce operative times. However, there is a paucity of evidence in the use of patient-specific instruments in anteriorizing tibial tubercle osteotomy (aTTO) to treat patellofemoral osteoarthritis. The purpose of this article is to describe the technique of using a patient-specific 3-dimensional printed surgical guide to perform an aTTO. The guide was designed in 3 dimensions preoperatively using patient imaging to facilitate patient-specific, accurate bone cuts and effective surgical execution for the aTTO.

The tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) was first described as a surgical procedure that involved shifting the tibial tubercle medially and distally to treat patellar instability.1 Over the years, indications for TTO have expanded to include a variety of patellofemoral disorders, such as (isolated) patellofemoral osteoarthritis (PFOA), focal patellar and trochlear chondral defects, and refractory pain related to malalignment—with or without instability. One of the growing indications for TTO includes isolated PFOA as an alternative to patellofemoral arthroplasty. The primary goal of TTO is to optimize knee biomechanics by offloading the chondral undersurface of the patella and improving patellar tracking and stability to alleviate pain and improve function. While the use of early TTO techniques such as the Maquet procedure has been largely abandoned due to their associated complications of infection and wound dehiscence, newer variations of TTO techniques, including the anteriorizing TTO (aTTO) described by Patel et al.,2 have produced improved outcomes and lower complication rates, making aTTO a viable option for managing patellofemoral disorders.

Patient-specific instruments (PSIs), particularly 3-dimensional (3D)–printed surgical guides, have shown benefits in orthopaedic surgeries, including better adherence to preoperative planning, reduced operative time, and enhanced surgical precision.3 Despite promising advantages of using PSIs like patient-specific 3D-printed surgical guides in osteotomies, such as high tibial osteotomies (HTOs), there remains limited evidence on the use of PSIs in TTO.3 The purpose of this Technical Note is to describe a step-by-step technique of anteriorizing tibial tubercle osteotomy, making use of a patient-specific 3D-printed surgical guide.

Preoperative Planning and PSIs

A thorough history and examination are performed. These should reveal isolated patellofemoral symptoms that have failed a trial of conservative management. The medial and lateral knee compartments should not be affected. A team comprising the primary surgeon, engineer, and project manager is required. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the knee is performed for preoperative planning. The osteotomy cuts and angles are planned using a virtual 3D model of the patient’s tibia (Fig 2). The coronal cut is planned to be made at one-third the distance from the anterior to posterior cortex of the tibia, to preserve the integrity of the posterior cortex (Fig 2A). This plane is determined by drawing a line across the midpoints of the medial and lateral tibial plateau (Fig 2D). An 85-mm coronal cut is planned from distal to proximal, before angulating anteriorly to make an oblique cut of 20 mm (Fig 2A). Based on the above planned cuts, K-wire location and dimensions (2 mm in diameter), and thickness of the saw blade (1.2 mm), the jig is designed virtually (Fig 3). The jig is customized to match and wrap around the anterior portion of the patient’s tibia, just inferior to the insertion of the patella tendon on the tibial tuberosity and at the medial tuberosity. After discussion and review within the team, a physical prototype of the jig and a 3D model of the patient’s tibia, fibula, and a section of patellar tendon are printed (Fig 4). Surgical Technique

The patient is put under general anaesthesia and positioned supine. After inflation of the tourniquet, a longitudinal incision 20 mm medial to the tibial crest and 20 mm inferior to the joint line is performed (Video 1). Soft tissue is carefully dissected in layers down to bone, and releases are performed to enable the PSI jig (3D Printing Centre, Singapore General Hospital) to sit securely onto the tibia bone. Once adequate exposure is achieved, the PSI jig is then inserted. Checks are made to ensure that the jig is in satisfactory contact with the medial tuberosity and wrapped around the tibial tuberosity just inferior to the insertion of the patellar tendon, as planned with the 3D-printed model. After ensuring a secure fit of the jig on the bone, three 2-mm K-wires are then inserted to secure the position (Fig 5A). The osteotomy cuts are made with the saw, using the jig as a guide (Fig 5B). The coronal cut is first made, followed by the oblique cut. Constant irrigation with saline is given during the saw cuts to prevent overheating and osteonecrosis (Fig 5C). Once the cuts are complete, anteriorization of the tibial tuberosity of 10 mm is achieved under gradual distraction (Fig 5D). Intraoperative fluoroscopy is used to ensure the integrity of the distal hinge (Fig 6 A and B). A femoral head allograft (Platinum Medical) is then prepared and inserted into the gap. This is done to promote bone healing. The TTO is secured with two 4.5-mm cannulated screws (60 mm proximally, 52 mm distally) (Synthes) placed in an anterior-to-posterior direction (Fig 6E). During screw placement, the posterior neurovascular structures are protected using a radiolucent Hohmann retractor. Sufficient screw purchase of at least 2 to 3 threads into the posterior cortex is ensured. The tourniquet is then released, and hemostasis is performed. The wound is subsequently irrigated and closed in layers up to the skin. Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients are not allowed to have range of movement (ROM) of the knee for the first week to allow for wound healing. This prevents infection and dehiscence. Subsequently, 0° to 45° of flexion can be allowed at the second to fourth weeks, followed by 0° to 90° in the fifth to sixth weeks, and finally, full ROM is accepted from the seventh week onward. Stitches are removed after 2 weeks if the wound is noted to be well healed (Fig 7). As for the weightbearing status, we encourage patients to use crutches for support for the first 2 months, with weightbearing of 10 kg with the foot in plantigrade for the first 2 weeks, followed by partial weightbearing of 50% body weight in the third to fourth weeks and full weightbearing as tolerated after the first month. Routine x-rays during follow-up appointments are recommended to monitor the integrity of the distal hinge (Fig 8 A and B). Discussion

In this Technical Note, we show and detail a successful application of a patient-specific 3D-printed surgical guide in aTTO, along with our recommendations on the technique (Table 1). The TTO was first introduced as a procedure to address instability and maltracking of the patella through correction of the knee mechanical axis. Over the years, the technique has evolved to include anteriorization (e.g., aTTO) to manage patellofemoral joint disorders like PFOA by offloading the patella cartilage.1,2 While the aTTO has been refined further in recent years to mitigate the common reported complications, such as infection and wound dehiscence in its early days, the aTTO remains a complex procedure that has also been historically associated with challenges in intraoperative precision and reproducibility of outcomes. The addition of a patient-specific guide in aTTO allows for improved accuracy of the osteotomy cuts, reduced surgical time, decreased use of intraoperative fluoroscopy, reduced manipulation and dissection for the surgery, and potentially reduced complications allowed by increased surgical precision (Table 2). The use of PSIs for the aTTO allows the surgical team to achieve a total anteriorization of the patella by 10 mm with high precision, as well as quick intraoperative preparation of the femoral head allograft for the osteotomy gap based on the planned size. We also noted positive clinical outcomes by 6 weeks postoperatively, including the resolution of preoperative symptoms, 0° to 90° knee ROM, and full weightbearing capacity. In line with recent studies that highlight the utility of PSI in planning and reducing the learning curve for complex knee surgeries and knee preservation surgeries (e.g., high tibial osteotomy), we
