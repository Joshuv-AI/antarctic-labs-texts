A phase 1 study of berzosertib (M6620, VX‐970) in combination with cisplatin and radiation in patients with locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (ETCTN 9950)

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAtaxia telangiectasia Rad3‐related (ATR) protein kinase regulates DNA damage response and is essential for tumor cell survival. Preclinically, ATR inhibition can sensitize tumor cells to radiation and chemotherapy. The authors conducted a phase 1 trial of berzosertib, a selective ATR inhibitor, in combination with definitive radiation and cisplatin in locally advanced head and neck squamous cell cancers (LA‐HNSCC).MethodsLA‐HNSCC patients received daily radiation (2 Gy per fraction to 70 Gy) and weekly intravenous (iv) cisplatin 40 mg/m2. Berzosertib was administered (iv) once weekly, starting with a pharmacokinetic lead‐in dose. Three berzosertib dose levels (DL) were tested: 120 mg/m2 (DL1), 160 mg/m2 (DL2), and 200 mg/m2 (DL3).ResultsForty‐one of 43 enrolled patients were evaluable for safety and preliminary efficacy assessments. Four patients experienced dose‐limiting toxicities (DLTs) in dose‐escalation: grade 4 thrombocytopenia (1), grade 4 respiratory failure (1), grade 3 renal injury and hypoxia (1), and inability to receive 90% of the planned radiation dose (1). DL3 was the recommended phase 2 dose, and 15 patients were enrolled at this DL as an expansion cohort. Objective response rates (ORR) in patient cohorts treated with any amount of berzosertib; treated with at least 50% of planned treatment; and in the expansion cohort were 78.8% (26 of 33), 78.8% (26 of 33), and 63.6% (7 of 11), respectively, after excluding eight inevaluable patients without post‐treatment imaging assessment.ConclusionsBerzosertib (200 mg/m2 iv weekly) was safe when combined with chemoradiation but did not improve complete response rate in LA‐HNSCC.Keywords: ATR, berzosertib, head and neck cancer, pharmacokinetics, radiation, safety

INTRODUCTION

Squamous cell cancers of the head and neck (HNSCC) are a heterogenous group of malignancies, most commonly involving the mucosal oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx, caused by exposure to tobacco and other carcinogens or by the human papillomavirus (HPV). 1 HNSCC is one of the most frequently diagnosed cancers worldwide, accounting for approximately 800,000 new cases and 400,000 deaths in 2022. 2 Approximately 60%–70% of HPV+ and HPV– HNSCC patients present with locally advanced (LA) disease, characterized by local invasion of the primary tumor, lymph node metastases, or both. 1 Given the desire for organ and function preservation and to optimize the cure rate, concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) is frequently employed, particularly for tumors originating in the oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx. 3 , 4 Despite advances in radiation techniques and supportive care, this remains a highly morbid treatment, and 20% and 50% of HPV+ and HPV– patients, respectively, still develop locoregional recurrence or distant metastases. 5 , 6

Cisplatin or platinum‐containing chemotherapy regimens have repeatedly demonstrated superiority over other agents for their radio‐sensitizing effect, 7 and for cisplatin‐eligible patients, this remains the standard‐of‐care. Other agents, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) targeting monoclonal antibodies and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, failed to improve locoregional or distant control in multiple randomized trials, when used instead of, or in addition to, cisplatin and concurrent radiotherapy, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 including in the favorable prognosis HPV+ patient population. 14 , 15 , 16 Similarly, anti–programmed death‐ligand 1 (anti–PD‐L1) agents, which improve overall survival (OS) when used in patients with recurrent or metastatic (R/M) HNSCC, 17 failed to improve progression‐free survival (PFS) when added to definitive CRT in biomarker‐unselected patients with LA HNSCC. 18 , 19 More recently, xevinapant, an oral antagonist of inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) demonstrated initial promise when it improved the locoregional control (LRC) rate and 5‐year OS when administered with cisplatin and radiation in a phase 2 trial in patients with LA HNSCC. 20 However, the confirmatory phase 3 trial failed to demonstrate a survival benefit with the addition of this agent. 21 Other strategies, including the incorporation of intensive, multi‐agent chemotherapy induction regimens before CRT, although effective at achieving tumor shrinkage, have also failed to improve survival outcomes when compared to CRT alone in multiple randomized clinical trials in high‐risk LA HNSCC. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 There remains a critical unmet need for mechanistically rational systemic therapy to improve the efficacy of CRT in patients with LA HNSCC.

The DNA damage response (DDR) is a complex network of signaling pathways involved in surveillance and repair of DNA damage to ensure genomic stability. 27 It is regulated by the ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) and ataxia telangiectasia Rad3‐related (ATR) protein kinases. The former is recruited to sites of double‐strand DNA breaks (DSBs) whereas the latter is activated by single strand DNA breaks (SSBs) and replication stress, both of which occur with chemotherapy and radiation. 28 ATM and ATR activation triggers DNA damage signaling to cell‐cycle checkpoints, leading to cell‐cycle arrest and homologous recombination repair. 29 ATM controls the p53‐dependent G1/S checkpoint, which is frequently impaired in many tumor types, including HNSCC, due to mutation or inactivation of TP53, prevalent in up to 85% of HPV(–) HNSCC and rendering it relatively radio‐ and chemoresistant. 30 Thus, tumor cells are more reliant on the ATR‐controlled S and G2/M checkpoints for DNA damage repair and cell survival, and consequently, ATR inhibition in these cells could exacerbate replicative stress, leading to accumulation of DSBs and synthetic lethal mitotic catastrophe. 31 By contrast, normal adjacent cells with an intact ATM are expected to tolerate ATR inhibition.

Berzosertib (formerly M6620, VX970, or VE‐822) is a highly potent and selective ATP‐competitive inhibitor of ATR, with >100‐fold weaker inhibition of related kinases ATM and DNA‐PK. 32 There is accumulating preclinical evidence that ATR inhibition can sensitize tumor cells to the effects of radiation therapy (RT) or chemotherapy. 33 , 34 , 35 Indeed, berzosertib displayed consistent synergy with cisplatin in seven human PDX lung cancer models 32 and two esophageal cancer xenograft models 36 ; with radiation against two pancreatic tumor xenograft models, 37 and with standard therapies in an esophageal xenograft model. 38 Another ATR inhibitor, ceralasertib (AZD6738) showed in vivo synergy with radiation in two head and neck carcinoma xenograft models 39 and with cisplatin in four head and neck carcinoma xenograft models. 40 Last, elimusertib (BAY 1895344) showed in vivo synergy with radiation in two head and neck carcinoma xenograft models. 41

In in vivo dosing‐schedule optimization studies, berzosertib effectively enhanced antitumor activity of gemcitabine or cisplatin when administered 12–24 hours after a cytotoxic agent but not when administered before or greater than 48 hours after a DNA‐damaging agent. Early‐phase clinical trials showed that berzosertib was well tolerated as monotherapy and in combination with various chemotherapeutic agents, including cisplatin, gemcitabine and topotecan. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 A phase 1 trial demonstrated safety in combination with RT in 16 patients with esophageal cancer. 47 Based on the mechanistic rationale of synthetic lethality with standard‐of‐care CRT and supportive preclinical and clinical evidence, we conducted a phase 1 dose‐escalation trial to determine the safety, tolerability, recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D), pharmacokinetics, and antitumor activity of berzosertib in combination with weekly cisplatin and RT in patients with LA HNSCC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

This study was sponsored by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and conducted at Experimental Therapeutics Clinical Trials Network (ETCTN) participating sites as protocol ETCTN 9950. The study protocol was approved by all local institutional review boards, performed in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT02567422). All patients provided written informed consent. Salient inclusion criteria included adult patients with AJCC VII clinical stage III or IV unresectable HNSCC of the larynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, oral cavity, paranasal sinuses or carcinoma of the neck of unknown primary site (regardless of HPV status), but not nasopharyngeal, skin and salivary gland carcinomas; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) ≤2; presence of measurable disease by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 48 ; and adequate marrow and organ function.

Study design

This phase 1, open‐label trial aimed to investigate the safety and tolerability of combining berzosertib with weekly cisplatin and RT in previously untreated, LA‐HNSCC. The primary end points included safety, tolerability, and the identification of the RP2D. Secondary end points included evaluation of berzosertib pharmacokinetics (PK), specifically a drug–drug interaction (DDI) between berzosertib and anti‐nausea agent, aprepitant, overall response rate (ORR) based on RECIST 1.1, and exploratory tissue‐based biomarkers of DNA damage as correlates of clinical outcomes. Patients were enrolled in cohorts of three subjects using a Bayesian adaptive escalation with overdose control (EWOC) design. 49 In stage I, the optimal dose of cisplatin (40 or 30 mg/m2 weekly) to combine with standard RT (70 Gy) and the minimum biologically relevant dose of berzosertib (120 mg/m2) would be tested. In stage II, escalating doses of berzosertib ranging from 160 to 280 mg/m2 would be tested alongside the dose of cisplatin (30 or 40 mg/m2) and RT (70 Gy) from stage I (see Table 1). Intrapatient dose escalation was not permitted. An expansion cohort of 15 patients was planned at the RP2D to obtain preliminary evidence of efficacy.

Treatment plan

Radiation

Treatment administration is outlined in Figure 1. RT was given at 2 Gy/day for a total of 70 Gy over 6–7 weeks. Treatment continued until completion of 70 Gy or disease progression, intercurrent illness prohibiting further treatment administration, intolerable toxicity or patient withdrawal. Missed doses could be made up by twice‐daily fractions (at least 6 hours apart) or weekend treatments. Radiation delivery was planned as intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric arc therapy along with daily image guidance. All gross areas of disease were required to receive up to a maximum of 70 Gy in 2 Gy per day fractions. Dose to high‐risk nodal stations could range from 50 to 60 Gy and to intermediate and low‐risk nodal regions could range from 44 to 60 Gy at the discretion of the treating radiation oncologist.

Treatment schema.

Cisplatin was administered intravenously (iv), once weekly concurrently with radiation starting at 40 mg/m2 with plan for a 1 level dose reduction to 30 mg/m2 in case of intolerability for a maximum of seven doses. Pre‐ and post‐cisplatin hydration or mannitol as well as a standard anti‐emetic regimen, incorporating a 5‐HT3 antagonist, NK antagonist and dexamethasone per local practice were recommended before cisplatin administration. Based on findings from the dosing‐schedule optimization studies, berzosertib was dosed iv weekly at least 24 (±4) hours from the end of cisplatin infusion, with a lead in on D‐7 before the start of chemoradiation to allow for single agent PK assessment, and then once every week of CRT for a total of up to eight doses.

Safety assessments

Toxicity was assessed and graded according to the NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. DLTs were assessed over the entire duration of CRT and for up to 3 weeks beyond the last fraction of radiation. Patients were considered evaluable for DLT determination if they: 1) experienced DLT while on treatment during week 1 after receiving any therapy; 2) experienced DLT during the DLT period after receiving at least one dose of cisplatin and berzosertib concurrent with 1 week (five fractions) of RT; and 3) did not experience DLT and received at least 90% of RT doses. DLT was defined as the following adverse events, if considered at least “possibly related”: 1) grade 4 hematologic toxicity lasting more than 7 days; 2) grade 4 neutropenia of any duration with fever ≥38.5°C; 3) grade ≥4 nausea and or vomiting despite standard supportive therapy; 4) grade ≥3 nonhematologic toxicity (excluding alopecia, anorexia, fatigue, infection without neutropenia, grade 3 elevation of transaminases, grade 3 hypomagnesemia, or grade 4 stomatitis, mucositis and/or dysphagia that resolves to grade 2 or less with a treatment break not to exceed 10 days from the scheduled day of chemotherapy; 5) inability to re‐treat patient within 2 weeks of scheduled treatment due to treatment‐related toxicity; 6) grade 4 radiation‐induced rash; and 7) inability to deliver at least 90% of the total radiation dose due to treatment discontinuation as a result of intolerable treatment‐related toxicity or a radiation treatment break of five or more consecutive fractions.

Tumor response assessment

18F‐FDG‐PET scan for tumor response assessment was obtained at 12 weeks post completion of CRT per standard of care. Subsequently, patients underwent CT or MRI imaging every 12 weeks for a total of 2 years post treatment completion. Response and progression were evaluated using the RECIST guideline (version 1.1). Response was analyzed separately for: 1) all patients who received any amount of the investigational agent; 2) those who received at least 50% of the planned treatment (radiation and systemic therapy); and 3) all patients in the expansion cohort treated at the RP2D.

Pharmacokinetic studies

Patients participated in optional berzosertib pharmacokinetic (PK) studies, for which EDTA plasma was collected before and at 0.5, 0.92 after start, and 0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 23, 48, and 72 h after the end of the 1‐hour iv infusion. Berzosertib is a CYP3A4 substrate whereas premedication for cisplatin includes fosaprepitant, a possible CYP inhibitor. Therefore, a potential DDI between berzosertib and cisplatin premedication regimen was assessed. PK was obtained on day –7 (berzosertib alone) and on cycle 1 day 2 (1 day after fosaprepitant with cisplatin and in the context of daily radiation). Each blood sample was centrifuged at approximately 1000g, and plasma was stored at –70°C or colder until analysis with a liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry assay validated to Food and Drug Administration guidance. 50 Plasma pharmacokinetic parameters were derived from the data by noncompartmental methods with WinNonlin (Certara). Statistical analyses for PK parameter values were performed using SPSS 27.0 for Windows (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, Illinois).

Diagnostic archival tumor sample submitted from each patient enrolled on the study was employed for post hoc correlative genomic profiling analysis to identify potential biomarkers with reasonable possibility of serving as an enrichment strategy for future efficacy studies of ATR inhibitors in this patient population. These include 1) loss of chromosome 11q based on whole exome sequencing; and 2) mRNA expression profiling of TP53, ERCC1, and XRCC1 based on whole transcriptome mRNA sequencing.

Whole exome sequencing

Diagnostic archival tumor samples were obtained from 33 patients. Two to 12 10‐μm slides were processed and resulting genomic DNA (gDNA) analyzed as described in the Supporting Information.

Whole transcriptome sequencing

Two to five 10‐μm slides were deparaffinized and the area of interest was macro‐dissected before RNA extraction, as described in the Supporting Information.

Statistical analysis

A projected sample size of up to 30 participants in the dose escalation phase was determined to ensure sufficient data for reasonably precise estimation of the maximum tolerated dose (MTD). The trial used an EWOC design, which had a greater than 50% probability of identifying the correct MTD and ensuring that more than 30% of patients were allocated to the true MTD. The DLT rate target for this trial was set at 33%. On completion of the dose escalation, the MTD was estimated as the median of the marginal posterior distribution of the MTD. The RP2D was defined as the dose at or below the MTD where therapy was determined to be tolerable. An additional 15 patients were enrolled at the defined RP2D.

The rate of complete response (CR) following standard of care chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for HNSCC is approximately 60%. 51 Our expansion cohort was limited to 15 patients allowing evaluation of efficacy only in an exploratory fashion. A sample size of 15 patients provided 82% power at a one‐sided significance level of 0.1 to detect an improvement in response if our investigational treatment has a true 85% CR rate. Duration of response (DOR), PFS, and OS rates were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between different groups using the log‐rank test, respectively. All data management and statistical analysis were conducted using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

RESULTS

Patients

Patient enrollment occurred from July 2017 to December 2021. Median age of all enrolled patients was 62 years (range, 45–76), 61% were male, 81% were Caucasian with commonest primary tumor sites of larynx (27%) and oropharynx (24%), 71% had stage IV disease, and 73% had p16 negative or unknown disease. Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table S1. Patients were well represented across sex and race categories, with most patients having an ECOG score of 0 or 1 (97.6%). Two of 43 enrolled patients inevaluable for DLT assessment or response were subsequently replaced. One patient, assigned to dose level 1 (DL1), experienced an infusion reaction to cisplatin during the first week, leading to study discontinuation, whereas the second patient, in dose level 3 cohort, developed complications following gastrostomy tube placement and did not initiate trial treatment. The remaining 41 patients were enrolled across berzosertib doses of 120 mg/m2, 160 mg/m2, and 200 mg/m2 (Table 1).

Safety

The starting berzosertib dose was 120 mg/m2 combined with 40 mg/m2 cisplatin and RT. Eleven patients were enrolled at this DL, with 10 evaluable for DLT assessment, of which two patients experienced DLTs. One patient developed grade 4 thrombocytopenia and could not receive any systemic therapy following week 4 of CRT. Another patient was only able to receive 60 Gy of a planned 70 Gy of RT due to grade 3 mucositis and dysphagia. At DL2, one of six treated patients experienced grade 4 respiratory failure requiring transient inpatient hospitalization. At DL3, 11 patients were enrolled, with 10 evaluable for DLT assessment. One patient was hospitalized for DLTs of grade 3 renal injury and hypoxia and grade 4 transaminitis leading to a hold on cisplatin for more than 2 weeks and eventual discontinuation of protocol therapy. Another patient died in week 5 of treatment after experiencing a cardiac arrest, determined by the treating investigator to be unrelated to study treatment. Based on the EWOC design, the MTD was estimated as dose DL3 (200 mg/m2), corresponding to the median of the marginal posterior distribution of the MTD. Given the totality of the data, the trial safety monitoring committee subsequently established DL3 (200 mg/m2) also as the RP2D, and 15 patients were enrolled at this dose as expansion cohort. A second on‐study death occurred in the expansion cohort in a male patient who experienced respiratory failure in week 5 of treatment, assessed by the treating investigator to be possibly related to RT.

The majority of patients (95%) experienced grade ≥3 toxicities not unexpected for the intensity of chemoradiation. Table 2 summarizes the treatment‐emergent adverse events that occurred in greater than 10% of treated patients. The most common side effects (occurring in >50% of patients) were: fatigue (80%), anemia (80%), mucositis (80%), radiation site dermatitis (78%), nausea (76%), dysphagia (71%), weight loss (71%), thrombocytopenia (68%), leukopenia (68%), dysgeusia (61%), and hypomagnesemia (61%). The complete list of adverse events by type and grade is available in the Supporting Information (Table S2).

Treatment compliance was high. Median number of cisplatin doses administered was five (range, 2–7) with 34 of 41 (83%) patients receiving five or more doses. Median cumulative cisplatin dose administered was 200 mg/m2, and 63.4% of patients were able to receive the critical threshold of 200 mg/m2 or higher dose concurrent with RT. The median RT dose administered was 70 Gy (range, 36–70 Gy), with 33 of 41 (80.5%) patients receiving 66 Gy or higher. Median number of berzosertib doses administered was seven (range, 3–8), and 34 patients (83%) received six or more doses.

Preliminary efficacy assessment

Patient disposition

Two of the 43 patients enrolled on study did not start trial treatment and were not evaluable for response. One withdrew from trial due to toxicities in week 6 of treatment. Two patients died during study treatment. Two additional patients discontinued treatment prematurely, after receiving 36 Gy and 60 Gy, respectively, of RT and died 3.5 and 4 months later, respectively, without having completed post‐treatment scans. Two patients were lost to follow‐up after completion of trial treatment. One of them was diagnosed with severe pharyngeal dysphagia and recommended alternative means of enteral feeding but continued oral alimentation despite this, ultimately experiencing grade 5 aspiration pneumonia 4 months post CRT completion without having completed a post‐treatment scan. For four other patients, post‐treatment scans or RECIST assessments were missing. An accounting of patients and response‐evaluable patients is provided in Table 3.

The ORR was 63.4% (26 of 41), 70.3% (26 of 37), and 46.7% (7 of 15) respectively for 1) all patients who received any amount of the investigational agent; 2) those who received at least 50% of the planned treatment (radiation and systemic therapy); and 3) all patients in the expansion cohort treated at the RP2D. The ORR for the three groups after excluding eight inevaluable patients without post‐treatment imaging assessment was 78.8% (26 of 33), 78.8% (26 of 33), and 63.6% (7 of 11), respectively. Responses were also stratified by p16 status—ORR was 81.8% (9 of 11) for patients with p16 positive disease and 56.7% (17 of 30) for patients with p16 negative or unknown status tumors.

Specifically in the expansion cohort, the CR rate was 45.5% (95% CI, 16.7%–76.6%,) among patients in whom post‐treatment assessment was completed. This low CR rate did not allow us to declare the combination treatment to be more active than the historical control CR rate of 60%. At a median follow‐up time of 25 months for the 33 patients assessable for response with post‐treatment imaging, ORR was 78.8% (18 complete responses and eight partial responses), three patients had stable disease (SD) and four experienced disease progression (PD) for a disease control rate of 87.9%.

Kaplan–Meier curves for DOR, PFS, and OS are presented in Figure 2. All treated patients were included in PFS and OS analyses, whereas more than half of the patients in the intention‐to‐treat population did not achieve a response and were censored in the DOR analysis. The probability of DOR beyond 1.5 years exceeds 74% (95% CI, 55%–92%). Median PFS for the entire cohort was 24.7 months (range: 1.4–32 months). Two‐year PFS rate was 63.6% (95% CI, 45%–75%) for the 33 patients with available post‐treatment evaluation. Median PFS was numerically better in p16(+) patients compared to p16(–)/unknown patients without meeting statistical significance (25.6 months vs. 15.1 months; p = .24) (Figure S8). The median OS was approximately 2.4 years (range, 0.12–2.24), with a 2‐year OS rate of approximately 80% (95% CI, 67%–92%).

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Pharmacokinetic profiles of berzosertib were assessed 10 patients who provided optional consent for participation. Five patients were treated at 120 mg/m2 and five at 200 mg/m2 (Table 4; Figure S1). Berzosertib displayed a biphasic plasma concentration versus time profile, with a half‐life of 17.4 hours, a volume of distribution of 564 L/m2, and a total clearance of 30.1 L/h/m2. We did not detect any significant difference in berzosertib PK profile (Cmax or area under the curve [AUC]) in samples collected before cisplatin or fosaprepitant and in samples collected 24 hours after cisplatin and fosaprepitant (p = .56 and p = .08, respectively). However, the 90% confidence interval of the average bioequivalence did not completely fall within the range of 0.8–1.25 for both Cmax (67%–114%) and AUC (75%–104%).

Correlative analysis

Prior works suggested potential association between genomic expression and genetic polymorphisms in DNA repair genes and inferior clinical responses, including PFS and OS. 52 , 53 We employed gDNA extracted from the diagnostic archival tumor samples obtained from 33 LA‐HNSCC patients enrolled on study to generate whole exome and transcriptomic sequencing data as described in the Supporting Information. We sequentially investigated in an exploratory manner whether there was any association between 11q loss and treatment responses. This locus harbors the ATM gene, the loss of which is hypothesized to confer vulnerability to ATR inhibition. We did not detect 11q loss in any of the samples. We subsequently assessed the association of specific DNA damage repair gene expression profiles and treatment responses using whole transcriptome data from 32 patients. There was no statistically significant association between the expression profiles of ERCC1, XRCC1, or TP53, and PFS or OS by Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figures S2–S7). There was a nonsignificantly improved PFS in patients with p16(+) tumors (2‐year PFS = 0.72; 95% CI, 0.39–0.90) compared with those with p16‐negative/unknown status (2‐year PFS = 0.42; 95% CI, 0.21–0.62; p = .24) (Figure S8).

DISCUSSION

We successfully tested the combination of ATR inhibitor and definitive chemoradiation. To date, this is the largest cohort of patients treated with this combination in a curative intent LA‐HNSCC population. The rationale for this study design came from our knowledge that: (1) 40%–60% of patients with LA HNSCC will develop tumor recurrence despite receiving aggressive multimodality CRT in the IMRT era 19 , 20 , 54 ; and (2) from extensive preclinical and early clinical studies demonstrating that ATR inhibition can lethally sensitize tumor cells to concomitant DNA damaging therapy such as platinum agents and RT, especially cells with alterations in other DNA damage repair proteins. 32 , 33 , 55 , 56

The safety of ATR inhibitors in combination with several chemotherapeutics, including platinating agents, has been well established in previous phase 1 trials. 42 , 43 , 45 , 46 Additionally, since the conduct of our trial, safety in combination with RT in a small cohort of patients with esophageal cancer has also been reported. 47 Increased hematologic toxicity (anemia, thrombocytopenia, or neutropenia) has consistently emerged as the most common high‐grade adverse events in patients treated with ATR inhibition. We similarly observed the frequency of grade ≥3 hematologic toxicities with the addition of berzosertib to CRT to be higher than what was reported in recent trials of CRT alone in HNSCC. 19 , 20 , 54 The other frequently observed toxicities on this trial were not unexpected with an intensive treatment course of CRT. Nonetheless, it will be prudent to monitor for increased toxicity, particularly hematologic, should this or a similar regimen move into larger studies with less restrictive eligibility especially in HNSCC patients who are more likely to present with multiple comorbid conditions which predispose to increased risk of adverse outcomes. In our study, two of 43 participants enrolled died within the DLT monitoring period but these events were deemed unrelated to protocol treatment. A similar rate of grade 5 events (2 of 48 patients) was reported in the control arm of the randomized phase 2 trial of CRT with placebo or xevinapant, both were also considered unrelated to treatment as observed in our current trial.

Efficacy analysis of this treatment combination is limited by the small sample size, early‐phase noncomparative design, different dose levels of berzosertib and incomplete assessment of efficacy post‐treatment in some patients. Within these constraints, a 2‐year PFS rate of 63.6% does not seem significantly better than recent historical controls. Notably, 2‐year PFS rate of 57% and 2‐year event‐free survival (EFS) rate of 56.2% were reported on the control arms of the Javelin 100 and Keynote 412 trials, respectively 18 , 57

Given that berzosertib is a CYP3A4 substrate and fosaprepitant a CYP3A4 inhibitor that is part of the standard premedication regimen for cisplatin, we assessed the potential DDI in this phase 1 study. With the modest data from 10 patients, we did not detect a statistically significant difference in berzosertib PK when dosed alone or within 24 hours of cisplatin and fosaprepitant administration. Observed values for clearance, volume of distribution, and half‐life were comparable to those reported in a previous population PK analysis. 44 Similarly, preclinical and translational studies indicated that high expression of markers of ATM/ATR signaling and DNA damage repair could associate with inferior antitumor effect of ATR inhibition. In exploratory correlative analyses, we did not identify statistically significant associations between the expression levels of ERCC1, XRCC1, or TP53 and survival outcomes. Although directional differences were observed in some comparisons, these did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and the exploratory nature of the analyses. Patients with HPV(+) showed a trend toward better PFS and OS, as would be expected. The single arm design and the modest sample size did not allow for assessment of treatment by biomarker interaction in this study.

In conclusion, the combination of weekly cisplatin, RT, and berzosertib was safe and tolerable. Although the rate of hematologic toxicity was slightly higher than recent CRT trials, other adverse events were comparable to historical data and rate of treatment compliance was high. Despite the expected mechanistic advantage and prior clinical experience of synthetic lethality seen with concurrent use of DNA damaging agents, this trial did not meet its preliminary efficacy end point of improved complete response rate compared to historical controls. There thus remains a critical unmet need for safe and effective systemic radio‐sensitizing regimens in this high‐risk disease.
