Successful Long-Term Multimodality Management of Facial Lesions in Tuberous Sclerosis Complex in an Adult Patient
Authors: Ji Yun Seo, Anna Kim, Yoo Sang Baek, Jiehyun Jeon
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAngiofibroma and shagreen patches are common cutaneous manifestations of tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) and have significant physical and psychological repercussions for patients. Several treatments have been proposed to improve lesions; however, clear treatment guidelines have not yet been presented. Thus, we introduce a case of angiofibroma and shagreen patch improved by application of pulsed dye laser, ablative fractional CO2 laser, and topical rapamycin, and present clinical implications for the treatment of angiofibroma and shagreen patch in TSC.Keywords: Angiofibroma, Sirolimus, Tuberous sclerosis

INTRODUCTION

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is an autosomal dominant neurocutaneous disorder caused by mutations in the tumor-suppressor genes TSC1 or TSC2, and exhibits various clinical manifestations such as multiple hamartomas in systemic organs, including the skin. Cutaneous manifestations of TSC include hypomelanotic macules, angiofibromas, fibrous cephalic plaques, ungual fibromas, and shagreen patches, among which facial angiofibromas are one of the most troublesome but common cutaneous manifestations, occurring in 75% to 90% of individuals with TSC. Its progressive course leads to significant morbidity and deformity in patients, adversely affecting quality of life. Various therapeutic options with various outcomes have been described to treat facial angiofibromas, including surgical excision, curettage, dermabrasion, laser ablation, electrocautery, cryosurgery, and topical rapamycin1. However, there are no established guidelines regarding treatment, and there are even less data for other TSC skin tumors such as shagreen patch. Herein, we present our experience in successfully managing facial angiofibromas and shagreen patch in TSC with a combination of pulsed dye laser (PDL), ablative fractional CO2 laser and topical rapamycin.

CASE REPORT

A 21-year-old male patient visited our department due to multiple erythematous to flesh colored papules and nodules on the face especially concentrated around nose and perinasal area, and erythematous elevated plaque on right mandible that persisted for more than 10 years (Fig. 1A, B). Angiofibroma was confirmed by skin biopsy of the facial lesion, and right mandibular lesion was clinically suspected as shagreen patch. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed the presence of cortical tubers and calcified subependymal tubers, and the patient was diagnosed as TSC based on clinical and image findings. Previously, he had intermittently ablated facial angiofibromas with CO2 laser for three times on approximately one to two-month interval which had poor outcome due to continuous recurrence.

Multiple erythematous to flesh colored papules, nodules and plaques on face, especially nose and perinasal area before treatment (A, B). Much improved angiofibromas and shagreen patches after 6 years of treatment without any adverse effects (C, D).

Since redness and volume of the lesions were the main cause of cosmetic problems, combinational treatment was administered; 595-nm PDL (V-beam Perfecta; Candela) and ablative fractional CO2 laser (eCO2 laser; Lutronic Corp.) were performed every four to six weeks, along with topical rapamycin cream (0.1% rapamycin in petrolatum) application2. Initially, the patient was instructed to apply topical rapamycin cream once a day, and PDL and ablative fractional CO2 laser were performed concomitantly on a weekly or biweekly basis. For PDL therapy, parameters were set to spot size 7 mm, pulse width 6 ms, fluence 8~10 J/cm2. Ablative fractional CO2 laser was performed after PDL, and we used parameters of spot size 4 mm, density 75 spots/cm2, pulse energy 50 mJ, peak power 30 w. After 8 months, most of the protuberant lesions had improved, and laser treatment interval was prolonged to monthly basis (we set the parameters to spot size 7 mm, pulse width 3 ms, fluence 7 J/cm2 for PDL, and spot size 4 mm, density 50 spots/cm2, pulse energy 30 mJ, peak power 30 w for eCO2 laser) and topical rapamycin was applied every two or three days on newly formed lesions. During the course of treatment, the patient had no adverse reactions such as scarring, bleeding, erythema or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Treatment with topical rapamycin was also well tolerated without sign of local irritation or systemic side effects such as aberrations in blood cell count or increased blood sirolimus concentration.

The patient has maintained monthly laser and topical rapamycin treatment for 6 years, and most of the angiofibromas and shagreen patches have shown remarkable improvement in tumor redness and size. The patient is now 28 years old and there is no notable recurrence thereafter (Fig. 1C, D).

DISCUSSION

Until topical rapamycin treatment was introduced, ablative treatments were mainly employed in order to minimize the size of angiofibroma lesions3. Rapamycin is a molecule that inhibits mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activity which is dysregulated in TSC, but the precise mechanism by which rapamycin improves TSC-associated angiofibromas remains unclear. It has been suggested that rapamycin induces immunosuppression by blocking the cell cycle of T cells and inhibits angiogenesis by reducing the production of vascular endothelial growth factor.

Application of topical rapamycin cream for the treatment of angiofibroma of TSC alone was first introduced in pediatric patients4. However, it seems that fully developed angiofibromas in adult patients are less responsive to topical rapamycin. While lesional erythema and sizes of small papules decreased in most topical rapamycin treated cases, large papules and plaques are less likely to show response. Park et al.5 found that topical rapamycin was ineffective in the treatment of papules larger than 4 mm.

Therefore, physical treatment to reduce the volume of the lesions has been frequently used for old and advanced lesions. Most of the previously reported cases were treated with ablative lasers such as Er-YAG and CO2 lasers. Although these methods may be effective for visibly reducing the size of the angiofibromas temporarily, they also have many drawbacks. In Papadavid et al.’s study6 of 17 tuberous sclerosis patients treated with CO2 laser, hypertrophic scars occurred in 41.1%, and hyperpigmentation was reported in 11.8% of patients. There is also a risk of high recurrence rate; a long-term followup study showed that angiofibroma recurred in 60.9% of patients after CO2 laser ablation, with a mean 3-year time to recurrence7. In addition, postoperative recovery time is required after ablative laser treatment which negatively affects the patient’s social life, and ablative laser treatment can only be performed for limited sessions.

In addition to laser ablation therapy, several other laser treatments, such as PDL laser and ablative fractional laser, have been attempted8,9. PDL was used to treat facial angiofibromas by targeting the vascular components of the lesion. PDL improved erythema in more than 90% of patients with predominant vascular components for a while but did not induce a decrease in fibrous component6. Ablative fractional laser has been used as an additional treatment because of their insufficient effectiveness in reducing the size of the fibrous component compared with ablative CO2 laser therapy.

Some additional factors might have played a role in making the patient’s facial lesions resistant to topical rapamycin treatment alone. First, there were persistent scar components due to several sessions of previous laser ablation therapies. Secondly, some of the lesions were longstanding, since he was in his twenties. Finally, several shagreen patches worsened in color and size. With a combination therapy of PDL, ablative fractional laser, and topical rapamycin application, there was marked and constant improvement. Many earlier studies have reported combinational therapy such as PDL, electrosurgery, ablative fractional resurfacing and topical rapamycin application, showing successful results1,5,6,8,9. Treatment utilizing multiple lasers has been known to maximize therapeutic effect of each modality while reducing the risk of scarring and dyspigmentation8. Topical rapamycin may have had additive effect in lesion regression and maintenance due to its antiangiogenic activity and by blocking proliferation of abnormal fibroblasts10. Unlike our case, a prospective study of 25 patients with TS has demonstrated failure of a 3-times weekly maintenance treatment of topical rapamycin cream to prevent TSC relapse11. This might be due to lack of efficacy with topical treatment only, and optimal treatment regimen for maintenance phase has yet to be elucidated.

Long-term care and management are important because genetic disorders are life-long diseases. Thus, even after our patient had achieved considerable improvement after short course of intensive treatment for several months, we have performed regular monthly session of laser treatment and topical rapamycin application in order to maintain its effect and prevent recurrence. It is significant in the sense that the patient was able to live a life without a post-treatment recovery period while undergoing all the proper treatment. There was no recurrence or aggravation ever since the combination therapy commenced without any adverse effects. After obtaining the best result that could be realistically achievable, the frequency of laser therapy was reduced. This case may have practical implications regarding the ongoing management of facial skin lesions such as angiofibromas and shagreen patches in adult TSC.
