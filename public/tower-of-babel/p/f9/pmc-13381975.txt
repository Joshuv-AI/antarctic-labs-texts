Clinical Experience With Gefapixant in a Specialized Chronic Cough Clinic: Navigating the P2X3 Pathway
Authors: Astrid Crespo-Lessmann, Daniel Bustamante, Eduardo Vélez Segovia, Esther Palones, Borja Zarate Tamames, Víctor Najas Sales, Noé Garin Escrivá
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Chronic cough, defined as a cough lasting longer than eight weeks, is one of the most frequent reasons for medical consultation in respiratory medicine. Within this population, a significant subset suffers from a refractory chronic cough (RCC) – defined as cough persisting despite optimal treatment of underlying conditions – or unexplained chronic cough (UCC), where no identifiable cause is found despite an exhaustive diagnostic workup. These conditions are no longer viewed merely as symptoms but are recognized as distinct clinical entities driven by neuronal hypersensitivity within the cough reflex arc, known as cough hypersensitivity syndrome. Scientific evidence suggests that this hypersensitivity is mediated by sensitized vagal afferent nerves, particularly C-fibers in the airways. P2X3 receptors located on these sensory fibers are activated by adenosine triphosphate released during airway inflammation or mechanical stress. In patients with RCC and UCC, these receptors become hyper-responsive. The emergence of gefapixant, a selective P2X3 receptor antagonist, marks a paradigm shift in management by blocking these receptors, reducing “noise” in the cough reflex, and raising the initiation threshold.

The clinical development of gefapixant culminated in the pivotal COUGH-1 and COUGH-2 Phase III trials.1 These studies used objective 24-hour cough frequency monitoring via electronic devices, moving beyond subjective reporting. In COUGH-1, a 45 mg twice-daily dose resulted in an 18.5% reduction in cough frequency at 12 weeks, while in COUGH-2, it achieved a 14.6% reduction at 24 weeks. Patients on this dose also reported meaningful quality-of-life improvements via the Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ). However, the primary drawback was the prevalence of taste-related adverse effects due to P2X3 involvement in taste signaling; 41% experienced dysgeusia, 15% ageusia, and 11% hypogeusia, leading to a 22% treatment discontinuation rate. Consequently, while the European Medicines Agency and Japanese regulators approved the drug, the FDA in the United States requested further evidence regarding its modest efficacy relative to its side-effect profile.

Until recently, real-world data outside controlled trials were scarce.2 A multicenter retrospective study of 272 patients in Japan provided the first glimpse into how gefapixant performs in daily practice. The cohort consisted predominantly of middle-aged women with a median disease duration of 39 months. Half of the patients showed a significant response, with 25% labeled as “super-responders” who improved within the first two weeks. Patients with higher baseline severity and specific laryngeal sensations, such as throat irritation or a “tickle,” tended to benefit more. Perhaps most interestingly, some responders maintained their improvement even after stopping the drug. This suggests that gefapixant might help “reset” the nervous system, breaking the self-perpetuating cycle of chronic cough.3

In our specialized cough clinic at a tertiary hospital, we conducted a one-year retrospective study of 35 adult patients (mean age 61.5 years, SD 11.2; 80% female).4 The main objective of this study was to describe the barriers to accessing this medicine. Our cohort exhibited complex comorbidities: 25% of RCC patients had associated CANVAS syndrome (Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome), 42% had confirmed gastroesophageal reflux disease, and 34.2% had significant environmental or occupational exposures. Furthermore, our department is a reference center for chronic cough and associated CANVAS, hence the high number of patients with this pathology in our series.5 The study received ethical approval (IIBSP-TOS-2023-133).

Before gefapixant was considered, patients underwent an exhaustive “pharmacological odyssey.” In the RCC group, prior treatments included proton pump inhibitors in 93%, antitussives in 56%, and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) in 50%. In the UCC group, PPI use reached 89%, followed by ICS at 70% and pregabalin in 60%. This heavy treatment burden underscores the desperation and polypharmacy often encountered before targeted P2X3 therapy is initiated. Regarding utilization, only 14 patients (40%) initiated treatment with gefapixant. Among the 21 patients who did not start, the primary barrier was the inability to afford the medication (23.8%), followed by loss to follow-up, logistical difficulties in sourcing the drug, and patient skepticism. Of the 14 treated patients, three (22%) had to discontinue treatment: one due to lack of efficacy after three months, one due to intolerable dysgeusia after two months, and one due to both factors after four months. Of these, three patients (21%) showed a significant improvement in cough (as measured by the Visual Analogue Scale), all of whom were patients with RCC (refractory chronic cough) secondary to CANVAS. Notably, these patients described their cough as an “urge to cough” preceded by laryngeal paresthesia (irritation, tickle, and secretion in the throat). Although this reduction may seem statistically modest – especially in CANVAS patients with high daily cough frequency – such a decrease, combined with an improvement in LCQ scores, can be life-changing (an increase of ≥1.3 points from baseline in the total LCQ score) and an increase in VAS scores from 62.9 to 86.5.

In conclusion, in our real-world descriptive experience, candidates for gefapixant in specialized settings are typically patients with long-standing disease who have failed multiple therapies, including those with rare neuropathic conditions like CANVAS. While gefapixant is a breakthrough, its integration is currently limited by economic factors and adverse effects. Real-world experience suggests that patient selection is key. The hypothesis of identifying patients with laryngeal paresthesia (triggers in the throat) may help clinicians find the “super-responders” who benefit most. Furthermore, the management of expectations regarding taste disturbances is crucial. Patients should be warned that dysgeusia usually appears within the first 10 days and may be transient for some, though it remains a primary reason for treatment discontinuation for others. As we move forward, the focus must be on identifying clinical biomarkers or sensory phenotypes that predict response, and on developing second-generation P2X3 antagonists (such as camlipixant or sivopixant) that may offer higher selectivity and fewer taste disturbances.
