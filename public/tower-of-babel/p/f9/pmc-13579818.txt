Intraoperative NIR-II fluorescence guidance for precise tumor margin assessment and maximized tissue preservation in breast surgery
Authors: Yiyin Zhang, Ke Li, Shuwei Zhang, Houpu Yang, Hailu Wang, Yiting Chen, Yenan Feng, Dingbao Chen, Zeyu Zhang, Shu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate margin assessment remains a challenge in breast-conserving surgery, where inaccuracy leads to excessive removal and compromised aesthetics. Although second near-infrared (NIR-II) fluorescence imaging offers high contrast, there is still a lack of clinically viable NIR-II equipment.MethodsWe develop an innovative NIR-II fluorescence navigation platform based on indocyanine green (ICG) that enables rapid deployment, operation-friendly workflow, and precision-at-hand capability for intraoperative guidance. Thirty-seven patients were recruited with 1 mg/kg ICG injection 45 minutes before surgery. Image quality was evaluated using perception-based image quality evaluator (PIQE) score, while quantitative analyses of fluorescence, including tumor-to-normal tissue ratio (TNR) and signal-to-background ratio (SBR), together with histopathology and a multi-level reader study, were performed to assess tumor contrast and diagnostic performance.ResultsCompared to conventional NIR-I imaging, our NIR-II system demonstrates performance with enhanced spatial resolution and a higher tumor-to-normal tissue ratio (4.383 ± 1.719 versus 3.154 ± 1.072, P < 0.0001) in 37 patients. The intraoperative NIR-II imaging demonstrates superior tumor detection sensitivity [90.63% (75.78%-96.76%) vs. 84.38% (68.25%-93.14%)], with 100% diagnostic accuracy in delineating malignant tumor margins. Ex vivo NIR-II imaging enables real-time, precise visualization of tumor margins and suggested its potential to reduce the volume of excision. (NIR-II–defined volume < NIR-I–defined volume < current resection volume). Through brief training, surgeons can effectively utilize NIR-II imaging, shortening the learning curve for young surgeons in tumor and margin assessment.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a clinically adaptable NIR-II imaging platform that expands intraoperative visualization without changing surgical workflow or adding operational burden, supporting standardized margin assessment and improved tissue preservation in breast surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02338-0.

Translational impact statement

We developed an ICG-based NIR-II fluorescence platform that enables rapid deployment and intuitive operation for intraoperative guidance. In clinical evaluation, NIR-II outperformed conventional NIR-I imaging, achieving superior spatial resolution and 100% accuracy in malignant margin delineation. The system reduced experience-dependent variability in tumor assessment among surgeons and required minimal training. This work establishes NIR-II guidance as a transformative approach for maximizing tissue preservation while ensuring oncologic safety in breast surgery.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2024 released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer [1], breast cancer continues to impose a major threat to women’s health globally, with an increasing incidence among younger populations [2, 3], which in turn has heightened expectations for postoperative cosmetic outcomes. Within this context, breast-conserving surgery (BCS) has emerged as an ideal strategy that enables complete tumor removal while preserving the natural contour of the breast [4]. Consequently, the demand for BCS has grown substantially in recent years [5, 6]. Conventional BCS primarily relies on preoperative imaging for tumor localization, such as ultrasonography and mammography. However, intraoperative margin assessment depends heavily on the surgeon’s palpation and visual inspection [7]. This experience-based approach is prone to error, particularly among less-experienced surgeons, often resulting in excessive tissue excision, elevating the risks of hematoma, infection, or sub-flap dead space [8, 9]. While a negative surgical margin is wildly regarded as tumor clearance of 1 mm [10], the intraoperative delineation of this precise boundary poses a significant challenge for surgeons in the absence of real-time guidance. But the accuracy of the margin is further compromised by tissue shrinkage during formalin fixation for pathological processing [11]. Therefore, there is a pressing need for an intraoperative visualization technique to assist surgeons in maximizing breast conservation while ensuring oncological safety.

To address the pressing need for precision in BCS, several innovative intraoperative diagnostic techniques have been developed [12–14]. A variety of molecular probes have been developed for breast cancer, each fulfilling a specific diagnostic role owing to its distinct mechanism of action. These fluorescence probes targeting membrane antigens, such as fluorescent nanoparticles targeting the estrogen receptor (ER) [15, 16], human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2) [17, 18], or those designed to interrogate the tumor microenvironment [19], etc. While novel fluorescent probe hold potential, their clinical translation is often hindered by unknown biosafety backgrounds. On the contrary, indocyanine green (ICG), a near-infrared window I (NIR-I, 700–900 nm) fluorophore, owing to its established clinical safety, has been widely used in breast surgery, particularly for sentinel lymph node mapping [20–22]. However, despite its widespread adoption, ICG-based NIR-I imaging remains constrained by limited tissue penetration, relatively low spatial resolution, and an unsatisfactory signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which collectively restrict its diagnostic accuracy and image quality [23, 24].

The second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 900-1,700 nm) [25] offers significantly reduced tissue absorption and photon scattering, along with negligible tissue autofluorescence, resulting in markedly enhanced image contrast, particularly advantageous for resolving deep-tissue structures in vivo [25, 26]. The absence of commercially available clinical devices limited the application of NIR-II imaging primarily to animal studies. Recently, Hu et al. have demonstrated that ICG-based NIR-II fluorescence imaging achieves higher tumor detection sensitivity and superior tumor-to-normal tissue contrast ratio (TNR) compared with conventional NIR-I imaging [23], which can also detect liver tumor lesions that CT and MRI missed, thereby improving disease staging accuracy and therapeutic precision [23]. While achieving precise margin assessment is critical for breast-conserving surgery, there remains a lack of a clinically mature imaging system specifically optimized for real-time intraoperative guidance in breast cancer surgery. Although intraoperative fluorescence imaging has shown success in other tumors [25], breast surgery imposes distinct requirements in terms of imaging geometry, field of view, and background interference, necessitating breast-specific system optimization rather than direct adoption from other indications.

Here, we conducted an exploratory study with a limited number of samples, designed to evaluate the feasibility of NIR-II fluorescence imaging for intraoperative guidance. This study establishes a real-time NIR-II imaging platform for intraoperative navigation in breast cancer surgery, offering a real-time, objective, and visual navigation solution. By narrowing the performance gap between surgeons of different experience levels (from medical students to chief surgeons), this innovative tool enables precise margin assessment and minimizes unnecessary tissue removal, providing a powerful and standardized approach to maximize the preservation with assured safety.

Materials and methods

Setup of the integrated NIR-I/II optical imaging system

The optical imaging system consisted of a laser excitation unit and two independent imaging acquisition subsystems for NIR-I and NIR-II. The laser excitation unit included a laser excitation device (808 nm wavelength, 30–50 mW/cm2 output power), an optical fiber, and a beam expander. The NIR-I imaging subsystem utilized a clinical endoscope camera (DPM-ENDOCAM-03, Zhuhai DI PU MEDICAL Technology Co., Ltd, China) employed in the operating room. The NIR-II subsystem comprised a high-sensitivity camera (Cheetah CL 16475, Xenics, Belgium) integrated with a long-pass optical filter (FEL1000, Thorlabs, USA). The exposure time for a single acquisition was set to 100 ms for both cameras, yielding 8-bit pixel images. The two imaging subsystems operated independently to minimize potential cross-talk.

In vitro validation of NIR-I/II imaging characteristics

A solution with a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL was prepared by mixing ICG powder and sterile water. This solution was loaded into capillary tubes with an average inner diameter of 1.0 mm. Two capillary tubes were placed parallel, 5 mm apart, in a container and submerged in milk-based scattering solution (contains approximately 87% water, 3% fat, 4% protein, 4% lactose, and minerals). NIR-I and NIR-II images were acquired sequentially as the submersion depth was increased in 1 mm increments, covering a range from 1.0 to 10.0 mm.

Patient recruitment, clinical data collection, and imaging acquisition

Thirty-seven patients were recruited from the Peking University People’s Hospital between April 2025 and Oct 2025 with malignant or benign breast tumors who were defined as having diagnostically challenging lesions that were inconclusive on conventional imaging and intraoperative gross examination. Preoperative imaging modalities, including mammography, enhanced MRI, and ultrasonography, were used to diagnose breast tumors. Additional inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and the provision of written informed consent (Table S1). A concentration of 1.0 mg/kg ICG (diluted with sterile water for injection) was intravenously injected into the patients 45 min before surgery (Fig. 1A). All specimens were assessed by NIR-I and NIR-II imaging ex vivo after surgical removal.

The assessment of malignant tumor margins was based on the surgeon’s subjective evaluation under NIR-II guidance after excision, and margins identified in this manner were defined as NIR-II fluorescent margins. Tissue samples were collected for pathological examination from four locations around the NIR-II fluorescent margin (superior, medial, inferior, lateral), each within a 1 mm distance. Tissue samples from imaging-defined regions of interest, such as spiculated tumor margins, were collected for histopathological validation by two experienced pathologists. For each sampled margin site, if no cancer cells were identified by pathological examination, the site was defined as a fluorescent margin with benign pathology. Conversely, if cancer cells were identified at the sampled margin site, the site was defined as a fluorescent margin with malignant pathology. No adverse events were reported after 24 h of ICG injection.

PIQE score

The imaging quality of NIR-I and NIR-II was quantitatively evaluated using the Perception-based Image Quality Evaluator (PIQE) score [27]. This model assesses image distortion based on noise and blockiness, providing a composite score between 0 and 100, where a lower score indicates superior image quality. PIQE score calculation and visualization were performed using MATLAB R2021a.

Fluorescence and signal-to-noise analysis

The fluorescence signal intensity under 8-bit pixel images was extracted using ImageJ 1.8.0, with the signal intensity range being 0–255, and pseudo-color conversion was performed for each fluorescence image. The tumor-to-normal tissue ratio (TNR) was calculated as the ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity of the malignant tumor to that of the surrounding normal tissue. Similarly, the signal-to-background ratio (SBR) was derived from the mean fluorescence intensity of the benign tumor region relative to the background. Black paper was used as the background.

Histopathology assessment

A standard histopathology assessment served as the gold standard for confirming the status of resected samples and the NIR-II fluorescent margin. The tissue processing involved formalin fixation, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at 5 μm. Finally, the sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as described.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the relationship between the NIR-II and NIR-I imaging, a paired t-test was employed to compare the quantitative data between two independent samples and a chi-square test was used to calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) by Graph Prism 9. Quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and percentage with 95% confidence interval (CI).

Reader study

To evaluate the practical utility of NIR-II imaging with pseudo-color enhancement in tumor assessment, a reader study was conducted involving 12 volunteers with four levels of clinical expertise. After training, volunteers were tasked with evaluating 10 image pairs, each with a native NIR-II and a pseudo-colored version and to determine tumor status and, for cases deemed malignant, each reader was asked to determine whether the queried margin was positive or negative based on the fluorescence signal. Their assessments were compared with the corresponding H&E-based histopathological findings, which served as the gold standard. The average accuracy rate will be compared among the four groups.

Results and discussion

Development of an integrated NIR-I and NIR-II multispectral imaging system for clinical situations

To apply the NIR-II imaging technique in breast surgery clinical practice, we developed a hybrid NIR-I/II multispectral optical system (400–1700 nm) to clinically evaluate NIR-II against conventional NIR-I imaging. The platform integrates a single laser excitation source with dual independent imaging channels for simultaneous NIR-I and NIR-II fluorescence data acquisition in a single operation.

In addition, we used intravenous administration, reducing the injected dose to 1 mg/kg compared with prior reports, and administering ICG within 45 min before surgery [28].

Comparison of NIR-I vs. NIR-II for ex vivo mimic breast tissue imaging

To evaluate the performance of the developed imaging system, we conducted an in vitro comparison of NIR-I and NIR-II imaging (Fig. 1B). Initially, both modalities clearly resolved the two-signal line of ICG dissolved in FBS and loaded in capillary tubes (0.2 mg/mL). While both NIR-I and NIR-II provided clear contrast through 1.0 mm of milk solution, a 4.0 mm layer extinguished the NIR-I signal (Fig. 1C), whereas NIR-II robustly identified the source, affirming its superior resistance to scattering (Fig. 1D), demonstrating its exceptional potential for deep-tissue imaging. Therefore, we chose the PIQE algorithm to quantify our imaging quality between NIR-I and NIR-II (Fig. 1E).

NIR-Ι/II imaging of breast cancer and benign breast tumors

Thirty-seven patients with breast cancer were enrolled for intraoperative NIR-II fluorescence navigation, and they completed a mammogram, ultrasound, and enhanced MRI before the operation. All patients received an intravenous injection of ICG (1.0 mg/kg) 45 min before surgery. Luminal-type and non-luminal-type of breast cancer were visualized under an NIR-I/II multispectral imaging instrument (Fig. 2A row 1–2). NIR-II imaging provided superior clarity and sharper tumor boundaries compared to NIR-I in invasive breast carcinoma. The Luminal-type breast cancers often exhibited polygonal morphology, whereas non-luminal subtypes tended to display more regular contours. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) exhibited significantly lower fluorescence intensity in NIR-II compared to invasive carcinoma, enabling effective differentiation between the two (Fig. 2A row 3).

For 7 patients with benign breast tumors or tissues, they exhibited significantly lower fluorescence intensity visually compared to invasive carcinomas under NIR-II imaging, with sharper boundary delineation compared to NIR-I (Fig. 2A row 4).

The PIQE score of the NIR-II images was lower than that of the NIR-I images in both malignant tumor (53.81 ± 15.56 versus 70.61 ± 10.44, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2B) and benign tumor or tissue (54.81 ± 18.86 versus 74.63 ± 4.81, P < 0.05, Fig. 2C), indicating a better imaging performance of the NIR-II images. We also found that the SD in the NIR-II images was greater compared to NIR-I images, reflecting a higher sensitivity of NIR-II to the underlying structural heterogeneity and scattering properties of the tissue.

NIR-II fluorescence imaging provided an enhanced tumor-detection rate in invasive carcinomas (sensitivity 90.63% versus 84.38%, Table 1). Quantitative analysis indicated that NIR-II imaging showed a significantly higher TNR than that of NIR-I (4.678 ± 1.666 versus 3.013 ± 1.024, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2D) in invasive breast cancer. Considering molecular subtypes, NIR-II imaging also showed significantly higher TNRs than NIR-I imaging in both luminal (4.528 ± 1.672 versus 2.894 ± 0.945, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2F) and non-luminal invasive breast cancer (5.429 ± 1.633 versus 3.609 ± 1.346, P < 0.05, Fig. 2G). Although NIR-II imaging provided better visual delineation of fibroadenoma boundaries, quantitative analysis revealed no significant difference in the signal-to-background ratio between fibroadenoma and normal tissue compared to NIR-I imaging (P = 0.186, Fig. 2E).

Precise NIR-II margin assessment enables smaller, safer, and visualized breast resections

NIR-II fluorescence margin assessment was conducted for all types of breast malignant tumors except Paget’s disease, leveraging its superior tissue penetration and high signal-to-noise ratio to achieve precise boundary delineation. The results were compared with the postoperative pathological results, showing that the NIR-II fluorescence imaging technique achieved 100% accuracy in differentiating malignant tissue margins, among all the 117 margins including 73 luminal-type invasive carcinomas, 16 non-luminal carcinomas, and 28 DCIS, demonstrating excellent diagnostic efficacy (Table 2).

NIR-II fluorescence imaging delineates detailed tumor margin and serves as a complement to other imaging modalities

Ultrasound, enhanced MRI and mammography are the most commonly employed imaging modalities before breast cancer surgery (Fig. 4A). However, they still have limitations in detecting details of tumor, and NIR-II fluorescence imaging could be a complement of those modalities. To precisely establish the spatial correlation between the tumor margins certified by fluorescence imaging and the histological structures in pathological sections, we performed serial sectioning of the tumor margin areas from two patients, with specific focus on the transitional zones covering “carcinoma-margin-normal tissue”.

For patient 1, which is a case of Luminal-type invasive carcinoma, a pathological assessment of the NIR-II-defined margin was performed (Fig. 4B). It typically presents with spiculated features on mammography, an identical spiculated pattern was observed in the NIR-II images. Subsequent pathological examination confirmed that these spiculations consisted of fibrotic stromal components accompanied by minor cancerous foci (Fig. 4C(i)). The regions of changing fluorescence intensity were also spatially correlated with the histologically confirmed positions of para-tumor tissue (Fig. 4C(ii)) and tumor (Fig. 4C(iii)). Transition from carcinoma to adjacent para-cancerous and normal tissues presented by NIR-II fluorescence imaging visualized the actual resection margin in operation.

In Patient 2, while intraoperative mammography detected no additional lesions (Fig. 4D, orange circle) adjacent to the primary tumor (Fig. 4D, red circle), both NIR-I and NIR-II fluorescence imaging revealed a suspicious focus (Fig. 4E, orange circle), with the latter providing superior clarity. The presence of DCIS at this site was subsequently confirmed by histopathology (Fig. 4F). This finding also underscores the potential of NIR-II imaging to serve as a valuable complement to conventional intraoperative imaging techniques.

Furthermore, ex vivo margin detection under NIR-II suggests potential for reducing unnecessary breast tissue excision (Fig. 4G). In Patient 1, the volume detected by NIR-II (44.74 cm3) was 19.01% and 27.59% smaller than that identified by NIR-I (56.49 cm3) and the actual area (61.79 cm3), respectively. Similarly, in patient 2, the NIR-II-derived area (17.27 cm3) was 1.27% and 70.63% smaller compared to the NIR-I (18.02 cm3) and actual areas (58.97 cm3). This approach effectively minimized surgical trauma and preserved a better breast aesthetic.

The advantages of the NIR-II and visible fluorescence hybrid surgical navigation mode

To evaluate whether this innovative imaging technology can reduce the reliance of early-career surgeons on empirical judgment, a total of 12 surgeons of varying professional ranks (from chief surgeons, attending surgeons, residents, to medical students) were enrolled to assess tumor characteristics and surgical margins based on NIR-II fluorescence images. All participants received a standardized explanation of the imaging protocol and evaluation criteria. Each reviewer was presented with two sets of images from the same tumor specimens: original NIR-II grayscale images and pseudo-color-enhanced NIR-II images. For each sample, volunteers were asked to determine tumor nature (benign or malignant) and assess margin status based on NIR-II image features (Fig. 5A).

The performance of the judgment of tumor characteristics was similar between medical students and residents, while chief surgeons and attending surgeons with more experience had better judgment of tumor characteristics. In the judgment of tumor margins, chief surgeons, attending surgeons, and residents showed similar performance, whereas medical students showed relatively lower accuracy (Fig. 5B). Importantly, even medical students, who had virtually little clinical or surgical experience, achieved comparable performance to senior surgeons in interpreting the pseudo-color images within 3 min, both in the judgment of tumor characteristics and tumor margins (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we developed an ICG-based NIR-II fluorescence imaging platform designed for rapid deployment in the operating room. The system integrates smoothly into existing surgical workflows and can be operated under ambient lighting conditions, enabling reliable signal acquisition without disrupting routine procedures. Its user-oriented design lowers the technical threshold for adoption and facilitates precise intraoperative guidance in real-world clinical settings. Importantly, we established a standardized administration protocol, including the timing and dosage of ICG in patients. This standardized approach enhances reproducibility and reduces variability in fluorescence performance across cases. Together, these features substantially improve the practicality and feasibility of translating NIR-II fluorescence imaging into routine intraoperative use.

Quantitative analysis confirmed that the fluorescence intensity for both windows decreased in a depth-dependent manner. Across all depths investigated, the signal intensity in the NIR-II region was consistently and significantly higher than that in the NIR-I, underscoring the superior imaging quality afforded by NIR-II with the multispectral instrument.

The difficulty in acquiring ideal reference images for quality control poses a significant challenge in many imaging techniques, such as clinical CT and intraoperative fluorescence imaging [29]. The PIQE algorithm effectively meets this need as a no-reference image quality assessment tool, which operates by analyzing the intrinsic statistical characteristics of an image itself, thereby highly harmonizing with the clinical application scenarios of fluorescence imaging [30]. In assessing fluorescence image quality, the PIQE algorithm is capable of effective sensitivity to noise, contrast, and blur, alongside a strong capability for artifact identification [27].

The definition of adequate margins in breast-conserving surgery has long been contentious, primarily due to the lack of real-time visualization tools, and the NIR-II fluorescence imaging presents a viable and accurate approach to address this critical shortcoming. Across different types of benign and malignant breast lesions, NIR-II imaging consistently demonstrated superior performance compared with conventional NIR-I imaging. This advantage was reflected in improved signal clarity and lesion delineation, suggesting that the longer emission wavelength of NIR-II may provide more reliable intraoperative visualization across heterogeneous pathological subtypes. A primary reason for the improved imaging quality in the NIR-II window is the substantially reduced photon scattering within biological tissues [31, 32]. As photons traverse biological media, they undergo scattering events that diffuse light, blurring images and degrading resolution, especially at depths exceeding 500 μm in the NIR-I window. In contrast, the longer wavelengths of NIR-II light experience less scattering, allowing photons to travel more directly through tissue [32]. This reduction in scattering directly translates to a higher signal-to-noise ratio and improved spatial resolution in NIR-II imaging. Due to the reduced scattering and absorption of photons, NIR-II light can penetrate biological tissues more effectively than NIR-I, resulting in a better imaging depth.

The combined benefits of increased penetration depth and reduced scattering also lead to higher spatial and temporal resolution in NIR-II imaging [33, 34]. Furthermore, owing to its longer wavelengths, NIR-II exhibits minimal spectral overlap with tissue autofluorescence [35], effectively avoiding its confounding effect. These capability allows researchers to monitor dynamic physiological processes and pathological changes within living organisms in real-time [36].

Our ex vivo imaging data from breast cancer patients demonstrate that the PIQE algorithm effectively captures the qualitative differences between NIR-I and NIR-II fluorescence imaging. The PIQE score is a viable metric for assessing the quality of NIR-I/II images primarily due to its no-reference evaluation characteristics and its sensitivity to various distortions [37]. Despite its advantages, NIR-II imaging remains susceptible to quality-degrading factors, such as system noise, tissue heterogeneity artifacts, uneven contrast agent distribution, and data-processing distortions [36, 38], for which the PIQE score provides a relevant quality assessment standard. In the evaluation of the pathological nature of fluorescent margins, NIR-II imaging showed higher sensitivity, PPV, and NPV than NIR-I imaging. Nevertheless, NIR-II imaging still had a false-positive rate of 9.37% and a false-negative rate of 20%. False-positive findings were mainly associated with breast fibroadenomas, which may exhibit abundant vascularity similar to malignant tumors [39], resulting in strong NIR-II fluorescence signals and potential confusion with malignancy. In actual surgical practice, however, surgeons may obtain additional clues from the tactile characteristics of the lesion. False-negative findings were more likely related to the limited penetration depth of fluorescence imaging, as deeper tumor tissue may show attenuated fluorescence signals. Despite these limitations in tumor characterization, NIR-II imaging achieved 100% accuracy in margin assessment in this study.

In addition to its technical advantages, NIR-II imaging demonstrated a high level of diagnostic concordance with histopathological findings. The fluorescence signal patterns closely corresponded to pathological outcomes, supporting its reliability in intraoperative assessment. Compared with other intraoperative imaging modalities, NIR-II imaging demonstrated the ability to detect DCIS lesions that had been overlooked by preoperative mammography. DCIS is defined as a pre-invasive lesion confined to the epithelial layer without breaching the basement membrane, thus lacking potential for lymphatic metastasis [40]. Since ICG enhances fluorescence in both malignant and inflammatory tissues, NIR-II imaging effectively differentiated invasive carcinoma from in situ lesions, thereby assisting surgeons in intraoperatively determining resection margins and deciding whether lymph node dissection was warranted.

Previous clinical studies have explored ICG-based NIR-I fluorescence imaging in breast-conserving surgery, mainly for localization of non-palpable breast lesions rather than direct margin assessment. Lee et al. [41] reported a 100% lesion identification rate using ultrasound-guided ICG fluorescence, with lower frozen- and final-margin positivity than ultrasound-guided skin marking. Liu et al. [42] also showed that ICG-guided localization was technically feasible and could achieve acceptable clear-margin rates with limited re-excision. A systematic review of 11 studies involving 366 patients further supported ICG fluorescence as a useful and safe method for intraoperative localization of non-palpable breast tumors [43]. However, direct fluorescence-based margin assessment remains less established. Yu et al. [44] evaluated ICG fluorescence for surgical cut-margin assessment and reported encouraging sensitivity but relatively limited specificity. Compared with these ICG-based studies, our study is the first to apply NIR-II fluorescence imaging for direct evaluation of breast tumor margins in clinical surgical specimens. Our results suggest that NIR-II imaging provides improved tumor-to-background contrast and more precise margin visualization, supporting its potential as an adjunctive tool for margin assessment in breast-conserving surgery.

Current consensus guidelines define adequate negative margins according to pathological criteria, including “no ink on tumor” [45] for invasive breast cancer and a 2 mm margin for DCIS [46]. However, these criteria are determined after pathological processing and do not directly provide surgeons with real-time intraoperative guidance. In addition, tissue deformation and shrinkage during fixation may further complicate the spatial correspondence between the intraoperative tumor boundary and the final pathological margin. Therefore, NIR-II imaging may help provide millimeter-scale and real-time visualization of tumor margins and support more precise intraoperative decision-making.

Furthermore, NIR-II imaging is relatively easy to adopt in clinical practice. The ex vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis required only approximately 3 min, with low technical demands in terms of both surgeons, pathologist and equipment, significantly shorter time compared to intraoperative frozen section and definitive histopathology, and low dependency on surgeon seniority, all of which support its feasibility for real-time intraoperative use.

Despite these promising outcomes, this study has several limitations that require refinement in future research. First, NIR-II imaging, as currently implemented, remains a two-dimensional modality, and its performance is still affected by tissue depth. This could lead to subtle discrepancies between the imaged margin and the actual histological margin. In future work, we will further optimize the imaging system from an engineering perspective to minimize the depth-related signal degradation and improve overall image quality. Secondly, the ICG used in this study relies on passive targeting rather than specific tumor targeting. This lack of specificity means ICG may accumulate non-selectively in benign lesions with increased vascular permeability, potentially leading to false-positive signals that complicate the differentiation between benign and malignant lesions—especially in cases where benign and malignant tissues are adjacent.

Conclusions

This study developed a combined NIR-I/II multispectral imaging system and clinically validated the superiority of ICG-based NIR-II fluorescence for intraoperative navigation in breast cancer surgery, especially in BCS. Through in vivo experiments and a 37-patient enrollment, NIR-II achieved higher image quality and signal-to-noise ratio. Importantly, NIR-II imaging offered a decisive clinical advantage by providing higher diagnostic specificity than NIR-I, and it also enabled 100% accurate identification of malignant margins with more precise boundaries than NIR-I or visual inspection, optimizing the balance between complete resection and tissue preservation. Precise visualization of marginal details was delivered by NIR-II fluorescence imaging, and it reduced diagnostic variability among surgeons and allowed rapid analysis within three minutes.

Importantly, this technique extends intraoperative visualization based on existing surgical procedures without altering the clinical workflow or imposing additional operational burden. By visualizing and magnifying regions that are otherwise difficult to judge, it facilitates more reliable discrimination of tumor margins. With further validation, this approach has the potential to support the development of a standardized intraoperative workflow, thereby improving reproducibility and enabling broader clinical adoption. In conclusion, this study represents a milestone in translational surgical imaging, with immediate potential to improve cosmetic and oncological outcomes in breast surgery.
