Mental health and legal statuses along the path to citizenship: A qualitative study of minority ethnic urban dwellers in Chiang Mai, Thailand
Authors: Chantal Herberholz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the lack of legal status, stateless persons often live in precarious situations that put them at risk of mental health problems, especially in urban settings. Moreover, pathways to citizenship may involve navigating different legal statuses over a lifetime, posing additional challenges. Yet, research on the nexus between legal status and mental health remains scant. This study examines the factors that influence the mental health of persons with different legal statuses in a Thai urban context.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative design. Data were collected through 47 in-depth interviews in Northern Thailand’s largest city in July 2023 using purposive and snowball sampling strategies. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis, based on a social determinants of mental health framework, was used to analyse the data.ResultsLegal status was related to multiple and interdependent conditions of precarity in the city. Together they created a compounding effect that threatened mental health. The more restricted the rights attached to a legal status, the greater the effect. Past negative life events, adversed employment conditions, and discrimination were key factors that led to mental health challenges. Obtaining permanent legal status, on the other hand, alleviated legal uncertainty and provided immediate benefits by enhancing access to fundamental rights, with positive effects on mental health. Yet, it cannot reverse pernicious long-term effects of past deprivations, such as foregone opportunities for education and employment, as well as past traumatic events on mental health.ConclusionsThe 2024 landmark move of Thailand’s Cabinet to accelerate progress in resolving citizenship problems for almost 500,000 persons should be accompanied by multi-pronged interventions aimed at improving the social determinants of mental health. In addition, access to mental healthcare should be facilitated.Keywords: Mental health, Urban precarity, Legal status, Ethnic minority groups, Thailand

Introduction

About 4.4 million stateless persons globally were reported in 2023.1 Stateless persons, meaning persons who do not hold any nationality, often face multiple forms of deprivation related to their status as well as ethnicity.2, 3 In consequence, they are at an increased risk of mental disorders.4, 5 Although there is a sizable body of literature on statelessness in general, very few studies focus on mental health outcomes of stateless persons and their determinants.5, 6 Instead, the literature largely concentrates on groups disproportionately affected by statelessness, such as asylum seekers and refugees, among whom mental disorders, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, are found to be highly prevalent.7, 8 Conducting mental health assessments using appropriate tools remains challenging though.9, 10

High rates of mental disorders among undocumented migrants, asylum seekers, and refugees are caused by conditions along the phases of migration, namely pre-migration in the country of origin, during the migration journey, and post-migration in the destination country.4, 11, 12, 13 In conflict-affected settings, pre-migration exposure to war events and post-displacement daily stressors, meaning stressful conditions of everyday life in post-displacement environments, have been identified as significant contributors to psychological distress.4 Recent work accentuates that mental health is influenced by social determinants in each migration phase,14, 15 spanning interrelated demographic, economic, neighbourhood, social, as well as environmental events domains as conceptualized by Lund et al.16 Examples include potentially traumatic experiences15, 17 and unmet basic needs (e.g. lack of food security, protection, and accommodation) throughout all migration phases,15 as well as poor socioeconomic factors, such as low income, unemployment, living in institutional accommodation, and lack of social support post-migration.12, 18

Legal status uncertainty post-migration is a risk factor for mental health explicitly recognized in several studies on migrant populations.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Not having a nationality adversely impacts social determinants of mental health and constitutes a major source of precarity, a multi-stranded concept that is typically associated with a lack of stability across life conditions.24, 25, 26 Unlike precariousness, a term used for the general uncertainty or insecurity related to life conditions, precarity is unequally distributed across the population.26 Butler27 defines precarity as a “politically induced condition in which certain populations suffer from failing social and economic networks of support”. Hence, precarity is a state experienced by “marginalised, poor, and disenfranchised people” such as those excluded from citizenship.26 Key aspects of life such as family relationships, work conditions, access to services, benefits as well as protection are permeated by legal status uncertainty28, 29 and, in the case of refused asylum-seekers, legal status change.30 Research suggests that status regularization, on the other hand, is positively associated with the mental health of undocumented migrants.31, 32

Urban areas, which attract most displaced populations around the world, have been described as environments that shape forms and levels of precarity.33, 34 In these settings, additional challenges can amplify mental health risks,35, 36 especially when legal status is uncertain. Risk factors for mental health in urban areas generally include social conditions, such as concentrations of low socio-economic status, limited social capital, and spatial or social segregation, as well as factors related to the physical environment, like higher levels of pollution, lack of green spaces, and more physical threats.37, 38, 39 Risk factors specific to migrants include, for example, strict immigration enforcement policies, low neighbourhood ethnic density, and exclusionary urban labour markets.30 Urbanicity post-migration, measured by population density, was found to increase the risk of psychosis for some migrant groups in the empirical study by Schofield et al.40

Thailand is used as an example in this study to qualitatively examine the interrelated factors that affect the mental health of stateless and formerly stateless persons in an urban setting since the country has one of the largest stateless populations globally, with 586,500 stateless persons registered in the national civil registration system.1 Neither the 1951 Convention nor the 1967 Protocol relating to the Status of Refugees has been signed by Thailand.41 Statelessness has been a long-standing issue in Thailand caused by discrimination, inconsistent and uncertain registration practices, as well as bureaucratic failures among other factors.42 Registered stateless persons are known as nationalityless persons in the Thai context. Nationalityless persons are granted temporary residence until completion of a legal status verification process.43, 44 Apart from offering a pathway to citizenship, the set of rights further include access to public education and healthcare, mobility within the province of registration, as well as employment rights as summarized in Supplementary Table 1.45, 46, 47 Most nationalityless persons belong to ethnic minorities residing in mountainous border areas.48 Stateless persons, on the other hand, are not afforded the same rights as nationalityless persons in Thailand since they are unregistered and unrecognized.43, 44 These terms are unique to the Thai context but have been associated with the concept of effective statelessness.6, 42 Effective statelessness refers to a condition where neither the right to citizenship can be exercised (because proof of nationality does not exist or is not accepted by authorities) nor the status of a stateless person be obtained.49 In the literature, the in-between of statelessness and citizenship has also been conceptualized as “liminal statelessness”50 or, focusing on the grey areas between documented and undocumented immigration statuses, as “liminal legality”.29, 51 Due to the protracted nature of statelessness situations, politically driven policy changes, as well as the flawed issuance of identification documents in Thailand, different legal statuses are held over a lifetime by those facing citizenship problems.42, 52, 53 In view of limited opportunities in Thailand’s remote areas, stateless and nationalityless persons are increasingly out-migrating.54, 55 Outward migration from rural into urban areas has been linked with precarious employment as well as precarity more generally in the literature.56, 57

Against this background, the objective of the present study is to qualitatively understand the factors that affect the mental health of stateless, nationalityless and formerly nationalityless persons of ethnic backgrounds in a city setting. First, this study extends the wider literature, that relates post-migration conditions to the mental health of groups disproportionately affected by statelessness, as it specifically focuses on stateless persons while accounting for the fact that most stateless persons are stateless in situ rather than displaced.58 This research applies the conceptual framework of Lund et al.16, 59 and examines its relevance to stateless persons. Second, this study is situated in an urban environment. Studies that examine mental health challenges faced by stateless persons in migratory or non-migratory situations either use data from camp settings or from rural areas5, 6 but not urban environments. Yet, these settings have been associated with a greater risk of poor mental health.35, 36 Third, this study accounts for diverse legal statuses on the pathway to citizenship, characterized by varying degrees of legal protection, thereby contributing to the literature on in-between legal statuses29, 51, 60 as well as the wider range of factors beyond documentation.50 Analysing the experiences of formerly stateless persons who obtained a permanent status, has become particularly relevant for policy following a recent announcement which stated that the Cabinet of Thailand approved expediting the citizenship application process for more than 480,000 persons, focusing on long-term residents and children born in Thailand.61

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative design using in-depth interviews to understand the factors that influence the mental health of persons with different legal statuses in an urban setting. Legal statuses considered in this study are statelessness, nationalitylessness, and former nationalitylessness. The semi-structured interview guide used for stateless and nationalityless respondents (Supplementary Text 1) revolves around the social determinants of mental health across the life-course framework developed by Lund et al.16 The framework applies a life-course perspective to illustrate how social determinants, such as early life adversity, migration, and sustained disadvantage, accumulate over time and shape mental health outcomes. Questions related to mental health aimed at eliciting feelings, emotions, and symptoms associated with mental health conditions. Mental health-related experiences and symptoms were solicited without the use of formal clinical assessments, reflecting a qualitative approach that prioritizes contextual understanding and centres participants’ lived experiences.62, 63, 64 Additional questions for formerly nationalityless persons, inspired by Blitz et al.,2 focus on legal status change and its impact on mental health (Supplementary Text 2). Ethical clearance was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee for Research Involving Human Research Participants, Group 1, Chulalongkorn University (Certificate of Approval number 142/66).

Participants

Participants were recruited purposively in Chiang Mai city through the network of a local foundation working with stateless and nationalityless persons (45 respondents) as well as snowballing (two respondents). Chiang Mai city is the largest city in Northern Thailand and the third largest in the country.65 About one fifth of nationalityless persons are registered in Chiang Mai province.48 The inclusion criteria were (i) considered stateless, nationalityless, or formerly nationalityless as defined in this study, (ii) belonging to an ethnic minority group, (iii) having lived in Chiang Mai city for at least three months, (iv) of working age (15 years and above), (v) able to speak or communicate in Thai.

Assessment of legal status

The assessment of statelessness is challenging due to problems with documentation and other evidence.42 Moreover, there is a large number of migrant workers in Chiang Mai, most of whom belong to the Tai-Yai ethnic group,66 which further complicates the assessment of legal status as the boundaries between these categories are often blurred. Legal status in this study is determined based on the assessment of the local team and respondents’ self-reports. Respondents who report that they do not have any identification documents are believed to be stateless (without legal status), while those holding an identification card (ID) starting with number zero, six or seven are considered to be nationalityless (with temporary legal status). Included in the first category are also unregistered stateless students in the Thai education system with a temporary identification number for school use starting with G (the so-called G-series).45 The 13-digit ID number starting with number zero has been issued to persons “without civil registration records” since 2005, while numbers six and seven were inter alia given to defined minority groups and their descendants.67 Possession of an ID has been used as indicator of legal status in other studies.6, 54, 68 Additionally, several probing questions were asked to confirm legal status. Formerly nationalityless persons (with now permanent legal status) are defined in this study as persons who received an ID starting with number five or eight. While the former is issued to Thai and alien persons who were permitted to add their names in house registration records (e.g. because they missed population surveys and were not registered in the first place), the latter is given to foreigners who were granted Thai nationality after birth or permission to permanently reside in Thailand.69 For the purpose of this study, acquisition of citizenship is defined as the receipt of ID cards starting with numbers five or eight since these represent the highest legal statuses that can be achieved by nationalityless persons.

Data collection

All but two in-depth interviews were arranged by the local foundation team, trusted for their longstanding support of persons with citizenship problems. They provided first information about the study, initiated contact, and helped develop rapport with respondents. Two respondents were identified based on recommendations from other participants. To ensure privacy and confidentiality, interviews were either conducted at the participant’s residence or at another prearranged location. In-depth interviews were conducted by the researcher, who has advanced Thai speaking skills, and a local Thai interpreter in Chiang Mai city in July 2023. The interpreter, trained by the researcher before the data collection to reduce translator bias, provided Thai-English interpretation. The interview guide was tested with two pilot participants who were not recruited for the main study. Prior to the interview, participants were provided with written and verbal information about the study’s purpose, procedures, risks and benefits, as well as participants’ rights. Informed consent was subsequently obtained and documented using a written informed consent form in Thai. Respondents were initially assigned a sequential identification number prior to the interview. After the data collection, these identifiers were randomized to further protect confidentiality. Interviews were completed within one hour on average and audio-recorded with the permission of interviewees. To record additional observations during the interviews, field notes were taken. Upon completion of the interview, participants received 150 Baht as a token of appreciation.

The sample size of 45 respondents was determined in cooperation with the local foundation based on their records. The aim was to recruit 15 participants from each of the three legal status categories, namely stateless, nationalityless, and formerly nationalityless, with the possibility of recruiting replacements from the two other categories. Saturation was reached before the planned sample size was achieved. The systematic review by Hennink and Kaiser70 confirms that saturation can be achieved with a relatively small sample size of between 9 and 17 interviews.

Data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim in Thai and subsequently translated into English. Familiarization with the data was achieved through transcript review and repeated reading. For the analysis of verbatim transcriptions, qualitative data analysis software (i.e. NVivo 12) was employed using thematic analysis following an inductive-deductive coding approach. Since cross-border migration is not the only situation in which statelessness arises and given that most stateless persons are stateless in situ,58 a social determinants of mental health framework was used rather than an approach centred on different migration phases. Initial codes were drawn from the conceptual framework of the social determinants of mental health by Lund et al.59 and Lund et al.,16 which is based on five interrelated domains, that is demographic, economic, neighbourhood, social, and environmental events and separates proximal and distal factors. Coding started deductively to provide structure, ensure theoretical relevance, and reduce bias.71 Additional codes were developed inductively in response to emerging patterns within the data. Themes predicted a priori were environmental events, economic factors, social factors, neighbourhood, and changes in life circumstances associated with a change in legal status (Supplementary Table 2). To accentuate the cross-cutting nature of ethnicity, a proximal factor in the demographic domain of the framework, and legal status, these are interwoven with each theme and sub-theme. Although legal status is not explicitly addressed in the framework, this study situated it within the demographic domain, given that the majority of stateless persons are from ethnic minority backgrounds.72 Field notes were used to capture contexts, document decisions made in the field, and record personal reflections on emotions, positionality, and other reflexive insights. The author brought expertise in the social determinants of mental health, legal status-related challenges, and qualitative research. With only one researcher involved, inter alia due to the sensitivity of the topic, rich verbatim quotations were provided to strengthen the validity of the findings. In addition, the findings were triangulated with the existing literature to further substantiate the findings.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The sample for this study (n = 47) predominantly comprised respondents who identified as Tai-Yai (Table 1). Most respondents were in possession of an ID at the time of the interview, holding either an ID starting with number zero, six or seven (43 %) or an ID starting with number five or eight (34 %). The remaining respondents reported that they did not hold any valid identification document (23 %). While some formerly nationalityless persons acquired citizenship only recently, others had it for more than a decade. The length of the legal status verification process ranged from about one to sixteen years. Respondents were mostly early adults, married, and living with others. More than one fourth did not receive formal education. Four respondents had familial ties with other respondents according to field notes. All respondents had a rural-to-urban migration background although reasons for migration and migration trajectories differred substantially. Most respondents migrated internally, and economic and education opportunities emerged as leading incentives for outward migration from remote villages to the city. Several respondents migrated in childhood with their parents who took up work in the city, but the majority left their villages in adolescence or as adults in search of education and/or (better) work. About one third of respondents were cross-border migrants who left Myanmar for Thailand, often in childhood or adolescence. They usually stayed with relatives or acquaintances in rural areas before migrating to Chiang Mai. Some left Myanmar to escape the ongoing civil war with ethnic minority groups and, in case of male respondents, compulsory conscription, others migrated in search of economic opportunities and a better life in Thailand.

Qualitative findings

This section presents participant responses organized by themes, with corresponding codes outlined in Supplementary Table 2. Participant labels were supplemented with sex, age, and legal status to provide context.

Environmental events: potentially traumatic experiences in the past had mental health consequences

Experiences related to environmental events, defined by Lund et al.59 as “serious disruptions of community functioning”, such as natural disasters, war and forced migration convey risk for mental illness, especially for children.59, 73 Among the respondents were four persons orphaned in childhood. Parental loss was another adverse childhood experience that can increase the risk of developing mental health conditions.74 One respondent recalled that he migrated in childhood, on foot with foster carers and other villagers, from Myanmar to Thailand after the military had burned down their houses, while a second respondent lost his parents at a young age on the way to a refugee camp on the border and migrated from there with foster carers. Similarly, other respondents were exposed to adverse childhood experiences such as violence (including sexual violence experienced by one female participant during adolescence and unrelated to conflict or war), armed conflict and forced migration, giving rise to persistent emotional distress. One respondent explained that she cannot forget the traumatic event even though it happened a long time ago.

The men always had to run. If they did not, they would be captured, forced to be porters, and would be beaten by the Burmese soldiers. My mother was also beaten. She was hit with a bamboo stick so hard that it broke, all over her body. … I was still young at the time, but I felt sorry for my mother because she could not even stand up. … To this day, I have never forgotten that event. It feels like it just happened. (R30, female, 33 years old, nationalityless)

Another respondent described the traumatic experience as follows:

When I arrived at the camp, it was because of the war. My biological parents were lost, they died. … It significantly impacted my mental health because I lost my parents. When I first arrived, I had not met my foster parents yet. I lived and ate with many others. I slept in what they called a “hut”. I had no clothes, was bitten by mosquitoes, and cried for many months. (R11, male, 37 years old, nationalityless)

Once in Thailand, cross-border migrants like in situ stateless and nationalityless persons were exposed to the risk of harassment, detention, and possibly deportation by the Thai authorities due to their legal status. Perceptions of police misconduct (and of the civil service more generally) as well as their focus on ethnic minorities with citizenship problems were widespread, leading to feelings of fear, stress and anxiety. Four respondents shared experiences of incarceration in childhood and adolescence, alone or with a parent. A desire to replace negative memories with constructive thoughts could be observed in one respondent. She laughed and said:

When I was selling flowers, when I was about ten years old, I got caught by the police, and that was very scary. … They said they would send me back to the border in fifteen days. But the good thing was that I had relatives with [identification] cards, so they came to release me. At first, I thought I would have to go back to Myanmar. I was really scared. I was caught together with people from different ethnic groups. I stayed there for seven days, and during that time, I started thinking about various things. That is when I learned about IDs and other things. If I had not been caught, I would not have known about it. (R40, female, 33 years old, nationalityless)

After recalling her childhood experience, she stayed silent for a while, appearing disconnected from the present moment. None of the respondents had or has access to mental health services.

Economic factors: intersecting conditions of precarity interacted to produce a compounded burden on mental health

Financial hardship, labour market discrimination in the city, limited educational opportunities, and limited access to healthcare emerged as central sub-themes in the economic domain, consistent with Lund et al.’s framework.59

Financial hardship

Several respondents experienced childhood poverty and three said that they were involved in begging during their childhood due to poverty. Family poverty is a further adverse childhood experience that may lead to poor mental health.74 Other respondents encountered financial hardship and food insecurity later in life. Emotions and feelings associated with these experiences include shame, fear, and stress. One adult respondent described the effect of having to ask for food as follows:

The worst, most terrifying and degrading moment … There was this one time, I had to ask for food from a foundation. I had nothing at all. At that moment, I felt like I was going to die. Honestly, I never thought that after having children, I would have to ask for food like this. Tears just came to my eyes. I had to do something, anything, so my children would not feel embarrassed. (R22, male, 35 years old, nationalityless)

About one third of respondents were exposed to precarious work in the city, offering little or no security and low wages. Casual or seasonal work in the informal sector is common, especially for those working as maids and house cleaners as well as on construction sites. Several respondents were working as street vendors. One fourth of respondents (all nationalityless or formerly nationalityless), on the other hand, were permanently employed by a company, many with an employment contract and social security coverage.

Economic impact of COVID-19

The coronavirus pandemic was experienced as a major negative shock affecting respondents’ livelihoods and mental health. Mental health challenges included stress, anxiety, and sleep problems. Most respondents encountered income losses and some faced food insecurity as a result. Several employers reduced the number of paid working days per month to be able to retain staff, while others dismissed workers, permanently or temporarily. Day labour demand also diminished and self-employed respondents such as street vendors were unable to work due to lockdown measures.

I did not go anywhere during that time. … I did not dare to go out. They [the family] would not let me because it would have been difficult to get treatment and we did not have the means to pay. So, I stayed in my room. As it [the pandemic] subsided, I came out. At that time, I basically only had instant noodles every day. (R9, male, 33 years old, stateless)

Government welfare schemes initiated in response to the pandemic like other social assistance programmes prioritize citizens. Several stateless and nationalityless respondents explained that they had never received any assistance due to their legal status.

Labour market discrimination

Discrimination is a key obstacle in the labour market with negative implications for mental health. It affects access to employment, career paths, promotion opportunities, as well as wages and gives rise to feelings of stress, anxiety, and hopelessness. Scale and scope, however, varied by legal status, with stateless and nationalityless respondents most affected and confined to low-productivity orlow-paying work. Discrimination in hiring due to legal status was common and amplified by poor educational outcomes.

There were many, many challenges. When I went to apply for a job, I did not have a card. I did not go and study. Most of the time, all I could get was housework. Other types of work were not available to me. (R13, female, 36 years old, stateless)

Some adults are like "Why didn't you finish school? Why can't you find a good job?" It is like "Why”. Then I would tell them that I cannot find work … This is stressful in the sense of “why don't I have opportunities like other people, why don’t I have the same rights as others”. … I have been worried, could not sleep, and sometimes even cried. There were moments when I felt stressed, thinking "If only I had Thai nationality, I would have more opportunities to earn money than with just this zero card”. (R38, female, 26 years old, nationalityless)

Wage discrimination was another type of discrimination mentioned by several respondents. Thai citizens frequently earned more than nationalityless and stateless persons and/or enjoyed additional benefits such as meals and drinking water.

Some people get 400 Baht, while others get 380 Baht [per day]. If someone does not have documents, they might get around 200–250 Baht. Anything is better than having no money, so we endure it. (R42, female, 46 years old, stateless)

Similarly, self-employed respondents peddling goods are exposed to discriminatory practices related to their legal status and ethnicity, especially since street vending is one of the occupations prohibited for foreigners.75 Conversely, others, most notably formerly stateless respondents, said that they did not face wage discrimination at their workplace and that they perceived wages to be aligned with qualifications.

Limited educational opportunities

Thailand adopted an Education for All policy in 1999 and passed a Cabinet Resolution on Education for Unregistered Persons in 2005, according to which 15 years of free education are offered to all children, irrespective of legal status.45 Access to school student loans, scholarship programmes, and higher education, as well as participation in school activities, however, remained challenging and often beyond reach. Education was seen by respondents as essential for obtaining direction in life and gaining (better) employment. Several respondents completed or were currently pursuing further, mostly non-formal, education. Graduation from a higher education institution, moreover, might be a requirement for obtaining citizenship depending on the pertinent application track.69

It [education] made my life better and gave me confidence. Had I not received education, I would not know where to be right now. Education and the educational system have helped me. (R2, male, 15 years old, stateless: G-series)

Ordaining as a Buddhist novice in the city was a pathway chosen by three young respondents and/or their parents to access further education. Like others they were separated from family members in their quest for education, which can lead to mental health problems as a formerly nationalityless person pointed out.

Sometimes, young novices cry because their parents are not with them. After ordaining, they might only get to see their parents once or twice a year. It is as if we are separated and belong to a different family, unrelated. … The parents go to work in Bangkok, here and there. Sometimes they disappear altogether and the child stays at the temple. (R16, male, 44 years old, formerly nationalityless)

Missed opportunities due to a lack of access to education or a lack of information about education opportunities were voiced by respondents who attended school before the Education for All policy was implemented. Many also recalled being teased and bullied at school and how it made them feel sad and not wanting to go to school.

Limited access to healthcare for some

Since a health insurance for (registered) persons with citizenship problems was launched in 2010 and successively expanded as part of Thailand’s universal health coverage system, access barriers and unmet healthcare needs were mostly reported by stateless respondents. Yet, many nationalityless and former nationalityless persons recalled that they encountered multiple barriers in the past, most notably discrimination and problems paying for healthcare services which caused emotional distress during the visit.

While waiting for the nurse, they told me that I have to pay. I have to pay. I was stressed, and I did not have the money to pay. (R37, male, 67 years old, stateless)

Delayed or inappropriate medical treatment associated with a legal status can have serious consequences such as the loss of loved ones and lead to mental health challenges. One nationalityless respondent shared memories of a highly distressing experience related to his mother’s illness that was treated for years at a community hospital. At that time, the mother’s identification card did not yet allow her to receive benefits under the government’s health insurance for persons with citizenship problems. Surgical procedures were postponed in the hope of benefitting from expanded health insurance coverage in the future. When his mother’s health condition deteriorated, she was referred to another hospital where she learned that the surgery could not be delayed any longer. The out-of-pocket estimate the family received exceeded their entire savings. Moreover, an upfront deposit was required. By the time the family had gathered the necessary information and prepared enough money for the deposit, it was too late. The mother suddenly went into shock, was rushed to the hospital, and passed away.

Formerly nationalityless respondents and respondents working in the formal sector were covered by mainstream public health insurance schemes, which offer comprehensive benefit packages delivered through public and private healthcare providers. Furthermore, the scheme applicable to formal sector workers, includes benefits unrelated to sickness such as, for example, unemployment support and old-age pensions. No access barriers to healthcare were reported by respondents registered in these schemes.

I had a surgery at a hospital that accepted social security, and they provided good care. (R45, male, 37 years old, formerly nationalityless)

Social factors: despite strong social integration, instances of social isolation and loneliness existed, undermining mental health

Respondents’ social network mainly comprised friends and relatives from their own or other ethnic groups, in the city as well as in their villages of origin. Notwithstanding, about one third of respondents stated that their social network also included Thai citizens, especially friends from school and work. Social networks are considered an important source of information and support, leading to improved mental state. In contrast, a few other respondents said that they had no close friends and no one to turn to if needed, giving rise to feelings of isolation, loneliness, and in some cases suicidal thoughts as the following quote from a respondent who experienced childhood war violence exemplifies.

Like when I have a problem, I do not know who could listen to me. Maybe if I had friends, I could tell them and it would probably be a good thing. One has to talk first to release feelings. It feels relieving. If I keep it to myself, sometimes, I even feel like ending it all. I wonder if it would be the end of everything. Living on can be hard sometimes. I think about it. (R30, female, 33 years old, nationalityless)

Respondents who reported feelings of isolation or loneliness included stateless respondents and some respondents who lived apart from their families.

Joining interest groups and volunteering helped respondents to form new relationships or deepen existing ones, as well as to better navigate the challenges associated with their legal status. Many respondents engaged in community activities were or had been involved in volunteer work to help ethnic minorities in remote areas, persons with citizenship or other legal problems, as well as vulnerable persons more generally. Volunteering is associated with knowledge acquisition, increased self-esteem, self-confidence and well-being.

I gained confidence. It is related to past lack of knowledge about citizenship issues. Now I have a lot of knowledge. There are people who make us see that there are others who may have more problems than we do. (R14, female, 20 years old, nationalityless)

I feel good when I see them succeed, especially when they have problems that are resolved. I feel happy for them even though I do not get anything. (R44, female, nationalityless)

Neighbourhoods: urban living conditions in low-income neighbourhoods showed some links with mental health symptoms

In Chiang Mai, most respondents lived in poor neighbourhoods with predominantly co-ethnics and other ethnic minorities, although a few said that they had primarily Thai citizens as neighbours. Hardly any of the respondents thought that they were poorer than their neighbours. Neighbours generally keept to themselves and there was little interaction. Neighbourhood conflicts and violence in their communities were uncommon. A few respondents, however, said that they occasionally faced sleep disturbances caused by neighbours or traffic, resulting in stress for those affected. Air pollution, heat, congestion, and the absence of green spaces nearby were other stressors pointed out by several respondents.

When it comes to issues like pollution, it can be stressful. … It makes me worry about my own health. … At home [a mountainous, rural village], the air is better. I have my parents and family at home, so I feel safe and at ease. (R25, female, 25 years old, nationalityless)

Legal status change: legal recognition brought immediate benefits that positively influence mental well-being

Receiving a temporary ID starting with number zero, six or seven is considered a turning point in respondents’ lives and a path towards citizenship. Its receipt conveys a feeling of identity as well as security. In addition, the card comes with some legal benefits, including access to healthcare services. The immediate effects of acquiring citizenship also primarily relate to having more rights and being able to exercise these. Effects accentuated by respondents included (i) freedom of movement, (ii) work and educational opportunities, (iii) increased recognition at work, (iv) land ownership, (v) access to social assistance, and (vi) better access to a range of services. Being able to present an ID starting with numbers five or eight simplifies identity verification processes, reduces paperwork and facilitates access to healthcare, education and financial services, as well as government services more generally. In addition, interaction quality when accessing public services improved.

When I did not have the card [starting with number eight], for example, when I went to the district office, they would talk to me in a somewhat condescending manner, not very politely. But once I got the card, it seemed like they started speaking a bit better. (R43, female, 31 years old, formerly nationalityless: acquired citizenship less than five years ago)

Change in legal status was associated with increased confidence, feeling happy, more secure, and equal to others, as well as having less worries. It profoundly reinforced feelings of identity as well as security. Overall, respondents said that they were able to live a much more convenient and comfortable life following the acquisition of citizenship.

It is like winning the first prize in the lottery. It is really true! … I am happy, very happy. … I kept looking at it [ID] when it was new. (R35, female, 44 years old, formerly nationalityless: recently acquired citizenship)

My salary went up from 11,000 to 13,000 and now it is 15,000 [Baht per month]. I can say that there have been significant changes due to having a nationality. … I feel that I have the right to be equal to others. When I do something, I feel more courageous. (R18, female, 36 years old, formerly nationalityless: recently acquired citizenship)

When I received the national ID card, I felt I have the right to speak up a bit. I have a voice, and I can dare to say that something is not right, like low wages. You could think about it, but how much to pay was up to them in the past. … We felt like we were constantly trying to restrain ourselves, reminding ourselves that we were not Thai citizens and did not have basic rights. But as soon as you have an ID card, you can make everything happen just like anyone else can. (R20, male, 29 years old, formerly nationalityless: acquired citizenship more than five years ago)

The fact that depriving long-term effects, especially those related to foregone education and career opportunities, cannot be alleviated by the acquisition of citizenship alone was only mentioned by a few. When asked whether there should be a compensation for lost opportunities given a life in limbo until acquisition of citizenship, younger respondents agreed and said that such compensation could take the form of educational opportunities, including scholarships, that would enable them to further their education. Two respondents added that their new ID starting with number eight does not convey all citizenship rights since it does not permit working in occupations that are restricted to natural born citizens. Occupations that require a natural born citizen status mainly refer to specific roles in government (e.g. judge, public prosecutor). The first number on their ID differs from the one used for natural born persons which they fear could potentially perpetuate discrimination.

Discussion

Consistent with the social determinants of mental health framework, factors from all domains were found to affect the mental health of respondents, albeit to different extents. Experiences of potentially traumatic events related to war, conflict and violence were described by respondents who migrated from Myanmar to Thailand. These and other potentially traumatic events experienced during the different phases of the migration process, such as detention, separation from parents and abuse, led to emotional distress and a long-lasting influence on mental health which echoes findings in the literature.7, 8 Moving to the city further intensified fears of detention and deportation, especially among stateless and nationalityless respondents due to their legal status. Compared to cities, remote villages are more protected spaces given that citizenship problems are experienced communally in these areas as highland ethnic minority groups have been systematically excluded from citizenship.42 Immigration enforcement stress has been associated with mental health conditions in the literature76, 77 and its intensity was found to differ across spaces.78

While rural to urban migration is seen as an important livelihood improvement strategy,54, 55 stateless and nationalityless respondents did not have the same opportunities for employment in the city compared to Thai citizens, which increased their vulnerability. Corresponding to prior findings,6 respondents reported experiences of discrimination in hiring, wage discrimination, as well as promotion discrimination and described their detrimental effect on mental health. Likewise, undocumented status is associated with worse employment conditions in the literature,25, 79, 80 while precarity and informality are considered a consequence of statelessness.50 Overall, the findings of this study confirm the negative effects of precarious employment as well as the influence of post-displacement characteristics more generally on mental health36, 81, 82 and highlight how respondents’ socio-economic position has been shaped by their legal status and ethnicity over the life-course. Missed learning opportunities and low educational achievements were found to be linked to limited career choices and changes. Barriers to education experienced included lack of access to student scholarships and loans, absence of education support, and stigmatization at school. Similar findings are documented in other studies using data from highlander populations as well as stateless persons in Thailand.6, 83, 84 Access to healthcare was another basic right stateless persons were commonly deprived of. While Thailand launched a health insurance for persons with citizenship problems in 2010, the scheme faced numerous implementation challenges.85 In contrast, formerly nationalityless respondents and respondents working in the formal sector were covered by well-developed mainstream public health insurance schemes. Most respondents, however, experienced access barriers to healthcare at some point in their lives and accentuated the detrimental consequences for their mental health. Lack of access to healthcare, both personally and within one's family, can significantly affect mental health, leading to untreated or under-treated conditions and, in some cases, preventable death.59

Research suggests that living in areas with high own-group ethnic density is a protective factor for mental health.86 Participants in this study mostly lived in urban neighbourhoods with predominantly co-ethnics and other ethnic minorities. Exposure to neighbourhood violence and disorder was rarely mentioned, but several respondents voiced environmental concerns. Hazardous seasonal air pollution levels are common in Chiang Mai and a risk factor for physical and mental health of residents.87

Social interactions and social support can be risk or protective factors for mental health conditions of migrants.15 Most respondents had social networks to rely on and considered these a key protective factor for mental health. Conversely, however, a number of respondents faced social isolation and social exclusion related to their legal status. Experiences of social isolation and social exclusion appeared to be specific to a few participants and may not represent a broader pattern. Volunteering was found to support mental health and identified a protective factor, which is in concordance with a recent umbrella review.15

Migration is a social determinant of mental health and moving from rural to urban areas can increase the risk for mental health conditions. The diverse experiences of respondents examined in this study demonstrated the complex and interrelated nature of legal status, ethnicity, and other social determinants of mental health. Respondents experienced multiple conditions of precarity in the city with compounding effect that threatens their mental health. These conditions are predominantly related to economic and social factors. Difficulties in securing decent employment, for example, are compounded by low levels of educational attainment, discrimination based on legal status and ethnicity, and lack of support.88 In addition, potentially traumatic experiences pre-migration may be compounded by post-migration factors, particularly lack of access to mental health services.15 Mendola et al.28 also emphasize that refugees in Germany experience interrelated risks that have a “propagation effect” on their vulnerability. Changes in circumstances related to these domains cause the intensity of urban precarity to fluctuate over time as also demonstrated for the case of temporary migrants in Chacko and Price.34

The acquisition of citizenship had immediate positive effects that substantially improved participation in civil life and the mental health of respondents. Similarly, status regularization positively influences mental health in studies that focus on undocumented migrants.31, 32 Benefits accentuated by formerly nationalityless participants in this study included freedom of movement, access to work and educational opportunities, increased recognition at work, ability to own land, (better) access to government services and assistance. These benefits, however, cannot undo the results of past deprivations as also discussed in Blitz et al.2

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its focus on a hard-to-reach and under-researched population, particularly within urban contexts. By anchoring the analysis in participants’ own words, the study connected lived experiences to a well-established framework of the social determinants of mental health, offering both depth and authenticity to its findings. The data, collected in 2023, also allowed for an assessment of how a large-scale shock such as the COVID-19 pandemic can exacerbate existing risk factors and negatively impact mental health. Another strength of this study is the use of purposive sampling with the help of the local foundation whose members have worked with persons experiencing legal status problems for years. This facilitated both participant identification and the assessment of legal status, specifically stateless, nationalityless, and formerly nationalityless, which is central to the study and contributes to the overall quality of the data.

However, as also discussed in Herberholz,6 assessment of legal status is nonetheless challenging, inter alia due to the cultural proximity of ethnic minorities on both sides of the Thai-Myanmar border. Besides, purposive sampling, based on the network of one single foundation, introduced selection bias which is a limitation of this study. Second, the relatively small sample size implies that the results cannot be generalized, nor does it permit an analysis of ethnic heterogeneity. Moreover, data were only collected in Chiang Mai city, the capital of Chiang Mai province which borders Myanmar. Therefore, the findings have to be understood in this context. Experiences of persons with citizenship problems who migrated to other cities, most notably Bangkok, will be different. Third, while responses to questions about mental health symptoms may indicate a mental health condition, no formal mental health assessment was conducted. Furthermore, none of the respondents had ever accessed mental health services or had a record of diagnosed mental disorder. Fourth, this study focuses on legal status and ethnicity and does not consider other causes of discrimination. Moreover, despite safeguards in place, the risk of errors in interpretation and translation cannot be ruled out.

Implications for future research

Future research should conduct formal mental health assessments using standardized tools. While mental health symptoms may indicate the presence of a mental health disorder, only a formal assessment can determine whether this is the case. In addition, future research should engage a range of stakeholders, including, for example, non-governmental organizations, healthcare providers, border patrol police, and local and national government agencies working with persons with legal status problems to facility policy translation.

Conclusions

This qualitative study pinpoints conditions of precarity in Chiang Mai city, primarily in the social and economic domains, and demonstrates that their intensity varied by legal status. Discrimination based on ethnicity and legal status was found across multiple, interrelated dimensions which posed a compounded threat to mental health. Policies aimed at reducing discrimination against persons with legal status problems should be actively pursued, possibly including the implementation of anonymous complaint and investigation services that offer protection and support. Formerly nationalityless persons, on the other hand, received most of the benefits associated with citizenship. However, they continued to bear the consequences of past deprivations. Hence, accelerating the processing time of citizenship applications, as envisaged in the proposal approved by the Thai Cabinet in 2024, is crucial since the consequences of prolonged statelessness and nationalitylessness cannot be remedied later in life. The examples discussed in this study further suggest that access to inclusive mental health services should be prioritized given that untreated mental health conditions can have serious consequences. Moreover, the resolution of citizenship issues must be accompanied by supporting policies that recognize and address the social determinants of mental health along the dimensions discussed in this study.
