Association of non-HDL cholesterol with plaque burden and composition of culprit lesion in acute coronary syndrome. An intravascular ultrasound-virtual histology study
Authors: Sreenivas Reddy, Raghavendra Rao K, Jeet Ram Kashyap, Vikas Kadiyala, Suraj Kumar, Debabrata Dash, Lipi Uppal, Jaspreet Kaur, Manpreet Kaur, Hithesh Reddy, Imran Ibni Gani Rather, Samir Malhotra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLipids play key role in coronary atherosclerosis. The role of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) in atherosclerotic plaques using intravascular imaging remains unclear. This study aimed to assess its relationship with coronary plaque features using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in acute coronary syndrome (ACS).MethodsA total of 601 patients divided into two groups: normal non-HDL-C≤130 mg/dl (n = 410) and high non-HDL cholesterol >130 mg/dl (n = 191). IVUS performed before coronary intervention.ResultsMean age 53.18 ± 12.29 years. No significant differences in hypertension, diabetes, and smoking between groups. Plaque burden was significantly higher among normal versus high non-HDL-C groups (79.59 ± 9.98 % vs. 81.61 ± 5.39 %; p = 0.001). At minimal luminal site, fibrofatty percentage was higher in normal non-HDL-C group (p = 0.027), while necrotic core greater in high non-HDL-C group (p = 0.033). Segmental analysis, necrotic core was significantly higher in percentage (p = 0.006) and volumes (p = 0.011) in normal versus high non-HDL-C groups. Total cholesterol (r = 0.099, p = 0.015), LDL-C (r = 0.081, p = 0.046), triglycerides (r = 0.083, p = 0.041),and non-HDL-C (r = 0.099, p = 0.015) positively correlated with plaque burden. Total cholesterol (r = 0.115, p = 0.005), LDL-C (r = 0.107, p = 0.009), and non-HDL-C (r = 0.105, p = 0.010) positively correlated with necrotic core volume. Linear regression analysis showed age and non-HDL-C as predictors of higher plaque burden. Multiple linear regression analysis; age, body mass index, and non-HDL-C were predictors of larger necrotic core volume.ConclusionNon-HDL-C levels were positively associated with plaque burden, measure of extent of atherosclerosis. It is closely associated with and is a predictor of necrotic core volume; a marker of plaque vulnerability. This IVUS study demonstrates potential role of non-HDL-C in causation of plaque in ACS.Keywords: Non-HDL cholesterol, Acute coronary syndrome, Plaque burden, Necrotic core, Intravascular ultrasound, Plaque vulnerability

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease represents a substantial portion of mortality and morbidity in developing countries. In India, coronary artery disease tends to manifest a decade earlier compared to Western countries, with acute coronary syndrome being the common presentation. The early onset of atherosclerosis is initiated by lipids, mostly contributed by low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Smooth muscle cell proliferation, extracellular matrix thinning, and inflammation potentiate atherosclerotic plaque formation. Lipid-laden macrophages are the chief components of the necrotic core situated within the plaque. Mean total cholesterol levels have remained relatively stable in most regions across the globe over the past few decades, except for East and Southeast Asian nations, wherein an increase of more than 0.1 mmol/L per decade was observed in either gender. The above change in the lipid profile pattern is attributed to increase in the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) levels.1 Epidemiological studies have established the superiority of non-HDL-C over LDL-C in predicting cardiovascular risk.2

Intravascular imaging with intravascular ultrasound assesses plaque burden, while virtual histology identifies plaque composition by analysing radiofrequency spectral data, offering excellent accuracy.3 The primary aim of this study was to ascertain the association between lipids and its components with coronary plaque characteristics and composition in culprit lesions presenting as acute coronary syndrome (ACS), utilizing intravascular ultrasound and virtual histology.

Materials and methodology

This prospective study included 601 consecutive patients with ACS between July 2017 and December 2021. The definitions of STEMI (ST elevation myocardial infarction), NSTEMI (non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction) and unstable angina were adopted from the standard guidelines.4,5 The patient's refusal of consent, cardiogenic shock, Killip class III-IV were excluded from the study. Patient demographics including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking, and dyslipidaemia, which are risk factors for coronary artery disease were recorded. Fasting blood samples were obtained from all participants after 8–10 h of overnight fasting and for patients undergoing primary percutaneous intervention (PCI) the fasting blood samples were obtained the following day of PCI. As per the guidelines elevated levels was considered when non-HDL >130 mg/dl.6 The calculation for non-HDL-C was derived by subtracting HDL-C values from total cholesterol.7 The Institutional Ethics Committee approval was acquired, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly followed.

Intravascular imaging procedure and analysis

All included patients received standard loading doses of antiplatelet therapy with aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel, or ticagrelor before coronary interventional procedures. Unfractionated heparin 70–100 U/kg was administered intravenously and adjusted to attain an activated clotting time in the therapeutic range during the intervention. All intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) pull backs were conducted using a 2.9 French size, 20-MHz, Eagle Eye® Platinum RX digital IVUS catheter (Eagle Eye, Philips Volcano, San Diego, CA, USA). IVUS pull-back was initiated before any balloon dilatation, at a length of 15 mm distal to the lesion until the aorto-ostial junction, at a speed 0.5 mm/s. The analysis was performed offline after recording the images on a DVD-ROM by two independent investigators (VK and RR) blinded to patient clinical details. Quantitative and qualitative IVUS analyses were performed according to the American College of Cardiology Clinical Expert Consensus Document on the Standards for Acquisition, Measurement, and Reporting of Intravascular Ultrasound Studies. The analysis was performed employing the Indec software (Echoplaque 4.3.12 J, Indec Medical Systems, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Cross-sectional images that appeared normal and were located within 10 mm on either side of the lesion were considered proximal and distal reference segments, with no major side branches adjacent to them. The lumen and media-adventitia interface were subjected to automatic border detection using a software to display the results. Plaque and media CSA were obtained by subtracting the lumen CSA from the EEM CSA. The plaque burden was calculated as the percentage of plaque and media CSA divided by the EEM CSA. The smallest lumen CSA denoting the image slice was considered the lesion, which also subtended the largest EEM and plaque plus media. The EEM CSA at lesion site divided by the average of the EEM CSA taken proximal and distal reference was considered remodelling index with positive >1.05 and negative <0.95 respectively. Volumetric analysis for both IVUS and virtual histology was performed along a 10 mm segment utilizing Simpson's method, with the minimal lumen area as the centre. Virtual histology analysis depicts colour-coded tissue in four components: green (fibrous), yellow-green (fibro-fatty), white (dense calcium), and red (necrotic core), and the analysis was represented in terms of absolute amounts and plaque area or volume in terms of percentages.

IVUS parameter assessment and reproducibility

A set of IVUS pullbacks was analysed twice by the same person with a gap of 3 months for intra-observer variability. The corresponding intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for repeated measurement was 0.90 (95 % confidence interval [CI] 0.79–0.95) for lumen measurements and 0.95 (95 % CI 0.89–0.97) for volumes. For the inter-observer variability, intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.93 (95 % CI 0.83–0.97) for lumen measurements and 0.94 (95 % CI 0.80–0.97) for volumes, thereby suggesting acceptable concordance.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the variables. Continuous variables are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as percentages. Categorical data were analysed using Pearson's chi-square test, and quantitative variables were analysed using Student's unpaired t-test. Spearman's rank correlation was used to determine the relationship between the lipid parameters and plaque features. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed using the forward method to identify predictors of plaque burden and necrotic core volumes to adjust for confounding variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analysis. The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 28.0) was used for all data analyses.

Results

Patients’ demographics and clinical features

This study included a total of 601 patients, and baseline clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Men were predominant across the groups. Regarding risk factors which included hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, and family history of premature coronary artery disease between the groups of the higher versus normal non-HDL cholesterol levels, no difference was observed. Among the higher non-HDL-C versus normal non-HDL-C groups, total cholesterol (p < 0.001), triglycerides (p < 0.001), and LDL (p < 0.001) levels were significantly higher. STEMI was the most frequent type of ACS presentation among the study groups with predominant involvement of the LAD as the culprit vessel (Table 1). Primary PCI accounted 17 % of patients.

Atherosclerotic plaque burden and composition

No differences were observed in lesion lumen diameter and lumen cross-sectional areas among the groups. However, the plaque burden at the minimal lumen site was significantly higher in the high non-HDL-C group than in the normal non-HDL-C group (p = 0.001) (Table 2). However, no difference was observed in plaque volumes between the two groups (p = 0.473). Likewise, no differences were found in vessel remodelling among the groups.

Relationship of lipid parameters with the lesion plaque burden and composition

The total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, and non-HDL-C levels were positively correlated with lesion plaque burden (Table 4). Triglycerides levels were positively correlated with plaque volume (p = 0.04). The total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and the non-HDL cholesterol demonstrated positive correlation with necrotic core percentage and the necrotic core volumes albeit weak correlation (Table 4). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that age and non-HDL-C levels, after adjustment, were predictors of plaque burden. Furthermore, age, body mass index (BMI), and non-HDL-C levels were predictors of necrotic core volumes (Table 5, Fig. 2).

Discussion

The salient findings of the current study are as follows. 1) High non-HDL-C levels were the key components affecting the culprit lesion plaque burden in ACS and showed a positive correlation with lesion plaque burden. 2) High non-HDL-C levels had a direct positive correlation with the necrotic core volume of atherosclerotic plaques. 3) Age and non-HDL-C levels were predictors of plaque burden; whereas age, non-HDL-C, and BMI were predictors of necrotic core volume, which is a measure of plaque vulnerability.

The initial step of atherosclerosis involves the binding of LDL to proteoglycans situated in the intima, which has the potential to lead to adaptive intimal thickening.2 Thereafter, LDL oxidation triggers an innate immune response, prompting endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells (SMCs) to express adhesion molecules. They further stimulate monocytes, leading to their migration and differentiation into macrophages and dendritic cells. These macrophages engulf the lipoproteins, transforming into foam cells.8,9 Foam cells and SMCs contribute to necrotic core development via apoptosis and secondary necrosis.10 Non-HDL-C composes the entire cholesterol mass which includes all atherogenic apo B lipoprotein particles. Very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) comprises approximately one-quarter of this cholesterol and the other three-quarters in the form of LDL with highly varied proportions.11 Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins causally influence every step of atherosclerosis and avidly cross the endothelial barrier. The achieved non-HDL-C levels appear to be more closely associated with coronary atheroma progression than with LDL-C levels.12

The necrotic core chemical composition has revealed direct accumulation of LDL and its accompanying cholesterol esters, cholesterol derived from erythrocyte membranes consequent to intraplaque haemorrhages and extracellular hydrolysis LDL situated in macrophages. This clearly emphasises the role of cholesterol in the atherosclerotic process. The fibrous cap overlying the plaque is mainly composed of proteoglycans, elastin, and collagen derived from SMCs.13,14

The predominant mechanisms underlying ACS are plaque rupture (55–65 %), plaque erosion (25 %), and calcified nodules (2–7%).15 Thinning of the fibrous cap leads to its rupture, exposing thrombogenic necrotic core contents to the bloodstream, causing arterial thrombosis.

In ACS, IVUS studies have shown larger plaque burdens and positive remodelling.16 Similar findings of a large plaque burden were observed along with positive remodelling in our patients with ACS. The plaque burden and plaque volumes indicate the extent of atherosclerosis. In their study, Young et al reported no difference in the studied grey-scale IVUS parameters between patients with hypercholesterolaemia and controls. However, it is important to note that 55.1 % of the involved participants had stable coronary artery disease.17 In the current study, patients with high non-HDL-C levels had significantly larger plaque burden in the culprit lesions in ACS. This suggests a prominent role of non-HDL-C in the accumulation of lipids in plaques and progression of atherosclerosis.

Plaque rupture is more prevalent along with larger necrotic core volumes in the culprit lesions of ACS.16,18, 19, 20 In a recent meta-analysis, the same was emphasised, where larger necrotic cores were associated with thin-cap fibroatheroma.21 In our study too in the culprit lesions with ACS, necrotic core percentage, and necrotic core volume were remarkably higher in the high non-HDL-C group than in the normal non-HDL-C group. The independent predictors of necrotic core volume in the culprit lesions of ACS were age and non-HDL-C. Therefore, these findings potentiate the pathological data, in which the necrotic core comprises foam cells and lipids. Age is reportedly a predictor of necrotic core volume in chronic coronary syndrome.22 Intravascular ultrasound studies have shown lesser amounts of calcium in the culprit lesions of ACS, which have been termed as “spotty calcifications”. Similarly, a pathological study demonstrated the presence of inflammation, calcification, and positive remodelling in ACS.23,24 Our study affirms the above findings by demonstrating the presence of dense calcium traits and positive remodelling in ACS with IVUS.

Multiple prospective epidemiological studies have demonstrated the superiority of both non-HDL-C and apo-B in comparison to LDL-C.11 The most probable explanation is that non-HDL-C is inclusive of all the cholesterol components especially VLDL and it also incorporates the LDL-particle number (LDL-P) which a better surrogate marker.11,25 Baseline non-HDL-C levels were a stronger predictor of cardiovascular disease mortality, whereas LDL-C levels were weak predictors irrespective of sex.2 Although LDL-C levels have been a standard target therapy for decades, recent evidence has emerged regarding the role of non-HDL-C in CHD management.26, 27, 28 In general, the treatment goals for non-HDL-C aim to be 30 mg/dl lower than LDL-C levels, although this remains unclear and could potentially be even lower.29,30

Conclusion

High non-HDL-C levels were associated with an increase in the plaque burden and had a direct positive correlation, suggesting its potential role in atherosclerotic plaque formation. Similarly, regarding plaque composition, elevated non-HDL-C levels showed a weak but positive correlation with necrotic core percentage and necrotic core volumes, undermining the role of non-HDL-C in plaque vulnerability leading to plaque rupture. Non-HDL-C levels were better predictors of lesion plaque burden; whereas non-HDL-C levels and age were good predictors of necrotic core volume (plaque composition). Therefore, management with guideline-directed medical therapy aimed at reducing the levels of non-HDL-C to normal or low is strongly suggested.

Limitations

This study is limited due to the fact that it was a single center study. A further limitation in the current study was the absence of assessment of inflammatory biomarkers. The inherent limitation of the VH-IVUS is its inability to differentiate thrombus and read as fibrous/fibro fatty traits.
