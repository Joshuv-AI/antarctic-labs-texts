Causal association between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy: A two‐sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yimeng Ruan, Ping Zhang, Xiangzhe Li, Xinru Jia, Dongwei Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo explore the causal relationship between cystatin C levels and different stages of diabetic retinopathy through Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsThe MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit provided the Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) data related to cystatin C (exposure). GWAS data for outcomes [DR, proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), severe non‐proliferative background diabetic retinopathy (SNPBDR)] were sourced from the FinnGen. Adopted Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW), MR‐Egger regression MR‐PRESSO, Weighted Median, Constrained Maximum Likelihood and Model Averaging (cML‐MA), Weighted model, Radial MR, and MR‐Lasso to estimate the causal relationship between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy. We conducted multivariable MR analysis to evaluate the independent causal effects of cystatin C levels on diabetic retinopathy.ResultsBased on the IVW method, we observed a causal relationship between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy [odds ratio (OR)random effect = 1.137, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.035–1.250]/PDR (ORrandom effect = 1.123, 95%CI: 1.004–1.255)/SNPBDR (ORfixed effect = 2.002, 95%CI: 1.343–2.986). Consistent findings were obtained through the cML‐MA method. Cochran's Q test suggested the presence of heterogeneity between the cystatin C level and instrumental variables in relation to diabetic retinopathy and proliferative diabetic retinopathy, respectively. After adjusting for outliers using MR‐PRESSO and Radial MR, it was observed that the statistical significance of the association between cystatin C level and diabetic retinopathy persists. Reverse MR analysis indicated that genetically related SNPBDR may influence the cystatin C level. In multivariable MR analysis, there were indications suggesting a causal relationship of cystatin C with the risk of DR/PDR/SNPBDR adjusting for confounders.ConclusionsThis study utilizes Mendelian randomization analyses to establish a causal relationship between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy, and reveals the impact of cystatin C on the risk of diabetic retinopathy, thus providing new evidence for clinical intervention of diabetic retinopathy.Keywords: Cystatin C, Diabetic retinopathy, Mendelian randomization

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a prevalent microvascular complication associated with diabetes, represents the majority of cases of blindness and visual impairment 1 . It is reported that there are over 100 million individuals with diabetic retinopathy globally; and it is anticipated that in the next few decades, there will be a significant increase in both the prevalence and disease burden of diabetic retinopathy worldwide, imposing a substantial strain on global health 2 , 3 . In addition, a study has demonstrated that diabetic patients with diabetic retinopathy exhibit a significantly elevated risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) and experiencing all‐cause mortality, which progressively escalates as the severity of diabetic retinopathy 4 . Therefore, the investigation of identifying risk factors for diabetic retinopathy is essential in order to provide guidance for clinical practice in the prevention and management of diabetic retinopathy.

Cystatin C, an endogenous inhibitor of cysteine proteases, serves as a biomarker for diabetic nephropathy and various complications associated with diabetes 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 . Limited observational studies have reported the association between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy. In comparison with diabetic patients without diabetic retinopathy, cystatin C was found to be elevated in diabetic patients with diabetic retinopathy, with a higher level observed in those with proliferative DR (PDR) compared with patients with non‐proliferative DR (NPDR) 10 . A cross‐sectional study indicated a robust correlation between increased serum cystatin C and sight‐threatening DR (STDR) among Asian Indians, indicating its potential as a promising biomarker for STDR screening 11 . Diabetic patients with high cystatin C levels are more likely to develop diabetic retinopathy 12 ; and cystatin C could potentially contribute to the risk of occurrence 7 . Cystatin C may be used as an important laboratory indicator to evaluate the risk of diabetic retinopathy among diabetic patients. However, these observational studies have not adequately adjusted for confounding factors, which may lead to the presence of spurious associations. Consequently, identifying the causal association between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy has important and practical implications for the prevention of diabetic retinopathy.

Mendelian randomization (MR) studies employ genetic variation as an instrumental variable to investigate the causal association between risk factors and outcomes, which are less affected by confounding factors than traditional retrospective studies 13 , 14 . Besides, MR can effectively address the issue of reverse causality inference, thereby capturing the enduring impact of exposure on outcomes 15 . Therefore, the objective of this research is to examine the causal association of cystatin C with diabetic retinopathy through MR analysis, in order to provide guidance for the development of screening and prevention strategies related to diabetic retinopathy.

METHODS

Study design

Figure 1 illustrates the bidirectional Mendelian randomization design, which is based on three assumptions 16 : (1) genetic variants showed an association with the exposure; (2) genetic variants were not linked to any confounder variables between exposure and outcome; (3) genetic variants only affect the outcome via the exposure.

Schematic representation of Mendelian randomization analysis.

Data sources

The MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (MRC‐IEU) (UKB) provided Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) data related to cystatin C (exposure), with a sample size of 389,834 17 . The GWAS data for DR, PDR, severe non‐proliferative background DR (SNPBDR) were sourced from the FinnGen database, with a sample size of 216,666, 212,889, and 204,663, respectively (https://www.finngen.fi/en/node/17). Summary‐level GWAS data for systolic blood pressure (SBP) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were obtained from the International Consortium of Blood Pressure and the Within family GWAS Consortium. The sample sizes for SBP and HbA1c were 757,601 and 45,734, respectively. Table S1 shows the detailed information regarding the phenotypes. The datasets utilized in our investigation are publicly available, and ethical approval from relevant institutional review boards has been obtained for each GWAS study included.

Selection of instrumental variables (IVs)

To generate the genetic instrumental variables, we detected nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that exhibited an association with exposure (cystatin C), employing a threshold criterion of P < 5 × 10−8. SNPs exhibiting linkage disequilibrium (LD) and being palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies were not considered for analysis (clumping: r 2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000). The utilization of the F statistic was necessary to confirm the strong association between IVs and exposure, and an F statistic exceeding 10 indicates a small likelihood of weak instrumental variable bias 18 .

Statistical analysis

We employed multiple methods, including Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW), MR‐Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR‐PRESSO), Weighted Median, MR‐Egger, Constrained Maximum Likelihood and Model Averaging (cML‐MA), Weighted mode, Radial MR, MR‐robust adjusted profile score (MR‐RAPS), and MR‐Lasso, to estimate the causal association of cystatin C with diabetic retinopathy. The IVW method is the primary approach for computing causal effects 19 . If heterogeneity is present, the findings should be interpreted by considering the results of an IVW random‐effects model; otherwise, the outcomes should be interpreted based on a fixed‐effects model. The findings from MR analyses are reported as P‐values, odds ratio (OR), and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochrane's Q value. Horizontal pleiotropy testing was conducted using MR‐Egger/MR‐PRESSO/Radial MR methods, where a P‐value greater than 0.05 suggests the absence of both heterogeneity and pleiotropy 20 . To evaluate the stability of the MR findings, a leave‐one‐out analysis was performed. Reverse MR was utilized to further validate the causal relationship between exposure and outcome. Furthermore, we also performed the multivariable MR analysis to evaluate the independent causal impacts of cystatin C on diabetic retinopathy outcomes by using multivariable IVW and MR‐Lasso methods. In this study, we used R 4.2.3 software to perform all statistical analyses, and package “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.5.7), “mr.raps” (version 0.2), “MRInstruments” (version 0.3.2), “MVMR” (version 0.4), and “MRPRESSO” (version 1.0) packages. was utilized to conduct MR analysis. P < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.

Univariable MR analysis

The results of the MR‐Egger method showed that horizontal pleiotropy was not present in all selected SNPs (P > 0.05). Cochran's Q test suggested the presence of heterogeneity between the cystatin C level and IVs in relation to DR and PDR, respectively (Table 1). Both MR‐PRESSO and Radial MR methods detected the outliers (rs9265121). Based on the IVW method (Tables 2 and S2), we found a causal relationship between cystatin C and DR (ORrandom effect = 1.137, 95%CI: 1.035–1.250, P = 0.008), cystatin C and PDR (ORrandom effect = 1.123, 95%CI: 1.004–1.255, P = 0.043), as well as cystatin C and SNPBDR (ORfixed effect = 2.002, 95%CI: 1.343–2.986, P = 0.001); namely, an elevated cystatin C level may be linked with an increased risk of DR, PDR, and SNPBDR. Consistent findings were obtained through the cML‐MA method (Figure 2). The scatter plot (Figure 3) and leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis (Figure 4) also further present these causal estimates. In addition, after adjusting for outliers (Table S2), the results indicated that there remains a statistically significant causal association between cystatin C level and diabetic retinopathy (P < 0.05). There was no heterogeneity between the cystatin C levels associated with SNPBDR, so we did not need to calculate the causality that adjusts for outliers. Meanwhile, we also used the radial plot to find outliers, and there was one outlier in the MR analysis between the cystatin C level and diabetic retinopathy (Figure S1). After the removal of outliers, the causal relationship between the cystatin C level and diabetic retinopathy was robust. However, no statistically significant correlation was observed between the cystatin C level and proliferative diabetic retinopathy.

Then, we performed a reverse MR analysis, considering DR/PDR/SNPBDR as the exposure and cystatin C as the outcome. The result indicated that genetically related SNPBDR (IVW: OR = −0.018, 95% CI: −0.031, −0.004, P = 0.009) may influence the cystatin C level (Table S3). There was heterogeneity between SNPBDR and IVs in relation to the cystatin C level, respectively (Table S4). However, due to the limited number of SNPs, we were unable to identify any outliers, thereby precluding the calculation of results following outlier correction (Table S3). Scatter plots suggested the consistent direction of the multiple methods (Figure S2). Table S3 also shows the result after adjusting for outliers in the reverse MR analysis.

Multivariable MR analysis

In the multivariable Mendelian randomization analysis, when adjusting for SBP and HbA1c individually, there was observed evidence suggesting a potential causal relationship between cystatin C and the risk of diabetic retinopathy (adjusting for SBP: IVW: OR = 1.152, 95%CI = 1.041–1.274, P = 0.006; adjusting for HbA1c: IVW: OR = 1.183, 95%CI = 1.063–1.316, P = 0.002; Table 3 and Figure 5). Adjusting for these confounders jointly, cystatin C was still related to an increased risk of diabetic retinopathy (IVW: OR = 1.173, 95%CI = 1.058–1.301). Likewise, the causal relationships of cystatin C and PDR (IVW: OR = 1.228, 95%CI = 1.089–1.385, P = 0.001), as well as cystatin C and SNPBDR (IVW: OR = 2.130, 95%CI = 1.350–3.360, P = 0.001) were found in multivariable MR analysis for adjusting SBP and HbA1c.

DISCUSSION

The present study employed Mendelian randomization analysis to investigate the causal link between cystatin C and the different stages of diabetic retinopathy. The results by several analyses, namely IVW, cML‐MA, MR‐PRESSO, and Radial MR methods, showed that there may be a causal relationship of cystatin C with DR/PDR/SNPBDR. After adjusting for SBP and HbA1c, the causal relationships between cystatin C and DR/PDR/SNPBDR were still robust. Our findings fill the knowledge gap regarding the contribution of cystatin C to the development of diabetic retinopathy at different stages, and also provide valuable insights for preventing and managing the development.

Cystatin C is a non‐glycosylated protein with low molecular weight, which belongs to the cystatin superfamily of inhibitors for cysteine proteases and plays a vital role in regulating the activity of extracellular protease 21 . Prior observational studies have explored the potential link between cystatin C and the risk of diabetic retinopathy 11 , 22 , 23 . Elevated serum cystatin C was found to exhibit an association with diabetic retinopathy risk and holds potential as a promising biomarker for diabetic retinopathy screening 11 . Due to the presence of confounding variables and the possibility of reverse causation, traditional observational studies may lack certain reliability 24 . An MR analysis can effectively reduce potential confounding biases that might exist in previous observational epidemiological studies, and has been used widely in clinical research 25 , 26 . Our research utilized MR analysis to examine the causal relationship of cystatin C with different stages of diabetic retinopathy from a genetic perspective. Using the IVW method, we found a causal relationship of cystatin C with DR/PDR/SNPBDR. However, it is important to exercise caution when interpreting the causal relationship between cystatin C levels and diabetic retinopathy and proliferative diabetic retinopathy due to the heterogeneity of IVs involved in the association. The heterogeneity of IVs stems from both statistical and biological factors. Statistical factors may encompass non‐linearity, a departure from the assumption of homogeneity, and different control groups under the monotonicity hypothesis. Furthermore, even if the instantaneous correlation during measurement remains constant, distinct genetic variants may be linked to diverse trajectories of exposure over time. Biological factors may encompass exposure that consist of multiple components with distinct causal effects. These exposures can intervene in various ways, with each intervention yielding distinct impacts on outcomes 27 . In this study, we considered the results of the IVW random‐effects model due to the heterogeneity of IVs. In addition, as shown in Tables S2 and S3, MR analysis was repeated after excluding SNPs that could potentially introduce heterogeneity. The results indicated that there remains a statistically significant causal association between cystatin C level and diabetic retinopathy. Multivariable MR analysis supported the causal association of cystatin C with SNPBDR, however, it should be noted that there is insufficient evidence to support an inverse causal relationship between cystatin C and SNPBDR.

Currently, the potential mechanism of cystatin C and the risk of diabetic retinopathy development is unclear, but it may involve the following aspects. More and more evidence shows shared pathogenic pathways between retinopathy and cystatin C; chronic inflammation plays a leading role in the development of diabetic retinopathy. Cystatin C is also a marker of inflammation 28 . Cystatin C may have the potential to increase the release of inflammatory factors (C‐reactive protein), leading to leukocyte adhesion and aggregation, ultimately causing a blockage of retinal capillaries and subsequent local ischemia 10 , 29 . In addition, diabetic retinopathy was significantly associated with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) 30 . As a regulator of extracellular activity of cysteine protease, cystatin C was found to exhibit a potential correlation with VEGF levels 31 . This suggests that the expression of VEGF may be one of the common genetic bases between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy 10 . Further investigation is required to validate these results and to investigate the possible underlying mechanisms.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents an initial attempt in utilizing MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship of cystatin C with the risk of different stages of diabetic retinopathy. However, some limitations should be noted in this study. First, the genetic data utilized in our analyses were derived from European populations, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to diverse ethnic groups. Second, the absence of individual study data hinders further exploration into the non‐linear association between cystatin C and diabetic retinopathy. Third, in the reverse MR analysis, the number of IVs exposed by SNPBDR is small, so the influence of weak instrumental variable bias cannot be excluded. In addition, due to the lack of individual information, age stratification cannot be performed. Lastly, despite employing results from the IVW random‐effects model and conducting MR analyses that excluded SNPs with heterogeneity, we acknowledge the potential bias arising from heterogeneity in the IVs.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study utilizes Mendelian randomization analyses to establish a causal relationship of cystatin C with the various stages of diabetic retinopathy risk, and reveals the impact of cystatin C on diabetic retinopathy, thus providing new evidence for clinical intervention of diabetic retinopathy.
