Microbiological profiles and clinical outcomes of critically ill surgical patients with lower gastrointestinal perforation in Japan: a single-center retrospective observational study
Authors: Takashi Nishikawa, Jiro Ito, Ryutaro Seo, Koichi Ariyoshi, Hiroyuki Mima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLower gastrointestinal perforation (LGP) is a surgical emergency disease that can result in secondary bacterial peritonitis. Microbiological studies on LGP are rare. The present study aimed to ascertain the microbiological profile of LGP in patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) at our institute after surgery. In addition, we investigated whether initial empirical therapy with vancomycin was associated with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted at Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital, Japan. The study population included all patients diagnosed with LGP who were admitted to the ICU after emergency surgery between 2017 and 2023. The primary outcome assessed was the microbiological profile of microorganisms isolated from ascites fluid and blood of the participants. The secondary end-points were in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support. We performed univariate and multivariate regression analyses to evaluate the end-points.ResultsDuring the study period, 89 patients were included in the analysis. The most commonly identified pathogen from the ascites cultures was Escherichia coli (65.2%), followed by Enterococcus spp. (51.7%). E. faecium was identified in 16 (18.0%) ascites samples. The microbiological profile of critically ill patients with LGP admitted to the ICU after surgery was similar to that of previous studies on intra-abdominal infection (IAI). Therefore, the initial empirical therapy in the IAI guidelines is more appropriate for LGP. Multivariate regression analysis suggested that the combination of initial empirical therapy with vancomycin was not associated with in-hospital mortality (odds ratio [OR] = 0.96, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.23–3.00, p = 0.955), duration of ICU stay (coefficient=-0.92, 95% CI -3.04–1.21, p = 0.393), or duration of ventilator-support (coefficient=-9.03, 95%CI -49.69–31.63, p = 0.659).ConclusionThe microbiological profile of critically ill patients with LGP admitted to the ICU after surgery was similar to that of previous studies on IAI. However, the frequency of E. faecium in the present study was higher than that in previous studies. Initial empirical therapy with drugs such as meropenem in combination with vancomycin for E. faecium was not associated with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support, after adjusting for confounding factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-024-02716-1.

Background

Gastrointestinal perforation is a surgical emergency that can lead to an acute abdomen. Lower gastrointestinal perforation (LGP) involves the small intestine, colon, and/or rectum and is a serious disease that can result in secondary bacterial peritonitis, which can leak into the abdominal cavity and culminate in sepsis [1, 2]. The therapeutic strategy for this condition entails surgical intervention at the perforation site and the administration of antimicrobial agents [2, 3].

Microbiological studies on intra-abdominal infection (IAI), including LGP, have been conducted to ensure the appropriate use of antimicrobial agents [4–6]. However, most microbiological studies on IAI included non-perforated conditions such as cholecystitis and abdominal abscess of the liver and pancreas, as well as gastrointestinal perforation of the stomach, duodenum, and lower gastrointestinal tract. Only a few studies have examined the microorganisms identified in LGP. LGP can result in secondary peritonitis, which is the most common cause of IAI [7, 8]. Most patients with LGP and secondary peritonitis develop sepsis, necessitating admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), and the reported mortality rate is 20–40% [9, 10].

Microbiological examination of LGP is vital for the appropriate administration of antibiotic agents and improvement of clinical outcomes. Several guidelines and reviews for IAI recommend empirical therapy such as piperacillin/tazobactam or carbapenem [3, 11–13]. Although some guidelines suggest that vancomycin should be considered for the treatment of IAI, including LGP [3, 11], this recommendation is controversial since only a few studies have conducted microbiological assessment of LGP and the association between patient outcomes and the combination of vancomycin with broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents as the initial treatment strategy.

Therefore, the present study aimed to ascertain the microbiological profile of LGP in patients admitted to the ICU at our institute after surgery. Furthermore, we investigated whether initial empirical therapy with vancomycin was associated with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support.

Methods

Study design and definition

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted at Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital, Japan. The study population included all patients diagnosed with LGP who were admitted to the ICU after emergency surgery between April 2017 and March 2023. The diagnosis of LGP was confirmed by surgical records. The choice of the procedure, viz. Hartmann’s operation or anastomosis, was based on the surgeon’s discretion during surgery. We excluded patients who did not undergo ascites fluid collection or blood culture tests during or before surgery.

Data collection

Patient data were extracted from the electronic medical charts. The extracted data included demographic information (age, sex, and body mass index), setting of onset, duration between diagnosis and surgery, microbiological data from ascites fluid and blood, antibiotic treatment, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE-II) score, and clinical outcomes such as in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support.

Microbiological assessment

The intra-abdominal samples for ascites cultures were obtained during surgery and stored in sterile packages by the surgeons. Blood cultures were obtained from patients with LGP preoperatively, and both aerobic and anaerobic bottles were filled with 10–20 mL of the specimen. Microbiological profiles were assessed in our institution’s laboratory.

Antibiotic regimen at the initial laparotomy

Intensivists determined the antibiotic regimen after emergency surgery. The empirical antibiotic regimen comprised broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as piperacillin/tazobactam and meropenem. Moreover, the intensivists determined if vancomycin should be administered for E. faecium and methicillin-resistant Streptococcus aureus (MRSA).

Clinical assessment and endpoint

The primary outcome measure was the microbiological profile of microorganisms isolated from ascites fluid and blood. The secondary endpoints were in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support. The duration of ventilator support was assessed in patients placed on ventilation upon admission to the ICU.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as the numbers of patients and percentages. We investigated whether LGP occurred in community-onset or hospital-onset cases. In addition, we classified the participants into two groups, viz. the vancomycin-free and concomitant-vancomycin-use groups, according to whether vancomycin was administered during the initial treatment. Cases of additional use of vancomycin were included in the vancomycin-free group.

We assessed the relationship between the patients’ background characteristics and clinical outcomes in the groups. The Mann–Whitney U-test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Additionally, culture-positive cases of E. faecium and MRSA were further examined to determine whether concomitant vancomycin administration was associated with the outcome. In the vancomycin-free group, the use of vancomycin and culture positivity for E. faecium and MRSA was investigated.

Moreover, we designated age, sex, and the SOFA and APACHE II scores as confounding factors [14, 15] and controlled for them in the multivariate regression analysis to determine the association between concomitant vancomycin use and clinical outcomes. All p-values were two-sided, and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The results of linear regression are presented as coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI), while those of logistic regression are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs.

All statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Jichi Medical University Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). It is a modified version of R commander (version 1.6-3), designed to include statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics [16].

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Ethics Committee of Kobe City Medical Center (approval number: zn231108, approved on November 14, 2023). All procedures in this study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Obtaining informed consent from all study participants was challenging because this observational retrospective cohort study was conducted using medical records. The need for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Kobe City Medical Center. We disclosed the contents of the study on our hospital’s website as an opportunity for participants to withdraw their consent.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

During the study period, 108 patients with LGP were admitted to the ICU after surgery. Nineteen patients were excluded because their samples were not obtained for culture, and 89 patients were included in the final analysis. Table 1 shows the characteristics, treatment details, and outcomes of all patients and the community- and hospital-onset groups. The study population comprised 43 women (48.3%), and the median age was 73 years. The median APACHE-II and SOFA scores of the entire patient population were 19 and 7 points, respectively. The preoperative antibiotic agents were administrated to 82 patients (92.1%). The median duration between diagnosis and surgery was 2.7 h. The mortality rate was 15.7%. The median duration of ICU stay and duration of ventilator support were 2.5 days and 18.2 h, respectively. There were 66 patients (74.2%) in the community-onset group and 23 patients (35.8%) in the hospital-onset group. The characteristics, except duration between diagnosis and surgery, the perforation sites, surgical procedure, and use of micafungin, and the outcomes, such as in-hospital mortality, did not differ significantly between the groups.

Table 2 shows the characteristics, treatment details, and outcomes between the monotherapy and combination (with vancomycin) therapy groups. The APACHE-II score (median values; 17.0 vs. 20.5, p = 0.002), SOFA score (5.0 vs. 7.0, p < 0.001), duration of ICU stay (1.8 vs. 2.8, p = 0.003) and duration of ventilator support (7.6 vs. 29.1, p = 0.009) differed significantly between the vancomycin-free and concomitant-vancomycin-use groups, while no such differences were observed in the mortality rate (10.3% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.252) and other characteristics. All patients received empirical antibiotic therapy, including meropenem or piperacillin/tazobactam, based on previously established clinical guidelines and reviews. Meropenem was used significantly more frequently in combination with vancomycin (51.3% vs. 94.0%, p < 0.001), while piperacillin/tazobactam was combined less frequently with vancomycin (43.6% vs. 6.0%, p < 0.001).

Tables 3 and 4 show the differences in microbiological findings according to the setting of onset and concomitant vancomycin use, respectively. Single or multiple pathogens were isolated from 85 ascites cultures (95.5%). Growth in blood culture was noted in 34 samples (38.2%). Among the cases with ascites culture-positive, the most commonly identified pathogen from the ascites cultures was Eschericia coli (65.2%), followed by Enterococcus spp. (54.1%), and Bacteroides spp. (47.2%). E. coli was the only extended spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacterium, which was isolated from 8 ascites samples and 1 blood sample. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was identified in 13 ascites and 2 blood samples. E. faecium was identified in 16 (18.0%) ascites samples and 2 (2.2%) blood samples. MRSA was isolated from 2 the ascites cultures of two patients but not from the blood culture. We compared the microbiological findings between the community- and hospital-onset groups, and the vancomycin-free and concomitant vancomycin-use groups. The frequency of bacterial isolation was not associated with the setting of onset and concomitant vancomycin use.

The microbiological profiles were stratified according to the site of LGP (Additional file 1). No case of transverse colon perforation was included. With the exception for the bacillus spp, no significant differences were observed in the microbiological profiles of the perforation sites. Moreover, mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support were not significantly associated with the perforation sites.

We extracted cases with culture positivity for E. faecium from the entire study population and evaluated the association between concomitant vancomycin administration and mortality using univariate analysis; however, we did not perform the same analysis for MRSA because of the limited sample size. All Enterococcus spp-positive cases including E. faecium were susceptible to vancomycin. Consequently, concomitant vancomycin use was not associated with in-hospital mortality (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.06–12.90, p = 0.931) (Additional file 2). In the vancomycin-free group, E. faecium and MRSA were detected in 5 and 1 cases, respectively. Two of these cases were administered vancomycin.

Finally, we performed multivariate analysis for the entire cohort and the hospital-onset group to evaluate the association of concomitant vancomycin use as empirical therapy with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support. Before analysis, we evaluated the association between the background and in-hospital morality (Additional file 3). There were significant differences in age (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.14, p = 0.020), sex (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–0.93, p = 0.038), the SOFA (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.13–1.82, p = 0.003) and APACHE II (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01–1.20, p = 0.028) scores, but not in duration between diagnosis and surgery (OR 2.19, 95% CI 0.63–7.60, p = 0.218). However, we designated the SOFA and APACHE II scores as confounding factors owing to the limited sample size. Table 5 shows the ORs and 95% CI obtained from the multivariate logistic regression model. Concomitant vancomycin therapy was not associated with in-hospital mortality. Table 6 presents the results of the multivariate linear regression analysis. The durations of ICU stay and ventilator support were not associated with concomitant vancomycin therapy.

Discussion

This observational study conducted a microbiological assessment in critically ill patients with LGP and found that the most commonly identified organisms were E. coli, Enterococcus spp., and anaerobic bacteria, whereas E. faecium was isolated in 18% of patients and ESBL-producing bacteria and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were isolated in approximately 10% and 15% of patients, respectively. Multivariate regression analysis suggested that the combination of initial empirical therapy with vancomycin was not associated with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support, regardless of setting of onset, after adjusting for the patients’ background characteristics.

First, the present study assessed the microbiological profile of patients with LGP and found that the most common pathogen was E. coli, followed by Enterococcus spp. and anaerobic bacteria. This result is similar to those of previous studies [4–6]. These microbiological characteristics did not differ with respect to the perforation site. Moreover, the frequency of isolated specimens, such as ESBL-producing bacteria, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and MRSA in the current study was 16%, 8%, and 2% of cases, respectively, which was approximately similar to that reported in previous studies [4–6]. This finding is important because the microbiological assessment was conducted in cases of LGP rather than IAI. Previous studies investigating the microbiological profile have also included cases of non-perforated disease, such as cholecystitis, diverticulitis, and enteritis, in addition to LGP. However, LGP resulting in secondary peritonitis accounts for 60% of IAI cases, and the mortality rate of patients with severe sepsis is as high as 40% [17–19], which underscores the clinical value of elucidating the microbiological profile of LGP.

The results of the present study suggest that the choice of antimicrobial therapy should be based on previous IAI guidelines and reviews. In other words, if the perforation is in the gastrointestinal tract, irrespective of the site of perforation, initial treatment with meropenem or tazobactam/piperacillin (which were used in this study) that have anticipated activity against the most commonly identified isolates seems feasible. Moreover, as noted in the guidelines, aggressive MRSA coverage is not necessary, and it is acceptable to consider coverage based on risk [3, 11]. In contrast, the treatment strategy for Enterococcus spp. may require consideration. Enterococcus spp. is abundant in the lower gastrointestinal tract [20] and had the second highest frequency of occurrence in our study. However, the unclear association between Enterococcus spp. and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients is a matter of consequence [21–23]. Ampicillin and piperacillin-tazobactam are broad-spectrum antibiotics that are effective against most Enterococcus spp., and the problem arises when E. faecium, which is resistant to ampicillin, is detected.

Prior studies have reported that E. faecium is identified in approximately 10% of ascites cultures in IAI, including nosocomial cases [6, 24]. In fact, this study suggested that the frequency of E. faecium isolation was 18%, which was higher than that reported in previous studies. Therefore, we hypothesized that the concomitant use of vancomycin might contribute to the improvement of the patients’ outcomes because E. faecium was more common in ascites cultures in cases of LGP than in those of IAI, and investigated the effect of combining piperacillin/tazobactam or meropenem with vancomycin as initial antibacterial therapy. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses revealed that the inclusion of vancomycin in combination with initial therapy was not associated with clinical outcomes, such as in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay, and duration of ventilator support, whether community- or hospital-onset. E. faecium infection is a problem in high-risk patients, such as those in immunosuppressive states and septic shock [24]. Although the mortality rate of E. faecium bacteremia is reported to be 10–30% [25–27], one of the reasons for this result is probably that no patient in this study developed E. faecium bacteremia. It is unclear whether an additional dose of vancomycin would affect patient outcomes in the presence of culture-positive E. faecium or gram-positive cocci, owing to the limited number of cases. Although several studies have evaluated the impact of E. faecium on patient outcomes, it is controversial whether initial treatment for IAI, including LGP due to E. faecium, is mandatory [28, 29]. It is important to accumulate more evidence by conducting large-scale studies at multiple centers to bolster research on the treatment of secondary peritonitis and septic shock due to LGP. We hope that this study contributes to the judicious use of vancomycin and forms the basis for further investigation.

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, it incorporated a single-center retrospective observational design, and the study population was limited to patients admitted to the ICU. In addition, almost 20% of patients were excluded because of the unavailability of the culture results. Thus, because the sample size was small, the external validity of this study may not be sufficiently high. Second, we investigated the addition of vancomycin to the initial antibiotic monotherapy and the effect of vancomycin itself in cases where LGP was not evaluated. In other words, the present study did not adequately investigate the association between vancomycin use and outcomes. Finally, we did not consider the risk factors for bacterial resistance, such as history of antibiotic administration within the past 100 days, dialysis, history of hospitalization, and detection of resistant bacteria. The increased risk of bacterial resistance may influence vancomycin use and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

We investigated the microbiological profiles of critically ill patients with LGP who were admitted to the ICU after surgery. The result was similar to that of previous studies on IAI. Therefore, initial empirical therapy with antibiotics that act against these organisms is more feasible for LGP, as stated in the IAI guidelines. The frequency of E. faecium was higher than that observed in previous studies. However, multivariate regression analysis revealed that initial empirical therapy with drugs such as meropenem in combination with vancomycin for E. faecium was not associated with in-hospital mortality, duration of ICU stay and duration of ventilator support, after adjusting for confounding factors.
