Valence-tuned electron bridge enables high-yield multi-electron HMF oxidation over spinel catalysts
Authors: Zhong-Ting Hu, Gan He, Xiaohuan Tao, Jinshu Tian, Mingwu Tan, Ni Ouyang, Jun-Long Li, Mian Hu, Jie-Xin Wang, Yihan Zhu, Dapeng Cao, Zhiyan Pan, Yong Wang, Xiaonian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The catalytic oxidation of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid is a key step in the production of bio-based plastics but remains limited by sluggish multi-electron transfer kinetics across multiple reaction intermediates. In this study, we address this long-standing challenge by introducing a Mn-O-Co electron bridge within spinel CoMn2O4 to mediate and accelerate electron transfer. Through precise valence state regulation, we engineer a heterogeneous electron bridge dominated by Mn4+-O2--Co3+ linkages, enabling more efficient electron flow. Experimental characterization and theoretical calculations reveal that the incorporation of Mn4+ significantly enhances electron delocalization across the bridge. The empty eg orbitals of Mn4+ (t2g3eg0) serve as efficient electron acceptors, creating an energy-level gradient with Co3+ (t2g4eg2) that favors directional electron transfer. Simultaneously, Mn4+ strengthens metal-oxygen covalency, further improving electron mobility. This engineered electron bridge structure enables highly efficient cooperation across the full six-electron transfer pathway in 5-hydroxymethylfurfural oxidation, driven by a dynamic electron compensation mechanism. As a result, an 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid yield of 98.1% is achieved. This work offers a valuable theoretical foundation for understanding cooperative electron transfer in heterogeneous catalysis and provides a rational strategy for designing efficient electron bridge structures.

Introduction

Biomass is poised to become a key pillar in the global energy transition, potentially supplanting petroleum1–3. As a vital bio-based platform compound, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) can be catalytically oxidized to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA), a crucial precursor for producing bio-based polyethylene furanoate (PEF)4–6. However, conventional HMF oxidation processes predominantly rely on noble metal catalysts (e.g., Pt, Pd, Au, Ru), whose high cost, scarcity, and susceptibility to deactivation limit their industrial viability7–11. Consequently, developing non-noble metal catalysts is a major research priority12,13.

The oxidation of HMF to FDCA involves a complex multi-electron transfer process (6e-), forming and converting sequential intermediates14,15. Sluggish electron transfer kinetics can lead to incomplete HMF oxidation, accumulation of undesirable intermediates, and poor catalytic selectivity. Thus, enhancing electron transfer kinetics is pivotal for optimizing HMF oxidation performance. Spinel-type materials, with their inherent redox versatility, demonstrate exceptional capability in dynamically mediating electron transfer to facilitate selective HMF oxidation towards FDCA16. As a representative mixed-valence spinel, cobalt-manganese spinel (CoxMn3-xO4) allows precise tuning of Co and Mn cation distribution across tetrahedral and octahedral sites17,18. Strategic approaches like Co/Mn ratio optimization, defect engineering, and support modification have successfully boosted its catalytic performance for selective HMF oxidation19–21. Despite this promising activity, the fundamental interaction mechanism between Co and Mn ions within the catalyst structure and their synergistic catalytic enhancement beyond single-metal oxides remains incompletely understood. Thorough investigation is needed to enable more precise CoxMn3-xO4 modification and provide deeper insights into its catalytic behavior.

In spinel structures, octahedral sites (Oh) typically serve as the active centers for redox reactions due to their stable coordination, substantial crystal field splitting energy, and significant structural adaptability22–25. While extensive research has focused on the intrinsic redox properties of transition metal ions at these sites, isolated analysis is inadequate for complex spinel like CoxMn3-xO4. Multivalent Co and Mn ions readily undergo disordering substitution, co-occupying octahedral sites26. This necessitates comprehensive consideration of inter-site synergistic effects. Critically, adjacent octahedra are interconnected via metal-oxygen-metal (M1-O-M2) bridges, forming electron bridge structures whose performance is governed by both geometric and electronic configurations. For instance, the Jahn-Teller effect induced by Mn3+ causes octahedral lattice distortion and asymmetric Mn-O bond lengths, altering the local coordination environment25,27. Concurrently, variations in the electronic structure of Co/Mn/O ions modulate the redox properties of catalytic centers28,29. Therefore, the electron bridge must be investigated as the fundamental electron transfer unit, with correlated geometric/electronic structure evolution. This approach provides new insights into multi-electron cooperative transfer mechanisms and is pivotal for establishing structure-activity relationships.

In this work, we successfully constructed an electron bridge structure dominated by Mn4+-O2--Co3+ (denoted C-CoMn2) through precise regulation of Mn valence states within the octahedral sites of CoMn2O4 spinel, as shown in Fig. 1. Distinct from the conventional Mn3+-O2--Co3+ configuration, incorporating Mn⁴⁺ effectively suppresses Jahn-Teller distortion. Moreover, the completely vacant eg orbitals of Mn⁴⁺ act as efficient electron acceptors. This, combined with Co3+ via bridging oxygen (O2-), forms a highly asymmetric electron transfer channel that significantly enhances multi-electron transfer efficiency by 1.6 times compared to conventional CoMn2O4 spinel during HMF oxidation. Results

Crystallographic identification

As shown in Fig. 2a, based on the differences in coordination capability and precipitation behavior of transition metals in excess ammonia solution, Mn2+ preferentially combines with OH- to form Mn(OH)2 precipitate while Co2+ tends to form stable [Co(NH3)6]2+ complexes with NH₃. Upon exposure to air, Mn(OH)2 and [Co(NH3)6]2+ can be further oxidized to MnOOH and [Co(NH3)6]3+ respectively. The excessive formation of MnOOH represents a critical factor inducing lattice distortion in CoMn2O4, since the immobilized Mn³⁺ in MnOOH lattice exhibits limited accessibility to oxidizing media, resulting in constrained oxidation kinetics that leads to Mn3+ accumulation and subsequent Jahn-Teller distortion. To suppress MnOOH formation, the strategy of preferentially generating stable [Co(NH3)6]3+ complexes was employed to enhance the oxidative capacity of the synthesis system, thereby effectively blocking the transformation of Mn(OH)2 to MnOOH. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the precursors (Fig. 2b) reveal complete disappearance of the characteristic MnOOH diffraction peak at 19.2° for C-CoMn2, accompanied by emergence of a new Mn7O13 phase at 12.3°, indicating extensive oxidation of Mn3+ to Mn4+. This valence state alteration directly influenced subsequent crystallization processes, with final product XRD analysis (Fig. 2c) demonstrating disappearance of characteristic peaks at 2θ = 29.4° and 32.9° for C-CoMn2, attributable to diffraction peak degeneracy caused by equivalency of (202)/(220) and (113)/(311) crystal planes, suggesting enhanced crystal symmetry. Comparative analysis with standard reference revealed that C-CoMn2 crystallizes in a cubic spinel structure (JCPDS#32-0297; Fd-3m), whereas T-CoMn2 exhibits a tetragonal phase (JCPDS#18-0408; I41/amd)30,31. Among them, C-CoMn2 exhibits diffraction peak positions closer to those of the standard MnCo2O4.5 reference material (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating a significantly higher oxygen coordination number compared to CoMn2O4. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analysis further confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 2) that the observed lattice spacings of 0.245 nm and 0.289 nm in C-CoMn2 correspond to the (311) and (220) planes of cubic phase structure, while the measured spacings of 0.306 nm and 0.267 nm in T-CoMn2 are assigned to the (202) and (113) planes of tetragonal phase. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 3) corroborated these findings. Atomic-scale arrangement was examined by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) (Fig. 2d). After Wiener filtering for noise reduction (Supplementary Figs. 4-5), the atomic-resolution simulation of C-CoMn2 (Fig. 2e) exhibits perfect face-centered cubic symmetry, fully consistent with the theoretical structural model (Fig. 2f). In this configuration, Co and Mn atoms randomly occupy both tetrahedral (8a) and octahedral (16 d) interstices of the spinel lattice, forming a uniformly distributed three-dimensional atomic network. Combined scanning transmission electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6) preliminarily identified high-intensity regions corresponding to octahedral sites, while low-intensity regions likely represent tetrahedral sites32. In contrast, the tetragonal phase sample (Fig. 2g–i) displays distinct lattice distortion. These results conclusively demonstrate that regulating Mn valence state distribution (increased Mn4+ content) effectively suppresses Jahn-Teller distortion, thereby stabilizing the cubic phase structure. In addition, the crystal structure modification was found to have no significant impact on key material properties such as morphology (Supplementary Fig. 7), specific surface area (Supplementary Fig. 8), and elemental distribution (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating that these variables are not the primary factors responsible for the subsequent changes in catalytic performance.

Electronic structure analysis

Building upon the afore mentioned structural characterization results, we systematically investigated the electronic structure evolution of the materials through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analyses. The C 1 s correction position is at 284.6 eV (Supplementary Fig. 10). Detailed examination of the Co 2p fine spectra (Supplementary Fig. 11) revealed characteristic peaks at 780.2 eV (2p3/2) and 795.3 eV (2p1/2) for C-CoMn2, with peak deconvolution confirming the coexistence of Co2+ (779.8 eV) and Co3+ (781.2 eV) species showing comparable relative abundance ratios to T-CoMn2 (Supplementary Table 1)33,34. In contrast, significant differences emerged in the Mn 2p fine spectra (Supplementary Fig. 12), where the integrated area of Mn4+ characteristic peak (642.1 eV) substantially increased from 30.6% to 67.2% in C-CoMn2 compared to T-CoMn2. This trend was further corroborated by changes in the Mn 3 s exchange splitting energy (Fig. 3a), where the ΔE value decreased from 5.05 eV to 4.55 eV, corresponding to calculated average oxidation states of +3.83 and +3.27 for C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2, respectively. The O 1 s peak analysis reveals that Mn4+ doping has negligible influence on the oxygen vacancy concentration (Supplementary Fig. 13). XANES analysis (Fig. 3b, c) revealed a distinct shift of the Mn K-edge absorption edge toward higher energy for C-CoMn2, indicating an increase in the oxidation state of Mn, with quantitative pre-edge analysis and linear combination fitting determining an average Mn oxidation state close to +3.9, significantly higher than that of T-CoMn2 (+3.4) (Supplementary Fig. 14), while the Co valence state remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 15). Bond analysis

Raman spectroscopy analysis further revealed characteristic changes in the local structure of the materials (Fig. 3d), showing a distinct blue shift of the A1g vibrational mode at 650 cm−1 corresponding to the symmetric stretching vibrations of Mn-O and Co-O bonds in octahedral sites, accompanied by significantly enhanced intensity of the Eg mode at 450 cm−1 (bending vibrations of metal-oxygen bonds in octahedral coordination)35,36. These observations indicate strengthened Mn-O bonding and markedly improved covalency of metal-oxygen bonds in C-CoMn2, which originates from the contraction of Mn4+ 3 d orbitals leading to increased orbital overlap with O2-. Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis provided further insights into the structural changes (Fig. 3e, f). Bond length analysis (Supplementary Figs. 16–18 and Supplementary Table 2-3) revealed a 0.02 Å shortening of the Mn-O bond in C-CoMn2 compared to T-CoMn2, confirming that covalency of the Mn-O bonds was enhanced. Characteristic peaks observed in the 2.0–2.8 Å range correspond to octahedral Cooh-O-(Mn/Co)oh and Mnoh-O-(Mn/Co)oh bridging structures, confirming the existence of Co-O-Mn electron bridges in octahedral sites. It should be noted that the Mn R-space spectrum shows a detectable feature in the 3.0–3.5 Å range, which corresponds to Mn occupying tetrahedral sites. This indicates that a portion of Mn resides in tetrahedral positions. In spinel catalysts, however, the octahedral sites are generally accepted as the active centers for redox transformations22,37–40. Thus, while tetrahedral Mn contributes little directly to the activity, its presence promotes the migration of Co into octahedral sites, facilitating the formation of Mnoh-O-Cooh electron-bridge structures. This observation is further corroborated by our wavelet transform analysis (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). The primary reason for this valence state distribution is attributed to the higher octahedral site preference energy (OSPE) of Co3+ and Mn3+/Mn4+, which preferentially occupy the octahedral sites in the spinel structure during crystallization, whereas Co2+ tend to occupy the tetrahedral sites (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Through systematic characterization, we elucidated the fundamental differences in metal valence distribution and electron transfer mechanisms between C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2 catalysts. Although both materials contain mixed valence states of Mn4+/Mn3+ and Co3+ in octahedral sites, forming two asymmetric electron bridge structures (Mn4+-O2--Co3+ and Mn3+-O2--Co3+) between adjacent octahedra, XPS and XANES analyses revealed that C-CoMn2 possesses an average Mn oxidation state of +3.8 with octahedral sites predominantly occupied by Mn⁴⁺, making Co3+-O2--Mn4+ the absolutely dominant electron transfer pathway. In contrast, T-CoMn2 with lower Mn⁴⁺ content primarily relies on the Mn3+-O2--Co3+ pathway for electron transfer.

Oxidation-reduction performance

NH3-TPD analysis (Fig. 4a) revealed that C-CoMn2 exhibits a higher concentration of medium-strength acid sites distributed within the 200–600 °C range, directly correlated with the enrichment of surface Mn4+ sites which function as Lewis acid centers to enhance surface electron-accepting capacity. H2-TPR results (Fig. 4b) demonstrated a low-temperature shift of reduction peaks for C-CoMn2, indicating decreased reduction barriers for metal active sites originating from enhanced electron delocalization effects in the Mn4+-O2--Co3+ configuration. Combined with O2-TPD data (Fig. 4c), C-CoMn2 displayed higher desorption rates of chemisorbed oxygen in the 350–550 °C range and a distinctive bulk lattice oxygen desorption peak above 700 °C, confirming its superior oxygen mobility. These results collectively demonstrate that the Mn4+-O2--Co3+ dominated electron bridge structure synergistically optimizes both electron-accepting sites through Mn4+ centers and electron transfer pathways, achieving simultaneous enhancement of electron-accepting capability and transfer efficiency that significantly improves the overall redox performance of the material.Fig. 4Redox performance testing.a NH3-TPD, b H2-TPR, c O2-TPD of C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2. d Time-dependent for HMF oxidation over C-CoMn2. e FDCA formation rate of Co3O4, Mn3O4, C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2 (μmol∙gcat−1 ∙ h−1). f Stability test of C-CoMn2 for five cycles reaction.

Catalytic performance evaluation under optimized reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 22) demonstrated that C-CoMn2 effectively promotes HMF conversion through preferential aldehyde group oxidation pathway, achieving 98.1% FDCA yield within 3 h (Fig. 4d). The FDCA formation rate reaches 1635 μmol∙gcat−1 ∙ h−1, surpassing most reported counterparts and some representative noble-metal catalysts. (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, T-CoMn2 showed only 61% FDCA yield, conclusively verifying the critical role of Mn4+-O2--Co3+ configuration in enhancing catalytic efficiency. Comparative studies revealed that the spinel catalyst with asymmetric octahedral metal sites (MnOh-O-CoOh) exhibited superior catalytic performance, whereas the monometallic oxides (Mn3O4 and Co3O4) showed relatively lower FDCA formation rates. In particular, Co3O4 demonstrated negligible activity for HMF oxidation, primarily due to the low-spin state (t2g6eg0) of octahedrally coordinated Co3+, which hinders electron transfer. To determine the spin states of Co3+ in our catalysts, we measured the M‑T curves and magnetic susceptibility of C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2 (Supplementary Fig. 23), from which the effective magnetic moments were calculated as μeff(C) = 4.258 μB and μeff(T) = 4.417 μB, respectively. These results agree well with the theoretical magnetic moment of high-spin Co³⁺, confirming that Co³⁺ exists in a high-spin state in both C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2. This high-spin configuration facilitates the acceptance of electrons transferred from Mn, thereby promoting the redox cycle between Co and Mn during the catalytic process. Furthermore, to clarify that the improvement in catalytic performance does not stem solely from the individual role of Mn4+, we evaluated the catalytic performance of MnO2 under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 24). The results show that the FDCA selectivity of MnO2 is only 19.9%, approximately one-fifth of that of C-CoMn2. This data effectively rules out the dominant influence of Mn4+ alone and further confirms that the synergistic interaction between Co and Mn (Mn4+-O2--Co3+) is the key factor responsible for the enhanced catalytic performance. Recycling tests (Fig. 4f) revealed excellent stability with over 96% FDCA yield retention after five consecutive runs, while post-reaction XRD and XPS analyses confirmed maintained crystal structure and metal valence states (Supplementary Figs. 25–26 and Supplementary Table 5), collectively demonstrating the exceptional stability of the Mn4+-O2--Co3+ electron bridge structure during HMF catalytic oxidation.

Electron transfer mechanism through DFT calculations

Under alkaline conditions (Fig. 5a), the oxidation of HMF proceeds preferentially via aldehyde group oxidation, initially forming the carboxylic acid intermediate HMFCA. Subsequent stepwise oxidation of the hydroxymethyl group yields the aldehyde intermediate FFCA, ultimately leading to complete conversion into the dicarboxylic acid product FDCA41–44. To elucidate the electron transfer mechanism in the CoMn2O4 spinel-catalyzed selective oxidation of HMF to FDCA, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. Differential charge density analysis (Fig. 5b, c) reveals a significant charge transfer of 11.4 e- at the MnOh site, markedly higher than the 7.8 e- observed at the CoOh site. This indicates that, compared to the Co site, the Mn site possesses a stronger electron-withdrawing capability from HMF. Further analysis revealed two distinct electron transfer pathways centered at the Mn site, namely MnOh-O-MnOh and MnOh-O-CoOh. The MnOh-O-MnOh pathway exhibited relatively low efficiency because of its symmetric electron density distribution. In comparison, the MnOh-O-CoOh pathway demonstrated remarkable advantages. The electronic structure asymmetry between Mn and Co created an ideal electron density gradient that established an efficient electron transport channel. Moreover, the reversible electron buffering effect of the Co3+/Co2+ redox couple effectively prevented electron accumulation-induced deactivation at the Mn site. The projected density of states (PDOS) further elucidates the electronic structure characteristics of the CoMn2O4 spinel (Fig. 5d, e). Compared with T-CoMn2, C-CoMn2 exhibits significantly enhanced hybridization between Mn 3 d and Co 3 d orbitals, while the overlap between Mn 3 d orbitals is relatively weaker. This disparity leads to pronounced energy-level splitting between Mn 3 d and Co 3 d orbitals, establishing an efficient electron transfer pathway through the MnOh-O-CoOh electron bridge. These findings directly demonstrate that the MnOh-O-CoOh hetero-electron bridge structure exhibits superior electron transfer efficiency compared to the Mn-O-Mn homo-structure. Based on the afore-mentioned electronic structure analysis, we employed Mn as the active site to calculate the adsorption energies and activation energies during the reaction process. As shown in Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 27, the adsorption energies of HMF on C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2 were determined to be −0.3 eV and −0.76 eV, respectively. These optimal moderate adsorption energies not only facilitate the initial adsorption of HMF but also promote the subsequent desorption of reaction intermediates. The catalytic reaction is initiated upon the effective adsorption of HMF molecules on the catalyst surface. The overall oxidation process involves two critical elementary steps: C-H and O-H bond activation/cleavage. Between these, C-H bond activation is generally recognized as the rate-determining step that governs the overall oxidation kinetics33,44. As revealed by density functional theory calculations (Fig. 5g, h and Supplementary Figs. 28–31), we systematically compared the bond cleavage energy barriers between C-CoMn2 and T-CoMn2 catalysts for these critical steps. The results demonstrate that C-CoMn2 exhibits remarkable superiority in C-H bond activation, with a transition-state energy barrier of 0.76 eV, representing a 34% reduction compared to T-CoMn2 (1.16 eV). To corroborate these DFT predictions, diffusion-free intrinsic kinetic measurements (Supplementary Figs. 32–33) yielded activation energies of 0.75 eV (C-CoMn2) and 0.95 eV (T-CoMn2), slightly lower than the DFT-calculated C-H activation barriers (0.76 eV vs. 1.16 eV) due to theoretical simplifications (intermediate steps, solvation). Consistent trends confirm C-CoMn2’s lower intrinsic barrier and validate that C-H bond activation is the reaction’s rate-determining step. This significant difference primarily stems from the distinctive electronic structure of C-CoMn2, particularly its d-band center position closer to the Fermi level, which effectively stabilizes key reaction intermediates. More importantly, in the subsequent O-H bond cleavage step, C-CoMn2 maintains outstanding catalytic performance, showing an activation energy of only 0.54 eV, which is 26% lower than that of T-CoMn2 (0.73 eV). These theoretical calculations are in excellent agreement with the experimentally observed significantly enhanced catalytic activity of C-CoMn2, unequivocally demonstrating the crucial role of the Mn4+-O2--Co3+ electron bridge structure in facilitating electron transfer and reducing reaction energy barriers.

Discussion

This study successfully established an efficient electron bridge structure dominated by Mn4+-O2--Co3+ within the octahedral sites of CoMn2O4 spinel through precise electronic structure regulation. This engineered bridge significantly enhances catalytic efficiency for the multi-electron oxidation of HMF to FDCA. Our results demonstrate that incorporating Mn⁴⁺ effectively suppresses Jahn-Teller distortion while simultaneously creating an ideal electron transfer gradient between its completely vacant eg orbitals and low-spin Co3+. This facilitates the formation of a highly delocalized electron transfer pathway via bridging oxygen. Within this unique structure, Mn4+ sites act as highly efficient electron acceptors, markedly enhancing HMF dehydrogenation efficiency. Furthermore, the shortened Mn-O bond length (0.02 Å) enhances metal-oxygen covalency, thereby reducing the energy required for the activation of C-H and O-H bonds in the core process (30%). The resulting energy-level-interlaced electronic structure provides a continuous driving force for multi-step oxidation, elevating the FDCA yield from 61% to 98.1% while maintaining excellent cycling stability (96% activity retention after 5 cycles). These findings elucidate the essential role of metal-oxygen synergistic catalysis in spinel oxides at the atomic scale, providing fundamental theoretical guidance for designing high-performance non-noble metal catalysts.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

The chemical substances used in the study were all of analytical purity grade and were not further purified. Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 99%), manganese nitrate tetrahydrate (Mn(NO3)2·4H2O, 98%), ammonium formate (99%), and methanol (HPCL) were ordered from Aladdin Reagent Company. Ammonia solution (25-28%, Ron Chemical Company). 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF, 98%), 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA, 98%), 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furan carboxylic acid (HMFCA, 98%), 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF, 98%), and 5-formyl-2-furan carboxylic acid (FFCA, 98%) were purchased from Shanghai Bide Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, 99%) was purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All experiments were performed using MILLI-QD ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm, RephiLe Bioscience).

Synthesis of CoMn2O4 spinel oxides

The cubic-phase CoMn2O4 was synthesized through the following procedure: At room temperature, 1 mmol of Co(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in 5 mL of ultrapure water under continuous magnetic stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Subsequently, ammonia solution (25–28% concentration) was slowly added dropwise into the Co2+ solution, followed by 30 min of magnetic stirring, during which the [Co(NH3)6]3+ complex gradually formed. Then, 2 mmol of Mn(NO3)2·6H2O dissolved in 10 mL of ultrapure water was introduced into the mixture, and the resulting solution was vigorously stirred for 2 h to form a homogeneous precursor solution. The mixed solution was then transferred into a ceramic evaporating dish and subjected to crystallization at 180 °C for 5 h in an oven, yielding the cubic-phase cobalt manganese spinel catalyst, denoted as Cubic CoMn2O4 (abbreviated as C-CoMn2). For comparison, by reversing the addition sequence of Co(NO3)2·6H2O and Mn(NO3)2·6H2O, a tetragonal-phase cobalt manganese spinel was obtained and designated as Tetragonal CoMn2O4 (abbreviated as T-CoMn2). For control experiments, Co3O4 and Mn3O4 were similarly prepared using their corresponding single metal salts following the afore-mentioned procedure.

Theory calculation

Theoretical calculations were performed using periodic density functional theory (DFT) within the CP2K software package45,46. The exchange-correlation potential was described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized-gradient approximation (GGA)47. Wave functions were expanded in a molecularly optimized double-Gaussian basis set, with an auxiliary plane wave basis set employing a cutoff energy of 500 Rydberg. Norm-conserving pseudopotentials modeled the core electrons, with valence electron counts of 15 (Mn), 17 (Co), 6 (O), 4 (C), and 1 (H)48. Brillouin zone integration used only the Γ-point. Dispersion corrections were incorporated using Grimme’s third-generation DFT-D3 approach49. The (001) surfaces of CoMn2O4 spinel were modeled using tetrahedral and cubic slab configurations (denoted T-CoMn2 and C-CoMn2, respectively). The T-CoMn2 unit cell has dimensions of 11.4616 × 11.4616 × 22.0010 Å and angles α = β = γ = 90°. The C-CoMn2 unit cell has dimensions of 16.5480 × 16.5581 × 22.6826 Å and angles α = β = γ = 90°. A 10 Å vacuum space minimized slab-slab interactions. Geometry optimization employed the BFGS method, with a convergence criterion of a maximum force below 0.01 eV Å⁻¹. Transition states (TS) for all elementary reactions were located using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method with six replicas50. The TS was identified as the highest energy image along the minimum energy path. The energy barrier (Ea) for each elementary reaction was calculated as the energy difference between the TS and the initial state (IS), excluding zero-point energy corrections.

After completing the structural optimization, this study performs a non-self-consistent calculation (Non-SCF Calculation) and obtains the local density of states (LDOS) of Mn and Co atoms in T-CoMn2 and C-CoMn2. Catalyst characterizations

The prepared catalysts were comprehensively characterized using various techniques. The microstructure and nanostructure of the materials were observed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Gemini 500) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F30 S-Twin). The chemical composition and elemental distribution of products were tested by energy dispersive X-ray (EDX, Oxford, Xplore80) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi). The crystal phases were characterized using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD, Thermo ARL Scintag X′TRA). The specific surface area and pore size distribution of CoMn2O4 were measured using the specific surface area and pore size analyzer (BSD-PS2). Prior to the measurement, the sample was degassed and dried under vacuum at 160 °C for 4 hours to remove any adsorbed moisture or impurities. The acidity and the strength of acid sites were further evaluated utilizing ammonia temperature programmed desorption (NH3-TPD, Micromeritics Autochem 2910). Prior to the adsorption step, the sample was pretreated in a He flow (30 mL/min) by heating from room temperature to 160 °C at a rate of 5 °C /min and held at this temperature for 1 h to remove moisture and impurities. After cooling to 50 °C, the sample was saturated with a 10% NH3/He gas mixture for 1 h. Subsequently, the system was purged with He (30 mL/min) for 1 h to remove weakly physically adsorbed NH3. Finally, the temperature was ramped from 50 °C to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under continuous He flow to desorb the chemisorbed ammonia, and the desorption signal was recorded by a thermal conductivity detector. The redox performance of the material was tested using oxygen programmed temperature desorption (O2-TPD) and hydrogen programmed temperature reduction (H2-TPR) tests. For O2-TPD, the procedure followed the NH3-TPD method with NH₃ replaced by O2. For H2-TPR, the sample was first dried under an Ar flow (30 mL/min) by heating from room temperature to 160 °C at 5 °C/min and holding for 1 h. After cooling to 50 °C, a 10% H2/Ar mixture was introduced for 1 h until the baseline stabilized. Finally, the temperature was ramped to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under the same 10% H2/Ar flow, and the desorption profile was recorded. The Co-edge and Mn-edge XAS spectra were collected at the XAFCA beamline of Singapore Synchrotron Light Sources (SSLS) using transmission mode. The M-T curve measurements were performed using a Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS-9, Quantum Design, USA).

Thermo-catalytic oxidation of HMF to FDCA

The HMF oxidation experiment was performed in an autoclave (25 mL). Typically, HMF (0.25 mmol), catalyst (0.05 g), NaHCO3 (2 equiv to HMF), and H2O (5 mL) were added into the autoclave and 1 MPa oxygen was charged into the reactor. Afterward, the reaction mixture was drastically stirred (600 rpm) at 130 °C for 3 h. After the reaction, the catalyst was separated from the reaction mixture by centrifugation, followed by washing with abundant water, and then directly reused in the next catalytic run after drying at 60 °C for 12 h.
