Cobalt single atoms anchored in crystalline carbon nitride for efficient peroxymonosulfate activation to combat antibiotic resistance
Authors: Haibei Li, Mengqiu Chen, Tangping Zhang, Chengyu Jia, Yue Zhao, Tianjiao Chen, Lirong Zheng, Jianwei Zhao, Shuqing Zhou, Danyang Shi, Zhongwei Yang, Dong Yang, Junwen Li, Min Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance poses considerable risks worldwide due to its threats to human health and ecosystem stability. Notably, aquatic ecosystems are recognized as reservoirs of antibiotic resistance contaminants. Therefore, developing effective strategies to mitigate antibiotic resistance in aquatic environments is urgently required. Herein, a cobalt single-atom–crystalline carbon nitride (Co-CCN) catalyst was fabricated and employed to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) inactivation and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs) elimination. Notably, the Co-CCN/PMS system attained approximately 6–7-log inactivation of diverse ARB within 20 min, and ARB membrane disruption was further verified based on cellular morphology imaging and protein leakage measurements. Additionally, the removal efficiency of tetA exceeded a 4-log reduction within 120 min. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and quenching tests confirmed that the Co-CCN/PMS system generated •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2 for highly efficient ARB inactivation and ARG removal. Besides, the Co-CCN/PMS system also demonstrated exceptional environmental robustness and high efficiency for treating real water. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicated that single Co atoms adsorb oxygen atoms from PMS via electrostatic interactions, accompanied by electron transfer from Co-CCN to activate PMS. Our findings provide atomic-level insights into PMS activation for mitigating antibiotic resistance in aquatic settings.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance has emerged as one of the most critical threats confronting global public health, with projections indicating potentially 10 million annual deaths by 2050 [1,2]. The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic drastically aggravated this crisis via the excessive use of antibiotics [3]. An increasing amount of evidence indicates that antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) are widely distributed across environmental matrices, and their dissemination may exacerbate clinical antibiotic resistance [4]. Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) are ubiquitously identified in diverse ecosystems, spanning aquatic systems, sediments, and atmospheric environments [[5], [6], [7]]. Aquatic environments serve as critical reservoirs for emerging contaminants such as ARB and ARGs [8]. These persistent waterborne contaminants pose multipathway exposure risks (dermal, ingestion, trophic) [9], promoting treatment-resistant infections and increasing healthcare costs. Critically, ARGs propagate via horizontal gene transfer, accelerating the evolution of “superbugs” [10]. The urgency of controlling antibiotic resistance in aquatic environments is underscored by this multifaceted crisis, necessitating effective strategies.

Disinfection is a fundamental barrier in water treatment systems, effectively preventing pathogen transmission and protecting public health [11]. Established techniques like ozonation, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and chlorination remain widely employed in water treatment systems for microbial control. However, the elimination of ARB and ARGs still needs to be considered. UV disinfection introduces the risk of reactivating ARB via photoreactivation, with substantial evidence demonstrating potential post-treatment bacterial regrowth [12,13]. Although chlorination and ozonation effectively inactivate ARB, these processes may facilitate the release of ARGs and subsequent environmental accumulation [[14], [15], [16]], thus raising public health concerns. These challenges highlight an urgent need to develop advanced disinfection technologies to efficiently remove ARB and ARGs in water treatment processes.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are regarded as a promising approach for combating antibiotic resistance by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), e.g., hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and singlet oxygen (1O2), through catalytic, photoexcitation, ultrasonic, or electromagnetic activation [17]. Persulfate-based AOPs generate ROS via photo/thermal/sonic activation, enabling simultaneous ARB reduction and ARGs degradation [18]. However, low photon conversion efficiency, high energy consumption, and substantial equipment costs limit persulfate-based AOPs, restricting their broader applications. Notably, peroxymonosulfate (PMS), with its unique asymmetric molecular structure among persulfates, can be activated directly via a plethora of nanomaterials in the absence of traditionally required reaction conditions [19]. Emerging studies demonstrate that nanomaterial-activated PMS enables efficient ARB inactivation and ARG degradation. An iron-cobalt bimetallic biochar has been reported to enable effective deactivation of ARB and elimination of ARGs [20]. Sulfur vacancy-rich, Mo-doped FeS2 nanosheets anchored onto activated carbon fibers (Mo-FeS2/ACFs) served as an efficient PMS activator for the simultaneous removal of ARB and ARGs from aqueous systems [21]. These advances present engineered nano-AOP systems as promising strategies for controlling antibiotic resistance in water treatment.

Single-atom catalysts (SACs), featuring near-unity atom utilization and tunable electronic structures, are highly efficient in PMS-based AOPs and have emerged as one of the most promising catalytic materials in this field [22]. Previous studies have shown that SACs featuring transition metal centers (e.g., Co, Cu, Fe, or Mn) are highly promising for PMS activation, among which Co-based SACs displayed the highest catalytic activity [23]. For instance, nitrogen-coordinated Co SACs demonstrated an order of magnitude improved efficiency compared with conventional nanomaterials for PMS activation and bisphenol A removal [24]. In addition, SACs offer a versatile platform for AOPs to eliminate ARB and ARGs [[25], [26], [27]]. Through rational design, SACs can activate PMS to generate selective ROS for this purpose. For example, a cobalt SAC anchored on MnO2 achieved rapid inactivation of concentrated ARB [25], while an iron SAC with Fe–N5 active sites attained efficient removal of ARGs [26].

Crystalline graphitic carbon nitride (CCN) is garnering growing attention owing to its outstanding chemical stability, non-toxic nature, and enhanced capability for separation and migration of charge carriers [28]. However, unmodified CCN suffers from a deficiency of active sites, making it difficult for PMS to be effectively adsorbed and activated [29]. Notably, atomically dispersed transition metals (e.g., Co, Ni, Ag, Cu) coordinated with electron-rich nitrogen atoms in the CCN matrix exhibit unique catalytic advantages. Unlike conventional carbon substrates (e.g., activated carbon, carbon nanotubes, graphene), which require additional doping to introduce nitrogen anchoring sites and often suffer from metal aggregation or site inaccessibility, the nitrogen-enriched framework of CCN provides abundant intrinsic M–NX coordination sites that stabilize single atoms and maximize their exposure through its layered structure [30,31]. Moreover, the robust electronic coupling between metal centers and the CCN framework modulates the local electronic structure, enhancing PMS adsorption and activation kinetics [32,33]. Additionally, the mild synthesis of CCN (500–600 °C) avoids the energy-intensive pyrolysis (>900 °C) required for N-doped carbons, offering cost and scalability advantages [34]. Herein, a cobalt single-atom–crystalline carbon nitride (Co-CCN) catalyst was synthesized, systematically characterized, and employed for PMS activation to generate ROS, achieving efficient ARB inactivation and ARG elimination. The findings provide new insights into ameliorating aquatic antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and chemical reagents

Two representative ARB strains—tetracycline-resistant Escherichia coli (TREC, Gram-negative) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC 43300, Gram-positive)—were first evaluated in bactericidal assays. To further validate the broad-spectrum inactivation capability of the approach, three additional ARBs were tested: vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VREF, ATCC 700802), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA). TREC refers to E. coli K12 strain MG1655 (ATCC 700926) harboring plasmid RP4, which contains the tetracycline resistance gene (tetA), ampicillin resistance gene (blaTEM-1), and kanamycin resistance gene (aphA). E. coli K12, MRSA, and VREF were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), while CRAB and CRPA were isolated from aquatic ecosystems [35]. PMS was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Melamine, cobalt(II) chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2·6H2O), potassium chloride (KCl), and lithium chloride (LiCl) were sourced from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory (China). Detailed procedures for the determination of residual PMS concentration are provided in Text S1.

Synthesis of catalysts

Carbon nitride (CN) was obtained through the calcination of 1 g melamine at 550 °C (5 °C/min heating rate) in air for 4 h. Thorough mixing was performed on a blend containing 0.6 g CN, 3.3 g KCl, and 2.7 g LiCl. The mixture was calcined at 550 °C for 4 h under argon at a heating ramp of 5 °C/min. The obtained sample was washed thoroughly with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol to remove residual metal chloride salts, and then dried at 70 °C for 12 h to yield the CCN material. Masses of 0.6 g CN, 100 or 200 mg CoCl2·6H2O, 3.3 g KCl, and 2.7 g LiCl were uniformly mixed. The resulting mixture was thermally treated at 550–600 °C under a continuous argon flow and held isothermally for 4–5 h to ensure complete calcination. The samples were rinsed successively with deionized water followed by anhydrous ethanol to eliminate residual salts, and dried at 70 °C to yield the final catalysts: Co1-CCN (with 100 mg CoCl2·6H2O) and Co2-CCN (with 200 mg CoCl2·6H2O).

Water disinfection

ARB, including TREC, MRSA, VREF, CRAB, and CRPA, were inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, followed by incubation at 37 °C on a shaker set to 250 rpm for about 12 h. The bacterial suspensions were adjusted to approximately 106–107 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL for subsequent inactivation assays. A 2 mL aliquot of the bacterial suspension was incubated at pH 7.3 with the following treatments: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; control), CCN (400 μg/mL), Co1-CCN (400 μg/mL), and Co2-CCN (400 μg/mL), each tested in the presence and absence of 20 μg/mL PMS supplementation. At each time point, bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and plated on nutrient agar for colony enumeration. In addition, water samples collected from the Haihe River and Tuanbo Lake (Tianjin, China) were subjected to a 20-min treatment with PBS (control), CCN, or Co2-CCN, each in the presence of PMS, followed by serial dilution and plating on nutrient agar to assess the disinfection efficacy of the Co-CCN/PMS system in real water matrices. SEM imaging and fluorescence microscopy of bacteria

For SEM imaging, bacterial suspensions were fixed in PBS containing 1 wt% glutaraldehyde–formaldehyde for 1 h, washed thrice with deionized water, and post-fixed with 1% OsO4 for 20 min. Bacterial samples were subsequently dehydrated by stepwise transfer through increasing concentrations of ethanol in water. Ultimately, bacterial specimens were mounted on silver tape, sputter-coated with palladium, and analyzed using high-resolution imaging with a Hitachi Regulus 8100-type microscope (Hitachi, Japan). TREC and MRSA cells, after 20-min incubation with PBS (control), CCN, or Co2-CCN, all in the presence of PMS, were pelleted via centrifugation and rinsed three times with PBS. Staining of live and dead cells was performed using a LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (Molecular Probes, USA) following the provided protocol. In brief, TREC and MRSA suspensions were stained in the dark for 15 min with a 1:1 SYTO9/PI mixture, then mounted onto slides and examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Olympus BX53, USA).

Protein leakage measurements

The protein leakage from TREC and MRSA induced by the Co-CCN/PMS system was determined with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). The standard curve for protein quantification was obtained following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial suspensions incubated for 20 min with PBS (control), CCN, or Co2-CCN, each supplemented with PMS, were subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred into 96-well plates preloaded with 200 μL of BCA solution prior to a 30-min incubation at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 562 nm was read using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

ARG detection using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assays

Detailed protocols for the RT-qPCR assays, including reaction mixtures and thermal cycling conditions, are provided in Text S2. All primers and standards were synthesized by Shanghai Shengong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The amplification efficiency was calibrated using five-orders-of-magnitude standard curves, ensuring RT-qPCR efficiencies between 90% and 110% and correlation coefficients (R2) of the standard curves no less than 0.99. Table S1 describes the primer sequences and resulting standard curves used for RT-qPCR assays.

The tetA-bearing RP4 plasmid was spiked into PBS (pH 7.3) or Haihe River water samples at an initial tetA concentration of approximately 108 copies/mL. These samples were then treated with PBS (control), CCN (400 μg/mL), and Co2-CCN (400 μg/mL), each in the presence of 20 μg/mL PMS. At designated time points, 200 μL aliquots were collected and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatants (2 μL each) were then directly used as DNA templates for RT-qPCR analysis. The concentrations of tetA are expressed as copies/mL. Employing RP4 plasmid-harboring E. coli as a model organism, the overall degradation of the plasmid-borne tetA gene in the Co2-CCN/PMS system was examined. Bacterial suspensions were exposed to PBS (control), CCN, or Co2-CCN (each supplemented with PMS), sonicated on ice, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was subjected to RT-qPCR assays for tetA quantification.

ROS detection and scavenger quenching experiments

The electron spin resonance (ESR) signals, spin-trapped by 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) for •OH, SO4•− and •O2−, and by 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinone (TEMP) for 1O2, were recorded with an EPR spectrometer (JES-X310, JEOL, Japan). To elucidate the primary ROS involved in ARB inactivation and ARG removal within the Co-CCN/PMS system, quenching tests were carried out. The scavengers used included tert-butanol (TBA, 0.01 mg/ml, Tianjin Guangfu Fine Chemical Research Institute, 99.0%) for •OH, methanol (MeOH, 0.01 mg/ml, Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Partnership Enterprise, ≥99.5%) for •OH and SO4•−, p-benzoquinone (p-BQ, 0.01 mg/ml, Aladdin, 97%) for •O2−, and L-histidine (L-His, 0.08 mg/ml, Sigma, 99%) for 1O2 [28,37]. Detailed methods for determining ROS levels in solution are described in Text S3, while those for intracellular ROS are described in Text S4.

Evaluation of the practical application potential of the Co-CCN/PMS system

Water samples were obtained from the Haihe River and Tuanbo Lake (Tianjin, China). Prior to analysis, they were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to eliminate indigenous bacteria. The TREC and MRSA stock solutions were diluted in the water samples to obtain final concentrations of 106–107 CFU/mL, respectively. Bacterial suspensions were exposed to PMS alone, CCN/PMS, and Co2-CCN/PMS, followed by serial dilution and plating on nutrient agar for bacterial enumeration. For comparison, the RP4 plasmid containing tetA was also added to the filtered water samples and exposed to PMS alone, CCN/PMS, and Co2-CCN/PMS. Subsequently, the treated water was subjected to RT-qPCR assays for tetA quantification.

Materials characterizations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations

The experimental details on the materials characterizations and DFT calculations are described in Text S5 and Text S6, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) or SPSS 19.0 (IBM, USA) was used for all statistical evaluations. For datasets satisfying Pearson normality assumptions, significant differences were determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test (two groups) or ANOVA (multiple groups). Otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Co-CCN

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to reveal the structure and morphology of the CCN and Co-CCN materials. The two Co-CCN samples with distinct cobalt loadings were denoted as Co1-CCN and Co2-CCN, corresponding to CoCl2·6H2O precursor quantities of 100 mg and 200 mg, respectively. Results showed that CCN displayed well-defined nanorod morphology (Fig. 1A and B). Further, incorporating single-atom Co did not significantly alter the micromorphology. Aberration-corrected scanning TEM (AC-STEM) identified numerous isolated bright spots dispersed throughout the carbon matrix (Fig. 1C), confirming the presence of atomically dispersed single-atom Co. Moreover, Co atoms were evenly incorporated into the CCN matrix, as demonstrated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1D). To elucidate the crystal structures of CCN, Co1-CCN, and Co2-CCN, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected (Fig. 1E). The diffraction peaks observed at 14.2° and 27.5° were indexed to the (100) and (002) planes of g-C3N4 (JCPDS 87-1526), assigned to in-plane triazine ring stacking and interlayer aromatic conjugation, respectively [38]. The absence of characteristic Co diffraction peaks in the Co1-CCN and Co2-CCN patterns indicated the potentially uniform distribution of Co within the CCN matrix. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was employed to analyze the chemical structures of CCN, Co1-CCN, and Co2-CCN. As shown in Fig. 1F, similar spectral features are observed, exhibiting typical CCN absorption bands at 800, 900–1700, and 3100–3600 cm−1, corresponding to heptazine ring breathing, aromatic C−N stretching, and N−H stretching, respectively [39,40]. These findings indicate that the chemical structure of CCN is well preserved even after incorporating Co.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was utilized to probe the surface chemistry of CCN, Co1-CCN, and Co2-CCN. The survey spectra revealed characteristic C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s peaks for all samples (Fig. 1G). Notably, no distinct Co 2p signal was detected in the Co1-CCN and Co2-CCN samples, potentially attributable to the relatively low loading of single-atom Co. Additionally, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) determined Co loadings of 0.13 wt% and 0.31 wt% for Co1-CCN and Co2-CCN, respectively (Table S2), confirming successful Co incorporation. High-resolution XPS measurements were conducted to further characterize the C 1s, N 1s, and Co 2p regions. As shown in Fig. S1A, the C 1s spectrum of CCN was deconvoluted into three peaks at 284.8, 286.2, and 288.3 eV, corresponding to C–C/C=C, C=O, and N–C=N bonds, respectively [41], whereas similar peak profiles were observed for Co2-CCN. Deconvolution of the high-resolution N 1s spectrum for Co2-CCN (Fig. S1B) yielded four peaks at 398.7, 400.2, 401.1, and 404 eV, attributable to C–N=C, N–C3, –NHx, and π excitations, respectively [42]. For Co2-CCN, the Co 2p region (Fig. S1C) featured a peak at 780.2 eV together with its satellite signals, which are diagnostic of the Co 2p3/2 level and the shake-up transitions typical of Co2+ ions [43].

The chemical state and coordination environment of single Co atoms in Co2-CCN were investigated using X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra. Fig. 2A shows the XANES spectra at the Co K-edge of Co2-CCN. Compared to the references (Co foil, CoO, Co2O3, and CoPc), Co2-CCN exhibited absorption edge positions similar to CoO, indicating that the predominant valence state of the single atoms was close to 2+. The k3-weighted Fourier transform of EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) analysis confirmed the absence of metallic Co−Co scattering in Co2-CCN, verifying the isolated single-atom Co coordination structure (Fig. 2B). Notably, a prominent feature at approximately 1.56 Å was observed for Co2-CCN, corresponding to the first-shell Co−N scattering path. The optimal EXAFS fitting results for Co2-CCN confirmed the coordination of a Co center with four N atoms (Fig. 2C and Table S3). Additionally, the calculated distance of Co−N bonds for Co2-CCN was 2.04 Å. In addition, the wavelet transform (WT) analysis revealed a distinct feature at about 5 Å−1, arising from the Co−N bond (Fig. S2) [44]. Efficient ARB inactivation by the Co-CCN/PMS system

The ARB inactivation performance of Co-CCN-activated PMS was investigated. Gram-negative tetracycline-resistant Escherichia coli (TREC) and Gram-positive methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) were selected as representative indicator bacteria. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, negligible ARB inactivation was observed over time, both with PMS alone (control) and in the presence of the CCN material combined with PMS. In contrast, the Co1-CCN/PMS and Co2-CCN/PMS systems showed a significant reduction in ARB viability (Fig. 3A and B), achieving over 6-log inactivation efficiencies for both ARB strains within 20 min (Fig. 3C and D). Notably, without PMS, Co1-CCN and Co2-CCN showed <0.2-log reductions against both TREC and MRSA within 20 min. The agar plates reflect the inactivation of TREC and MRSA (Fig. 3E and F), revealing excellent inactivation performance facilitated by the Co-CCN/PMS system. To further evaluate the broad-spectrum antibacterial performance of Co-CCN/PMS system, its efficacy was tested against several clinically critical ARB strains, including vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VREF), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA). The results indicated that the Co1-CCN/PMS and Co2-CCN/PMS systems demonstrated exceptional bactericidal activity, with approximately 6–7-log reductions against all three tested antibiotic-resistant strains (Fig. S3A–F), suggesting potent inactivation of multiple clinically relevant ARB. Taken together, these findings indicate that the Co-CCN/PMS system has broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against various ARB. Notably, Co2-CCN exhibited superior bacterial inactivation compared to Co1-CCN (Figs. 3A, S3A and S3B), attributable to its higher Co content, which enabled enhanced PMS activation. Thus, Co2-CCN was selected for further study. The cyclic inactivation performance of the Co-CCN/PMS system against ARB was investigated as previously reported [35]. The Co2-CCN/PMS system exhibited sustained high-level disinfection performance, achieving >6-log inactivation of both TREC and MRSA over five consecutive cycles (Fig. S4A and B). After five cycles, the Co2-CCN material exhibited only minor structural changes relative to its pristine nanorod morphology and largely retained this shape (Fig. S5A and B). These findings demonstrate the excellent stability and reusability of the Co2-CCN/PMS system for sustained ARB inactivation.

Next, SEM was used to observe bacterial cell morphology after Co2-CCN/PMS treatment. TREC and MRSA exhibited intact cellular morphology with smooth cell membranes when treated with the PMS-only or Co2-CCN/PMS systems (Fig. 3G and H). After exposure to Co2-CCN/PMS treatment for 20 min, the majority of ARB exhibited distinct cellular deformation and membrane collapse. Following bacterial membrane disruption, essential intracellular components, including critical proteins, inevitably leaked into the extracellular medium [45]. Thus, the protein contents released from ARB under various treatment conditions were measured. The results demonstrated a significant increase in bacterial protein leakage after the Co2-CCN/PMS treatment (Fig. 3I and J), aligning with the antibacterial results. Additional evidence for these observations was obtained from live/dead bacterial fluorescence staining. After 20-min exposure to PMS alone or CCN/PMS, numerous live TREC and MRSA showed green fluorescence (Fig. S6). Conversely, red fluorescence dominated after 20-min Co2-CCN/PMS exposure. Notably, the localization of red fluorescence in most cells signified widespread membrane disruption. Collectively, these findings revealed that the Co-CCN/PMS system effectively compromised bacterial membrane integrity.

Efficient elimination of ARGs by the Co-CCN/PMS system

Cell membrane damage leads to intracellular ARGs leaking into the extracellular medium. These ARGs display environmental persistence and facilitate horizontal gene transfer, thus propagating antibiotic resistance [46]. Thus, the efficacy of the Co-CCN/PMS system in eradicating ARGs was evaluated systematically. The elimination of the RP4 plasmid-encoded ARGs (tetA) was investigated using the Co2-CCN/PMS system. Previous studies demonstrated that DNA hairpin structure stability hindered ARG elimination more significantly than ARB inactivation [47]. Consistent with ARB inactivation data, the tetA removal by the PMS-only and CCN/PMS systems was negligible (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the Co2-CCN/PMS system showed much higher tetA removal performance than PMS or CCN/PMS, attaining over 4-log reduction within 120 min. Notably, compared with the other recently reported nano-AOP systems, the Co-CCN/PMS system had the highest ARG removal efficiency (Table S5). To assess whether the Co2-CCN/PMS system maintained its activity throughout the reaction, residual PMS and ROS levels were measured. A reaction time of 120 min resulted in a modest decline of the PMS concentration to 14.4 μg/mL, corresponding to a residual level of 70.6% (Table S6). Concurrently, higher ROS generation was detected in the Co2-CCN/PMS system compared to PMS alone and CCN/PMS controls (Fig. S7). The system’s stability was demonstrated by the persistence of PMS and the sustained generation of ROS throughout the 120 min reaction period. The leaching of Co ions from the Co2-CCN/PMS system was further quantified. After 120 min of reaction, the concentration of Co ions released was found to be approximately 5.96 ng/mL (Table S7). Next, the Co2-CCN/PMS system was employed to assess its ARG degradation efficacy during the inactivation treatment of ARB. Employing TREC (RP4 plasmid-harboring E. coli) as a model organism, the overall degradation of the plasmid-borne tetA gene was examined using the Co2-CCN/PMS system. The PMS-only and CCN/PMS systems exhibited minimal tetA removal after 120 min of treatment (Fig. 4B). Notably, the Co2-CCN/PMS system exhibited markedly superior degradation efficacy, exceeding a 4-log total reduction. Simultaneously, the inactivation rate of ARB was monitored, and complete inactivation was observed after 10 min of Co2-CCN/PMS treatment (Fig. S8), corroborating the findings presented in Fig. 3A. These findings demonstrate that the Co-CCN/PMS system simultaneously inactivated ARB and eliminated the released ARGs.

Mechanistic insights into ARB and ARG elimination by the Co-CCN system

EPR experiments were conducted to identify the reactive species in the Co-CCN/PMS system. No EPR signals corresponding to •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2 were observed for CCN and Co2-CCN in the absence of PMS (Fig. 5A–C). Strong signals for •OH, SO4•−, and •O2− were observed in the Co2-CCN/PMS system, whereas only negligible signals were observed in the CCN/PMS system. Furthermore, characteristic 1O2 signals were observed in the Co-CCN/PMS system, displaying a substantially higher intensity than those in the CCN/PMS system. These findings identified that •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2 were the dominant species in the Co2-CCN/PMS system. To elucidate the contribution of ROS to ARB inactivation in the Co-CCN/PMS system, quenching experiments were conducted using specific scavengers: TBA (for •OH), MeOH (for •OH and SO4•−), p-benzoquinone p-BQ (for •O2−), and L-His (for 1O2). Correspondingly, the radical quenching experiments showed that TBA, MeOH, p-BQ, and L-His all significantly inhibited ARB inactivation mediated by the Co2-CCN/PMS system (Fig. 5D and E), establishing the pivotal contributions of •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2 in the ARB inactivation mechanism. Further radical quenching experiments were performed to elucidate ROS involvement in ARG removal by the Co2-CCN/PMS system. The results showed that ARG degradation efficiency achieved by the Co2-CCN/PMS system was significantly inhibited upon adding TBA, MeOH, p-BQ, and L-His (Fig. 5F). ROS (e.g., •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2) are strong oxidants that inactivate bacteria by damaging cellular components—including the peptidoglycan layer, electron transport chain, genomic materials, ribosomes, and functional proteins—while also compromising membrane integrity, leading to increased permeability and leakage of intracellular contents [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. To investigate the site of ROS action, intracellular ROS levels were measured in the Co2-CCN/PMS system using flow cytometry (Fig. S9). Following Co2-CCN/PMS treatment, intracellular ROS levels significantly increased, supporting that ROS were released into the aqueous phase, penetrated cells, and induced cellular damage leading to intracellular ARG degradation. Furthermore, ROS generated in the Co2-CCN/PMS system can also degrade ARGs released extracellularly.

DFT computations were conducted to investigate the structural characteristics of Co-CCN. The adsorption efficiency of PMS on the catalyst surface critically determines its activation performance. Therefore, the adsorption characteristics of PMS on CCN and Co-CCN were systematically examined. In Fig. 5G–I, PMS contains three distinct types of O atoms: a hydroxyl (O1), peroxyl (O2), and terminal oxygen (O3), all capable of coordinating with the metal sites in Co-CCN, and the corresponding adsorption configurations were denoted Co-CCN-O1, Co-CCN-O2, and Co-CCN-O3, respectively. Exothermic adsorption was calculated across all three configurations of Co-CCN (Fig. 5J and Table S8), demonstrating the spontaneity of the adsorption process and the stable binding of PMS on the Co-CCN surface. The adsorption energy of PMS on Co-CCN (−4.33 to −4.46 eV) is significantly more negative than that on CCN (−0.16 to −0.22 eV), demonstrating stronger PMS adsorption affinity for Co-CCN. The bond lengths for Co atoms interacting with adsorbed O were also compiled in Table S8. The charge-density differences for Co-CCN-O3 adsorption in Fig. 5K reveal substantial charge transfer between PMS and Co-CCN, demonstrating the chemisorption characteristics of their interaction. Additionally, PMS/Co-CCN exhibited a higher electron transfer value (0.86 e) than PMS/CCN (0.47 e; Table S9), indicating that stronger PMS binding to the Co-CCN catalyst surface facilitated electron transfer. The projected density of states (PDOS; Fig. 5L) analysis of CCN demonstrated the absence of significant near-Fermi-level electronic states, confirming its characteristic semiconductor behavior. The coordination of Co to N induced distinct near-Fermi-level electronic states, demonstrating enhanced conductivity via Co coordination. These findings indicated Co-CCN had a superior PMS adsorption affinity and charge transfer capacity compared with CCN.

Investigation of the practical application potential of the Co-CCN/PMS system

To evaluate the disinfection performance of the Co-CCN/PMS system in real water, samples were collected from the Haihe River and Tuanbo Lake. The physicochemical characteristics of the river water are listed in Table S10. The Co2-CCN/PMS system achieved >99% bacterial inactivation in Haihe River and Tuanbo Lake within 20 min, demonstrating rapid elimination of viable bacteria (Fig. S10A and B).

The disinfection performance in Haihe River and Tuanbo Lake water samples was tested using TREC and MRSA as representative microorganisms (Fig. 6A and B). A rapid reduction in viable bacterial concentration was observed within 30 min under the Co2-CCN/PMS system, resulting in approximately 6-log reductions. In contrast, the control and CCN groups showed minimal bacterial inactivation over time with PMS treatment. Subsequently, ARG removal in natural waters was assessed using the Co2-CCN/PMS system by spiking water samples with RP4 plasmid harboring tetA. As shown in Fig. 6C, tetA degradation was minimal in the PMS-only and CCN/PMS systems after 120 min of treatment. In contrast, the Co2-CCN/PMS system exhibited markedly enhanced performance, achieving approximately 4-log reductions in tetA. Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully synthesized a Co-CCN catalyst featuring atomically dispersed Co–N4 active sites, which enabled sustained and efficient PMS activation. The Co-CCN/PMS system demonstrated remarkable disinfection performance, achieving approximately 6–7 log inactivation of various ARB within 20 min, along with effective ARG removal. Mechanistic investigations revealed that Co-CCN activated PMS to generate multiple reactive radicals—including •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and 1O2—which collectively contributed to the robust elimination of both ARB and ARGs. The atomically dispersed Co sites facilitated strong PMS adsorption and enhanced interfacial electron transfer, underpinning the catalytic efficiency of the system. Furthermore, the Co-CCN/PMS system exhibited strong environmental robustness, achieving effective ARB and ARG removal in natural water matrices, underscoring its potential for real-world water treatment applications. These findings position Co-CCN as a promising candidate for advanced oxidation processes aimed at mitigating antibiotic resistance in aquatic environments. However, translating this technology to practice requires addressing key challenges, including the scalability and cost of Co-CCN synthesis, as well as the catalyst’s recoverability and long-term stability in continuous-flow systems. Future efforts should focus on developing magnetically separable Co-CCN composites or immobilizing Co-CCN onto membranes, optimizing synthetic routes to reduce costs, and assessing long-term system performance under environmentally relevant conditions. Addressing these aspects will be critical to advancing Co-CCN/PMS systems toward practical deployment in water treatment infrastructure.
