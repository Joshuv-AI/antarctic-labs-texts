Impact of high-intensity interval training on cardiometabolic health in patients with diabesity: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Sameer Badri Al-Mhanna, Barry A Franklin, Mark A Tarnopolsky, John A Hawley, John M Jakicic, Emmanuel Stamatakis, Jonathan P Little, Linda S Pescatello, Deborah Riebe, Walter R Thompson, James S Skinner, Sheri R Colberg, Jonathan K Ehrman, George S Metsios, Helen T Douda, Norsuhana Omar, Abdullah F Alghannam, Alexios Batrakoulis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of high-intensity interval training (HIIT) on cardiometabolic health-related outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes and obesity (diabesity).MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases were searched from inception up to November 2024. The search strategy encompassed the following keywords: diabetes, obesity, and HIIT. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) recruiting adult participants with diabesity and comparing HIIT per se for ≥ 2 weeks in duration with non-exercise standard treatment were included.ResultsA total of 18 RCTs qualified involving 504 patients (52/48 women/men ratio; 55.0 ± 11.8 years; 31.0 ± 6.9 kg/m2). Body mass [standardized mean differences (SMD) -0.36 kg, 95% confidence intervals (CI) -0.71 to -0.01], body mass index (SMD -0.57 kg/m2, 95% CI -0.92 to -0.21), waist-to-hip ratio (SMD -1.68, 95% CI -2.50 to -0.86), fasting blood glucose (SMD -0.64 mmol/L, 95% CI -1.03 to -0.24), glycated hemoglobin (SMD -1.08%, 95% CI -1.68 to -0.47), fasting insulin (SMD -0.79 mIU/L, 95% CI -1.28 to -0.31), homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (SMD -0.95, 95% CI -1.43 to -0.47), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (SMD -0.64 mg/dL, 95% CI -1.23 to -0.06), triglycerides (SMD -0.64 mg/dL, 95% CI -1.02 to -0.26), and total cholesterol (SMD -0.66 mg/dL, 95% CI -1.23 to -0.08) improved compared to standard treatment without exercise.ConclusionsThe present findings suggest that HIIT improves several markers of metabolic health and cardiovascular risk, even without significant body composition improvements in patients with diabesity.Open science framework registry. https://osf.io/rtb42 Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01974-4.Keywords: Exercise, Intermittent training, Type 2 diabetes, Obesity, Body composition, Blood pressure, Glucose metabolism, Lipid metabolism, Aerobic capacity

Introduction

Diabesity, a contemporary medical condition, signifies the coexistence of both type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and obesity, which are interconnected by varied pathophysiological mechanisms [1, 2]. Diabesity is a significant global health concern, contributing to higher cardiovascular disease (CVD) rates due to the associated metabolic abnormalities and cardiovascular dysfunction [3, 4]. Patients with glucose homeostasis derangements and excess visceral adiposity generally exhibit a sedentary lifestyle and physical limitations, resulting in suboptimal levels of musculoskeletal and cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) [5, 6]. These factors are associated with elevated metabolic risk indicators [7, 8]. Consequently, there has been a shift towards non-pharmacological interventions, such as moderate-to-vigorous-intensity aerobic activity to enhance cardiometabolic health. This issue is timely and of heightened concern to medical practitioners, healthcare professionals, and public health policymakers who are focused on the health effects and costs of diabesity [9, 10].

Regular exercise is strongly recommended for the primary prevention and treatment of diabesity [11–14]. The American College of Sports Medicine and other global health organizations endorse vigorous intermittent aerobic exercise and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) as time-efficient and effective exercise modes to manage metabolic disorders [15, 16]. The latter type of exercise enhances CRF, a key indicator of overall health, by involving the performance of brief bouts of high-intensity physical exertion, followed by short periods of active recovery, typically through a low-volume training approach (2-3x/week; 20–25 min/session) [17, 18]. According to the exercise prescription guidelines from the American College of Sports Medicine, the American Diabetes Association, and the World Health Organization, selected individuals with cardiometabolic conditions may engage in and benefit from vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise (≈75 min/week), regardless of body composition outcomes [11, 12, 19–22].

Low-volume HIIT interventions have been reported as safe and well-tolerated exercise strategies for selected patients with cardiometabolic dysregulation, inducing varied improvements in aerobic capacity, glucose control, and weight loss, respectively [11, 17, 23–27]. As a powerful physiological stimulus, HIIT may improve indices of cardiovascular function, as well as low-grade chronic inflammation and antioxidant capacity [24, 28–31]. Moreover, progression to vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise over time is associated with lower mortality compared with no exercise or moderate-intensity aerobic exercise [32], playing a key role in the management of diabesity and exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation [33]. However, further validation is needed to update current exercise prescription guidelines [11, 12, 17], as previous studies often failed to clarify differences between vigorous and HIIT, the full range of cardiometabolic indices, as well as the presence of diabesity [24, 31, 34, 35].

Although HIIT has been shown to improve cardiometabolic markers in people with T2DM or obesity, irrespective of concomitant health issues, the current evidence is limited, particularly for individuals with diabesity. The lack of consensus on the duration and structure of HIIT sessions stems from the significant methodological diversity observed among HIIT protocols reported in the literature. Thus, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effects of HIIT on varied cardiometabolic indices in patients with diabesity, including the associated changes in anthropometrics, body composition, glucose and lipid metabolism, blood pressure, and CRF.

Methods

Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement [36] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [37]. The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework registry (https://osf.io/rtb42). Because the investigation did not involve individual patient data and all included studies were previously published, approval from the institutional bioethics committee was not required.

Literature search strategy

A systematic electronic search was conducted to identify relevant reports from several databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Four authors (S.B.A.M., N.O., A.F.A., and A.B.) applied a strategic combination of keywords and Boolean operators ("AND" and "OR") to conduct the literature review up to November 2024. The search terms ("Diabetes") AND ("Exercise" OR "Training"), as outlined in Table S1, were used to locate relevant studies. The search strategy was designed around the PICOS framework: (P) Population: patients with T2DM and concurrent overweight/obesity; (I) Intervention: high-intensity interval training (HIIT); (C) Comparator: no exercise or standard treatment; (O) Outcomes – primary: anthropometric measures [body mass, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, hip circumference, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)], body composition (body fat, fat mass, and fat-free mass), lipid metabolism [high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG)], glucose metabolism [fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting insulin, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)]; secondary outcomes due to their independent association with cardiometabolic health: resting/exercise measures of cardiovascular function [systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), resting heart rate (RHR), and CRF (VO2max)]. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included. Moreover, the reference lists of the selected reports and relevant systematic reviews were reviewed to identify additional studies that met inclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (i) participants diagnosed with T2DM and concurrent overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) with or without additional comorbidities; (ii) individuals aged 18 years or older; (iii) utilization of standard treatment with HIIT as the primary intervention; (iv) research assessing at least one primary outcome of interest in human participants, while secondary outcomes were considered if relevant to cardiometabolic health; (v) full-text publications available in peer-reviewed journals up to November 2024; (vi) no restrictions on language; and (vii) inclusion of only RCTs; and (viii) compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Exclusion criteria included: (i) studies involving a mixed sample of patients with T2DM, overweight/obesity, and other chronic conditions per intervention arm; (ii) studies involving young people (< 18 years); (iii) reports in which the effects of HIIT intervention cannot be isolated due to the incorporation of exercise training as a component of a multidimensional treatment (e.g., combination of diet and HIIT); (iv) investigations that did not evaluate any of the primary outcome measures of interest; (v) studies employing short-term exercise interventions (< 2 weeks); and (vi) review articles, case reports, studies lacking a control group, and those with ambiguous, unclear or missing data.

Study selection

A linear evaluation approach was employed by three researchers (S.B.A.M., A.F.A., and A.B.) to determine eligibility. This process began with a preliminary screening of titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed review of full texts, when appropriate. Reports that met these requisites were then rigorously evaluated against predefined inclusion criteria. Any disagreements or uncertainties were resolved with the involvement of a fourth author (N.O.), who conducted an independent review using the same methodology. EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) was used to organize and manage the search results.

Data extraction

The data collection and extraction process from relevant studies was conducted independently by two authors (S.B.A.M. and A.B.) after thoroughly reviewing the full-text reports. The included RCTs (n = 18) yielded comprehensive datasets containing details such as the lead author, publication year, demographic information, gender distribution, sample size, exercise intervention specifics (e.g., frequency, intensity, duration, and type), study duration, and predefined outcomes. Risk of bias assessment

Two authors (S.B.A.M. and A.B.) independently assessed the risk of bias in individual studies following the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [38]. In instances of divergence or ambiguity, the perspective of a third author (N.O.) was sought to facilitate consensus. The evaluation considered the following domains: (i) random sequence generation; (ii) allocation concealment; (iii) blinding of participants and study personnel; (iv) blinding of outcome assessors; (v) completeness of outcome data; (vi) selective reporting of outcomes; and (vii) other potential sources of bias as described in the above-referenced Handbook (Figure S1). Based on these criteria, studies were classified into three categories of risk (e.g., low, some concerns, or high) depending on the number and severity of biases identified.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4, developed by the Cochrane Collaboration (https://revman.cochrane.org/info). A random-effects model was applied to present the findings, while heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q-test and the I2 statistic, as previously described (19). Heterogeneity levels were interpreted following established guidelines: an I2 value of 0%–40% was deemed acceptable, 30%–60% indicated moderate heterogeneity, 50%–90% represented substantial heterogeneity, and 75%–100% reflected considerable heterogeneity [39]. To estimate effect sizes, mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were utilized. MD was chosen when outcomes were measured consistently on the same scale, whereas SMD was used for outcomes measured on different scales or in varying units. The choice between MD and SMD was based on the characteristics of the outcomes and the variability among included studies. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Grading quality of evidence

Two authors (S.B.A.M. and A.B.) independently assessed the quality of evidence for both primary and secondary outcomes using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework, available at https://www.gradepro.org [40]. This evaluation considered factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, placing the evidence into very low, low, moderate, or high quality categories. In cases of disagreement or uncertainty, a third author (N.O.) was consulted to facilitate resolution. Evidence quality was upgraded based on criteria such as substantial effect size, dose–response relationships, or when plausible confounders would either reduce an observed effect or suggest a false effect in its absence. Conversely, downgrading could occur due to risk of bias, inconsistency in results, indirect evidence, imprecision, or publication bias (Table 2). Egger's regression test was used to detect funnel plot asymmetry, which may indicate bias or heterogeneity, particularly for outcomes with contributions from fewer than ten studies of varying sizes [39, 41]. The analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software version 4 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA) [42].

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis for cases where the I2 statistic exceeded 50%. This analysis specifically targeted participants with comorbidities in studies investigating the effects of HIIT on BMI, body mass, waist circumference, FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, and TG, distinguishing between studies that reported comorbidities and those that did not.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was used to evaluate the impact of the risk of bias on the assessment of performance and detection outcomes.

Equity, diversity and inclusion

The team of authors demonstrated diversity in age, gender, academic/clinical backgrounds, and geographic origins, with researchers from varied career stages. The study sample was inclusive, without limitations based on region, race, education, socioeconomic status, or other demographic factors.

Results

Literature search and selection

From the aforementioned literature search (Fig. 1), a total of 24,323 studies were initially identified. After deleting duplicates, 19,408 studies remained for further evaluation. A review of titles and abstracts using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in the exclusion of 19,380 studies. The full text of the remaining 28 reports was then reviewed in detail, leading to the exclusion of 8 for specific reasons (Table S2). Additionally, two records were identified as follow-up studies of trials included in the review (Table S3). Accordingly, 18 studies involving data from 504 sedentary or inactive patients (52/48 women/men ratio; age: 55.0 ± 11.8 years; BMI: 31.0 ± 6.9 kg/m2) were included in the final review.

PRISMA flow diagram

Literature characteristics

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the included studies, which were published between 2005 and 2024. Of these, 12 studies (67%) were published between 2005 and 2019, while six studies (33%) were published between 2020 and 2024. The trials were conducted in 12 countries, across six continents: Asia (n = 5, 28%), Europe (n = 7, 39%), North America (n = 1, 5.5%), South America (n = 1, 5.5%), Australia (n = 2, 11%), and Africa (n = 2, 11%). Specifically, 10 out of the 18 trials (56%) were conducted in high-income countries [43–51], five trials (28%) were conducted in upper-middle-income countries [52–56], and three trials (16%) were conducted in lower-middle-income countries [57–59]. Seven (39%) of the 18 trials reported comorbidities [44, 48, 50, 56, 58, 60, 61]. Fifteen (83%) of the studies conducted short-term exercise interventions lasting 8–12 weeks [44–48, 50–52, 54–57, 59–61], and three (17%) studies conducted long-term exercise interventions lasting 16–24 weeks [43, 49, 58]. Seventeen (94%) of the studies incorporated supervised exercise interventions, and one (6%) study incorporated unsupervised interventions. Fourteen (78%) of the studies were conducted in laboratory-based settings, while four (22%) were undertaken in field-based conditions. The mean adherence rate was 89%, with a range of 59% to 100% across all HIIT intervention groups. The most frequently reported HIIT protocol was a 12-week stationary cycle ergometer supervised lab-based routine conducted three times a week (25–30 min/session), incorporating 4–12 intervals (1–4 min) at 85%−90% of maximum heart rate (HRmax) separated by 1–3 min exercise bouts at 60%−70% of HRmax.

Risk of bias assessment results

Figure 2 provides an overview of the risk of bias assessment. A large number of eligible studies raised concerns about blinding of outcome assessment, whereas others demonstrated an unclear risk of bias about randomization and allocation concealment. In contrast, only a small number of studies showed a high risk of bias related to participant/personnel blinding, a common issue in exercise training studies. Most studies, however, exhibited a low risk of bias regarding missing outcome data and selective reporting. Detailed risk of bias judgments for each eligible study, categorized by domain, are shown in Figure S1. The certainty of the findings for the outcomes ranged from very low to low, with the evidence downgraded for several reasons, including small sample sizes in both standard treatment and HIIT groups, high risk of bias, and moderate to substantial heterogeneity (Table 2).

Summary of the risk of bias assessment

Primary outcomes

Anthropometrics

Changes in body mass and BMI were reported in 14 and 13 trials, involving 406 and 429 participants, respectively. HIIT revealed a significant reduction in body mass (SMD −0.36 kg, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.01; I2 = 64%; p = 0.04; very low certainty) (Figure S2a) and BMI (SMD −0.57 kg/m2, 95% CI −0.92 to −0.21; I2 = 68%; p = 0.002; very low certainty) (Figure S2b) compared with standard treatment. Waist circumference, hip circumference, and WHR were reported in eight, one, and three trials, involving 262, 40, and 97 participants, respectively (Figures S2c, S2d, and S2e). No significant changes were observed in waist and hip circumferences between HIIT and standard treatment. However, HIIT reduced WHR (SMD −1.68, 95% CI −2.50 to −0.86; I2 = 63%, p < 0.0001; very low certainty) compared with standard treatment.

Subgroup analyses of studies that did not report comorbidities (body mass: eight trials, n = 228; BMI: nine trials, n = 290) showed a significant reduction in body mass (SMD −0.53 kg, 95% CI −0.99 to −0.06; I2 = 65%; p = 0.31; very low certainty) (Figure S2a) and BMI (SMD −0.52 kg/m2, 95% CI −0.96 to −0.08; I2 = 69%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2b) in HIIT compared with standard treatment. In contrast, subgroup analyses of studies that reported comorbidities showed no significant difference in body mass and BMI between HIIT and standard treatment. Subgroup analyses of studies that did not include comorbidities (waist circumference: five trials, n = 182) reported reduced waist circumference (SMD −0.59 cm, 95% CI −1.12 to −0.05; I2 = 66%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2c) in HIIT compared with standard treatment.

The sensitivity analysis for body mass showed changes in the effect estimate (SMD −0.29, 95% CI −0.72 to 0.14; I2 = 57%; p = 0.18) [47, 48, 54, 55, 57]. Nevertheless, BMI remained unchanged. Following the sensitivity analysis for waist circumference, WHR, fat-free mass, and body fat, no substantial changes in the effect estimate were identified [43, 47, 52, 54, 55, 57].

Body composition

Body fat, fat mass, and fat-free mass were assessed in three, one, and five trials, involving 98, 23, and 137 participants, respectively. No significant differences were noted in these variables between HIIT and standard treatment (Figures S2f and S2g).

Glucose metabolism

FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR were evaluated in 14 (n = 433), 14 (n = 437), 9 (n = 254), and 10 trials (n = 278), respectively. HIIT elicited a significant reduction in FBG (SMD −0.64 mmol/L, 95% CI −1.03 to −0.24; I2 = 73%; p = 0.002; very low certainty) (Figure S2h), HbA1c (SMD −1.08%, 95% CI −1.68 to −0.47; I2 = 87%; p = 0.0005; very low certainty) (Figure S2i), fasting insulin (SMD −0.79 mIU/L, 95% CI −1.28 to −0.31; I2 = 69%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2j), and HOMA-IR (SMD −0.95, 95% CI −1.43 to −0.47; I2 = 70%; p = 0.0001; very low certainty) (Figure S2k) compared with standard treatment.

Subgroup analyses of studies that did not report comorbidities (FBG: nine trials, n = 262; HbA1c: nine trials, n = 266; fasting insulin: five trials, n = 130; HOMA-IR: six trials, n = 154) showed meaningful improvements in FBG (SMD −0.52 mmol/L, 95% CI −1.02 to −0.02; I2 = 73%; p = 0.04; very low certainty) (Figure S2h), HbA1c (SMD −0.99.%, 95% CI −1.88 to −0.09; I2 = 90%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2i), fasting insulin (SMD −0.87 mIU/L, 95% CI −1.67 to −0.08; I2 = 76%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2j), and HOMA-IR (SMD −0.82, 95% CI −1.32 to −0.33; I2 = 52%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2k) in HIIT compared with standard treatment. Moreover, subgroup analyses of studies reporting comorbidities (FBG: five trials, n = 171; HbA1c: five trails, n = 171; fasting insulin: four trials, n = 124; HOMA-IR: four trials, n = 124) demonstrated significant improvements in FBG (SMD −0.85 mmol/L, 95% CI −1.51 to −0.19; I2 = 75%; p = 0.01; very low certainty) (Figure S2h), HbA1c (SMD −1.23%, 95% CI −1.93 to −0.54; I2 = 75%; p = 0.0005; low certainty) (Figure S2i), fasting insulin (SMD −0.76 mIU/L, 95% CI −1.40 to −0.12; I2 = 66%; p = 0.02; low certainty) (Figure S2j), and HOMA-IR (SMD −1.13, 95% CI −2.15 to −0.12; I2 = 85%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2k) in HIIT compared with standard treatment.

There were no changes in the effect estimate after the sensitivity analysis for FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR [43, 45, 47, 55, 57, 59, 61].

Lipid metabolism

LDL-C, TG, TC, and HDL-C were assessed in 11 (n = 326), 13 (n = 388), 13 (n = 379), and 12 (n = 366) trials, respectively. HIIT exhibited a significant decrease in LDL-C (SMD −0.64 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.23 to −0.06; I2 = 83%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2l), TG (SMD −0.64 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.02 to −0.26; I2 = 68%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2m), and TC (SMD −0.66 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.23 to −0.08; I2 = 85; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2n), but not in HDL-C (Figure S2o) when compared with standard treatment.

Subgroup analyses were conducted on studies that did not report comorbidities (LDL-C: eight, n = 211; TG: eight, n = 257; and TC: eight, n = 241) showed a significant reduction in LDL-C (SMD −0.92 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.67 to −0.17; I2 = 83%; p = 0.02; very low certainty) (Figure S2l), TG (SMD −0.54 mg/dL, 95% CI −0.87 to −0.22; I2 = 38%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2m), and TC (SMD −0.75 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.44 to −0.06; I2 = 83; p = 0.03; very low certainty) (Figure S2n) following HIIT as compared with standard treatment. Furthermore, subgroup analyses of studies reporting comorbidities (TG: five, n = 131; and HDL-C: four, n = 115) demonstrated a significant reduction in TG (SMD −1.04 mg/dL, 95% CI −2.05 to −0.03; I2 = 85%; p = 0.04; very low certainty) (Figure S2m), whereas HDL-C increased (SMD 1.76 mg/dL, 95% CI 0.06 to 3.46; I2 = 92; p = 0.04; very low certainty) (Figure S2o) following HIIT as compared with standard treatment.

Sensitivity analysis for TC and LDL-C revealed changes in the effect estimate for TC (SMD −0.75, 95% CI −0.65 to 0.15; I2 = 87%; p = 0.10) and LDL-C (SMD −0.20, 95% CI −0.95 to 0.56; I2 = 82%; p = 0.61) [43, 45, 47, 55, 57, 61], while no changes were observed for HDL-C and TG.

Secondary outcomes

Cardiovascular function

SBP, DBP, and RHR were reported in 11, 12, and seven trials, involving 327, 364, and 206 participants, respectively. HIIT significantly lowered SBP (SMD −0.70 mm Hg, 95% CI −1.12 to −0.28; I2 = 69%; p = 0.001; very low certainty) (Figure S2p) and DBP (SMD −0.49 mm Hg, 95% CI −0.94 to −0.05; I2 = 75%; p = 0.03; very low certainty) Figure S2q) when compared with standard treatment. No meaningful reduction was observed in RHR for HIIT compared with standard treatment (Figure S2r). Sensitivity analysis for SBP and DBP showed no changes in the effect estimate [57, 59, 61–64]. VO2max was assessed in 12 trials (n = 333). HIIT significantly increased VO2max (SMD 1.70 mL/kg/min, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.37; I2 = 84%, p < 0.0001; very low certainty) (Figure S2s) compared with standard treatment. No alteration was found in the effect estimate for VO2max.

Publication bias

To evaluate potential publication bias in outcomes such as body mass, BMI, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, HbA1c, FBG, HOMA-IR, SBP, and DBP, random-effects funnel plots were generated for outcomes supported by a minimum of 10 studies [65]. These plots, encompassing varying sample sizes across the included studies, demonstrated symmetrical distributions for body mass, BMI, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, HbA1c, FBG, HOMA-IR, SBP, and DBP outcomes, suggesting minimal risk of publication bias (Figure S3). Conversely, the funnel plots for VO2max exhibited noticeable asymmetry, indicating a potential risk of publication bias (Figure S3). Egger's linear regression test was used to investigate publication bias in further detail [41]. The results showed no significant bias for body mass, BMI, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, HbA1c, FBG, HOMA-IR, SBP, and DBP, with p-values of 0.12, 0.39, 0.22, 0.45, 0.22, 0.07, 0.61, 0.67, 0.14, 0.33, and 0.14, respectively. However, a significant effect estimate for VO2max (1.785; p = 0.01) confirmed asymmetry in the funnel plot, reinforcing concerns about potential publication bias for this outcome (Figure S3). To address this issue, the trim-and-fill method was employed to estimate and adjust for potentially missing studies on the left side of the mean. After adjustment, the recalibrated effect estimate for VO2max was reduced to 1.31, rendering it negligible and statistically non-significant. This finding indicates that the initial effect estimate may have been influenced by publication bias and that the true effect size, after adjustment, is closer to zero (Figure S3).

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides a novel perspective on how HIIT affects varied cardiometabolic health markers in patients with diabesity. Our results showed that HIIT significantly improves selected outcomes, such as body mass, glucose control, blood lipids, blood pressure, and CRF, but not body composition in these middle-aged patients (Fig. 3). Furthermore, a high compliance rate was noted in all the eligible RCTs, likely due in part to the supervised nature of the HIIT sessions, which require substantively less weekly time compared with moderate-to-vigorous aerobic, resistance, or combined exercise training [66–68]. Considerable research has also identified lack of time as a common perceived barrier to exercise among adults [69]. Accordingly, HIIT offers a promising exercise modality for individuals with diabesity and associated metabolic health disorders. However, despite the ample number of studies, the current findings should be interpreted with caution because the quality of evidence in many of the included RCTs is relatively low due to the limited number of participants.

Anthropometrics and body composition

This meta-analysis found that HIIT leads to a modest reduction in body mass, although other anthropometric and body composition variables remained unchanged. This reduction may be attributed to associated increases in resting metabolic rate, post-exercise fat oxidation, training-induced appetite suppression, or combinations thereof [70]. The observed physiological adaptations may be related to the increases in aerobic and anaerobic fitness that are induced by HIIT. These training effects also include significant skeletal muscle adaptations that are oxidative in nature [70]. Nonetheless, the present study did not examine the potential role of complementary pharmacologic approaches to enhancing weight loss in patients with diabesity syndrome. The potential for anti-diabetic medications to promote independent and additive weight loss when used in conjunction with HIIT has been reported [71]. Further investigation into this phenomenon is warranted and should be a priority in subsequent research studies. The absence of significant beneficial alterations in other anthropometric and body composition measurements in response to HIIT, as compared with the standard treatment, underscores the adjunctive role of combined aerobic and resistance training as well as dietary habits in facilitating favorable changes across a comprehensive array of relevant outcomes in patients with diabesity [19, 72–74].

Glucose and lipid metabolism

Regular HIIT appears to be effective in reducing visceral fat and enhancing insulin resistance in patients with impaired metabolic health, even in the absence of concomitant alterations in body composition [75]. Importantly, a minimal level of weekly engagement in vigorous intermittent aerobic activities can markedly improve glucose control in adults with metabolic disorders [76, 77]. The present findings suggest that HIIT exerts a favorable impact on glucose metabolism in patients with diabesity, which may reduce their risk of CVD events. Chronic HIIT has been shown to enhance glucose management in this cohort by decreasing visceral and ectopic fat accumulation. These indices of adiposity have been reported to impair insulin sensitivity, induce inflammation, and exacerbate oxidative stress and adverse immunologic changes [78–80].

HIIT has been demonstrated to enhance metabolic health by stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis and myogenesis. The metabolic stress induced by high-intensity exercise activates AMP-activated protein kinase–peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α signaling, resulting in increased mitochondrial density and oxidative capacity [18]. Concurrently, HIIT has been revealed to promote muscle remodeling, characterized by the activation of satellite cells, the growth of capillaries, and an augmentation in lean mass. This, in turn, has been shown to elevate energy expenditure [81]. These adaptations have been shown to enhance insulin sensitivity through a number of mechanisms, including enhanced glucose transporter type 4 translocation, AMP-activated protein kinase activation, and reduced lipid accumulation [82]. This suggests that HIIT may be more effective than moderate-intensity continuous training in improving glucose regulation [27].

Individuals exhibiting metabolic dysfunction frequently present with abnormal blood lipid levels and related cardiometabolic issues, increasing their risk of developing metabolic syndrome [83]. HIIT has been shown to improve lipid homeostasis in people with compromised metabolic health, potentially by modulating glycolipid balance and reducing low-grade chronic inflammation, even without an accompanying weight loss [84]. Accordingly, a variety of exercise regimens may favorably modify components of the blood lipid profile in adults with metabolic dysfunction [16, 28, 29, 85–89]. This assertion is further substantiated by the present findings.

Cardiovascular function

Adults with excess adiposity and insulin intolerance frequently exhibit elevated resting blood pressure levels and reduced levels of CRF [3, 4]. The beneficial effects of HIIT on blood pressure and aerobic capacity may be related to the associated improvements in glucose and lipid metabolism and body mass, decreasing the likelihood of cardiovascular dysfunction as compared with their control counterparts [75, 90]. HIIT has been shown to induce increases in blood flow, resulting in augmented vasodilation, which directly impacts the magnitude of flow-mediated dilation among previously inactive individuals with excess adiposity and vascular dysfunction [91–93]. Additionally, the enhancement of CRF following HIIT regimens is likely attributed to both central and peripheral adaptations, including greater motor unit recruitment, improved cardiovascular hemodynamics, plasma volume, skeletal muscle mitochondrial proliferation, and increased capillary density [94]. These physiological adaptations likely lower mortality rates in patients with metabolic health derangements who are a high risk of developing heart failure [3].

Future directions

Further research is needed to determine the optimal frequency, intensity, and duration of HIIT to maximize cardiometabolic health gains. This objective should be addressed not only through traditional single-component protocols (Table 1 and Fig. 3) but also through hybrid-type, multi-component regimens[19, 86, 95–97]. Furthermore, future high-quality RCTs should be undertaken to clarify the effects of HIIT on additional under-studied cardiometabolic health-related indices, including oxidative stress, redox status, low-grade chronic inflammation, and liver function in patients with diabesity. Establishing the safety and effectiveness of unsupervised, home-based HIIT interventions could also provide a readily accessible and time-saving alternative to traditional moderate intensity continuous training for enhancing cardiometabolic health on a broader scale in this escalating patient cohort.

Limitations

This review has some methodological limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. The HIIT interventions revealed significant heterogeneity across studies, potentially introducing bias in the review of RCTs. Thus, the heterogeneity observed in certain outcomes might be due to the small number of qualifying studies. Moreover, the lack of data on the usage and modification of adjunctive pharmacologic agents for T2DM and its comorbidities during the interventions may also represent a potential confounder that was unaccounted for. Finally, the safety of initiating HIIT in previously sedentary middle-aged and older adults with concomitant diabetes and obesity, and likely underlying CVD, has been questioned, especially in unsupervised, unmonitored non-medical settings [98, 99]. A more conservative approach might be for HIIT to follow an uneventful moderate-to-vigorous program in selected patients with diabesity.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis offers unique data on the impactful role of HIIT in favorably modifying multiple cardiometabolic health indices in middle-aged patients with diabesity. The present findings suggest that HIIT elicits beneficial changes in selected physiological variables, including anthropometrics, glucose and lipid metabolism, resting blood pressure, and CRF when compared with standard, non-exercise treatment regimens. These results suggest a potential role for the incorporation of HIIT as an adjunctive non-pharmacological treatment option for the clinical management of selected patients with diabesity. To fortify and broaden these findings, it is critical that future RCTs evaluate medically supervised group, work-based, and home-based HIIT interventions, with specific reference to safety, effectiveness, and adherence.
