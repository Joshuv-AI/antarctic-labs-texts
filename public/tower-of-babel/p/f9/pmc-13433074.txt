Characterization of mandibular third molar impaction patterns and a CBCT-derived radiographic extraction difficulty in a Saudi cohort: A retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Hassan Ahmed Assiri, Ebtihal Saad Aldakheel, Asma Abdullah Alamri, Yara Abdulaziz Alrumaih, Lama Alnami, Sonia Egido-Moreno, Mohammad Shahul Hameed, José López-López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mandibular third molar (MTM) impaction is the most frequent type of impacted tooth. Therefore, we aimed to report cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)-based MTM impaction patterns in a Saudi cohort and to investigate the relationship between these patterns and a CBCT-derived radiographic extraction difficulty. This retrospective cross-sectional study screened 200 consecutive archived CBCT scans (May–October 2025) from King Khalid University Dental Hospital. Sixty-nine patients who had 1 or more impacted MTMs were analyzed and met the eligibility criteria. Impaction type (bony/partial), angulation of Winter (vertical/mesioangular/horizontal/distoangular), and the orientation of the buccolingual crown were measured. The elevator fulcrum-based depth surrogate was used to assess radiographic difficulty (mild/moderate/high) against the distal thirds of the roots of the second molar beside it. Among screened CBCTs, 69/200 (34.5%; 95% confidence interval: 28.3–41.3) demonstrated ≥1 impacted MTM. Within the analyzed cohort (n = 69), mesioangular angulation was the most frequent. Winter’s angulation was associated with radiographic difficulty on the right side (χ2 = 27.85, df = 6, P = .0001; Holm-adjusted P = .0004) but not on the left after adjustment (χ2 = 13.54, df = 6, P = .035; Holm-adjusted P = .070). Buccolingual orientation was associated with radiographic difficulty on the right (χ2 = 23.80, df = 10, P = .008; Holm-adjusted P = .025) but not on the left (χ2 = 3.84, df = 8, P = .872). The metric of MTM angulation and the buccolingual orientation were found to be side-dependent, related to a CBCT-based radiographic difficulty grade. Since difficulty was measured radiographically (not relative to operative outcomes) and the impacted-case sample was modest, findings need to be taken with caution and confirmed prospectively using surgical time, complications, and patient-centered outcomes.

1. Introduction

Third molar impaction is the inability of a tooth to erupt into functional occlusion because of the blockage of the tooth by other teeth, soft tissue, or bone, and has an increasing prevalence in dental practice based on population and study design.[1–3] Radiographic research in the Middle East has shown that cases of mandibular third molar (MTM) impaction are substantial and significant in Saudi cohorts, among other clinical factors.[4,5] Impacted third molars may cause a variety of pathological processes, such as pericoronitis, adjacent teeth root resorption, dental caries, cyst formation, and, in some instances, neoplasms.[6–8] Additionally, the impaction of third molars can exacerbate mandibular crowding and compromise oral health-related quality of life, leading to postoperative pain, swelling, and trismus.[9–12] Several classification systems are used to describe impacted third molars. Winter’s classification categorizes MTMs in relation to the angulation of the second molar, whereas the Pell and Gregory system relates the depth of impaction in relation to the occlusal plane and relationship to the ascending mandibular ramus.[13] These classifications assist in the planning of surgeries by imposing standard descriptors of the type and hardness of impaction.[14] Although they are commonly used, traditional classifications rely mostly on 2-dimensional imaging and not on the 3-dimensional characteristics of the buccolingual orientation or the position in relation to the key body parts, which restricts their predictive power in terms of operative complexity. The pathogenesis of third molar impaction is a combination of factors.[15] The lack of retromolar space, the size of the mandibular ridge, growth, angulation, the size of dentition in relation to the size of the jaw, and systemic factors are among the factors that would make a patient prone to impaction.[16–18] The size of jaws and some types of faces can decrease the space available, which can lead to impaction, and the rate of eruption is further altered by variations in the inclination of the posterior dentition and the rate of development of teeth. Research among Saudi populations has pointed out that the lower the retromolar space and jaw size, the higher the rates of MTM impaction.[4,9] Preoperative imaging is important in the evaluation of the impacted third molars. Although panoramic radiography is commonly used because of its availability and reduced radiation, it suffers from distortion, projection geometry, and overlapping of body organs.[6,7,10,19,20] Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has solved these shortcomings by offering 3-dimensional imaging of the position of the teeth, their morphology, and their spatial position relative to other anatomical features, such as the mandibular canal. CBCT gives clinicians an opportunity to recognize high-risk impaction, preempt surgical challenges, and develop interventions more accurately than traditional 2-dimensional imaging.[10,19–21] Although the use of CBCT has been on the rise, very minimal data have been compiled on impaction and 3-dimensional orientations in Saudi populations, especially when it comes to the association with radiographic difficulty. A description of these trends can enhance risk stratification and surgical planning, as it involves angulation and buccolingual orientation that cannot be represented using conventional indices. To our knowledge, buccolingual crown orientation together with a CBCT-based fulcrum difficulty surrogate has not previously been characterized in a Saudi cohort, which constitutes the specific contribution of this study. The primary aim of this study was descriptive: to characterize CBCT-based MTM impaction patterns in a Saudi cohort. A secondary, exploratory analytical aim was to examine the prespecified associations between impaction features (Winter’s angulation and buccolingual crown orientation) and a CBCT-derived radiographic surrogate of extraction difficulty. We postulated that poor angulation and noncentral buccolingual orientation would be aligned with high levels of radiographic difficulty grades, which can be used to elicit insights on preoperative risk assessment and individual surgical planning.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This cross-sectional retrospective study examined previous CBCT images of patients who visited King Khalid University Dental Hospital (College of Dentistry), Saudi Arabia. The retrospective design was selected to provide a more effective way of using available CBCT data without subjecting the participants to extra radiation. CBCT images were obtained sequentially from May 2025 to October 2025 for routine clinical indications (e.g., planning of implants, assessing the third molar, and assessment of pathology not in the third molar area). The chosen timeframe additionally guaranteed a progressive and representative sample of routine clinical cases.

2.2. Participants and sampling criteria

Of 200 consecutive CBCT examinations, 69 individuals with 69 examinations were found to fit the eligibility requirements and were studied to obtain the characteristics of MTM impaction. The patients who had at least 1 MTM with fully developed roots at the age of 17 years and above were the target of the study. CBCT scans were included and had to be of diagnostic quality, without any significant artifacts, and able to visualize the entire third molar of the mandible. The patients were excluded if they had a history of maxillofacial trauma, had undergone previous orthodontic treatment of the third molars, had lost or extracted permanent teeth in the posterior mandible, had craniofacial dysplasia, dentofacial anomalies, or endocrine conditions that might have changed the normal morphology of the third molar. The number of CBCT examinations to be done per patient was limited to one to prevent intraindividual correlation, and bilateral impactions were registered and analyzed by side.

This sampling methodology was used to ensure that the sampled CBCT scans would be representative of standard clinical patients whose third molars could be assessed, as well as to reduce the confounding variables that may affect the impaction patterns or the radiographic difficulty measure. Nevertheless, the analyses of impaction patterns and radiographic difficulty were restricted to the subset that experienced impaction (n = 69) to prevent type I error, having less statistical power, especially to conduct side-stratified subgroup tests and enlarge confidence intervals (CIs). This is why inferential analyses were considered exploratory.

2.3. Variables

The primary outcome was a CBCT-based radiographic difficulty grade (mild, moderate, or high). The variables related to exposure were the type of impaction (bony vs partial), the angulation of Winter (vertical, mesioangular, horizontal, or distoangular), and the orientation (buccolingual or lingual) of the crown in axial slices. Other variables were side (left/right), sex, age group, and number of root canals. Outcome and exposure variables were chosen on a clinical basis. The CBCT radiographic grade of difficulty was used as a standardized surrogate measure of operative difficulty and impaction type. Winter’s angulation and buccolingual orientation are measures of established anatomy that affect surgical difficulty. Potential covariates were listed as side, sex, age, and root canal number that could influence the impaction properties or difficulties of extraction. Such a distinct separation between dependent and independent variables made the analysis structured and interpretable.

2.4. CBCT acquisition and analysis

All CBCT images were acquired using a KaVo OP 3D Pro unit (KaVo Dental, Biberach, Germany) with an 8 × 15 cm field of view, 90 kVp, 6.3 mA, 4.5 seconds, and a 0.2 mm voxel size. Images were analyzed in OnDemand3D software, version 1.0 (build 1.0.10.7462, x64 edition; Cybermed Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). The field of view, that is, 8 × 15 cm, was selected to cover the whole area of the third molar of the mandible with the least amount of radiation. The scans were evaluated by 4 examiners (3 dental interns and 1 oral and maxillofacial radiologist) to balance trainee calibration and clinical expertise. Disagreements were resolved by consensus to maintain consistent categorical assessment; inter-examiner reliability was assessed separately and is reported below.

2.5. Impaction assessment

Angulation was classified on sagittal reconstructions based on Winter’s classification. Buccolingual crown orientation was noted on axial slices, and the orientation was classified as mesiobuccal, mesiolingual, buccal, lingual, vertical, or distolingual. Winter’s classification was chosen as it has been widely used in clinical research to classify the angulation of the MTM.[22,23] The buccolingual orientations were defined with respect to a line bisecting the mandibular second molar on the axial plane to ensure that the description remained similar across patients (Fig. 1). Any discrepancies between examiners were resolved through consensus to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the assessments.

The orientation of the MTMI in relation to the line bisecting the adjacent second molar perpendicular to the line extending from the buccal plates to the lingual plates through the crown of the MTMI. It shows a slight buccal orientation of the MTMI. MTMI = mandibular third molar impaction.

2.6. Radiographic difficulty grading

The estimation of difficulty was based on a sagittal reconstruction of the CBCT by using a fulcrum-based depth surrogate elevator to the second adjacent molar root. Three horizontal reference lines were drawn: tangential to the occlusal plane of the MTM; to the maximum contour of the MTM; and tangential to the point of estimation of the fulcrum. The grades of difficulty were defined as mild when the fulcrum point lay at or coronal to the cervical third of the distal root of the adjacent second molar, moderate when it lay at the middle third, and high when it lay at the apical third. This radiographic grading system was adopted as an internally defined, non-validated surrogate of operative difficulty, supported by earlier reports of a correlation between fulcrum position and extraction complexity, and it requires prospective validation against operative outcomes (Fig. 2). To minimize possible bias, examiners were blinded to patient demographics and information about their sides when assigning the difficulty grades. Although not a clinically proven endpoint, this technique offers a reproducible structure for comparing the anatomy with the anticipated extraction challenge.

Location of the fulcrum point (note the yellow arrow indicating the point).

2.7. Measurement validation

Inter-examiner reliability was assessed on a random 10% subset (8 patients and 12 MTMs) independently rescored by 2 examiners (HA, an oral and maxillofacial radiologist, and EA), each blinded to the original scores and to one another. Agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa for the nominal variables (Winter’s angulation and buccolingual orientation) and quadratic-weighted kappa for the ordinal difficulty grade. Agreement was almost perfect for Winter’s angulation (κ = 0.88) and the difficulty grade (weighted κ = 0.83), and moderate for buccolingual orientation (κ = 0.60).

2.8. Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp, Armonk) was used for data analysis. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, along with a 95% CI (Wilson method). Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test was used to test associations when there were low cell counts, and Cramer’s V was used to measure the magnitude of the effects. To reduce type I error inflation in cases of multiple comparisons, the Holm–Bonferroni method was used for the prespecified list of side-specific tests of hypotheses to test angulation versus radiographic difficulty in Winter and buccolingual orientation versus radiographic difficulty (4 tests). Regarding the count of root canals, the Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare the sexes, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed to compare age groups. A two-sided P value below .05 was considered statistically significant after the prespecified hypotheses had been taken into consideration. A post hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that, at α = 0.05 and 80% power, the achieved side-specific samples (n = 62 and n = 44) were able to detect only moderate-to-large associations (Cramer’s V ≈ 0.33–0.39; w ≈ 0.47–0.56), consistent with the exploratory interpretation of the subgroup analyses. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the analyzed cases comprised all eligible impacted MTMs identified among the 200 consecutively screened scans, and all side-stratified subgroup analyses were prespecified but exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

2.9. Ethical consideration

The ethics committee at King Khalid University (KKU-29-2025-14) approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All analyzed CBCT images were fully de-identified before analysis. At the initial presentation to the College of Dentistry, all patients had signed the institutional consent form (DQF09-01), which authorizes the use of their radiographic records for teaching and scientific publication; given the retrospective design and the use of anonymized data, the ethics committee waived the requirement for additional study-specific informed consent.

3. Results

Out of 200 consecutive CBCT examinations screened, 69 patients were identified with at least 1 impacted MTM, yielding a prevalence of 34.5% (95% CI: 28.3–41.3). The analyzed cohort comprised 42 males (60.9%) and 27 females (39.1%), with most participants aged between 20 and 40 years (76.8%; Table 1). Smaller proportions were observed in the younger (<20 years, 10.1%) and older age groups (41–60 years, 8.7%; >60 years, 4.3%). These demographic distributions provide context for interpreting impaction patterns and suggest a predominance of impacted MTMs in young adult males within this Saudi cohort.

4. Discussion

Main findings: The current CBCT-based research provides a detailed account of the patterns of MTM impaction in a Saudi cohort and their relation to a CBCT-based radiographic difficulty scale. Comparison with previous studies: In line with earlier research findings regarding the region, mesioangular impactions were the dominant type of impaction, followed by vertical and horizontal angulations.[17,18,22–25] These results support the fact that mesioangular orientation is the prevailing anatomical pattern in young populations of the Middle East during the adult phase and have some implications for clinical planning and risk stratification. The novel part of our work is not the prevalence pattern, which mirrors regional data, but the quantification of buccolingual crown orientation and a CBCT fulcrum difficulty surrogate as 3-dimensional descriptors.

The clinically plausible association between Winter’s angulation and radiographic difficulty was observed. Mesioangular and horizontal impactions usually necessitate more bone removal or odontotomy and have been associated with more operation time, complication rates, and postoperative morbidity than vertical impactions.[26,27] In our analysis, the association was statistically strong on the right side but not on the left side when we corrected for several comparisons. Such asymmetry is probably due to the lack of statistical power and sparsity of cells in left-sided contingency tables and not actual biological differences. Notably, the direction of the effect was generalized across sides, implying that the nonsignificant left-side outcome might be a type II error and not a clinically significant difference. Additionally, the 3-dimensional measure of buccolingual crown orientation, which cannot be achieved with traditional 2-dimensional imaging, could determine the accessibility of the tooth, equipment, and risk during surgery, especially when the tooth lies lingually or near the mandibular canal.[10,19–21] It has been a result of this research that buccolingual orientation was correlated with radiographic difficulty on the right but not the left. Although these findings are biologically plausible, they must be approached with caution because the sample size of subgroup analyses is small and the comparison of sides of stratification is exploratory. Clinical implications: However, our results suggest that there is a possibility of added value of CBCT to determine 3D impaction features that are not necessarily visible with panoramic imaging. Similarly, the radiographic grading method employed in this research was a fulcrum-based measure that was made to be an anatomical surrogate of the lever mechanisms and depth relative to the second molar adjacent to it in the same molar. The most common difficulty indices, including the Pederson index, are commonly taught and used, and it has been indicated that they poorly predict the outcome of the operation, including the duration of extraction or the occurrence of postoperative complications.[28,29] Additionally, most existing indices were created with the help of panoramic radiography and do not consider buccolingual orientation or 3-dimensional root morphology that is visible with the help of CBCT.[6,10] The current implementation with 3D anatomical characteristics creates extra granularity that has the potential to enhance preoperative risk assessment, but this should not be regarded as a substitute for clinical judgment. It has been reported that root canals are variable in third molars and can vary across populations.[30,31] The sex difference in canal number in the current cohort was nominal and was not significant after correction of multiple tests, indicating that the results could be considered as hypothesis-generating but not conclusive. More significant research using larger sample sizes should be conducted to investigate population-specific variations in root canals and how they could be used to plan surgery. Generally, the value of 3-dimensional imaging in the preoperative measurement of MTM impaction has been highlighted in this study. CBCT enables clinicians to detect complicated anatomical aspects, plan surgical operations, and customize operative procedures to specific patients. Although others were constrained by the sample size and exploratory research, the results form a part of an increasingly emerging body of evidence that 3D imaging can play an important role in reducing risk stratification and patient counseling.

4.1. Limitations

There are several limitations associated with this study that should be considered when interpreting the findings. To start with, the study sample included only 69 impacted cases in 1 tertiary care center, limiting the statistical accuracy, the power of subgroup analysis, and the ability to generalize to other settings. Second, the primary outcome, a fulcrum-based radiographic difficulty grade, was a surrogate and had not been compared with operative outcomes such as extraction time, surgical complexity, or complications. Third, since CBCT tests are not performed on a regular screening basis but on a selective basis based on clinical indications, the rate of CBCT scans with MTM impaction cannot be considered the prevalence of the population. Finally, despite using the Holm–Bonferroni correction to adjust for prespecified hypotheses, type I error might still be present in exploratory comparisons, and such results need careful interpretation. Irrespective of these constraints, the paper offers valuable information on the possible utility of CBCT-derived 3-dimensional descriptors, such as buccolingual crown orientation, for radiographic risk stratification and preoperative planning in MTM management. CBCT can help improve the prediction of surgical complications and the ability of clinicians to customize operations for each patient by providing anatomical detail not visible on standard 2-dimensional imaging. Future research: In the future, research on larger and more diverse groups should be conducted to achieve a more generalized population. Further studies on fulcrum-based radiographic grading and other 3D descriptors are significant for clinically relevant endpoints, including extraction time, intraoperative difficulty, changes in surgical technique, complication rates, and patient-reported outcomes, such as postoperative pain and functional recovery. Combining the CBCT-based measures with the current difficulty indices could additionally improve predictive modeling and evidence-based recommendations for MTM management.

5. Conclusion

Mesioangular impaction of the third molar of the mandible was the most common pattern in this retrospective CBCT cohort. Angulation of the Winter, as well as buccolingual crown orientation, correlated with a grade of radiographic difficulty derived using CBCT, but the side-specific data were affected by small sample size and multiple testing. These findings demonstrate the possible usefulness of 3-dimensional CBCT descriptors in risk evaluation and surgical planning. Future, sufficiently powered experiments matching CBCT-based measurements with operative outcomes, complication rates, and patient-reported outcomes are justified to test and improve this radiographic grading methodology before it can be recommended as a standardized clinical measure of difficulty.
