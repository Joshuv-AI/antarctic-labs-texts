Use of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy and high-power Er:YAG laser in the treatment of drug- or radiation-induced bone necrosis: Case series
Authors: Larissa C. de Freitas, Fabianne S. Lima, Luciane H. Azevedo, Alyne Simões
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) and medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaws (MRONJ) are debilitating complications that compromise bone repair in patients, who have undergone radiotherapy or treatment with anti-resorptive/anti-angiogenic drugs. Once established, these lesions are difficult to manage and reduce patients’ quality of life. The combination of low- and high-power lasers, through photo biomodulation therapy and antimicrobial photodynamic therapy preceding surgical laser debridement, can provide satisfactory results in the treatment of these patients. Thus, the purpose of this work is to describe three cases associating these therapies for the management and treatment of ORN and ONMRM, offering an alternative, safe and effective therapy for these conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) and Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) are serious and debilitating complications secondary to radiotherapy or treatment with anti-resorptive or anti-angiogenic drugs. These injuries are challenging because they involve necrotic bone in impaired metabolism, which is unable to repair the damage properly.[12]

Conventional treatments for this condition are debridement (surgical or not) and medication (antibiotics, pentoxifylline-tocopherol-clodronate).[3] It is also possible to treat these lesions with low- and high-power lasers; however, there are few cases reported in the literature using these techniques, especially when combined.[4]

The use of low-power lasers for photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT), in the red or infrared spectrum, and antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has already been reported as a beneficial therapy for controlling infected bone necrosis. This is due to the antimicrobial effects of aPDT combined with effects of PBMT, which stimulate the healing process through cell proliferation, collagen production, modulation of inflammation and angiogenesis.[3]

High-intensity laser treatment (HILT) can replace the conventional surgical technique for debridement of necrotic bone tissue remaining after PBMT and aPDT. For this purpose, CO2, Nd:YAG and Er:YAG lasers can be used. The Er:YAG laser works in the infrared spectrum, acting superficially and reducing the risk of heat transmission to adjacent structures, making surgery more precise and less damaging to healthy tissues.[456]

The aim is to describe a case series of ORN or MRONJ, surgically treated with Er:YAG laser, after management with aPDT, to present a therapeutic modality for lesions that might be difficult to resolve.

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A 75-year-old patient undergoing alendronate treatment for osteoporosis developed significant pain after the extraction of tooth 18, radiating to the middle third of the face. Clinically, there was exposed necrotic bone [Figure 1a] and the mucosa was hyperemic with edema. The combination of clinical data and the use of bisphosphonate led to the diagnosis of MRONJ.

Treatment was started with PBMT and aPDT [Figure 1b, c and Table 1]. From the first session, the patient reported an improvement in pain symptoms and the clinical aspect improved with each session, with a reduction in exposed necrotic tissue, inflammation, and edema [Figure 1a–d]. Case 2

A 65-year-old female patient had been taking alendronate to treat osteoporosis for 12 years, presented with significant pain symptoms and edema following the extraction of tooth 46. Physical examination showed tumefaction in the body of the mandible, with hyperemic mucosa and the presence of a fistula draining purulent secretion [Figure 1g], without clinically evident bone exposure.

The clinical and imaging findings led to the diagnosis of MRONJ. The patient had already been instructed on oral hygiene using antiseptics and had been medicated with cephalexin, but without clinical improvement. Treatment was then proposed with PBMT and aPDT using fiber optic irradiation through the fistula [Figure 1h and Table 1]. The procedures were carried out weekly, in a total of 11 sessions, reducing pain, inflammation and suppuration. Then surgical removal of the bone sequestration with a high-power Er:YAG laser was performed [Figure 1i and j and Table 1].

The surgical specimen was sent for anatomopathological analysis and the diagnosis of MRONJ was confirmed. The patient continued to receive the same PBMT and aPDT protocol for 11 sessions, then another necrotic bone fragment was spontaneously expelled [Figure 1k] and the intraoral fistula was completely closed [Figure 1l]. The patient is being followed up with no signs of recurrence 13 weeks after the fistula was closed.

Case 3

A 64-year-old HIV-positive male patient diagnosed with a tumor in the tonsil region was treated with surgical removal associated with six sessions of chemotherapy with cisplatin and 33 sessions of radiotherapy in the head and neck region. At the same time, the patient had been taking oral alendronate for five years to treat osteoporosis.

The patient sought treatment with exposed necrotic bone in the region of tooth 46 [Figure 1m], pain and edema in the region of the mandibular body. Due to the clinical history of radiotherapy in the region, the diagnosis of ORN was established. Treatment started with weekly PBMT and aPDT sessions [Figure 1n and Table 1].

After 38 sessions, when the condition had stabilized, showing a reduction in pain, erythema and exposed necrotic tissue [Figure 1o], the patient underwent debridement using a high-power Er:YAG laser [Figure 1p and q and Table 1] to remove the remaining necrotic bone and promote wound closure. The patient is currently undergoing weekly PBMT and aPDT sessions on the remaining bone exposure, with 12 sessions after surgery to the moment [Figure 1r].

DISCUSSION

Bone necrosis induced by radiation or drugs has a higher incidence in the mandible, frequently caused by local trauma, during or after cancer treatment, such as tooth extraction.[12] Therefore, it is important for the patient to seek a dentist before starting treatment with antiresorptive drugs or radiotherapy, to assess the need for tooth extraction and the elimination of infectious focus.[78]

Once installed, ORN and MRONJ are difficult to manage and significantly reduce the patient’s quality of life. The combination of low- and high-power lasers could be a promising treatment, as PBMT and aPDT can prepare the tissue before surgical intervention, reducing pain, inflammation, exposed necrotic tissue and microorganisms at the site. The combination of these therapies provides less invasive and less painful procedures compared to traditional treatments, halting the progression of the lesion and promoting tissue repair.[491011]

The use of PBMT and aPDT in the treatment of MRONJ has been successfully presented in the literature,[312] but the management of ORN through the combination of low and high-power lasers is still poorly reported.[4] The publication of new clinical cases is necessary to support future clinical studies, presenting new alternatives for the treatment of these conditions.

CONCLUSION

The outcomes of the clinical cases presented, with total resolution of bone necrosis in two of them, suggest that the combination of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy followed by debridement of the bone remnant with a high-power Er:YAG laser can be presented as an effective therapy for the management of challenging cases.
