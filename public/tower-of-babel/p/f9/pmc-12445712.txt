Contribution of high BMI to the global burden of cardiovascular disease among young adults aged 20-39 from 1990 to 2021: A systematic analysis for the Global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Jiaqi Hao, Mengkai Lu, Rui Sun, Xiuya Guan, Yuanlong Hu, Muxin Zhang, Zhiyuan Zhang, Xinhai Cui, Xin Wen, Chao Li, Chunxiao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding temporal trends in the burden of cardiovascular disease (CVD) attributable to high body mass index (BMI) in young adults is important for effective and targeted prevention strategies and measures. We aimed to provide comprehensive estimates of CVD deaths and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) rate attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years at the global, regional, and national levels, as well as temporal trends from 1990 to 2021.MethodsWe calculated age-standardized deaths rate and DALYs rate as well as estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study data on deaths and DALYs for CVD attributable to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years.ResultsAmong non-communicable diseases, the burden of cardiovascular disease attributed to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years is much higher than chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes and kidney diseases, digestive diseases, and neoplasms. Among specific types of cardiovascular disease, ischemic heart disease accounted for the highest proportion, while stroke had the largest increase in the rate of DALYs. an increase in age-standardized DALYs rate over time in low-to-middle SDI regions, a decrease in age-standardized DALYs rate over time in high SDI regions, and a decrease in health inequality in 2021 compared with 1990.ConclusionIn 2021, the high burden of cardiovascular disease attributed to high BMI among young adults varied by region, country, gender, and Socio-demographic index (SDI). The greater burden of cardiovascular disease among young adults in low - and middle-income countries may be linked to unhealthy diets, health awareness, and lifestyles among young adults in low - and middle-income countries. Given the potential for improvement in high BMI, initiatives targeting this risk factor could significantly reduce the burden of CVD. Thus, prioritizing cost-effective policies and interventions is urgently needed.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Young adults, High body mass index, Global

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death globally, and according to statistics, 17.9 million people died from cardiovascular disease in 2019, accounting for 32 % of all deaths worldwide [1]. In a series of patients <50 years of age admitted to hospital for myocardial infarction, one-fifth were <40 years of age, so young adults (20–39 years of age) are also at high risk for CVD [2].

There are many risk factors for CVD, such as smoking, high blood pressure, high blood sugar, hypercholesterolemia, and high BMI [3]. CVD accounts for more than two-thirds of deaths due to high BMI [4]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, 2.5 billion adults (18 years and older) will be overweight, of which 890 million will be obese. Global adult obesity has more than doubled since 1990 [5]. High BMI is defined in the GBD database as greater than 20–23 kg/m2. It is estimated that in 2019, high BMI caused 5 million deaths from non-communicable diseases such as CVD, diabetes, cancer, neurological disorders, chronic respiratory diseases, and digestive diseases [6].

Obesity among young adults is therefore a huge challenge to global health. In addition to health risks, obesity in young adults carries a huge socio-economic burden [7]. A cost-of-illness approach to estimating the economic impact of overweight and obesity on countries suggests that total economic costs will quadruple in high-income countries between 2019 and 2060, and increase 12–25 times in low- and middle-income countries. Reductions in the prevalence of overweight and obesity can reduce economic impacts [8].

A timely understanding of the global burden of CVD in young adults due to high BMI is therefore critical for policymakers to inform the implementation of primary prevention and control. Based on data from GBD 2021, we explored the association between high BMI and CVD deaths, DALYs rate among young adults aged 20–39 years by region, country, gender, and social development index and further analyzed trends in health inequalities in this burden, in the hope that our findings will inform policymaking and efforts to prevent, treat, and manage CVD globally.

Methods

Estimates of CVD deaths, DALYs rate, and the number of DALYs attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years were obtained using the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). This study utilized age-stratified data from the GBD 2021, which encompasses pooled health data from 360 billion person-years of observation across 204 countries and territories. Our analysis was restricted to individuals aged 20–39 years, representing a subset of 108 billion person-years of observation (approximately 30.06 % of the total GBD database) after applying standard GBD quality controls.

The SDI is a composite measure used to determine where a country or other geographic area falls within the development spectrum. Expressed on a scale of 0 to 1, it is a composite average of the per capita income, average educational attainment, and fertility rankings of all regions in the GBD study. The 204 countries and territories are categorized into five groups based on the SDI quintiles: low SDI, low-middle SDI, middle SDI, medium-high SDI, and high SDI regions.

To account for measurement error, potential bias, and modeling-induced uncertainty, a 95 % uncertainty interval (UI) was used for all estimates. UI analyses were used in the GBD to address heterogeneity that may arise from sampling error and nonsampling variance.

The study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2013). Ethical approval was not required as the study was based on public data and did not involve any personal information.

Statistical analysis

Deaths, DALYs rate, and corresponding age-standardized rate (ASR) were used to assess trends in high BMI-associated CVD in young adults aged 20–39 years. DALYs were calculated by summing the years lived with disability (YLDs) and years of life lost (YLLs) [9].

The calculation of the ASR is based on the age structure of a standard population and is therefore a necessary and representative indicator when considering differences in the age structure of multiple populations. When comparing deaths and DALYs rate, the ASR is an important indicator that effectively excludes the influence of age factors between regions or countries with different age structures. Results

Comparison of time trends in CVD burden attributable to 12 risk factors among individuals aged 20–39 and all ages,1990–2021

In terms of the EAPC at a global level, high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose, as key cardiovascular risk factors, exhibit a significant upward trend in deaths rate and DALYs among the young population aged 20–39 years, as well as across all ages. Notably, the increase is significantly more pronounced in males than in females. High BMI-attributable DALYs exhibited significantly faster growth in young adult males compared to the all-age population, highlighting the urgent need to enhance the prevention and control of metabolic risk factors among young adults. Occupational risk factors demonstrate marked age-related differences, with a declining trend observed in the 20–39 age group, while an upward trend is evident across all ages. This may reflect the protective effects of occupational safety measures on younger individuals and the cumulative impact of exposure on older populations. These findings underscore the necessity for targeted intervention strategies (Supplementary Table 1).

Global burden of non-communicable diseases attributable to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years, 1990–2021

From 1990 to 2021, the number of non-communicable disease deaths and deaths rate due to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years increased each year, with the burden of CVD being much higher than that of chronic respiratory disease, diabetes and kidney disease, digestive disease, and neoplasms. The number of CVD deaths increased from 24,846 (95 % UI: 12,781, 38,778) in 1990 to 52,311 (95 % UI: 25,366, 80,129) in 2021; the deaths rate per 100,000 population increased from 1.13 (95 % UI: 0.58, 1.77) in 1990 to 1.76 (95 % UI: 0.85, 2.69) (Fig. 1).

Trends in the burden and duration of CVD attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years in 204 countries and territories, 1990–2021

In 2021, among 204 countries globally, India and China had the highest number of CVD deaths and DALYs due to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years, with India having 7750.05 (95 % UI: 3384.67, 12,294.61) deaths and high number of DALYs of 451,027.55 (95 % UI: 193,575.87, 720,132.81), China deaths were 6994.30 (95 % UI: 2740.54, 11,947.93), and the number of DALYs reached 428,311.41 (95 % UI: 163,250.78, 729,415.65). CVD deaths and DALYs rate per 100,000 population attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years were notably elevated in American Samoa and Egypt, with deaths rate of 8.78 (95 % UI: 3.41, 14.38) and 8.60 (95 % UI:4.07, 13.08), respectively, and DALYs rate of 519.66 (95 % UI: 193.38, 844.42) and 513.50 (95 % UI: 241.37, 778.72) (Supplementary Figure 1).

In terms of annual percentage changes in country-level estimates, DALYs for CVD due to high BMI showed an increasing trend in 99 countries, a decreasing trend in 85 countries, and remained stable in the others from 1990–2021, with the most pronounced increase in the rate of DALYs occurring in Zimbabwe 6.50 (95 % CI: 5.46, 7.56), followed by Bangladesh 4.96 (95 %CI: 4.56, 5.36) and Viet Nam 4.30 (95 %CI:3.68, 4.92), which also increased rapidly, and the countries with a more pronounced decrease in the rate of DALYs were Luxembourg −4.68 (95 %CI:−5.03, −4.33), Switzerland −4.2 (95 %CI: −4.48, −3.92) and Norway −4.05 (95 %CI: −4.32, −3.77) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, CVD deaths due to high BMI in young adults showed the same trend as the DALYs rate (Supplementary Figure 2).

Temporal trends in the burden of 5 specific CVD attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years

There are five main categories of CVD, namely aortic aneurysm, atrial fibrillation and flutter, hypertensive heart disease, ischemic heart disease, and stroke, with ischemic heart disease having the highest burden of CVD attributable to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years, and globally, ischemic heart disease accounted for 61.2 % of the total number of deaths from CVD, and 58.2 % of the total number of DALYs from CVD, of which the burden of aortic aneurysm and atrial fibrillation and flutter disease attributable to high BMI in young adults accounted for a relatively small portion of the burden of CVD (Fig. 3A).

Discussion

Our research has found that among the 12 risk factors attributable to CDV, the deaths rate and DALYs rate caused by high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose are on the rise, with a more significant increase in younger men than in younger women. This indicates the importance of strengthening the prevention and control of metabolic risk factors for young adults aged 20–39. Supporting this, our findings show that both deaths and DALYs from CVD attributable to high BMI in this age group have more than doubled globally between 1990 and 2021, with deaths and DALYs rate trending upward in most countries and regions, and the burden being notably higher in young males than in females. In terms of specific types of CVD, the rate of DALYs for stroke attributable to high BMI was the fastest rising among young adults, and rate of DALYs for atrial fibrillation and flutter attributable to high BMI among young adults were trending upward and increasing substantially in all 5 SDI and 21 GBD regions globally, but because atrial fibrillation and flutter account for a small proportion of total CVD attributable to high BMI, deaths, and DALYs were small, so both deaths and DALYs rate are low.

A large body of evidence suggests that the association between high BMI and CVD is explained by an increased risk of common cardiovascular risk factors in obese adults, such as increased prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [11]. Studies are showing a significant increase in overweight and obesity in low- and middle-income countries [12], consistent with our study. In terms of SDI, the burden of CVD associated with high BMI among young adults in low SDI, such as Oceania, Western Sub-Saharan Africa, and Southeast Asia, has risen over time.

Whereas high SDI, such as Australasia, Eastern Europe, and High-income Asia Pacific, showed a decreasing trend in the burden of CVD associated with high BMI, except for High-income North America, a high SDI region, which differed from the pattern in other high-income countries. High-income North America favors a more “Western diet” [13]. By “Western diet” we usually mean a high intake of refined carbohydrates, added sugars, fats, and foods of animal origin. As a result, the burden of CVD among young adults in High-income North America with high BMI is much higher than expected.

The significant penetration of some transnational supermarket chains into low- and middle-income countries has led to an increase in access to cheaper processed, high-fat, added-sugar, and salt-laden foods in low- and middle-income countries [14]. The affordability of “ready-to-eat” foods and the increase in fast food chains have made ready-made foods and highly sweetened beverages more convenient and popular among young adults, whose unhealthy dietary patterns predispose them to obesity [14]. This also leads to increased exposure to CVD risk factors, which accounts for the increased burden of CVD caused by high BMI among young adults in low- and middle-income countries. A systematic evaluation and meta-analysis suggest that healthier dietary patterns are more expensive than less healthy ones [15]. In low-middle SDI regions, dietary patterns are less healthy, so the risk of CVD is higher than in high SDI groups [16,17]. High SDI countries have sufficient financial resources to launch programs and initiatives aimed at promoting healthy foods [15].

Public health and policy efforts should be aimed at reducing the price of healthier dietary patterns, and some studies have shown that reducing the price of healthier dietary patterns improves food and nutrient consumption and increases health benefits in low and middle SDI groups [18]. Therefore, dietary modification is a key way to reduce the prevalence of obesity and, similarly, the risk of CVD, especially in the context of continued reductions in physical activity and increases in sedentary time.

Regarding the obesity paradox, i.e., obese patients with CVD have a better prognosis compared to non-overweight/non-obese patients [19]. However, some researchers have suggested that being overweight does not seem to be associated with a longer lifespan and a greater burden of CVD over that lifespan. The obesity paradox appears to be caused primarily by early diagnosis of CVD [20].

While reducing high BMI is theoretically feasible through lifestyle interventions, practical implementation remains challenging due to moderating factors such as socioeconomic disparities and public health policies. To achieve effective BMI management, strategies must integrate these multifaceted determinants. Strategies to address the threat of CVD due to high BMI in young adults aged 20–39 years are threefold: (1) Address modifiable risk factors for BMI, such as improving dietary patterns, reducing consumption of high-calorie, high-fat and high-sugar beverages, and increasing intake of healthy foods such as pulses, coarse grains, and vegetables; and increasing physical activity and avoiding sedentary patterns of physical activity. (2) Address the social factors that influence BMI, such as the unbalanced economic environment in low- and middle-income countries, the large number of fast-food choices, and the limited space for safe outdoor recreation; provide economic incentives and disincentives for agricultural producers, food manufacturers and retailers, and consumer choices, and reduce these environmental factors that lead to high BMI. (3) States develop public health measures and interventions to invest more in risk prevention, such as taxing high-calorie foods and sugary beverages or subsidizing healthy foods to encourage consumption.

Limitation

This study analyzed the burden of high BMI in CVD in young adults based on GBD data for 2021. Although high BMI can be defined as obesity, other indicators related to the definition of obesity, such as waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat index, etc., were not included in the data set. The overall quality of GBD estimates is fundamentally dependent on the accuracy of the data used in modeling, and the reliability of the registered data may be questionable in some countries.

Conclusion

From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of CVD due to high BMI among young adults aged 20–39 years was increasing. Although global health inequality is declining, young adults in low - and middle-income countries bear a greater burden of CVD, which may be related to unhealthy diets, health awareness, and lifestyle among young adults in low - and middle-income countries. There is an urgent need for targeted development and implementation of effective strategies and interventions to reduce the burden of CVD in young adults due to high BMI and to reduce the expenditure of national healthcare resources in this area.
