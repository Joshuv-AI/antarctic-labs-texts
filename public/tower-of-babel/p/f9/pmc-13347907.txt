Evaluating the role of youth-onset diabetes mellitus in the context of food insecurity and preventive care: a repeated cross-sectional study using propensity score weighted machine learning analysis
Authors: Yu-Sheng Lee, Raymond C Stetson, Heaven Hollender, Matthew Evan Sprong, Xueli Huang, Karan Kumar, Urja Nimitkumar Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouth-onset diabetes mellitus (DM) is an increasing public health concern, especially among adolescents facing socioeconomic challenges. Food insecurity (FI), a key social determinant of health, has been linked to adverse metabolic outcomes, but its association with youth-onset DM and the role of preventive care remain unclear.MethodsUsing repeated cross-sectional data from the National Survey of Children’s Health (2016–2021), we examined the association between FI status (food secure, mild FI and moderate-to-severe FI) and youth-onset DM among adolescents aged 10–17 years. We also evaluated whether usual preventive care modified this association. Propensity score weighting (PSW) was used to reduce the imbalance of measured confounders across FI groups. These weights were applied in the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator logistic regression for variable selection and in the final multivariable logistic regression models.ResultsIn the PSW analysis of 66 560 adolescents, 432 had youth-onset DM. Moderate-to-severe FI was associated with higher odds of DM than food-secure adolescents (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.35 to 1.77; p<0.0001), whereas mild FI was not associated with youth-onset DM. Among adolescents with preventive care visits, both mild FI (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.37; p=0.0126) and moderate-to-severe FI (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.46 to 1.92; p<0.0001) were associated with higher odds of DM. Among those without preventive care visits, FI was associated with lower odds of having a reported DM diagnosis, especially in the moderate-to-severe FI group (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.35; p=0.0002).ConclusionsModerate-to-severe FI was associated with youth-onset DM, and this relationship varied by access to preventive care. Adolescents with FI who lack regular preventive care may have fewer opportunities for DM diagnosis. Public health strategies should integrate FI screening, equitable access to preventive services and culturally responsive approaches to chronic disease prevention.Keywords: Adolescent Health, Endocrinology, Epidemiology, Health services research, Machine Learning

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) in children and adolescents has emerged as a growing public health concern, with increasing global prevalence and serious long-term health consequences. In the USA, type 1 diabetes (T1D) accounts for about 86% of all diabetes cases among youth aged 10–19 years, whereas type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounts for a smaller but increasing proportion. In 2017, the prevalence of T1D among youth was 21.5 per 10 000, while the prevalence of T2D among youth increased from 3.4 per 10 000 in 2001 to 6.7 per 10 000 in 2017.1 2 Around 18% of adolescents aged 12–18 had pre-diabetes between 2005 and 2016.3 A complex interplay of genetic, behavioural and social determinants of health (SDoH) influences the development and management of youth-onset DM.4

Food insecurity (FI) is a foundational SDoH, characterised by inconsistent access to sufficient food for a healthy life.5 In the USA, FI is primarily associated with economic hardship, especially low income and financial constraints that limit regular access to adequate food. Studies indicate that roughly 17.3% of children experienced household FI.6 Children and adolescents struggling with FI are prone to malnutrition and metabolic issues, along with poorer physical and mental health outcomes and disparities. FI is also linked to higher haemoglobin A1c levels in adolescents.7 Although studies have related FI to DM among adolescents, findings from nationally representative studies remain inconclusive.8 9 Moreover, the role of healthcare access, particularly access to paediatric preventive care, in moderating this relationship remains underexplored.10 11 Although studies have found that early screening for T1D is beneficial, the benefits of pre-T2D screening are mixed. The US Preventive Services Task Force found no direct evidence that screening asymptomatic children and adolescents for T2D or pre-diabetes improves health outcomes.12 Even so, indirect evidence suggests that identifying pre-diabetes in youth remains valuable13; it is especially beneficial for those children and adolescents facing household FI, provided they can access healthcare visits and receive support for preventive services.11

Since FI is influenced by social factors rather than occurring randomly, children and adolescents with different FI categories might systematically differ in socioeconomic and household characteristics associated with youth-onset DM.5 6 Therefore, a propensity score weighting (PSW) method was employed to reduce the imbalance of measured confounders across FI categories in this study.14,16 We further examined whether access to preventive care services influenced this association. Our research questions are: (1) Is FI among adolescents associated with youth-onset DM? (2) How does consistent access to usual preventive care services influence the equity of diagnosis of DM in FI and food-secure adolescents?

Materials and methods

Study design and source of data

This repeated cross-sectional study design used the US National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH) database from 2016 to 2021, following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to ensure methodological rigour and transparency in reporting. Since 2016, NSCH data files can be combined to increase the analytic sample size and to investigate time-series trends using multiple years of non-overlapping sampling data. The NSCH surveyed children aged 0–17, collecting information about health conditions, families, neighbourhoods, access to healthcare and social contexts across 50 US states and the District of Columbia, a federal district that is not part of any state but is included as a separate population source.17

Participants

The 2016 NSCH cohort was conducted from June 2016 to February 2017, screening 139 923 households, of which 67 047 were eligible, resulting in 50 212 completed child-level interviews. The 2017 cohort ran from August 2017 to February 2018, during which 59 135 households were screened; of these, 29 343 were deemed eligible, resulting in 21 599 completed interviews. The 2018 NSCH ran from June 2018 to January 2019, screening 176 052 households; 38 140 were deemed eligible, and 30 530 interviews were completed. The 2019 NSCH was conducted from June 2019 to January 2020, screening 180 000 households, of which 35 760 were eligible, resulting in 29 433 completed interviews. The 2020 NSCH took place from June 2020 to January 2021, screening 93 840 households, yielding 51 107 eligible participants and 42 777 completed interviews. Lastly, the 2021 NSCH cohort was conducted from July 2021 to January 2022, screening 106 000 households; 62 010 were deemed eligible, and 50 892 interviews were completed. The overall response rates range from 86% to 92%, with a total of 225 443 valid interviews conducted. The NSCH compared response rates across various demographic and socioeconomic subgroups to highlight disparities in participation. The weighting process began with a base household weight, adjusted for screener and child-level non-response, subsampling and participation in topical surveys. Final adjustments included ranking to demographic controls and trimming of extreme weights. Despite some respondent-non-respondent differences, these steps effectively reduced non-response bias and improved the survey’s representativeness.17 18 Additional information can be found on the survey’s associated website at https://www.childhealthdata.org/learn-about-the-nsch/NSCH.

In this study, ‘adolescents’ were defined as individuals aged 10–17 years.2 We excluded individuals aged 0–9 years due to the low prevalence of T2D in this age group and clinical guidelines recommending screening starting at age 10 or the onset of puberty.2 19 In addition, children generally rely on their parents for health behaviours, such as diet, activity and healthcare visits, whereas adolescents have more autonomy, making the social determinants, including FI and preventive care visits, more directly measurable and interpretable.11 20 After excluding those with missing data on FI status, DM or covariates used in the propensity score (PS) model, 66 560 adolescents aged 10–17 were included in the subsequent analysis (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Outcome and covariates

The primary outcome of this current study was youth-onset DM. The question that NSCH asked parents of children was, ‘Has a doctor or other healthcare provider ever told you that this child has diabetes?’ If the answer was ‘yes’, the survey then asked: ‘Does this child currently have diabetes?’ If both responses were ‘yes’, the adolescent was considered to have youth-onset DM.

The 27 covariates included adolescent-level factors (age, race/ethnicity, preterm birth, physical activity, comorbid conditions such as allergy to food, drug or insect, cystic fibrosis, asthma, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, overweight or obese, heart condition, chronic physical pain, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, anxiety, depression), parental and household characteristics (parent’s highest educational level, parent’s health status, adult smoking in the household and type of insurance), access to care (usual source of paediatric preventive care) and SDoH (household income, FI, household food and cash assistance, neighbourhood access to sidewalks, parks/playgrounds and recreation centres), as well as cumulative adverse childhood experiences (ACE) score.

FI was estimated based on the question, ‘Which of these statements best describes your household’s ability to afford the food you need during the past 12 months?’ The responses were: (1) we could always afford to eat good nutritious meals, (2) we could always afford enough to eat but not always the kinds of food we should eat, (3) sometimes we could not afford enough to eat and (4) often we could not afford enough to eat. Adolescents who responded (3) or (4) were defined as moderately to severely FI, while those who reported (2) were considered to have mild FI. Adolescents who reported that (1) they could always afford to eat good nutritious meals were categorised as no FI.21 Usual source of paediatric preventive care was derived from the question, ‘During the past 12 months, how many times did this child visit a doctor, nurse, or other healthcare professional to receive a preventive check-up?’ The responses were ‘one or more’ versus ‘none’. We defined one or more as having a usual source of paediatric preventive care.

Statistical analysis methods

Continuous variables were summarised as the mean and SD. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%). In table 1, p values were obtained from univariate logistic regression models assessing the association of each variable with youth-onset DM after PSW, rather than from tests comparing FI groups. A false discovery rate of 0.05 was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Covariate balance after weighting was evaluated using absolute standardised differences, with values >0.10 indicating residual imbalance.

Propensity score weighting

The PSW was used to create a pseudo-population in which the distributions of measured covariates were more similar across FI groups, thereby improving the validity of group comparisons and reducing potential confounding effects.14 15 Because the primary goal of this approach was to reduce measured confounding by improving covariate comparability across FI groups, the weighted estimates should be interpreted as associations in the complete-case PSW pseudo-population rather than as directly nationally representative estimates of US adolescents. Accordingly, we did not construct a combined survey-weighted and propensity-weighted estimator for population-representative inference. Since FI was classified into three categories (food secure, mild FI and moderate-to-severe FI), PSs were estimated for this three-level exposure using generalised boosted models (GBMs) based on measured covariates, including adolescent characteristics, parental and household factors, health conditions, access to care, SDoH and ACE scores. The average treatment effect weights derived from the estimated PSs were applied to improve comparability of covariates across the groups before analysing the association between FI and youth-onset DM. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed across FI groups using absolute standardised differences; values >0.10 indicated residual imbalance.16 PSW derived from the GBMs was applied in subsequent analyses. Specifically, the estimated average treatment effect weights were incorporated into the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regressions for variable selection and into the final logistic regression models examining the association between FI and youth-onset DM. However, the original NSCH survey sampling weights were not combined with the PSWs, and no combined analytic weight was used. These weighted analyses were used to examine the association between FI status and youth-onset DM and to evaluate whether access to preventive care services influenced this association. Accordingly, the reported ORs reflect associations in the complete-case PSW pseudo-population rather than in the full original NSCH adolescent sample.

LASSO logistic regression model

To identify predictors of youth-onset DM, we used LASSO logistic regression, which incorporates a penalty to control model complexity and reduce overfitting, thereby improving generalisation. Unlike traditional regression, LASSO adds a constraint to coefficient estimates. The dataset was randomly split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets, and variable selection was performed using 10-fold cross-validation. In this process, the data are divided into 10 subsets, with each serving as a validation set while the rest form the training set. This is repeated 10 times, and the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was chosen based on cross-validation performance.17 22 After applying the LASSO variable selection, we used the selected variables in a multivariable logistic regression to estimate the OR along with the 95% CIs for youth-onset DM. We further investigated the modification effects between FI and usual preventive care visits.

Missing data

For variables with missing values, we used a complete-case approach (listwise deletion). Observations with missing data on FI, youth-onset DM or any covariate included in the PS model were excluded because the implemented PS estimation procedure required complete covariate information.23 This complete-case restriction was applied before PS estimation; therefore, the reduction in sample size did not reflect trimming, lack of overlap or exclusion based on propensity score values.

All analyses were performed in SAS package V.9.4 (SAS Institute) or the R package V.4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2024. R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna; https://www.R-project.org/).

Results

Study sample and weighted characteristics

Out of 111 910 eligible participants, 66 560 were included in the complete-case analysis, while 45 350 (40.5%) were excluded due to missing data on FI, youth-onset DM or covariates in the PS model. Compared with those included, excluded participants were more socially disadvantaged and had a slightly higher prevalence of youth-onset diabetes (online supplemental table 1). Table 1 presents the weighted characteristics of the complete-case analytic sample after PSW. In this complete-case analytic sample, 432 adolescents had diabetes and 66 128 did not. A total of 13 492 (20.3%) adolescents reported household FI, including 12 065 with mild FI and 1427 with moderate-to-severe FI. Among the 432 adolescents with youth-onset DM, 118 (27.3%) came from households experiencing mild or moderate-to-severe FI.

Main association between FI and youth-onset DM

The LASSO logistic regression method selected eight variables: FI, usual source of paediatric preventive care, ACE score, overweight or obese, allergy to food, drug or insect, asthma, cystic fibrosis and heart condition as predictors for youth-onset DM in the final parsimonious model with an optimal minimum λ of 0.001193 (online supplemental figure 1). FI was found to be independently associated with youth-onset DM. Specifically, adolescents from households with mild FI had 1.12-fold higher odds of having a reported diagnosis of youth-onset diabetes (95% CI 0.98 to 1.29, p value=0.0926), whereas those from households with moderate-to-severe FI had 1.55-fold higher odds (95% CI 1.35 to 1.77, p value <0.0001) than those from food-secure households. Adolescents without a usual source of preventive care were less likely to be diagnosed with DM (OR=0.13, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.18, p value <0.0001). Allergy to food, drugs or insects, as well as being overweight or obese, were also identified by LASSO as risk factors for youth-onset DM, with OR=2.61 (95% CI 2.33 to 2.91, p value <0.0001) and OR=1.41 (95% CI 1.26 to 1.58, p value <0.0001), respectively (table 2).

Effect modification by preventive care access

Although FI was found to be associated with youth-onset DM, this association was influenced by the availability of a usual source of paediatric preventive care visits. Among adolescents who had regular preventive care visits, those from households with mild FI (OR=1.19, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.37, p value=0.0126) and moderate-to-severe FI (OR=1.67, 95% CI 1.46 to 1.92, p value <0.0001) had higher odds of a reported diabetes diagnosis than those from food-secure households. Among adolescents without regular preventive care visits, the association was reversed, that is, those from households with moderate-to-severe FI had the lowest odds of a reported diabetes diagnosis (OR=0.11, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.35, p value=0.0002), and those from households with mild FI also had lower odds (OR=0.27, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.58, p value=0.0007) than those from food-secure households (figure 3A). Sensitivity analysis

Because cystic fibrosis was rare in the study population and no cases were observed in the moderate-to-severe FI group, we repeated the multivariable logistic regression after excluding cystic fibrosis from the model. The results remained essentially the same, indicating that the sparse data on cystic fibrosis did not affect the main outcomes (online supplemental table 3). We also evaluated the robustness of the observed associations in analyses without PSW. The direction of the associations remained consistent with the main PSW results, although the estimated ORs were somewhat higher in the unweighted model (online supplemental table 4).

Discussion

The US NSCH data were used to investigate the association between FI and youth-onset DM using PSW and LASSO logistic regression. Among the 66 560 adolescents aged 10–17 included in the complete-case PSW analytic sample, 13 492 (20.27%) reported household FI, including 12 065 with mild FI and 1427 with moderate-to-severe FI. We found a significant association between household FI and youth-onset DM, while demonstrating that access to usual paediatric preventive care modifies this association. Adolescents from households with FI, particularly those experiencing moderate-to-severe FI, faced a higher risk of youth-onset DM compared with those from households without FI. This finding is consistent with previous research linking FI to adverse metabolic outcomes, including obesity, metabolic syndrome and T2D in both children and adults. Prior studies have also highlighted that households with FI often rely on low-cost, energy-dense foods, which contribute to poor dietary quality, increased obesity risk and insulin resistance, which are precursors to diabetes.7 8 24

Additionally, the association between overweight/obesity and youth-onset DM identified in this study is well documented in the literature. Obesity is a significant risk factor for insulin resistance and T2D in children, emphasising the importance of weight management in diabetes prevention.25 Allergy to food, drugs or insects was positively associated with youth-onset DM in the final model, consistent with recent evidence linking chronic inflammation to metabolic dysfunction.26 These findings reinforce FI as an SDoH influencing metabolic regulation and disease risk.27 Although previous studies linked secondhand smoke (SHS) to diabetes, we did not find an association after adjusting for covariates. This discrepancy may be due to differences in SHS measurement; our study used adult household smokers as a proxy, which did not capture exposure intensity, unlike prior research that used self-reports or biomarker data (eg, serum cotinine levels) to accurately measure it.28

One novel aspect of this current study is the effect modification between FI and access to the usual source of paediatric preventive care. Adolescents with FI who had regular visits were more likely to be diagnosed with DM, while those without access were underdiagnosed. This may be because households with FI had fewer resources, such as insurance and financial support, and less knowledge about the disease, compared with households without FI, when accessing healthcare services. This underdiagnosis could put adolescents at risk of uncontrolled blood sugar levels, potentially further harming their health.29 A study found children from households with FI were 2–3 times more likely to delay or avoid healthcare than food-secure ones. This delay in care can lead to the progression of undiagnosed conditions.30 Although previous studies suggested that early screening is not beneficial for T2D youth,12 our results suggest that adolescents in food-insecure households lacking regular preventive care were often missed in early detection. This emphasises the importance of regular preventive visits in the community.31 These findings point to a dual burden for adolescents living in food-insecure households and emphasise the need for equitable preventive care to address these disparities.

Although not typically labelled as SDoH, preventive care reflects broader structural factors like healthcare access, insurance, parental education and socioeconomic stability.32 It serves as a proxy for disparities in SDoH, which can worsen for households with FI, leading to missed opportunities for early detection of conditions like diabetes. Integrating FI screening into routine paediatric preventive care, as recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (Screening Technical Assistance and Resource Center: Developmental Screening 2025), may facilitate early intervention by connecting families to nutritional and community healthcare resources. Moreover, combining food assistance with community-based nutrition education and diabetes prevention programmes may yield synergistic benefits in reducing DM risk.33 Ensuring equitable access to both adequate nutrition and preventive healthcare services is essential for reducing the burden of youth-onset DM.34 A policy approach that integrates social support and health systems can promote health equity and better long-term outcomes.35

Addressing the complex interplay between FI, healthcare access and youth-onset DM requires a multifaceted public health approach, including family and community contexts.11 Although federal programmes such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Programme and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Programme for Women, Infants, and Children have historically improved FI and diet quality, threats to funding, like proposed Medicaid cuts, call for innovative, decentralised solutions. In the face of shrinking federal safety nets, state and local governments, community coalitions, non-profits and schools must lead in promoting health equity for at-risk youth. Expanding Medicaid preventive coverage, investing in school and community health programmes and removing barriers such as transportation and language access can aid early screening and management.33 34 36 37 When strategically implemented, state-level Medicaid may allow more flexibility in expanding preventive service coverage without relying on federal mandates. Additionally, public-private partnerships, including those with healthcare systems, food banks, community health centres and technology providers, can help fill service gaps.38 39 For instance, collaborations between Federally Qualified Health Centres and local food distribution programmes can integrate nutritional support with paediatric care and diabetes prevention education. School-based health services—mobile units, trained nurses and integrated health education—are essential, especially in low-income areas.40 Furthermore, municipal policies, like taxes on sugary drinks, zoning for healthy foods and investments in walkable infrastructure, can also lower DM risk at the population level.

Although this study has several strengths, including the use of a large national survey dataset and the application of propensity score weighting to improve the balance of measured confounders across FI groups, it also has limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the NSCH data limits causal inferences; however, it still allows the assessment of associations between FI and childhood DM. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm causal pathways. Second, parent-reported DM status by healthcare providers may potentially lead to misclassification and recall bias. Third, the NSCH data were unable to differentiate between specific types of diabetes (T1D vs T2D), limiting disease-specific insights. Fourth, unmeasured factors such as dietary intake and genetic predisposition were not included in the analysis. Additionally, cystic fibrosis was rare in the analytic sample, with no cases observed in the moderate-to-severe FI group. Therefore, the estimate in the original model should be interpreted with caution due to potential sparse-data bias. However, sensitivity analyses excluding cystic fibrosis yielded largely unchanged results, indicating that the main association between FI and youth-onset diabetes was not driven by this sparse covariate. Fifth, while the study highlights the role of access to preventive care, it does not assess the quality of care received, which could be a key factor in diabetes management and prevention. Sixth, this study used a complete-case approach before propensity score estimation, excluding 45 350 of 111 910 eligible adolescents (40.5%). Because excluded participants were more likely to experience FI and social disadvantage and had a slightly higher prevalence of youth-onset diabetes, selection bias cannot be ruled out. Therefore, our findings may not generalise to the full eligible NSCH adolescent population and may underestimate the association between FI and youth-onset diabetes. Lastly, the study used data from the US population. Therefore, caution is necessary when applying these findings outside the USA, as differences in demographics, healthcare systems and other factors may influence their generalisability.17

Conclusion

The results signify the importance of viewing youth-onset DM not merely as a clinical condition, but as one deeply embedded in broader structural and social determinants. Preventive care utilisation, while not a traditional SDoH, is a powerful indicator of healthcare equity. When access to routine preventive services is lacking, especially among food-insecure populations, opportunities for early identification, diagnosis and intervention are lost, placing vulnerable youth at heightened risk of adverse long-term health outcomes. Given current threats to federal public health funding, including proposed cuts to Medicaid and other safety-net programmes, alternative strategies must be prioritised. Community-level interventions, state Medicaid innovations, school-based health initiatives and public-private partnerships can offer viable and scalable approaches to fill service gaps. Additionally, as recommended by leading health organisations, incorporating routine FI screening into paediatric preventive care can serve as a critical entry point for connecting families to nutritional, behavioural and clinical resources.

Overall, this study advances the understanding of how intersecting social and healthcare factors contribute to youth-onset DM. It calls for a coordinated public health response that ensures equitable access to both adequate nutrition and consistent preventive care. Without addressing these underlying structural barriers, efforts to reduce the burden of youth-onset DM will remain incomplete. Future research should explore longitudinal data, assess the quality of care and examine culturally responsive intervention models that integrate food security, chronic disease prevention and community health equity.
