Consolidated bioprocessing of inulin-containing feedstock to succinic acid by newly isolated Enterobacter sp. GAP1
Authors: Marcin Podleśny, Tomasz Szymczak, Jagoda Kucharska, Monika Szymajda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Enterobacter strain which can directly utilize inulin was isolated from soil environment for succinic acid biosynthesis.•Co-fermentation cultures were done using seven carbon sources including inulin and glycerol.•The possibility of submerged and semi-solid fermentation was demonstrated, depending on the consistency of the inulin-containing substrate (liquid or solid-state biomass).•Up to 29.4 g/L SA from inulin YSA = 0.43 g/g; PSA = 0.153 g/L*h and 17.9 g/L from ground and dried JA tubers YSA = 0.47 g/g; PSA = 0.147 g/L*h were obtained.•Jerusalem artichoke was first used as a non-grain substrate for SA production in consolidated bioprocess (CBP).Keywords: Succinic acid, Inulin, Enterobacter, Jerusalem artichoke, Fermentation

Introduction

Consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) is the most economically viable strategy for the industrial fermentation of raw materials that cannot be utilised directly in the fermentation process. It is an excellent technology that reduces the number of unit operations and the overall capital cost of the process [27]. The main challenge with this technology is finding a suitable microorganism that can hydrolyse the feedstocks and produce the desired product from the saccharified sugars. One example of a raw material that is biotechnologically desirable but demanding is inulin, which is mainly found in the roots and tubers of selected plants, such as chicory, dandelion, dahlia or Jerusalem artichoke [36]. Chemically, inulin is a mixture of oligo- and polysaccharide chains consisting of fructose monomers linked together by β(2→1) bonds [11]. In addition to its numerous nutritional and medical applications [4,29] this fructan is increasingly being used as a desirable raw material for the biosynthesis of ecological fuels and green chemicals, such as second-generation bioethanol, 2,3-butanediol, lactic acid and succinic acid ([8,9]; [39]). The non-grain crop nature of inulin sources is another important advantage in an era of intense competition for raw materials between the fuel and food industries. In this context, Jerusalem artichoke is of particular interest as it can grow in infertile soil and is resistant to plant diseases, thus avoiding competition with grain crops for arable land [3]. It has been demonstrated that inulin-type fructans in Jerusalem artichoke can be converted into ethanol via direct fermentation using Kluyveromyces marxianus yeast with endogenous inulinase [48] and Saccharomyces cerevisiae [16]. However, there are currently no reports on consolidated bioprocessing for succinic acid production using inulin as a feedstock. Developing such a solution would enable an effective process to be proposed for producing one of the main products of green chemistry, succinic acid, using a plant-based raw material whose cultivation does not compete with food crops. Developing an efficient process for the microbiological synthesis of succinic acid from inulin would open up a much wider range of applications, as this acid could be used to produce many other industrially important chemical compounds currently obtained from petroleum-derived raw materials. Using succinic acid, which is easily biodegradable, to replace some of the petroleum-derived raw materials could significantly reduce the level of environmental pollution caused by chemical plants synthesising them [20]. The availability of adequate amounts of affordable succinic acid would enable the environmentally friendly production of polyols and polyurethanes, including the production of fully biodegradable polybutylene succinate. Bio-succinic acid could also be used to make the chemical synthesis processes of unsaturated polyester resins, alkyd resins, additives for lubricants and oils, surfactants, and plasticisers more environmentally friendly [6].

After the carbon source selection, the second most important element in a well-selected microbiological fermentation medium is the nitrogen source [30]. Among the nitrogen compounds used in biotechnology, both organic and inorganic nitrogen sources can be found. The former, in addition to nitrogen of organic origin, often contain additional growth factors, such as vitamins or mineral salts [41]. The latter, in turn, most often only contribute inorganic nitrogen to the nutrient balance, but are characterised by a favourable purchase price [47]. The nitrogen source chosen for the process largely depends on the needs of the production microorganism and the metabolic pathway involved in producing the desired metabolite.

Bacteria that can efficiently produce succinic acid without genetic modification include Actinobacillus succinogenes [43], Anaerobiospirillum succiniciproducens [21], Basfia succiniciproducens [33], Mannheimia succiniciproducens [37], and strains belonging to Enterobacter genus [28,40]. Although the Enterobacter genus was the last to be described, it is also becoming increasingly popular as a producer of other microbial metabolites. This is the case when producing ethanol from glycerol or mannitol [14,44], as opposed to long-established technologies based on starch. Hydrogen is another industrially desirable product with numerous applications, including as a potential future fuel. Bioproduction of hydrogen could provide an alternative to obtaining it from natural gas or via energy-intensive water electrolysis. Some Enterobacter strains can efficiently produce hydrogen using a wide range of waste products, including glycerol [22,32].

In this study, we report a strain of the Enterobacter genus (strain GAP1 deposited at the Polish Collection of Microorganisms (PCM) under the accession number B/00502) that can efficiently utilise inulin from Jerusalem artichoke without inulin hydrolysis pretreatment. The selected strain can utilise inulin in both submerged and semi-solid state fermentation. Flask and bioreactor cultivations were conducted, and the chromatographic profile of microbial products and fermentation substrate preparation using Jerusalem artichoke tubers were analysed to investigate the possibility of efficient succinic acid production in a consolidated bioprocess using inulin as a promising biotechnology feedstock.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

All chemicals used in this study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and were analytical grade. The food-grade inulin was prepared from chicory (Zielony Listek, Poland). The Jerusalem artichoke tubers were purchased from BIO Koszalin in Poland and were of the Albik cultivar.

Preparation of tuber extract and powder

Jerusalem artichoke tubers were washed with tap water to remove soil, then sliced into thin pieces. These were pressed mechanically and filtered through cotton cloth to obtain a residue-free liquid (RFL) containing inulin. Alternatively, the slices were dried at 60 °C for 24 h in an oven. The dried slices were then ground into a fine powder using a ceramic mortar to produce Jerusalem artichoke powder, which was stored and used for cultivation in bioreactors and for semi-solid fermentation.

Microorganism, medium and succinic acid fermentation

The GAP1 strain of Enterobacter species was a soil isolate originating from a soil sample taken in Puławy, Poland and deposited at the Polish Collection of Microorganisms (PCM), which is located at the Institute of Immunology and Experimental Therapy of the Polish Academy of Sciences in Wrocław, Poland, under the accession number B/00502. Prior to use, the culture stock was stored frozen at −80 °C with the addition of 20 % (w/w) glycerol. The seed culture medium was Brain Heart Infusion broth (pH 7.4; Oxoid, UK). The seed cultures were cultivated at 28 °C and 160 rpm in 50 ml sealed anaerobic bottles containing 30 ml of medium, inoculated with 0.1 ml of the −80 °C glycerol stock culture. Overnight cultures were used to inoculate the fermentation medium (5 % (v/v)).

Batch fermentations were conducted in duplicate in 100 mL serum bottles with a working volume of 50 mL, capped with gas-tight butyl rubber stoppers, or in 2 L Simax screw-on bottles containing 600 mL of fermentation medium. Bioreactor fermentations were performed in 1 L bioreactors (Infors, Multifors 2, Switzerland) with a working volume of 0.8 L.

The fermentation medium for submerged fermentation consisted of the following: carbon source 20 – 100 g/L (sterilised separately and added before inoculation); yeast extract 15 g/L; NaCl 0,1 g/L; K₂HPO₄ 1 g/L; CaCl₂ 0.2 g/L; neutralising agent 0 – 30 g/L (sterilised separately and added before inoculation) and distilled water. The concentrations of the carbon sources varied depending on the experiments performed. For co-fermentation tests, the following concentrations were used: glycerol 20 g/L; glucose and glycerol 20/20 g/L; lactose and glycerol 20/20 g/L; sucrose and glycerol 20/20 g/L; inulin and glycerol 20/20 g/L; pectin and glycerol 20/20 g/L; starch and glycerol 20/20 g/L. For tests with different concentrations of inulin, the following were used: 20 g/L, 40 g/L, 60 g/L, 80 g/L and 100 g/L of inulin. Tests concerning the nitrogen source and different magnesium carbonate concentrations were performed at an inulin concentration of 40 g/L. For tests with residue-free liquid (RFL) from JA tubers, the amount of RFL was standardised for the inulin concentration, which in this case was 40 g/L. For the comparison of succinic acid production using glycerol/inulin and glycerol/lactose systems, the glycerol concentration was 30 g/L in both cases, while the lactose and inulin concentrations were both set at 50 g/L. The bioreactor culture using only inulin was carried out at an initial concentration of 100 g/L, whereas for the culture using powdered Jerusalem artichoke tubers, the concentration was also 100 g/L. The medium was sterilised at 121 °C for 20 min. During bioreactor cultivations, sterile antifoam 204 (Sigma-Aldrich) was added as required, as was sterile MgCO₃ to prevent a decrease in pH below 6.8.

Semi-solid state fermentations were carried out in capped 50 ml Falcon tubes containing 6 g of hydrated JA tuber powder that had been previously autoclave sterilised (the water-to-powder ratio was 3:1). Falcon tubes were used because they can be centrifuged directly to separate solids from the medium, allowing the supernatant fluid to be collected for analysis. This approach also minimises measurement error resulting from transferring semi-solid material for examination after fermentation. Prior to fermentation, 3 g of a sterile concentrated medium consisting of 0.05 g/ml YE, 0.0004 g/ml NaCl and 0.02 g/ml K₂HPO₄ was added at a volumetric ratio of 1:2 with sterile hydrated JA tuber powder. Finally, a sterile neutralising agent (0–3 % w/w) was aseptically mixed with the other medium components.

Analytical methods

Samples for the detection of carbohydrates, succinic acid and by-products were prepared by centrifuging the culture broth at 6000 g for 5 min. After dilution with water (1:1), the resulting supernatant was analysed using a high-performance liquid chromatography system (Prominence, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an anion exchange column (Aminex HPX-87H, Bio-Rad) and a refractive index detector. The mobile phase was 0.03 M sulfuric acid at 50 °C [7]. Cell growth was monitored by measuring the absorbance of the fermentation broth at 600 nm (OD600) when using inulin as carbon source. For studies with residue free liquid (RFL) obtained from mashed tubers of Jerusalem artichoke or with powdered JA grounded tubers, the number of viable Enterobacter sp. GAP1 cells was estimated using the pour plate technique on BHI agar after incubation.

Results and discussion

Co-fermentations of glycerol, glucose, lactose, saccharose, inulin, pectin, and starch by Enterobacter sp. GAP1

Previous studies using the Enterobacter sp. LU1 strain have shown that fermentative glycerol consumption can be effective when lactose is added as a co-substrate. This finding formed the basis of tests conducted using the Enterobacter sp. GAP1 strain. As in the study by Podleśny et al. [28], glucose, lactose and sucrose were tested as co-substrates for glycerol consumption. However, this time the list was extended to include pectin, water-soluble starch and inulin, which are industrially important biotechnological raw materials. The addition of lactose, inulin or starch significantly influenced glycerol utilisation. The statistically highest glycerol utilisation was observed with the addition of lactose and inulin. Shaken cultures conducted in injection bottles confirmed the effectiveness of using lactose as a co-substrate in glycerol fermentation under the tested conditions [28]. The addition of lactose led to the complete utilisation of the glycerol in the medium, whereas the absence of a co-substrate resulted in glycerol consumption of no >17 %. In the first case, the yield of succinic acid was approximately 0.67 g per 1 g of substrates used; from glycerol alone, almost 1 g of succinic acid was obtained per gram of glycerol utilized — the best result among the tested systems. Such high yield is consistent with the results of other studies that have used glycerol for microbial succinic acid production using both wild bacterial strains [13] and genetically modified microorganisms [25]. It is also consistent with the maximum theoretical yield of succinic acid production from glycerol under anaerobic conditions: 1 mol of succinate per mole of glycerol, or 1.3 g of succinic acid per 1 g of glycerol consumed [18]. This phenomenon is explained by the redox potential of the glycerol molecule and its utilisation in the anaerobic succinic acid biosynthesis pathway. Glycerol is a more reduced molecule than glucose, making it a more efficient carbon source for synthesizing reduced products like succinic acid [12]. Interestingly, adding inulin as a co-substrate produced excellent results in terms of both the degree of glycerol consumption (91 %) and the yield of succinic acid (almost 0.79 g per gram of substrates used). This was the best result for the tested co-substrates. This confirmed the effectiveness of using inulin as a co-substrate for producing succinic acid from glycerol with the Enterobacter sp. LU1 strain. The lowest values for the degree of glycerol utilisation and succinic acid yield were observed with glucose and saccharose at 7.5 % and 0.12 g SA/g substrates, and 7.6 % and 0.13 g SA/g substrates respectively. Adding pectin improved the situation slightly, with results of 22.9 % and 0.1 g SA/g substrates. Adding starch was considered the third best co-substrate after lactose and inulin, with results for this polysaccharide of approx. 37 % for the degree of glycerol utilisation and 0.68 g succinic acid per gram of substrates used. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 1.

The impact of increasing inulin concentration, the type of nitrogen source and the choice of neutralising agent on succinic acid production by Enterobacter sp. GAP1

Obtaining satisfactory amounts of succinic acid through microbiological production requires the use of large quantities of substrates. High concentrations of these can exert significant osmotic pressure on bacterial cells at the start of fermentation, which negatively affects their multiplication rate and productivity in terms of succinic acid production. To investigate this, bottle experiments were conducted with increasing concentrations of inulin. Concentrations ranging from 20 g/L to 100 g/L were tested. No negative effect of increasing inulin concentration on succinic acid production was observed in the case of tested substrate after 24 and 120 h of cultivation. As the concentration increased, so did the concentration of the resulting succinic acid, reaching a maximum of over 17 g/L after 120 h of cultivation (Fig. S1). The statistically highest succinic acid concentration was observed in media containing 80 or 100 g/L of inulin, as determined by probes taken after 120 h. On the first day of cultivation, the statistical highest results were observed in the medium containing 100 g/L of inulin. The next step was to select the nitrogen source. Both organic and inorganic nitrogen sources were therefore examined. These included ammonium bicarbonate, ammonium chloride, ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, sodium nitrate, ammonium polyphosphate, urea, peptone, and yeast extract. The concentration of each was determined at 3 g/L, a fivefold reduction compared to previous fermentations. The cultures were conducted for 144 h and samples were taken for analysis at 72 h and at the end of the culture period. The statistically highest succinic acid concentrations were recorded using yeast extract: 7.86 g/l after 144 h of culture and 2.73 g/l after 72 h. Slightly lower succinic acid concentrations were achieved when peptone was used, especially after 72 h of culture (2.51 g/l) (Fig. S2). After 144 h of culture, the cultures with urea and then ammonium polyphosphate as a nitrogen source achieved the next highest succinic acid concentrations. Interestingly, in the latter case, the succinic acid concentration in samples tested after 72 h of culture equalled 70 % of the best value recorded for yeast extract. These results are consistent with literature reports and confirm the higher efficiency of organic nitrogen sources in terms of final succinic acid concentration and biosynthesis rate [28,45]. However, the cost advantage of inorganic sources requires careful consideration of the possibility of using these simple substances, which may be more beneficial in the overall economic calculation. This approach is often employed in industrial bioprocesses for the large-scale production of low-molecular-weight microbiological compounds, but is limited to microbial strains without auxotrophic traits [1]. In organic acid production processes, a key component of fermentation media is the addition of a neutralising agent to prevent the pH of the culture medium from dropping below a level that inhibits the growth and productivity of the bacterial strain producing the acid. In this case, the acid is succinic acid. Previous studies on succinic acid production using the Enterobacter sp. LU1 strain demonstrated that magnesium carbonate was the most effective neutralising agent [28]. Therefore, the same neutralising agent was used in the current study, with the magnesium carbonate concentration selected for succinic acid production in the range of 10 to 30 g/l. The statistically highest succinic acid concentrations were recorded using 25 and 30 g/L of magnesium carbonate. Experiments showed that increasing the magnesium carbonate concentration increases the final concentration of produced succinic acid up to 25 g/l. However, when the concentration was increased further to 30 g/L, no difference in the final succinic acid concentration was observed compared to the culture conducted at an initial magnesium carbonate concentration of 25 g/L. The detailed results of the experiments are presented in Fig. S3.

Residue-free liquid (RFL) and Jerusalem artichoke powder as substrates for succinic acid production using Enterobacter sp. GAP1 - submerged or semi-solid state fermentations

The experimental results obtained previously prompted us to conduct fermentation cultures using raw material containing inulin, i.e. Jerusalem artichoke tubers. After washing, the tubers were grated into a fine pulp and the resulting juice was used as one of the substrates in the described culture. The experiments were conducted in Simax bottles with a total capacity of 2 L. The fermentation medium was prepared at a volume of 600 ml, with 200 ml of this being the filtrate from the grated JA tubers. For culture A, the additional carbon source was glycerol. The cultures were conducted at a temperature of 28 °C with stirring at 140 rpm. Fermentation was completed after six days. After 96 h of incubation, the highest succinic acid concentrations (over 32 g/L) were recorded in the culture with a mixed carbon source (Fig. 2A), where, in addition to the tuber filtrate, there was also glycerol. For cultures using only the filtrate, the maximum succinic acid concentration was approximately 24 g/L (Fig. 2B). Succinic acid was not the only product of fermentation conducted in this way, of course. By-products included formic acid, lactic acid, acetic acid, ethanol and 2,3-butanediol. A continuous increase in the concentrations of acetic acid and 2,3-butanediol was observed until the end of the culture. The remaining products (formic acid, lactic acid and ethanol) reached maximum concentrations during the incubation period, after which a decrease was noted. This indicates the ability of the tested strain to metabolise these fermentation products under changing batch culture conditions by using specific enzymes for metabolic incorporation. The microbial utilisation of the aforementioned metabolites is well characterised in the literature, with these metabolites being treated as carbon [19,24,31] and energy (Leonhartsberger et al. [15]; [34,38]) sources. The consistency of the prepared raw material (grated Jerusalem artichoke tubers) led to the idea of conducting fermentation to produce succinic acid in media with reduced water content. The cultures conducted so far were submerged, with water as the main component of the production substrates and the reaction medium. In the case of the fermentations described below, however, water was added to the medium at the end of the preparation process, together with the necessary dry ingredients. The main reaction medium was the pulp obtained after grating the Jerusalem artichoke tubers. The effect of adding a neutralising agent on succinic acid production was tested in the media prepared in this way at the beginning. Of the tested chemical compounds, magnesium hydroxide produced the highest succinic acid concentrations (over 8 g/L), while calcium carbonate produced the lowest (2.48 g/L) (Fig. S4). The addition of magnesium carbonate or sodium carbonate enabled succinic acid concentrations exceeding 5 g/L to be achieved (5.43 and 5.03 g/L, respectively). A concentration slightly below 5 g/L was achieved by adding potassium carbonate (4.96 g/L succinic acid), while adding ammonium bicarbonate produced a concentration of slightly over 4 g/L. The better results obtained using magnesium hydroxide than magnesium carbonate may be due to the higher reactivity of magnesium hydroxide compared to its carbonate salt [10]. Thus, the chemical properties of magnesium hydroxide may favour more effective neutralisation of produced organic acids in an environment with limited water content. Subsequent experiments on the cultivation of Enterobacter sp. GAP1 in media with reduced water content focused on adding magnesium hydroxide to the culture medium. Due to its alkaline nature, adding it in one portion at the beginning of cultivation seems inadvisable. A better solution would be to add it successively during cultivation, to avoid significantly increasing the pH of the culture medium. Therefore, successive additions of magnesium hydroxide to cultures with reduced water content were tested. Portions of magnesium hydroxide were added at the beginning of cultivation, after 3 and 6 days of cultivation process. In this experiment, the duration of cultivation was also taken into account, with options of 3, 6 or 9 days. After three days, cultivation in samples 3–1 was terminated, and samples 6–2, 9–2 and 9–3 were supplemented with 0.05 g of Mg(OH)₂ and mixed in evenly. After six days of cultivation, the experiment was completed in samples 6–1 and 6–2. A portion of 0.05 g of Mg(OH)₂ was added to sample 9–2. After nine days, the final variants of cultivation were completed (i.e. 9–1, 9–2 and 9–3) (Fig. 3). The results obtained allow us to observe a positive correlation between the total amount of magnesium hydroxide added and the succinic acid concentration obtained in the samples. Adding a single portion of magnesium hydroxide resulted in the lowest succinic acid concentration (12.63 g/L). Better results were achieved by adding a double portion of magnesium hydroxide (22.81 g/L of SA); however, the highest concentrations of succinic acid were obtained by adding magnesium hydroxide three times during cultivation. Moreover, the key factor enabling us to observe the aforementioned differences was the duration of the cultivation process. Extending the culture by three days increased the succinic acid concentration by over 15 %, compared to the concentration obtained on the sixth day of culture (26.43 g/L and 22.81 g/L SA, respectively). Succinic acid production by Enterobacter sp. GAP1 during bioreactor cultivations on inulin-containing media

Using bioreactors in biotechnological processes allows to control key parameters and modify them smoothly in response to the specific growth needs of bacterial production strains. Thanks to bioreactors, we can maintain the desired levels of key cultivation parameters for the production process, such as temperature, pH, mixing speed and aeration. Bioreactors also facilitate the verification of the bioprocess in terms of its scalability and suitability for industrial production. Consequently, using a bioreactor is a natural stage in the development of each bioprocess, particularly for submerged cultures, for which we have access to apparatus solutions with volumes of several hundred thousand litres. The situation is more complicated in the case of solid-state fermentation because, despite the existence of dedicated solutions, scaling up to the level of industrial production is much more difficult due to certain thermodynamic limitations of the process [2]. In turn, the heterogeneity of the solid substrate makes it difficult to control parameters such as temperature, moisture and aeration. Using bioreactors to cultivate Enterobacter sp. GAP1 and produce succinic acid using inulin as the feedstock was therefore the next logical step after our previous laboratory experiments. To this end, a production system was tested using inulin as the sole carbon source. Bulk magnesium carbonate was used to neutralise the produced succinic acid, maintaining the pH of the culture fluid at a level above 6.8. In the described culture, stirring at 200 rpm was used without aerating the production system. This approach enabled a final succinic acid concentration of <30 g/L to be achieved within 192 h of culturing the Enterobacter sp. GAP1 strain (Fig. 4). In this case, the total concentration of by-products did not exceed 26 g/L, and the analysed metabolites included formic acid, acetic acid, ethanol and 2,3-butanediol. For the next approach, a inulin-containing medium supplemented with glycerol was used. The remaining process parameters were unchanged from the previous culture. Adding glycerol increased the final concentration of produced succinic acid, which exceeded 37 g/L (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, this concentration was achieved in a shorter time: after culturing the Enterobacter sp. GAP1 strain for 168 h. The addition of glycerol also reduced the amount of by-products produced to a level of no >10.5 g/L, which led to the elimination of formic acid and ethanol biosynthesis. These results are consistent with previous observations by Lee et al. [13] of succinic acid production by Anaerobiospirillum succiniciproducens. They noted that using glycerol as the carbon source decreased the formation of the by-product acetic acid by more than sixfold. Therefore, adding a suitable co-substrate is one way to reduce the amount of by-products produced. The accumulation of such metabolites usually negatively affect both yield and downstream processing, thereby reducing overall bioprocess efficiency. Another way to increase bioprocess efficiency would be to introduce genetic modifications that alter the metabolic pathways involved in the production of succinic acid and the energy management function of bacterial cells [50]. The above changes may be subject to redirecting carbon flux by blocking competitive succinic acid biosynthesis pathways [49], overexpression of enzymes involved in succinic acid formation [17], modifications within global regulatory genes [26] or balancing the redox ratio (NADH/NAD+) of the bacterial cell [35]. Eliminating excess by-products will also help to reduce the cost of purifying succinic acid, which accounts for 50–80 % of the total cost of producing bio-succinic acid [5]. The use of specific fermentation conditions, including fed-batch cultures with recycling of bacterial cells in the process [23], a two-phase batch process [42] or fermentation with simultaneous removal of acidic products (ISPR – In Situ Product Removal) [46], also brings interesting effects in the field of increasing the efficiency of succinic acid production. Implementing the above methods to reduce by-product formation could improve the production process of succinic acid using Enterobacter sp. GAP1 and inulin feedstock in future studies.

To enable comparison with the production system proposed in earlier studies on different Enterobacter strain [28], a culture was also carried out using a medium containing lactose and glycerol. Using these substrates allowed for a slight increase in the final succinic acid concentration (41.7 g/L), but resulted in a twofold increase in by-product concentration, with formic acid accounting for the largest proportion (Fig. 5B). In the final stage of the experiments, a culture was carried out using powdered JA tubers as a substrate for producing succinic acid with the Enterobacter sp. GAP1 strain. This raw material was not subjected to prior enzymatic pre-treatment or thermal inulin extraction. This approach yielded a final succinic acid concentration of approximately 17.6 g/L (Fig. 6). The results were obtained after 120 h of cultivation, at which point the process was terminated due to the first signs of entering the stationary growth phase and inhibition of succinic acid biosynthesis. Further research is planned to identify and eradicate the limiting factors of the bioprocess and thereby enhance the succinic acid biosynthesis method using Jerusalem artichoke tubers as a carbon source.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel fermentation strategy was developed for succinic acid production by Enterobacter sp. GAP1 strain. Submerged and semi-solid fermentations were performed using inulin as the carbon source, allowing for the production of up to 29.4 g/L of succinic acid without any inulin pretreatment. This is the first time that Jerusalem artichoke has been used as a non-grain substrate for efficient SA production in a consolidated bioprocess (CBP).
