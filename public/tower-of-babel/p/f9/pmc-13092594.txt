Innovative wound management of refractory venous ulcers with topical oxygen therapy
Authors: Zachary E Williams, Impreet Singh, Natalie Marks, Enrico Ascher, Anil P Hingorani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTopical oxygen therapy (tOT) is a novel treatment method capable of expediting granulation tissue formation in patients with non-healing lower extremity venous leg ulcers (VLUs). tOT provides cyclic oxygen with compression and is able to be administered at home, unlike chamber-based oxygen therapy. Although previous randomized prospective trials have demonstrated effectiveness of tOT in treating ulcers of diabetic etiology, its ability to promote healing in refractory venous ulcers requires additional exploration. Thus, we investigated preliminary outcomes of tOT administration in treatment-resistant VLUs.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective review of treatment outcomes among 31 patients with 32 total extremities with VLUs following longitudinal administration of tOT. All patients received managed Medicaid approval for tOT after each ulcer failed to resolve following multiple alternative therapies, including Unnaboot compression, sclerotherapy, thermal ablation, iliac vein stenting, and debridement. Patient response to tOT was determined by assessing mid-treatment progression of ulcer length and width, in addition to final ulceration status at the conclusion of therapy. Mean treatment length, total ulcer duration, peak ulcer length, and peak ulcer width were determined for each patient and compared between healed and unhealed VLUs.ResultsThe average age across all individuals was 73 ± 19 years (range, 27-99 years). Fourteen patients (45%) were male, with a racial breakdown of 18 White (58%), five Hispanic (16%), six Black (19%), and two Asian (6%) patients. Comorbid conditions included hypertension in 31 patients (100%), hyperlipidemia in 15 (48%), and diabetes in 12 (39%). Four patients (13%) demonstrated a former history of smoking, whereas three patients (10%) were currently using tobacco products during the study period. Total duration across all VLUs was 1075 ± 1004 days. The average duration of tOT was 265 ± 233 days, whereas the average pretreatment ulcer duration was 718 ± 842 days. The mean ulcer length was 7.6 ± 6.8 cm, and the mean ulcer width was 5.7 ± 5.0 cm (range, 2-24 cm for both). Following tOT administration, 11 VLUs (34%) healed entirely, nine ulcers (28%) improved but did not completely heal, eight (25%) remained unchanged, and four (13%) worsened despite treatment. The median time to healing among the 11 VLUs that healed completely was 121 days. For ulcers that did not heal, the mean duration of tOT was 333 ± 261 days. No differences were observed in the pretreatment VLU duration (P = .54), maximum length (P = .50), or maximum width (P = .80) of healed vs unhealed VLUs.ConclusionsTwenty of the 32 refractory VLUs (62.5%) treated with tOT either decreased in size or healed entirely after failing multiple previous therapies. Three of the 11 ulcers (27.3%) that healed completely recurred following topical oxygen therapy.Keywords: Chronic wound healing, Lower extremity venous leg ulcers, Topical oxygen therapy

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs) represent a significant health care challenge as the most advanced stage of chronic venous insufficiency, accounting for 70% to 80% of lower limb ulcers, especially in patients aged 65 and older.1,2 VLUs exhibit markedly substandard healing rates due to impaired perfusion and a persistent inflammatory state, often requiring at least 3 months to achieve resolution despite appropriate medical management, or even failing to heal altogether. Even after successful healing, recurrence among VLUs remains common, with reported 1-year recurrence rates between 33% and 42%.3 In addition to the poor outcomes of venous ulcers with high-pressure compressive therapy alone, patient comorbidities such as ischemic peripheral arterial disease or congestive heart failure may further limit the efficacy of conventional modalities, thus underscoring the need for alternative or adjunctive approaches. Accordingly, this study was undertaken to examine this gap in the current literature and explore the potential role of an adjunctive therapy in treating lower extremity wounds of venous etiology.

Suboptimal perfusion due to chronic venous insufficiency and subsequently decreased oxygen tension at chronic wound sites impairs metabolite synthesis necessary for collagen deposition and neoepithelization, illustrating the necessity of oxygen availability for ulcer healing. Systemic oxygen administration, classically achieved via the use of hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) chambers, is well-established for treating oxygen-deficient ulcers with diabetic and arterial etiologies.4 Although this therapeutic modality has demonstrated improved outcomes, especially in the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), a limited availability to specialized care facilities and an extensive daily time commitment over multiple months largely restricts its longitudinal usage within the broader community.5 Additionally, the financial burden imposed by HBOT remains significant, with some reports estimating the cost of treating a DFU between $17,875.80 and $23,834.40, although this valuation appears to be decreasing with time.6 Multiple medical risks are associated with HBOT as well, most commonly including tympanic membrane rupture, tension pneumothorax following rupture of preexisting blebs secondary to alterations in chamber pressure, and hypoglycemic episodes in insulin-dependent diabetic patients without food access during therapy.

Topical oxygen therapy (tOT) is a novel treatment for refractory lower extremity ulcerations that overcomes the traditional limitations of HBOT by delivering targeted oxygen directly to wound sites, offering increased therapeutic accessibility with minimal compromise to wound healing.7 Prior investigations have demonstrated the efficacy of tOT as an adjunctive therapy for DFU management, including the only randomized control trial to assess tOT usage in lower extremity ulcerations, which randomized patients to receive either standard-of-care therapy or standard-of-care therapy supplemented with tOT.8 However, the remaining literature remains sparse regarding the potential of this novel therapy for recalcitrant VLUs, either independently or as an adjuvant to standard wound care.

Given the need for more robust evidence surrounding the benefit of tOT as supplementation to standard care plans, we conducted a single-center retrospective analysis of 31 patients with 32 long-standing VLUs referred to our clinic after previously failing multiple standard treatment modalities despite compliance with therapy. The primary aim of this report was to characterize healing outcomes and recurrence patterns of refractory venous leg ulcers treated with adjunctive tOT following unsuccessful management with conventional venous and wound-care techniques. These results intend to inform future research in further optimizing medical management for this chronic disease process.

Methods

Patient characteristics

We retrospectively chart-reviewed all patients with treatment-resistant lower extremity VLUs receiving longitudinal tOT with the TWO2 Extremity Chamber system from Advanced Oxygen Therapy Inc at a single center over a 3-year period. Data was collected between April 2021 and April 2024. Patients included in this study were referred from multiple external institutions after repeatedly failing conventional therapies, presenting with superficial, chronic (>30 days) CEAP class 6 wounds that were not covered with eschar or slough, and therefore amenable to topical administration of oxygen. All patients were assessed for lower extremity vascularization before initiating tOT, with confirmation of palpable pedal pulses or an ankle-brachial index measuring >0.6 in each subject. Patients presenting with concomitant peripheral artery disease necessitating revascularization were excluded from this study, defined per Society of Vascular Surgery guidelines.9 Ulcers that presented as deep or craterous were also excluded from this report. Demographic information obtained from patients’ medical records included patient age, gender, and race, where race was subdivided into White, Hispanic, African American, and Asian based on patient self-identification. Baseline comorbidities recorded from chart review included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and tobacco smoking (either former or current). This project was exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board at Total Vascular Care, as the data was retrospective and de-identified, and no patient consent was required for the purposes of this study. This study adhered to all principles outlined within the Declaration of Helsinki.

Techniques

Prior to administration of topical oxygen, all patients received treatment authorization via Medicaid after failing to demonstrate wound improvement secondary to alternative therapeutic modalities. All venous ulcerations included in this study were determined to be recalcitrant following referral from external sources, given previously documented failure of all appropriate venous interventions. The most commonly employed therapies preceding tOT include Unnaboot compression, sclerotherapy, varithena, thermal ablation (a composite of radiofrequency ablation and laser ablation), iliac vein stenting, and debridement. All patients received and failed at least one or more of the above treatment modalities prior to starting tOT. These therapies were not standardized across all patients prior to tOT initiation, but rather individually determined by their clinic provider based on patient comorbidities and venous wound status at presentation. The interval between venous interventions and initiation of tOT was variable as well, given that these procedures were performed based on individual clinical indications rather than as components of a standardized topical oxygen protocol. For the purposes of topical oxygen administration, a cyclical pressurization device was utilized to facilitate oxygen delivery directly to the wound site via a disposable, non-prescriptive plastic extremity covering extending above the knee. The specific device model provided to patients within this study for the purpose of home oxygen administration was the TWO2 Extremity Chamber system from Advanced Oxygen Therapy Inc. Prior to tOT, patients received initial debridement of the wound site in clinic to ensure proper exposure of the wound bed to therapeutic oxygen. Upon beginning tOT, patients were recommended to receive home treatment four times weekly for 90 minutes per session. At each home session, patients placed a single-use plastic boot over the affected site, where the machine applied 100% O2 at a constant flow rate of 10 L/min and cyclical pressures between 10 and 50 mbar. In accordance with the TWO2 Extremity Chamber instructions for use, the plastic extremity covering was inflated to 1.03 atm, followed by placement of this chamber over the wound and inflation of a proximal cuff to ensure proper seal between the chamber environment and the outside atmosphere. This single-use limb chamber was attached to an Aeroneb humidifier, which utilizes sterile distilled water to provide humidified oxygen directly to the wound site. Outside of each home session, patients received standard wound care including cleaning and debridement in conjunction with conventional compression dressing (CCD) placement at regular weekly follow-up in our clinic. Patient adherence to therapy was assessed through self-reported feedback obtained during these follow-up visits throughout the treatment period. Wound dimensions (length and width) were also measured during clinic sessions using standard clinical measurement tools including a ruler, tape measure, or calipers to evaluate interval changes and overall healing progression.

Statistical analysis

Ulcers were designated as “healed” if total resolution was achieved throughout the course of tOT administration and designated as “unhealed” if the wounds persisted despite treatment. tOT treatment was concluded after an ulcer was determined to have healed. Among unhealed ulcers, wounds were sub-stratified as “Improved,” “Unchanged,” or “Worsened,” depending on their recorded size at the conclusion of the study period. Ulcers classified as “Improved” demonstrated a reduction in total surface area at the conclusion of the study period as recorded in patient charts. Similarly, VLUs classified as “Unchanged” demonstrated no difference in measured surface area at the end of the study period, whereas those deemed “Worsened” were recorded to have a greater surface area at the conclusion of the study as compared with the onset of treatment. Ulcers were noted to have recurred if they were appreciated during a follow-up visit to the clinic secondary to initial resolution of the wound. In addition to total ulcer duration and the duration of tOT treatment, a pretreatment duration was also determined for each VLU, defined as the duration for which the ulcer persisted from initial presentation until initiation of tOT. The pretreatment duration of each VLU, as well as the maximal ulcer length and maximal width were each compared between healed and unhealed ulcers using Welch’s two-sample t-test. A value of a P < .05 was considered statistically significant for the purpose of our analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2024 Version 16.89.1 (Microsoft Corporation). GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (528) software was used for presentation of data.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Our retrospective review enrolled 31 patients with 32 individual extremities presenting with VLUs refractory to conventional treatment modalities. Ultimately, those included in this report constitute 1.2% of the 2493 total patients receiving care for venous ulcerations through our clinic between April 2021 and April 2024. Of the 31 patients, 45.2% (n = 14) were males, and 54.8% (n = 17) were females. The mean age of all patients was 72.5 ± 19.1 years (range, 27-99 years). The study included a racial distribution of 18 White (58%), five Hispanic (16%), six Black (19%), and two Asian (6%) patients. One hundred percent of patients (n = 31) demonstrated a comorbid diagnosis of hypertension at baseline, whereas 48% (n = 15) and 39% (n = 12) were found to be comorbid for hyperlipidemia and diabetes mellitus, respectively. Twelve percent of patients (n = 4) had a recorded history of prior tobacco use, whereas 10% of patients reported current smoking of tobacco products (Table). The primary indication for initiation of localized tOT was unsatisfactory wound healing following treatment with one or multiple alternative therapies. Such previously failed therapies included Unnaboot compression in 29 patients (94%), sclerotherapy in 11 patients (35%), great saphenous vein (GSV) varithena in 10 patients (32%), small saphenous vein (SSV) varithena in seven patients (23%), GSV thermal ablation in 23 patients (74%), SSV thermal ablation in 17 patients (55%), thermal ablation of perforating veins in five patients (16%), iliac vein stenting in 19 patients (61%), and debridement in two patients (6%) (Fig 1). In total, 81% of patients (25/31) received prior treatment of all venous reflux via sclerotherapy, varithena, or thermal ablation without demonstrating clinically significant resolution of venous wounds.

Outcomes

The mean duration of tOT administration from treatment initiation to the cessation of therapy was 265 ± 233 days among all VLUs, whereas the median time to healing among ulcers that healed following tOT was 121 days. The average total duration across all VLUs was 1075 ± 1004 days from initial presentation at our office to ultimate resolution or the end of the study period. The average duration of treatment with topical oxygen was 149 ± 106 days among healed ulcers and 333 ± 261 days for those that healed incompletely. The mean total duration of all VLUs within the healed group beginning at presentation to their ultimate resolution was 763 ± 866 days. Ulcers that did not heal entirely demonstrated an average duration of 1239 ± 1052 days from initial presentation to the conclusion of the study period (Fig 2). The average pretreatment duration for all VLUs was 718 ± 842 days; however, no significant difference was observed when comparing healed VLUs and unhealed VLUs (590 days vs 789 days; P = .54).

Topical oxygen therapy (tOT) treatment and ulcer duration. The mean duration of tOT treatment was 265 ± 233 days among all ulcers, with a breakdown of 149 ± 106 days among healed ulcers and 333 ± 261 days for those that did not heal. The average total duration across all venous leg ulcers (VLUs) was 1075 ± 1004 days from initial presentation to ultimate resolution or the end of the study period. Mean VLU duration was 763 ± 866 days for healed ulcerations and 1239 ± 1052 days for those that did not heal completely.

The maximum dimensions for each VLU were also measured between index presentation and the end of tOT administration. The mean length was 7.6 ± 6.8 cm (range, 2-24 cm), whereas the mean width was 5.7 ± 5.0 cm (range, 2-24 cm). When comparing healed vs unhealed ulcers for either ulcer length (6.5 vs 8.2 cm; P = .50) or ulcer width (6.0 vs 3.9 cm; P = .80), no statistical significance was determined to exist (Fig 3). Ultimately, 11 of the 32 total VLUs (34%) healed entirely following tOT administration, whereas nine ulcers (28%) improved but did not resolve completely. Eight ulcers (25%), however, remained unchanged after tOT administration at the conclusion of the study period, and four ulcers (13%) worsened despite treatment (Fig 4). Following the study period, several patients whose VLUs did not heal with topical oxygen continued adjunctive tOT at home in addition to standard wound care, whereas others transitioned to standard therapy alone due to insurance limitations. A minority of patients also discontinued tOT following the conclusion of the study as a result of treatment noncompliance. Follow-up data was reviewed in April 2025 for patients with ulcers that healed completely during tOT administration, 1 full year from the end of the initial study period. At this time, three of 11 VLUs (27.3%) had recurred either during continued maintenance with tOT or following cessation of therapy, with an average follow-up duration of 388 ± 365 days.

Discussion

Chronic VLUs are infamously difficult to completely heal and prevent re-occurrence, imposing a burden on patients’ quality of life and the overarching health care framework. Despite contemporary standard-of-care wound management and external compression therapy, VLUs are largely characterized by impaired healing, with previously reported resolution rates varying widely between 17% and 95%.10,11 Recurrence rates remain alarmingly high as well, as 18% to 56% of patients with healed VLUs re-present within 12 months of wound closure.12,13 Due to their chronic nature, VLUs inflict a massive $14.9 billion economic burden across public and private health care systems annually. Moreover, the extensive pain, discomfort, and impaired mobility that characterize these wounds carry major social ramifications, including an increased risk for depression and further deterioration in patient quality of life.

Given the high prevalence and recurrence rates of VLUs, the development of innovative therapeutic modalities is crucial to improve patient outcomes. Although current literature extensively explores the efficacy of HBOT across wounds of various etiologies, the efficacy of tOT in similar pathologies remains widely understudied. Currently, three major systems of oxygen delivery are commonly employed, including devices providing normobaric continuous delivery of oxygen, those conferring constant low pressures at the wound site, and those that offer cyclic pressurization in addition to oxygen administration.7 Although all forms of tOT can be administered through any oxygen-permeable wound dressing, such as Unnaboot, gauze, or skin substitutes, each modality varies in the degree of clinical evidence that support their individual therapeutic potential. Although the lower constant pressure devices have previously demonstrated high rates of effectiveness for wound healing, their clinical backing largely consists of case series or uncontrolled trials.14,15 In contrast, multiple prospective studies and randomized control trials underwrite the positive effects on wound healing offered by both continuous delivery of oxygen and cyclical pressure devices, especially in DFUs.8,16, 17, 18 Specifically, Blackman et al conducted a prospective controlled study comparing cyclic tOT against advanced moist wound therapy for DFU treatment, demonstrating 82.4% and 45.5% of wounds healed completely in the tOT and control groups, respectively. Moreover, the median time to wound closure for patients receiving tOT was determined to be 56 days, as compared to 93 days in the control group.18 As these investigations have largely focused on the use of tOT in treating diabetic and arterial ulcerations, data that assess the potential usage of tOT for VLUs remains limited. Our study’s objective was to address this literature gap by examining the healing outcomes and recurrence rates of severe, refractory venous ulcers treated with tOT following the failure of all prior treatment methodology despite appropriate therapeutic compliance.

Although standard treatment methodologies for chronic VLUs prioritize the use of CCDs, studies comparing standard care against tOT to assess VLU resolution have shown promise for this modality. Tawfick and Sultan, in 2009, previously conducted a single-center observational study out of Ireland to compare treatment outcomes between tOT and CCDs in chronic VLUs.19,20 Notably, their work demonstrated a significantly reduced healing time for ulcers treated with tOT, regardless of the duration or the size of the ulcer, with a complete absence of ulcer recurrence among those that healed completely following tOT.19 In their initial 2009 report, Tawfick and Sultan demonstrated a mean healing time of 45 days for patients receiving tOT as compared with 182 days for patients treated with CCDs.19 Ultimately, 80.4% of ulcers (37/46) treated with tOT experienced complete resolution vs only 35.1% (13/37) of those from the CCD cohort. Additionally, none of the 37 VLUs that completely healed following tOT administration reoccurred by 12 months, whereas 38.5% fully healed ulcers (5/13) treated with compression redeveloped.19 Although this study, as well as our own, contained patients presenting with immedicable ulcerations recalcitrant to alternative treatments, our observed median time to VLU healing was relatively protracted at 121 days, with a recurrence rate of three of 11 (27.3%) following tOT. Despite these similarities in VLU status at presentation, our study population consisted of outpatient clinic referrals following a lack of improvement with standard wound care, whereas Tawfick and Sultan recruited inpatient admissions from the vascular unit of a tertiary referral center. Our patient cohort also received tOT as an adjunct to standard wound care, in contrast to Tawfick and Sultan’s stratification of patients into individual CCD and tOT groups via an intention-to-treat analysis.

A follow-up 2013 study by these authors further assessed the clinical outcomes of tOT as compared with CCDs for the treatment of refractory VLUs, finding that 76% of VLUs treated with tOT healed completely by 12 weeks, in contrast to only 46% of the CCD cohort.20 This subsequent work with expanded cohort sizes showed similar improvements in the efficacy of tOT over CCD, including a significant reduction in the median time to complete wound healing for patients receiving tOT, from 57 days compared with 107 days in those managed with compression.20 The authors also reported a mean reduction in surface area of 96% at 12 weeks following tOT compared with a 61% reduction for CCD patients, thus emphasizing the success of this modality in reducing ulcer burden at treatment conclusion.20 Importantly, tOT outperformed CCD within each substratification of ulcer duration and ulcer surface area, demonstrating substantially shorter healing times regardless of the duration (P < .001) or surface area of the ulcer (P < .001).19,20 Within the tOT cohort itself, the median time to complete ulcer resolution remained unaffected by either the duration of the ulcer or the size of said ulcer.20 Similarly, our findings reinforced the lack of statistical significance in pretreatment ulcer duration (P = .54) or maximal length (P = .50) and width (P = .80) measurements when comparing healed and unhealed VLUs in patients receiving tOT. As such, the results presented above suggest additional evidence to substantiate the conclusions made by Tawflick and Sultan regarding the association of tOT with an acceleration of venous wound healing independent of both ulcer size and duration prior to treatment initiation. Our work therefore attempts to generate hypotheses for future research surrounding the potential suitability of tOT as an adjunct to standard wound therapy for all patients presenting with refractory VLUs, regardless of their individual characteristics. Of note, this study is the first non-industry-sponsored report out of the United States to assess the relationship between tOT administration and VLU healing at a single center with a standardized protocol.

Despite the benefits of topical oxygen outlined in these studies, multiple impediments exist that restrict its generalized usage within the population of the United States. Access to this treatment modality varies from state to state, as limited insurance coverage through managed Medicaid and the Veteran’s Association medical system remains a significant barrier to the widespread availability of tOT. As insurance coverage continues to expand in response to growing clinical evidence, however, the increased accessibility of tOT in addition to its demonstrated clinical efficacy positions it to become an integral component in standard wound care for lower extremity ulcers. Certain contemporary tOT devices are also limited by their necessitation of an electrical outlet for the entire 90-minute session. Patients are therefore confined to specific locations during active treatment, despite the ability of this modality to be comfortably applied in the outpatient setting. In contrast, newer models deliver tOT via portable, rechargeable oxygen generators powered by lithium-ion batteries, further enhancing convenience without tethering patients to their walls.7 Topical oxygen administration is relatively contraindicated in ulcers with barriers to oxygen penetration, due to the nature of oxygen delivery through diffusion. Such barriers include necrotic tissue and eschar, which should be debrided prior to therapy as they further limit the diffusion capacity of oxygen.21 Wounds with inadequate arterial perfusion, untreated deep venous thrombosis, and acute thrombophlebitis serve as additional contraindications for this novel treatment modality.22

Limitations

This study maintains a single-center, retrospective design with a patient population derived from external referrals from nearby institutions due to the chronicity and refractory nature of their wounds. Therefore, selection bias exists within our study, as those selected for inclusion are not representative of the standard VLU upon initial presentation, but rather elderly patients with the most advanced, recalcitrant ulcers. This focus may limit the generalizability of the reported data but also underscores its clinical relevance, as tOT showed measurable benefit in a population with a minimal likelihood of healing. Given that tOT is primarily indicated for ulcers of such chronicity, addressing this selection bias requires additional evidence obtained through randomized control trials. We are therefore also unable to confirm the total duration of the patients’ venous ulcers prior to their initial presentation at our clinic and thus report the duration of such VLUs since that date. Patient adherence to tOT was unable to be measured outside of verbal feedback and regular wound assessment as treatment was administered at home without direct monitoring. Consequently, variability in compliance may have influenced observed healing outcomes and represents a potential source of additional confounding. As treatment was not administered in a clinic setting, those conducting the study were also unable to control for leakage outside of the intrinsic device components meant to mitigate equilibration with the external atmosphere. Similarly, although the recommended flow rate and treatment pressures were input directly into the device console per physician recommendations, oxygen delivery to the wound site was not precisely measured, given the home application of this therapy. Owing to its retrospective nature, this study did not include a control arm with patients receiving conventional compression therapy alone, thereby precluding direct comparison between such patients and our cohort receiving tOT. The limited sample size of our patient cohort restricts the overall power of this study as well, leading to an increased propensity for type II errors. Additionally, individual measurements of ulcer height and width remain suboptimal in the evaluation of total ulcer size, given their multidimensionality and often irregular contour. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, we were unable to assess the depth of these ulcers in addition to their two-dimensional measurements. Additional improvements in three-dimensional measurement technology will determine the surface area of VLUs via direct image analysis and further enhance the accuracy of future data. We also acknowledge that some VLUs worsened during or following treatment with tOT in this study, for which we are evaluating the etiology in a separate investigation.

Conclusions

This study investigates the efficacy of a specific tOT modality in treating 32 refractory VLUs, evaluating healing progression and recurrence rates. Following longitudinal treatment with tOT, 20 of the 32 total ulcers (62.5%) either deceased in size or healed completely, whereas three of 11 of these ulcers (27.3%) recurred after therapy. These patients had already failed all prior treatments, including surgical wound closure, home wound care, and iliac vein stenting, thus representing the oldest demographics with the most chronic ulcerations, selected for inclusion as an absolute last-resort management option. Despite this, tOT promoted VLU healing in a substantial proportion of this population. Given the initial success of tOT in improving healing outcomes, reducing pain, and enhancing patient independence, we are in the process of liberalizing this therapy beyond the most severe patients, including patients who fail to respond to venotonic agents. Nonetheless, further research is warranted to establish its role as a first-line or adjunct therapy for chronic VLUs.
