Abdominal Wall Schwannoma: A Rare Case of Target Sign on Ultrasonography
Authors: Noboru Takanashi, Takayuki Tajima, Chieko Seto, Asuka Sakamoto, Miki Mori, Haruka Ikoma, Jun Hashimoto, Satomi Asai, Osamu Chino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSchwannoma is a benign tumor derived from Schwann cells of peripheral nerves. Schwannomas arising within muscle or fat exhibit characteristic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings called the target sign, split fat sign, or fascicular sign. We present a case in which ultrasound findings revealed a well‐defined encapsulated mass showing the “target sign,” which is commonly associated with MRI diagnosis of schwannomas. A 71‐year‐old man was referred to our hospital after a clearly demarcated abdominal wall mass was detected on plain computed tomography (CT) during a medical checkup at another hospital. The patient's chief complaint was mild discomfort and slight pain. He had a history of appendicitis surgery and mild insomnia, and no related family history was noted. There were no principal features that would raise suspicion of von Recklinghausen's disease (NF1) or neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). Ultrasonography revealed a well‐defined, capsular mass with a diameter of just under 3 cm within the rectus abdominis. Therefore, we performed MRI, which showed the same target sign observed on ultrasonography. Our imaging findings strongly supported the diagnosis of schwannoma. Tumor resection was planned; however, due to the ongoing coronavirus outbreak, the patient strongly preferred to postpone treatment until the outbreak had subsided. He was followed up in the outpatient clinic, which was performed approximately one year later, and no change was observed during follow up. Histopathological examination revealed schwannoma. The patient was discharged 2 days after surgery, and no evidence of recurrence was found on imaging one year later.

Summary We report a case in which the target sign observed on ultrasonography, consistent with findings on magnetic resonance imaging, was useful in the diagnosis of an intramuscular schwannoma, which is often difficult to diagnose.

Introduction

Schwannoma is a benign tumor originating from Schwann cells of peripheral nerves. According to the Soft Tissue Tumor Registry in Japan, schwannomas are the second most common benign soft tissue tumor after lipomas [1]. Schwannomas are most commonly detected in the head and neck, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves of the extremities; however, they can occur in any part of the body [2].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may reveal a ‘target sign,’ reflecting biphasic histology with central Antoni A (high cellularity) and peripheral Antoni B (low cellularity) regions [3]. Ultrasonography may show similar internal features, but often fails to visualize nerves within muscle tissue.

This study aims to clarify the clinical and ultrasonographic features of abdominal wall schwannomas. A PubMed search using the terms “Schwannoma,” “Ultrasonography,” and “abdominal wall and/or Rectus abdominis” identified eight relevant articles [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. We highlight the potential utility of ultrasonography in the early diagnosis of schwannomas, particularly in resource‐limited settings where MRI is unavailable.

Case History/Examination

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his family for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

A 71‐year‐old man was referred to our hospital for further evaluation following a computed tomography (CT) scan performed at another institution in April 2021, which revealed a suspicious mass near the midline of the upper abdomen. The patient reported local discomfort and mild pain. His medical history included appendicitis and insomnia. No clinical features suggestive of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) or type 2 (NF2), such as café‐au‐lait spots, neurofibromas, or bilateral vestibular schwannomas, were observed.

Ultrasonography revealed a well‐defined hypoechoic mass with posterior acoustic enhancement in the left rectus abdominis. A central hyperechoic area was noted, consistent with the target sign (Figure 1A,B). Superb microvascular imaging showed minimal internal blood flow (Figure 1C), supporting a presumptive diagnosis of schwannoma.

Non‐contrast CT image revealed a well‐defined hypodense mass relative to the left rectus abdominis (Figure 2). MRI revealed a well‐defined abdominal wall mass with imaging features suggestive of a schwannoma (Figure 3). A percutaneous needle biopsy was performed; however, no tumor component was obtained. Based on imaging findings, schwannoma within the rectus abdominis sheath was diagnosed, and surgical resection was planned.

Due to concerns regarding general anesthesia during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the patient elected to postpone surgery and underwent outpatient monitoring for approximately one year.

Treatment was performed under general anesthesia via a 3 cm skin incision directly above the tumor. An elastic hard tumor capsule was identified beneath the rectus abdominis fascia. Enucleation was performed along the capsule. As the fascial defect was minimal, primary closure was achieved without the use of a prosthetic mesh.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

Differential diagnoses for abdominal wall tumors included schwannoma, neurofibroma, lipoma, fibromatosis, and desmoid tumor. Imaging findings were suggestive of schwannoma, characterized by the presence of the target sign. Fine‐needle aspiration cytology (FNA) was nondiagnostic, but enucleation proceeded based on imaging. Macroscopically, the tumor measured less than 30 mm in diameter, was elastic and hard, and had a yellowish‐white cut surface. Histology revealed a fibrous capsule with biphasic Antoni A and B regions, including Verocay bodies (Figure 4). Immunohistochemistry showed S‐100 positivity and negativity for actin‐SM, Desmin, and CD34. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma.

Hematoxylin and eosin stain. (A) Antoni A regions are seen in the center and Antoni B regions in the periphery. (B) Verocay bodies observed on strong magnification, supporting the diagnosis of a schwannoma. No mitotic figures observed.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. At one‐year follow‐up, ultrasonography and CT showed no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Abdominal wall schwannomas are rare peripheral nerve sheath tumors, and their ultrasonographic characteristics remain poorly described in the literature. A PubMed search identified eight previously reported cases [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], in addition to the present case. The mean age was 52.6 years, with a slight male predominance. We reviewed the ultrasonographic findings described in these nine cases (Table 1). Some reports suggest that soft tissue tumors ≥ 50 mm in size are more likely to be malignant. In the literature reports cited in the present study, the tumor diameters ranged from 10 to 80 mm (mean 46 mm, median 56 mm).

All nine cases included in the literature review had hypoechoic masses with well‐defined borders. This search revealed that internal echo assessment was confirmed in seven of nine cases, and all seven cases had heterogeneous internal echoes. Internal blood flow was observed in four of six cases (80%) in which Doppler imaging was performed. These findings suggest that abdominal wall schwannomas typically present as hypoechoic masses with well‐defined borders, heterogeneous internal echoes, and internal vascularity.

Preoperative imaging—including ultrasonography, CT, and MRI—led to a diagnosis of neurogenic tumor in four of the nine cases (44%), but only two cases (22%) were correctly identified as schwannomas. A hallmark feature of schwannomas is the presence of entering and exiting nerves; however, within muscle tissue, these cannot be reliably distinguished from muscle fibers [12]. This limitation may contribute to the difficulty in achieving a definitive diagnosis based solely on imaging.

Appropriate imaging and preoperative biopsy are recommended for the diagnosis of soft tissue tumors [1]. The diagnostic accuracy of FNA for schwannomas is estimated at only 13%, making definitive diagnosis challenging [13]. Perhaps for this reason, of the nine cases of abdominal wall schwannoma, including our case, only two underwent preoperative biopsy. Although not performed in this case, core needle biopsy (CNB) can be effective in some cases, but there are reports that it can be as difficult to diagnose as FNA due to degeneration and other factors.

In our case, the histopathological analysis revealed a central region rich in cellular components and characteristic “Verocay bodies,” consistent with schwannoma, while the peripheral areas showed myxoid and edematous degeneration in low‐cellularity zones, indicating the presence of a target sign.

A retrospective review of ultrasonographic findings revealed internal echoes in seven of nine cases and identified the target sign in three out of seven cases (43%). The detection rate of the target sign was similar between ultrasonography and MRI, suggesting its utility in diagnosing abdominal wall schwannomas, particularly when entry and exit signs are not discernible.

On MRI, the target sign appears as a high‐intensity rim and low‐intensity center on T2‐weighted images. On ultrasonography, it manifests as a biphasic pattern with a hyperechoic center within a hypoechoic mass. This biphasic pattern has been correlated with both histopathological and imaging findings. Histopathologically, it reflects central Antoni A tissue, rich in spindle cells, and peripheral Antoni B tissue, which has low cellularity and a myxoid matrix [14]. Koga et al. [15] reported that the MRI target sign was present in 118 of 199 schwannomas, with a specificity of 100% and sensitivity of 59%. Similarly, ultrasonographic studies have shown that 16 of 32 schwannomas (50%) exhibited a biphasic pattern. In a study of peripheral nerve schwannomas, Yu et al. [16] reported that the target sign was observed in 82.5% of cases on elastography, occurring significantly more frequently than in non‐schwannoma lesions (p < 0.001). Additional ultrasonographic features—including regular morphology, cystic degeneration, polar blood supply, and the target sign—yielded sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy rates of 95.24%, 96.08%, and 95.76%, respectively. These findings suggest that elastography enhances the diagnostic performance of ultrasonography. Nevertheless, the target sign may occasionally be present in other soft tissue tumors. Therefore, diagnosis should be based on a comprehensive assessment of tumor morphology, internal echo pattern, posterior echo enhancement, and vascularity.

Ultrasonography offers several advantages: it is low‐cost, noninvasive, and repeatable, making it particularly valuable in resource‐limited settings where MRI is not readily available.

In conclusion, schwannomas exhibit various imaging features; however, the presence of a target sign on ultrasonography—despite its limited sensitivity—should prompt consideration of schwannoma, particularly when MRI or biopsy findings are inconclusive. This finding is especially valuable in cases, such as the present one, where entry and exit signs are not identifiable.
