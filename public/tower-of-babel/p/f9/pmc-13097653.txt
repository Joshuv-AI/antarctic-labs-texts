Characterization and evaluation of a phage cocktail targeting Salmonella enterica in a Turkey farm
Authors: María Gabriela Vela-Chauvin, Darío X Ramirez-Villacis, Carolina E Armijos, Michael Narvaez, Francisco Quelal-Madrid, Gabriela Bustamante, Carla Torres-Sobrevilla, Alexis Debut, Fernando Corredor, William Calero-Cáceres, António Machado, Sonia Zapata-Mena

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalmonella enterica is a major food-borne pathogen strongly associated with poultry products, causing over 150 million human infections annually despite extensive control measures. Among its serovars, S. enterica serovar Infantis is highly prevalent on poultry farms and represents an increasing food safety and public health concern. Alternative strategies to reduce Salmonella dissemination are urgently needed, and bacteriophages have emerged as promising biocontrol agents.ResultsIn this study, we enriched and characterized a phage cocktail recovered from wastewater and propagated with Salmonella Infantis. The cocktail underwent a comprehensive stability evaluation, including tolerance to a range of temperatures (4–50 °C), acidic conditions (loss of infectivity at pH 2 and pH 4), and oxidative stress (remaining stable after exposure to hydrogen peroxide concentrations up to 100 ppm). It demonstrated the ability to reduce preformed Salmonella biofilms by 39.9%. Genomic characterization was performed via Illumina sequencing, revealing the presence of nine phages belonging to a distinct genera (Kuttervirus, Berlinvirus, Jacunavirus, Rosemountvirus, Felixounavirus, Mooglevirus, Zindervirus, Astrithrvirus, and one unclassified). Importantly, virulence factors, antimicrobial resistance genes, or lysogenic elements were not detected in the genomes of eight of the nine phages analyzed; for one phage, incomplete genomic information prevented full assessment, supporting the genetic safety of the cocktail. Field evaluation under commercial turkey-rearing conditions showed that both control and phage treated poultry houses tested negative for Salmonella at the end of the production cycle. Therefore, treatment efficacy could not be conclusively determined. However, the study supports the feasibility and safety of applying enriched phage lysates in a commercial production setting, as phage application did not adversely affect production parameters.ConclusionsThese findings support the use of well-characterized enriched lysates, as promising candidates for biocontrol strategies to improve food safety and reduce the burden of Salmonella in poultry production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04849-4.Keywords: Salmonella enterica, Phage therapy, Turkey production, Metagenome assembled genomes

Background

Salmonella enterica is recognized as one of the leading pathogens responsible for foodborne illness [1]. According to the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), an estimated 150 million cases of foodborne illness caused by Salmonella occur annually, raising significant public health concerns [2, 3]. Poultry and poultry-related products are frequently associated with this bacterium, as they act as main reservoirs of non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) owing to the ability of Salmonella to colonize and persist in the poultry digestive system [4]. In fact, the Food Safety and Inspection Services (FSIS) reported that nearly 16.8% of cases in the United States are directly linked to these food sources [5]. Salmonella sp. includes more than 2600 known serotypes, while many are incapable of colonizing poultry, those that can pose a significant public health risk, as they can cause disease in humans. Salmonella Enteritidis, Typhimurium, Newport, Javiana, Heidelberg, Montevideo, Braenderup, Muenchen, Infantis, and Saintpaul are the most reported serovars [4, 6].

Turkey has also been associated with Salmonella outbreaks [7–9], with approximately 5.5% of Salmonella sp. cases linked to this food matrix [6]. Rostagno et al. reported an overall prevalence of this bacterium in turkey flocks of 33.3% in a study in the United States [10], whereas another study in the European Union reported a 13.6% prevalence in breeding flocks [11]. Cui et al. explored the occurrence of Salmonella in turkey skin and reported a prevalence of 42% [12]. Cason et al. reported that the prevalence of this pathogen in ground turkey was 15.9%, whereas other studies from Germany and the United States have reported over 20.8% prevalence in the same food matrix [6, 11, 13]. In Ecuador, the overall prevalence of Salmonella in poultry farms is estimated at 41.4%; however, specific prevalence data for turkey farms are not yet available [14].

In addition to its health impact, Salmonella sp. also poses serious challenges to the food industry, particularly due to the growing issue of antimicrobial resistance, which complicates efforts to reduce its prevalence. The poultry industry uses different strategies to control Salmonella sp., including good agricultural practices (GAPs), irradiation, ozone, chemical disinfectants, antibiotics, and vaccines (only for S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium) [1, 2, 15]. However, none of these approaches have proven fully effective, and some are losing efficacy as resistance continues to rise. Therefore, a new alternative is required to enhance food safety, one of the most promising, is bacteriophages [1, 2, 16].

Bacteriophages (phages) are natural predators of bacteria and are currently being investigated as tools for controlling Salmonella sp. Their specificity makes them safe for use, as animals, plants, and even the commensal microbiota are not affected by their application [1, 17, 18]. For these reasons, certain phages have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) antimicrobial food additives [1, 19]. SalmoFresh from Intralytix, Inc, PhageGuard from Micreos Food Safety and Inspektor from PhageLab are some commercially available products that use phages as antibacterial compounds [20–22]. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated their potential effectiveness in controlling Salmonella sp. in liquid eggs [23, 24], chicken meat and carcasses [16, 25–29], laying hens [30] and chicken models [19, 31–34].

Few studies have evaluated the effectiveness of phages for the control of Salmonella in turkey meat, carcasses or rearing. For example, Higgins et al. investigated the use of phages in commercially processed turkeys and reported a statistically significant reduction in Salmonella sp. recovery of more than 50% [35]. Other studies conducted on turkey breasts and ground turkey have also demonstrated that phages are effective against Salmonella sp. in these matrices [36, 37]. However, in a study in 2007, Higgins et al. applied phages obtained from broiler chicks to turkey poults and did not observe a statistically significant reduction in Salmonella sp. levels [38]. For this reason, the application of bacteriophages as a strategy to control Salmonella sp. on turkey farms, requires further understanding and investigation.

In this study, we enriched and characterized a phage cocktail from a wastewater sample collected in Quito and evaluated its potential as a biocontrol agent against Salmonella sp. on a turkey farm. The efficacy of the cocktail was validated through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assays, to assess its activity, stability and safety.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Salmonella enterica and Escherichia coli strains were obtained from the Parasitology Laboratory repository at Universidad San Francisco de Quito (USFQ) (15 strains: 12 S. enterica and 3 E. coli) and from poultry farms (88 S. enterica strains). Most bacterial strains were isolated from farm environments, chicken/turkey ceca, chicken meat, or meat flour (Supplementary Table 1). Four collection reference strains were also used in this study (S. enterica serovar Infantis ATCC 51741, S. enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC 14028, S. enterica serovar Enteritidis ATCC 13076, and E.coli ATCC 25922). All strains were preserved in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) + 20% glycerol at -20 °C and revived in Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) medium at 37 °C overnight. Salmonella serovars were identified via multiplex PCR which amplifies 5 Salmonella Typhimurium (STM) and 5 Salmonella Typhi (STY) genetic loci that lead to different band patterns according to the serotype [39, 40].

Phage enrichment

For phage enrichment, a wastewater sample from the Machángara River (-0.18506369555738206, -78.4148951900149) was collected during the dry season (February 2024) and taken to the laboratory for storage at 4 °C. The Benson (1990) [41] method was used with slight modifications. We used nine bacterial strains of S. Infantis (8748,9202,9203,9204,9205,9206,9207,9208, and 9209) isolated from a turkey farm as hosts to produce a cocktail further known as MT83.

Phage titers

Viral titers were calculated for the nine host strains. For this purpose, each strain was incubated in TSB at 37 °C for 4–6 h until the exponential phase was reached. Serial dilutions of the MT83 cocktail were prepared in 10-fold steps, generating dilutions from 10− 1 to 10− 6. Subsequently, 100 µL of each dilution and 100 µL of each bacterium were mixed and plated by the double-layer agar method using TSA (0.7%) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. After incubation, the number of plaques was counted and used to determine the viral titer [42].

Cocktail host range

The host range of this cocktail was tested against 103 Salmonella sp. strains (including the hosts) and 4 strains of E. coli (Supplementary Table 1) using a modification of the Azeredo and Sillankorva spot test [43]. To determine lytic activity, the scoring system described by Huang et al. [44] was used with slight modifications. Strains were classified according to the following categories: strong lytic activity or complete clearance (4), strong lytic activity with bacterial colonies in the cleared zone (3), isolated plaques (2), weak lytic activity with substantial turbidity throughout the cleared zone (1), and no clearing (0). Strains with scores of 3 or 4 were considered susceptible to the phage cocktail and were used to calculate the percentage of lytic activity for each serotype. following preliminary tests, further characterization of the phage cocktail was carried out using a single host strain. S. Infantis (9205) was chosen due to its multidrug-resistant phenotype determined by a Kirby-Bauer assay, which revealed resistance to ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, making it a relevant model for potential therapeutic applications.

Determination of optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI)

The MOI indicates the proportion of phages to host bacteria in an infection [45]. To determine the best MOI for this cocktail of S. Infantis cells were infected with different ratios of MT83 (MOIs of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001). Each mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min allowing the phages to be adsorbed. Subsequently, 5 mL of TSB was added to each tube, and the cultures were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, all the samples were filtered with a 0,22 μm filter [16, 45]. Phage titers at each MOI were determined using a spot test assay, as described in the stability experiments. Each assay was performed in triplicate in three different experiments.

Thermal and pH stability

For thermal stability assessment, the cocktail was incubated at 25, 37, 50, 70, and 90 °C for one hour, followed by rapid cooling using a cooling rack [18].

For pH stability evaluation, the pH of the cocktail was adjusted to 2, 4, 8, or 9, as previously reported for the digestive tracts of chickens and turkeys [46–48]. The desired pH values were obtained by adding 2 M HCl or 2 M NaOH. After one hour of incubation, the pH was readjusted to approximately pH 7 using the same solutions [1].

To determine whether temperature or pH affects phage viral titer, we performed a spot test. Briefly, 100 µL of exponential phase bacteria was mixed in 5 mL of top agar and poured into a Petri dish containing bottom agar, after which 10 µL of the phage dilution from each treatment was spotted over the bacterial lawn. The Petri dishes were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Phages not exposed to any temperature (4 °C) or pH change (pH 6.7 ± 0.3) were used as controls. Each assay was performed in triplicate in three different experiments.

Hydrogen peroxide sensitivity test

For this assay, the MT83 cocktail was exposed to hydrogen peroxide solutions (100, 50, and 25 ppm) for one hour at room temperature. After incubation, the viral titer was calculated using the spot test as described above. Phages with no treatment or water supplemented with different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (200, 100, 50, and 25 ppm) without phages were used as controls [49]. Each assay was performed in triplicate in three different experiments.

Biofilm treatment

Biofilm eradication and optical density (OD)

S. Infantis 9205 cultures were grown overnight, and a 1 × 10⁷ colony-forming unit (CFU)/mL inoculum was prepared in TSB. A 200 µL aliquot was added to 96-well plates, with media-only controls (sterility controls), and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions for biofilm formation. After incubation, planktonic cells were removed by aspirating the liquid media and gently washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. Then, 150 µL of MT83 phage cocktail (MOI of 1 or 0.1) was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for another 24 h. Finally, the biofilms were washed twice with PBS (pH 7.4) [17, 50–52]. After the washes, 100 µL of PBS was added to each well, and the biofilm was vigorously detached and resuspended in saline solution. OD determination was subsequently performed using a MULTISKAN Sky spectrophotometer (Thermo-Scientific) at 570 nm. TSB not inoculated and TSB inoculated with bacteria without phages were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Each assay was performed in triplicate in three different experiments.

Viable cell quantification

To determine the viable cells present within the biofilm samples, a CFU counting assay was performed. Triplicate samples from each treatment and control were serially diluted. Then, we applied the three-drop technique, where triplicate volumes (10 µL) of dilutions 10− 6 and 10− 5 from each sample were dropped on TSA agar and allowed to run down the plate to spread the drop. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, after which the colonies were counted [50, 51]. Each assay was performed in triplicate in three different experiments.

Fluorescence microscopy analysis

Fluorescence staining was performed using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (L7012, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following the manufacturer’s instructions with slight modifications, 3 µL of DAPI (diluted 1:20 from the original stock) was added to three wells, each containing 100 µL of PBS and the resuspended biofilm from either the treatments or the control group. The plate was then incubated in the dark for 15 min. After DAPI staining, 3 µL of LIVE/DEAD working solution (0.167 mM SYTO 9 and 1 mM propidium iodide (PI) was added to the same wells. The plate was incubated again in the dark for another 15 min [53, 54]. Next, 2 µL of mounting solution was placed on a glass slide and mixed with 5 µL of the stained sample. A coverslip was placed on top.

Imaging was performed using an Olympus BX50F4 epifluorescence microscope (Olympus Optical Corporation, Japan) equipped with an AmScope MU633-FL digital camera (AmScope, USA), and images were captured using AmScope software (v 3.7). A total of 15 images per fluorescence filter were taken for each sample, ensuring that the entire coverslip area was covered [55]. The number of cells per field was determied with Cell Profiler software (v 4.2.8), and the number of cells/mL was reported for subsequent statistical analysis. Two different experiments were performed.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy was used to obtain images of the phages from concentrated suspension of MT83 1 × 109 plaque-forming units (PFU/mL). For TEM visualization, 10 µL of MT83 was placed onto a pure carbon-coated copper grid, then the sample was stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (PTA) for 1 s. Micrographs were acquired at 80 kV using a FEI-Tecnai G20 Spirit Twin microscope, equipped with an Eagle 4 K HR camera [56].

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

A high-titer phage suspension was used for DNA extraction with the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Before extraction, the sample was treated with 1 U/µL RNase-free DNase (RQ1 DNase, Promega) to eliminate host DNA. The extracted DNA was then lyophilized and sent for sequencing using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (FISABIO, Spain). The quality of the sequencing data was assessed by examining average base quality, GC content, and adapter contamination [57, 58]. The raw reads are available under the accession number SRR34735845, bioproject: PRJNA1294783. Non-viral reads were extracted prior to assembly (refer to Supplementary Fig. 1 to visualize the full pipeline). Phage assembly was performed in MetaSpades (v 4.2.0) [59–62] on the Galaxy platform (v25.0.2.dev0) through the public server at usegalaxy.org [63]. Metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) contigs were analyzed in Phigaro (v.2.4.0) [64] and the Phastest platform [65] to remove non-phage contigs. Homology between viral contigs was established via rBLAST (v 1.5.0) [66]. Contigs with 99% homology were aligned and clustered to improve the MAG quality with DECIPHER (v.3.4.0) [67]. All contigs maintained their original sequence, except MAG MT83_8, for which the consensus sequences increased by 9320 pb compared with the longest contig in the cluster. Phage MAGs were annotated using pharokka (v 1.3.2) [68] and Bakta (v 1.9.4) [69] through galaxy.eu server and the Phabox platform(v 2.0) [70]. Phage MAGs graphics were generated using the Proksee platform [71]. To determine virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes, we used AMR Finder Plus (v 3.12.8) [72], ABRicate (v 1.0.1) [73], and the virulence factor database VFDB [74]. To confirm that the phages found were not lysogenic, CheckV(v 1.0.3) [75] on the Galaxy Europe, and Phabox platforms (v 2.0) were used [70]. The NCBI BLAST tool was used to identify similar sequences, to determine related phages and for taxonomic assignment [76].

Field application of the MT83 cocktail in Turkeys

One field trial under commercial rearing conditions was carried out on a turkey farm in Ecuador. This farm belongs to a company that handles most of the production and processing cycle of the birds (breeding, rearing, fattening, slaughtering, processing, and marketing). The farm has four houses, which were all part of the trial. Two were used as controls for their regular treatment (acidifying agent), and two were used for phage therapy. All the houses had similar flock sizes, feed and feed additives, litter compositions, biosecurity measures and vaccination schedules [32]. Due to a sanitary issue, ciprofloxacin was administered in H3. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes information on each house regarding production conditions. All the houses were positive for Salmonella sp. according to the traditional methodology described in ISO 6579-1:2017 [77] (turkey age between 50 and 57 days). Phage application was carried out only in two houses on days 95 (House 2) and 99 (House 1). The phage suspension was administered in drinking water and by aspersion at final viral titers of 1.5 × 104 and 1.5 × 105 PFU/mL, respectively. The drinking water supply was stopped 2 h before dosing to make the birds thirsty and accelerate the uptake of phages [32]. Phages were added to the drinking water at one dose three days before slaughter and by aspersion for three consecutive days.

To determine whether the treatment was effective, 30 cecal samples per house were taken from the slaughterhouse and transported at 4 °C. Laboratory testing was performed within 24 h after sampling, and traditional methodology was used to detect the presence of Salmonella sp [77].

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for non-parametric data (Kruskal-Wallis) was used to determine significant differences with Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner (DSCF) Post Hoc Adjustment for multiple comparisons between all pairs of groups, where p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant [78, 79].

We found that MT83 was able to produce good lysis (3 or 4 in lysis scale) in 81.3% of the tested strains (Table 1), being more effective against S. Infantis, which is one of the most frequent serovars in poultry and turkey farms [6, 11]. Unfortunately, the cocktail had no efficacy in controlling other serovars such as S. Amsterdam, S. Derby, and S. Albany. As expected, the MT83 cocktail did not affect E. coli strains, indicating its specificity for S. enterica.

Viral titer and MOI

The average viral titer was 2.7 × 109 PFU/mL, ranging from 8.0 × 108 to 4.9 × 109 PFU/mL (Supplementary Table 3).

The most effective MOIs for MT83 were 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001, which resulted in viral titers one log higher than those obtained with MOIs of 1 and 10, and two log higher than those obtained with an MOI of 100 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 4).

Stability analysis under various pH and temperature conditions

The phage viability remained stable between pH 7–9 and within the temperature range of 4–50 °C. These results revealed that the MT83 cocktail exhibits broad tolerance to variations in temperature and pH, a key characteristic for effective phage therapy. However, viral titers were significantly reduced (p < 0.05) under acidic conditions (pH 2 and 4) and at elevated temperatures (70 °C), as shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6. Complete inactivation occurred following exposure to 90 °C. Conditions of pH 7 and 4 °C were used as controls, reflecting typical storage conditions and confirming the phage´s enhanced stability under these parameters.

Hydrogen peroxide sensitivity test

The Cocktail MT83 showed no sensitivity to hydrogen peroxide, as the viral titer remained stable despite 1 h of exposure to 25, 50, or 100 ppm of the chemical compound (Fig. 3). There was no significant difference (p > 0.05) in the viral titer for any of the treatments (Supplementary Table 7).

Biofilm treatment

Our results revealed that compared with the untreated control, the phage cocktail MT83 achieved 39.9% biofilm reduction, indicating a significant decrease in biofilm biomass (p < 0.001) after 24 h of exposure at a MOI of 1. In contrast, treatment at a MOI of 0.1 was not effective, resulting in only a 1.2% biofilm reduction (p = 0.936) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 8). In the colony-forming unit assays, the results were similar, as the percentages of viability were 74.2% and 98.4% at MOIs of 1 and 0.1, respectively. In this case, significant differences with the control were only found with the MOI 1 treatment (p < 0.001).

Genomic analysis

The genome analysis allowed the identification of 9 double-stranded DNA phages with sizes ranging from 11 to 151 kb in the MT83 cocktail (Table 3). Seven of them are related to other Salmonella phages previously reported, one to an Enterobacteria phage, and one to a Pectobacterium phage based on whole-genome BLASTn analysis against NCBI Refseq database (Supplementary Tables 9 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Overall, 51.9% of the CDCs detected in all phages corresponded to functional genes which were classified into 4 groups: Regulatory/Auxliliary, Lysis module, DNA replication and packaging, and structural genes (Fig. 7). The remaining 48.1% corresponded to Hypothetical proteins, being phage MT83_9 and phage MT83_6 the genomes with more and less unknown function genes (66.7% and 21.7%, respectively). Phages MT83_1, MT83_3 and MT83_8 had tRNAs. The VFDB, AMR Finder, and ABRicate results revealed that none of the phages carried any virulence or antimicrobial resistance-related genes. Additionally, all phages were categorized in Phage AI and Phabox as virulent phages, with no presence of lysogenic genes, as confirmed with CheckV. All of these characteristics are important for phage application, showing that this cocktail seems safe for use as a biocontrol agent. The complete and partial genome sequences were deposited into the GenBank database with the accession numbers listed in Table 3.

Phages were identified using BLASTn against nucleotide databases, revealing that they belong to eight different viral genera across multiple families, and one remained unclassified (MT83_5). Seven identified genera have previously been associated with Salmonella phages (Kuttervirus, Felixounavirus, Rosemountvirus, Zindervirus, Berlinvirus and Jacunavirus) and the remaining genera have been associated with different Enterobacteria strains such as Citrobacter sp., Shigella flexneri, and E. coli.

Kuttervirus, Felixounavirus, Mooglevirus and Rosemountvirus are characterized by contractile tails; Zindervirus, Astrithrvirus and Berlinvirus possess short, non-contractile tails; and Jacunavirus features a long, non-contractile tail. MT83_5 showed the highest similarity to Salmonella phage vB_SeEM_UALMA_PCSE1 which has been previously reported as a myovirus [80]. These morphologies are consistent with our TEM results, which revealed representatives of all three morphotypes (Table 2).

Field application

To ensure that the cocktail administered through drinking water and house aspersion did not negatively impact turkey performance, we compared production parameters from all houses with the reference ranges recorded on the farm throughout 2024 (Table 4). All the parameters were within the ranges indicating the safety of the cocktail, as it did not negatively affect the growth or health of the animals.

Discussion

Poultry is the most widely consumed source of animal protein in Ecuador and worldwide, largely because of its low cost, culinary versatility, and favorable nutritional profile [81, 82]. In Ecuador, annual poultry production exceeds 518,000 metric tons, of which more than 18,000 metric tons correspond to turkey meat [83]. Despite extensive industry efforts, the persistence of Salmonella sp. remains a major public health concern [84]. The prevalence in poultry products is high, with rates of 38.1% in raw meat and 55.1% in chicken carcasses in Ecuador [14, 85]. These data underscore the urgent need for novel strategies to control this pathogen in poultry production systems. In this study, we enriched, characterized, and evaluated the MT83 phage cocktail, both in vitro and in a field trial on a commercial turkey farm, as a potential tool to mitigate Salmonella in this context.

The MT83 cocktail exhibited a broad host range, lysing 81.3% of the strains tested across ten serotypes while maintaining strict specificity against Salmonella sp. and showing no lytic activity against Escherichia coli. This genus-restrictive spectrum can be considered advantageous, as it reduces the risk of disrupting beneficial microbiota [44, 86–88]. Nevertheless, some authors highlight the utility of polyvalent phages capable of infecting multiple bacterial species, particularly in animal husbandry settings where mixed infections are common [89, 90].

Interestingly, not all strains belonging to the same serotype exhibited susceptibility to the cocktail MT83, indicating that sensitivity was determined at the strain level, reinforcing the complexity of phage-bacteria interactions, as reported in other studies [1, 91] where phages have different lytic efficiencies in Salmonella strains from the same serotypes. This could be explained by the fact that the host range is not a fixed property; it can evolve over time, changing the specificity, and it depends on the physiological state, phage resistance mechanisms, and receptor availability of each strain [92, 93].

Multiplicity of infection (MOI) assays revealed that lower MOIs (0.001-0.1) produced higher viral titers, which is consistent with previous studies showing that efficient replication often occurs at low ratios, specifically 0.001 and 0.01 [1, 16, 94–96]. Amini et al., Wójcicki et al., and Sonalika et al. reported maximum replication and bacterial reduction at MOIs of approximately 0.01 [84, 97, 98]. However, other studies have shown that higher MOIs (> 100) may control the bacterial population more effectively [37, 99]. Most likely, at such elevated MOIs, “lysis from without” occurs more frequently, rapidly killing host cells by overwhelming concentrations of lysis-phage-derived enzymes [84, 99]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the optimal MOI is phage-specific and may vary depending on the efficiency of adsorption and the underlying lysis mechanisms employed by each phage [45]. From an industrial standpoint, cocktails such as MT83 that replicate effectively at low MOIs are particularly attractive, as they reduce production costs and facilitate scale-up [97].

MT83 remained viable between pH 7–9 and temperatures ranging from 4 to 50°C, similar to observations in other Salmonella phages [1, 100]. At 70 °C, the titer decreased significantly (30%), and complete inactivation occurred at 90 °C, which is consistent with previous reports that most tailed phages are inactivated above 80°C [44, 101]. Some exceptions exist, such as phage Vb_SalP_TR2, which tolerates exposure to 80 °C [16], underscoring the importance of phage-specific characterization. In practical terms, the stability profile of MT83 is compatible with farm environments, where phages are mainly exposed to avian body temperature (42 °C) [102, 103].

The pH of the poultry gastrointestinal tract ranges from approximately 2–9 [46–48]. Therefore, our MT83 cocktail tolerated alkaline conditions but lost viability at pH 2 and 4, retaining only 56–74% infectivity. Similar acid sensitivity has been described in other Salmonella phages [1, 33, 98, 104, 105]. Sada and Sisay noted that pH values between 5 and 9 are generally tolerated by most tailed phages [18]. Despite the loss of viability under acidic conditions, more than half of the phages remained viable. This trait is crucial, considering that phages are frequently exposed to low-pH environments on poultry farms due to the use of acidifying agents in water and the inherent acidity of the poultry stomach [33]. Kuźmińska et al. demonstrated that administering phages after feeding chickens minimizes phage inactivation, as gastric acid is neutralized by food, increasing phage survival [33]. Therefore, the MT83 cocktail should preferably be administered to fed animals, and the time of its application should be optimized in future in vivo trials.

As previously mentioned, some farms utilize acidifying agents to reduce bacterial loads in poultry drinking water [33]. In particular, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is commonly employed for water disinfection [106]. The MT83 phages were resistant to hydrogen peroxide at concentrations up to 100 ppm, which is twice as high as those typically used for drinking water disinfection [107]. These findings suggest that the MT83 cocktail could be compatible with existing water-treatment practices. Moreover, Hayes et al. evaluated the effects of various chemical compounds on phages and reported that hydrogen peroxide is not an effective virucidal agent, as it requires high concentrations to inactivate them [49].

Salmonella biofilms are major contributors to persistence in poultry facilities and food-processing environments [108]. Therefore, phage therapy could be an alternative for control. We found that MT83 reduced biofilm biomass by 39.7% at a MOI of 1; these results are comparable to the reductions reported by Ning et al. (~ 56%) [109] and Islam et al. (44–48%) [110]. In contrast, a marked loss of activity was observed at a lower MOI (0.1), where biomass reduction dropped only 0.9%, underscoring a strong MOI-dependent effect. These results have been previously reported by Hosny et al. [111] and in studies using Pseudomonas phages, where biofilm disruption occurred at MOIs of 10 and 1 but not at 0.1 [112, 113], emphasizing the dose dependence of phage-mediated biofilm disruption.

In addition to dose dependence, this decline could reflect limited phage diffusion and adsorption within the sessile biofilm state, due to extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) matrix restricting phage and phage receptors access to embedded bacterial cells [112–114]. Higher MOIs are likely to enhance biofilm degradation, as phages possess depolymerases capable of breaking down extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), thereby disrupting the biofilm matrix and facilitating phage bacterial infection and subsequent replication. In contrast, lower MOIs result in reduced enzyme concentrations, potentially limiting their diffusion and hence their efficacy against biofilms [115, 116]. Cell viability results reinforce these statements as with a higher MOI 1 there was a 25.8% reduction against control, whereas at MOI 0.1 only 1.8% reduction was achieved possibly due to low adsorption efficiency.

Biofilm biomass reduction reflects combined removal of bacterial cells and degradation of extracellular matrix, whereas cell viability measurements indicate reduction in bacterial cell count [112]. Both parameters are essential for understanding phage activity in biofilms, as they help distinguish if disruption is due to phage-associated enzymes only or if a productive phage infection is also happening. In this study, results suggest that enzymatic activity plays an important role, as greater reductions were observed in total biomass. However, the decrease in colony forming unit and cell viability assays indicate that phages somehow replicate and phage disruption seems to be a result from both enzymatic action and phage replication. Although biofilm reduction by the MT83 cocktail was moderate in this initial study, even partial disruption could have significant industrial implications by weakening biofilm resilience and facilitating cleaning procedures. Additionally, the combination of the MT83 cocktail with detergents or enzymatic treatment may enhance outcomes, justifying further research.

In the TEM analysis, morphologies corresponding to siphovirus, myovirus, and podovirus were detected in the MT83 cocktail. The results aligned with the virion dimensions reported by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) for each corresponding phage family. For example, Salmonella phage MT83_7 belongs to the Cajensviridae family, which, according to the ICTV, typically has head diameters ranging from 50 to 70 nm and tail lengths between 200 and 230 nm, values that closely match our morphological measurements. Similarly, MT83_6 and MT83_4, classified as podoviruses from the Autotranscriptaviridae and Autosignataviridae families, respectively, displayed head sizes between 49.1 and 81.7 nm and tails shorter than 20 nm, which is in agreement with literature-reported metrics [117]. Additionally, MT83_8, MT83_5, MT83_3, MT83_2, and MT83_1 (all myoviruses) belong to different families; nevertheless, their dimensions fall within the reported sizes [80, 118]. Furthermore, the most prevalent morphology detected in the TEM assay was icosahedral head and contractile tail typically found in myoviruses. This observation corroborates our genomic findings, which identified that five out of nine phages exhibited this morphology. Six phages present in the MT83 cocktail showed strong similarity to previously reported Salmonella phages and one to an Enterobacteria phage, isolated from dairy and poultry farms, pond water, and wastewater around the world [119–124]. However, MT83_5 shared only 79.16% similarity with Salmonella phage vB_SeEM_UALMA_PCSE1 and exhibited a query coverage of just 36%. Interestingly, when vB_SeEM_UALMA_PCSE1 was first sequenced, no significant matches to known sequences were identified [80]. This low similarity and limited coverage suggest that MT83_5, together with its closest relative, may represent new or underexplored phages, that are not that closely related.

Another noteworthy finding was that MT83_9 showed its closest match to Pectobacterium phage DU_PP_III. Although this phage has previously been associated with the Salmonella phages Astrihr and Assan, Miroshnikov et al. reported that its encapsidation and portal proteins cluster with homologs from Salmonella phages [125]. MT83_9 shares 87.43% nucleotide similarity with 74% query coverage to its closest NCBI reference, suggesting that it is also an underexplored phage that warrants further investigation.

Genomic analyses revealed that seven phages had completeness values exceeding 95% according to CheckV, with genome sizes falling within the ICTV-reported ranges for their respective families or the most closely related Salmonella phage reference (Supplementary Table 10) [117]. Notably, MT83_3 displayed a slightly smaller genome (73.9 kb) compared to the Andersonviridae reference range (78.3–92.1 kb) [117]. This difference may reflect the completeness level estimated by CheckV, which indicated 83.9% coverage for MT83_3. Similarly, MT83_8 presented a much smaller genome (45.3 kb) relative to the Andersonviridae range, which is consistent with its lower completeness value of 51.41% reported by CheckV.

The recovery of partial or fragmented phage genomes has been frequently reported in other metagenomic studies [126–128]. These outcomes are likely explained by the relatively low abundance of certain viral populations, sequencing quality and depth, and intrinsic plasticity and mosaicism of viral genomes, which sometimes lead to chimeric contigs that are difficult to resolve [129, 130]. These findings highlight the complexity of the phageome and emphasize the need for more refined bioinformatic tools specifically tailored to viral and phage genomics. The use of combined long and short-read sequencing strategies may improve genome recovery [126, 128]. However, an important limitation remains: many bioinformatic pipelines rely on viral databases that are not fully curated or complete, which can restrict accurate classification and assembly [129, 130].

Regardless of genome completeness, all MAGs were annotated. We found that 48.1% of the predicted CDSs were classified as hypothetical proteins, a common finding in phage genomics due to the substantial amount of dark viral matter that remains uncharacterized [45]. All phage genomes analyzed contained terminase genes, which play a central role in DNA packaging [131]. Additionally, genes encoding structural components such as head and tail proteins, lysis-associated proteins (including holins, endolysins, and Rz-spanins) and DNA replication enzymes (such as DNA polymerases, DNA ligases, DNA primases, and DNA helicases) were consistently identified.

Interestingly, all phages, except MT83_8 and MT83_9, encoded at least one CDS associated with host interaction and evasion, although the specific mechanism varied among them. For example, Salmonella phage MT83_1 harbors a gene encoding a superinfection exclusion protein, which prevents secondary phage infections by blocking the entry of additional phages into an already infected host cell [2]. This mechanism has been previously described in other Salmonella phages, such as Kenya-K19 and Kenya K-37 [132]. In addition, Salmonella phage MT83_1 encodes a MazG-like pyrophosphatase, which is implicated in the depletion of the starvation alarmone (p)ppGpp. By modulating the host´s stress response and reducing abortive infection events, this enzyme promotes successful phage propagation [133]. Notably, the same gene was also identified in Salmonella phage MT83_3 and MT83_5.

Several phages also carry genes that contribute to evasion of host defense mechanisms, particularly those targeting phage DNA. Salmonella phage MT83_2 possesses a Gam-like protein, that is functionally analogous to the RecBCD nuclease inhibitor found in Salmonella phage MT83_6. Both proteins inhibit the host RecBCD system, preventing degradation of phage DNA and thereby supporting successful infection [134–136]. Salmonella phage MT83_4 employs a different strategy, encoding an Ocr-like anti-restriction protein that inhibits host restriction enzymes. This mechanism has been previously reported in Salmonella Przondovirus K30 [132, 137].

Salmonella phage MT83_6 is particularly notable for its multifaceted defense evasion strategies. In addition to the RecBCD inhibitor, it possesses a toxin-antitoxin inhibitor protein that suppresses the host´s suicidal response, thereby ensuring phage survival [135]. Moreover, this phage harbors a DNA methyltransferase that modifies phage DNA in a way that allows it to effectively evade host restriction enzyme activity [132, 137]. Salmonella phage MT83_7 and MT83_5 also encode DNA methyltransferases, suggesting convergent strategies among different phage genera to circumvent bacterial restriction systems.

Salmonella phages MT83_1, MT83_3, and MT83_8 contains tRNAs within their genomes, a feature commonly observed in large-genome phages [138]. These tRNAs are thought to increase the efficiency of gene expression during phage replication, thereby reducing latency [138, 139]. The presence of tRNAs has also been more frequently associated with lytic phages than with temperate ones, and with phages displaying broader host ranges, since tRNAs may facilitate adaptation to diverse bacterial hosts [138–140]. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that members of the Kuttervirus, Mooglevirus, and Felixounavirus genera are typically characterized by wide host ranges [1, 141–143].

For therapeutic application, phages must be strictly lytic, as lysogeny can compromise treatment efficacy. Additionally, they should not carry genes encoding virulence factors or antimicrobial resistance genes [144]. Eight out of nine of the MT83 phages met these criteria. Genome annotation revealed no antimicrobial resistance genes, virulence factor genes, integrases, repressors, recombinases, or other lysogeny-associated genes. However, for MT83_8, safety profile could not be confirmed as almost half of the genome is missing. It is noteworthy that the annotated region lacks any undesirable genes, and Mooglevirus members have previously been reported to be strictly lytic [145], with no virulence or antimicrobial resistance genes identified [143, 146–148].

Taken together, these findings support the safety of the MT83 cocktail as a farm-level biocontrol tool, showing a broad host range across different Salmonella serotypes, stability under a range of environmental conditions, and the absence of undesirable genetics elements in the sequenced genomic regions.

When any farm-level intervention is implemented, it is essential to evaluate potential adverse effects on animal performance, such as weight loss, increased mortality, or increased feed conversion rates [19, 31]. In this study, none of these parameters were negatively impacted by phage therapy, reaffirming the safety of the MT83 cocktail for use in poultry farming. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of large-scale application of a Salmonella phage cocktail in a commercial turkey farm setting. Previous studies have focused on chickens or were conducted under controlled conditions; nonetheless, these studies also reported no negative impact on production performance [31, 149, 150]. In some cases, phage applications even reduce mortality and enhance weight gain [149, 151, 152].

Regarding the control of Salmonella sp., all poultry houses in this study tested negative for the pathogen at the end of the production cycle. However, because the control houses also tested negative, the observed elimination of Salmonella cannot be specifically attributed to the phage treatment per se. Clearance of the pathogen may instead reflect the combined effects of biosecurity measures, good rearing practices, environmental factors, sampling limitations or a combination thereof.

In the control houses, acidifying agents were used as part of the routine protocol. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that phage treatment and acidification may lead to comparable outcomes under commercial conditions. To draw definitive conclusions regarding phage therapy, additional field trails with alternative study designs would be required. However, under commercial production systems, control houses must receive some form of intervention, as leaving animals untreated is not feasible due to food safety and animal welfare requirements. Consequently, similar outcomes may be observed if the study design or intervention strategy are not modified.

Despite these limitations, previous studies have reported successful phage therapy outcomes in poultry. For example, Clavijo et al. reported complete elimination of Salmonella sp. in phage treated groups [31], while Pelyuntha et al. observed a reduction in prevalence from 70% to 0% after five phage doses [153]. Other studies, including those involving intentional inoculation with Salmonella sp., have also reported significant reductions following phage application [154–156]. However, Higgins et al. noted a different outcome in turkey poults, observing an initial reduction until 24 h and then followed by bacterial rebound after 48 h [38]. This highlights the potential for bacteria to evolve phage resistance. To mitigate this, phage cocktails are recommended, since it is less likely that bacteria will simultaneously develop resistance to multiple phages [19, 153, 157]. According to the literature, phages from distinct genera often recognize different bacterial receptors, which could lead to an enhanced efficacy and host range [84, 153, 158, 159]. We found that the MT83 cocktail contains nine phages belonging to different genera, this diversity probably contributes to the increased efficacy and broader host range of our cocktail. However, further experiments will be required to fully demonstrate and confirm this hypothesis and to determine synergism or antagonism withing these phages.

Despite these promising results, this study has certain limitations. First, the stability, biofilm, and MOI analyses were conducted using only one (9205) of the nine bacterial host strains of the MT83 cocktail, which may limit the generalization of these findings. The long-term stability of the phage cocktail and the potential emergence of bacterial resistance were not evaluated in this study, which is important as field-applied phages must be continuously updated, given that circulating Salmonella serovars and their resistance patterns may change over time [16]. The genomic analyses relied on currently available databases, which remain incomplete; consequently, several predicted genes could not be functionally annotated. Sequence completeness in MT83_8 does not allow us to conclusively rule out the presence of virulence, lysogeny or antimicrobial resistance genes in the unassembled region. Regarding the phage lysate, because individual phages were not separately characterized, potential antagonistic or synergistic interactions among phages were not assessed, and further analyses would be required to provide additional insight. Finally, it should be noted that in our trial, the control houses tested negative, and no replicate trials were conducted. This limitation underscores the need for follow-up studies to validate these preliminary findings, given the challenges of controlling all variables under field conditions. Future research should address the optimal dosage, timing of administration, long-term functionality, combination of therapies and detailed analysis of phage-bacteria interactions in real-world environments. However, these results suggest that the MT83 cocktail represents a promising candidate for phage therapy in turkey farms.

Conclusions

The MT83 phage cocktail, which was enriched from wastewater, demonstrated broad lytic activity against multiple Salmonella serovars, high stability under farm-relevant conditions, and genomic features consistent with its safety and therapeutic potential. Its ability to reduce pre-formed biofilms and its apparent efficacy in preliminary field applications further support its promise as a biocontrol strategy in turkey production. However, this study has several limitations, including the lack of replication in field trials, the observation that control flocks also became Salmonella-negative, and partial activity against certain serotypes and biofilms. These findings should therefore be considered preliminary. Future research should focus on multi-farm, replicated trials, optimization of phage dosage and timing, evaluation of phage resistance development, and the potential for combining phage therapy with other interventions. Addressing these gaps will be essential to validate the MT83 cocktail as a sustainable, long-term solution for controlling Salmonella sp. in poultry production systems.
