In vitro activity of cefiderocol against Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolated from adult patients with cystic fibrosis
Authors: Saied Ali, Sinead McDermott
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a key pathogen in cystic fibrosis (CF), driving pulmonary decline and exhibiting resistance through virulence factors and adaptive mutations. Cefiderocol (FDC) is a novel siderophore cephalosporin with activity against Gram-negative bacteria. We aimed to assess the in vitro efficacy of FDC against P. aeruginosa isolates in a CF population.Methods. The study was conducted in a tertiary hospital with a specialist adult CF service. All first isolates of significant respiratory pathogens among this cohort are cryopreserved at −80 °C. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing to FDC was performed as per European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing Disk-Diffusion (version 10) for all stored isolates of P. aeruginosa from 2017 to 2022 inclusive.Results. Eighty-five isolates from seventy-one patients were included. Resistance phenotypes comprised 19% (n=16) multidrug-resistant (MDR), 16% (n=14) extensively drug-resistant (XDR) and 24% (n=20) pandrug-resistant (PDR), with 24 % (n=20) exhibiting the mucoid phenotype. Overall, 85% of isolates were susceptible to FDC, with a mean inhibition zone of 25.2 mm. Antimicrobial activity was retained in 81% of MDR, 86% of XDR, 60% of PDR and 90% of mucoid isolates. Seventy-four per cent of meropenem-non-susceptible isolates remained susceptible to FDC, compared with lower susceptibility to ceftolozane–tazobactam (42%), tobramycin (36%) and ciprofloxacin (22%).Conclusion. FDC exhibited excellent in vitro activity against P. aeruginosa from adults with CF, including highly resistant and mucoid phenotypes. These findings highlight its potential as a salvage option in this high-risk population and provide the first Irish surveillance data to inform antimicrobial stewardship and future clinical use.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive, multi-system disease manifested through mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator gene, leading to ineffective anion–chloride–channel activity [1]. The resulting impaired mucociliary clearance facilitates endurance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in the lung following exposure and, if untreated, the establishment of persistent infection [2]. Prevalence increases with age, with 60–80% of adults with CF eventually becoming chronically infected with P. aeruginosa. It has been well recognized that chronic infection is associated with accelerated decline in lung function and mortality in individuals with CF when compared to age-matched CF patients without P. aeruginosa [34].

In addition to the organism’s propensity to form biofilms and convert to a mucoid phenotype by hyperproduction of alginate [5], emerging resistance to traditional anti-pseudomonal antimicrobials (due to the production of β-lactamases, outer membrane factors and overexpression of efflux pumps) results in significant therapeutic challenges [6]. Therefore, there is a need for new treatment strategies to overcome antimicrobial resistance mechanisms and improve clinical outcomes.

Cefiderocol (FDC) is a novel cephalosporin composed of a catechol-form siderophore and a β-lactam base with lateral chains analogous to ceftazidime and cefepime [7]. As well as exhibiting stability against hydrolysis by various β-lactamases, it exploits an additional and distinctive mechanism to gain entry into bacterial cells – an action mediated via iron-transport channels – such that neither porin loss nor upregulated efflux pumps have been found to significantly impact susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to this antimicrobial in isolation [89].

With the increasing incidence of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR) and pandrug-resistant (PDR) P. aeruginosa strains [10], FDC may prove an invaluable asset among the anti-pseudomonal armamentarium, especially for management of infections in the immunocompromised and those with underlying chronic respiratory illnesses such as CF.

Studies assessing the efficacy of FDC against P. aeruginosa isolated from CF patients remain limited, but an emerging body of evidence highlights both its potential and its challenges. In vitro investigations have shown that FDC maintains excellent activity against MDR and XDR P. aeruginosa, including isolates resistant to ceftolozane–tazobactam and ceftazidime–avibactam [11]. However, antimicrobial resistance can emerge, with mutations identified in ampC, ampD, TonB-dependent receptors and the response regulator pirR, which may contribute to reduced susceptibility [1112]. Importantly, clinical data from the compassionate use programme in the USA, Canada and Europe between April 2016 and November 2020 demonstrated that FDC achieved physician-reported clinical response rates of 69–100% and 28-day all-cause mortality rates of 0–33 % among patients with MDR and carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa, including those with CF [13]. Collectively, these studies underscore both the value of FDC in managing difficult-to-treat P. aeruginosa infections and the need for ongoing surveillance of resistance mechanisms, particularly in high-risk populations such as individuals with CF.

Against this background, our study aimed to evaluate the in vitro activity of FDC against a collection of P. aeruginosa isolates from adult CF patients attending the Irish National Adult Centre for CF over a 5-year period. In doing so, we provide the first surveillance report of its kind in Ireland, offering an essential evidence base to guide prescribing practice and inform future policy development.

Methods

Study setting

St. Vincent’s University Hospital, part of the St. Vincent’s Healthcare Group, is a 600-bed academic teaching hospital providing clinical care across more than 50 different medical specialties. The hospital provides tertiary referral services for a number of specialist services, including adult CF. Approximately 300 patients are currently engaged with the specialist adult CF service [14].

FDC is licensed for use in the Republic of Ireland and, according to national prescribing guidance, is reserved for cases where no suitable alternatives exist [15]. During the study period, none of the patients attending the CF service had been treated with FDC.

Isolates

Sputum samples from CF patients are routinely cultured on six different selective agar media – Staph/Strep, chocolate, McConkey, Staphylococcus aureus ID Elite, Sabouraud dextrose and Burkholderia cepacia specific – to allow isolation of specific pathogens, which are then identified using the VITEK-MS MALDI-TOF instrument. A selection of significant respiratory bacterial isolates is cryopreserved at −80 °C, including all first isolates of noteworthy pathogens, known isolates with differing phenotypes [e.g. mucoid varieties and markedly more antimicrobial-resistant strains of known pathogens based on macroscopic examination of agar-culture plates and on local antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), respectively] and isolates dispatched to reference facilities for additional testing. All P. aeruginosa preserved from 2017 to 2021 inclusive were included in the study.

Susceptibility testing

AST to a panel of anti-pseudomonal antimicrobials is performed as per European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) Disk-Diffusion (version 10) [1617]. Antimicrobials routinely tested locally include anti-pseudomonal penicillins (piperacillin–tazobactam), cephalosporins (ceftazidime), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), aminoglycosides (amikacin, tobramycin), monobactams (aztreonam) and carbapenems (meropenem). For this study, amikacin was not included in the analysis as local clinical use is limited, whereas tobramycin remains the aminoglycoside most relevant in the CF population. AST to FDC was conducted utilizing 30 µg antimicrobial discs on standard Mueller–Hinton agar plates inoculated with a 0.5 McFarland suspension of the test strain. According to EUCAST, broth microdilution requires the use of iron-depleted media for accurate MIC determination [18]. However, disc diffusion is recommended as the preferred method due to technical challenges with broth microdilution, including skipped wells and issues with reproducibility [19]. Disk diffusion inherently mimics iron-depleted conditions, and zone diameters ≥22 mm were interpreted as indicating susceptibility [1617 20]. AST to ceftolozane–tazobactam, ceftazidime–avibactam and meropenem–vaborbactam is performed for select isolates only by gradient-strip MIC determination E-tests (bioMérieux) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure accuracy and consistency, all isolates were tested in duplicate, and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was included as the quality control reference strain. AST to polymyxins (colistin) is not performed locally.

Definitions

MDR was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one (≥1) agent in three or more (≥3) antimicrobial categories for which P. aeruginosa susceptibility is generally expected. XDR was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one (≥1) agent in all but two or fewer (≤2) antimicrobial categories (i.e. bacterial isolate remains susceptible to only one or two categories). PDR was defined as non-susceptibility to all agents in all antimicrobial categories tested [10].

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this investigation was to evaluate the in vitro activity of FDC against a collection of P. aeruginosa isolates from adult CF patients.

Data processing

All antimicrobial susceptibility data were collected and processed through Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

Eighty-five P. aeruginosa isolates from 71 patients were examined during the study period. The average patient age was 30 years, with the average age at which P. aeruginosa was first isolated being 24 years (interquartile range [IQR] 18–28) and the average age of isolation of a mucoid phenotype being 32 years (IQR 25–38).

As shown in Fig. 1, 41% (n=35) of isolates were classified as non-MDR, 19% (n=16) MDR, 16% (n=14) XDR and 24% (n=20) PDR, with 24 % (n=20) exhibiting the mucoid phenotype.

Proportion of P. aeruginosa isolates in different antimicrobial resistance categories.

Overall, only 13 isolates (15%) were non-susceptible to FDC, with an average disc-diffusion zone diameter of 25.2 mm. FDC demonstrated good activity across antimicrobial-resistant subsets, remaining active against 74% of meropenem non-susceptible isolates, 81% of MDR isolates, 86% of XDR isolates, 60% of PDR isolates and 90% of mucoid isolates (Fig. 2).

Proportion of P. aeruginosa isolates susceptible and non-susceptible to FDC across antimicrobial-resistant subsets.

Isolates non-susceptible to FDC (n=13) retained very limited treatment options; only 23.1% (n=3) remained susceptible to ciprofloxacin (CIP), tobramycin (TOB) or ceftolozane-tazobactam (C/T) and 7.7% (n=1) to aztreonam (ATM), while the remaining three isolates were resistant to all tested agents (Fig. 3).

Cross-susceptibility of P. aeruginosa isolates. Heatmap showing the percentage of isolates susceptible to each anti-pseudomonal agent (rows) within non-susceptible subsets (columns). Numbers in parentheses indicate the number of isolates per subset.

Among the comparator agents, meropenem (MEM) was the least efficacious, with 50 isolates (59%) demonstrating non-susceptibility. High levels of resistance were also observed for other β-lactams, with 46 isolates (54%) non-susceptible to each of piperacillin–tazobactam (TZP), ceftazidime (CAZ) and ATM, 45 isolates (53%) to CIP, 34 isolates (40%) to TOB and 29 isolates (34%) to C/T.

Cross-resistance was most pronounced among the β-lactam agents. Very limited reciprocal activity was observed between TZP and CAZ, with ≤2.2% of isolates in each non-susceptible subset remaining susceptible to the other. In other words, if an isolate was non-susceptible to TZP, it was likely to also be non-susceptible to CAZ and vice versa. Similarly, MEM and ATM demonstrated little efficacy against β-lactam non-susceptible cohorts, with susceptibility rates consistently ≤12% (Fig. 3).

By contrast, non-β-lactam agents retained comparatively higher activity. TOB showed >30 % susceptibility among TZP-, CAZ-, MEM- and ATM-non-susceptible isolates and 23.1% activity within the FDC-non-susceptible group. CIP provided more modest activity, with 17–26% susceptibility across most β-lactam non-susceptible subsets. C/T was also effective, with 37–47% of isolates in other β-lactam-resistant groups remaining susceptible.

Discussion

Treatment of Gram-negative infections has become increasingly challenging due to the global rise of MDR strains. Among these, P. aeruginosa remains a particularly problematic pathogen because of its diverse resistance mechanisms and adaptability to the host environment. In CF, P. aeruginosa is especially difficult to eradicate once established, driving morbidity, mortality and accelerated lung function decline [21]. Contributing factors include impaired mucociliary clearance, altered innate immunity and bacterial adaptation within the CF airway niche [2223]. Given the chronicity of infection and frequent requirement for antimicrobial treatment, MDR P. aeruginosa has emerged as an escalating problem, with European surveillance reporting carbapenem resistance rates approaching 18% and CF studies noting MDR prevalence in up to 40–50% of adult patients [2426]. In our cohort, 58.8% (n=50) of isolates displayed significant resistance in vitro (19% MDR, 16% XDR and 24% PDR), underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic options.

FDC is one such potential agent; currently licensed in Europe for the treatment of infections caused by aerobic Gram-negative organisms in adults with limited treatment options [2728]. In our cohort of P. aeruginosa isolates, FDC demonstrated marked in vitro activity, with 85% of all isolates deemed susceptible. Activity was preserved across antimicrobial resistance phenotypes, including 81% of MDR, 86% of XDR, 60% of PDR and 90% of mucoid phenotypes, as shown in Fig. 2 . Notably, 74% of MEM-non-susceptible (NS) strains remained susceptible. This is clinically significant as MEM is frequently employed as first-line intravenous therapy for CF pulmonary exacerbations, guided by both physician practice and international treatment guidelines [2129 30]. By contrast, susceptibility rates within the same MEM-NS subset were lower for C/T (42%), TOB (36%) and CIP (22%), reinforcing FDC’s potential as a salvage option when standard β-lactams fail.

The mechanism of action of FDC explains its retained activity in antimicrobial-resistant strains. Drug entry occurs primarily via receptor-mediated iron transport, with porin channels acting as a secondary pathway [69]. Unlike many other β-lactams, porin loss alone does not markedly impair FDC susceptibility, although resistance may develop when combined with other mechanisms. Once internalized, FDC dissociates from its siderophore moiety and binds to penicillin-binding protein 3 (PBP3), inhibiting cell wall synthesis [78]. In addition, FDC has been reported to interfere with biofilm development. Experimental studies suggest that by mimicking bacterial siderophores, FDC stimulates increased production of natural siderophores such as pyoverdine and pyochelin, as well as upregulation of siderophore transporters during iron acquisition [3132]. These processes destabilize biofilm architecture by altering iron availability and increasing antimicrobial penetration, thereby enhancing the overall bactericidal effect. Such activity is particularly relevant in the CF lung, where biofilms contribute substantially to chronic infection and antimicrobial tolerance [33]. This aligns with our finding that FDC retained activity against the majority of mucoid isolates (90%), representing the biofilm-adapted phenotype, suggesting that its biofilm-related advantages may translate into clinically meaningful efficacy in this population.

Despite its novel mode of action, several bacterial adaptations have been shown to compromise its activity, as alluded to above. Overproduction or structural alteration of AmpC increases hydrolysis, ampD loss results in derepression of AmpC and mutations in TonB-dependent receptors or their regulator pirR impair siderophore uptake and substitutions in ftsI reduce PBP3 binding [1112 26]. These mechanisms often act in tandem, conferring broad cross-class antimicrobial resistance as reflected in our cross-susceptibility heatmap (Fig. 3). In our cohort, 13 isolates (15%) were non-susceptible to FDC, and cross-class antimicrobial resistance was common. For example, none of the FDC-NS isolates retained activity to TZP, CAZ or MEM, and only 23.1% (n=3) remained susceptible to CIP, TOB and C/T and 7.7% (n=1) to ATM. Moreover, 3 of the 13 were resistant to all anti-pseudomonal agents tested, highlighting the rarity but seriousness of pan-resistance underlining the need for ongoing surveillance to detect such high-risk phenotypes. Although molecular testing was not performed to confirm the underlying genetic mechanisms, the resistance phenotypes observed are consistent with multifactorial pathways previously linked to FDC non-susceptibility [1126].

Overall, our findings align with international surveillance. In the SIDERO program, which evaluated P. aeruginosa isolates from respiratory, bloodstream and urinary tract infections across multiple geographic regions and clinical settings, <0.1% of isolates had FDC MIC >4 µg ml−1, i.e. non-susceptible, and susceptibility rates among those that were non-susceptible to C/T and ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA) were 99.8% and 100 %, respectively [3435]. Comparable results have also been reported in CF-specific investigations. Across multiple cohorts, FDC demonstrated low MIC50 values (0.25–0.5 µg ml−1), preserved activity in 70–80 % of MEM-NS isolates and >90 % susceptibility in MDR and XDR subsets [1112 25]. However, longitudinal analyses highlight that antimicrobial resistance may emerge under selective pressure, with ≥4-fold MIC increases and cross-resistance documented in up to one-fifth of patients after exposure to other β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations [2636]. Mechanistically, these changes were linked to the abovementioned adaptations. Collectively, the data illustrate that FDC retains broad activity at baseline, but its efficacy is threatened by the potential for antimicrobial resistance to develop through several previously mentioned molecular routes, particularly in high-risk populations such as CF.

Studies in patients with CF are inherently challenging due to the progressive nature of the disease and the wide variation in severity and organ involvement, which substantially influence the outcomes of in vivo drug evaluation. This challenge is even greater for novel therapeutics like FDC, where its use is generally restricted by guidelines and licensing to situations where no other suitable options are available. Therefore, available evidence is largely limited to microbiological outcomes rather than clinical efficacy. Observational studies of patients with CF chronically infected with P. aeruginosa report initial susceptibility to FDC exceeding 90%, yet resistance has been observed to develop during treatment in about 6–12 % of sequential isolates, frequently accompanied by cross-class resistance to other β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations [1125 36]. Indirect evidence from non-CF populations is also supportive. In the Phase III APEKS-NP study, FDC was non-inferior to high-dose, extended-infusion MEM in nosocomial pneumonia, suggesting adequate lung penetration [37]. Intrapulmonary pharmacokinetic studies further confirm that epithelial lining fluid concentrations of FDC are comparable to those of other β-lactams used for respiratory infections, such as CAZ and cefepime, and closely parallel plasma levels, indicating rapid distribution into the lung compartment [38]. Taken together, these data suggest that while clinical experience in CF remains limited, FDC is likely to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the respiratory tract. Our findings of strong in vitro activity, particularly in difficult-to-treat MEM-NS and mucoid isolates, support its consideration as a salvage option in CF, pending clinical validation.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single national referral centre with a modest sample size restricted to respiratory isolates, limiting generalizability. Selection bias towards more antimicrobial-resistant isolates may have inflated antimicrobial resistance rates. No molecular testing was performed to confirm resistance mechanisms, and antimicrobial susceptibility to several newer agents (CZA, imipenem–relebactam and meropenem–vaborbactam) and colistin was not assessed. Finally, clinical data on antimicrobial exposure, immunomodulator use and treatment outcomes were not available.

Despite these limitations, our study contributes valuable local surveillance data for a high-risk CF population. P. aeruginosa remains a formidable pathogen, and timely administration of effective therapy guided by in vitro susceptibility is essential to reduce morbidity and mortality. FDC demonstrates promising activity against MDR, XDR and even PDR isolates in this cohort, but its restricted licensing – reserved for cases where no suitable alternatives exist – underscores the importance of antimicrobial stewardship, resistance surveillance and careful clinical application.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic evaluation of FDC activity against P. aeruginosa isolates from adults with CF in Ireland. FDC demonstrated consistent in vitro activity across antimicrobial-resistant phenotypes, with 85% overall susceptibility, including 81% of MDR, 86% of XDR and 60% of PDR isolates. Antimicrobial activity was retained in 74% of MEM-NS strains and 90% of mucoid isolates, which represent the most clinically challenging phenotypes in CF care. These findings indicate that FDC remains active in settings where conventional anti-pseudomonal agents may fail.

By establishing the first national dataset, this work provides surveillance evidence directly relevant to Irish clinical practice and antimicrobial stewardship. The results support the potential role of FDC as a salvage option in CF, particularly in the context of restricted prescribing limited to cases without suitable alternatives. As multidrug resistance continues to rise, these data may help inform stewardship strategies and shape policy regarding the integration of novel agents into CF management. This study thereby provides a baseline for future surveillance and planning in the era of escalating antimicrobial resistance.
