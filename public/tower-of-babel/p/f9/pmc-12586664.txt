Postsynthetic modification of amine functionalized MIL-101(Cr) loaded with Pd nanoparticles for sustainable Suzuki coupling reactions
Authors: Mehdi Abroudi, Azadeh Tadjarodi, Zolfaghar Rezvani, Modarres Dehghani, Afsaneh Mollahosseini, Seyed Masoud Seyed Ahmadian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMIL-101(Cr) metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) possess advantageous properties, such as high surface area and porosity, which render them effective as catalytic supports. In this study, MIL-101(Cr) powder was functionalized with putrescine (PUT) via post-synthetic modification (PSM). Subsequently, a novel strategy was employed to incorporate palladium nanoparticles into the functionalized MIL-101(Cr) framework. The resulting Pd-loaded, functionalized MIL-101(Cr) catalysts were assessed for their catalytic efficiency in Suzuki–Miyaura carbon–carbon cross-coupling reactions. In particular, these catalysts were used to couple phenylboronic acid with various aryl halides (Ar–X, where X = I, Br, Cl, F). To maximize catalytic performance, key reaction parameters including the amount of catalyst, the amount and type of solvent, and the reaction time were optimized. Introduction

C-C cross-coupling reactions, such as Hiyama, Sonogashira, Suzuki-Miyaura, and Heck, illustrate powerful synthetic tools for generating new natural products, including heterocycles, molecular electronics, dendrimers, and conjugated polymers1–9. The Suzuki-Miyaura reaction, in particular, is a robust and versatile method for synthesizing unsymmetrical biaryl compounds. Biaryl motifs are a crucial class of substances that are extensively utilized in pharmaceuticals, advanced materials chemistry, and agrochemicals10–12. The Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reaction is typically carried out using a palladium catalyst13–16. While nickel (Ni) and copper (Cu) catalysts have also been employed for this reaction, they generally exhibit lower efficiencies compared to palladium-based systems17,18. Palladium-catalyzed C–C coupling reactions are considered one of the most significant advancements in synthetic organic chemistry due to their high efficiency, rapid reaction rates, high turnover frequencies, and excellent selectivity19,20. However, the recyclability of these expensive metal catalysts after the reaction can be extremely challenging. These issues can be addressed by immobilizing the metal catalysts on solid supports. Several other distinctive metal catalysts, such as platinum (Pt), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu), and gold (Au), have also been employed and proven effective when supported on solid materials for the Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reaction21–23. While nickel (Ni) and copper (Cu) catalysts have also been employed for this reaction, they generally exhibit lower efficiencies compared to palladium-based systems24,25. As a type of porous material, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are notable for being composed of organic linkers and secondary building units (SBUs)26. MOFs are widely used for catalysis, gas storage and separation, as well as in functional materials such as magnets, optical devices, and electrical components27. MOF-based materials have emerged as excellent supports for the immobilization of molecular catalysts due to their significant advantages, including their ordered crystalline structure, high porosity and surface area, ease of operation, and availability in a variety of topologies and particle sizes28–31. These benefits have facilitated the development of dynamic MOF-immobilized catalysts, which provide access to species that are not easily accessible under homogeneous conditions32–37. One of the intriguing features of certain types of MOFs is the presence of open metal sites, also known as coordinatively unsaturated sites (CUSs), where the metal center is partially coordinated with an organic linker and serves as an adsorption site. Examples of MOFs with such CUSs include CPO-27, Cu₃(BTC)2, and MIL-10138–40. Furthermore, post-synthetic modification (PSM) allows organic linkers containing amino groups within MOFs to interact and bind with transition metal ions41, resulting in the creation of a range of MOF-based materials with exceptional physical and chemical properties. In this context, MIL-101(Cr)-NH2 has been extensively investigated as a promising candidate for PSM due to its favorable thermal stability, relatively large pores, and notable chemical stability in water and several organic solvents42. As an example, Wang et al. synthesized the Co(II)@Cr-MIL-101-P2I catalyst by adding pyridine-3-aldehyde to the amino group of MIL-101(Cr)-NH2, and employed it as a heterogeneous catalyst for highly efficient olefin epoxidation43. Additionally, Rostamnial et al. synthesized ethylenediamine-modified Cr-MOF via post-synthetic modification at the open metal sites, and these materials were used in the Hantzsch condensation reaction44. Furthermore, Min Ji and co-workers prepared a dual heterogeneous catalyst (DMEDA/IL–NH2-MIL-101) and utilized CO2, propylene oxide, and aniline to produce N-aryloxazolidin-2-one in the absence of valuable metals and/or harmful catalysts45. In this work, a functionalized MIL-101(Cr) was prepared with putrescine (PUT) using a post-synthesis method. After coordinating the putrescine ligand to the coordinatively unsaturated metal sites (open metal sites) of MIL-101(Cr), a novel method for creating intertwined Pd complexes on the surface of MIL-101(Cr) was explored. Then, its performance as heterogeneous catalyst for Suzuki coupling reaction was evaluated. Compared to earlier catalysts, these catalysts exhibit higher efficiency in carbon-carbon coupling reactions, resulting in higher yields and shorter reaction times. The use of this catalyst in S-M coupling reactions offers several advantages, including rapid reactions, excellent performance, and reusability.

Experimental section

Materials and physical techniques

All substances were used directly after being purchased from Merck. The Shimadzu S 8400 infrared spectrometer set up Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) with KBr pellets with a resolution of 4 cm− 1 within the range of 4000 –400 cm− 1. The Shimadzu QP-5050 GC-MS machine was used to record GC-MS samples. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted using the STOE-STADIVARI copper X-ray diffraction gauge (Cu Kα = 0.154 nm, 40 kV / 40 mA current, and scan speed of 3 ° per minute). The BET (Microtrac Bel Corp Belsorp mini II) method was used to investigate the isothermal adsorption / desorption of the produced tests. Elemental mapping, energy dispersive X-ray analysis, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were performed on the Zeiss Sigma VP apparatus. K-Alpha scientific Fisher thermos was used to take measurements for X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Thermal stability was demonstrated using thermogravimetry (TGA) employing SDT Q600 V20.9. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was used for the initial elemental analysis.

Synthesis of metal–organic framework MIL-101(Cr)

Using the hydrothermal technique, Bromberg et al.46. Synthesized MIL-101(Cr), a type of MOF with these desirable open metal sites. In a 100 mL hydrothermal autoclave reactor, a mixture of terephthalic acid (C2H4(CO2H)2) (5.00 mmol, 0.82 g), chromium nitrate (Cr(NO3)3⋅9H2O) (5.00 mmol, 2.00 g), and distilled water (24 mL) was heated at 220 °C for 18 h. The resulting light green solid was collected after cooling and repeatedly rinsed with hot ethanol, dimethylformamide (DMF), and deionized water. The combination then underwent further hydrothermal treatment before being filtered to remove any inactive terephthalic acid. The powder was soaked in ethanol at 80 °C for 24 h and then dried at 150 °C overnight.

Synthesis of putrescine (PUT) link to coordinatively un-saturated metal sites (CUS) of metal–organic framework MIL-101(Cr)

The synthesis of Putrescine (PUT) linked MIL-101 was carried out using a previously reported method47. 75 mL of anhydrous toluene was added. Soon after, 5 mmol (500 µL) of putrescine was gradually introduced into this mixture, which was then refluxed for 12 h. The final sample was obtained by filtration, repeated washing with a large amount of ethanol, and drying overnight at room temperature.

Synthesis of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT

To synthesize MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, 100 mg of the previously prepared MIL-101(Cr)-NH2 was heated in 10 mL of dry ethanol with 0.15 mL (0.2 mmol) of glutaraldehyde (GLUT) for 8 h. After cooling, the mixture was filtered, and the precipitate was washed with ethanol. Finally, the resulting material was dried at 90 °C overnight.

Synthesis of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (0) catalyst

To synthesize MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, 100 mg of the previously prepared MIL-101(Cr)-NH2 was heated in 10 mL of dry ethanol with 0.15 mL (0.2 mmol) of glutaraldehyde (GLUT) for 8 h. After cooling, the mixture was filtered, and the precipitate was washed with ethanol. Finally, the resulting material was dried at 90 °C overnight. To obtain MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (II), the product is dried for 24 h at 50 °C in a vacuum oven. The MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (0) catalyst is formed by reducing the Pd (II) precursor with sodium borohydride during preparation in ethanol, by mixing 100 mg of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (II) with 20 mL of an ethanol solution containing 10 mg of sodium borohydride (NaBH4). The final step involves washing the product three times with acetone and deionized water, followed by drying in a vacuum oven48. According to ICP analysis, this compound has a Pd concentration of 0.19 mmol/g.

Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reaction

The catalyst, MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, along with 1 mmol of potassium carbonate (K2CO3), solvent, and aryl halide (ArX, where X = F, Cl, Br, I), was added to a 25 mL Erlenmeyer flask and refluxed at 80 °C in an oil bath according to the prescribed protocol. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC. To isolate and dry the organic phase, dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) and magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) were used separately. Additional details about the products, including the NMR spectra, are provided in the supporting information.

Results and discussion

Characterization

Synthesis

Figure 1 outlines the preparation of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd catalyst. The first step involved the preparation of MIL-101(Cr) via a hydrothermal method, as reported previously. In the second step, MIL-101 was linked with putrescine (PUT). In the third step, glutaraldehyde (GLUT) was used to treat the terminal nitrogen atoms of PUT that were attached to MIL-101. Finally, palladium chloride (PdCl2) was anchored onto the surface of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, and with the aid of sodium borohydride, PdCl2 was reduced to palladium (Pd) particles. The Suzuki-Miyaura (S-M) cross-coupling reactions in this study were conducted using this catalyst. The method was optimized by adjusting several factors, including the catalyst type, solvent type, and temperature, to achieve the best conditions for catalytic Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reactions involving various aryl halides. Additionally, experiments were performed to evaluate the reusability of the catalyst.

The overall synthesis of the MIL-101-PUT-GLUT-Pd catalyst.

XRD

Figure 2 shows the powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, MIL-101(Cr)-PUT, and MIL-101(Cr). The distinctive peaks of MIL-101(Cr) appeared at 2.85°, 3.40°, 4.05°, 5.18°, 5.88°, 8.44°, 9.09°, and 10.33°, which are consistent with previous observations49,50. After grafting with putrescine (PUT), the unique peaks of MIL-101(Cr) remained in the XRD pattern of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT; however, minor alterations were observed in the intensities of these peaks (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the reflection positions of the MIL-101(Cr)-PUT and MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT patterns were substantially similar (Figs. 2a, b, and c). The introduction of palladium particles had minimal impact on the appearance of the MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT surface and did not significantly affect its crystallinity51 (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, this lack of noticeable change in the Pd particles suggests that their quantity is very low or that the Pd particles are quite small in all samples52–54.

The powder XRD patterns of MIL-101(Cr) (a), MIL-101(Cr)-PUT (b), MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT (c), and MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (d).

FT-IR

The FT-IR spectra of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, MIL-101(Cr)-PUT, and MIL-101(Cr) are compared in Fig. 3. In Fig. 3(a), the FT-IR spectrum of MIL-101 displays strong, prominent peaks at 1400, 1508, and 1620 cm− 1, indicating the presence of O-C = O and C = C vibrations in benzene dicarboxylate. C-H deformation vibration frequencies are observed at 748, 885, 1017, and 1164 cm− 155. Cr-O vibrations were detected at 588 cm− 1, supporting the synthesis of MIL-101(Cr). In the FT-IR spectra of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT (Fig. 3(b)), the peaks at 1050 and 1576 cm− 1 are attributed to C-N bond stretching and N-H plane stretching vibrations, respectively. Notably, the peak at 1050 cm− 1, associated with the MIL-101(Cr) sample, overlaps with the amine peak at 1576 cm− 156,57. Additionally, the − NH region was located to the broadband from 3412 to 3248 cm− 158. The results of the testing indicated that MIL-101 had been successfully grafted with PUT. The FT-IR spectrum shows a prominent peak corresponding to the C = O group associated with the aldehyde of glutaraldehyde. This peak disappears in the FT-IR spectrum of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT, where it is replaced by a peak at 1700 cm− 1 corresponding to the C = N group, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c). This spectrum closely resembles that of MIL-101-PUT, indicating the successful bonding of glutaraldehyde to MIL-101(Cr)-PUT. Figure 3(d) presents the FT-IR spectrum of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, which is nearly indistinguishable from that of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT (c).

The FT-IR spectra of MIL-101(Cr) (a), MIL-101(Cr)-PUT (b), MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT (c), and MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (d).

SEM

According to the SEM images of the catalyst, the structure of MIL-101(Cr) appears to have remained unchanged throughout the functionalization process. The crystal samples exhibit an octahedral shape (Figs. 4a–d). The same principles apply to crystal size distribution, with sizes ranging from 193 to 322 nm (see Figures S1 in Supplementary Information). Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis reveals the presence of palladium in MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd (Fig. 5). The elemental mapping study of the MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd catalyst, depicted in Fig. 6, shows that the elements Cr, O, C, N, and Pd are uniformly distributed across the catalyst, providing further confirmation of its composition.

TEM

High-resolution TEM analysis was conducted on the MIL-101-PUT-GLUT-Pd sample. (Fig. 7) demonstrates that the modified catalytic substrate contains uniformly dispersed dark spots on the surface, corresponding to well-distributed Pd nanoparticles throughout the framework. The heterogeneous dispersion and small size of Pd nanoparticles indicate that the functionalization with PUT and subsequent crosslinking with GLUT effectively anchors Pd species, preventing agglomeration and uncontrolled growth. Such nanoscale dispersion of Pd nanoparticles maximizes the number of exposed active sites, which is expected to enhance catalytic efficiency. Also, the particle size distribution histogram indicated (Figure S2 in Supplementary Information), that the average size of the palladium particles on the catalytic substrate was 3.77 nm.

TEM analysis of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd catalyst.

As shown in Fig. 8, both MIL-101(Cr) Figs. 8(a) and MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd Figs. 8(b) exhibited typical type-I adsorption-desorption isotherm curves, with no capillary condensation and no hysteresis loop at relative pressures above 0.8. The unactivated MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd and displayed BET specific surface area values of approximately 770 m2/g and 2229 m2/g, respectively, with total pore volumes of 0.8187 cm3/g and 1.3761 cm3/g. Additionally, the micropore diameters of unactivated MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, as shown in Figs. 8(c) and 8(d), respectively, were measured to be between 1.2 and 1.9 nm. XPS

Figure 9(a) illustrates the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd, revealing the presence of five elements: oxygen, chromium, nitrogen, carbon, and palladium. Figure 9(b) displays an asymmetric plot with two main peaks and two satellite peaks, identified as the metallic Pd(II) and Pd⁰ species, respectively. The Pd(II) 3d₃/₂ peak was observed at 343.1 eV, while the Pd(II) 3d₅/₂ peak was observed at 337.8 eV, with traces of Pd(II) species detected within the MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT support59–62. However, the bands at 341.1 and 335.5 eV were attributed to Pd0 3d3/2 and Pd0 3d5/2, respectively63,64.

TGA

All TGA curves for MIL-101(Cr), MIL-101-PUT, MIL-101-PUT-GLUT, and MIL-101-PUT-GLUT-Pd are now presented in a single Fig. 10 for direct comparison. The TGA profiles reveal three main weight-loss regions: (i) RT–150 °C – minor weight loss due to physically adsorbed water and residual solvents; (ii) ≈ 150–330 °C – gradual loss of more strongly bound water and initial degradation of surface-bound organic moieties; (iii) ≈ 330–450 °C – major framework decomposition associated with terephthalate linker degradation and collapse of the MIL-101(Cr) structure. Above this range, the curves level off to a stable baseline corresponding mainly to Cr₂O₃ (and in the Pd-loaded sample, Pd species) plus a fraction of thermally resistant carbonaceous residue.

As seen in the halogen series, the order of reactivity is Ph-I > Ph-Br > Ph-Cl > > Ph-F, with the highest yield of 95% obtained for Ph-I. Additionally, the presence of electron-withdrawing groups, such as -NO2, -CN, and -OCH3, on the aryl halide significantly enhances the speed and efficiency of the Suzuki-Miyaura reaction, as illustrated in Entries 7, 8, 9, 10, and 13. For example, NO2-Ph-Br (Entry 7) demonstrates a higher reaction rate and efficiency compared to Ph-Br (Entry 2) due to the electron-withdrawing effect of the -NO2 group on the aryl halide ring. The rate of the Suzuki-Miyaura reaction was reduced when aryl halides containing electron-donating groups, such as -NH2, -OH, and -CH3, were present, as demonstrated in Entries 4, 5, 6, 12, and 14. For instance, CH3-Ph-I (Entry 4) exhibits lower speed and efficiency compared to Ph-I (Entry 1) due to the presence of the electron-donating group on the aryl halide ring. The NMR analysis data of the products are presented in Figures S7–S16 of the Supplementary Information file.

An interesting observation regarding the efficiency of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd is its performance with aryl halides containing fluorine, achieving approximately 40% yield in the Suzuki reaction (Entries 10 and 15). This highlights the effectiveness of palladium in this reaction, as few catalysts can react with aryl halides containing fluorine (Table 5).

Also, the hot filtration test to investigate the heterogeneous nature of catalyst was performed. Two separate Suzuki reactions were carried out using bromobenzene and phenylboronic acid as starting materials. Both reactions were allowed to proceed for one hour. In one reaction, the catalyst remained present throughout the entire duration. In the second reaction, the catalyst was removed by filtration after 10 min. The results showed that the reaction with the catalyst present throughout reached a yield of 91%. The results indicate and confirm that the catalyst is heterogeneous, as it was completely removed from the reaction medium upon filtration (Figure S3 of the Supplementary Information file).

Reusability assessment of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd after several catalytic performances

Reusing catalysts in coupling reactions is crucial from both environmental and financial perspectives. Therefore, we examined the potential of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd to be reused as a catalyst for the coupling reaction of bromobenzene and phenylboronic acid in water. To evaluate the stability of the catalyst, the analyses including FT-IR, SEM and TGA were performed (see Figures S4, S5 and S6, respectively in Supplementary Information). The results indicated that this catalyst could be reused up to five times with only a slight decrease in its activity (as shown in Fig. 11).

Conclusions

This study presents a novel method for synthesizing palladium-embedded particles using MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT and investigates their catalytic activity in the Suzuki-Miyaura coupling reaction. MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd exhibited outstanding catalytic activity in carbon-carbon bond formation, as demonstrated by its high efficiency in reactions involving bromobenzene and phenylboronic acid. The use of MIL-101(Cr)-PUT-GLUT-Pd in Suzuki-Miyaura coupling reactions offers several advantages, including shorter reaction times, exceptional performance, and enhanced catalyst reusability.
