Safety evaluation of Butyricicoccus faecihominis DSM 34943
Authors: Jeffrey Pitt, Mark R Bauter, Ritesh Kumar, Oliver Hasselwander, Ashley A Hibberd, Helene Kane, Qiong Wang, Isabelle Auzanneau, Stéphanie Bry, Elisabeth David, Pauline Seguinot, Frank Burns, Amy B Smith, Miriam Carnovale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel strain of Butyricicoccus faecihominis DSM 34943 was isolated from the feces of a healthy human donor. In order to fully assess the safety of this strain, a full genomic assessment, further in-vitro characterization and a combined in-vivo subchronic 28-day and 90-day toxicity study is reported herein. B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was susceptible to antibiotics of human clinical relevance, was sensitive to bile and gastric juice pH conditions, and does not exhibit any aspects of virulence. This strain also demonstrates the ability to engraft the gastrointestinal tract of rats, persisting with continuous administration of the strain until the end of the study. Exposure to 2000 mg/kg BW/day B. faecihominis DSM 34943 did not produce any evidence of toxicity after either 28- or 90-days of exposure and did not translocate across the gastrointestinal barrier. Therefore, the NOEL for B. faecihominis DSM 34943, administered for 28- or 90-days, was determined to be the limit dose at 2000 mg/kg/day in male and female rats, a level which meets or exceeds calculated dose equivalent of 1.97 × 1011 CFU/kg/day. B. faecihominis DSM 34943 activity was also evaluated in a DIO mice model and supplementation of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 resulted in significant improvement in insulin sensitivity, leptin, resistin and cholesterol levels, suggesting that this strain has potential as a safe next generation probiotic for metabolic health.Keywords: Subchronic toxicity, Genomic safety, B. faecihominis DSM 34943, Safety, Probiotics, Rats

Introduction

Butyricicoccus faecihominis was proposed as a new species of the genus Butyricicoccus by Takada et al. in 2016 when they described a butyrate-producing bacterium isolated from human faeces [43]. Butyrate is a key microbiota-derived SCFA metabolite and energy source for colonocytes. Butyrate has been associated with an important role in health, particularly gastrointestinal health [22] and metabolic health [18], [37].

The Butyricicoccus faecihominis strain DSM 34943 (also identified as Butyricicoccus faecihominis ODS-26) was isolated from the stool of a healthy adult and can be taxonomically classified to the species B. faecihominis. The genome-wide average nucleotide identity (gANI) of DSM 34943 is above the representative species cutoff of 95 % similarity to the type strain B. faecihominis DSM 100989 [10]; however, it is also similarly close to the type strain Agathobaculum butyriciproducens, and thus it is possible that a future taxonomic review may result in a re-classification of these two species [1]. The type species B. faecihominis is designated as Biosafety Level 1. A recent analysis of correlation networks identified Agathobaculum butyriciproducens as a putative keystone species, which are taxa in the gut microbiome that play a critical role in maintaining community structure and function [4].

Recent studies have found reduced levels of Butyricicoccus spp. in the intestinal microbiota of individuals with type 2 diabetes and end- or late-stage renal disease [19], [23], [44]. Butyricicoccus spp. were positively correlated with Blautia in human fecal samples, and a metabolic interaction between B. wexlerae and B. faecihominis was shown in vitro [23]. In addition, B. faecihominis was found to be significantly enriched in the normal-transit group in a study of 94 subjects with subjective constipation [49].

Safety of a strain belonging to a different species of Butyricicoccus, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, was demonstrated by genomic and preclinical assessment in vivo [42] as well as in human volunteers [5].

In order to comprehensively assess the safety of B. faecihominis DSM 34943, this strain was further characterized and evaluated for genomic safety, phenotypic antimicrobial resistance, potential hemolytic activity, lactate production and acid/bile tolerance. Any potential sub-chronic activity was also investigated using a combined 28-day and 90-day oral toxicity study in rats, which included translocation analysis. The ability of this strain to engraft in the gastrointestinal tract has also been assessed in this study. In addition, Butyricicoccus faecihominis DSM 34943 was evaluated for its efficacy in improving metabolic endpoints in a DIO mouse model. The results of this comprehensive analysis are described herein.

Materials and methods

B. faecihominis DSM 34943 in vitro testing and genomic screening for characterization and safety analysis

For in vitro testing, media and consumables were deoxygenated for 48 h, and all work was performed in an anaerobic chamber. B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was cultured onto Brucella blood agar to test for hemolytic activity. A commercial assay kit was used to determine D-/L-lactate production from the culture supernatant following the manufacturer’s instructions (D-/L-Lactate Rapid Assay kit, Megazyme, Chicago, IL). The survival rate of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 following gastric acid and bile salt challenge was tested by resuspending the cell pellet in Butterfield’s Phosphate Buffer after 24 h of growth in reinforced clostridial medium (RCM). A 1:100 dilution of resuspended cells with modified gastric juice (0.32 % wt/v pepsin + 0.2 % wt/v NaCl, pH 3.5) was made (T0) and then incubated for 1 h (T1). For bile salt tolerance testing, a final concentration 0.3 % Difco™ Oxgall was used (T0). Three serial dilutions (6 replicates) of each with or without gastric acid or bile salt were pour plated with RCM agar with mucin, and colonies were enumerated after 24 h incubation at 37°C.

The genome of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was sequenced using both Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) technology on the Sequel System (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) and 2 × 150 bp reads on the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The Circular Consensus Sequences (CCS) workflow in the PacBio tool SMRT Link was used to generate highly accurate consensus sequences, also called HiFi or CCS reads, and Illumina short-reads were used for error correction. The de novo genome was assembled using SPAdes [3] on CCS error-corrected reads, then circularized [24] and qualitatively evaluated with BUSCO [41]. The presence of antibiotic resistance and virulence genes was screened using the BV-BRC v3.36.16.5 [36] which searches the CARD v3.2.8 [25], NDARO v3.11.20 [16], the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB) [29] and their own curated database of virulence genes [48]. The genome was additionally screened for genes related to hemolysis and the production of biogenic amines.

The phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility profile was analyzed by Biosafe (Biological Safety Solutions Ltd., 70210 Kuopio, Finland) using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute standard M11, 9th ed. CLSI [11], with the exception that the agar dilution method using Brucella agar was used to test fosfomycin. The antimicrobial susceptibility tests were performed in triplicate using custom Sensititre™ FINBIOS1, Sensititre™ EULACBI2, Sensititre™ YANO2B 96-well plates under anaerobic conditions at 35 ± 2ºC for 48 h ± 2 h using Brucella broth supplemented with hemin (5 µg/mL), vitamin K1 (1 µg/mL) and 5 % lysed horse blood. The OD600 was adjusted to 0.125 (±0.01), which corresponded to approximately 8.6 × 106 CFU/mL. The cell suspension was further diluted in broth to 5 × 104 CFU/well for the broth microdilution method or 105 CFU/spot for the agar dilution method. Bacteroides fragilis DSM 2151 and Eggerthella lenta DSM 15644 were used as control strains. The MIC cut-off values were compared to published reference breakpoints from the European Food and Safety Authority panel for Gram-positive bacteria (EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed [13] and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing panel for anaerobic bacteria without species-specific breakpoints [15] if available.

Preclinical efficacy in a diet induced obesity (DIO) mouse model

B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was evaluated for its efficacy in improving metabolic endpoints in a DIO mice model. A total of 36 male nine-week-old C57Bl/6 J DIO and 12 male C57Bl/6 J mice were included in the study (12 animals per group) purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). For the animal study, Group 1 animals were maintained on PMI Nutrition International Certified Rodent Chow No. 5 CR4 upon arrival. Groups 2–4 animals were maintained on Research Diet D12492. Animals were single housed in polycarbonate cages which contained appropriate bedding. Animals were distributed to the treatment groups on study day −1, in such a way as to generate cohorts with no significant differences with respect to body weight and non-fasted blood glucose based on measurements from Day −1.

The animals were taken from their home cage and placed into a plastic restrainer compatible with the body composition analysis instrument. Insulin tolerance test (ITT)

The animals are weighed and placed in a clean cage without food the evening prior to ITT. Access to water is provided throughout the procedure. At ∼0700 h, animals will be dosed as indicated in the experimental design table. At ∼0800 h (1-hour post dose) all mice will be receive an intraperitoneal injection of insulin (dose 0.5 U/kg at 5 mL/kg). Blood glucose will be checked via tail snip using a hand-held glucometer (Abbott Alpha Trak) at the following times relative to insulin dose: 0 (just prior to insulin dose), 15, 30, 45, 60 and 90 min. Following the final blood glucose measurement, food is returned to the cages.

28- and 90-day repeated dose study in rats

The 28- and 90-day study was conducted in accordance with Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) regulations (US FDA, 21 CFR 58; OECD, ENV/MC/CHEM(98)17), except for the translocation analysis. The test facility was accredited by AAALAC International, and the protocol was reviewed and approved by the appropriate test facility animal welfare authorities prior to study initiation.

B. faecihominis DSM 34943 concentrate

B. faecihominis DSM 34943 probiotic strain was propagated using anaerobic fermentation. An optimized blend of nutrients, including proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals were mixed with water, sterilized, and inoculated with B. faecihominis DSM 34943. Following fermentation, the media was removed by centrifugation and the resulting cell slurry was freeze-dried.

Test system and animal husbandry

Forty-five male and 45 female experimentally naïve Sprague-Dawley CD® IGS rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories, Inc. (Raleigh, NC USA). The animals were between 6 and 7 weeks of age at study initiation and individual weight variation did not exceed ±20 % of the mean weight of each sex.

The animals were individually housed in cages which conformed to the size recommendations in the latest Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [34]. Each animal was given a sequential number in addition to being uniquely identified with a Monel® self-piercing stainless steel ear tag. Litter paper placed beneath the cage was changed at least three times/week. The animal room had a 12-hour light/dark cycle and environmental controls were set to maintain temperature and relative humidity ranges of 21 ± 2°C and 30–70 %, respectively. The animals were acclimated to the housing facilities for at least five days prior to testing and released from quarantine based on acceptable health status. Feed, 2016 Certified Envigo Teklad Global Rodent Diet® (Envigo Teklad, Inc.) and filtered tap water were available ad libitum during acclimation and throughout the study. There were no known contaminants found in the food or water that would interfere with the results of this study.

Study design

The overall study design was based on the OECD and US FDA guidelines for toxicity studies in rodents [35], [17]. While the overall study design followed those standard 90-day guideline studies, additional animals were included in each group for evaluation after 28-days of exposure to B. faecihominis DSM 34943 test concentrate. When there are no expectations of toxicity, these guidelines allow a limit-test, which means only one test group at a maximum exposure of 5 % in the diet or equivalent to 1000 mg/kg BW/day is evaluated. However, because of the intended use and that B. faecihominis, is a commensal intestinal bacterium in humans, the maximum dose (limited by formulation viscosity) of 2000 mg/kg BW/day was used in this study.

Animals were randomized according to stratification by body weight into two stratified groups of 15 males and 15 females, with body weights within 20 % of the mean for each sex. Within each group, 5 animals/sex were assigned to the 28-day arm and 10 animals/sex were assigned to the 90-day arm.

Dose formulation preparation and procedures

The B. faecihominis DSM 34943 test material was supplied in individual sachets, and formulations were made under anaerobic conditions daily. Sachets were allowed to equilibrate to room temperature for approximately 30 min. The B. faecihominis DSM 34943 test material was mixed weight to volume (w/v) in sterile, anaerobic phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Mediatech, Inc., Manassas, VA), supplemented with 0.05 % L-cysteine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ward Hill, MA), under anaerobic conditions. The Control group received PBS supplemented with 0.05 % L-cysteine. The formulations were stirred at ambient temperature until a visually homogeneous mixture was achieved. Individual doses were calculated based on the most recent weekly body weights and were adjusted each week to maintain the targeted dose level for all rats (i.e., mg/kg BW/day). All doses were administered at 10 mL/kg. The control group received the vehicle only, at the same dose volume as the test animals. Each animal was dosed by oral gavage using a stainless-steel ball-tipped gavage needle attached to a syringe. Dose administration occurred daily (7 days/week). The dose solutions and/or individual syringes were maintained in anaerobic conditions during dose administration. The first day of administration was considered Day 1 of the study.

Prior to initial dosing on Day 1 of the study, samples from the dose preparation were collected from the top, middle, and bottom for each concentration. The vehicle control mixture was sampled from the middle of the dose preparation only. Dose preparations were also sampled at the middle and end of the study for verification of dose concentration. Dose preparations were verified for viable CFU content at the beginning, middle and end of the study according to the following method (Section 2.3.5).

Enumeration of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 dosing solutions

Anaerobic PBS + L-cysteine (90 mL) were added to a sterile bottle in a Bactron EZ anaerobic chamber. In the anaerobic chamber, five anaerobic YCFAC plates were inoculated with 100 μL of the dosing formulation. Plates were inverted and incubated in the anaerobic chamber at 37 ± 2°C for 9 days. Experimental variables

Animal Observations: During the acclimation period, the eyes of all rats were examined by focal illumination, indirect ophthalmoscopy and, when indicated, slit-lamp microscopy. Mydriatic eye drops were administered prior to ophthalmoscopy and the eyes were examined in subdued light. These procedures were repeated on all surviving test animals prior to test termination. All animals were observed at least twice daily for viability. Cage-side observations of all animals were performed daily during the study. Potential clinical signs of toxicity included, but were not limited to: changes in skin, fur, eyes, and mucous membranes; occurrence of secretions and excretions; autonomic activity (e.g., lacrimation, piloerection, pupil size, unusual respiratory pattern); changes in gait, posture and response to handling as well as the presence of clonic or tonic movements; stereotypies (e.g., excessive grooming, repetitive circling), and bizarre behavior (e.g., self-mutilation, walking backwards).

Functional Observational Battery (FOB): Once at the end of each exposure period, 5 rats/sex/group were randomly for a FOB evaluation. Each rat was evaluated during handling and while in an open field for excitability, autonomic function, gait, and sensorimotor coordination (open field and manipulative evaluations), reactivity and sensitivity (elicited behavior), and other abnormal clinical signs, (e.g., convulsions, tremors unusual or bizarre behavior emaciation, dehydration, and general appearance. The rats were observed in random order and without the observer having knowledge of the treatment group. In addition to the above observations, Forelimb and Hindlimb Grip Strength and Foot Splay measurements were obtained.

Motor activity (MA) was evaluated with FOB assessments for the same animals. Activity was monitored using an automated Photobeam Activity System® (San Diego Instruments, Inc.). The system monitored up to twenty animals during one session. An equal number of animals assigned to Motor Activity assessment from all dose groups were evaluated in each session, when possible. Each animal was placed into a polycarbonate solid bottom cage and the evaluation phase began immediately for that animal. Each animal was evaluated for a single one-hour phase, with photobeam count accumulated over six 10-minute intervals.

Body Weight: Individual body weights were recorded at least two times during acclimation. Animals assigned to the study were weighed on Day 1 (prior to study start) and weekly thereafter. The animals were also weighed immediately prior to necropsy in order to calculate organ-to-body weight ratios. Body weight gain was calculated for selected intervals and for the overall study period.

Food Consumption: Food consumption was measured and recorded to coincide with body weight measurements: at least twice during acclimation and weekly thereafter on the same days as body weights were recorded.

Clinical Pathology: Hematology, coagulation, clinical chemistry and urinalysis evaluations were performed at the end of their respective points of terminal sacrifice (after 28 days of administration or after 90 days of administration). Animals were fasted overnight prior to blood collection. Blood samples were collected from the sublingual vein or vena cava/abdominal aorta, under isoflurane anesthesia. Hematology samples (∼500 μL) were collected in tubes containing K2EDTA; coagulation samples (∼1.8 mL) were collected in tubes containing 3.2 % sodium citrated; clinical chemistry samples (∼1000 μL) were collected in tubes containing no preservatives. Where appropriate samples were centrifuged to separate plasma or serum. For urinalysis evaluation, rats were placed in metabolism on the day prior to blood collection and urine was collected overnight. The following parameters were evaluated:

Hematology - hematocrit, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, platelet count, red blood cell count, red cell distribution width, reticulocyte count, white blood cell count (total and absolute differential).

Coagulation - activated partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time.

Clinical Chemistry - alanine aminotransferase, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, bilirubin (total), creatinine, cholesterol (total), electrolytes (calcium, chloride, potassium, sodium), glucose (fasting), gamma glutamyl transferase, globulin and A/G (albumin/globulin) ratio (calculated), lipoprotein (LD & HD), phosphorus, protein (total), sorbitol dehydrogenase, triglycerides, urea nitrogen.

Urinalysis - bilirubin, blood, color and clarity, glucose, ketones, microscopy of centrifuged sediment, pH, protein (total), quality, specific gravity, volume, urobilinogen.

Anatomic Pathology: At the respective terminal sacrifices, all animals were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia. Randomly selected animals in the study (5 animals/sex/group at 28-days and 10 animals/sex/group at 90-days, including decedents) were subjected to a full necropsy, which included examination of the external surface of the body, all orifices, musculoskeletal system and the thoracic, abdominal, pelvic and cranial cavities and their contents. The following tissues were weighed as soon as possible after dissection to avoid drying; adrenals (combined), brain, epididymides (combined), heart, kidneys (combined), liver, ovaries with oviducts (combined), testes (combined), spleen, thymus, uterus. The pituitary, thyroid/parathyroid and prostate and seminal vesicles with coagulating gland were weighed after 24 h in 10 % neutral buffered formalin. Organ-to-body weight and organ-to-brain weight ratios were calculated. The following tissues were preserved in 10 % neutral buffered formalin (exceptions indicated below) for histopathological evaluation: accessory genital organs (prostate and seminal vesicles), adrenals, all gross lesions, aorta, bone (femur), bone marrow (femur and sternum), brain (medulla/pons, cerebellar and cerebral cortex), cecum, cervix, colon, duodenum, esophagus, Harderian gland, heart, ileum with Peyer’s patches, jejunum, kidneys, larynx, liver, lungs, lymph node (mandibular and mesenteric), mammary gland, nasal turbinates, nose, ovaries, oviducts, pancreas, parathyroid, peripheral nerve (sciatic), pharynx, pituitary gland, rectum, salivary glands (sublingual submandibular, and parotid), skeletal muscle, skin, spinal cord (cervical, mid-thoracic and lumbar), spleen, sternum, stomach, thymus, thyroid, trachea, urinary bladder, uterus, vagina. The eyes, epididymides, optic nerve and testes were preserved in modified Davidson’s fixative and then stored in ethanol.

Histopathology: Histological examination was performed on the preserved organs and tissues of the animals from the control and B. faecihominis, DSM 34943 test groups on animals necropsied at both 28 and 90 days. The fixed tissues (as described above) were trimmed, processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned with a microtome, placed on glass microscope slides, stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined by light microscopy. Both gross-to-microscopic correlations, and the incidence of microscopic findings were recorded. Grading criteria for microscopic observations were: Normal, finding not present; Minimal, a focal, subtle, or trivial change; Mild, an easily identifiable change of limited severity and/or distribution; Moderate, an obvious change with normal tissue remaining; Marked, an extensive change that obliterates much of the normal tissue; Severe, a maximal change, and Present, a finding for which grading is not appropriate. The anatomic pathology findings from the histological slide evaluation were peer reviewed.

Translocation Analysis: Samples from five randomly selected animals/sex/group at the end of each exposure period (28 and 90 days) were collected for translocation analysis. Whole blood (0.5 mL), liver and mesenteric lymph nodes (0.2–0.5 g of tissue) were excised and placed in deoxygenated PBS + 0.05 % L-cysteine. Tissue samples were homogenized under anaerobic conditions and aliquots of selected homogenized and/or diluted samples were plated on pre-reduced Yeast Casitone Fatty Acids Agar with Carbohydrates (YCFAC) plates and incubated in anaerobic conditions at 37 ± 1°C for 3–9 days to achieve adequate colony growth for counting. All colonies present were counted, and morphology assessed for seven characteristics, including size, form, elevation, margin, surface, opacity, and pigmentation. Colonies were selected and replated on fresh YCFAC plates and regrown for nine days at 37 ± 1°C, under anaerobic conditions, after which DNA was isolated. If there were more than five colonies of a given morphology on plates from a single animal tissue, only five colonies of that morphology were subject to further analysis. The average CFU/gram of tissue was calculated based on the amount of sample collected and dilution factors for each specific sample.

All plates were visually inspected for CFU growth after the second incubation and individual colonies were counted. The mean CFU/gram of tissue was calculated using the amount of respective sample evaluated, plate colony growth and factoring for applicable dilutions.

The sensitivity of the method was determined for each sample type using the average amount of tissue in each preparation, assuming a minimum detection limit of 1 CFU/plate and factoring for dilution of samples including plated volumes. Dilutions of the sample preparations either prior to inoculation or prior to plating were accounted for in the calculations by additional multiplication of the DF by the appropriate factor.

Microbial DNA was extracted, subjected to PCR amplification of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene variable region 4 (V4), and sequenced on a MiSeq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as previously described [9], [8]. For each tissue sample of origin, the morphology was assessed and a taxonomic designation was assigned to the sequences by comparison to the Genbank database [7]. Taxonomic assignments based on the V4 16S rRNA gene cannot distinguish strains, and for some taxa, may not differentiate species.

Statistical analysis

In-Life Data: For all in-life endpoints that were identified as multiple measurements of continuous data over time (e.g., body weight parameters and food consumption), treatment and control groups were compared using a repeated-measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA). Significant interactions observed between treatment and time as well as main effects were further analyzed by a post hoc multiple comparisons test (e.g., Dunnett’s test) of the individual treated groups to control.

Organ Weight and Enumeration/Translocation Data: When warranted by sufficient sample sizes, all endpoints with single measurements of continuous data within groups (e.g., organ weight, organ-to-body weight and organ-to-brain weight) were evaluated for homogeneity of variances c and normality. Where homogeneous variances and normal distribution were observed, treatment and control groups were compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When one-way ANOVA was significant, a comparison of the treated groups to control was performed with a multiple comparisons test (e.g., Dunnett’s test). Where variances were considered significantly different, groups were compared using a non-parametric method (e.g., Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric analysis of variance). When non-parametric analysis of variance was significant, a comparison of treated groups to control was performed (e.g., Dunn’s test).

Statistical Methods (Clinical Pathology): Preliminary analysis was conducted using Barlett’s test for homogeneity and Shapiro-Wilk test for normality. When an individual observation was recorded as being less than a certain value (e.g., below the lower limit of quantitation), calculations were performed on half the recorded value. For example, if bilirubin was reported as < 0.1 (or ≤0.1), 0.05 was used for any calculations performed with that bilirubin data. When an individual observation was recorded as being greater than a certain value (e.g., above the upper limit of quantitation), calculations were performed on the recorded value. For example, if specific gravity was reported as ≥ 1.100 (or ≥1.100), 1.100 was used for any calculation performed with that specific gravity data. If preliminary test was not significant, a one-way analysis of variance was performed followed by Dunnett's test. If the preliminary test was significant, log transformations of the data to achieve normality and variance homogeneity were used. If the log transformation failed, a non-parametric method (e.g., Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric analysis of variance) wase used. When non-parametric analysis of variance was significant, a comparison of treated groups to control was performed (e.g., Dunn’s test).

In vivo Engraftment in rats

Fecal matter was collected from each animal prior to test substance administration, and once/week for weeks 1–3 and once every three weeks thereafter beginning on Day 42. A single fecal pellet was collected for each animal at the respective time points indicated and stored frozen at −20 ℃ until used for microbiota profiling. Using the same protocol described in Kumar et al., [27] the 16S rRNA profiling was performed on rat fecal pellet samples collected from days pre-dose, 8, 44, and 87. Using sterile forceps, rat fecal pellets were placed into the wells of a Bead Plate from the DNeasy PowerSoil HTP 96 Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol and then subjected to 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing as previously described [27]. Briefly, the variable V4 region (515 F/806 R primers) was amplified and sequenced for 2 × 250 cycles on the MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting reads were clustered at 99 % similarity, and those with abundance > 0.1 % were assigned taxonomy against the RDP 16S rRNA taxonomy training set no. 18 [47].

Results

In vitro probiotic properties and strain safety

Hemolytic and D-/L+ -lactic acid

No hemolytic activity was observed for B. faecihominis DSM 34943 after 48 h incubation on Brucella blood agar, and no hemolysin-related toxin genes were identified in the genome. Therefore, this strain is not known to have any hemolytic properties.

The level of D-/L+ -lactic acid measured in the supernatant of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was close to zero and below the minimum level that could be accurately detected by the kit (Table 1).

Genomic safety and antimicrobial resistance testing

The genome of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was assembled into 5 contigs (3224,904 bp, 53.75 % G+C content, and 3283 coding sequences). It has a 95.6 % gANI with the type strain B. faecihominis DSM 100989, which is above the representative cut-off of 95 % for bacterial speciation [10]. The results of the genomic screening for antibiotic resistance genes identified one match, which was to the tetracycline resistance-related tet(W) gene with 97 % amino acid similarity. No known virulence factors, hemolysin genes, genes required to produce the biogenic amines histamine or tyramine, or toxins that would present a safety concern for eukaryotic cells were detected in the genome using an amino acid similarity threshold of 80 %.

The antimicrobial susceptibility profile for B. faecihominis DSM 34943 is shown in Table 1. The MIC values were equal to below the recommended breakpoints for both reference panels, the EFSA MIC cut-off values for Corynebacterium and other Gram-positive bacteria as well as the EUCAST MIC cut-off values for anaerobic species without established breakpoints. They only exceptions were for the antibiotic class of aminoglycosides (gentamycin, kanamycin and streptomycin). The MIC value for tetracycline exceeded the EFSA MIC cut-off value within one dilution step, which is considered an accepted normal variation around the mean and does not indicate resistance [12]. No reference breakpoint values were available for ciprofloxacin, colistin, daptomycin, fosfomycin, penicillin or trimethoprim against Butyricicoccus.

Preclinical efficacy in a diet induced obesity (DIO) mouse model

B. faecihominis DSM 34943 improved several relevant metabolic endpoints and markers in this DIO model. Mice on a high fat diet (HFD) showed a significant increase in body weight as compared to mice on normal chow suggesting the manifestation of metabolic disorders in the mice on HFD. Resistin and leptin levels were measured in serum and the group receiving B. faecihominis DSM 34943 showed significant improvements (Fig. 1A and B). An insulin tolerance test was performed on day 83 and B. faecihominis DSM 34943 group showed significant improvement in insulin tolerance as compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 2A-B). Cholesterol (p = 0.007) and LDL (p = 0.08) levels showed similar trends in the group receiving B. faecihominis DSM 34943 (Fig. 3A-B). qNMR measurements showed improvement in total fat weight in the mice receiving B. faecihominis DSM 34943 (p = 0.06) as intervention (Fig. 4). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was measured as a biomarker for liver function and it showed a similar trend, B. faecihominis group showed improvements in serum levels as compared to control group (Fig. 5).

28- and 90-day repeated dose study in rats

Dose formulation

Stability: The percent change in the CFU content of the test substances over the course of the study (Day 1, 46 and 92) was −0.39 % and the overall stability was determined to be 99.6 %.

Homogeneity: Analysis of the Day 1 dose preparations resulted in a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 0.97 %. The test substance was considered to be homogenously distributed in the dose mixtures at all study concentrations.

Concentration Verification: The analysis of Day 1 samples resulted in 89.4, 90.9, and 90.9 % of the target concentration. The Day 46 samples resulted in 89.2 % and the Day 92 samples resulted in 172.5 % of target concentrations.(Table 3)

Clinical pathology

There were no adverse treatment-related changes in hematology, coagulation, clinical chemistry, or urinalysis parameters. Three hematology endpoints, hematocrit at 28-days and at 90-days, hemoglobin at 28-days and red blood cell counts at 28-days were significantly increased in the B. faecihominis DSM 34943 treated males. Because these values were within historical control ranges (Table 4), occurred only in a single sex and did not occur at both evaluation time points (hematocrit and RBC count), they were not considered to be biologically relevant nor related to B. faecihominis DSM 34943 treatment.

Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was statistically increased in males at 90-days (Table 5). As the APTT change was not consistent across timepoints (28-day and 90-days) or sexes and was within historical control ranges (Table 5), this effect was not considered treatment-related nor biologically relevant.

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (p < 0.01) and sorbitol dehydrogenase (SDH) levels (p < 0.05) were significantly increased at 90-days for B. faecihominis DSM 34943-treated males (Table 4). Increased mean ALT in males administered B. faecihominis within the historical control range, and there were no hepatic or renal histopathological correlates. Thus, this finding was not considered to be an adverse event. SDH levels were significantly decreased (p < 0.05) at 28-days for B. faecihominis DSM 34943-treated females. While the SDH effect did occur in both sexes, the SDH values were within the historical control range and did not occur at both timepoints (female at 28-days and male at 90-days), the difference was not considered biologically relevant nor treatment related.

Anatomic pathology and histopathology

There were no macroscopic observed at necropsy and no microscopic observations (Table 6) that were attributable to B. faecihominis DSM 34943 administration. Males and females receiving B. faecihominis had small statistically significant decreases in absolute adrenal weights at the Day 28 necropsy. Adrenal glands from 28-day animals were not processed for histopathological evaluation; however, a similar decrease in absolute adrenal gland weights was not observed in the rats sacrificed at 90-days and there were no effects on the adrenal-to-body weight or adrenal-to-brain weight endpoints. This indicates that the adrenal weight differences were likely a random occurrence at Day 28. No other organ weight differences (absolute organ weight, organ-to-body weight and organ-to-brain weight) were identified at necropsy for at either 28-days or 90-days of exposure (Table 7).

Discussion

The butyrate-producing species Butyricicoccus faecihominis was first described by Takada et al. in 2016. Butyrate is a key microbiota derived SCFA metabolite and energy source for colonocytes that has been associated with human health in particular gastrointestinal health and metabolic health. The Butyricicoccus faecihominis strain DSM 34943 was evaluated for its potential for metabolic health in a DIO mouse model and we demonstrated improvements in insulin tolerance and plasma lipids in particular total cholesterol. These effects may be linked to reductions in resistin and leptin levels that were also observed. These results suggest that B. faecihominis DSM 34943 presents a novel candidate for a next generation probiotic for human health.

B. faecihominis is a common human gut commensal that has been detected in greater than 50 % of individuals with a mean abundance of 0.09 % in the fecal microbiome of healthy adults [31]; however, strains of B. faecihominis are not part of our traditional food chain and have not been used as probiotics previously. Hence, we conducted a comprehensive safety assessment of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 including genomic safety, phenotypic antimicrobial resistance, potential hemolytic activity, lactate production, acid/bile tolerance as well as a subchronic 28-day and 90-day oral toxicity study in rats with translocation analysis.

The results of the in vitro and genomic safety evaluations for B. faecihominis DSM 34943 demonstrated the strain has a favorable safety profile as a probiotic. B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was non-hemolytic and did not exhibit any aspects of toxigenicity such as production of biogenic amines or D-lactate. The strain showed sensitivity to bile and acidic conditions when directly exposed suggesting that enteric protection may be required when provided for human consumption. There were no genes related to virulence or toxin production identified in the genome [14]. B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was phenotypically susceptible to antibiotics of clinical relevance for humans, with the exception of the antibiotic class of aminoglycosides ([12]; EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed [13]). Aminoglycosides, however, are not active against obligate anaerobes because their uptake across the bacterial cell membrane depends on energy derived from aerobic metabolism; hence, they are intrinsically resistant [6]. Additionally, there was a tetracycline resistance-associated tet(W) gene identified in the genome of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 that is identical to the protein sequence found in Bifidobacterium breve strains, and similarly, other Bifidobacteria including probiotic strains. These strains also frequently carry a transposon adjacent to the tet(W) gene [40], [45]. Antibiotic susceptibility testing found that B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was phenotypically sensitive to tetracycline, as the MIC was only one dilution above the breakpoint which is considered within a normal variation. Several studies have documented that the presence of a single tet(W) gene does not correlate well with resistance to tetracycline, and rather increased resistance is mediated through multiple genes [20], [2], [26], [32]. The function of tet(W) in a phage-associated genomic island was studied in Lawsonia intracellularis, and the authors hypothesized that this protein and mobile element may rather be important for host colonization and environmental adaptation [46]. This hypothesis is consistent with the number of Bifidobacterium species that carry a tet(W) gene but remain sensitive to tetracycline [20], [2], [26].

A literature search revealed no non-clinical toxicity studies of B. faecihominis and to our knowledge this is the first report of an OECD guideline study evaluating sub-chronic toxicity with a B. faecihominis strain. The current study established the lack of systemic toxicity associated with B. faecihominis DSM 34943. As reported, there were some differences in various parameters such as increased forelimb grip strength in males, increased hematocrit, hemoglobin and red blood cells in males, decreased APTT (males) and PT (females), increased ALT and SDH in males, decreased SDH in females. and altered urinalysis parameter in males at 90-days. None of these effects were considered to be adverse or related to treatments with B. faecihominis as they were within historical control ranges, did not occur in both sexes, and did not occur at both sampling timepoints.

Therefore, the NOEL for exposure periods (28-day and 90-day) was greater than 2000 mg/kg body weight/day (1.97 1011 CFU/kg body weight/day). Applying a 100-fold minimum required safety factor when applying animal study data to humans, this is equivalent to a daily dose of 1.34 × 1011 CFU/day for a 70 kg human adult.

We also studied the presence of of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 in rat fecal pellets and demonstrated a gradual increase in the relative abundance of the strain from day 8 to day 87 as compared to the PBS control. This data supports a possible engraftment of B. faecihominis DSM 34943, however, it is not known if B. faecihominis will persist without continuous administration, or if the observed engraftment is transient. Furthermore, we investigated the translocation potential of B. faecihominis DSM 34943. Despite the high dose tested (1.97 ×1011 CFU/kg BW/day) and an increase in relative abundance of the strain within the GI tract of the rats to around 5 % over the course of the study, we did not see any signs of translocation or any other signs of increased gut permeability. While the data from the translocation analysis shows that molecular characterization of the small numbers of isolates obtained from liver, kidney and mesentery samples were in both control and treatment groups, there were no statistically significant differences in the number of colonies isolated between control and treatment groups. Of the isolates sequenced, 43 were not differentiable from the test strain by 16S V4 analysis; however, these were distributed across isolates from vehicle control as well as treatment groups. Translocation studies often result in low numbers of colonies per rodent in both control and test substance animals and this has been demonstrated even for probiotic strains with a very long history of safe use [33], [50]. Low levels of bacteria have been identified in extra-intestinal sites of healthy rodents without evidence of pathology [21], [28], [38], [39]. The absence of higher bacterial counts in extra-intestinal locations coupled with a lack of adverse hematology and microscopic findings indicates that B. faecihominis DSM 34943 will not translocate from the gastrointestinal tract in healthy populations, as indicated by this study.

Health benefit of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 was evaluated in a DIO mice model where mice on high fat diet were gavaged daily with B. faecihominis DSM 34943. Introduction of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 resulted in significant improvement in leptin and resistin levels in the serum as compared to the vehicle control group. Insulin tolerance test (ITT) also showed similar benefits as compared to the vehicle control group. The lipid profile showed that B. faecihominis DSM 34943 can improve serum levels of cholesterol and LDL and resulted in improved fat weight by end of the study. Liver function was evaluated by measuring ALT levels and showed lower ALT levels in the group receiving B. faecihominis DSM 34943.

Isolated from the feces of a healthy human adult, B. faecihominis DSM 34943 presents a promising next generation probiotic that has been established as safe for human ingestion given the comprehensive genomic, phenotypic, in vitro, and in vivo data presented herein. In addition, the strain has shown beneficial activity in relation to insulin sensitivity and blood lipids in a DIO model, therefore human clinical studies are warranted to understand the association of this strain and human health. This study has summarized the comprehensive safety profile and metabolic activity of B. faecihominis DSM 34943 and supports the potential use of this commensal microbe as a next generation probiotic.
