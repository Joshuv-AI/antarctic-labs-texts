BiCervi: Pap smear microscopy image dataset for cervical cancer detection and classification
Authors: Hope Mbelwa, Judith Leo, Crispin Kahesa, Asafu Munema, Elizabeth Mkoba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer is still a major public-health challenge, especially in low and middle-income countries where there are limited numbers of specialists to review Pap smears. While Pap smear screening is widely used, manual reading is slow and can vary from one expert to another, which makes data-driven decision support increasingly important. However, Pap smear imagery datasets from African screening settings are still limited, slowing down the development and fair evaluation of machine-learning models. This article presents a curated dataset of Pap smear microscopy images collected at The Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Tanzania. Images were obtained using a digital microscope with a 10 × /0.25 objective lens, and stored as RGB JPEG images at a resolution of a 2592 × 1944 pixels. The Bethesda System for reporting cervical cytology was used to label the images and organize them into eight diagnostic categories: Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US), Atypical Squamous Cells, cannot exclude High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (ASC-H), Atypical Glandular Cells (AGC), High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL), Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (LSIL), Negative for Intraepithelial Lesion or Malignancy (NILM), Squamous Cell Carcinoma, and Adenocarcinoma. Labels were independently reviewed by two experts for consistency, and disagreements were resolved through adjudication to reach a final decision; images that could not be labeled with sufficient confidence were excluded during quality control. The resulting dataset comprises 3,000 images and can be used to develop Artificial Intelligence-based tools for cervical cancer detection.

Background

The Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, emphasizes good health and well-being through prevention, early detection, and equitable access to quality care. In many low and middle-income countries, cervical cancer remains a major health burden, partly because screening services can be limited and specialist review of Pap smear slides is time-consuming [1]. Although Pap smear cytology is a well-established screening method, progress toward Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted screening tools is often slowed by the lack of well-curated, locally representative, and publicly accessible datasets [2], especially those that capture the variability seen in routine microscopy such as staining differences, artifacts, and overlapping cells [3].

This dataset was developed in collaboration with the Pathology Laboratory at the Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Tanzania. The goal was to create a practical, research-ready resource that can help accelerate reproducible work in cervical cytology AI. Specifically, this effort focused on i) collecting and organizing Pap smear microscopy images that reflect real laboratory conditions and a wide range of cervical cytology findings, ii) applying a consistent annotation and quality-check workflow (including diagnostic labels and relevant metadata, where available) so the data can be used reliably for training and benchmarking, and iii) releasing the dataset with clear documentation and usage guidance to support model development, validation and future extensions toward clinically meaningful decision-support tools.

Data Description

The dataset is composed of 3,000 Pap smear microscopy images placed within eight different class folder directories; each directory represents a specific category of classification as described by The Bethesda System (TBS) for reporting of cervical cytology [4], such as: Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance, Atypical Squamous Cells, cannot exclude High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion, Atypical Glandular Cells, High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions, Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions, Negative for Intraepithelial Lesion or Malignancy, Squamous Cell Carcinoma, Adenocarcinoma as shown in Table 1 [5]. For quality assurance, all labels were independently reviewed by a second pathologist, disagreements were resolved through consensus and third-review adjudication when needed. Basic image quality control included blur and focus filtering, artifact removal, and duplicate checks. The dataset release includes a README file describing the class-specific directory structure, file naming convention, and abbreviations used for folder names, providing guidance to help users navigate and use the dataset effectively. The dataset exhibits substantial class imbalance across diagnostic categories. NILM is the largest category with 1,447 images (48.2%), and Adenocarcinoma the smallest with only 9 images (0.3%), resulting in an approximate imbalance ratio of 160.8:1 between the largest and smallest classes. This distribution reflects the natural uneven occurrence of cervical cytology findings in routine clinical screening settings, where normal findings and pre-invasive abnormalities are generally more common than invasive malignant cases.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data acquisition procedure

The data were collected at the Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI), Tanzania, between April and October 2025. Pap smear slides were retrieved from laboratory archives and sorted into slide storage boxes with the assistance of cytotechnologists and histopathologists. The slides were then photographed using a digital microscope equipped with a 10 × /0.25 objective lens. One or two images were captured from each Pap smear slide depending on the availability of diagnostically relevant fields of view observed during image acquisition. Additional fields were captured only when they provided complementary morphological information that could improve representation of the specimen, and no slide contributed more than two images to the dataset. A consistent approach was applied to each sample to ensure a standardized microscopy imaging process using the same microscope setup and a fixed set of acquisition parameters, including objective magnification and illumination settings. The resulting images were saved as 2592 × 1944-pixel RGB JPEG files. To preserve patient privacy and confidentiality, all patient identifiers were removed before dataset preparation. Consequently, patient-level linkage information is not available in the dataset release.

Imaging configuration

Images were acquired using a digital microscope with a 10 × /0.25 objective lens with standard acquisition settings. The microscope produced images with a native resolution of 2592 × 1944 pixels, saved in RGB JPEG format. No resizing operations were performed before release of the dataset to maintain original visual characteristics and cellular morphology. Image collection was conducted under constant illumination settings and acquisition parameters to minimize the variability introduced by the imaging setup. The JPEG compression level was determined by the default settings for the microscope acquisition software and was not separately recorded at the time of data collection. In addition, spatial calibration information e.g., micrometers per pixel was not available from the microscope system during image acquisition and is therefore not included in the dataset release.

A 10 × /0.25 objective lens was chosen to offer a larger field of view that retains the general cellular distribution patterns and contextual morphological information routinely taken into account in cervical cytology screening. Higher magnifications such as 20 × or 40 × can show more detailed nuclear morphology, but these magnifications tend to capture smaller regions in the image and may reduce the contextual information available in a single field of view. The current dataset was not designed for detailed nuclear morphometric analysis but rather for image-level diagnostic categorization and field-level pattern recognition. Therefore, the selected magnification represented a practical balance between preserving sufficient cellular detail and capturing broader spatial context within each field of view.

Annotation protocol

This dataset provides image-level diagnostic annotations only. Each microscopy image was assigned a single diagnostic category according to the Bethesda System for reporting cervical cytology. No object-level annotations, such as bounding boxes, polygon annotations, or segmentation masks, were generated during this release. Labels are represented through class-specific directory structures and accompanying metadata files describing image identifiers and diagnostic categories. Two experts independently verified the consistency of the diagnostic labels. Disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus to arrive at the final annotation. Formal inter-rater agreement statistics such as Cohen’s kappa coefficient were not calculated in this study. The dataset was designed primarily for image classification and related representation-learning tasks.

Data preprocessing and quality control

During data collection, the preprocessing activity involved image cleaning, annotation, and labeling [6]. Images were initially stored in Google Drive for temporary holding and review. A pathologist double-checked the images to verify class validity and overall quality, and unclear images were discarded. Further evaluation of image quality was performed before inclusion in the dataset. Images with large out-of-focus areas, motion blur, severe staining artifacts, obscured fields of view, duplicate captures, or diagnostic uncertainty that could affect label reliability were excluded. This quality-control process was conducted to improve the consistency and usability of the final dataset. Final annotation and labeling were completed jointly by the pathologists and the researcher, after which the images were organized into class-specific folders and uploaded to the Zenodo repository [5]. The Pap smear images were renamed using the convention (class-abbreviation_z), where z is a sequential index from 1 to n, and n is the total number of images in that class.

Limitations

The data presented in this work has been predominantly obtained from the Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Tanzania, thus may be biased by factors of time and location (April to October, 2025). The data may not demonstrate all of the variability present in other laboratories, screening programs, microscopes, staining processes, and patient populations therefore may not accurately represent the full spectrum of cervical cytology data in global contexts.

Additionally, the dataset has class imbalance, with the largest class (NILM, 1,447 images) and the smallest class (Adenocarcinoma, 9 images) which creates an imbalance ratio of about 160.8:1. Such imbalance can lead to biasness during supervised model training, where classifiers tend to favor majority categories and lose sensitivity for rare malignant classes. Hence, any statistical comparison with underrepresented categories should be taken with caution, and direct multi-class learning approaches may provide unstable performance estimates of these classes. Future studies may explore class-weighted loss functions, focal loss, oversampling techniques, data augmentation and hierarchical classification approaches to mitigate these challenges. Despite the limitations, this dataset remains valuable for binary screening tasks, such as distinguishing normal from abnormal findings and supports the development and evaluation of automated approaches for early cervical cancer detection.
