Trichobezoar with Rapunzel syndrome: a case report of a 23-year-old female with trichophagia
Authors: Hashim G. Ismaeil Teeso, Mohamed A. Alian, Eltahir A. Eltahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Trichobezoars (hair concretions) are uncommon and are typically found in young women with psychiatric issues, who often deny eating their own hair (trichophagia). Continuous ingestion over time can cause hair to lodge in the stomach along with mucus and food. In some cases, the trichobezoar can extend past the pylorus into the jejunum, ileum, or even the colon, a condition known as Rapunzel syndrome. Only a few cases have been reported from Sudan.Clinical presentation:We reported a case of Trichobezoar with Rapunzel syndrome in a 23-year-old female with a history of trichophagia, managed by laparotomy and anterior gastrostomy. The post-operative period went smoothly, and the patient was discharged in good condition before being referred to the psychiatric department.Clinical discussion:Diagnosing trichobezoar is difficult, especially in low-resource settings, where it relies on clinical expertise. Surgery is the cornerstone of management; a multidisciplinary approach is important to address the underlying cause.Conclusion:Trichobezoar, although it is a rare medical condition, should not be overlooked as a differential diagnosis in young females presenting with nonspecific upper abdominal complaints and an epigastric mass. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are essential to prevent complications and recurrence.

Introduction

A bezoar is a solid mass of poorly digested or undigested material that builds up in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and is considered an uncommon medical condition. Throughout history, bezoars formed in animals were used as remedies for snakebites, epilepsy, and even the plague[1,2]. They still form part of traditional medicine used by many cultures[2].

Overall, bezoars are present in the stomach in fewer than 0.5% of individuals undergoing esophagogastroduodenoscopy and in the small intestine in 0.4%–4.8% of cases with intestinal obstruction[1].

Bezoars are divided into four groups based on their composition. (1) Phytobezoars are made of indigestible material found in fruits and vegetables. (2) Trichobezoars are composed mainly of a patient’s hair, though hair from animals, carpets, or toys can sometimes be found. (3) Pharmacobezoars are composed of various undigested medicines. (4) Other bezoars form a diverse group that includes collections of different substances such as pieces of paper, gloves, shellac, Styrofoam, cement, milk curd, and several other materials[3].

Rapunzel syndrome is a rare type of trichobezoar. Its clinical presentation varies from no symptoms to general, intermittent upper GI issues like epigastric discomfort, pain, abdominal bloating, loss of appetite, and weight loss. It can also present with gastric outlet obstruction symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and constipation[4]. The most common presenting symptom is abdominal pain in 70.2%, and the most common sign is a palpable mass in the upper abdomen in large bezoars[5].

These patients may have breath with a foul odor due to the decomposition and fermentation of fats[2], which, along with other clinical information and history, can raise suspicion of a gastric bezoar.

Complications can arise from the large intragastric mass, which increases intraluminal pressure and may lead to erosions, ulceration, or gastric emphysema. The pressure from the mass might lead to biliary obstruction or acute pancreatitis[2,5], resulting in additional symptoms such as upper GI bleeding or jaundice. In rare cases, it can cause complete gastric obstruction with perforation and an acute abdomen[5]. There are multiple therapeutic modalities described for the treatment of trichobezoar; however, surgery remains the gold-standard treatment[6].

Laparoscopy can be used for small- to moderate-sized trichobezoars. Few authors have reported laparoscopic removal of large trichobezoars[7,8].

This case has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria for case reports[9].

Case report

A 23-year-old woman arrived at the Department of Internal Medicine with a 5-month history of central abdominal pain accompanied by early satiety, nausea, intermittent vomiting, an abdominal mass, and unintentional weight loss. The abdominal pain was described as dull, persistent, and mainly localized to the central and epigastric regions.

The patient reports a long-standing habit of pulling out and eating her own hair, consistent with trichotillomania and trichophagia.

On examination, the patient appeared well, not in distress, and was normotensive. She was not pale or jaundiced.

Abdominal examination revealed a firm epigastric mass that was not tender, dull to percussion, and moved with respiration. There were no signs of guarding or peritonism. The clinical picture is suggestive of a trichobezoar (gastric hairball), which is among the differential diagnoses.

A CT scan of the abdomen revealed a large, well-defined intraluminal gastric mass with a mottled, heterogeneous appearance, containing trapped air. The mass was seen extending from the stomach into the duodenum and reaching the proximal jejunum, consistent with Rapunzel syndrome. There was no evidence of perforation or distal bowel obstruction (Figs 1,2). Hemostasis was ensured, and the abdominal wall was closed in layers. The patient recovered smoothly from anesthesia, tolerated the postoperative period well, and had an uncomplicated recovery. Bowel function gradually returned, and she was progressively advanced to an oral diet as tolerated. She was discharged on postoperative day 7 in good condition.

Due to her history of trichophagia, the patient was referred to the Psychiatry Department for further assessment and treatment.

Discussion

Trichobezoars are uncommon and often linked to psychiatric disorders, especially in young females. Bezoars can form in individuals with normal GI physiology and anatomy. However, patients with altered GI anatomy and/or motility are at higher risk of developing bezoars[10].

The risk factors for bezoar formation are: partial gastrectomy, with or without vagotomy; diabetes mellitus with gastroparesis; and other systemic diseases that may cause disturbances of gastric motility[10]. Other potential factors are: impaired mastication, particularly in edentulous patients, increased dietary fiber content, cystic fibrosis, and psychiatric diseases[11,12].

It is often linked to psychiatric disorders, particularly trichotillomania with trichophagia, and may also be associated with obsessive-compulsive, depressive, anxiety, and intellectual disabilities, especially among young females. Bezoars can form in individuals with normal GI physiology and anatomy. However, patients with altered GI anatomy and/or motility are at higher risk of developing bezoars. Only a few cases of trichobezoar have been reported from Sudan. Elnaim described a Sudanese series of three young women with gastric trichobezoar, in which the condition represented the first presentation of underlying psychiatric disorders[13]. The study highlights the rarity of this condition in our setting and the importance of recognizing early psychiatric associations. Similar to his findings, this patient presented with delayed symptoms and required laparotomy for definitive management.

However, unlike in the case series, this patient developed Rapunzel syndrome, with the trichobezoar extending beyond the pylorus into the duodenum and proximal jejunum. This underscores the wide variation in clinical presentations[13].

The way a bezoar presents varies depending on its content. Lactobezoars are typically seen in premature infants and newborns and may present with feeding difficulties, abdominal swelling, irritability, and, sometimes, vomiting. A physical exam may detect a palpable abdominal mass in these patients[14]. Pharmacobezoars may cause symptoms of gastric outlet obstruction, but they can also produce pharmacological effects. Consequently, these patients face a higher risk of drug toxicity[15].

Trichobezoars can develop over several years, initially causing mild symptoms like nausea or early fullness. As they enlarge, symptoms may include epigastric pain, gastric outlet obstruction, ulceration, GI bleeding, or even perforation.

The diagnosis of a trichobezoar begins with a detailed medical history, with particular focus on the patient’s dietary habits[2,16]. Trichotillomania and trichophagia may be observed in female patients with concurrent psychological or behavioral conditions such as depression and personality disorders. When a trichobezoar is suspected, the physical examination may reveal severe halitosis and patchy hair loss[16]. Symptoms can include abdominal pain, a sensation of fullness in the epigastric region, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, dysphagia, or hematemesis; additional symptoms such as hypotension, altered mental status, and shock may develop due to life-threatening complications like GI bleeding and obstruction, pancreatitis, necrosis of the visceral wall, and subsequent perforation[1–3]. Blood tests may show signs of anemia, electrolyte imbalances, trace element deficiencies, hypoproteinemia, and malabsorption of iron and vitamins[2,16]. A well-defined abdominal mass in the epigastric region is palpable in 85% of patients[17]. In Elnaim’s series, the most common finding is an epigastric mass. In this patient, the most prominent clinical signs and symptoms were vomiting, abdominal pain, nausea, and a palpable abdominal mass. The gold standard diagnostic investigation of trichobezoar is the upper GI endoscopy, and it can also be used for treatment in selected cases with small-sized trichobezoars[1,2,12,13,16].

An abdominal X-ray may raise clinical suspicion of a trichobezoar. In contrast, an abdominal U/S depicts the trichobezoar as a non-vascularized mass within the stomach or other hollow viscus; an abdominal CT scan can also reveal a mass and is very helpful in detecting small intestinal trichobezoars[1,2,12,16]. In this case, the abdominal CT scan was the diagnostic imaging modality.

Surgery remains the preferred treatment because it allows for examination of the entire GI tract and is associated with higher success rates and fewer complications[6]. For small- or medium-sized trichobezoars, laparoscopy may be an option. Only a few authors have reported the laparoscopic removal of large gastric trichobezoars[7,8].

Similar to findings reported in previous case series, this patient presented with delayed symptoms and required laparotomy for definitive treatment[13].

Conclusion

Trichobezoar, although it is a rare medical condition, should not be forgotten as a differential diagnosis in young females presenting with vague upper abdominal complaints and an epigastric lump.

Early detection and collaborative care are essential to prevent complications and recurrence.
