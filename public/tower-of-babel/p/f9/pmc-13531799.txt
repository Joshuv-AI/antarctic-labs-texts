Green-synthesized lanthanum nanostructures modulate redox balance and phytochemical responses in lead-stressed Zataria multiflora
Authors: Iman Motezaker, Mohammad Reza Baezzat, Vahid Tavallali, Omid Espargham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLead (Pb) toxicity disrupts redox homeostasis and impairs phytochemical stability in medicinal plants; however, nanomaterial-based approaches to alleviate oxidative stress under Pb stress have not been extensively studied. In the present study, lanthanum-based nanostructures (La-based NCs) were prepared using an eco-friendly method employing Arabic gum and spermine, and their efficacy in alleviating Pb-stress-induced oxidative damage in Zataria multiflora was investigated. The plants were subjected to different levels of Pb contamination (0, 250, 500, and 750 mg/kg) and then treated with La-based NCs at doses ranging from 0 to 200 ppm. The effect of Pb toxicity (750 mg/kg) led to an increase in indicators of oxidative stress, including H₂O₂ and MDA, along with a significant decrease in the activity of phytochemicals and antioxidant response. Treatment with La-based NCs significantly improved these effects dose-dependently, showing the optimal dose at 100 ppm. At 100 ppm of La-based NCs treatment, oxidative damage was decreased by 40–60%, whereas antioxidant responses were stimulated by 35–70%. Simultaneously, metabolic stability was enhanced through the recovery of essential oils and related phytochemical characteristics. Particularly, La-based NCs stabilized with Arabic gum consistently showed greater activity than those stabilized with spermine, suggesting a stabilization-mediated influence on redox reactions and plant-NCs interactions. This study demonstrates that green-synthesized La-based NCs have potential applications as modulators of oxidative stress and antioxidants within the plant system under Pb stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09532-4.Keywords: Zataria multiflora, Lead stress, Lanthanum nanostructures, Antioxidant defense system, Secondary metabolites

Introduction

Medicinal plants are valuable sources of bioactive compounds with major roles in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmetic industries [1, 2]. Plants synthesize large numbers of secondary metabolites such as phenolics, flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids that play roles in their defense mechanisms against environmental stresses as well as in their medicinal activities [3, 4]. Among them, Shirazi thyme (Zataria multiflora Boiss), an Iran endemic species belonging to the Lamiaceae family, has great interest because of its elevated essential oil percentage and extensive variety of phytochemicals including thymol, carvacrol, and rosmarinic acid [5, 6]. The quality and quantity of these metabolites are greatly influenced by environmental factors and abiotic stresses including drought, salinity, and heavy metal contamination [7].

Among many environmental contaminants, lead (Pb) is one of the most hazardous heavy metals that adversely affects plant metabolism and growth [8]. Pb is a non-biodegradable metal that gets accumulated in the soil through industrial emissions, phosphate fertilizers, and contaminated irrigation water [9]. When Pb is absorbed by plants, it interacts with physiological processes such as nutrient uptake, ionic homeostasis, photosynthesis, and nitrogen metabolism [10]. Its accumulation in plant tissues triggers the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to lipid peroxidation, chlorophyll degradation, DNA damage, and eventual cell death [10, 11].

In medicinal plants, heavy metal phytotoxicity is of primary concern since it has a tendency to alter secondary metabolite biosynthetic pathways, thus lowering their pharmacological quality [12]. Heavy metal stress, in the guise of Pb, has been proven to have negative impacts on the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, e.g., phenolics and flavonoids, reduce antioxidant capacity, and inhibit essential oil production in medicinal crops [10]. These biochemical alterations are often attributed to the inhibition of the activity of the major enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway and the impairment in the antioxidant defense system [13].

Nanotechnology based interventions have recently emerged as prospective approaches to alleviate heavy metal mediated stress in plants. Nanostructures (NCs) possess unique physicochemical properties such as high surface area, catalytic power, and redox potential that enable them to modulate physiological and biochemical reactions under stress conditions [14–16]. Among rare earth based nanomaterials, lanthanum-base nanostructures (La-based NCs) and lanthanum oxide (La₂O₃) have garnered considerable interest because of their high stability, ion exchange property, and potential in enhancing plant stress tolerance [17, 18]. It has been reported that suitable concentrations of La-based NCs have been capable of improving the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), the efficacy of photosynthesis, and combat oxidative stress in heavy metal stressed plants [19, 20].

Nonetheless, NCs synthesis method is a significant parameter that decides nanostructures' biological behavior, toxicity, and stability. Conventional chemical synthesis pathways employ toxic reducing agents and organic solvents, which are detrimental to the environment and human health [21]. Green synthesis is an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative that takes advantage of biological entities such as plant extracts, polysaccharides, and polyamines as reducing and stabilizing agents [22]. Among them, Gum Arabic, a water soluble natural polysaccharide, has been widely used due to its abundance of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and sulfate functional groups that facilitate reduction and stabilization of metallic ions to form biocompatible and uniform NCs [23, 24].

A biologically important molecule, spermine, a naturally occurring polyamine, is an important regulator of plant growth, cell division, and abiotic stress response [25]. Polyamines such as spermine trigger stress tolerance by modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes, stabilization of cellular membranes, and inhibition of lipid peroxidation [26]. Spermine can also be utilized as a mild reducing and capping agent in the fabrication of NCs, controlling particle size along with surface functionality [27]. Hence, the application of Gum Arabic and spermine in the green synthesis of La-based NCs can result in NCs with improved physicochemical stability and biocompatibility [24].

Given the detrimental effects of heavy metals on medicinal plants and the need to maintain their phytochemical integrity, the application of La-based NCs synthesized via green chemistry can be a novel and efficient strategy for enhancing stress tolerance and sustaining bioactive compounds [10, 14]. However, despite growing interest in rare earth NCs, there is still a lack of studies examining the effects of these NCs on the phytochemical characteristics of medicinal plants under Pb stress, particularly in Zataria multiflora Boiss.

The current research work attempts to fill in this void by considering lanthanum nanoparticle (La-based NCs) priming of rare earth origin. It will further concentrate on the effect of different stabilizers and their impact on the physiological activities of La-based NCs. Although the earlier research work concentrated on NCs and their influence on plant's resistance to stress conditions, very few research works focused on the importance of stabilization system to affect NCs-plant interaction and their physiological consequences. In this study, we proposed the hypothesis that biologically produced La-based NCs would be capable of alleviating Pb-induced oxidative stress in Zataria multiflora through redox homeostasis maintenance, prevention from cellular membrane damage, and increased levels of phytochemicals in plants. In addition, as both La based NCs were prepared through two different systems of reductions and stabilization, they showed differential physiological activities in Zataria multiflora under Pb stress condition. Considering the objectives of current study, physiological/biochemical approaches have provided information about the mechanism rather than molecular level analysis.

Materials and methods

Instruments and analytical equipment

All analyses and measurements made in this work were carried out through high-precision laboratory equipment. The pH of the experimental solution was maintained by a pH meter (Model PP-50, Sartorius, Germany). Quantitative analysis of volatile essential oil constituents was done on a gas chromatograph (GC) (Model 7890 A, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an HP-5 column (30 m × 0.32 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness). For the qualitative analysis of the volatile components, a gas chromatograph mass spectrometer (GC–MS) (Model 5975 C, Agilent Technologies, USA) coupled with an HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) was employed.

Antioxidant activity assays were performed using a microplate reader (Model ELx808, BioTek, Germany). Sample weighing was achieved with a digital analytical balance (Model CP324S, Sartorius, Germany) with a precision of ± 0.0001 g. Green synthesis of La-based NCs was conducted using an adjustable amplitude and frequency ultrasonic homogenizer (Model Sonics Vibra-Cell, Sonics & Materials, USA). Refrigerated centrifuge (Model 3-16PK, Sigma, Germany) was used for purification and separation of NCs during synthesis. Drying of the nanostructure was carried out in a drying oven (Model APT.line™, Binder, Germany). UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometry (Model Lambda 950, PerkinElmer, USA) was used to quantify the electronic transitions and optical absorbance of the synthesized NCs. Hydrodynamic particle size distribution and colloidal stability of the foliar suspensions were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements using a NanoQ analyzer (Microtrac, Japan) at 25 °C with three replicates per sample. Functional group vibrations and the surface chemistry of the resulting NCs were characterized on a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Model Tensor II, Bruker, Germany) equipped with an ATR crystal over the range 400–4000 cm⁻1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻1. Morphological studies of the NCs were conducted using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Model MIRA4, TESCAN, Czech Republic) and TEM (EM-900, Zeiss) to analyze particle size, shape, and topography of the surface. The XRD patterns were collected using an X’Pert PRO MPD diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands). The elemental compositions of samples were analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy in combination with SEM.

Extraction and HPLC analysis of gum Arabic and mushroom extracts

Commercial grade edible gum Arabic was purchased and used directly without any purification. White button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) waste was collected from a local industrial mushroom farming unit and used as raw material for green extraction of spermine.

To extract spermine-enriched polyamine fraction, 150 g of fresh mushroom waste was first washed with distilled water and homogenized in the presence of 225 mL of distilled water using a domestic blender for 60 s (30 s at low speed and 30 s at medium speed). The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was recovered. The remaining solid residue was re-extracted with 600 mL of distilled water, and the two supernatants were pooled, lyophilized, and stored as the aqueous mushroom extract (AME) [28, 29].

To obtain a spermine enriched extract, the solid residue of the aqueous extraction was treated with 550 mL of 0.1 M HCl for 15 min at room temperature with shaking on an orbital shaker. This extraction process was repeated three times, and all the supernatants were collected together. The pH of the collective extract was adjusted to 7.2 ± 0.1 using NaOH. This solution thus received was subjected to cation-exchange chromatography on Amberlite MAC-3 (H⁺ form) resin. The column was maintained at pH 6.5–7.2, and the constituents bound were eluted using 10 × 30 mL portions of 0.5 M HCl. The spermine-containing fractions were pooled, concentrated in vacuo, and desalted using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) cartridge (Strata™-XL, Phenomenex, USA). The spermine-enriched fraction was finally freeze-dried and stored at − 20 °C for later use in the green synthesis of La-based NCs.

For the identification of phenolic compounds and polyamines present in the gum Arabic and white button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) residues, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) techniques were employed. Samples of dried and sieved (60-mesh) gum and mushroom powders were extracted using 80% methanol in an ultrasonic bath following a maceration protocol, as described for phenolic analysis in plant matrices [30]. The obtained extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 42 paper and stored at 4 °C until analysis. Phenolic compounds were analyzed using an HPLC system equipped with a diode array detector (HPLC–DAD) and a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm). The mobile phase consisted of methanol and 0.1% phosphoric acid in a gradient mode with a flow rate of 1 mL min⁻1, and detection was performed at 280 nm [31]. Identification was achieved by comparing the retention times and UV–visible spectra with those of authentic standards.

For polyamine determination (spermine, spermidine, and putrescine), samples were derivatized with dansyl chloride and analyzed using HPLC equipped with a fluorescence detector (HPLC-FLD) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 340 nm and 510 nm, respectively. The procedure followed the established methods of Seiler and Knödgen [32] with minor modifications. Quantification of phenolic compounds and polyamines was expressed as mg L⁻1 and mg kg⁻1 dry weight, respectively.

Further validation criteria for spermine-enriched polyamine fraction are summarized in Table S1. In summary, the final dried spermine-enriched fraction was isolated by repeated acid extraction, cation-exchange enrichment, acid elution, SPE purification, and freeze drying. Polyamine identification was carried out by retention time comparison with standards following dansyl chloride derivatization through HPLC-FLD analysis. Retention time of spermine, spermidine, putrescine, and cadaverine were 7.20, 6.50, 9.80, and absent, respectively. Calibration curve of spermine was linear over 0.01–2.00 mg L⁻1 with R2 = 0.9993, LOD = 0.004 mg L⁻1, and LOQ = 0.012 mg L⁻1. Hence, the fraction was termed spermine-enriched fraction instead of purified spermine.

Green synthesis of La-based NCs

Lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate (La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O) was used as the lanthanum ion precursor to prepare NCs. Gum Arabic and spermine obtained from white button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) waste were used as natural reducing and stabilizing agents in the green synthesis process. Initially, aqueous solutions of spermine and gum Arabic were separately prepared and mixed with an equal volume (1:1 v/v) of 20 mM La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O solution under vigorous stirring. The resultant mixtures were then subjected to ultrasonic irradiation with an ultrasonic homogenizer (Sonics Vibra-Cell) at constant amplitude and frequency for nucleation and uniform La-based NCs growth.

The reaction mixtures were then subjected to sonication followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 30 min, after which the resulting precipitates were separated carefully. The obtained La-based NCs were washed twice with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted species and impurities. The purified NCs were oven dried at 80 °C for 4 h to obtain fine dry powders. La-based NCs in their dried form were stored in sealed containers for further physicochemical and morphological analysis.

Prior to foliar application, the dried La-based NCs were suspended in distilled water to obtain suspensions at concentrations of 50, 100, 150, and 200 mg L⁻1. Then, each suspension was sonicated in the Sonics Vibra-Cell ultrasonic homogenizer at 40% amplitude and 20 kHz frequency for 15 min in pulsed mode (5 s on/5 s off) just before spraying. The pH of the obtained suspensions was brought to the range of 6.8–7.0. All suspensions were used within 30 min after preparation.

Plant material, seedling preparation, and experimental design

Seeds of Zataria multiflora Boiss (Shirazi thyme) were obtained from the Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Center of Fars Province, Iran (29°37′N, 52°32′E; elevation 1,481 m above sea level). Healthy and uniform seeds were selected and stored in dry conditions at 3–4 °C for two months to break dormancy. Seed viability and germination percentage were assessed before sowing to confirm seed quality. Seeds were sown in March in plastic pots (20 cm height) filled with a substrate consisting of (1:1:1 v/v/v) of leaf mold, perlite, and sand. The pots were maintained under greenhouse conditions (25–28 °C) with regular irrigation and routine horticultural care until seedling establishment.

Uniform seedlings at the 6–8 leaf stage were transplanted into plastic pots containing 5 kg of soil. The experiment was conducted as a factorial arrangement based on a completely randomized design with three biological replicates. The experimental unit was one 5-kg pot. Each treatment combination consisted of three pots, and each pot was considered one biological replicate. One uniform seedling was maintained in each pot after transplanting. Pots were randomly assigned to positions in the greenhouse to satisfy the assumptions of the completely randomized design.Factor one of the experiments was the stress caused by Pb at four concentrations: 0, 250, 500, and 750 mg Pb/kg dry soil. Pb stress was induced by applying the corresponding quantity of Pb(NO₃)₂ solution to the soil and mixing it well prior to transplanting so that the required concentration could be attained uniformly in each pot.The second factor consisted of nine foliar treatments based on La-based NCs: Control, La-Gum50, La-Gum100, La-Gum150, La-Gum200, La-Spm50, La-Spm100, La-Spm150, and La-Spm200. The numerical values indicate the concentration of La-based NCs used for foliar spraying (mg L⁻1). The Control treatment received no La-based NCs and was sprayed with distilled water only. Thus, 36 treatment combinations were established, each replicated three times. Accordingly, the experiment included a total of 108 experimental units/pots (4 Pb levels × 9 foliar treatments × 3 biological replicates).La-based NCsThe foliar application was done in two stages, the first during the beginning of blooming and the second when the plant reached 50% blooming stage using a hand-held sprayer to ensure even foliar cover. All the plants were grown in identical greenhouse environments. At the full flowering stage, plants were harvested and washed with distilled water to remove adhering impurities. The aerial parts were collected for subsequent phytochemical and biochemical analyses. For analyses requiring dry material, samples were placed in paper bags and dried under laboratory conditions at 25 ± 2 °C until constant weight, then ground into fine powder using an electric grinder and stored in airtight containers until further analysis.

Morphological, physiological, and biochemical assessments

Morphological characteristics

Leaf number, leaf area, root length and shoot length, and fresh and dry biomass were measured at the end of the experimental period. Leaf number per plant was counted manually, whereas leaf area was measured using a digital leaf area meter (LI-3100C, LI-COR Biosciences, USA). The plants were carefully dug out from soil, and roots were shaken well to remove soil particles for biomass measurement. Shoot and root sectors were separated and weighed individually to record their fresh weights. The samples were oven-dried for 72 h at 70 °C until they reached constant weight and were weighed using an analytical balance to yield dry biomass. Shoot and root lengths were recorded using a standard laboratory ruler with millimeter precision. Aside from that, inflorescence fresh weights were also determined at flowering stage by subjecting the same to drying. All morphological measurements were conducted under the same laboratory conditions in order to ensure reproducibility and consistency of data.

Physiological characteristics

Leaf discs were used to analyze the membrane stability index (MSI) following the procedure of Petrov et al. [33] with slight modifications. Leaf discs were kept fresh and put into tightly corked test tubes with 20 mL of double-deionized water, and the samples were kept in a water bath at 70 °C for 10 min. The initial electrical conductivity of the solution (C₁) was then measured using a conductivity meter (Model CON700, Eutech Instruments, Singapore). The tubes were then placed in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and the final electrical conductivity (C₂) recorded. The membrane stability index was calculated using the Eq. 1.

Relative water content (RWC) of leaves was measured as per the method standardized by Barrs and Weatherley [34]. Leaf discs were weighed at the time of sampling for the fresh weight (FW), floated in 10 mL distilled water in covered Petri dishes, and refrigerated at 4 °C for 48 h to reach the highest turgidity level. The turgid weight (TW) was taken after removing surface water gently. The samples were dried at 70 °C for 48 h to obtain the dry weight (DW). Biochemical attributes

The process for the extraction of the phytoconstituents involved subjecting the dried plant parts to the maceration process through the use of 80% (v/v) ethanol as the extraction solvent. This involved mixing 1 g of powdered plant material with 100 mL of 80% ethanol, followed by shaking for 24 h using an orbital shaker at room temperature to enable the movement of the soluble constituents into the solvent. The extracts obtained were then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for further analyses [35].

Total anthocyanin content was estimated as per Mazza et al. (2004). Acidified methanol extracts from fresh leaves were kept refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 h and then centrifuged at 10.000 rpm. Clear supernatant was measured at 550 nm, and anthocyanin concentration was calculated considering the molar extinction coefficient of 33.000 cm2 mol⁻1 [36].

Free proline concentration was determined according to the method of Bates et al. (1973). Dried and powdered leaf samples (0.1 g) were shaken in 10 mL 3% sulfosalicylic acid and filtered. To 2 mL filtrate, 2 mL acid ninhydrin reagent (0.25 g ninhydrin in 30 mL glacial acetic acid and 20 mL orthophosphoric acid) and 2 mL glacial acetic acid were added. The suspension was then heated in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 1 h, rapidly cooled with an ice bath, and extracted with 4 mL of toluene [37]. The content of total soluble proteins was calculated according to the Bradford procedure [38] using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a reference. Leaf tissues (0.5 g) were pulverized in liquid nitrogen and extracted in 2 mL Tris buffer at pH 8.0 with 0.05 g PVP, and then centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min. 20 µL aliquot of the extract was added to 5 mL of freshly prepared Bradford reagent (0.01 g Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 in 5 mL ethanol and 10 mL 85% phosphoric acid, diluted to 100 mL with distilled water). Absorbance was measured after 5 min at 595 nm, and protein content was calculated from BSA standard curve.

Oxidative damage and antioxidant enzyme activities

To assess the level of oxidative damage, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in leaves were analyzed. H2O2 content was determined according to Loreto and Velikova [39], whereas MDA concentration was measured following the method of Heath and Packer [40].

Specifically, 0.1 g fresh leaf tissue was homogenized with 1% (w/v) cold trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. Then 500 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 500 µL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h, and absorbance was read at 390 nm in a spectrophotometer. For MDA estimation, 0.2 g of the fresh leaf material was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.1% (w/v) TCA and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min. Then, 1 mL of supernatant was combined with 4 mL of 5% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in TCA, and the mixture was put in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min, rapidly cooled on ice, and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 15 min. Clear supernatant absorbance was measured at 400, 532, and 600 nm (PerkinElmer Lambda 25, USA), and MDA concentration was determined from an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻1 cm⁻1. For isolation of antioxidant enzymes, 0.5 g fresh leaf material was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1.5 mL 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer with pH 7.0 with 2% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), 0.1 mM EDTA, and 2 mM ascorbate. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for enzymatic assays. Superoxide dismutase activity was measured according to the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [41] by inhibition of NBT photoreduction. The reaction mixture of 1 mL comprised 935 µL of 50 mM pH 7.8 phosphate buffer with 0.1 mM EDTA, 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 15 µL of 0.12 mM riboflavin, and 50 µL enzyme extract. The reagent well was kept under dark conditions, while test samples were exposed to fluorescent light (20 W) for 15 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 560 nm. Catalase (CAT) activity was recorded by monitoring the loss of absorbance due to the breakdown of H₂O₂ at 240 nm as per Aebi [42]. The assay buffer was 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 15 mM H₂O₂, and 100 µL enzyme extract. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the breakdown of 1 µmol H₂O₂ min⁻1 mg⁻1 protein with an extinction coefficient of 40 mM⁻1 cm⁻1. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured following Nakano and Asada [43]. Reaction mixture of 1 mL employed 600 µL of 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.0, 100 µM EDTA, 100 µL of 5 mM ascorbate, 100 µL of 1 mM H₂O₂, and 100 µL of enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm was recorded for 30 s. Activity of guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) was estimated by the method of Chance and Maehly [44] with slight modifications. The reaction mixture contained 2.5 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 1 mL of 20 mM guaiacol, 0.1 mL of 40 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance resulting from tetraguaiacol formation was monitored at 470 nm following 1 min, and GPX activity was calculated based on an extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM⁻1 cm⁻1 and expressed in µmol tetraguaiacol formed per min per mg protein.

Element release test from soil

The standard method of neutral ammonium acetate extraction (1 M, pH = 7) was used for assessing availability of mineral elements in soil, according to established protocols commonly adopted in studies dealing with soil fertility [45]. After air-drying and homogenization, 5 g of sieved soil were combined with 50 mL of the extracting solution (soil-to-solution ratio of 1:10) and shaken for 30 min at 180 rpm. The suspension was kept at 25 °C for 24 h; then, it was filtered on Whatman No. 42 filter paper. Afterwards, the clear filtrate was analyzed for K, Ca, Mg, Pb, and La concentrations using Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (AAS) or Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS). Essential oil content and quality analysis

The content of essential oil in the aerial parts of the plant was determined after drying and powdering the samples using a Clevenger-type apparatus hydrodistillation. The content of essential oil was calculated per one plant. For the measurement of essential oil quality, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis was performed according to the procedure of Ilić et al. [46]. Qualitative and quantitative analysis was carried out using GC-FID and GC–MS. For GC–MS analysis, an Agilent 7890 N gas chromatograph equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness) was used. The temperature program in the oven was: initial oven temp 60 °C held for 0 min, then ramped at 3 °C min⁻1 to 280 °C, and held for 20 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL min⁻1. The GC was connected to a Hewlett-Packard 5972 mass selective detector (MSD) operated at 70 eV with mass ranges 40 to 550 m/z. Peaks were identified by matching relative retention indices computed against n-alkanes to literature values [47] and by matching mass spectra to reference libraries like NIST/EPA/NIH NIST2000 and Wiley/NBS Registry of Mass Spectral Data (7th edition, electronic version).

Statistical analysis

The experiment was analyzed as a factorial arrangement based on a completely randomized design with three biological replicates.. Data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the significance of treatment effects.. The comparison of means was made using Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) at the 1% (p ≤ 0.01) and 5% level of probability (p ≤ 0.05). Statistical analysis was conducted with the SPSS software (version 23, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality in the data was confirmed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and data not fulfilling the assumption of normal distribution were square-root transformed before analysis. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).

The Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation heatmap were obtained using R software version 4.3.1 with the following packages: FactoMineR, factoextra, ggplot2, corrplot, and pheatmap. In PCA and correlations, only the biological response variables that were measured were included in the active variables, namely shoot length, root length, number of leaves, leaf area, fresh weight of shoot, root fresh weight, inflorescence fresh weight, dry weight of shoot, dry weight of root, MSI, RWC, anthocyanin, proline, protein, H₂O₂, MDA, SOD, CAT, APX, GPX, Pb, K, Ca, Mg, La, and EO. The treatment variables such as the level of Pb added, La-Gum dosage, and La-Spm dosage were not included in the active PCA and correlation matrices to avoid considering them as biological response traits. Prior to PCA, all biological response variables were standardized using Z-score transformation, that is, they were centered on their mean and standardized by dividing them with their respective standard deviations in order to remove the effect of scale differences. Thus, PCA was done using the correlation matrix generated using standardized variables. PCA and correlation analyses were performed using treatment means. The Pearson correlation values were computed using the same biological response variables except for treatment coding variables.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical characterization of gum arabic and mushroom waste (Agaricus bisporus)

The phytochemical profile of the gum Arabic extract, as presented in Table 1, revealed the presence of several key phenolic compounds with distinct bioactive functions. Among these, gallic acid showing a retention time of 3.2 min and a concentration of 0.132 mg/L was the most abundant. This compound is recognized as a strong antioxidant and reducing agent capable of contributing to the reduction of trivalent metal ions such as La3⁺. Chlorogenic acid (7.8 min, 0.045 mg/L) acts as a metal chelating compound with the ability to bind and stabilize metal ions. Caffeic acid, with a concentration of 0.088 mg/L, also plays an important role in green reduction processes, and its presence alongside other phenolics enhances the overall antioxidant capacity of the extract. Catechin (12.3 min, 0.059 mg/L), a member of the flavanol group, contributes significantly to free radical scavenging and the stabilization of NCs. p-Coumaric acid (15.9 min, 0.021 mg/L) and ferulic acid (17.4 min, 0.016 mg/L) possess phenolic and methoxy groups that enable them to establish chelating interactions with metal ions, and in addition to their antioxidant roles, they can act as capping agents during the synthesis of La-based NCs. Compounds such as vanillic acid, quercetin, and ellagic acid were not detected in the analyzed sample, likely due to their low concentrations or differences in botanical source and extraction conditions. Overall, the simultaneous presence of reducing and chelating phenolic compounds in gum Arabic highlights its strong potential as a natural and biocompatible agent for the green synthesis of La-based NCs.

In the polyamine fraction, spermidine (6.5 min, 0.312 mg/kg dry weight) and spermine (7.2 min, 0.286 mg/kg dry weight) were identified as the dominant components. Characterization of La-based NCs

UV–visible spectroscopic analysis

UV–Visible absorption spectrum of the La-based NCs prepared using gum Arabic (La-gum NCs) was measured in the wavelength range 190–890 nm. As evident from Fig. 1a, the spectrum showed a well-defined and strong peak of absorption at UV with maximum at around 215 nm. Smaller shoulders at 265 nm, 290 nm, and 337 nm were also seen. Above 300 nm, the absorbance intensity significantly dropped and was close to a constant value (~ 0.1–0.2) between the visible (400–800 nm) range. There were no visible region absorption bands. The above results guarantee that the UV absorbance is strong and the visible region optical transparency is high for the La-based NCs synthesized.

Representative UV–Vis absorption spectrum of La-based NCs synthesized using (a) gum Arabic and (b) spermine over the wavelength range of 190–800 nm

The UV–Visible absorption spectrum of La-based NCs synthesized using spermine (La-spm NCs) was recorded in the wavelength region 190–890 nm (Fig. 1b). The spectrum indicated a strong and sharp absorption peak at about 224 nm, with a smooth decrease in the absorbance at higher wavelengths. The absorbance values decreased steadily above 300 nm and tended towards constant values (< 0.15) in the entire visible range (400–800 nm). Compared to the sample synthesized using gum Arabic (λmax = 215 nm), the NCs synthesized with spermine exhibited a slight red shift of about 9 nm. For both NCs, there was strong absorption in the UV region and very weak absorption in the visible region.

The UV–Vis absorption spectra of La-gum and La-spm NCs contained evident optical characteristics, as a sign of the effect of the stabilizing agents on the electronic structure and optical properties of the nanomaterials. La-gum NCs exhibited a well-defined absorption at about 215 nm, characteristic of π → π* and n → π* electronic transitions. High UV absorption is attributed to the presence of functional groups such as -OH and -COOH in gum Arabic that are involved in interactions with La3⁺ ions, enhancing the formation of NCs with higher colloidal stability and optical features [48]. On the other hand, the La-spm NCs exhibited a red-shifted absorption band at around 224 nm. Spermine is a polyamine with over one amino group and therefore may be complexing with La3⁺ ions and hence exhibiting the shift. The shift is a transformation of electronic surroundings and new energy bands due to the interaction of spermine and La3⁺ ions [49]. The two NCs possessed low absorption in the visible region (400–800 nm), which indicates wide band gaps and likely transparency to the visible spectrum. Such optical transparency can be useful for application in UV protection, photocatalysis, and optoelectronic devices where low visible range absorption is desirable [50]. The red shift observed in La-spm NCS compared to La-gum NCs ones could be due to difference in coordination environment surrounding La3⁺ ions and the resultant electronic structure. Such shifts are usually observed in metal–ligand complexes and could affect the optical properties of nanomaterials [48, 51]. Briefly, stabilization agent choice has a deep impact on La-based NCs optical properties. Gum Arabic provides a natural biocompatible surface that enhances UV absorption and colloidal stability, while spermine introduces amino groups that change the electronic structure and produce a red shift in the absorption spectrum. Recent research strongly supports current evidence regarding band-edge and red-shift in UV–Vis spectra of La-gum and La-spm NCs through the influence of the stabilizing agent type and resulting coordination environment on electronic and optical properties of La-based NCs [23, 48, 51, 52].

DLS and zeta analysis

The hydrodynamic size distribution of La-gum NCs and La-spm NCs was evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using the SBL intensity distribution algorithm (Fig. 2a, b). The intensity-based DLS profiles showed one main size population for both samples, although the two stabilizing agents resulted in different distribution patterns and polydispersity values. For La-gum NCs, the SBL intensity distribution showed a main peak with a mode diameter of 716.84 nm and a mean diameter of 706.20 nm. The Z-average diameter was 865.19 nm, and the PDI value was 0.713, indicating a broad hydrodynamic size distribution and considerable colloidal polydispersity (Fig. 2a). For La-spm NCs, the main peak appeared at a mode diameter of 684.45 nm, with a mean diameter of 686.78 nm. The Z-average diameter was 723.82 nm, and the PDI value was 0.249, indicating a narrower size distribution and better colloidal uniformity than La-gum NCs (Fig. 2b). Overall, the DLS results indicated that both La-gum NCs and La-spm NCs existed in suspension mainly as nanoscale colloidal aggregates rather than as fully dispersed primary particles.

Representative intensity-based DLS size distribution of La-based NCs obtained using the SBL analysis algorithm: (a) La-Gum NCs and (b) La-Spm NCs; representative zeta potential distribution of La-based NCs: (c) La-Gum NCs and (d) La-Spm NCs. Measurements were performed in three technical replicates per sample

The zeta potential of La-gum NCs and La-spm NCs was determined for the evaluation of their surface charge properties and colloidal stability (Fig. 2c, d). The average zeta potential of La-gum NCs was found to be + 47.22 mV (Fig. 2c), whereas the average zeta potential of La-spm NCs was somewhat greater, + 48.51 mV (Fig. 2d). The rather high absolute values of zeta potential of both nanosuspensions imply the presence of adequate surface charge on the nanocrystals, which was expected to cause electrostatic repulsion between the particles in the dispersion medium. The slight difference between the values of zeta potential for both types of nanosuspensions implies similar surface charge properties of the two stabilizers.

These hydrodynamic sizes are in agreement with the aggregation of biopolymer coated nanocrystals into solvated clusters rather than discrete primary particles in aqueous solution [53–55]. This phenomenon has been observed in other plant and biopolymer based NCs wherein the diameters acquired from DLS are generally higher than the core diameter because of the hydrated layer and association of the particles [56]. In this regard, the current La-gum and La-spm NCs can be considered to be in the normal size range of stable colloidal nanomaterials [57], although the La-spm NCs showed a more compact and homogeneous distribution, as reflected by its lower PDI.

The observed significantly positive values of zeta potential obtained for both the NCs are consistent with the findings related to the cationic stabilization of nanomaterials where the surface charge helps in providing electrostatic repulsion and slowing down the process of aggregation [58, 59]. As compared to selected previously reported based on green synthesis or capped by natural polymer, it is evident that the current formulations exhibit relatively high surface charge [60, 61]. Notably, the gum Arabic NCs was more heterogeneous, likely reflecting the intrinsically complex and polydisperse nature of gum Arabic [62], whereas spermine-based stabilization produced a narrower distribution, suggesting a more uniform surface coating [63]. Overall, the present results suggest that both NCs had sufficient colloidal charge to remain dispersed during preparation and foliar application, with La-spm NCs exhibiting the more favorable balance between size uniformity and surface charge.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectra of La-based NCs synthesized using gum Arabic (Fig. 3a) and spermine (Fig. 3b) reveal clear vibrational characteristics that ensure the successful stabilization and formation of La-based NCs by the different functional groups of the organic capping agents. For the La–gum NCs, the spectrum revealed typical absorption peaks corresponding to both the polysaccharide stabilizer and the La–O framework. A low-frequency band at 616.75 cm⁻1 was allocated to the La–O lattice vibrations, which provided unequivocal evidence for the formation of La–O/La–OH oxide phases. The strong band at 1044.06 cm⁻1 was attributed to C–O and C–O–C stretching vibrations of glycosidic linkages in gum Arabic, which confirmed the presence of a polysaccharide coating. The band at 1425.68 cm⁻1 was attributed to CH₂ bending and/or symmetric COO⁻ stretching, showing carboxylate and alkoxy groups with the potential for chelating La3⁺ ions. The absorption at 1616.61 cm⁻1 overlapped H–O–H bending (adsorbed water) and C = O stretching of carbonyl groups, showing hydrogen bonding and chelation between gum hydroxyl/carbonyl groups and La centers. A maximum at 2928.59 cm⁻1 was indicative of C–H stretching vibrations of organic groups, and a wide O–H stretching band (3200–3600 cm⁻1) ensured the existence of hydroxyl groups. Together, these features demonstrate that gum Arabic was well adsorbed on the surface of the NCs as a capping and stabilizing agent by its –COOH and –OH groups, and La–O vibration confirms the formation of the inorganic La-based phase, qualitatively verifying gum Arabic assisted synthesis and stabilization of the NCs.

Representative FTIR spectrum of La-based NCs synthesized based on (a) gum Aabic and (b) spermine

In the La–spm NCs, the FTIR spectrum showed a series of typical organometallic and amine-associated bands, and evidence for coordination of spermine with La3⁺ ions was provided. There were several low-frequency peaks at 450.67, 527, 614.97, 632.23, and 748.73–808.23 cm⁻1, which refer to La–O/La–OH lattice modes and La–N vibrations, typical of metal ligand coordination between La3⁺ centers and the spermine amino groups. 853.17, 907.50, 935.42, and 976.65 cm⁻1 peaks were attributed to NH₂ and CH₂ wagging/rocking motions and C–N stretching of the polyamine backbone. The region 1018.26–1266.61 cm⁻1 was characteristic of C–N stretching and aliphatic chain skeletal vibrations, normally slightly shifted upon metal coordination. Bands at 1323.16, 1355.18, 1404.70, 1463.56, and 1488.54 cm⁻1 were attributed to CH₂ bending and NH deformation modes. The relatively strong maximum at 1535.05 cm⁻1 and that at 1632.74 cm⁻1 are related to N–H bending, which coincides with H–O–H bending of adsorbed water, and can be explained as reflecting hydrogen bonding and protonation of amino groups to some extent. A weak band around 2209.72 cm⁻1 could be caused by amine overtone or combination bands of amine vibrations. The band at 2922.69 cm⁻1 was assigned to C–H stretching of spermine chains, whereas no distinct carbonyl band around 1700 cm⁻1 indicated negligible oxidation of the organic moiety. Overall, these findings confirm that spermine acted as a coordinating and stabilizing ligand to develop an amine-rich organic shell enveloping the the La-based inorganic phase through La–N coordination and hydrogen bonding to yield stable NCs..

The appearance of a distinctive La–O lattice mode at ~ 616–620 cm⁻1 in the gum-stabilized sample conforms to observations of lanthanum oxide/hydroxide phase development for La-based NCs, where La–O modes are generally seen in the range of 500–650 cm⁻1 [64, 65]. The strong C–O/C–O–C band observed at ~ 1044 cm⁻1 in the gum-stabilized spectrum corroborates earlier reported FTIR characteristics of gum Arabic and gum-capped NCs, both of which exhibit typical polysaccharide backbone bands between 1000–1100 cm⁻1; this supports assignment to a polysaccharide surface coating [66, 67]. Characteristically occurring bands of carboxylate/alkoxy functionality (≈1426 cm⁻1) and broad hydroxyl features (3200–3600 cm⁻1) were recognized to be markers of surface groups present in the gum derived that are known to bind metal centers in other biopolymer-stabilized NCs systems, thereby corroborating the proposed La-gum interfacial interactions [52, 66].

In spermine stabilized sample, low-frequency band sequence (≈450–808 cm⁻1) and shifts and intensity changes in NH₂/C–N related modes (≈850–980 cm⁻1 and 1018–1266 cm⁻1) are attributed to metal–amine coordination characteristic of polyamine functionalized NCs; the same spectral features are seen for spermine/spermidine functionalization of metal and silica systems in which coordination and hydrogen-bonding alter amine vibration patterns [63, 68]. The existence of recorded N–H bending bands (~ 1535 and ~ 1633 cm⁻1) and the lack of presence of a strong carbonyl band near 1700 cm⁻1 ascertain the preservation of the organic ligand (spermine) in the form of unchanged or oxidized modification a feature typical to other green syntheses where polyamine shells are not significantly oxidized upon coordination with metal containing cores [63]. Comparative spectral analysis identifies complementary stabilization chemistries: gum Arabic shows intense polysaccharide bands (C–O, O–H, COO⁻) typical of carbohydrate capping and spermine exhibits more distinct amine-related features (C–N, NH₂ deformations and La–N indicators). These FTIR pattern differences are in line with previous observations that selection of biogenic/capping agent dictates surface functionality and coordination mode on lanthanide and transition metal oxide NCs [48, 64, 66].

SEM and TEM analysis

The morphologies of La-based NCs that were synthesized using gum Arabic and spermine as stabilizing and capping agents were examined by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 4. The SEM image of the La–gum NCs (Fig. 4a) showed irregular and heterogeneous morphologies, including elongated, occasionally porous, thin sheet-like, and densely aggregated structures. Representative feature of approximately 165.1–291.7 nm were observed, suggesting non-uniform growth.. This heterogeneity is likely due to the role of gum Arabic as a natural polymeric stabilizer that regulates nucleation and growth kinetics upon synthesis, leading to the formation of NCs with complex surface topography and rugged texture. The rough and irregular surfaces of the micrograph further exhibit the material's heterogeneity, which could impact its physicochemical reactivity.

RepresentativeSEM and TEM images of the La-based NCs synthesized using (a, c) gum Arabic and (b, d) spermine

Contrarily, the SEM image of La-spm NCs (Fig. 4b) exhibited bulk like and agglomerated structures, showing both nanoscale features and larger agglomerates. The sizes measured ranged from nanoscale features (~ 99.9 nm and 238.6 nm) to microscale agglomerates (approximately 1.028 μm and 2.012 μm). This morphology suggests the formation of early nanosized nuclei followed by successive aggregation into larger microscale structures. Spermine, as a polyamine with multiple –NH₂ functional groups, likely functioned as a chelating and coordinating agent during the initial stages of synthesis, controlling the nucleation. However, during the growth afterwards, resulting in the coexistence of nano and micro sized domains. The relatively smooth surfaces and sharp edges observed in some regions indicate some crystallinity as well as oriented growth of the NCs.

Comparatively, the La–gum NCs were primarily in the nanoscale regime and showed better homogeneity and dispersion, whereas the La–spm NCs samples showed higher clustering and size dispersion. This is because the two capping agents have different stabilizing modes: gum Arabic, through its polysaccharide network and steric hindrance, performed well in managing particle growth and aggregation, while spermine, as a cationic molecule and with strong metal ligand coordination, triggered fast nucleation followed by agglomeration. Overall, these results indicate that gum Arabic forms finer, more uniformly distributed, and better stabilized La-based NCs compared to spermine, which forms composite nano structures with higher aggregation.

Moreover, TEM was used to study the internal structure and nanostructure of La-gum NCs, as shown in Fig. 4c. From the TEM image, it is clear that there are small, dark contrast zones that correspond to La-based NCs dispersed in the matrix. These particles have almost spherical shapes with nano-scale sizes, smaller than 50 nm based on the scale bar. Since these particles are less dense and aggregated, it implies that gum Arabic plays an effective role as a stabilizer and capping agent for the formation of La-based NCs. Because of the high content of hydroxyl and carboxyl groups present in the polysaccharide gum Arabic structure, it can act as a sterically stabilized and surface-passivated material.

From the TEM image of the La-spm NCs (Fig. 4d), it is evident that the morphology of La-spm NCs exhibits a suggests where there is a high probability for the aggregation of particles compared to the gum Arabic NCs. Well-defined dark spheres are observed in the form of large clusters, mainly embedded in an organic environment. Even though each NCs is within the range of nanometers, there is a high probability that these NCs can cluster into clusters due to their molecular nature. Spermine has been shown to be a linker between La-based nuclei through electrostatic interaction and coordination chemistry.

The SEM and TEM images indicatehow the character of the stabilizing agent profoundly influences the morphology, aggregation habit, and growth mode of La-based NCs. The contrasts between the morphologies of La–gum NCs and La–spm NCs agree with the structural and chemical profiles of capping biomolecules used for synthesis. Other studies have also shown that biopolymers such as gum Arabic or plant polysaccharides can be able to modulate nucleation and restrict particle growth by steric stabilization and hydrogen bonding [69, 70]. The relatively smaller and more homogenous NCs obtained with gum Arabic in this work are thus supported by other studies in which gum Arabic effectively prevented agglomeration and yielded well dispersed rare earth oxide NCs [71, 72].

By contrast, the spermine-mediated synthesis yielded NCs of mixed nanoscale and agglomerated morphology. This discovery aligns with previous discoveries that indicated polyamines, such as spermine and spermidine, could be multidentate ligands that engage strongly with powerful electrostatic and coordination with metal ions, which facilitate rapid nucleation but also facilitate secondary agglomeration [73, 74]. These larger microscale clusters here occur by this mechanism and suggest that while spermine aids early stabilization through coordination, its great binding ability might cause cross-linking between juxtaposed nuclei and lead to agglomeration. This explains the difference between the small primary particle size (< 50 nm) observed under TEM and the larger microscale aggregates detected in the SEM micrographs..

In addition, the morphological heterogeneity of La–spm NCs agrees with previous findings where La-based NCsof hybrid nano/micro-sized aggregates were synthesized using polyamine-directed synthesis and possessed relatively smooth surfaces and moderate crystallinity [75]. This suggests that spermine is able to induce partial ordering of the surface of the nanostructure, maybe through directional coordination of –NH₂ groups with La3⁺ centers, affecting crystal growth dynamics [76].

Overall, the comparative morphological behaviors in this work are substantiated adequately by existing literature. Gum Arabic-based synthesis would have better chances of achieving small, stable, and well-dispersed NCs due to steric and surface passivation effects of the polysaccharides [72, 77, 78], whereas spermine systems possess greater aggregative tendency and size distribution because of their polycationic coordination behavior [79]. These results collectively support the premise that biomolecular characteristics of the stabilizing agent are of utmost significance in regulating the growth, size, and dispersion of La-based NCs.

XRD analysis

The crystalline structure and phase-related features of the synthesized La-gum NCs and La-gum NCs showed several diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 12.28°, 13.00°, 15.68°, 16.84°, 18.28°, and 19.12° (Fig. 5a). Comparison with the standard ICDD database indicated that these reflections were mainly consistent with La oxide/hydroxide-related crystalline phases, including La2O3 (ICDD card No. 05–0602) and La(OH)3 (ICDD card No. 36–1481). These results support the formation of crystalline La-containing oxide/hydroxide phases during the gum Arabic-assisted synthesis. The relatively intense diffraction peak at 2θ = 15.68° suggests the presence of preferentially oriented crystalline domains, which may be associated with characteristic planes of the La-containing phase. The broadening of the diffraction peaks indicates the nanocrystalline nature of the sample and may also reflect structural disorder and the contribution of amorphous organic components originating from the gum Arabic matrix. Using the Scherrer equation and considering the diffraction peak at 2θ = 15.68° with an approximate FWHM of 0.2°, the average crystallite size of La-gum NCs was estimated to be about 40 nm. The XRD pattern of La-spm NCs also showed crystalline La-containing phases (Fig. 5b). The main diffraction peaks appeared at 2θ values of 14.6°, 19.12°, 28.92°, 29.04°, and 30.12°. These reflections showed agreement with the standard patterns of La2O3 and La(OH)3, suggesting the coexistence of La oxide- and hydroxide-related phases in the spermine-mediated nanostructure. The strong peak at 2θ = 19.12° suggests a dominant crystalline orientation, which may be attributed to preferential growth of La-containing crystalline domains. The peaks observed in the 28–30° region may be assigned to hydroxide-related contributions, suggesting partial hydroxylation during synthesis. The broad diffraction peaks, with FWHM values in the range of approximately 0.3–0.5°, further support the nanocrystalline nature of the La-spm NCs. Based on the Scherrer equation using the peak at 2θ = 19.12° with an approximate FWHM of 0.4°, the average crystallite size of La-spm NCs was estimated to be approximately 25–30 nm. Minor peaks at 32.52° and 34.48° may be related to trace secondary phases, residual impurities, or additional hydroxide-related crystalline contributions. Overall, the XRD results support the presence of nanocrystalline La oxide/hydroxide-related phases in both La-gum NCs and La-spm NCs. The estimated crystallite size was approximately 40 nm for La-gum NCs and 25–30 nm for La-spm NCs, indicating that the type of stabilizing/capping agent influenced the crystallite growth of the La-based NCs.

Representative XRD patterns of La-based NCs synthesized using (a) gum Arabic and (b) spermine

Diffraction patterns of La-gum and La-spm NCs indicate that both NCs have developed a hybrid crystal-amorphous structure which is a typical trait of green synthesis when the organic stabilizing agents (gum Arabic and spermine) surround the inorganic core [80]. The coexistence of La2O3 and La(OH)3 phases is attributed to the high oxophilic nature of La; in aqueous synthesis environments, the surface of lanthanum oxide NCs undergoes rapid hydration to form a more stable hydroxide layer [81, 82].

A critical observation is the influence of the stabilizing agent on the final crystallite size. The smaller crystallite size of La-spm NCs (25–30 nm) compared to La-gum NCs (40 nm) suggests that spermine, as a low-molecular-weight polyamine with high cationic charge density, provides more effective capping of the crystal nuclei, thereby restricting further grain growth during the precipitation process [83, 84]. In contrast, the bulky and highly branched structure of gum Arabic, while providing excellent colloidal stability as evidenced by the zeta potential results, may permit the formation of larger crystalline domains. This behavior is likely associated with the steric arrangement and lower packing density of the polymer chains around the growing nuclei, resulting in less effective suppression of crystal growth than low-molecular-weight capping agents [85, 86]. Similar size-control mechanisms have been documented in recent green synthesis studies, highlighting that the steric hindrance and functional group density of the capping agent are the primary determinants of the final NCs dimensions [85, 87].

EDS analyses

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used as a semi-quantitative elemental screening method to examine the surface elemental composition of the synthesized samples (Fig. 6). For La-gum NCs (Fig. 6a), carbon was the dominant detected element, accounting for approximately 99.78 wt% and 99.96 at%. This high carbon signal is consistent with the carbon-rich nature of gum Arabic and may also reflect the contribution of the carbon-based support or organic matrix during EDS measurement. A trace amount of Ni was also detected, which may be related to minor contamination, sample preparation, or instrumental contribution.

Representative EDS spectra of La-based NCs synthesized using (a) gum Arabic and (b) spermine as stabilizing agents

For La-spm NCs (Fig. 6b), carbon and nitrogen were the main detected elements. Carbon accounted for approximately 56.75 wt% and 60.48 at%, whereas nitrogen accounted for approximately 43.23 wt% and 39.51 at%. The presence of a strong nitrogen signal is consistent with the polyamine structure of spermine and suggests the contribution of spermine-derived organic components in the synthesized material.

La was not detected in the EDS spectra of either sample, which primarily reflects the organic-rich surface composition of the synthesized materials. This is attributed to the thick organic shell formed by gum Arabic (polysaccharide matrix) and spermine (amine-rich coating) capping agents surrounding the inorganic cores [88, 89]. Since EDS is a surface-sensitive technique with a limited sampling depth, the heavy organic matrix coverage leads to severe attenuation or masking of the X-ray signals originating from the underlying La atoms. Consequently, while EDS confirms the successful incorporation and dominant presence of the organic stabilizers on the NCs’ surface, it cannot provide direct elemental confirmation of La. The presence of lanthanum is instead robustly confirmed by the XRD patterns, which clearly demonstrate the presence of crystalline La-containing phases.For the La-Gum NCs, the minor Ni signal is more likely attributable to trace contamination arising from sample preparation, the specimen holder, or instrumental background, rather than representing an intrinsic component of the synthesized NCs [90]. Similar limitations of EDS in detecting rare or buried mineral domains have been observed in other biopolymer-assisted NCs, where the organic coating can mask the underlying mineral phase [91].

For the La-spm NCs, the simultaneous detection of carbon and nitrogen is consistent with the polyamine nature of spermine and supports the presence of a nitrogen-containing organic shell on the surface of the material [92]. In this respect, the EDS profile is chemically consistent with the synthesis strategy, as spermine is expected to act as both carbon-rich and nitrogen-rich features [93]. However, the absence of detectable La in either sample means that EDS cannot be used here as direct evidence of La incorporation. This does not invalidate the synthesis results, but it does highlight the limitation of EDS for these samples, particularly if La is present at low concentration, heterogeneously distributed, or protected by an organic matrix. Since EDS probes only a limited interaction volume and the X-ray signals from underlying phases can be attenuated by overlying organic coatings, buried inorganic domains may not be readily detected [94, 95], this method is best considered as supportive rather than confirmatory in this case.

Under Pb₅₀₀, control plants had a sharp decline to 11.52 cm2 (44.2% decrease), but La-Gum₁₀₀ and La-Spm₁₀₀ recovered the leaf area to 18.92 cm2 (64.3% increase) and 17.56 cm2 (52.4% increase), respectively. At Pb₇₅₀, control was on the lowest leaf area (8.79 cm2), while La-Gum₁₀₀ and La-Spm₁₀₀ increased leaf area to 15.8 cm2 (79.8% increase) and 13.64 cm2 (55.2% increase), respectively. For Pb levels, on average, La-Gum treatments were 5.9% larger in leaf area than La-Spm treatments.

Shoot growth was similarly severely hindered by Pb stress but greatly boosted by La-based NCs (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7c). Under Pb₀, the highest shoots were recorded by La-Gum₁₀₀ (52.51 cm), (20.3% increase). La-Gum₅₀ and La-Spm₁₀₀ registered shoot lengths of 47.89 cm and 48.24 cm, (9.7–10.5% increase, respectively). Under Pb₅₀₀, shoot length in control plants fell to 26.18 cm (40% reduction), whereas La-Gum₁₀₀ increased shoot length to 36.85 cm (40.8% rise) and La-Spm₁₀₀ to 33.28 cm (27.1% increase). At Pb₇₅₀, shoot length was severely repressed (20.14 cm in control), whereas La-Gum₁₀₀ recorded 29.46 cm (46.3% increase) and La-Spm₁₀₀ 24.17 cm (20% rise). Across treatments, mean shoot length was higher under La-Gum than under La-Spm NCs.

Root elongation exhibited similar patterns of response to Pb and NCs treatment (Fig. 7d). Pb stress significantly reduced root length, whereas La-Gum₁₀₀ improved this trait (p < 0.01). At Pb₀, La-Gum₁₀₀ developed roots to a length of 19.49 cm, a 25.4% increase relative to the control value of 15.54 cm. La-Gum₅₀ and La-Spm₁₀₀ developed roots 10.9% and 13.1% longer, respectively. Under Pb₂₅₀, root length was reduced to 9.68 cm, or 37.7% below, in the control, but rose to 15.8 cm (63.2% above the control) under La-Gum₁₀₀ and to 13.74 cm (41.9% above) under La-Spm₁₀₀. Under Pb₅₀₀, root length dropped to 6.51 cm in the control (or 58% below the initial length) but rose to 12.72 cm (94.4% above the control) under La-Gum₁₀₀ and to 8.64 cm (32.7% above the control) under La-Spm₁₀₀. Under elevated stress (Pb₇₅₀), root elongation was only 5.1 cm in the control, whereas La-Gum₁₀₀ and La-Spm₁₀₀ increased it to 9.87 cm (93.5% increase) and 8.1 cm (58.8% increase), respectively. Average root elongations were 12.3 cm for La-Gum and 10.7 cm for La-Spm, indicating that gum-stabilized NCs performed 13.4% better.

The present study showed that Pb contamination significantly reduced the vegetative growth of Z. multiflora, as reflected by decreases in leaf number, leaf area, shoot length, and root length with increasing Pb concentration. These inhibitory effects are consistent with the known phytotoxicity of Pb reported in diverse plant species, where Pb has been associated with impaired nutrient uptake, cell division, photosynthetic performance, and membrane integrity [96, 97].

Notably, foliar application of La-based NCs mitigated the negative effects of Pb on the measured morphological traits. Among the tested treatments, the gum Arabic-stabilized formulation at 100 ppm (La-Gum₁₀₀) produced the most consistent improvement in morphological traits. This response pattern is in line with reports that La-based NCs can improve seedling growth or reduce stress-related growth inhibition in some plant species, possibly through effects on physiological and antioxidant processes [18, 48, 98]. For instance, Hanif et al. reported the growth-enhancing effects of biogenic La₂O₃ NCs on citrus, supporting the concept that properly formulated and dosed La-based nanomaterials can enhance plant performance [64].

The greater effectiveness of La-Gum NCs compared with La-Spm NCs may be related to differences in particle stabilization and plant-surface interactions. First, gum Arabic is a high-molecular-weight polysaccharide that acts as an effective coating and steric stabilizing agent. It tends to produce stable colloidal NCs with sustained properties and reduced aggregation, features that may enhance bioavailability and support more favorable interactions with plant tissues [99]. Recent research on gum-stabilized lanthanide NCs supports enhanced nano-bio compatibilities and controlled surface chemistry when gum arabic is used as a capping agent [48]. Second, gum-based coatings can mediate interactions with root/shoot surfaces and the leaf cuticle differently than small polyamine ligands, promoting gradual ion release and sustained beneficial signaling rather than rapid, transient exposure [100]. Together, these two effects plausibly explain the consistent 5–15% morphological advantage observed for La-Gum across treatments in our study.

Spermine-functionalized particles (La-Spm NCs) also improved growth compared to the untreated lead stress control, albeit to a lesser extent than La-Gum NCs. This result is consistent with previous studies indicating that polyamines (including spermine) can enhance stress tolerance by modulating antioxidant systems and stabilizing membranes [101–103]; however, their effectiveness as nanoparticle ligands depends on the resulting particle stability, surface charge, and propensity for aggregation. Spermine-coated iron NCs have been shown to increase surface charge and improve plant-related responses in other systems, indicating spermine's potential to enhance nanoparticle-plant interactions while introducing variability related to aggregation and dosing [63].

Comparison with studies on thyme and related Lamiaceae species provides contextual support for our findings. Nano-formulations of thyme extracts and other nanobiotechnological interventions have been reported to improve stress tolerance (e.g., under salinity), antioxidant status, and the stability of secondary metabolites results that align with our observation of NCs preserving leaf area and organ growth under heavy metal exposure [104, 105]. For example, a recent study showed that Zataria multiflora nano-extracts enhanced salinity stress tolerance in basil by improving antioxidant capacity and morphological preservation, suggesting that nano-based approaches can consistently sustain morphology and biochemical resilience in this genus [106].

At the physiological level, the morphological protection conferred by La-based NCs in our experiments likely reflects several interactive mechanisms: (1) enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and reduced oxidative damage, (2) improved nutrient uptake and membrane stability, and (3) altered plant responses to Pb exposure, potentially through modulation of metal uptake and translocation pathways. Reviews and mechanistic studies on NCs under abiotic stress emphasize these pathways as recurrent modes through which NCs alleviate metal and other stresses [96, 107]. Green-synthesized metal oxide NCs have been shown to enhance abiotic stress tolerance through similar physiological and enzymatic adjustments, supporting the role of nano-interventions in restoring plant growth under adverse conditions [108].

The high negative impact of increased soil Pb on shoot and root biomass in this study is in agreement with many reports of Pb mainly inhibiting the activity of the root meristem, nutrient and water uptake, and photosynthetic performance, thus ultimately depressing whole-plant growth [9, 109]. The pattern we saw of sharp inhibition of roots at moderate to high Pb and a proportionally more severe reduction of aboveground biomass follows the typical phenotypic responses reported for Pb toxicity across herbaceous species and confirms that root damage is an early and dominant cause of biomass loss under Pb stress [110–112]. Biomass partial recovery due to La-based NCs (La-Gum and La-Spm), especially at the 100-ppm dose, agrees with several works discussing growth promoting effects of rare earth elements and La in particular [113]. According to Yin et al. [114] and Luo et al. [115], low doses of La can stimulate nutrient uptake, root activity, and dry matter accumulation, whereas excessive La turns out to be inhibitory an inverted-U dose response that agrees with the peak at 100 ppm and decline at higher concentrations in our data. Thus, our results support the idea of an optimum La-NC concentration which promotes metabolic activity without introducing La-derived stress.

There are several mechanistic routes that can explain the mitigation of Pb damage by La-NCs in the present experiments. Nanomaterials may interact with heavy metals in the rhizosphere, potentially affecting Pb availability to roots [116]. Similar rhizosphere-level interactions have been reported for Fe, Si, and magnetite NCs [117–119] and may help explain the biomass recovery observed after La-based NCs treatment in our study. Second, NPs and NCs often improve antioxidant capacity and nutrient balance in plants, enabling them to tolerate normally toxic metal concentrations [14, 120]. Third, the positive impact of La-NCs on the root traits (most especially at 100 ppm) indicates improved root functioning that is, higher water and nutrient uptake which ultimately underpins improvements in aboveground growth. This agrees with the results obtained by Luo [115], and other works where treatment with La resulted in increased APase activity, root fine-structure, and nutrient acquisition, which further translated into higher biomass and reproductive organ development under non-stress to moderate-stress conditions [121–123]. The stronger response of shoot fresh weight mean squares in our ANOVA most likely reflects this downstream amplification of subtle, root-level improvements into larger aboveground biomass gains [124]. Central Comparing La-Gum and La-Spm, while both NC types improved growth, the slightly superior and more consistent performance of La-Gum100 in several traits may be due to differences in NC stability, release kinetics of La3⁺, and interactions with soil organic matter. In the case of NCs, for example, polymer or biopolymer stabilized REE NPs, appears that the carrier matrix and surface chemistry strongly alter the persistence and bioavailability of NPs; more stable carriers may produce a gradual, sustained release, benefiting plants under chronic stress [125, 126].

These results show that Pb stress negatively affected the measured physiological and biochemical traitsof Zataria multiflora, such as MSI, RWC, and metabolic attributes including anthocyanin, proline, and total protein. In the literature, heavy metals induce oxidative stress, leading to peroxidation of lipids, damaging cell membranes and resulting in loss of physiological functions [127]. For example, Si and Fe NCs alleviated Pb stress in rice by enhancing antioxidative systems and reducing Pb uptake [116]. Application of La-based NCs, especially at 100 ppm, partially attenuated Pb-induced changes in MSI and RWC, even under high Pb concentration. This work agrees with that previously carried out on Solanum nigrum by Guo et al. (2023) in which foliar or hydroponic La (III) enhanced biomass, promoted endocytosis, and increased tolerance to Pb and Cd by strengthening the antioxidant defense system [122, 128]. Such an increase in anthocyanin and proline under Pb stress is probably a classic defensive response anthocyanins acting as non-enzymatic antioxidants and proline contributing to osmotic adjustment and macromolecule protection [129, 130]. However, the moderated accumulation of these metabolites in La-treated plants suggests that La-based NCs may have reduced stress-related metabolic responses. This is in line with the broader role of NCs in stress alleviation; the authors referred to the different ways NCs can reduce heavy metal bioavailability, boost antioxidant defenses, and stabilize membranes to mitigate such stress, for example, by regulating transport genes or scavenging reactive oxygen species [14]. The decrease in total protein content under Pb, and its partial recovery under La-based NC treatments, further suggests a protective effect against Pb-induced protein loss [121]. Pb toxicity has been shown to down regulate the synthesis of proteins and increase the activity of protease through oxidative damage; hence, La-mediated effects may contribute to better preservation of protein content, possibly through improved membrane stability and reduced oxidative stress [121, 131]. However, while La treatment looks highly beneficial, there is a need to exercise caution: low-dose La has been shown to induce endocytosis in plant cells, potentially increasing the intake of not just La but also toxic metals such as Pb and disturbing elemental homeostasis [132]. This duality suggests that even though La can mitigate some of the effects of Pb stress, its application has to be carefully optimized, and long term risk assessments are in order for example, accumulation in edible tissues. Overall, our findings suggest that La-based NCs, particularly La–Gum₁₀₀, may help mitigate selected physiological and biochemical effects of Pb stress in Zataria multiflora.

At 250 ppm Pb, La–Gum100 lowered MDA from 0.89 to 0.54 µmol g⁻1 FW (39.3% reduction), whereas La–Spm100 reduced it to 0.63 µmol g⁻1 FW (29.2%). Similar trends occurred at 500 ppm Pb, where La–Gum100 and La–Spm100 decreased MDA by 35% and 25.6%, respectively. At 750 ppm, La–Gum100 reduced MDA by 38.3% and La–Spm100 by 25.2%. These results indicate that La-based NCs reduced MDA accumulation, suggesting lower lipid peroxidation under Pb stress.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity increased significantly in response to Pb stress (Fig. 10c). In untreated plants, SOD rose from 25.61 U mg⁻1 protein at Pb0 to 64.09 U mg⁻1 protein at 750 ppm (≈150% increase), indicating enhanced ROS detoxification. La-based NCs further stimulated SOD activity, with La–Gum100 showing the strongest effect. At 500 ppm Pb, La–Gum100 increased SOD from 51.22 to 65.10 U mg⁻1 protein (~ 27% rise), while at 750 ppm it increased SOD by ~ 29.5%. La–Spm100 also enhanced SOD, though less effectively, showing 13% increases at 500 and 750 ppm Pb. The highest SOD activity occurred in plants treated with La–Gum₁₀₀ at 750 ppm Pb, indicating that this treatment enhanced SOD activity under severe Pb stress.

Catalase (CAT) activity exhibited a similar pattern (Fig. 10d). CAT increased from 21.97 to 43.94 U mg⁻1 protein as Pb rose to 500 ppm, but declined at 750 ppm (35.15 U mg⁻1 protein), indicating inhibition under extreme stress. La–Gum100 consistently enhanced CAT activity across Pb levels. In Pb free plants, La–Gum100 increased CAT by ~ 27%. At 500 ppm Pb, CAT increased from 43.94 to 55.93 U mg⁻1 protein (~ 27.4%), and at 750 ppm by ~ 30% compared to the untreated plants. Activity decreased at nanoparticle concentrations > 150 ppm, suggesting inhibitory effects at high doses. La–Spm100 also enhanced CAT but less markedly, with 15% and 16% increases at 500 and 750 ppm Pb, respectively.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity rose sharply with increasing Pb levels (Fig. 10e), from 23.98 U mg⁻1 protein at Pb0 to 59.95 U mg⁻1 protein at 750 ppm (~ 150% increase). La–Gum100 significantly promoted APX activity at all Pb concentrations, increasing activity by 25–26% at 500 and 750 ppm Pb. La–Spm100 also enhanced APX but to a lesser extent (16–17% increases). Maximum APX activity occurred at 750 ppm Pb with La–Gum100.

Guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) activity followed the same trend (Fig. 10f). GPX increased from 1.23 to 3.08 U mg⁻1 protein (~ 150% rise) with increasing Pb. La–Gum100 enhanced GPX by ~ 23% at 500 ppm and ~ 26% at 750 ppm Pb. La–Spm100 caused smaller increases (~ 16%). Activity decreased at NCs concentrations above 150 ppm, suggesting a possible dose-dependent inhibitory effect.

The Pb-induced increases in H₂O₂ and MDA, together with changes in SOD, CAT, APX, and GPX activities, are consistent with previous reports on heavy metal stress and nanoparticle-mediated stress alleviation [133–135]. Pb and other heavy metals are known to induce ROS overproduction, lipid peroxidation and the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes as a primary defense, which is widely observed across species. These findings agree with the large increases in H₂O₂ and MDA and the induction of SOD/CAT/APX/GPX that we measured [133]. Indeed, other parallel studies using metal and metal-oxide NPs, such as Fe-, Si-, and CaO-NPs, have reported similar mitigation patterns, that is, reduced H₂O₂/MDA and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities [116, 136], which support the interpretation that some engineered NCs may reduce Pb-induced oxidative responses. The comparison in performance between this two La formulations is in line with the literature stressing the role of particle surface chemistry and capping agents for bioactivity and biocompatibility: indeed, gum-Arabic or other biopolymer cappings frequently enhance the colloidal stability and decrease phytotoxicity while enhancing the biological efficacy, which could well be the reason for the higher protection provided by La-Gum in the present study [23, 48]. For example, spermine coatings, similar to La–Spm in the present study, have been used to enhance nanoparticle plant interactions and stress tolerance (e.g., spermine-coated Fe NPs) but may exhibit variable efficacy with different metal cores and plant species [63, 137], consistent with our observation that La–Spm was beneficial but less effective than La–Gum.

Mechanistically, the observed increase in H₂O₂/MDA under Pb stress and their partial reduction by La-based NCs may be related to mechanisms reported for NPs, including changes in metal availability, antioxidant responses, and membrane stability [138–140].

These protective effects are consistent with the enhanced biocompatibility and antioxidant potential reported for biosynthesized nanomaterials, which are considered promising alternatives to conventionally synthesized NCs.

[141, 142]. Similar reductions in oxidative markers and increases in antioxidant enzyme activities have also been reported in plants treated with appropriately formulated NCs [116, 136]. In summary, the weight of current experimental and review literature [64, 113, 143] supports our interpretation that green synthesized La-based NCs when properly formulated and dosed can attenuate Pb induced oxidative stress in herbs like Zataria multiflora through lowering ROS and lipid peroxidation and enhancing SOD/CAT/APX/GPX activities.

Soil nutrient availability, metal partitioning, and essential oil

Analysis of variance (Table 7) indicated that Pb, La-based NC treatments, and their interaction significantly affected soil nutrient availability and essential oil yield (p ≤ 0.01). Among the soil-related indices, available Pb exhibited the highest mean square, indicating that it was the most sensitive parameter to the applied treatments and showed very high variability in response to different Pb levels and La-based NCs. Regarding essential oil yield, the main effect of Pb with a mean square value of 1.854 was significant at the 1% level, demonstrating that Pb stress markedly affected essential oil production and accumulation in Zataria multiflora. In addition, the effects of La-based NCs treatments and the Pb × T interaction were also significant at the 1% level, highlighting the effective role of La-based NCs in moderating the adverse impacts of Pb stress on secondary metabolism and essential oil biosynthesis. This is particularly important because even small changes in essential oil yield were accurately detected, thereby increasing the reliability of the results. The significance of the interaction effect for all traits, including essential oil yield, indicates that the plant response to La-based NCs treatments was dependent on the level of Pb contamination. Notably, the significant relationships between rhizosphere soil indices and essential oil yield suggest an association between nutrient/Pb availability around the root zone and plant metabolic performance; however, these relationships should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct soil-modifying effect of foliar-applied La-based NCs. Overall, these findings suggest that foliar-applied La-based NCs improved plant tolerance to Pb stress and were associated with more favorable rhizosphere nutrient and Pb availability, probably through plant-mediated systemic responses rather than direct modification of bulk soil chemistry.

Foliar application of La-based NCs, particularly at the optimal concentration of 100 ppm (La–Gum100), was associated with improved rhizosphere nutrient availability and better plant nutritional status under Pb stress. Under the highest Pb stress level (750 ppm), La–Gum100 increased available potassium to 152.4 mg kg⁻1, representing a 54.1% improvement compared with the corresponding control. Similarly, available calcium and magnesium increased by 34.1% and 56.6%, respectively. From a detoxification perspective, La-Gum100 was associated with a reduction in DTPA-extractable/rhizosphere-available Pb from 330.8 to 121.5 mg kg⁻1, corresponding to a 63.3% decrease relative to the Pb-stressed control. A comparable reduction in measured available Pb was also observed at moderate stress levels, where La-Gum100 lowered this value by 64.1% at 500 ppm Pb.

Comparison between the two types of NCs clearly demonstrated the superior performance of La–Gum NCs over La–Spm NCs across all Pb levels. At 500 ppm Pb, La–Gum100 was 32.7% more effective than La–Spm100 in reducing available Pb. In terms of La release dynamics, La–Gum NCs exhibited higher and more controlled La availability, releasing 24.8 mg kg⁻1 under non stress conditions compared with 18.5 mg kg⁻1 in La–Spm100 (34.1% higher). This release advantage remained under severe Pb stress (750 ppm), where available La in La–Gum100 was approximately 40% higher than in the corresponding La–Spm treatment.

The results of essential oil yield analysis in Zataria multiflora demonstrated that lead (Pb) stress had a significant effect on essential oil production (Table 7). As shown in Fig. 11, in the untreated control, increasing Pb concentration from 0 to 750 ppm reduced essential oil yield from 3.10% to 1.75%, corresponding to a 43.5% decrease under severe Pb stress. This pronounced reduction is attributed to Pb induced disruption of secondary metabolism and inhibition of aromatic compound biosynthesis under oxidative stress conditions. Application of La-based NCs, particularly the La-Gum100 treatment, markedly alleviated this reduction. Under non stress conditions (Pb0), La-Gum100 increased essential oil yield to 3.67%, representing an 18.4% improvement compared with the corresponding control. Under severe Pb stress (750 ppm), La-Gum100 increased essential oil yield to 2.60%, which was 48.6% higher than the untreated control at the same Pb level. These results suggest that La-based NCs helped maintain essential oil yield under Pb stress. Comparison between the two types of NCs showed that La-Gum NCs were consistently more effective than La-Spm NCs across all Pb levels. For instance, at 500 ppm Pb, essential oil yield under La-Gum100 was approximately 20.8% higher than under La-Spm100. Furthermore, the optimal concentration of La-based NCs was identified as 100 ppm, while higher concentrations (150 and 200 ppm) exhibited reduced effectiveness, likely due to inhibitory or phytotoxic effects at elevated nanoparticle doses.

Chemical compositions of Zataria multiflora essential oil affected by different levels of lead stress and La-based NCs treatments

At 500 ppm Pb, the negative effects of Pb were more pronounced, with thymol in the control treatment decreasing to 15.50% and linalool to 12.35%. Under this stress level, La-Gum100 played a crucial role in maintaining essential oil components, raising thymol to 20.15% and linalool to 14.42%. Additionally, carvacrol content, which declined from 36.50% to 30.85% in the control, was partially preserved by La-Gum100.

Under the highest Pb stress (750 ppm), the greatest reduction in valuable essential oil components was recorded, with thymol dropping to 12.75% and linalool to 10.45%. Nonetheless, La-Gum100 maintained its efficacy, elevating thymol to 16.45% and linalool to 12.28%, while carvacrol decreased from 39.15% to 33.55%.

Monoterpene hydrocarbons displayed a similar trend; for instance, α-pinene decreased to 2.65%, 2.25%, and 1.85% at 250, 500, and 750 ppm Pb, respectively, but La-Gum100 improved these values to 3.25%, 2.78%, and 2.38%. Oxygenated compounds such as 1,8-cineole also declined from 1.28% under control conditions to 0.58% at 750 ppm Pb, yet La-Gum100 increased it to 1.12%.

Pb contamination has an extremely well documented negative effect on soil nutrient availability and downstream plant secondary metabolism, and this results corroborate the general pattern of high Pb levels that strongly reduce available K, Ca, and Mg in soil, depressing the essential oil yield in Zataria multiflora [144–146]. This finding is in agreement with the wide literature about the toxicity of Pb: for instance, a comprehensive review indicated that Pb interferes with nutrient uptake, water relations, causes oxidative damage, and disrupts metabolic pathways, and impairs growth and yield in many plant species [8, 147]. Importantly, this finding that application of La-based NCs especially La–Gum at 100 ppm ameliorates these deleterious effects aligns with emerging evidence that carefully designed soil amendments or bio management strategies can support aromatic or medicinal plants in contaminated soils [148]. For instance, a recent phytomanagement study has demonstrated that aromatic species grown on metal polluted soils, combined with soil conditioners or microbial inoculants, maintained acceptable essential oil yields while immobilizing heavy metals in soil, reducing their bioavailability and limiting translocation into plant tissues [149, 150]. Similarly, earlier pot studies on mint species under Pb (and Cr) stress showed a reduction in essential oil yield and modifications in oil composition [151–153]. In those experiments, Pb exposure inhibited root growth, disturbed nutrient uptake, and altered enzymatic activity, finally reducing EO content. These findings resemble the decrease in yield of EO and concentrations of its components which this study observed under Pb stress in Z. multiflora.

However, there is also evidence to the contrary: in a study on a non typical aromatic grass, heavy metal contamination (Cd, Pb) actually stimulated essential oil yield, which would suggest that under low to moderate heavy metal stress, some species may up regulate secondary metabolism as an adaptive response [150].

Because the La-based NCs were applied by foliar spraying, the observed changes in rhizosphere nutrient availability and extractable Pb should not be interpreted as evidence of direct modification of soil chemistry by the NCs. Instead, these responses are more likely associated with plant-mediated mechanisms. Improved plant physiological performance and root activity under La-Gum100 treatment may have altered rhizosphere processes, including root exudation, nutrient uptake dynamics, and Pb partitioning, thereby influencing the concentrations of extractable nutrients and Pb measured in the rhizosphere soil.From a practical point of view, these findings support the possibility to valorise, through NCs amendments or phytomanagement strategies, the productivity and high-value output (EO) of aromatic plants cultivated on contaminated soils, thus providing an environmental remediation with economic return [154]. This view is shared by recommendations given in recent reviews, which support the use of aromatic plants for phytomanagement due to their low risk of heavy-metal transfer into final products, such as essential oils, along with economic values [155, 156].

Besides nutrient cycling in soil and increased yield of essential oils, partitioning of Pb (Supplementary Table S2) demonstrated that Pb content was always higher in roots than aerial parts, suggesting limited mobility of Pb between root and shoot. In all treatments containing Pb, La-Gum100 decreased Pb content in shoots compared to the respective control treatments, which contain Pb only, without changing high translocation index. On the other hand, accumulation of La in roots and shoots proved its effective absorption, although the level of La in essential oils and after distillation residue was rather low. The latter observation is of particular importance in terms of phytomanagement, since it proves that La-Gum100 not only increases plants' resistance to Pb, but also decreases the contamination of harvested product.In order to clearly establish the threshold for concentration at which there is transition from growth enhancement to growth inhibition, a complementary seed germination and seedling growth test was conducted with a broader range of La-based NCs dose. The test provided evidence of a dose-dependent hormesis effect whereby doses of 50–100 ppm induced seed germination and vigor in seedlings while doses above 100 ppm resulted in decreased root elongation and growth vigor (Table S3). This is significant since it confirms the selected dose of 100 ppm as the optimum dose in the main test and suggests that higher doses might lead to phytotoxicity effects. Together, these findings indicate that La-Gum100 provides the most favorable balance between efficacy and safety among the tested concentrations.

The observed distribution of lead, with higher accumulation in roots compared to shoots, confirms that Z. multiflora uses a root sequestration strategy to protect its photosynthetic apparatus from heavy metal toxicity [9, 157, 158]. The significant reduction in shoot lead content after foliar application of La-Gum100, without a concomitant change in the transport index, suggests that NCs do not necessarily block the transport pathway itself, but rather enhance overall plant vigor or modify root surface sequestration capacity through systemic signaling [159, 160]. Since the NCs were applied foliarly, this distal response is likely mediated by basipetal systemic signaling and induced tolerance mechanisms. In this process, physiological priming initiated by La at the leaf surface triggers systemic signaling cascades that influence root behavior, potentially enhancing cell wall lignification or vacuolar sequestration of Pb [161, 162]. This interpretation avoids the unsustainable claim of direct immobilization of lead in soil by foliar application and instead focuses on the plant's internal physiological flexibility [163, 164]. The dose-dependent response observed in the germination and seedling trials provides clear evidence of hormesis, a phenomenon where low-level stress (50–100 ppm La NCs) stimulates biological performance [163, 164], while higher concentrations (> 100 ppm) become inhibitory [165, 166]. The decline in growth vigor at 150–200 ppm likely stems from lanthanide-induced oxidative stress [162] or the interference of La ions with essential divalent cations (like Ca2+) in cellular signaling and cell wall integrity [161, 167]. This biphasic response justifies the selection of 100 ppm as the optimal sweet spot for maximizing growth and essential oil yield while avoiding phytotoxicity [168].

From a phytomanagement perspective, the negligible levels of La in the essential oils are highly significant. Since essential oils are produced via steam distillation, the low volatility of La-based NCs and their ionic derivatives ensures they remain in the distillation residue (waste) rather than the final aromatic product [150, 169]. These findings suggest that La-Gum100 may represent a promising strategy for improving the cultivation of medicinal plants in Pb-contaminated soils while preserving the quality of the harvested essential oil [170].

Principal componet analysis (PCA) and correlation heatmap

The correlation heatmap (Fig. 12a) showed that shoot length, root length, leaf area, and biomass were positively intercorrelated, reflecting the fact that all these growth parameters increase in parallel in favorable conditions. In contrast, plant Pb content was negatively correlated with most growth-related traits, while K, Ca, and Mg showed positive associations with growth performance. Physiological parameters such as RWC and MSI were positively related to growth, whereas markers of oxidative stress (H₂O₂ and MDA) showed negative associations, further emphasizing the inverse relationship between stress and vigor. Antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX, and GPX) were positively intercorrelated, suggesting a coordinated enzymatic defense response. These relationships were further summarized by PCA (Fig. 12b), in which the first two principal components explained 87.3% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 80.3% and PC2 for 7.0%. The loading pattern indicated that growth- and water-status-related traits were mainly oriented in one direction, whereas oxidative stress markers and Pb accumulation were positioned in the opposite direction. Notably, NCs treatments La-gum and La-spm shifted sample distribution toward an improved physiological status, with La-NCs associating more strongly with morphological resilience and aligning with biochemical defense markers. Together, the PCA and correlation heatmap support an inverse association between growth performance and oxidative stress, while the mitigation effects of La-gum and La-spm treatments should be discussed based on treatment-level statistical analyses.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the significant findings regarding the efficacy of La-based NCs in mitigating Pb stress, several limitations must be addressed in future research. Although direct quantification of Pb and La in plant tissues was performed, the present study did not investigate the precise spatial distribution or subcellular localization of these elements. Characterizing whether these elements are sequestered in the cell wall or compartmentalized within vacuoles—using advanced imaging techniques such as Synchrotron-based X-ray Fluorescence (SR-XRF) or TEM–EDX—would provide a more definitive understanding of the internal detoxification pathways. Furthermore, while the observed changes in antioxidant defense systems and osmolyte accumulation provide strong indirect evidence of stress alleviation, they do not fully elucidate the underlying molecular and genetic triggers. Future investigations incorporating transcriptomic and proteomic profiling are essential to identify the specific gene networks and metal transporters modulated by La-based NCs. Finally, it is important to note that these results were obtained under controlled greenhouse conditions. To ensure the practical applicability of these NCs, long-scale field trials are necessary to evaluate their long-term stability, environmental fate, and potential impacts on soil microbial communities under fluctuating climatic and edaphic conditions.

Conclusion

The present work illustrates how green synthesized of La nanostructures (La-based NCs) can act as an efficient nanoscale intervention to offset the physiological and phytochemical disturbances induced by Pb stress in Zataria multiflora. Using natural gum Arabic and spermine, two unique La-based NCs platforms were generated, both of which influenced plant performance by different types of biochemical and structural interactions. In this respect, the La-based NCs synthesized using gum Arabic showed superior functional impact continuously, implying that the nature of the stabilizer is a key determinant factor for nanoparticle plant compatibility and activity. Altogether, these results emphasize the potential of designed La-based NCs to enhance antioxidant defense, metabolic integrity, and growth with essential oil quality in medicinal plants against heavy metal stress. Beyond their implications on Zataria multiflora, the findings further develop the concept that rationally designed, biocompatible NCsmay be utilized as eco-friendly means to protect crop yield and phytochemical quality in polluted ecosystems.
