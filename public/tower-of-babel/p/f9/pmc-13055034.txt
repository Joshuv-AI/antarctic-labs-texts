“Sometimes it's the bullying”: an exploratory qualitative study of church leaders' perceptions of dietary health of black American children and youth
Authors: Laura O. Howard, Penny A. Ralston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChildhood obesity rates continue to increase, especially for Black Americans. Few church-based health interventions have focused on children and youth. The purpose of this exploratory qualitative study was to determine church leaders' perceptions of the dietary health of children and youth.MethodAs a follow-up study to the Health for Hearts United Dissemination Trial, a focus group was conducted with one of the participating churches in North Florida that had identified child dietary health as a priority. Five adults and three youth (n = 8) participated, guided by four questions. The focus group was recorded, transcribed and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsThree major categories, five themes and six subthemes were identified. The categories included cultural background, motivators and barriers, and potential dietary initiatives. Themes and subthemes included learned behaviour (commitment and habits), family history, motivating factors (role models), challenging factors (parent involvement, bullying, consistency), and interventions (programs and practices).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that improving the health of children within the church context is complex. The participants identified several barriers, but also identified motivating factors and were enthusiastic about possible church programs and practices to improve the dietary health of children.

Childhood obesity in the United States continues to increase, affecting close to 20% of children (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2025). Obesity is a particular problem for Black American children. It is estimated that 24.8% of non-Hispanic Black children are obese compared to 16.6% of non-Hispanic White children (Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2025). Children who are obese are more likely to become adults who are obese, increasing their risk of heart disease, type II diabetes and cancer (Bass & Eneli, 2015; Cote et al., 2013; Kansra et al., 2021; Powell-Wiley et al., 2021). Lifestyle factors, such as diet, can greatly affect the risk of childhood obesity. There is considerable evidence that interventions for children focused on improving dietary health can help combat this growing epidemic, especially school-based and community-based dietary interventions that have been shown to increase the intake of fruits and vegetables in children (See for example Hawkes et al., 2015; Heelan et al., 2015; Ismail et al., 2021; Ruiz et al., 2019; Verdonschot et al., 2023; Wolfenden et al., 2021).

As an example of community-based programs, church-based health interventions have been shown to be effective in changing health behaviours and improving health status of congregants (see for example, the following review articles: Burton et al., 2017; Campbell et al., 2007; DeHaven et al., 2004; Derose et al., 2019; Lancaster et al., 2014; Maroney et al., 2023; Yeary et al., 2012). Further, there are several established faith-based interventions in the literature that highlight efficacy of these programs for Black American adults, including Body & Soul (diet) (Campbell et al., 1999; Resnicow et al., 2001), Faith, Activity and Nutrition (FAN) (diet and physical activity) (Wilcox et al., 2013), WORD (weight management) (Kim et al., 2008), and Health Smart (diet and physical activity) (Tucker et al., 2017), among others.

However, few church-based health intervention studies have been conducted with children. To our knowledge, only one random control trial could be found in the literature. Resnicow et al. (2005) Go Girls was a 6-month weight control intervention (20–26 sessions) for overweight Black American female adolescents (comparing high intensity and moderate intensity groups) that included physical activity and nutrition components and was conducted in 10 churches. There were no significant findings for weight or for secondary outcomes (clinical measures). The authors concluded that the intervention was not effective in reducing adiposity, although there were some positive findings for high attenders. The remaining studies in the literature are primarily feasibility studies, including those that have: (a) integrated health information into church structures (i.e., Sunday School or Vacation Bible School) for Black American or Hispanic children and resulted in increased nutrition knowledge (Davis et al., 2011; Wilmoth et al., 2018) and increased physical activity (Trost et al., 2009); (b) implemented a 12-week church based physical activity intervention with Black adolescent girls (n = 41) that improved attitudes, self-efficacy and intention and improved BMIs and other clinical factors (Thompson et al., 2013); (c) investigated a community-based cross-church blood pressure control intervention for African American adults (n = 10) and children (n = 7) that resulted in significant increases in fruit and vegetable consumption at post-test for adults and trends toward increases in children (Ralston et al., 2016). Finally, Dunn et al. (2020) examined views regarding obesity prevention efforts (healthy eating and physical activity) for children and youth, which was conducted with primarily Caucasian church leaders. Although other studies have been conducted with children in church settings, these have involved other topics such as sexual health (Jemmott et al., 2020; Lightfoot et al., 2014; Magezi et al. 2025; Powell et al., 2017).

Thus, there is a scarcity of church-based health intervention studies involving children that are concerned with health in general and dietary health in particular, especially in Black American churches. Considering the high rates of childhood obesity in Black Americans and the scarcity of diet-related interventions for children and youth in churches, studies are needed that examine perceptions regarding children's dietary health as a baseline for planning interventions. In particular, exploratory qualitative studies can provide foundational information to more effectively determine directions for future research. Further, investigating the construct of dietary health allows for the broadest examination of not only eating behaviours but also social determinants that might affect food preferences, acquisition and consumption. Thus, the purpose of this exploratory qualitative study was to determine church leaders' perceptions of and possible ideas for addressing the dietary health of children and youth. The specific objectives of this study were to: (a) determine church leaders' perceptions of the dietary health of children and youth, and (b) identify church leaders' ideas for improving the dietary health of children and youth. In the study, we explore the application of socio-ecological theory on a post hoc basis in relation to the findings including intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational and community levels (McLeroy et al., 1988).

Methods

Design and setting

This research is a part of a broader effort to reduce cardiovascular risk in Black American adults through developing, testing the effectiveness of, and disseminating the church-based intervention, Health for Hearts United (HHU), using community-based participatory research (CBPR) approaches (Ralston et al., 2022). This section includes a brief description of HHU and the church identified for the study.

Health for hearts united

Health for Hearts United (HHU), developed as a part of the longitudinal trial conducted between 2008 and 2013, is an 18-month intervention developed using CBPR approaches in a two-county area in North Florida with a high prevalence of CVD risk factors and mortality rates (Ralston et al., 2014). The intervention was framed around three conceptual components: awareness building (individual knowledge development), clinical learning (individual and small group educational sessions), and efficacy development (recognition and sustainability) (Ralston et al., 2017). The intervention included three types of programs for the treatment churches: (a) church-initiated, (b) joint church-staff, and (c) staff-directed standard programming. Both the treatment and comparison churches received data collection health promotion (display of generic health materials and healthy snacks). In addition, the intervention included four key messages: eat better, move around more, reduce stress, and take charge of your health (see Ralston et al., 2017 for an extensive description of the project and intervention). The efficacy of HHU was established with significant increases in fruit/vegetable consumption between baseline and 18 months for treatment churches compared to the comparison churches (Ralston et al., 2020). Subsequently, the HHU Dissemination Trial was conducted between 2013 and 2017 using a shorter version of HHU with 30 additional churches, training 168 health leaders who delivered cardiovascular awareness events that reached 586 participants (Ralston et al., 2022). Preliminary findings show that the shortened HHU intervention resulted in health leaders having significant increases in fruit and vegetable consumption between pre- and post-test and outreach participants having significantly increased CVD awareness (Ralston et al., 2022).

Description of church

The church for this follow-up study was located in a medium-sized community in the urban county of the target geographical area in North Florida and participated in the sustainability phase of the HHU Dissemination Trial post-funding. The church is non-denominational with an approximate membership of 250, including about 50 children. The church designated Black American children's health as a priority following successful completion of the HHU Dissemination Trial in 2019. This was in keeping with a constant theme of “reaching youth” that the investigators heard from church health leaders (Ralston et al., 2022). Considering the church's expressed interest, the investigators approached the pastor in 2020 about conducting a focus group with church leaders to further address the dietary health of children and youth to assist the church in possible planning. Thus, this focus group, which was conducted in March 2020 immediately before the COVID-19 pandemic shutdown, served as the basis for an exploratory study to determine whether church leaders might identify themes of interest that HHU could address in a larger study to develop and determine the effectiveness of a church-based dietary intervention for children and youth. Although only one focus group was the basis for this study, there is increasing evidence that smaller, exploratory studies with depth of qualitative data, compared to seeking “saturation,” can yield important new knowledge (see, for example, Guest et al., 2017; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Vasileiou et al., 2018). The study was approved by the Florida State University Institutional Review Board (STUDY00000409).

Participants

The participants (n = 8) were selected based on their leadership in areas related to health and youth development in the church. The inclusion criteria included serving as a pastor, health leader, youth leader or being a youth in the church. The exclusion criteria included those not having these backgrounds. The participants, all Black American, included five adults (aged 29–49) and three youth members of preadolescent age (aged 12–14), representing the senior pastor and associate pastor (n = 2), health leaders (n = 2), adult youth leader (n = 1), and youth (n = 3). Although youth did not meet the criterion of a “leader”, their participation was intentional to make certain youth voices were included in the discussion since the goal was to determine perceptions of the dietary health of children and youth. All of the adults were female except for the pastor. The youth participants included two females and one male and were the children of adult participants. Among the adults, three were married, and two were single/never married. Three had bachelor's degrees, one had a master's degree, and one had some college. All were employed full-time. All participants provided written informed consent. Parents provided consent for their participation. Parental consent was secured from the youth's parents, and the youth provided assent.

Focus group questions and data collection

Four questions were developed by the lead investigator for the focus group (see Table I) and reviewed by the principal investigator. The first question, asked by the principal investigator, was: “We know you were involved in Healthy Hearts and you may have been involved in other church-based health programs. In general, do you feel that church-based health programs have been impactful to the church? If so, in what ways?” The purpose was to link the focus group to the broader health initiative that occurred during Healthy Hearts. The next three questions, led by the lead investigator were: “What are your overall perceptions of the dietary health of the children in your church?” “What are some ideas that you have to improve the dietary health of the children in your church?” “What ideas do you have that could be done at this church to improve the dietary health of children?” Probes were included to ensure that both youth and adult participants would be engaged (e.g., the probes for the perceptions of dietary health were: availability of food, selection of food and eating habits). There was an easy flow of responses from the questions and no apparent hesitation in the conversation. The focus group lasted for one hour and was audio recorded with additional notes taken by the principal investigator.

Facilitation strategies focused primarily on the flow of questions and understanding any possible dynamics among those present. Since the pastor was a part of the focus group, we watched for power dynamics through identifying possible agreements with his comments or quietness among participants to not speak out. These were not noted in the meeting nor in the transcriptions, perhaps due to the pastor's style of empowering church members. In addition, we watched for any power dynamics between the parents and their children. Again, these were not noted during the focus group nor in the transcriptions. The children, for example, shared freely their experiences in school which is shown in the quotes later in the paper. Thus, it appeared that participants were speaking freely and were not constrained by relationships within the focus group.

Data analysis

The focus group recording was transcribed by the lead investigator and cross-checked by the principal investigator for completeness and accuracy. The transcriptions were coded line by line to identify various concepts using qualitative thematic analysis (Bryman, 2016; Potter, 2009). The initial codes were guided by the focus group questions. Emerging concepts were identified from the coded data and grouped together into categories. These categories were then grouped into themes and subthemes, placed on an Excel spreadsheet and colour coded. As a separate part of the analysis, the principal investigator independently reviewed the transcriptions without the codes and then coded selected data (Potter, 2009). In a subsequent meeting, the lead investigator and principal investigator compared their coding to determine inter-rater reliability. In two subsequent meetings, the data were triangulated and any disagreements were resolved to reach consensus on the themes.

Results

The analysis of the focus group data yielded three major categories on church leaders' perceived dietary health of the children and youth: cultural background, motivators and barriers and potential dietary initiatives (see Table II). Themes and subthemes included learned behaviour (commitment and habits), family history, motivating factors (role models), challenging factors (parent involvement, bullying, consistency) and interventions (programs and practices). Cultural background

Cultural background refers to the past experiences of the participants, which may be contributing factors to their attitudes toward the dietary health of children and youth. Learned behaviour and family history were the main themes that emerged. Within learned behaviour, the subtheme of commitment and habits emerged.

Learned behaviour

The participants indicated that learned behaviour was derived from impediments both within the church as well as at home. One barrier was commitment, which referred to dedication by church members to perform a certain behaviour, as it is related to dietary health within the church. The participants expressed concern that there would not be full commitment from church members and that the workload would fall on a few members. One of the adult participants commented, “We have work to do, and it's not one or two people's job.” There was an acknowledgment by the pastor that the health ministry would have his full commitment, “It's exciting to have a health ministry. It's especially exciting to have it from a pastoral standpoint to be fully committed to it.”

In addition, habits or repeated behaviours learned at home and in church environments also contributed to learned behaviour. The participants commented on the interplay of habits formed at home and at church. One adult participant expressed this way: “There are habits that we try to form, certain things at church that are changed when they get home and that's a realistic challenge.” The convenience of fast food was also seen as a contributor to unhealthy dietary habits. For example, one adult participant commented, “But even with eating habits, I mean it's just easy to leave here tonight and go to a fast food pay window somewhere and pull in and you convince yourself just one time.” Another adult comment was “And so it's… I'm not naïve to the challenge. It is great, and we are battling against everything that's made easy. Whether it's fast food whatever, everything is made easier.” Several participants remarked on how the habits that are formed at home and within the church can have a lasting impact on overall dietary health.

Family history

Family history was defined as the medical history of family relatives. Repeated comments were made by the participants about the impact of their family history on their current health. Multiple participants mentioned that family members had chronic medical conditions, such as diabetes and high blood pressure. One adult participant stated, “Diabetes and high blood pressure [are] prevalent on both sides of my family.” Another adult participant stated, “My parents, both my parents now battle with high blood pressure and with diabetes, [with] my dad's side dating back generations.” They also voiced concern that this family history increased their own risk of developing chronic medical problems. For example, one of the adult participants expressed how her mother's health issues resulted in becoming more conscious of her own health. She stated, “I'm big on health because of my mother... had cardiovascular disease.”

Motivators and barriers

Motivators were defined as factors that have a motivational effect on the dietary health of the children and youth at the church. Barriers were defined as challenges that prevented changes in dietary health. The two subthemes that emerged were motivating factors and challenging factors.

Motivating factors

Motivating factors included one subtheme: role models.

Role models. Role models were mentioned repeatedly, and the respondents referred to role models present in both the home and the church. There was agreement that churches had an influence on behaviours, particularly dietary habits. For example, an adult participant remarked, “I can't say anything that happened to me in high school that I used for my upbringing. Everything, every single thing I learned about living, about maturing, came out of the church.” Another adult participant commented, “I do think about the patterns. My wife and I are setting an example [with what] we put in front of them, and it's a major charge.”

Challenging factors

Challenging factors included three subthemes: parent involvement, bullying, and consistency.

Parent involvement. Parent involvement was seen as a barrier that could affect the success of church programs or initiatives. The participants expressed the need not only to have the children involved, but also to have parents fully engaged with potential initiatives. This concern was highlighted by this comment from an adult, “Hopefully we can get the parents to buy in…” Another participant stated, “I know the hardest, the challenging part is starting with parents. It's getting the parents involved. Getting them excited about it, which will help motivate the kids.” The participants also mused about the hopeful results of the engagement. One adult stated, “…I think doing this hopefully will be an eye-opener for a lot of us as parents to really get involved and think about the choices that we're giving our kids…about food that they eat.” Yet, there were cautionary notes as well as stated by this adult participant: “But the other side to the parents is the sensitivity about it. How do we go about saying certain things without hurting their feelings [by] exposing them?”

Bullying. The bullying and insecurity of the children were topics that were repeatedly brought up by the participants. There were concerns about the children being bullied due to being overweight and being targeted because of the food they had in their school lunches. A youth participant stated “... sometimes it's the bullying that makes a person not want to do what's right for them...” One of the youth participants posed these questions to the focus group, “If you came to school with fresh vegetables, fresh fruit things of that nature. What would your peers say to you? Like would you be comfortable doing that? Would you be comfortable being singled out so to speak because that's typically what's going to happen? If they look different than the rest of the children.” There were also concerns that the bullying was leading to a poor self-image and insecurities, as suggested by this quote from an adult participant: “So it is a concern because you start noticing how kids walk around with their heads down because they're so ashamed” and “You see a child who will not look at you eye to eye they are just so ashamed, whether they're being told that at home or at school.”

Consistency. Consistency refers to the role that the church plays in the dietary health of children and youth. The participants were concerned that not having a consistent message at home and church would hamper the success of any dietary intervention. This included the food that was available at church events, such as desserts and fried foods. The participants recognized that the multiple influences existed including home and school environment that could have an effect on their dietary habits. The following statements from the adult participants reflect this concern: “So for us, for me from a pastoral standpoint, it's being consistent [about] what the charge is and why that's important. It relates to a lot of things that we learned... in church, and you know churches do a lot of functions with food involved. And usually it is going to be fried something...”

Potential dietary initiatives

The potential dietary initiatives referred to ideas that the participants had to improve the dietary health of children and youth in the church. The subtheme that emerged from the data was programs and practices.

Programs and practices

The participants had several ideas for programming initiatives for their church. Multiple participants mentioned making the programs engaging for the children. For example, an adult participant stated, “They are not going to do really well with a lecture series, but I think engaging them in it, involving them in that and understanding that there's a history behind you know, why we eat what we eat.” Another adult commented, “So making it fun for them making it where like the cooking class allowing them to cook certain things and teaching them, of course that reading the labels things of that nature where it's their involvement [and] their input as well.” Additionally, it was suggested that the program use visuals as “shock factor” educational tools for dietary health. An adult participant commented, “I think also … there's a need for shock factor, I mean just the reality of I'm talking about shock factor with visuals.”

To assess the need for the direction of programming, a practice suggestion was made by multiple participants to develop an anonymous survey to assess the needs of the children, youth and parents, including any challenges. Adult participants commented, “I think the survey would help because you stop missing the mark. You now are being directed based on your responses. You kind of know. Okay, here's what some of the needs are” and “I think we should start off with an anonymous survey on the students, on the children as well as the parents.” It was clear during the focus group that the participants wanted to ensure that they were addressing the specific needs of their church without any judgment. Thus, best practices for program development were clearly being considered.

Discussion

This study sought to determine church leaders' perceptions of and possible ideas for addressing the dietary health of children and youth. The findings from this focus group showed that developing a church-based intervention focused on child dietary health involves many factors. Additionally, the focus group revealed that improving dietary health is complex and requires engagement from both the children and their families. A key finding of this study is that the participants identified several barriers, such as parent involvement, bullying and consistency, and cultural background, such as habits, that could prevent the creation of a successful dietary intervention to address childhood obesity. This suggests that the participants not only saw the church as a setting for creating initiatives but also recognized the challenges that exist. For example, they realized that there could be a lack of consistency between what could be learned in church dietary programs and behaviours at home (i.e., habits) or in the school environment (i.e., bullying). This was evident in previous studies, which highlighted the hesitation by church members to incorporate sexual education into a health promotion program because this information would be perceived at home (Lightfoot et al., 2014; Powell et al., 2017). Further, Trost et al. (2009) study that tested the feasibility of Shining Like Stars physical activity (PA) intervention at Sunday School in four churches in Kansas (205 parent/child dyads), found that only 39% of parents implemented at least one PA activity at home with their children. They recommended developing a parallel parental support intervention to accompany Shining Like Stars. The Thompson et al. (2013) study to change attitudes of Black adolescent girls about PA made similar recommendations about including other family members in the intervention. Our findings, along with those in the literature, suggest that church-based programs to address the dietary health of children and youth will need to be family-based programs that encourage and motivate parents as well as other family members.

One barrier related to the school environment that was particularly surprising was bullying in that youth bringing healthy food in their lunches would be singled out by other youth. The literature on bullying generally focuses on the dietary health of the bully perpetrator (i.e., the person who is being the bully) (El-Sahar & Sopeah, 2019; Jackson & Vaughn, 2018; Jackson et al., 2017; Jackson, 2017; Kircholl & Sanchez, 2019; Okada et al., 2024a, 2024b). Yet studies of bully victims as a result of their food choices appear to be rare. One study of two middle schools found that bullying was identified as a barrier for students to be physically active but not for eating nutritious meals in school (Bauer et al., 2004). Our findings on bullying suggest that when developing church-based programs with children and youth, psychosocial aspects need to be considered so that youth develop strategies for addressing situations where their desired healthy lifestyle may be ridiculed or made intimidating by others. This may be why the literature on church-based dietary programs involving children is limited in that as minors children have diverse environments influencing them, which may limit intervention effectiveness.

Although the participants noted these potential barriers, the data suggest that they were also optimistic. They realized the history of diseases in their families, were motivated by the role models present in both the home and the church and were enthusiastic about possible programs and practices to improve the dietary health of children in their church. In sum, they were optimistic about the church environment for promoting health. These results are supported by the Dunn et al. (2020) qualitative study of church leaders' views of obesity in that two major themes identified included (a) concerns about health issues facing children in their congregation, and (b) community and existing church structures that can play a role in health behaviours. Other literature discusses how churches provide the social network and support crucial for the success of interventions to change behaviour (Davis et al., 2011; Lightfoot et al., 2014).

With regard to theory, the socio-ecological model, including intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational and community levels, can be applied to the results of this study. The subthemes of commitment and habits can be viewed as intrapersonal or individual factors since they are learned behaviours that can affect dietary choices. The interpersonal level is particularly relevant for this study. The subthemes of role models, parental involvement and bullying were discussed throughout the focus group by multiple participants, who acknowledged the influence that social groups can have on behaviour. Focusing on the church as a setting for health promotion is an important aspect at the organizational level. Finally, with regard to the community level, the results of this study and follow-up intervention development may have implications for other churches within the broader study, given cross-church relationships (Ralston et al., 2022). In sum, the socio-ecological model is supported as a framework for this study.

Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and limitations. A strength includes selecting a church that previously had been involved in the broader study to reduce CVD risk using CBPR approaches. This previous relationship contributed to the participants being very engaged during the focus group. Their familiarity with church-based health promotion from the experience with the broader project allowed for a very robust discussion about childhood dietary health. Further, the project team's willingness to reach out to request the focus group may enhance health promotion sustainability in this church, again a key principle in community-based approaches. Ultimately, this church's previous involvement in the broader project may be an advantage in creating new programs and/or practices to improve the dietary health of children and youth.

With regard to limitations, this study involved only one focus group with a small representation from the church. The opinions voiced during the focus group may differ from the rest of the church members. Additionally, the participants were selected by the pastor of the church, which may have introduced bias in the results. And having the pastor as a participant in the focus group may have prevented some of the participants from being fully forthcoming with their answers, especially the youth. However, it was interesting that it was the youth who freely offered comments on bullying in reference to school lunch. Finally, it may be difficult to generalize the results of this focus group to other churches and communities since the study was limited to only members from one church in a particular community. Clearly, the needs of this church may differ from churches that may have a different demographic makeup.

Implications for practice

This research provides implications for practice. First, this study has demonstrated the importance of pastors in church-based health in that the pastor fully cooperated with and participated in the focus group. Second, the participants highlighted getting input from both parents and children before addressing dietary health in churches and the need to get buy-in from both. Third, consistency was emphasized to ensure that what children are being exposed to in dietary health is encouraged in parents' food habits. Fourth, the challenge of bullying suggests the need to consider children's multiple environments in promoting dietary health. Finally, difficulty in bringing about change due to cultural factors was balanced with motivators and potential dietary initiatives that may assist with moving the health program forward.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that addressing the dietary health of children in a church setting will require careful consideration of several factors, including multiple barriers that may ultimately affect their success in programming. Yet this study also suggests that Black American church leaders can draw on their cultural backgrounds, identify motivators and engage each other in tackling these challenges. Future studies that include a broader number of churches and focus group participants will be needed to verify these findings and to work towards the development and evaluation of dietary health interventions for children and youth in the church setting. However, this study has identified several factors related to the dietary health of Black American children and youth and ideas for programs and practices to guide this planning.
