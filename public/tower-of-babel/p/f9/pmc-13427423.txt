IL-1–Activated Inflammatory Cardiac Fibroblasts Exacerbate Acute Coxsackievirus B3 Myocarditis
Authors: David S Elias, Wonyoung Jo, Monica V Talor, Camille Jaime, Abdel Daoud, Ina Sulkaj, Martin Květoň, Nadan Wang, Ondřej Fabián, Vojtěch Melenovský, Daniela Čiháková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey words: IL-1, inflammatory fibroblasts, plasticity, viral myocarditis

One-third of the patients with biopsy-confirmed myocarditis have a poor prognosis, with a 10-year mortality rate of 50%.1, 2, 3 In the acute setting, patients with myocarditis can present with ventricular arrythmias, hemodynamic instability, or cardiogenic shock.4 These patients have a mortality rate of 12% to 15% after 5 years.5 Current therapeutic strategies for acute myocarditis center on the management of myocarditis sequelae such as arrhythmia management, heart failure treatment, and general immunosuppression for specific etiologies of disease.4,6, 7, 8, 9, 10 No therapeutic options that address the drivers of disease are available because of a limited understanding of the mechanisms that perpetuate cardiac inflammation. Viral infections are the most common causes of myocarditis, and cardiotropic enteroviruses such as coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) account for 14% of myocarditis cases.11,12 CVB3 myocarditis modeling in mice is a useful tool to dissect the cellular processes that promote inflammation.

Cardiac fibroblasts are largely derived from the epicardium and endocardium and canonically maintain extracellular matrix (ECM) integrity and orchestrate fibrosis following cardiomyocyte injury.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Growing evidence now positions fibroblasts as central regulators of immune homeostasis and inflammation.18, 19, 20 Cardiac fibroblasts that influence immune responses through the production of inflammatory mediators are termed inflammatory fibroblasts (IFs). Through single-cell RNA sequencing, we now understand that IFs constitute a transcriptionally unique subset of all cardiac fibroblasts.21, 22, 23 In terms of their activation, innate sensing pathways such as toll-like receptor agonists and cytokines such as IL-1β, TNFα, IL-6, IFNγ, IL-33, IL-17A, and IL-13 have all been shown to activate the IF phenotype.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31

We have previously demonstrated that IL-17A signaling to cardiac fibroblasts induces production of chemokines and cytokines such as CCL2, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), CXCL1, and IL-6.24,25,32, 33, 34 These cytokines play important roles in recruiting proinflammatory neutrophils and monocytes during cardiac disease.21,24,25 Studies have shown that reducing the influx of neutrophils or proinflammatory Ly6Chi monocytes into the heart during CVB3 myocarditis alleviates acute myocarditis.24,35,36 Using a genetic approach, we observed that ablating cardiac fibroblast–specific IL-17A signaling protected hearts from developing post–myocardial infarction cardiac dysfunction, demonstrating that the inflammatory functions of fibroblasts can have major implications on disease outcomes in the heart.30 Unlike experimental autoimmune myocarditis (EAM), which is IL-17A driven, the contributions of IFs to the pathogenesis of non–T helper 17 (Th17) inflammatory diseases such as viral myocarditis are unclear.34,37 The presence and function of IFs in CVB3 myocarditis have not been described, and their influence on acute myocarditis severity and immune cell recruitment remains unknown.

In this study, we sought to define the contributions of IFs to CVB3 myocarditis disease pathogenesis. In CCL2-mCherry reporter mice, we demonstrated that IFs are activated early during CVB3 myocarditis. Using bulk RNA sequencing studies, we revealed that IFs enrich for expression of Th1-, Th2-, and Th17-related cytokines while downregulating fibrosis-related pathways. We also found that IL-1β induced inflammatory fibroblast activation in primary murine cardiac fibroblast cultures. Furthermore, IL-1β–activated IFs induced Th1 or Th2 expression programs in response to the addition of cytokines such as IL-27 and IL-13, respectively, while maintaining their original IL-1β–induced expression program. Importantly, fibroblast-specific deletion of IL1r1, the primary receptor for IL-1β and IL-1α, protected mice against severe acute myocarditis. Finally, we identified IFs in human myocarditis biopsies and published single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, highlighting their translational relevance. In human disease, we could histologically identify CCL2+vimentin+ and eotaxin+vimentin+ fibroblasts both in lymphocytic and eosinophilic myocarditis. Therefore, we experimentally demonstrate that IFs significantly contributed to CVB3 myocarditis pathogenesis and are a potential target for therapeutic development.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type C57Bl/6 mice (JAX 000664), C57BL/6-Tg(Pdgfra-cre)1Clc/J (JAX013148), B6.129S7-Il1r1tm1Imx/J (JAX 003245), B6.Cg-Ccl2tm1.1Pame/J (JAX 016849) were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. PDGFRacre mice were bred to IL1r1fl/fl mice to generate PDGFRacreIL1r1fl/fl mice. Eight- to 12-week-old mice were used in each experiment, and no animals were excluded in each study. Mice were housed in specific pathogen–free animal facilities at Johns Hopkins University. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act and the principles in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The Animal Care and Use Committee of Johns Hopkins University approved all methods and protocols used in this study.

To induce CVB3 myocarditis, experimental animals (8-12 weeks old) were infected with about 1 × 102 PFU of heart-passaged CVB3 virus intraperitoneally in 100 μL of HBSS (Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution). To minimize confounders, we alternated between viral infection and placebo, and all experiments were performed in the same facility. Mice were randomized to treatment or controls by cages using a random-number generator.

Generating heart-passaged coxsackievirus B3

To generate heart-passaged CVB3 viral stocks, 4-week-old C56BL/6 males were infected with 5 × 105 PFU of tissue culture–derived Nancy strain CVB3. After 3 days, hearts were harvested and homogenized in 10% DMEM (Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium). Homogenate was centrifuged for 20 minutes at 795g at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing infectious virus was collected.

Primary cardiac fibroblast cultures

Hearts from wild-type or PDGFRacre mice were perfused with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 3 minutes, minced into 1-mm pieces, and placed in MACS C-tubes with 5 mL of HBSS containing 3,000 units of collagenase II and 300 units of DNase I (Worthington). An autoMACS was used to first mechanically dissociate tissue using the built-in heart program 1. C-tubes were then placed in a 37 °C rotating incubator at 200 rpm for 35 minutes to enzymatically digest tissue. The final mechanical digestion was performed using heart program 2 of the autoMACS to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were plated on T25 tissue culture flasks, and after 4 hours, the media was removed and the flasks were washed with PBS to remove nonadherent cells. Fibroblasts were grown in complete tissue culture media (DMEM with 4.5 g/L glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 25 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin G, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 20% fetal bovine serum [FBS]). Fibroblasts were grown to 90% confluency and removed by rinsing with PBS then detaching with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at room temperature until cells detached. Trypsin was neutralized with 10× volume of complete tissue culture media. Fibroblasts from passages 2 to 4 were used for in vitro experiments. For stimulation, cells were seeded on 24- or 48-well plates and stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng/mL), IL-17A (50 ng/mL), IFNγ (50 ng/mgL), IL-4 (10 ng/mL), IL-22 (20 ng/mL), IL-13 (50 ng/mL), or IL-33 (10 ng/mL) (Peprotech).25 After the indicated time, supernatants were collected and frozen at −80 °C while cells were lysed and homogenized using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen).

Human cardiac fibroblasts

Human cardiac fibroblasts were obtained from Cell Applications Inc. Cells were quickly thawed and grown on T25 tissue culture flasks in complete media containing DMEM, 20% FBS, 4.5 g/L glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 25 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 250 ng/mL amphotericin B. For stimulation, cells were plated in 24-well plates and stimulated with IL-1β (100 ng/mL), TNFα + IL17 A (100 ng/mL each), poly I:C (10 μg/mL), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (10 ng/mL), imiquimod (5 μg/mL), or ODN2006 (1 μM) for 24 hours. Subsequently, supernatants were collected and frozen at −80 °C while cells were lysed and homogenized using TRIzol reagent.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR

RNA was isolated from cells using either TRIzol phenol-chloroform extraction (Invitrogen) for in vitro culture experiments or Qiagen RNeasy (Qiagen) standard protocols for cells undergoing fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). RNA (200-500 ng) was reverse transcribed using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad) to generate single-stranded cDNA. Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) was used for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and real-time cycle thresholds were detected using the CFX Opus Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative normalized expression, which was normalized to either Gapdh or Hprt. Primers used can be found in Supplemental Table 1.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Ultra-low input RNA sequencing

The SMARTSeq HT ultra-low input kit (Clontech) was used for full-length cDNA synthesis and amplification, and the Nextera XT library (Illumina) was used for sequencing library preparation. Briefly, cDNA was fragmented and adaptor was added using Transposase, followed by limited-cycle PCR to enrich and add indices to the cDNA fragments. Sequencing libraries were validated using the Agilent TapeStation and quantified by using Qubit Fluorometer as well as by qPCR (KAPA Biosystems).

The sequencing libraries were multiplexed and clustered on the HiSeq lanes. After clustering, the flowcell was loaded on the Illumina HiSeq instrument according to the manufacturer's instructions. The samples were sequenced (2× 150 bp paired-end configuration).

Reanalysis of public single-nuclear RNA sequencing data

Data preprocessing and quality control

Publicly available individual single-nucleus RNA sequencing datasets from ischemic cardiomyopathy (GSE145154), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (GSE181764), and cardiac sarcoidosis patients (GSE 205734) were processed using the Seurat (v4) R package. To ensure high-quality data, nuclei were filtered to remove those with fewer than 500 detected genes or more than 5% mitochondrial content, which may indicate nuclear envelope rupture. Each dataset was normalized independently using the SCTransform function to account for differences in sequencing depth and technical noise.

Dataset integration and batch correction

To account for technical variation across different samples and cohorts, we performed data integration using the Seurat SCTransform workflow. First, we identified the top 3,000 highly variable features shared across all datasets using the SelectIntegrationFeatures function. These features were then used to prepare the objects for integration via PrepSCTIntegration. Integration anchors were subsequently identified using FindIntegrationAnchors, using the SCT-normalized data. Finally, the datasets were merged into a unified, batch-corrected assay using the IntegrateData function.

Initial analysis

The integrated dataset was scaled, and principal component analysis using 50 dimensions was performed by the RunPCA function followed by nonlinear reduction using the RunUMAP function. FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions were used to cluster the data, which were separated into biologically relevant clusters using known markers. Fibroblasts were further subset, and supervised clustering was performed to delineate 3 fibroblast populations: inflammatory fibroblast (COL1 and CCL2), myofibroblasts (ACTA2), and remaining fibroblasts.

Initial bioinformatics analysis

Image analysis and base calling were conducted by the Control Software (HCS). Raw sequence data (.bcl files) generated from Illumina Novaseq were converted into fastq files and de-multiplexed using Illumina's bcl2fastq conversion software (v2.17). One mismatch was allowed for index sequence identification. Reads were aligned and count matrices were generated using Kallisto. Differential gene expression was performed using the DESEQ2 package in R Statistical Software (v4.5; R Core Team, 2025).

Flow cytometry

Hearts were perfused with 1× PBS for 3 minutes and then excised and placed in HBSS. One-half of each heart was chopped into 1-mm pieces using a safety razor and placed in MACS C-tubes with 5 mL of HBSS containing 3,000 units of collagenase II and 300 units of DNase I (Worthington). An autoMACS using heart program 1 was used to first dissociate tissue mechanically. C-tubes were then placed in a 37 °C rotating incubator at 200 rpm for 35 minutes to enzymatically digest tissue. The final mechanical digestion was performed using heart program 2 of the autoMACS to obtain a single-cell suspension. Ice-cold FACS buffer (5 mL) was used to dilute the hearts, and single cells were resuspended in 500 μL of ACK buffer for 3 minutes, which was subsequently quenched with ice-cold FACS buffer (5 mL). Finally, hearts were filtered through a 40 μm strainer. Single-cell suspensions were washed with PBS and stained with Zombie NIR dye (1:2000) + FcgRII/III blocked with a-CD16/CD32 (1:100) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Cells were washed with FACS buffer and stained with a cocktail of fluorescently-conjugated antibodies in FACS buffer (0.5% BSA, 2 mM EDTA in PBS) at 4 °C for 30 minutes. Cells were then fixed with 200 μL of BD Cytofix Fixation Buffer (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer's directions. Antibodies are found in Supplemental Table 2. Samples were acquired on Cytek Aurora 5-laser cytometer running Spectroflo 3.0 and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.9, Becton, Dickinson and Company).

For FACS, single-cell suspensions were prepared as above, but cells were not fixed at the end of staining. Cells were stained with the indicated antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C and either propidium iodide or DAPI to gate out dead cells and debris.

Myocarditis severity assessment

Hearts were cut in half longitudinally, and one-half of the heart was fixed in formalin-free tissue fixative (Sigma). Tissue was embedded and cut into 5-μm serial sections. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess myocarditis severity. Sections were scored by a single independent, blinded investigator using the following method to provide an unbiased, quantitative assessment of myocarditis severity: Myocarditis inflammation was quantified using a QuPath-based digital pathology pipeline. Training slides were first manually annotated to indicate areas of inflammation or normal myocardial tissue. Myocarditis severity was calculated as the percentage of total heart tissue classified as inflammation. Immune-rich areas were defined by high nuclear density with hyperchromatic hematoxylin signal predominating over eosin staining, whereas intact myocardium exhibited abundant eosin-stained cardiomyocyte cytoplasm with nuclei that were less densely aggregated. These annotations were applied across the cardiac tissue to train a supervised object classifier employing a Random Forest machine learning algorithm to enable reproducible segmentation of immune-rich and intact regions. As an orthogonal validation, per-cell H&E intensities were quantified and plotted, clearly separating cardiomyocytes (abundant eosin with lower nuclear hematoxylin) from infiltrating leukocytes (strong nuclear hematoxylin with reduced eosin staining).

Immunohistochemical staining of mouse tissue slides

Unstained slides were heated at 60 °C for 20 minutes followed by immersion in Trilogy (Cell Marque, catalog number: 920P-06) and heated in a pressure cooker for 20 minutes. After cooling, slides were rinsed in distilled water and treated for 5 minutes with a Dual Blocking solution (Dako, catalog number: S2003). Primary incubation (vimentin 1:200, Abcam, catalog number: ab92547; CCL2 1:150, Thermo Fisher, catalog number: MA5-17040) was carried out for 1 hour, slides were rinsed, and slides were incubated with the secondary antibodies (Leica, catalog number: PV6119 or PV6114) for 30 minutes. Slides were rinsed before incubating in DAB chromogen (Vector Laboratories, catalog number: SK-4100) for 4 minutes. Slides were rinsed before being counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 minute (Thermo Fisher, catalog number: 72221), followed by ClearRite (Thermo Fisher, catalog number: 6901) for 1 minute, followed by Bluing reagent (Epredia, catalog number: 6769001) for 1 minute before a sequential dehydration in ethanol. Slides were mounted with OPTICMOUNT 1 (Mercedes Scientific, catalog number: MER7720) and left to cure overnight.

Immunohistochemical staining of human tissue slides

Tissue sections were obtained from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks of endomyocardial biopsies from patients with lymphocytic or eosinophilic myocarditis at the Clinical and Transplant Pathology Center at the Institute for Clinical and Experimental Medicine, Prague, Czech Republic. Patient characteristics are in Supplemental Table 3. Sections of 3-μm thickness were prepared for H&E staining, and 4 μm sections were used for immunohistochemical detection. The sections were dried overnight in a heated drying oven. H&E staining was performed using Mayer's hematoxylin following a standard protocol. Immunohistochemistry was conducted on a Roche BenchMark ULTRA PLUS automated staining system.

Antigen retrieval for all markers was conducted in the BenchMark ULTRA PLUS instrument using the heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) technique with Cell Conditioning 1 solution (Ventana Medical Systems; catalog number: 950-124). The primary antibodies used included anti-vimentin (Agilent Technologies, catalog number: M0725, clone: V9) at a dilution of 1:500, anti-MCP1/CCL2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number: MA5-17040, clone: 2D8) at a dilution of 1:200, anti-eotaxin/CCL11 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, catalog number: sc-373767, clone: C-3) at a dilution of 1:100, and anti-PDGFRA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number: MA5-41209, clone: JF104-6) at a dilution of 1:100.

The detection of bound primary antibodies was achieved using the ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit (Ventana Medical Systems, catalog number: 760-500), which included a secondary antibody. Upon completion of the immunohistochemical staining in the automated system, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Each stained slide included both positive and negative controls to ensure the reliability of the staining process and the specificity of the antibodies used.

Statistical methods

Data in the figures were presented using actual data points with the mean ± standard deviation or median value displayed for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively.

Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using the Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Comparisons among >2 groups used 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc test based on data distribution. Brown-Forsythe or Welch's ANOVA alternatives were used when the assumption of equal variance was violated. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Sidak's post hoc test was used to analyze longitudinal changes. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to determine how fibroblasts positive or negative for mCherry clustered.

GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 (GraphPad Software) was used for statistical analyses, and a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Number and frequency of inflammatory fibroblasts in the heart is highest on day 3 of CVB3 infection

We previously reported that inflammatory cytokines such as IL17A can induce IFs and induce production of several inflammatory chemokines and cytokines such as CCL2.24,25,32 IFs primarily expressed CCL2 as one of the main differentially expressed genes in the IF cluster; therefore, we used CCL2 production as a marker for IFs.25 To examine the kinetics of IF induction during CVB3 myocarditis, we used CCL2-mCherry reporter mice and characterized IFs during CVB3 myocarditis by flow cytometry (Supplemental Figure 1A). CCL2-mCherry mice have CCL2 and mCherry separated by an HA peptide followed by an aphthovirus 2A cleavage site that allows mCherry to serve as a 1:1 marker for CCL2 expression via ribosome skipping. To identify IFs in vivo, CCL2-mCherry mice were infected with CVB3 and sacrificed on day 3, 7, 10, or 14 postinfection, with naïve mice serving as controls (Figure 1A). We then detected CCL2-mCherry positive cells by multiparameter flow cytometry. Out of all live cells, CCL2 production first appeared on day 3 and peaked between days 7 and 10 of CVB3 myocarditis. By day 14, the proportion of CCL2+ cells returned to day 3 levels (Figure 1B). Interestingly, cardiac fibroblasts (CD45−CD31−FEEDER(mefSK4)+PDGFRα+) comprised the largest proportion of CCL2+ cells on day 3 until infiltrating Ly6C+ monocytes/macrophages overtook CCL2 production during days 7 and 10 of CVB3 myocarditis (Figure 1C). Fibroblasts contributed minimally to CCL2 production from day 7 to day 14 (Figure 1C). CCL2+ fibroblasts, as a proportion of all cardiac fibroblasts, were highest on day 3 (25%, P < 0.001), declined by days 7 to 10, and returned to naïve levels by day 14 (Figure 1D). The amount of CCL2 production by cardiac fibroblasts as assessed by the median fluorescence intensity of the mCherry reporter signal follows the same trajectory, with a peak at day 3 of CVB3 myocarditis (Figure 1E). Immunohistochemistry staining of mouse hearts on day 10 of CVB3 myocarditis with vimentin and CCL2 demonstrated IF localizing both within and outside of immune foci throughout the myocardium (Supplemental Figure 1B). Next, we used known markers of fibroblast activation Sca-1 and Ly6C to determine how these markers track with CCL2 production.32,38 In the naïve state, about 65% of all cardiac fibroblasts are Sca-1+, but from days 3 through 10 of CVB3 myocarditis, Sca-1+ fibroblasts account for about 90% of total fibroblasts before dropping down to naïve levels at day 14 (Supplemental Figures 1C to 1D). Interestingly, throughout the time points assessed, over 95% of CCL2+ fibroblasts were Sca-1+ (Figure 1G). Similarly, while only 20% (and 50% on day 3) of total cardiac fibroblasts were Ly6C+ (Supplemental Figure 1E), Ly6C expression ranged between 60% to 80% of CCL2+ fibroblasts (Figure 1H).

All together, these results suggest that IFs are a major contributor to total CCL2 production early during CVB3 myocarditis and may play a critical role in initiating inflammation during disease.

RNA sequencing reveals that CCL2+ inflammatory fibroblasts promote Th1, Th2, and Th17 immune responses while downregulating profibrotic genes

Aside from CCL2 production, we wanted to transcriptionally assess how else IFs might contribute to the immune response during CVB3 myocarditis. We performed bulk RNA sequencing on sorted CD45−CD31−FEEDER+ mCherry positive and negative cells from CCL2-mCherry reporter mice on day 3 of CVB3 (Supplemental Figure 2A). Principle component analysis showed that fibroblasts negative for mCherry clustered tightly together, while mCherry-positive fibroblasts clustered more loosely together (Figure 2A). Additionally, we confirmed that the mCherry-positive fibroblasts had higher normalized Ccl2 counts than the negative population (Supplemental Figure 2B). Several chemokines typical of Th1, Th2, and Th17 responses were differentially upregulated in the mCherry-positive fibroblasts. For example, IFs not only produced CCL2, but they also differentially expressed Cxcl2 and Cxcl5, which are involved in neutrophils recruitment, Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl11, which recruit T cells, and Ccl11, which recruits eosinophils (Figures 2B to 2C). Furthermore, these fibroblasts also differentially expressed Il6, Csf2 (GM-CSF), and Tslp, indicating that they locally regulate immune cell phenotypes. We validated upregulation of Ccl2, Cxcl2, and Csf2 by IF by qPCR of sorted mCherry-positive and -negative fibroblast populations (Supplemental Figures 2C to 2E). Using the DecoupleR package in R, we performed pathway inference analysis to understand which transcriptional pathways were most active in IFs. Not surprisingly, pathways involved in inflammatory signaling, namely, NFκB, TNFα, Pi3K, and JAK-STAT signaling, were all upregulated in IFs (Figure 2D). However, we did observe a decrease in TGFβ pathway activity that was driven by a decrease in gene expression of ECM genes such as Comp, and Mfap4 and myofibroblast genes such as Acta2 (Figures 2D to 2E, Supplemental Figure 2F). Interestingly, although IFs upregulate Il11 transcription, they downregulate their own expression of Il11ra1 and Il11ra2. This suggests that IFs themselves may only indirectly contribute to fibrosis. Finally, we performed gene set enrichment analysis to further understand which cellular processes are active or downregulated in IFs. Processes involving granulocyte, monocyte, and lymphocyte chemotaxis and their activation were enriched in mCherry-positive fibroblasts (Figure 2E). Additionally, IFs upregulated genes involved in cell-cell adhesion (Icam1) and angiogenesis, while strongly downregulating genes involved in ECM-related processes (Figure 2E). Our sequencing results indicate that CCL2-positive IFs downregulate a profibrotic phenotype in vivo and play important roles in producing Th1, Th2, and Th17-related chemokines.

IL-1 signaling is a potent inducer of the inflammatory cardiac fibroblast phenotype

The etiology of cardiac injury can influence what cytokines are released in the cardiac microenvironment and shape the resulting immune response. We next tested whether cardiac fibroblasts can respond to diverse inflammatory cues in the cardiac milieu and adopt an IF phenotype. We isolated primary murine cardiac fibroblasts and cultured them in the presence of IFNγ, IL-4, IL-17A, IL-22, IL-1β, or IL-13 for 24 hours and assessed gene expression changes by qPCR. Cytokines were chosen to reflect the Th1, Th2, and Th17 inflammatory microenvironments found in various etiologies of myocarditis such as viral, autoimmune, giant cell, and eosinophilic myocarditis. Our previously published work demonstrated that IL-17A signaling to cardiac fibroblasts induced expression of myeloid recruiting and activating genes such as Ccl2, Csf2 (GM-CSF), and Cxcl1, which we reproduced in this study25 (Figures 3A to 3E). IL-1β stimulation also led to expression of a similar set of genes as IL-17A stimulation, albeit at greater levels (P < 0.001 for all comparisons) (Figures 3A to 3E). IL-1β strongly promoted expression of genes involved in cell-cell interactions such as Icam1 and Sele (E-selectin) (Figure 3A). While IL-1β induced a myeloid recruitment and activation promoting signature in cardiac fibroblasts, IFNγ induced Cxcl10 and Ccl11 expression, which recruit T cells and eosinophils, respectively (Figures 3A, 3F to 3G). We were also able to measure highly elevated levels of CCL2, GM-CSF, CXCL1, and CXCL2 at the protein level with IL-1β stimulation (Figures 3H to 3K). Given the strength of IL-1β induction of the IF phenotype, we compared IF induction by IL-1β, cGAMP, and various damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as HMGB1 and ATP. While IL-1β strongly induced expression of Ccl2, Il6, and Cxcl1, cGAMP induced low, though not significant, levels of Ccl2 and Il6 (Supplemental Figures 3A to 3C). Neither HMGB1 nor ATP induced expression of these myeloid promoting cytokines (Supplemental Figures 3A to 3C). Both IL-1β and IL-1α equally induced expression of Ccl2, Il6, and Cxcl1, while LPS (a TLR-4 agonist) induced moderate expression of these genes (Supplemental Figures 3D to 3F). However, TLR3, TLR 7/8, and TLR 9 agonism did not significantly induce expression of Cxcl1 and Ccl2 (Supplemental Figures 3D to 3F). In addition to IFNγ, we found that 2′3′cGAMP and TLR3 agonism both induced Cxcl10 expression (Supplemental Figures 3G to 3H). In summary, our data demonstrate that cardiac fibroblasts have the capacity to respond a variety of environmental cytokines or PAMP/DAMPs and are poised to amplify inflammation after cardiac insults.

We were interested in understanding whether IFs transcriptionally upregulate genes involved in ECM remodeling. Interestingly, IL-1β greatly upregulated expression of Mmp9 and Mmp13, though none of the stimulation conditions significantly upregulated Col1a1 or Col3a1 (Supplemental Figures 4A to 4D). Taken together, these data suggest that IFs may play an important role in promoting inflammation through ECM degradation and chemokine-mediated immune cell recruitment.

Examining inflammatory fibroblast plasticity

We next investigated whether the IF phenotype persisted after removal of IL-1β or addition of other cytokines to the environment. We first activated IFs for 72 hours with IL-1β, followed by replacing the media with IL-1β–deficient media for 24 hours. As expected, IL-1β induced expression of Ccl2, Cxcl1, and Csf2 (GM-CSF) when continually present in the media (Figures 4A to 4C). To our surprise, we saw that the IFs quickly downregulated expression of Ccl2, Cxcl1, and Csf2 in the absence of IL-1β for 24 hours (P < 0.001) (Figures 4A to 4C). From our bulk RNA sequencing dataset, we observed that IFs in vivo upregulated IL13ra1, Il10ra, and Il10rb (Figure 2B). We hypothesized that increasing susceptibility to IL-13 and IL-10 signaling may function as a potential off switch for IF activity. To test this, we stimulated primary murine cardiac fibroblasts with IL-1β for 24 hours. For the following 24 hours, we spiked in either IL-13 or IL-10 to the media and measured gene expression by qPCR. Rather than suppressing gene expression, both IL-13 and IL-10 significantly enhanced Il6, Ccl2, and Cxcl1 gene expression (Figures 4D to 4E). IL-10 was a stronger inducer of Cxcl1 expression than IL-13 in the presence of IL-1β (Figure 4E). Furthermore, Ccl11 was only expressed when IL-13 was present in the media, indicating that IL-1β–activated IFs can respond to Th2-related stimuli (Figure 4F). These results further demonstrate that multiple cytokine signaling pathways can be simultaneously active in IFs. The complex interactions of the inflammatory microenvironment can augment proinflammatory signaling or even induce additional immune responses.

Blocking IL-1 signaling to fibroblasts attenuates the severity of CVB3 myocarditis

Given the potency of the induction of the IF phenotype by IL-1β or IL-1α, we sought to investigate whether fibroblast-specific IL-1 signaling influences the pathogenesis of acute viral myocarditis. We crossed PDGFRαcre (JAX 013148) with IL1r1fl/fl mice (JAX 028398) to generate PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice, where the IL1 receptor is deleted in PDGFRα-expressing cells. In the heart, cardiac fibroblasts comprise 90% of PDGFRα-expressing cells at the naïve state and during CVB3 myocarditis (Supplemental Figures 5A to 5B). To confirm that cardiac fibroblasts from PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice were insensitive to IL-1β signaling, we cultured primary cardiac fibroblasts from Cre control and mutant mice in the presence of IL-1β. Only primary cardiac fibroblasts cultured from PDGFRαcre but not PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice responded to IL-1β stimulation in vitro based off expression of IL-1β–induced genes such as Ccl2, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Il6 (Supplemental Figures 5C to 5F). Furthermore, we confirmed the absence of IL1r1 expression in PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice by qPCR of MACS-sorted cardiac fibroblasts from wild-type or PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl during the naïve state or at days 3 and 5 post-CVB3 (Supplemental Figure 5G).

We performed qPCR on heart homogenates taken at naïve, day 3, day 7, day 10, and day 14 of CVB3 myocarditis for Il1b to determine when IL-1β–mediated signaling would be involved in disease. Il1b was almost undetectable in the naïve and day 14 hearts, but we observed upregulation of transcript levels from day 3 to the peak of inflammation at day 10 of CVB3 myocarditis (Figure 5A). To assess the impact of the loss of fibroblast–IL-1 signaling on CVB3 myocarditis severity, we induced CVB3 myocarditis in PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice or Cre controls (Figure 5B). The trajectory of weight loss over time significantly depended on genotype between PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl and PDGFRαcre mice (Figure 5C). PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice proportionally lost less of their baseline weight than controls by day 7, although this only trended to significance in post hoc testing (difference between means = 4.73% ± 1.87; P = 0.0771) (Figure 5C). However, no significant differences in heart weight–to–body weight ratios were observed between the groups at day 10 (Supplemental Figure 6B). To assess myocarditis severity in an unbiased manner, we used a machine learning algorithm in QuPath to classify histological features as inflamed or noninflamed and compare the proportion of total tissue that was identified as inflammation between the 2 groups. Overall, myocarditis severity was significantly less in the PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice compared to Cre controls (difference between means = 7.963 ± 3.020; P = 0.013) (Figures 5D to 5E). Further enumeration of immune cells numbers by flow cytometry (Supplemental Figure 6A) demonstrated an almost 50% reduction in total CD45+ cells in PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice compared to Cre controls (difference between means = 873.9 ± 258.1; P = 0.002) (Figure 5F). Though comparison of the relative make-up of immune cell subsets as a percentage of total CD45+ cells did not reveal significant differences between the groups (Figure 5G), we observed a large decline in the absolute numbers of monocytes (CD64loCCR2+Ly6C+), monocyte-derived macrophages (CD64+CCR2+Ly6C+), eosinophils, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells in mutant mice compared to control mice (Figures 5H to 5M). B cell, γδ T cell, and neutrophil numbers were unchanged between control and mutant mice (Supplemental Figures 6C to 6E). To determine if diminished inflammation, as seen by number of CD45 cardiac infiltrating cells, was associated with an increase in CVB3 viral load in the heart, we quantified viral transcripts in cardiac tissue by qPCR using CVB3-specific primers and found no differences at day 10 (Supplemental Figure 6F). On day 10 of CVB3 myocarditis, there were no differences in serum CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2, or in IL-6 levels in mutant mice (Supplemental Figures 6G to 6J). Given the early involvement of IFs during CVB3 myocarditis, we transcriptionally profiled isolated fibroblasts from PDGFRαcre or PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice on day 3 of CVB3 myocarditis by bulk RNA sequencing. Differential gene expression between the groups showed that fibroblasts from conditional knockout mice differentially expressed less chemokines such as Cxcl1, Cxcl5, and Cxcl13 (Figure 5N, Supplemental Figures 7A to 7D). Pathway inference analysis demonstrated downregulation of NFκB, MAPK, and TGFβ signaling in IL1r1 null fibroblasts, whereas TNFα signaling was upregulated (Figure 5O). Therefore, these results show a decline in myeloid recruiting capabilities of IFs in the absence of IL-1 signaling.

We also assessed sex as a biological variable and found no differences in CVB3-induced weight loss (Supplemental Figure 8A). Overall, CD45+ cells (Supplemental Figure 8B), eosinophils (Supplemental Figure 8E), neutrophils (Supplemental Figure 8F), CD4 T cells (Supplemental Figure 8G), and CD8 T cells (Supplemental Figure 8H) were significantly decreased in male conditional knockout mice, whereas the effect in females did not reach statistical significance.

IL-1 signaling to cardiac fibroblasts does not affect chronic CVB3 myocarditis

We next sought to determine how the decrease in inflammation during acute CVB3 myocarditis in PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice might affect chronic cardiac function. Again, we infected PDGFRαcre or PDGFRαcreIL1r1fl/fl mice with CVB3 and sacrificed them on day 35. Mice with a fibroblast-specific Il1r1 deletion had a decreasing trend in heart weight–to–body weight ratios than those without the deletion (Supplemental Figure 9A). To assess the extent of fibrosis, we stained longitudinal heart sections with Masson's trichrome stain to calculate fibrosis as a percentage of ventricular area. No differences in ventricular fibrosis were observed between the groups (Supplemental Figures 9B to 9C). Hearts from PDGFRαcre controls were minimally fibrotic, which is consistent with how C57Bl/6 are resistant to adverse remodeling in the CVB3 myocarditis model. Cre control mice and conditional knockout mice had similar ejection fraction, fractional shortening, and interventricular septal width during systole and diastole by M-mode echocardiography (Supplemental Figures 9D to 9G).

Human cardiac fibroblasts recapitulate inflammatory fibroblast features

Finally, we sought to validate the inflammatory fibroblast phenotype using human cardiac fibroblasts. We cultured human cardiac fibroblasts in the presence of IL-1β, TNFα + IL17 A, poly I:C (TLR 3 agonist), LPS, imiquimod (TLR7/8 agonist), or ODN2006 (TLR9 agonist) and assessed expression of select genes by qPCR. Again, we saw that IL-1β strongly induced upregulation of genes involved in myeloid inflammation such as Ccl2, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Csf2, Il1b, and Il6 (Figures 6A to 6F). None of the TLR agonists induced transcription of these genes. Additionally, we saw that costimulation of IL-17A and TNFα strongly induced expression of the same genes as IL-1β (Figures 6A-6F). Except in the cases of Ccl2, Csf2, and Il6 (Figures 6A, 6D, and 6F), IL-1β was just as potent as the combination of IL-17A and TNFα costimulation in inducing expression of Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Il1b (Figures 6B to 6C, 6E). Our collaborators from the Institute for Clinical and Experimental Medicine (IKEM) then obtained cardiac biopsies or explants from patients with either lymphocytic or eosinophilic myocarditis and performed immunohistochemical staining to identify vimentin+CCL2+ or vimentin+eotaxin+ inflammatory cardiac fibroblasts. After aligning slide images, we performed pixel classification to identify DAB+ staining and then performed a colocalization analysis to quantify double expression of vimentin with either CCL2 or eotaxin (Figure 6G). Regardless of the subtype of myocarditis, we were able to observe vimentin+CCL2+ (Figure 6H) and vimentin+eotaxin+ IFs, although vimentin+eotaxin+ fibroblasts were exceedingly rare (Figure 6I). We found CCL2+ fibroblasts to be similarly abundant in lymphocytic versus eosinophilic myocarditis (Figure 6H). Finally, we reanalyzed publicly available human single-nucleus RNA sequencing datasets on cardiac tissue from dilated cardiomyopathy (GSE145154), ischemic cardiomyopathy (GSE145154), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (GSE181764), and cardiac sarcoidosis patients (GSE 205734) (Figures 6J to 6K). After subclustering on cardiac fibroblasts, we performed supervised clustering to identify inflammatory fibroblasts as fibroblasts expressing COL1A1 and CCL2, myofibroblasts as ACTA2-positive, and pooled all remaining fibroblasts together. Comparison of clustering between disease states revealed that inflammatory fibroblasts were present to varying degrees in all groups but particularly enriched in ischemic cardiomyopathy and sarcoidosis patients (Figure 6K). Inflammatory fibroblasts were also enriched for inflammatory genes, including FOSL1, IL1R1, STAT3, and STAT6, as shown in the dot plot (Figure 6L). These genes were also present in the myofibroblast population, although at a lower level (Figure 6L). In summary, these data support the role of an IL-1β–induced inflammatory phenotype in human cardiac fibroblasts and indicate that IFs are present in various etiologies of human heart disease, including myocarditis.

Discussion

Biopsy-confirmed cases of viral myocarditis have only a 50% 10-year survival rate after acute disease.1,2,39 Chronic inflammation is implicated in cases of acute myocarditis that progress to inflammatory dilated cardiomyopathy.40 Inflammatory fibroblasts have emerged as key regulators of immune activity across diverse tissues, yet their role in viral myocarditis has remained largely undefined. In this study, we demonstrate that IFs are rapidly activated during acute CVB3 myocarditis, adopt a highly inflammatory transcriptional program, and significantly amplify cardiac immune infiltration through IL-1–dependent signaling. Identifying IFs in human cardiac disease positions IFs as a potential target of therapeutic interventions.

Our results support a role for IF in early amplification of inflammatory processes before infiltrating immune cells dominate the propagation of proinflammatory cascades during peak disease. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated early IF activity in disease progression. For example, “injury response” fibroblasts were predominantly detected on day 1 after myocardial infarction, while inflammatory stromal cells were detected by day 1 after skeletal muscle injury.23,41 Our data showing CF responsiveness to innate sensing pathways such as TLRs and STING or through the detection of inflammatory cytokines in the microenvironment supports IFs playing a role as sentinel cells involved in sensing the environment during disease. IL-1β was the only cytokine of those tested that induced Mmp9 and Mmp13 expression, suggesting that IL-1–activated IFs contribute to early cardiac remodeling to allow for immune cell recruitment into the tissue parenchyma.42

RNA sequencing of ex vivo CCL2+ fibroblasts revealed that IFs expressed Th1, Th2, and Th17 cytokines, suggesting they can participate in immune responses regardless of the type of cardiac injury. Furthermore, we believe this demonstrates that identifying IFs by CCL2 production may capture most of the inflammatory subpopulation during acute disease. To date, no unique surface markers have been found that uniquely identify IFs, including from our bulk RNA sequencing data. Our study identified that IFs universally express Sca-1 and are enriched for Ly6C expression. While Sca-1+ and Ly6C surface expression does not specifically identify IFs, it does enrich for them relative to other fibroblast subsets and has been used to describe nonspecialized fibroblast subsets across tissues.32,38 Therefore, strategies to identify IFs should include a fibroblast marker (ie, PDGFRα or vimentin), CCL2, or another inflammatory cytokine, and Sca-1 as a useful but not necessary marker.

Regarding profibrotic potential, our data indicate that IFs in early CVB3 myocarditis may be distinct from myofibroblasts based on downregulation of genes such as Acta2, Comp, and Fbn2, as has been observed in IL-1–activated inflammatory cancer–associated fibroblasts (iCAFs).43 However, IFs express Il11 and upregulate Meox1, suggesting that they can indirectly promote fibrosis by signaling in-trans to other fibroblast subsets or are poised to differentiate into a profibrotic state.44 Recent studies have shown that IL-1β, through activating the transcription factor Meox1, induces a profibrotic Postn+FAP+ fibroblast subset that is diminished in vivo with IL-1β blockade in a transverse aortic constriction model.45 In our study, deletion of Il1r1 in fibroblasts did not affect its expression on day 3 of CVB3 myocarditis, suggesting that, in overtly inflammatory cardiac diseases, other pathways may contribute to Meox1 expression (data not shown). Studies using RNA velocity pseudotime analysis from single-cell RNA sequencing data conflict on the differentiation trajectory of IFs. Pseudotime analyses attempt to use progressive changes in the transcriptomes of cells to predict cellular differentiation trajectories.46 Forte et al23 found that there is a pseudotime trajectory from IFs to myofibroblasts after myocardial infarction. Conversely, Abe et al21 identified that the pseudotime trajectory from quiescent fibroblasts to IFs is distinct from the trajectory of quiescent fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, without any transitions between the terminal populations, in transverse aortic constriction. Although these results have not been experimentally determined, they suggest that disease etiology may influence the fate of IFs.

IL-1 signaling is known to be pathogenic during viral myocarditis, though which cells mediate its proinflammatory activities are not known.47, 48, 49 IL1r1 deletion on fibroblasts attenuated acute CVB3 myocarditis and led to reductions in CCR2+Ly6C+ monocytes/macrophages as well as CD3+ lymphocytes and NK cells. Our results recapitulated observations that global IL-1 blockade decreases inflammation, macrophage, and T-cell recruitment in CVB3 myocarditis, highlighting how CFs mediate the pathogenicity of IL-1 signaling.48 The reduction in myeloid recruitment is consistent with decreased chemokine expression in IL1r1-deficient fibroblasts The decline in monocyte-derived macrophage recruitment may have downstream effects on T-cell recruitment, as previously demonstrated in EAM, myocardial infarction, and checkpoint inhibitor–induced myocarditis.35,50,51 These reductions occurred without impairing viral clearance, indicating that targeting fibroblast-specific IL-1 signaling may attenuate inflammation without hampering antiviral defense.36,48

The translational relevance of IFs is supported by the human in vitro CF data mirroring the cytokine profiles observed in mice, suggesting relevance of this pathway in human disease. Furthermore, data from single-nucleus RNA sequencing studies from human disease demonstrate a stronger enrichment for IFs in ischemic cardiomyopathy and sarcoidosis, which have stronger associations with an overt inflammatory response than dilated cardiomyopathy or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.52, 53, 54 Preclinical blockade of IL-1RAP (a shared coreceptor for IL1α, IL-1β, IL-33, and IL-36 signaling) attenuated CVB3 myocarditis severity and reduced the influx of monocytes, granulocytes, and CD4 T cells to the heart.55 Clinically, case reports have hinted at the efficacy of anakinra (IL-1Ra biologic) in treating acute myocarditis, while observations from the ARAMIS trial demonstrated the safety of anakinra in patients with myocarditis.47,56 The CANTOS trial demonstrated a reduction in myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death with high-dose canakinumab (anti-IL-1β) in high-risk patients with previous myocardial infarction.57 Collectively, these studies show promise for targeting the IL-1 pathway in inflammatory heart disease. IFs may serve as biomarkers for disease activity or for therapeutic response to biologics such as canakinumab or anakinra.

One limitation of this study is that while CCL2 is a robust marker of IFs during acute disease, CCL2-independent inflammatory fibroblast subsets may exist. However, our bulk RNA sequencing results suggested that this approach sufficiently enriched for IFs producing Th1, Th2, and Th17 cytokines and chemokines. Finally, C57BL/6 mice are resistant to chronic remodeling and dilated cardiomyopathy in the CVB3 model, limiting our ability to assess long-term consequences of IF modulation.58,59 However, involvement of this pathway in chronic myocarditis may be supported by other investigators, who have observed a cardioprotective phenotype when IL1r1 NFκB signaling and inflammasome signaling are abrogated in fibroblasts during myocardial infarction, atherosclerotic plaque rupture, pressure overload, and arrythmias.21,60, 61, 62

In summary, our work identifies IL-1β–activated inflammatory fibroblasts as central amplifiers of acute cardiac inflammation during CVB3 myocarditis. By integrating cytokine signals and producing a broad array of chemokines, IFs shape the immune landscape of the infected heart. Targeting IL-1 signaling in fibroblasts represents a promising strategy to reduce inflammation while preserving antiviral immunity, and the presence of IFs in human myocarditis underscores their potential as therapeutic targets.
