8-Hydroxyquinoline Series Exerts Bactericidal Activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis Via Copper-Mediated Toxicity
Authors: Amala Bhagwat, Arielle Butts, Eric Greve, Yan Cheung, Eduard Melief, James Gomez, Deborah T. Hung, Tanya Parish
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
New drugs and mechanisms of action targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis are urgently needed to solve the global pandemic of tuberculosis. We previously demonstrated that the 8-hydroxyquinoline series has rapid bactericidal activity against M. tuberculosis. In this work, we determined that the activity of the 8HQ series is potentiated by copper ions and that the activity is dependent on copper since activity was reduced when copper was depleted from the medium. We determined that exposure to 8HQs led to an increase in intracellular copper. The increase in copper ions was specific since we saw no changes for other metal cations (zinc, iron, magnesium, manganese, or calcium). We observed the transient generation of reactive oxygen species after 8HQ exposure which disappeared by 24 h. Inhibition of growth could be partially relieved by scavenging hydroxyl radicals. We excluded the possibility that 8HQs are toxic by DNA intercalation. We screened a panel of hypomorph strains and identified sensitized strains. The pattern of sensitized strains did not suggest a specific target, but metalloenzymes, proteins with Fe–S clusters, and cell envelope biosynthetic enzymes were highlighted. These data suggest that 8HQs do not have a specific intracellular target, but act as copper ionophores, and that the mode of action is via copper-dependent toxicity.

Tuberculosis (TB) is the leading cause of death in the world from bacterial infections.1 There was a 4.5% increase in the number of people newly infected with TB from 2020 to 2021, with 10.6 million people infected in 2021.1 Multidrug-resistant and extremely drug-resistant tuberculosis infections are important with almost half a million cases of rifampicin-resistant TB every year. Furthermore, amidst the COVID-19 pandemic there was a reduction in the diagnosis and treatment of patients infected with TB, thereby increasing its global and economic burden.1 Thus, there is a need for novel antitubercular agents and to understand their mechanism of action in order to better predict modes of drug resistance.

8-Hydroxyquinolines (8HQs) are small planar molecules with an ability to form complexes with divalent metal ions.2 8HQs have many biological activities including antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, anti-Alzheimer, and antiviral properties.2,3 Some of the biological properties of 8HQs arise from their ability to chelate metals;4 for example, metal chelation of 8HQs is useful for anticancer applications and the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease.2,4 In these uses, the chelation of metals leads to reduced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby avoiding oxidative stress and cellular damage.4

8HQs have antimicrobial activity against a spectrum of bacterial pathogens including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus,3Streptococcus mutans, and Streptococcus sobrinus.5 We previously demonstrated that 8-hydroxyquinoline analogues (8HQs) had activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis.6 Several analogues had MICs < 5 μM, and representative molecules were rapidly bactericidal.9 8-hydroxyquinoline activity against M. tuberculosis is potentiated by copper and data support the proposal that it can form a 1:1 complex with copper in solution.7

In this study, we were interested in understanding the mechanism of antitubercular action of 8HQs against virulent M. tuberculosis.

Results

8-Hydroxyquinolines Are Copper Ionophores

We previously identified the 8HQ series as potent antitubercular agents with rapid bactericidal activity.6 We wanted to determine the target or mode of action of the series. It has previously been noted that 8HQs interact with metal ions, and the activity of 8HQ itself was potentiated by copper ions (Cu2+).7 We determined whether copper ions had any effect on the activity of a range of 8HQ analogues from our prior study (Figure 1). We measured the MICs of each analogue against aerobically cultured replicating M. tuberculosis in the presence of increasing amounts of Cu2+ (Figure 2). The addition of copper increased the potency of all analogues in a dose-dependent fashion (Figure 2A). For two molecules, the increase was >10-fold. We also confirmed the synergy between Cu2+ and 8HQs using BLISS analysis (Figure S1). Since we demonstrated that all analogues behaved similarly and were potentiated by copper, we selected two representative molecules for further study.

The standard microbiological medium used for the M. tuberculosis culture contains 7 μM Cu2+, so our MICs were all measured in the presence of copper ions, albeit at a low concentration. We wanted to determine if 8HQ activity was dependent on copper, so we measured MICs in the presence of the copper-specific chelator ammonium tetra thiomolybdate (TTM). In the presence of TTM, compound activity was reduced (increased MIC) (Figure 2B). At the highest concentration of TTM, we observed >25-fold loss of activity for TPN-0086910 and 15-fold loss of activity for TPN-0086937. We confirmed antagonism using BLISS analysis (Figure S2). These data indicate that the presence of copper ions is required for the potency of the molecules.

Since it is possible that 8HQs could interact with other divalent metal cations, we tested their interaction with iron, manganese, magnesium, or zinc in a similar fashion. No synergy or antagonism was seen with any metal ion except copper (Figure S3), confirming that this is a specific mechanism of action.

Taken together, these data demonstrate that Cu2+ is required for the antimicrobial activity of 8HQs against M. tuberculosis. According to the scheme of Ding and Lind,8 where if the compound’s effect is amplified by increasing the metal concentration, it is classified as a “metal ionophore”; the 8HQ analogues are copper ionophores.

Exposure to 8HQs Leads to Increased Intracellular Copper in M. tuberculosis

Since 8HQ activity is dependent on and potentiated by Cu2+ ions, we hypothesized that exposure to 8HQs would lead to an increase in intracellular copper. To determine whether there was copper accumulation in M. tuberculosis, we used two different methods to quantify the intracellular metal content. We selected two compounds from the series for further study.

We first used the fluorescent probe PhenGreen SK to measure the intracellular concentration of Cu2+ ions. Since our microbiological medium contains several metal ions, we first confirmed the specificity of the probe. We did not see any reactivity to Fe3+ or Zn2+ and confirmed that the probe could detect changes in Cu2+ concentration within our range (Figure S4). We measured the intracellular Cu2+ concentration in bacterial cell lysates after exposure to 8HQs with varying extracellular Cu2+ concentrations (Figure 3). We used metal-free Sauton’s medium and added CuSO4 where indicated. There was no difference in the Cu2+ content of the bacterial cells when copper alone was added to the medium (Figure 3A,B). In the absence of extracellular copper, the addition of 8HQs to the medium had no effect on the intracellular concentration of Cu2+. In contrast, when copper was present in the medium, we saw an 8HQ-dependent increase in the intracellular Cu2+ content. The increase in intracellular Cu2+ was seen at the MIC concentration for both compounds, confirming a correlation between the copper boost and antibacterial activity.

(C, D) Total copper content was measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). In order to measure the total copper content, we used inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Since ICP-MS allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple metal ions, we also measured the intracellular content of Cu, Mg, Mn, Fe, Ca, and Zn. Metal ion content was determined in the lysates as well as in the culture supernatant and wash fractions. We saw no change in the concentration of metals in the supernatant or wash fractions in the presence of 8HQs. Using ICP-MS, we were able to observe a small increase in total copper content in the bacteria cultured with 100 μM Cu2+ as compared to those in Cu2+-free medium (Figure 3C,3D). In the presence of 8HQs, there was a further increase in total copper content at concentrations at or above the MIC. There was no increase in the content of any other metal ion in the presence of Cu2+ and/or 8HQ (Figures S4–S7). We determined whether a similar boost happened in the presence of another metal ion; we added 100 μM Zn2+ and measured the total metal ion content. There was no change in the content of zinc or other metal ions after 8HQ exposure (Figures S4–S7). These data confirm that 8HQs act as specific copper ionophores.

8-Hydroxyquinolines Generate a Copper Stress Response in M. tuberculosis

High copper concentrations are toxic to bacterial cells, and M. tuberculosis has a copper response, which includes induction of ctpV, which encodes CtpV, a copper exporter.9,10 We monitored the expression of ctpV by measuring mRNA levels after exposure to 8HQs (Figure 4). We noted an increase in the expression of ctpV consistent with the increased intracellular copper content and the induction of a copper stress response.

8-Hydroxyquinolines Generate Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) in M. tuberculosis

Copper is a redox-active metal that can act as a catalyst in the Fenton reaction, thereby generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). We determined whether 8HQ exposure led to the production of intracellular ROS in M. tuberculosis. We detected the production of ROS after exposure to TPN-0086910 or TPN-0086937 after 90 min of exposure (Figure 5A). ROS production was seen at concentrations slightly higher than the MICs (about 10 μM).

8HQs exposure leads to the production of reactive oxygen species in M. tuberculosis. M. tuberculosis was exposed to compounds for 90 min; ROS was detected using the fluorescent dye dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate. (A) Compound alone. (B) Compound plus 5 mM thiourea. Data are the mean and SD of two independent experiments.

We had previously noted that the generation of ROS by econazole was not linked to bacterial death.11 We tested whether this was the case for ROS generated by 8HQ exposure. We determined MICs in the presence of the ROS scavengers TEMPOL and thiourea, which scavenge superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, respectively. The addition of TEMPOL had no effect on the 8HQ activity. In contrast, the addition of thiourea reduced the potency of both molecules by 5-fold (Table 1). We excluded the possibility that thiourea was forming a complex with 8HQ in the medium via UV–vis spectrometry (Figure S9). We measured ROS generation in the presence of thiourea and found that it completely abrogated the 8HQ-dependent generation of ROS (Figure 5B). These data support the hypothesis that hydroxyl radicals are involved in the mechanism of action of the 8HQs. Interestingly, when we looked for ROS after 24 h of exposure to 8HQs, we did not see any signal (Figure S8). This suggests that, although the production of ROS is transient, it is sufficient to cause cellular damage, leading to loss of cell growth/viability. Since thiourea resulted in a complete lack of ROS induction, but molecules still inhibited growth, ROS-induced damage is not the sole mechanism of action.

8-Hydroxyquinolines Do Not Intercalate into DNA

Since 8HQs are planar molecules, we explored the possibility of DNA intercalation and damage as a mechanism of 8HQ-mediated cell death. We incubated the 8HQs with linear plasmid DNA and subjected this to gel electrophoresis; intercalation should lead to an increased size and decreased mobility in the gel. We did not see any evidence of intercalation into linearized DNA by either 8HQ (Figure 6). In the presence of the positive control, cisplatin, there was a small increase in the DNA size. Thus, we excluded the possibility that 8HQs could cause DNA damage by intercalation.

Metalloenzyme Hypomorphs Are More Sensitive to 8HQs

We used PROSPECT (PRimary screening Of Strains to Prioritize Expanded Chemistry and Targets) to determine the mode of action of the 8HQs. Using this technology, we tested a pool of >400 recombinant M. tuberculosis strains where each strain is depleted for one protein target. We identified hypomorph strains with increased sensitivity to the 8HQ analogues (Table 2 and Figure 7). The observed pattern did not share any obvious similarities to other known targets and was classified as “specific but undetermined”. For TPN-0086910, a single strain was sensitized, the galE1 (UDP galactose-4-epimerase) hypomorph. This strain appeared more sensitive at only a single concentration (6.25 μM), so the effect was not strong. For TPN-0086937, several strains were sensitized, although galE1 was not one of them. Two of the top three sensitized hypomorphs were Ppm1 (polyprenol monophosphomannose synthase) and AdoK (adenosine kinase); both are Mg2+ metalloenzymes which could be affected by copper displacement of the Mg2+ ion. The β carbonic anhydrase Zn2+-metalloenzyme (CanB) hypomorph was similarly sensitive. NrdH and NrdF2 hypomorphs were more sensitive; NrdH is involved in the biogenesis of iron–sulfur clusters, and NrdF2 is a redox-active protein that converts thioredoxin disulfide to thioredoxin dithiol.

Since several metalloenzyme hypomorphs were sensitized and they are from different cellular processes, we do not believe that these are targets of the 8HQs, but instead that this signature is indicative of disruption of metal ion homeostasis. It is unlikely that 8HQs directly bind to all of these proteins to directly disrupt their function but rather that the strains are more broadly sensitive to a disruption of metal ion homeostasis, i.e., a change in the relative levels of metal ions. These data are consistent with our proposed mechanism that 8HQs kill M. tuberculosis via disruption of metal ion homeostasis by acting as copper ionophores.

Discussion

Our data indicate that the activity of 8HQ and its analogues are copper-dependent and that the molecules act as ionophores according to the definition of Ding and Lind.8 Previous work in other organisms had demonstrated metal ion chelation activity and biological activity resulting from the reduced availability of metal ions to catalyze the Fenton reaction (and generating ROS). If 8HQs acted as chelators in M. tuberculosis, then we would expect to see their activity abrogated by the addition of copper. We see an increase in intracellular copper levels, both as free ions and as total copper content, and the generation of ROS. This suggests that the 8HQs are unable to act as chelators once inside the bacilli; this may be reflective of a different redox environment, which changes their properties, or the presence of reductases or other enzymes capable of stripping copper out of the weak complex. The ionophore activity was specific for copper, and 8HQs did not affect the uptake or intracellular levels of other metal ions. Our data are consistent with a previous study using an avirulent, auxotrophic, ESX1 deletion strain of M. tuberculosis in which copper potentiated 8HQ activity and resulted in increased intracellular free copper ions.7 Our data confirm that this also occurs in a fully virulent, prototrophic strain of M. tuberculosis, which retains all five functional Esx secretion systems. The latter is important since Esx3 is known to be involved in iron and zinc homeostasis in M. tuberculosis, and the avirulent strain has a deletion of the entire Esx1 system.

The increase in intracellular copper mediated by 8HQs led to transient production of ROS. Scavenging of hydroxyl radicals reduced the activity of 8HQs and reduced the production of ROS, suggesting that at least part of the mechanism of action is via ROS-mediated cellular damage.

The sensitivity of several hypomorphs in Mg2+ metalloenzymes and redox proteins supports the mechanism of copper-mediated toxicity and the disruption of metal ion homeostasis. The excess of copper is likely to impact many metabolic processes since there are many metalloenzymes that could be affected, and it is known that excess copper can disrupt the function of proteins with Fe–S clusters.12−14 Copper disrupts proteins containing Fe–S clusters as well as proteins involved in Fe–S biogenesis and assembly. In Bacillus subtilis(14) and Escherichia coli, excessive copper leads to upregulation of genes involved in Fe–S biogenesis. In B. subtilis, copper stress destabilizes SufU required for the maturation of Fe–S clusters and in E. coli, the Fe–S clusters of dehydratases proteins are major targets of copper toxicity. We expect that a similar disruption of Fe–S cluster biogenesis and assembly would happen in M. tuberculosis.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that 8HQs function in M. tuberculosis as copper ionophores, resulting in increased intracellular copper, the production of ROS, and the disruption of metal ion homeostasis. Thus, their mechanism of action is mediated via copper toxicity.

Methods

Bacterial Culture

M. tuberculosis H37Rv London Pride (ATCC)15 was cultured in 7H9-Tw-OADC (Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% Middlebrook OADC Enrichment and 0.5% w/v Tween 80) or in Sauton’s medium prepared in plastic (1.33 g/L l-asparagine, 0.66 g/L citric acid, 0.177 g/L potassium diphosphate, 0.056 g/L disodium phosphate, 0.035 g/L sodium chloride, 0.833 g/L Tween 80) plus metal ions as indicated.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

MICs were determined in 96-well plates as previously described.16 Compounds were prepared as 10-point, 2-fold dilutions in DMSO. Metal ions or ammonium tetra thiomolybdate (TTM) were prepared as an 8-point, 2-fold dilution in the medium. Compounds were added to assay plates using a Tecan D300e dispenser. Mid log phase M. tuberculosis was harvested and adjusted to an OD590 of 0.06; 35 μL of the culture was inoculated into 65 μL of 7H9-Tw-OADC in 96-well plates. Plates were incubated for 5 days at 37 °C and OD590 read using a Synergy H1 plate reader. Positive and negative controls were included on each plate for maximum and minimum growth and used to determine % growth for each experimental well. The MIC was defined as the concentration at which >90% growth inhibition occurred. BLISS analysis was conducted using Combenefit software.

Determination of Intracellular Cu2+ Concentration

Mid log phase M. tuberculosis cultures were harvested, washed twice with metal-free Sauton’s medium, and resuspended to an OD590 of 0.5. Metal ions and/or compounds were added to 5 mL aliquots of culture and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h.

To measure the intracellular Cu2+ concentration, cells were washed twice and resuspended in 1 mL of metal-free Sauton’s medium and lysed using a bead beater set at 6.0 m/s speed, 2 cycles of 30 s. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation and lysates were filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. Lysates (100 μL) were dispensed into black, clear bottom, 96-well plates, and PhenGreen SK (Invitrogen) was added to 0.2 μM. Plates were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark, and fluorescence was measured at 502/532 nm (Ex/Em). Cu2+ was determined from a standard curve.

To measure the total intracellular metal ion concentration, cultures were harvested, washed twice with metal-free Sauton’s medium plus 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), washed once with metal-free Sauton’s medium, and resuspended in 1 mL of Sauton’s medium. Bacteria were lysed using a bead beater set at 6.0 m/s speed, 2 cycles of 30 s. The supernatant and wash fractions were retained for analysis. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation, and lysates were filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. Lysates (1 mL) were treated with 100 μL of Trace Metal grade Nitric Acid (Sigma) at RT for 30 min, 50 μL of concn, HCl was added, and samples were frozen on dry ice.

For ICP-MS analysis, the Agilent 7850 ICP-MS instrument was set up using the standard quartz sample introduction system configuration, consisting of a MicroMist nebulizer, Scott-type spray chamber, and one-piece torch with a 2.5 mm id injector. Nickel sampling and skimmer cones were used. Sampling was facilitated by the Agilent SPS4 autosampler; internal standards were added online via the sample delivery peristaltic pump and mixing. The instrument was optimized using default autotune conditions with a low matrix setting. Samples were measured in helium collision mode for optimum interference removal while maintaining excellent sensitivities. The settings used for operation were: 1550 W forward power, 8 mm sampling depth, 1.05 L/min carrier gas, 400 μL/min solution uptake rate, 2 °C spray chamber temperature, and 4.6 mL/min Helium cell gas flow rate. Samples were diluted with 2% HNO3/0.5% HCl. The Agilent 7850 ICP-MS was calibrated with multiple standards from 0 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 ppb for all analytes (Cu, Zn, Mn, Mg, Fe, Ca) followed by the treated samples for elemental analysis.

Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

M. tuberculosis was grown to an OD590 of 0.8, harvested and resuspended in 7H9-Tw at an OD590 of 1.0. 40 μM of dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate was added, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were washed and resuspended in 7H9-Tw and exposed to the compounds for 90 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence was measured at 485/535 nm (Ex/Em).

CtpV Expression

Mid log phase M. tuberculosis was harvested, washed twice in metal-free Sauton’s medium, and resuspended at a final OD590 of 0.5. Cultures were exposed to compounds for 1 h, harvested, and resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol. Cell lysates were generated using a beater at 6.0 m/s speed, 40 s for one cycle, cell debris was removed by centrifugation, and the lysate was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. Samples were extracted with chloroform-isopropanol extraction, and RNA was precipitated with ethanol. DNA was removed using RNase-free DNAase and purified using the RNeasy RNA purification kit (Qiagen). The BioRad iScript cDNA synthesis kit was used to convert 100 ng of RNA template to cDNA. No reverse transcriptase control was included for each sample. Samples for RT-PCR were prepared by using the Qiagen Quantinova Probe PCR kit. Thermo Fisher Custom gene Taqman assay probe-primer mix for ctpV (Rv0969) was used for targeting the ctpV gene. sigA gene was used as a reference gene. RT-PCR was performed on Bio-Rad CFX Connect. The CT (cycle threshold) value for ctpV and sigA expression each condition was determined and converted to its respective copy number with the help of a CT vs Copy number standard curve for M. tuberculosis genomic DNA. The ratio of the copy numbers was determined to calculate normalized expression.

DNA Intercalation Assay

pUC19 plasmid was linearized with BamHI. 100 ng portion of linear pUC19 was incubated with compounds in a final volume of 25 μL of buffer Tris pH 8.0 at 37 °C for 1 h. DNA was run on 0.8% agarose gel at 100 V for 1 h in 1× TAE and stained after electrophoresis with 3× GelRed (Biotium).

Hypomorph (PROSPECT) Screen

Compounds were tested against a pool of 400 hypomorph strains as described.17 Briefly, a dose response covering 8-point dilutions was tested against the library. Strains were grown aerobically, harvested at the mid log phase, and resuspended in 7H9-Tw-OADC plus acetate and 1 μg/mL ATc (theoretical OD600 of 0.0075). Cultures were incubated for 14 days and heated to lyse; genomic DNA was amplified and Illumina sequenced. The growth ratio (GR) for each hypomorph was calculated by normalizing to the maximum growth control (DMSO alone) as previously described.17
