Evaluation of antimicrobial efficacy of two commercially available probiotics against Enterococcus faecalis: An in vitro study
Authors: A. Arvind Kumar, S. Santhoshi, J. S. Beautlin, K. T. Manoj, N. J. Nagaraj, R S Mohan Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:The antimicrobial activity of probiotics determined by colony-forming units when used against Enterococcus faecalis thereby can be used as an alternative intracanal medicament in root canal therapy.Aim:To evaluate the two commercially available probiotics antibacterial effectiveness against E. faecalis in root canal treatment.Settings and Design:In vitro study.Subjects and Methods:Sixty-two extracted mandibular premolars were decoronated and instrumented up to ProTaper Gold F2 (Dentsply, USA) using 2.5% sodium hypochlorite irrigation followed by 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. Samples were autoclaved and inoculated with the test organism E. faecalis (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 29212) for 7 days, kept in a sealed container at 37°C. Sixty samples were divided into four groups (n = 15): Group 1 – Cyraflora (Saccharomyces boulardii), Group 2 – Econorm (Bacillus species), Group 3 – 2% chlorhexidine gel, and Group 4 – calcium hydroxide. Then, the respective intracanal medicaments were placed inside the root canal space and sealed with glass ionomer cement (GIC). After 7 days, coronal GIC was removed, irrigated, dried, and dentinal scrappings were taken by using H files, and bacterial counts were evaluated.Statistical Analysis:One-way analysis of variance was used to examine the data, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for intergroup comparisons.Results:The findings demonstrated the efficacy of probiotics S. boulardii and Bacillus species against E. faecalis, with a decrease in colony-forming units following probiotic use.Conclusion:As an alternate intracanal medication, probiotics can be utilized to effectively combat E. faecalis.

INTRODUCTION

Three key elements are essential for successful root canal therapy – thorough debridement, effective disinfection, and three-dimensional obturation of the root canal system. Despite meticulous clinical procedures, failures may still occur due to factors such as persistent periapical infections, residual cysts, and foreign body reactions.[1] Numerous studies have demonstrated that the persistence of microorganisms within the root canal system is the primary cause of endodontic treatment failure.[1]

Among the microorganisms associated with failed root canal treatments, Enterococcus faecalis is the most frequently isolated pathogen. It is a Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic bacterium known for its remarkable ability to survive in adverse environmental conditions. E. faecalis can endure high alkaline pH levels, resist nutrient deprivation, and survive prolonged periods of starvation. Within the root canal system, it has the ability to exist as a single organism and form biofilms independently, without the need for synergistic microbial support.[2] Its ability to penetrate dentinal tubules, adhere to collagen, and form biofilms significantly enhances its resistance to conventional intracanal medicaments, making complete eradication challenging.

Conventional intracanal medicaments such as calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) and chlorhexidine (CHX) are widely used to eliminate residual microorganisms. However, their effectiveness against E. faecalis is often limited due to the buffering capacity of dentin, restricted penetration into dentinal tubules, and microbial resistance mechanisms. These limitations highlight the need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies to improve root canal disinfection outcomes.[3]

Probiotics have emerged as a promising biological approach in recent years. Probiotics are defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts. The term “probiotic,” meaning “for life,” was first introduced by Lilley and Stillwell in 1965.[4] Unlike conventional antimicrobial agents that aim to eliminate microorganisms indiscriminately, probiotics act by modulating the microbial ecosystem. They inhibit pathogenic microorganisms through mechanisms such as competitive exclusion for nutrients and adhesion sites, production of antimicrobial substances such as bacteriocins and organic acids, and disruption of biofilm formation.[5]

Probiotics have been widely used in the medical and dental fields, particularly for reducing plaque accumulation and preventing periodontal diseases and dental caries. However, their application as intracanal medicaments in endodontics remains relatively unexplored. Certain probiotic strains, such as Saccharomyces boulardii and Bacillus species, have shown antimicrobial potential against oral pathogens, including E. faecalis, by interfering with biofilm formation and reducing bacterial virulence.[5]

Therefore, considering the limitations of conventional intracanal medicaments and the emerging potential of probiotics, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of two commercially available probiotics (S. boulardii and Bacillus species) against E. faecalis.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design

This in vitro experimental study was conducted to evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of two commercially available probiotics against E. faecalis.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined based on previous similar in vitro studies evaluating the antimicrobial efficacy of intracanal medicaments against E. faecalis, ensuring adequate statistical power and reliability of results.[5] A total of 62 extracted human mandibular premolars were included in the study.

Sample selection and preparation

Sixty-two freshly extracted human mandibular premolars with single straight canals and fully formed apices were selected. Teeth with caries, cracks, restorations, or anatomical anomalies were excluded.

The teeth were cleaned of soft tissue debris and stored in 0.1% thymol solution until use. Standardization of root length was achieved by decoronating the teeth to 16 mm from the apex using a diamond disc (NMDNEXUS Medodent, India) under water cooling.

Working length was established by inserting a #10 K-file into the canal until visible at the apical foramen and subtracting 1 mm. Biomechanical preparation was performed using the ProTaper Gold rotary system (Dentsply Sirona, USA) up to size F2.

Irrigation protocol

During instrumentation, canals were irrigated with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite to dissolve organic tissue and provide antimicrobial action. After completion of instrumentation, a final rinse was performed using 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 1 min to remove the smear layer, followed by saline irrigation to eliminate any residual chemicals.[678]

Sterilization of samples

All prepared samples were placed in sterile containers and autoclaved at 121°C for 15 min to ensure complete sterilization. Sterility was confirmed by incubating selected samples in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth and checking for turbidity.

Microbial inoculation

A standard strain of E. faecalis (ATCC 29212) was used. The bacterial suspension was prepared in BHI broth and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard.

All 62 samples were inoculated with the bacterial suspension and incubated at 37°C in a sealed environment for 7 days to allow biofilm formation. Fresh broth was replenished periodically to maintain bacterial viability.[9]

After incubation, 60 samples were selected and randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 15). The remaining two samples were used to confirm biofilm formation.

Preparation and placement of intracanal medicaments

Probiotic preparations (Cyraflora and Econorm) were obtained, and the contents were dissolved in sterile distilled water to obtain a uniform suspension. Approximately 30 µL of the prepared probiotic solution was introduced into the canals.

Similarly, 2% CHX gel and Ca(OH)2 paste were used as intracanal medicaments.

All medicaments were placed into the root canal space using a lentulo spiral to ensure uniform distribution. The coronal access was sealed with glass ionomer cement (GIC), and all samples were incubated at 37°C for 7 days.

Collection of samples and microbial analysis

After 7 days, the temporary restoration (GIC) was removed. The canals were irrigated with 5 mL of sterile distilled water to remove residual medicament and dried using sterile paper points (Sure-Endo, Korea).

Dentinal shavings were collected from the canal walls using sterile H-files. The collected samples were transferred to sterile culture media, and microbial analysis was performed by counting colony-forming units (CFU), which serves as a quantitative measure of viable bacteria.[10]

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the data collected from colony-forming units. When P < 0.05, the values were deemed as statistically significant. To determine whether there were any notable differences between the groups, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for the intergroup comparison of the colony-forming units. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive analysis was given in the form of mean ± standard deviation.

DISCUSSION

Achieving a fluid-impermeable barrier of the root canal system and meticulous removal of necrotic debris, diseased pulp tissue, and microorganisms are key factors for successful endodontic therapy.[10] Clinical findings and radiographic evaluation play an important role in determining treatment outcomes. However, despite advancements in endodontic procedures, persistent infections remain a major cause of failure. Siqueira reported that the persistence of microorganisms within the root canal system is the primary reason for endodontic failure.[11]

The most frequently isolated microorganism in cases of apical periodontitis is E. faecalis. It is distinguished by its ability to survive under harsh environmental conditions, including high alkaline pH levels up to 11.5, nutrient deprivation, and prolonged starvation. Evans et al. demonstrated that E. faecalis resists alkaline environments through proton pump mechanisms that maintain intracellular pH.[12] In addition, Love (2004) reported its ability to invade dentinal tubules and adhere to collagen, while Distel et al. (2002) showed that it can form biofilms independently without the need for other microorganisms. These biofilms protect the bacteria from host defenses and antimicrobial agents, making eradication difficult in clinical practice. This necessitates the development of novel decontamination strategies to improve root canal disinfection and treatment outcomes.[1314]

Ca(OH)2 is the most commonly used intracanal medicament due to its antibacterial properties. Its mechanism of action involves the release of hydroxyl ions, creating a highly alkaline environment that denatures bacterial proteins and damages cell membranes. Bystrom and Sundqvist and Sjogren et al. (1991) demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing intracanal bacterial load. It is often used alone or in combination with 2% CHX to enhance its antimicrobial efficacy. However, its effectiveness against E. faecalis is limited.[1516] Furthermore, Haapasalo and Orstavik reported that the buffering capacity of dentin reduces the antimicrobial effectiveness of Ca(OH)2. Residual Ca(OH)2 may also interfere with the setting reaction of zinc oxide eugenol-based sealers. These limitations highlight the need for alternative intracanal medicaments.[17]

CHX has emerged as a promising alternative due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, substantivity, and biocompatibility. Mohammadi and Abbott (2009) reported that CHX is effective against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms.[18] Its bacteriostatic and bactericidal action is attributed to its cationic nature, which allows it to bind to negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, leading to leakage of intracellular components, as described by Basrani et al. (2004).[19] Also demonstrated its effectiveness against E. faecalis. However, CHX lacks tissue-dissolving ability and may not completely eliminate microorganisms within deeper dentinal tubules or mature biofilms.[19]

Probiotics have recently gained attention as a novel biological approach in endodontics. They help restore microbial balance by promoting beneficial microorganisms and inhibiting pathogenic bacteria. Their mechanism of action includes competitive inhibition for nutrients and adhesion sites, production of antimicrobial substances such as bacteriocins, organic acids, and hydrogen peroxide, and disruption of biofilm formation.[20]

Several studies support the antimicrobial activity of probiotics against E. faecalis. Koll-Klais et al. (2005) demonstrated that Lactobacillus species inhibit the growth of E. faecalis.[21] Hasslöf et al. (2010) reported that probiotics produce bacteriocins that suppress pathogenic bacteria,[22] while Strus et al. (2001) showed that organic acids produced by probiotics create an unfavorable environment for bacterial survival.[23] Furthermore, Ravi et al. (2023) evaluated commercially available probiotics and observed a significant reduction in CFU counts of E. faecalis, which is consistent with the findings of the present study.[5]

The antimicrobial effect of probiotics may also be attributed to their ability to disrupt biofilms. Vuotto et al. (2014) reported that probiotics interfere with biofilm formation and reduce microbial virulence.[24] In addition, Plaza-Diaz et al. (2019) explained that probiotic-derived metabolites such as lipoteichoic acid and bacteriocins inhibit bacterial adhesion and biofilm development. This explains the reduction in CFU observed in the present study following probiotic application.[25]

CFU analysis is a reliable method for quantifying viable bacteria. In the present study, CFU results showed a significant reduction in bacterial count following probiotic application, confirming their antimicrobial potential in an in vitro environment.

The findings of this study suggest that probiotics can serve as potential adjuncts or alternatives to conventional intracanal medicaments. Unlike Ca(OH)2, which E. faecalis can resist, probiotics offer a multifactorial mechanism targeting bacterial adhesion, growth, and biofilm formation.[26] However, as noted by Meurman and Stamatova (2009), the effectiveness of probiotics depends on factors such as strain type, concentration, and environmental conditions.[27]

Further in vivo studies and clinical trials are required to validate the clinical applicability of probiotics in endodontic therapy.

CONCLUSION

Based on the results of the in-vitro study, it can be concluded that probiotic-based approach for decontamination of the root canal system is equally effective when compared to Ca(OH)2 group intracanal medicament against E. faecalis.
