Integrated Geochemical Modeling of Trapping Mechanisms and Sensitivity Analysis for Optimized CO2 Sequestration in Saline Aquifers
Authors: Jemal W. Fentaw, Hossein Emadi, Heinze Lloyd, Stephen Ekwaro-Osire, Chinedu J. Okere, Dorcas S Eyinla, Abir Kebir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Geological carbon sequestration (GCS) in deep saline aquifers is a critical climate mitigation strategy, but its long-term effectiveness depends on the combined impact of structural, residual, solubility, and mineral trapping mechanisms. Despite substantial research done on individual trapping processes, integrated evaluations under realistic reservoir conditions remain limited, reducing the predictive reliability of CO2 storage performance. This study develops a high-resolution, 3D compositional model of the Strawn formation in northeast King County, Texas, using CMG-GEM software to investigate coupled physical and geochemical trapping processes. Unlike the previous studies, this model couples all four trapping mechanisms in a single framework, providing a quantitative and mechanistic understanding of CO2 fate in the subsurface. The validation of the model grid was performed using grid sensitivity analysis on selected performance parameters. A comparative analysis is presented between two-dimensional and three-dimensional modeling approaches to highlight the importance of spatial dimensionality in accurately capturing CO2 plume dynamics and assessing storage efficiency. Results show that solubility trapping, when effectively combined with structural and residual mechanisms, yields the highest amount of CO2 sequestered (43%), followed by mineral trapping (40%). A comprehensive sensitivity analysis, conducted on factors affecting such solubility trapping, demonstrates that the efficiency of the integrated trapping increases with higher injection rates, prolonged injection years, higher porosity, elevated pressures, lower temperatures, and reduced vertical and horizontal permeabilities. These findings offer crucial insights for optimizing reservoir and operational parameters to improve containment and long-term storage. The integrated modeling approach presented here offers a robust and transferable framework for evaluating and designing site-specific carbon storage systems on a scale.

Introduction

As the urgency to curb global greenhouse gas emissions increases, geological carbon sequestration (GCS) has emerged as a pivotal solution for achieving decarbonization and net-zero climate targets by storing CO2 in deep geological reservoirs, including saline aquifers, depleted oil and gas reservoirs, basalt, and unmineable coal seams. − Among these geological storage options, deep saline aquifers and depleted reservoirs are widely recognized for their favorable characteristics. Deep saline aquifers have a vast global availability, making them accessible near emission sources, have high volumetric capacities, and are compatible with multiple CO2 immobilization pathways. Unlike depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, saline aquifers are not constrained by prior fluid production, making them highly scalable for long-term storage initiatives. Depleted reservoirs, on the other hand, have proven integrity as they hold hydrocarbons for millions of years, offer large storage capacity, and have existing infrastructures for injection and monitoring, which reduces the capital costs. −

However, the effectiveness and security of CO2 sequestration in such systems are governed by the interplay of physical and chemical trapping mechanisms, which include structural trapping, capillary or residual gas trapping, solubility or dissolution trapping, and mineralization trapping, each contributing uniquely across spatial and temporal scales. −

Structural trapping, also called stratigraphic or hydrodynamic trapping, is the physical containment of the injected CO2 beneath an impermeable layer of rock or caprock. It is often the first line of containment for the buoyant CO2 as it moves upward due to its lower density compared to formation water, especially at the early phase of storage or before other trapping mechanisms come into play, as it sequesters large amounts of CO2 quickly. This type of trapping mechanism relies on the existence of impermeable caprock layers, salt domes, faults, or folds that form geological closures to physically restrict the upward migration of buoyant CO2. − Since supercritical CO2 is less dense (0.6–0.7 g/cm3) than formation water (1–1.05 g/cm3), the compromised caprock will lead to a CO2 leakage when the CO2 migrates or rises due to buoyancy. ,, Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the sealing capacity of the caprock, seismicity induced by injection, and factors that enhance this trapping mechanism, as well as to develop a robust monitoring technique for effective structural trapping.

In addition to the containment of CO2 through structural barriers, residual or capillary trapping enhances the immobilization of CO2. While structural trapping provides the initial containment by physically preventing upward migration of CO2, residual trapping retains the disconnected, nonbuoyant portions of the CO2 plume within the pore network. In this trapping mechanism, the injected CO2 initially displaces the brine water. Then, when the injection stops, CO2 continues to migrate upward, while the brine descends due to the density difference. This will lead to CO2 being trapped in the pore throats of the rock or in the pore spaces as immobile droplets because of the capillary forces. Residual or capillary trapping immobilizes CO2 at the pore scale through snap-off and capillary pressure effects, even in systems with partial caprock integrity or postinjection pressure fluctuations, and 49–83% of the initial CO2 saturation can be stored. ,

Solubility trapping refers to the dissolution of CO2 into formation brine, resulting in a denser CO2-rich aqueous phase that suppresses buoyant rise and stabilizes the deeper plume. Once CO2 is dissolved in the formation brine, it will not leave the solution unless there is a significant pressure drop. ,− The solubility trapping reduces the mobility of CO2 within the reservoir and enhances storage security because of CO2 dissolution. CO2 solubility is the main limiting factor for the capacity of such trapping. Thus, enhancing the solubility and credibly predicting the interaction between CO2 and formation minerals will improve solubility trapping.

Mineral trapping, on the other hand, is the most permanent but slowest mechanism, resulting from geochemical reactions that convert dissolved CO2 into stable carbonate minerals over geological time scales. , During mineral trapping, CO2 will be converted into solid carbonate minerals, such as magnesite or calcite, through natural mineralization processes. Once the CO2 comes into contact with the brine, it forms carbonic acid (H2CO3) or acidifies the formation fluid. H2CO3 is a weak acid that is ready to dissociate immediately. Rocks that are good candidates for mineral trapping include basalt, carbonate, shale, and sandstone formations containing feldspars and clay minerals, as clay minerals release cations during dissolution. ,

The other method for CO2 storage involves storing it in a hydrate form, where CO2 is injected into high-pressure and low-temperature porous formations containing water, which are conducive to the formation and stability of CO2 hydrates. There are different mechanisms of CO2 sequestration through gas hydrates, which are either through direct CO2 hydrate formation or through CO2–CH4 exchange. The direct CO2 hydrate formation occurs by injecting CO2 into hydrate-forming environments, including deep ocean water, subseafloor saline formations, subpermafrost region, and depleted formations that are partially saturated with water. The CO2–CH4 exchange mechanism is where the CO2 injected into a natural gas hydrate reservoir is sequestered while producing methane simultaneously. The method of CO2 sequestration in gas hydrates is a relatively emerging mechanism that involves a dual-phase trapping process, as CO2 is converted into a solid crystalline structure, and can also be dissolved in the formation water. Researchers have been investigating various mechanisms to enhance hydrate formation. These include a study by Li et al. done on CO2 sequestration via underlying methane hydrate production, where CO2 hydrate sequestration is coupled with methane production. According to this study, CO2 can be injected into a layer of sediments that exists above methane hydrates, creating a dual horizontal well arrangement that enhances CO2 hydrate formation by creating a cooling effect during methane hydrate dissociation. Injecting CO2 into the overlying sediment also reduces the risk of leakage by creating a secondary sealing mechanism. Another study by Hu and Xiao also discussed methods for enhancing CO2 hydrate formations, which include thermodynamic methods that utilize additives to reduce the temperature and pressure required for hydrate formation, as well as kinetic methods to enhance the interaction between gas and water.

Considering the multifaceted interplay of all the trapping processes, a growing number of studies have employed advanced machine learning (ML) and hybrid models to improve the prediction of storage performance, predict CO2 trapping efficiency, and identify dominant parameters influencing storage capacity. These include Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Support Vector Regression (SVR), decision tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), and Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN). These models, trained on large numerical simulation data sets, can emulate multiphase flow behavior with high precision and dramatically reduced computation time, facilitating uncertainty analysis and site screening for large-scale geologic carbon storage projects. − The KAN and KAN_MLP models achieved the highest accuracy (R 2 = 1), confirming that CO2 storage capacity is the most influential parameter (81.12%), while depth contributes least (13.28%). CO2 capacity also correlates negatively with chemical composition and positively with pore size and surface area, emphasizing structural control on adsorption. Further, models like XGBoost, RF, and SVR demonstrated excellent accuracy in predicting CO2 trapping in saline aquifers (R 2 = 0.9993). Simulation-based studies compared gas-downhole water sink-assisted gravity drainage (GDWS-AGD) and Gas-Assisted Gravity Drainage (GAGD) processes, showing superior oil recovery and reduced water cut for CO2-based GDWS-AGD. Proxy ML models, such as RBF (Radial Basis Function) networks, enhanced CO2 trapping from 75 to 83% with minimal leakage. Gradient boosting and Least Squares Support Vector Machine (LS-SVM) models further improved the accuracy and reliability of solubility and residual trapping predictions. , While primarily applied to underground natural gas storage (UNGS), hybrid artificial intelligence models combining LSSVM, artificial neural networks (ANN), and metaheuristic optimization have demonstrated exceptional performance in predicting deliverability and injectivity. Their success suggests strong potential for adaptation to CO2 storage operations, where similar physical parameters govern injection rate, formation pressure, and storage integrity. −

Field-oriented studies, such as the S3 reservoir and Tahe oilfield in China, have developed comprehensive efficiency-modeling frameworks to quantify the CO2 storage potential under realistic geological conditions. These models incorporate reservoir heterogeneity, multimechanism trapping, and uncertainty evaluation, providing a more accurate estimation of CO2 storage efficiency and capacity in complex carbonate systems. ,

Moreover, since CO2–brine interfacial tension (IFT) directly influences capillary pressure and residual trapping, several robust ML-based frameworks have been proposed to predict IFT under varying pressure, temperature, and salinity conditions. Hybrid models integrating optimization algorithms (e.g., Particle Swarm Optimization, PSO, and Genetic Algorithm, GA) with regression or neural architectures have achieved high predictive accuracy, enabling better assessment of structural and residual trapping potential in diverse saline systems. − Wettability is another key factor controlling CO2 mobility and residual trapping. Recent innovations employ deep learning and hybrid intelligent models to predict dynamic contact angles as functions of mineralogy, pressure, temperature, and brine composition. Such models improve the prediction of CO2 distribution and trapping capacity by capturing the time-dependent wettability alterations observed in laboratory and field studies. ,

Moreover, effective monitoring is essential in CCS projects for verifying CO2 containment, evaluating storage integrity, and detecting potential leakages early. Seismic and nonseismic geophysical methods, including Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR), microseismic monitoring, gravity, electrical, and electromagnetic techniques such as Controlled-Source Electromagnetic (CSEM) and Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT), are widely used to track CO2 plume migration from deep reservoirs to the surface. Seismic monitoring methods are postclosure monitoring techniques used to track the movement of CO2 within the reservoir. Advancing automated monitoring systems capable of detecting anomalies and initiating rapid responses remains critical for the safe and efficient implementation of CO2 geological storage. ,−

Overall, these approaches accelerate CCS research, optimize injection strategies, and enhance confidence in storage.

While the individual characteristics and benefits of these mechanisms are well-documented in published research, the interactions among them, especially under field-representative conditions, remain insufficiently explored. Most prior studies have focused on one or two trapping processes, overlooking the comprehensive sensitivity analysis of operational and geological factors that affect the performance of the storage system. − Some including investigated the solubility trapping using a multiphase-reactive transport model by monitoring the pH variation, pressure distribution, and CO2 migration patterns. Others, including, , studied the residual trapping mechanism, focusing on its sensitivity to various factors, and quantifying and analyzing residual saturation in a saline aquifer using a 2D glass model and numerical simulation. This creates a significant gap in our ability to predict CO2 behavior, assess storage security, and design optimal injection strategies for real-world reservoirs. No research has been conducted on the site-specific behavior of these trapping mechanisms and the physical and geological factors in the Strawn formation.

To address this challenge, the present study develops a fully compositional, high-resolution 3D numerical model of the Strawn Formation, a sandstone aquifer located in the northeastern part of King County, Texas. This formation presents a realistic testbed due to its favorable depth, pressure, temperature, and rock-fluid properties. Using the CMG-GEM simulator, we implement a multimechanism modeling approach that captures the dynamic coupling of structural, residual, mineral, and solubility trapping, enabling the simulation of CO2 injection, migration, and immobilization over decadal time scales. Unlike previous studies, we combine all four trapping mechanisms into a single trapping framework under realistic field-scale conditions. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no prior research was carried out to investigate CO2 trapping behavior in this particular formation. This reinforces the novelty of our study, as it demonstrates how the assessment and quantification of multimechanism trapping can be conducted in a realistic geological environment.

Beyond model development, this study conducts a systematic sensitivity analysis to quantify the influence of key operational and geological parameters, including injection rate, reservoir pressure and temperature, porosity, and permeability, on the effectiveness of solubility trapping within the integrated system. These insights are crucial for refining site selection criteria, designing safer injection operations, and enhancing the predictive accuracy of long-term sequestration performance.

Ultimately, this work contributes to a deeper, field-scale understanding of how multiple trapping mechanisms interact synergistically under realistic subsurface conditions, filling a critical knowledge gap and providing a robust framework for optimizing CO2 storage in saline aquifers. It advances the science of GCS modeling and its practical application in climate mitigation strategies on a scale.

Methodology

Geochemical modeling of GCS requires a robust understanding of static and dynamic reservoir behavior. Static models establish the initial framework by characterizing fluid and rock properties, which are generally assumed to remain constant during CO2 injection. These models incorporate data from well logs, relative permeability curves, and seismic surveys. In contrast, dynamic models simulate time-dependent processes such as CO2 plume migration, changes in reservoir pressure, fluid saturation, and phase behavior. Accurate modeling depends on clearly defined conceptual site models, comprehensive reservoir data, an understanding of dominant flow regimes (viscous versus capillary), and the appropriate selection of modeling approaches, whether equilibrium-based, solubility-controlled, or kinetically driven.

In alignment with these principles, this study employs the CMG-GEM 2024 software developed by the Computer Modeling Group (CMG). CMG-GEM is a leading compositional simulator to perform integrated geochemical modeling of CO2 injection and trapping behavior. It accurately simulates multicomponent phase behavior, CO2 solubility in brine, geochemical reactions with minerals, as well as multiphase flow in porous media. It captures the fluid properties and system phase behavior variations as a function of pressure, temperature, and composition in both conventional and unconventional reservoirs. CMG-GEM is commonly used in CO2 storage research and is widely validated and accepted in industry for CCS projects.

The modeling framework captures static and dynamic processes using input data derived from the Strawn formation in King County, North Texas.

Effective CO2 storage requires a systematic and well-characterized site selection process. This begins with regional assessments and progresses to detailed evaluations using geological, technical, environmental, and economic criteria. Site characterization is iterative, combining existing and newly acquired data throughout all project stagesfrom selection to postclosureto evaluate geology, hydrogeology, rock properties, faults, and well integrity. Advanced frameworks, including multicriteria decision-making (MCDM) and numerical modeling of multiphase reactive flow, help assess storage performance under varying conditions and uncertainties. Integrating regional screening with advanced site-specific analyses ensures safe, efficient, and reliable long-term CO2 storage in formations such as saline aquifers or depleted reservoirs. −

The saline aquifer in King County, which is employed in this study, is illustrated in the red circle in Figure . It is part of the nonproducing Strawn Formation located in the northeastern region of the county. The site was selected due to its proximity to the 690 MW Oklaunion coal-fired power plant, which provides a practical source of anthropogenic CO2. Additionally, a substantial portion of the Strawn formation exhibits moderate-to-high porosity, favorable permeability, being a sandstone lithology, and considerable formation thickness, making it a suitable candidate for long-term CO2 sequestration. The primary reservoirs in this field are the Strawn sandstone and limestone of Permian age, specifically Strawn 5400’ sand and Strawn 5400’ lime. This potential storage site is simulated in the current study to examine the various trapping mechanisms under homogeneous reservoir conditions. A single-permeability approach is adopted, with fractures not explicitly considered.

Saline aquifer in the Strawn formation, King County (modified after ref ).

This study adopts static modeling, which involves grid setup, fluid characterization, and calibration of the static model, followed by consideration of boundary conditions. The study employs a homogeneous reservoir average porosity and permeability distribution model with realistic vertical layering to mimic structural layering and conduct a quantitative analysis of structural, residual, solubility, and residual trappings. The study then investigates the sensitivity of these trapping mechanisms to key factors that affect them. The reservoir is assumed to be homogeneous, and no explicit modeling of fractures or fault networks is provided, which makes it possible to emphasize the quantification of the relative role of the four trapping mechanisms in a controlled environment. Nevertheless, we do not overlook that heterogeneity and natural fractures may have a strong effect on CO2 migration and trapping efficiency in the conditions of a real field. This is because heterogeneous formations have the potential to greatly enhance the CO2 storage rate and decrease the likelihood of leakages by scattering the injected CO2 across a larger volume of rock, therefore reducing the constant buoyant gaseous column on capillary seals. Thus, heterogeneity, like other geological, hydrodynamic, economic, and regulatory criteria, is one of the important criteria for site selection for CO2 storage.

Model Generation

The model construction began by defining a representative section of the field, using the petrophysical data and geometrical characteristics of the formation. It is a homogeneous model using average permeability and porosity. The dimensions of the reservoir used for this model are 31,300 ft L × 21,000 ft W × 200 ft H (9.54 km L × 6.4 km W × 60.9m H). The dimensions are derived from geological mapping and detailed reservoir characterization of the injectable reservoir, whereby the areal extent is defined by the boundary of the four-way dip course, indicating the structural trap suitable for containment. The vertical dimension, or height, is represented by the net pay thickness, determined from intervals exhibiting acceptable porosity and permeability.

The size of each grid block in the lateral directions is 62m, and there are 15 layers of variable thickness in the Z direction to account for the geometric closure required mainly for structural trapping. A very low permeability (1e –6 mD) is assigned to the top layer, which acts as a caprock to mimic vertical sealing. A similar layer is assumed at the bottom of the reservoirs, which means that the top and bottom boundaries of the reservoirs are closed or no-flow boundaries. The reservoir is also laterally confined, and its spatial limits were obtained through geological mapping and structural interpretation of the formation boundaries. Thus, the total grid size will be 154 in X, 103 in Y, and 15 layers in the Z direction, giving a total grid of 237,930. The 3-dimensional model created using CMG-Builder 2024 is shown in Figure below.

3D representation of the reservoir model.

The reference depth for the reservoir is 1636 m (5368 ft), with an initial reservoir pressure of 12 MPa (1740 Psi), and the water–gas contact (DWGC) at 1580 m (5184 ft). CO2 is injected into six perforations at layers 8–13 in the Z-direction. The base case model assumes an injection rate of 1 MtCO2/year, which is the surface gas rate (selected based on the injection rate of global CCS projects and literature), and at an injection pressure of 27 MPa (3916 Psi), with a 20-year injection period and a simulation time frame of 200 years.

The CO2 injection was conducted at a rate of 4570 m3/day for 20 years, followed by a 200-year monitoring period. The simulation results of the grid validation are presented in Figure . It is observed in this figure that coarser grids or grids with fewer active block grids (cases 1 and 2) result in a higher CO2 dissolution or overestimate the amount of CO2 dissolved, which is the amount of CO2 dissolved in the formation water (solubility trapping) over time (year, abbreviated as yr). As the grid resolution increases to case 3, the behavior of CO2 dissolved shows convergence, and expanding the refinement beyond case 5 (or 154 × 103 × 15 = 237,930 grids) does not significantly affect the predicted amount of CO2 dissolved. This phenomenon is a numerical artifact associated with the representation of the interface between CO2 and brine and phase mixing. A coarser grid enables the CO2 saturation front to be more diffused, artificially enlarging the contact area between brine and CO2 in the grid block. As calculations of phase equilibrium are done at the block scale, the simulator presumes that a greater proportion of CO2 is exposed to the brine. Thus, overestimating dissolution cannot be done on a smaller grid, which is supported by previous studies, including ref .

Results and Discussions

The simulation results are discussed in the following section, providing a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness and contributions of the four trapping mechanismsstructural, residual, solubility, and mineral trappingover time under different surface and reservoir conditions for the safe and effective geological storage of CO2. The analysis began by simulating the four trapping mechanisms individually, followed by the development of a model that combined all four. Then, the effect of each factor on these trapping mechanisms was analyzed through a sensitivity analysis.

Structural Trapping

As explained in the introduction section, structural trapping physically restricts the upward migration of injected CO2 by confining it beneath a low-permeability caprock, thereby enabling other mechanisms, including residual, solubility, and mineral trapping, to become more dominant over time.

In this model, the geometric closure for structural trapping is represented by varying the vertical grid thickness of the layers along the Z direction or along the reservoir thickness. A very low-permeability layer is placed at the top to mimic the structural high, which acts as a seal to ensure vertical containment. The study analyzed structural trapping using a three-dimensional (3D) reservoir model, with CO2 injected at a constant rate of 4570 m3/day (approximately equal to 1 million tons per annum) for 20 years. Simulation results in Figure below illustrate the evolution of the CO2 plume, which migrates upward due to buoyancy forces and accumulates beneath the caprock.

3D simulation result for gas saturation during structural trapping.

The upward migration of the supercritical CO2 plume (Figure ) underscores the critical importance of caprock properties in ensuring storage security. Specifically, the caprock must possess ultralow permeability, typically within the range of 10–18–10–21 m2, to effectively prevent leakage through pore spaces. Additionally, it is essential to have a reservoir with sufficient pore volume, which is a function of thickness, lateral extent, and porosity, to withstand long-term pressure buildup and seal integrity. In contrast, a high capillary pressure is required to counterbalance the buoyant forces exerted by the nonwetting CO2 phase. If any of these caprock characteristics are compromised, the risk of CO2 leakage to overlying formations, including underground sources of drinking water (USDW), increases substantially.

Furthermore, the result revealed that much of the CO2 plume remains confined near the caprock, with minimal lateral or downward migration due to the reservoir’s stratigraphic structure. Significant portions of the reservoir, indicated by regions with persistent brine saturation (shown in blue), remain uncontacted by CO2 throughout the injection period. This nonuniform plume distribution reinforces the effectiveness of structural confinement in concentrating the CO2 beneath the caprock.

The 3D model simulation result for the time-dependent behavior of structurally trapped CO2 is shown in Figure a. The amount of trapped CO2 increases rapidly during the active injection phase (first 20 years) and begins to plateau after injection ceases, eventually stabilizing approximately at 20 years after injection ceased. The simulation result shows that the cumulative injection of CO2 is 1.41779 × 109 mol at reservoir conditions, and the accumulated CO2 in structural trapping is 1.41688 × 109 mol. Concurrently, bottom-hole pressure (Figure b) rises throughout the injection phase and levels off after injection stops, reflecting system equilibration. Importantly, CO2 remains entirely in the supercritical phase during and after injection, as illustrated in Figure c, while it is recommended to inject CO2 at a pressure and temperature where it is supercritical. This diminishes complications associated with gaseous CO2, such as enhanced leakage potential, caprock degradation, and the need for more intensive monitoring systems. Throughout the process, the fraction of CO2 present in the gas phase remains negligible, further supporting the robustness of the storage conditions.

The normalized rate is the scaled injection rate that reflects the reduction in the spatial domain, and it is obtained by multiplying the original rate (4570 m3/day), which was for 154 × 103 × 15, by a scaling factor, which is the ratio of the total grid in 2D to the total grids in 3D. The simulation results in Figure indicate that while a 2D model offers computational efficiency, it tends to underestimate the amount of CO2 trapped and overpredicts bottom-hole pressure and vertical plume migration. This is because the 2D models do not consider the lateral flows. The simulation demonstrates that the 2D model results in a lower CO2 trapping capacity and an earlier stabilization period (Figure a), almost when injection ceases, and higher bottom-hole pressures (Figure b). Consequently, 2D models may not adequately capture the complexity of subsurface CO2 behavior and could lead to misleading conclusions about storage performance. The use of 3D modeling is therefore essential for the realistic and reliable prediction of CO2 trapping in structurally complex formations, and it is used throughout this study.

Residual Trapping

Residual trapping plays a vital role in enhancing the effectiveness of CO2 storage by immobilizing CO2 within pore spaces through capillary forces, thus complementing structural trapping. Together with structural trapping, it limits the lateral and vertical migration of CO2 gravity currents, offering improved security of long-term geological sequestration.

This model incorporates residual trapping using the CMG-GEM simulator, by activating hysteresis functions using the code “HYSKRG” in CMG-CEDIT that accounts the hysteresis effect. Specifically, Killough model, which is applied under the “gas hysteresis model for all Rock types” coupled with Land’s trapping model, is used to simulate the relative permeability hysteresis of the gas phase. The drainage and imbibition end points used to generate the relative permeability curve are as follows. Connate water saturation (S WCON)= 0.25, initial water saturation (S w) = 0.5, critical gas saturation (S GCRIT) = 0.05, end point brine relative permeability (k rw) = 0.9, end point gas relative permeability (k rg) = 0.5.

These functions simulate the variation in relative permeability based on saturation history, a key characteristic of multiphase flow in porous media. During injection, CO2 saturation increases along the drainage curve of gas relative permeability (K rg). As CO2 accumulates beneath the caprock due to buoyancy-driven migration, brine is displaced downward, causing an initial decrease in water saturation. However, as the system undergoes imbibition driven by pressure redistribution or injection cessation, brine re-enters the pore spaces, and gas relative permeability follows the imbibition curve due to hysteresis. This mechanism results in the entrapment of disconnected gas clusters; a process referred to as residual or capillary trapping.

Figure illustrates the CO2 plume distribution with residual trapping activated, showing a more dispersed and immobilized CO2 configuration than structural trapping alone. Including residual trapping increases the residual gas saturation to a value of 0.746 at the 13th layer, or 1688 m depth, thereby reducing the net migration of CO2 toward the caprock. Solubility Trapping

Solubility trapping enhances CO2 storage security by enabling the dissolution of injected CO2 into formation water. Once dissolved, CO2 transforms into aqueous solutions including carbonic acid and bicarbonate ions, significantly reducing mobility. This mechanism is a secondary yet crucial barrier against leakage, particularly when caprock integrity is uncertain. Dissolved CO2 becomes denser than free-phase CO2, enhancing downward migration rather than buoyant rise, thus lowering the risk of escape through faults or fractures. ,

The efficiency of solubility trapping is influenced by several reservoir parameters, including temperature, pressure, brine salinity, and rock properties such as porosity and permeability. These variables determine the solubility capacity of the reservoir and ultimately affect the amount and rate of CO2 dissolution. Key indicators of solubility trapping in reservoir systems include: the degree of CO2 saturation, the total volume of CO2 dissolved over time, the spatial extent of the CO2 plume, and the depth distribution of dissolved CO2 within the formation.

In this study, solubility trapping was modeled using the Harvey method (1996) to estimate Henry’s constant, which is a function of temperature, pressure, and salinity, and hence accounts for thermal effects. While it is built into CMG, Henry’s law is employed for modeling the solubility of CO2 in the aqueous phase. The Peng–Robinson equation of state (EOS) which is also built into CMG-GEM, is used to calculate gas density and for thermodynamic equilibrium, while Kestin and Rowe, and Chou are used for calculating the aqueous phase viscosity and density. , The model employed reservoir-specific conditions of 119 °F and 1740 psi, along with a brine concentration or aqueous phase salinity of 0.1 mol/kg, which represents a typical deep saline formation. The brine salinity is assumed to be constant. Molecular diffusion of CO2 is modeled by employing Fick’s second law of diffusion, which describes the change in the concentration of CO2 over time. The B-Dot Model, which is also incorporated into CMG-GEM, is utilized for the aqueous phase activity model, allowing for the calculation of the activity coefficient to determine the activity.

The model in this study incorporates structural, residual, and solubility trapping mechanisms to evaluate the integrated impact on CO2 plume behavior and storage performance.

Mineral Trapping

Mineralization, or mineral trapping, is regarded as the most secure and long-term sequestration of CO2 in geological formations, which involves the geochemical reaction between the dissolved CO2 and minerals with divalent ions such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+ in the storage formation to form stable and solid carbonate minerals.

In carbon mineralization, there are three general stages: first, gaseous CO2 is dissolved in an aqueous solution to form bicarbonate and carbonate ions. As CO2 dissolves in the formation water, it will make brine corrosive due to the formation of carbonic acid. This promotes the dissolution of divalent cation-bearing minerals since pH is lowered. Second, cations such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+ will be released into the solution as minerals dissociate. Third, stable carbonates will be formed. The dilution of CO3 2–(aq) in the formation water will increase pH back which makes the carbonate ion and divalent cations form carbonate minerals (precipitation).

Since the formation selected for this study (Strawn formation in King County) is a sandstone aquifer, the representative mineral with volume fractions considered as the constituents of sandstone rock are quartz (65%), calcite (5%), dolomite (3%), K-Feldspar (6%), and Illite (12%). Sensitivity Analysis

As discussed in earlier sections, solubility trapping significantly reduces the upward migration of CO2 plumes toward the caprock while also increasing the overall amount of CO2 that can be securely stored in subsurface formations. The effectiveness of this trapping mechanism is influenced by various reservoir and operational factors, including initial pressure and temperature, porosity, permeability, injection rate, heterogeneity, and aquifer thickness. Understanding the influence of these parameters is crucial for reducing uncertainties associated with geological CO2 storage. This can be achieved through a systematic sensitivity analysis.

In this study, a sensitivity analysis is carried out using a solubility trapping model that incorporates both structural and residual trapping mechanisms. The focus on solubility trapping is not only due to previous justifications but also because it offers several distinct advantages over other trapping mechanisms. One of the key benefits is that it reduces reliance on caprock integrity, since dissolved CO2 is less mobile and hence less likely to escape, making it particularly useful even in reservoirs with limited confinement. Additionally, CO2 dissolved in brine tends to remain stable over the long-term, with a significantly lower risk of leakage through faults or fractures than free-phase CO2. Solubility trapping also suppresses buoyancy forces, as the presence of dissolved CO2 results in a denser brine that limits vertical migration. Furthermore, solubility trapping enhances the potential for mineral trapping, because dissolved CO2 reacts more readily with formation minerals. Compared to mineral trapping, which occurs over geologic time scales, solubility trapping takes place over much shorter periods, making it more practical for early phase CO2 containment.

Given these advantages, optimizing solubility trapping is a key step toward improving the safety and effectiveness of carbon storage. The following subsections present the sensitivity analysis of six major parameters that affect the performance of solubility trapping.

Injection Rate

To evaluate how injection rate influences CO2 sequestration, simulations were performed using five different injection rates: 1188, 2375, 4570, 9500, and 19,000 m3 per day. Results from CMG-GEM simulations are presented in Figure a for the amount dissolved and Figure b for the amount trapped. The results clearly show that increasing the injection rate leads to a higher volume of CO2 being dissolved in the formation brine and trapped due to capillary and structural mechanisms. It is clearly seen that the amount of CO2 trapped is more sensitive to the injection rate compared to the CO2 dissolution.

Reservoir Temperature

When CO2 is injected into a saline aquifer formation, the ambient reservoir temperature influences the CO2 behavior, including its density and solubility. It is unlike the case of depleted formations where the injected CO2 undergoes a rapid expansion, resulting in a cooling effect known as the Joule–Thomson effect, as the reservoir pressure is lower than the pressure at which CO2 will be in a supercritical state. In this study, simulations were conducted at three different reservoir temperatures: 104 °F (40 °C), 122 °F (50 °C), and 140 °F (60 °C) to study the effect of the reservoir temperature on CO2 sequestration. The model employed Harvey’s method for Henry’s law constant correlation in aqueous phase, which is built into the modeling software (CMG). The Rowe-Chou for aqueous density correlation and the Kestin correlation for aqueous viscosity, which are also built into CMG, are used to model the temperature effect. The simulation result in Figure shows that as the reservoir temperature decreases, the amount of CO2 trapped increases significantly, while the CO2 dissolution shows a slight increase.

Effect of reservoir temperature on CO2 sequestration.

Lowering the reservoir temperature from 60 to 50 °C results in a 9% increase in CO2 sequestration, while a further reduction to 40 °C yields an additional 13% increase. This can be explained through the effects of temperature on CO2 solubility, dissolution rate, and chemical reactivity. First, CO2 solubility in water is inversely related to temperature. At lower temperatures, CO2 dissolves more readily into the formation brine, increasing the amount available for trapping. Second, the rate of dissolution is faster at lower temperatures, which supports more efficient short-term trapping. Third, temperature affects the chemical equilibrium of CO2 reactions in brine. When CO2 dissolves in water, it forms carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen and bicarbonate ions as follows: CO2+H2O↔H2CO3↔H++HCO3− 5

This dissociation reaction is exothermic, meaning that higher temperatures shift the equilibrium toward the undissociated form of carbonic acid, thus reducing the concentrations of hydrogen and bicarbonate ions. Conversely, at lower temperatures, the equilibrium favors dissociation, increasing the acidity of the solution and further enhancing solubility trapping.

In Situ Reservoir Pressure

The in situ reservoir pressure is a critical parameter in geological CO2 storage, as it directly influences storage capacity and containment security. The pressure in a given reservoir depends on geological and operational factors such as formation depth, location, and lithology. In this study, the influence of the in situ reservoir pressure on CO2 sequestration was examined by simulating three pressure scenarios: 12 MPa (1740 Psi), 15 MPa (2176 Psi), 20 MPa (2900 Psi), and 25 MPa (3600 Psi).

Simulation results presented in Figure show that higher reservoir pressures lead to a higher overall CO2 sequestration, including both dissolved and trapped CO2. This is because at high in situ reservoir pressure, CO2 remains in a supercritical state, which allows it to occupy more space and reduces the tendency of CO2 migration. Additionally, the high-pressure condition increases the dissolution of CO2 in formation water, which increases the solubility trapping.

Effect of reservoir pressure on CO2 sequestration.

The effect of in situ reservoir pressure on sequestration becomes more pronounced after the injection phase ends, particularly in the amount of CO2 that is dissolved. For example, 30 years after injection ceased, increasing the reservoir pressure from 12 to 15 MPa resulted in a 11% increase in CO2 sequestered. However, increasing the pressure from 15 to 20 MPa led to a comparatively minor (8%) increase, indicating diminishing returns at higher pressures, and further increasing to 25 MPa even resulted in a lower return (5%). Thus, the optimal in situ reservoir pressure, approximately 20 MPa, enables enhanced CO2 sequestration.

Injection Duration

In addition to reservoir characteristics and operational parameters, the duration of CO2 injection plays a key role in determining the amount of CO2 that can be securely stored. Simulation scenarios were conducted for injection durations of 10, 20, and 30 years to investigate this effect.

The results, illustrated in Figure , show that extending the injection period increases the total volume of CO2 sequestered in the reservoir. Both the dissolved and trapped CO2 volumes rise with prolonged injection, indicating a sustained capacity of the reservoir to accommodate CO2 over time. This trend suggests that longer injection timelines allow more CO2 to interact with formation brine and rock, enhancing the effectiveness of solubility and residual trapping mechanisms. Prolonged injection also gives the CO2 plume more time to equilibrate within the reservoir, facilitating better utilization of the available pore space and improving overall storage efficiency.

Effects of Rock Porosity

Rock porosity is one of the most influential factors in CO2 sequestration. It determines the storage capacity available for free-phase CO2 and CO2 dissolved in brine. In this study, porosity values of 5, 10, 20, and 30%, were simulated to evaluate their impact on storage performance.

Figure a demonstrates that increasing porosity enhances the volume of CO2 that can be dissolved and trapped. A 5% increase in porosity, from 5 to 10%, resulted in a substantial 41% rise in the total amount of CO2 sequestered. Further increasing porosity from 10% to 20% yielded a 55% additional gain in storage. However, the percentage increment in CO2 sequestration by increasing the porosity from 20 to 30% and from 30 to 40% resulted in an increment of 34 and 19% respectively, indicating 20% porosity as a threshold beyond which increases in porosity offer diminishing benefits. On the other hand, the result in Figure b reveals that higher porosity also limits the horizontal plume migration beneath the caprock, as formations with higher porosity provide more pore space for CO2 to be dissolved and trapped, thereby limiting vertical migration to the caprock and hence the horizontal migration.

(a) Effect of porosity on CO2 storage and (b) effect of porosity on horizontal plume migration of CO2.

The rate of additional CO2 dissolution is more sensitive to porosity changes, compared to the amount of CO2 trapped. This implies that much of the additional CO2 will dissolve in brine in higher-porosity rocks. Moreover, plume migration behavior is also influenced by porosity. In lower-porosity formations, the CO2 plume advances more quickly toward the caprock and tends to spread beneath the top of the formation, increasing the risk of leakage. In contrast, higher-porosity rocks provide more pore space to accommodate the injected CO2, limiting vertical and lateral migration. This is because there are fewer pores to contain CO2 in low porosity formations, which prevents CO2 from penetrating deeply into the formation. Instead, the CO2 accumulates in available pores and migrates toward the caprock more aggressively as the pressure builds up due to injection.

It is also essential to consider the mechanical stability of the formation. Although higher porosity enhances storage potential, it may also be associated with weaker rock structures, which could increase the likelihood of fracture development or the formation of leakage pathways. Therefore, it is paramount to have a very low-permeability caprock, especially for formations with high porosities, to prevent the upward migration and leakage of CO2, as having high permeability alone does not ensure the CO2 containment.

Effect of Permeability

While porosity determines the storage capacity of a reservoir, it does not solely define the efficiency of CO2 sequestration. The interconnectivity of the pore spaces, represented by permeability, is a critical factor that governs fluid flow and the effectiveness of CO2 injection and entrapment. In this study, the impact of horizontal and vertical permeability on CO2 storage was evaluated through simulations using horizontal permeability (K h) values of 101, 20, and 5 mD.

To account for vertical permeability (K v), the empirical relationship for sandstone reservoirs, which was proposed by Iheanacho et al. 2012 was applied, and the equation is Kv=0.0513(ChØe)2.1196 6 where Ø e is the effective porosity, taken as 0.17 for this model. This correlation calculated the corresponding K v values as 44.65, 8.03, and 1.85 mD for K h values of 101, 20, and 5 mD, respectively.

Figure presents the simulation results for these scenarios. Lower horizontal permeability leads to greater CO2 trapping because lower permeability restricts CO2 mobility, increasing the residence time of CO2 within the formation and enhancing both solubility and residual trapping mechanisms. The results suggest that although high porosity offers more storage space, it must be complemented by appropriate permeability characteristics to ensure efficient CO2 retention.

Effect of permeability on CO2 sequestration.

Additional simulations were conducted to further isolate the effect of vertical permeability by keeping the horizontal permeability constant at 101 mD while varying K v using the previously calculated values. The simulation results presented in Figure show that the highest CO2 sequestration occurred at the lowest vertical permeability, which is 1.85 mD. This outcome underscores the significance of vertical permeability in plume control and containment.

Effect of vertical permeability.

The sensitivity analysis conducted on this integrated CO2 trapping model, which includes structural, residual, solubility, and mineral trapping on the above six parameters, revealed that injection rate and porosity are the top two parameters (first and second, respectively) that exhibit the highest sensitivity. The number of injection years and temperature are the third and fourth sensitive parameters. Permeability and pressure are the fifth and sixth sensitive parameters, respectively.

Conclusions

This study develops and applies an integrated, compositional modeling framework to evaluate the coupled performance of structural, residual, solubility, and mineral trapping mechanisms for CO2 sequestration in deep saline aquifers, using the Strawn Formation in King County, Texas, as a representative site. Through high-resolution 3D simulation with CMG-GEM, we capture the spatiotemporal evolution of the CO2 plume and provide a mechanistic understanding of how each trapping mechanism contributes to overall storage integrity by simulating CO2 injection for 20 years, followed by a 200-year monitoring period.

Among the mechanisms evaluated, solubility trapping emerged as the most effective method when deployed in synergy with structural and residual trappings, resulting in the highest amount (43%) of CO2 sequestered, followed by mineral trapping (40%), evaluated 130 years after injection ceased. It is also found that solubility and residual trappings dominated in the early period, which was evaluated ten years postinjection.

While the study provides theoretical and practical contributions, as well as a transferable methodology applicable to a wide range of saline aquifers, supporting more informed site selection, risk assessment, and long-term performance evaluation of GCS projects, the model is a homogeneous model that is done by considering the average reservoir properties to provide insight into the factors influencing the CO2 sequestration in the selected formation. Hence, it is recommended to conduct a heterogeneous model that incorporates actual structural complexities and site-specific mineralogical data, as well as addressing factors such as CO2 impurities, thermal conduction across the caprock, and fault permeability, for a more realistic spatial representation of the formation properties. It is also recommended to develop a generalized correlation for the sensitivity of each factor discussed in this study by employing dimensionless parameters to have a generalized application for various sites.
