AKAP2/PKA complex coordinates primary ciliogenesis, ciliary trafficking and Shh signaling
Authors: Francesco Chiuso, Giuliana Giamundo, Emanuela Senatore, Laura Rinaldi, Francesca Rizzo, Simona Aversano, Antonio Giuseppe Bianco, Alfonso Carleo, Elena Alexandrova, Alessandro Weisz, Jack Brendan Jordan, Iolanda Carratù, Stephane Audebert, Luc Camoin, Maria Chiara Zizolfi, Leandra Sepe, Paolo Maiuri, Luca Lignitto, Ivan Conte, Antonio Feliciello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary cilium is a sensory organelle present in all eukaryotic cells that acts as a signaling hub that receives, integrates and transmits signals carried out by extracellular stimuli, playing a fundamental role in cell behavior, development and tissue homeostasis. Genetic mutations in genes encoding for structural components and regulators of primary cilium underlie severe developmental defects, also known as ciliopathies. Understanding cilium biology may, thus, provide novel therapeutic strategies for ciliopathies. Here, we report that A-Kinase Anchor protein 2(AKAP2) dynamically localizes within the primary cilium and forms complexes with kinesin motor proteins and components of the intra-flagellar transport system. Genetic deletion experiments and functional analysis demonstrate that AKAP2/PKA complex controls cilium biogenesis and intraciliary trafficking of cargo receptors, acting as a positive regulator of Shh pathway. In the medaka fish model, depletion of AKAP2 affects ciliogenesis and ciliary trafficking, delays embryonic development and significantly increases the embryonic death rate.Our findings identify AKAP2 as a ciliary scaffold protein that finely controls ciliogenesis, intraciliary trafficking and Hedgehog signaling cascade. Manipulating ciliary AKAP2 signaling, thus, provides a new therapeutic window for ciliopathy disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03092-8.Keywords: Primary Cilium, Shh, AKAP2, PKA, Signaling

Introduction

Primary cilium is a sensory organelle present in all eukaryotes that plays an essential role in cell growth, differentiation, metabolic pathways, development, and survival [1]. A variety of extracellular messengers, such as hormones, growth factors and neurotransmitters act through dedicated receptors located at the ciliary membrane to activate downstream signaling cascades that control development and tissue homeostasis [2]. Genetic mutations affecting the integrity or functions of primary cilia are causally linked to the pathogenesis of ciliopathies characterized by retinal dystrophy, amaurosis, obesity, mental retardation, polycystic kidney disease, orofacial digital defects, and other signs of ciliary dysfunction [1, 3, 4]. Despite extensive knowledge of the clinical features and genetic mutations responsible for ciliopathy disorders, the mechanisms underlying these diseases are still not well characterized and the therapeutic options for these disorders remain far from being realized.

Ligand-mediated activation of G protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) induces dissociation of Gαs subunit and consequent activation of adenylate cyclase that, in turn, synthesizes cAMP. Protein kinase A (PKA) is the most important effector enzyme of cAMP action. PKA is present within eukaryotic cells as tetrameric (R2C2) inactive holoenzymes, type I (PKAI) and type II (PKAII), whose structure and properties are determined by their regulatory (R) subunits. The binding of cAMP to regulatory (R) subunits induces the dissociation of PKA holoenzymes and the release of PKAc subunits which, in turn, phosphorylate a variety of intracellular substrates, thus promoting important biological effects [5, 6]. The differential tissue distribution and sensitivity of R subunits to cAMP molecules dictate the different biological responses of the cell to GPCR activation. In eukaryotic cells, PKA is targeted to subcellular compartments by direct interaction with a group of structurally distinct but functionally homologous proteins, namely A-Kinase Anchor Proteins (AKAPs). These form transduceosome units that focus PKA signaling to discrete intracellular sites, contributing to integrate and locally amplify cAMP signaling [7]. Within these signaling units, AKAPs also compartmentalize attenuating enzymes of cAMP waves, generating a highly regulated flux of signals to downstream targets.

PKA is a resident enzyme of primary cilium [1, 3, 8, 9]. Activation of PKA by ciliary cAMP negatively regulates the Sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway by preventing the translocation of the glioma-associated transcription factor-2 (GLI2) to the nucleus [1, 10–12]. Stimulation of Smoothened (SMO), an atypical G protein-coupled receptor localized at ciliary membrane, by Shh ligand induces a rapid hydrolysis of cAMP by specific phosphodiesterases and consequent inactivation of PKA [13, 14]. The release of the inhibitory constrain of PKA induces the translocation of GLI2 to the nucleus and activation of Shh-dependent gene transcription [15]. Evidence supports the existence of an AKAP module within the primary cilium that finely controls local activation of PKA. Thus, the orphan G protein-coupled receptor GPR161 binds and targets PKAI to the primary cilium, contributing to PKA-mediated inhibition of basal Shh pathway [4, 16–19]. Moreover, the prototypic PKAII anchor protein AKAP150 was identified as resident of the ciliary compartment in renal mIMCD3 cells [20, 21]. However, the mechanisms and the biological contributions of ciliary PKAII-specific AKAPs, including AKAP150, in cilium biogenesis and Shh signaling were largely unexplored.

Here, we provide evidence that the multiscaffold, actin-associated AKAP2 dynamically localizes at primary cilium. A combinatorial approach of genetic manipulation, in situ immunostaining assays and transcriptomic analysis, both in retinal cells and in Medaka fish model, unveiled a key role of AKAP2 in essential aspects of ciliogenesis, ciliary trafficking and developmental pathways.

Materials and methods

Medaka stocks

In accordance with institutional guidelines for animal research, the Cab-strain of wild-type Medaka fish (Oryzias latipes) was kept under standard conditions of 12 h of dark and 12 h of light at 27 °C, and embryos were staged according to Beccari et al. [22]. Ethics approval is not necessary for this study that focuses solely on analyzing and up-hatching embryos because they are incapable of independent feeding at this stage of development in accordance with the law on animal experimentation by the Italian Ministry of Health; Department of Public Health, Animal Health, Nutrition, and Food Safety (D.Lgs. 26/2014).

Morpholino and mRNA injection in medaka embryos

Morpholino (Mo; Gene Tools LLC, Oregon, USA) was designed against the ATG initiation codon within the 5’ untranslated region of the olAKAP2 gene (5’-TGTGATGAAACTGACGGATTCCTGG-3’). Mo-Ol-AKAP2 was injected at 0.21 mM concentration into one embryo at the one/two-cell stage. Morpholino injections specificity and off-target effects were excluded as previously described [23]. In vitro synthesis of mouse wild-type AKAP2, AKAP2F and AKAP2RII mRNAs were performed following manufacture’s instruction [23]. mRNAs were injected at 25–100 ng/µl to observe dose-dependent phenotypes and selected working concentrations were 75 ng/µl for injections. For somatostatin (SST) (Sigma, 38916-34-6) and purmorphamine (Pur) (Abcam, ab120933) treatment, chorions from injected embryos were removed with the hatching enzyme at St24. Then, injected embryos were treated with 10 µM SST or 10 µM PUR for 3 h and 6 h, respectively.

Whole-mount immunostaining

Embryos at Stage 24 were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, 2X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 0.1% Tween-20. The fixed embryos were detached from chorion and washed with PTW 1X. Embryos were digested 7 min with 10 g/ml proteinase K and washed two-fold with 2 mg/ml glycine/PTW 1X. The samples were fixed 20 min in 4% paraformaldehyde, 2X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 0.1% Tween-20, washed with PTW 1X and then incubated for 2 h in FBS 1%/PTW 1X, at room temperature. The embryos were incubated with mouse anti-acetylated α-tubulin antibody 1:200 (6- 11B-1; Santa Cruz, SC23950), anti-GFP 1:500 (Invitrogen, A-6455), anti-SSTR3 1:200 (Proteintech, 20696-1-AP) and anti-GPR161 1:100 (Proteintech, 13398-1-AP) overnight at 4 °C. The samples were washed with PTW 1X, incubated with the secondary antibody, Alexa-488 goat anti-mouse IgG, Alexa-594 goat anti-mouse IgG, Alexa-488 goat anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa-594 goat anti-mouse IgG (ThermoFisher) and then with DAPI. Finally, the embryos were placed in glycerol 100%.

AKAP2 knock out CRISPR/Cas9 strategy

CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing technology was performed by using the LenticrisprV2 plasmid to knockout (KO) AKAP2. Short guide RNA (sgRNA) against AKAP2 (0.1 pmoL/µL sg RNA pairs 5-cacatcgagctcagtaatag-3 and 5-CTATTACTGAGCTCGATGTG-3) was introduced to the Lenticrisprv2 plasmid through digestion/ligation cycles using BsmbI and T4 ligase (Neb).

Briefly, Human Embryonic Kidney 293T cells, HEK293T, were maintained in DMEM Complete medium (EuroClone, Pero (MI), Italy) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, FBS (Merk), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco), and 1% l-glutamine (Gibco), and used as packaging cells for lentiviral production. HEK293T cells were transfected by calcium-phosphate, using a DNA mix containing LenticrisprV2 (10 µg) harboring or not anti-AKAP2 human specific gRNA and psPAX2 (Addgene #12260; 18 µg) and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259; 13 µg) as packaging vectors. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cell supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.45 µM filter, and 20 µg/mL of polybrene (Merk) was added. ARPE-19 cell media was then replaced with infective media, and cells were incubated for 6 h. Later, the infective cell medium was replaced with normal medium and, after an additional 24 h, 1 µg/mL of puromycin was added to start selection. T7 assay to confirm the efficiency of gRNA was performed.

Stable transfectants were isolated by collecting individual clones, which were further amplified. Single clones and bulk culture (pool) were analyzed by Western blotting to verify gene inactivation. Whole-cell lysate was prepared by lysing cells in a solution containing 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Igepal, 1× protease inhibitor and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford protein assay. Proteins were loaded on 7% SDS–PAGE and detected by Western blotting. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and then incubated with the specific primary antibody anti-AKAP2 antibody (Novus Biological NB100-61603). Prestained Protein Ladders were purchased from Bio-rad laboratories, Inc. Italy.

Library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from wild type and PALM2AKAP2 knockout ARPE-19 cells by using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Once extracted, RNA purity and concentration were assayed by NanoDrop™ 2000/2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen) by Qubit RNA High Sensitivity Kit (Invitrogen), respectively. The integrity was evaluated with the aid of TapeStation 4200 (Agilent) and RNA ScreenTape Assay (Agilent). 500 ng of each sample were used as input for sequencing libraries preparation with TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina Inc.) following the provided by the manufacturer protocol. Libraries quality was analyzed by TapeStation 4200 (Agilent) and D1000 ScreenTape Assay (Agilent), whereas concentration was measured by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen) using Qubit High Sensitivity dsDNA kit (Invitrogen). Equimolar pool of libraries was diluted to a concentration of 1.6 pM and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina Inc.) in 2 × 75 paired-end mode.

Functional and comparative RNA-seq analysis

Data preparation (in terms of Log2-transformation, background correction, and quantile normalization of counts-per-million data) and comparative analysis between AKAP2 KO and AKAP2 WT groups were performed using ‘limma’ R package [24]. Lowly expressed transcripts (exhibiting a count per million value lower than 1) were preliminary filtered out. Only transcripts presenting a p-value lower than 0.05 at Benjamini-Hochberg test of the False Discovery Rate (BH-FDR) were considered as Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) and selected for further analysis. Heatmap Ward’s clustering based on Euclidean distance of DEG scaled expression values were carried out by pheatmap R package (v.1.0.12).

Enrichment and functional network analysis of DEGs were obtained through QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis tool (QIAGEN Inc., https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/IPA).

Figures were produced using ggplot2 R package (v. 3.5.1). The RNA-seq data have been deposited to the functional genomics data collection (ArrayExpress) with the data set identifier E-MTAB-15,339.

Expression analysis of ciliary genes by RT2-PCR assay

The 96-well plate human primary cilium RT2 PCR assay (Cod. PAHS-127Z) against 84 genes belonging to primary cilia pathways, was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Arrays were run on the Applied Biosystem Step One Plus Real-Time PCR, using an SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (QIAGEN). cDNA synthesis was performed using RT2 First Strand Kit (QIAGEN) on RNAs extracted from human ARPE-19 wt or AKAP2 KO. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed normalizing gene expression cycle threshold (Ct), replicated five times, on the average Ct of four different control genes (GAPDH, ACTB, and HPRT1). Fold change values are then presented as average fold change > 2 (average ΔCt). Data from five independent arrays were analyzed using Qiagen RT2 profiler PCR array data analysis version 3.5 (http://pcrdataanalysis.sabiosciences.com/pcr/arrayanalysis.php).

Mass spectrometry analysis

The mass spectrometry data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the data set identifier PXD063616.

AKAP2 interactomics – preparation of affinity purification samples for mass spectrometry

HEK293T cells were transfected for 24 h with either a 2×FLAG–2×Strep-tagged empty vector or 2×FLAG–2×Strep-tagged AKAP2, each in three biological replicates. Immunopurification was performed using Strep-Tactin Sepharose (IBA, #2-1201-010), and bound proteins were eluted with desthiobiotin (IBA, #2-1000-025). A total of 1,098 proteins were identified in the analysis. The elution buffer contained 1× desthiobiotin and 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Enriched samples (n = 3 biological replicates) were loaded onto NuPAGE™ 4–12% Bis–Tris acrylamide gels according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Life Technologies). Protein migration was interrupted as soon as the proteins stacked in a single band. Protein-containing bands were stained with Imperial Blue (Pierce), cut from the gel and digested with high sequencing grade trypsin (Promega) before being analyzed by mass spectrometry. Briefly, gel pieces were washed and destained using few steps of 100mM NH4HCO3. Destained gel pieces were shrunk with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate in 50% acetonitrile and dried at RT. Protein spots were then rehydrated using 10mM DTT in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8.0 for 45 min at 56 °C. This solution was replaced by 55 mM iodoacetamide in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8.0 and the gel pieces were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. They were then washed twice in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate and finally shrunk by incubation for 5 min with 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate in 50% acetonitrile. The resulting alkylated gel pieces were dried at room temperature. The dried gel pieces were reswollen by incubation in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8.0 supplemented with 12.5 ng/µl trypsin (Promega) for 1 h at 4 °C and then incubated overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were harvested by collecting the initial digestion solution and carrying out two extractions; first in 5% formic acid, then in 5% formic acid in 60% acetonitrile. Pooled extracts were dried down in a centrifugal vacuum system. Peptides were further reconstituted in 15 µl water/acetonitrile/ (98/2) containing 0.05%TFA. 10% of each sample was analyzed twice by liquid chromatography (LC)-tandem MS (MS/MS) using a Q-Exactive plus Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) online with a nanoRSLC Ultimate 3000 chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA). First, peptides were concentrated and purified on a pre-column PepMap100 C18, 2 cm × 100 μm I.D, 100 Å pore size, 5 μm particle size in solvent A (0.1% formic acid in 2% acetonitrile). In the second step, peptides were separated on a reverse phase LC EASY-Spray C18 column PepMap RSLC C18, 50 cm × 75 μm I.D, 100 Å pore size, 2 μm particle size (Thermo Fisher) at 300 nL.min − 1 flow rate and 40 °C. After column equilibration peptides were eluted from the analytical column by a three-step linear gradient (2.5%–27.5% solvent B [80% acetonitrile 0.1% formic acid in water] for 100 min, 27.5%–40% for 20 min and a last elution 40%–90% for 2 min). For peptide ionization in the EASY-Spray nanosource in front of the mass spectrometer, the spray voltage was set at 1,9 kV and the capillary temperature at 250 °C. The mass spectrometer was used in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode with the following parameters. First, MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap in the range of m/z 375–1500 at a FWHM resolution of 35,000 measured at 200 m/z. AGC target was set at 1 × 106 with a Maximum Injection Time of 60 ms. MS2 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap with a resolution of 17,500 after isolation of the parent ion in the quadrupole and fragmentation in the HCD cell under collision Energy of 27%. DIA parent ion range was from 400 to 1000 m.z − 1 divided into 24 m.z − 1 windows.

AKAP2 interactomics – data processing protocol

Relative intensity-based label-free quantification (LFQ) was processed using the DIA-NN 1.8 algorithm [25]. Raw files were searched against the Human database extracted from UniProt on the 10th of January 2023 and containing 20404 entries with the addition of a protein contaminant bank [26]. The following parameters were used for searches: (i) trypsin allowing cleavage before proline; (ii) one missed cleavage was allowed; (iii) cysteine carbamidomethylation (+ 57.02146) as a fixed modification and methionine oxidation (+ 15.99491) and N-terminal acetylation (+ 42.0106) as variable modifications; (iv) a maximum of one variable modification per peptide allowed; and (v) minimum peptide length was 7 amino acids and a maximum of 30 amino acids. The match between runs option was enabled. The precursor false discovery was set to 1%. DIA-NN parameters were set on Single-pass mode for the Neural Network classifier, Robust LC High precision for quantification strategy and RT-dependent mode for Cross-run normalization. The library was generated using a Smart profiling setup. The main output file from DIA-NN was further filtered at 1% FDR and LFQ intensity was calculated using our DIAgui package at 1% q-value (https://github.com/marseille-proteomique/DIAgui [27]. The statistical analysis was done with the Perseus program (version 1.6.15.0) [28]. Quantifiable proteins were defined as those detected in above 70% of samples in at least one condition. Protein LFQ normalized intensities were base 2 logarithmized to obtain a normal distribution. Missing values were replaced using data imputation by randomly selecting from a normal distribution centered on the lower edge of the intensity values that simulate signals of low abundant proteins using default parameters (a downshift of 1.8 standard deviations and a width of 0.3 of the original distribution). To determine whether a given detected protein was specifically differential, a two-sample t-test was done using permutation-based FDR-controlled employing 250 permutations.

Scratch assay and data analysis

ARPE1 WT and KO cells were seeded at 80% confluency in 12-well plates. After 24 h, a straight scratch was created in the cell monolayer using a P200 pipette tip. Cells were then washed once with 1 mL of DPBS to remove cellular debris, followed by the addition of 2 mL of the appropriate growth medium [29]. The cell-free area created by the pipette tip was monitored over 15 h using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope equipped with an incubator chamber to maintain a constant temperature of 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Trasmitted light images were captured with a Hamamatsu camera every 10 min using a custom macro in ZEN 2012 [30]. The free area was quantified at each frame of the time-lapse sequence using a custom macro developed in ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Normalized free area ratio was then computed and averaged for each experimental condition (n = 18 for both, from 3 independent experiments) using R. The following R libraries were employed: stringr (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=stringr), ggplot2 (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/), ggpubr (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggpubr), RColorBrewer (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=RColorBrewer), ggsignif (https://const-ae.github.io/ggsignif/) and ggpattern (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggpattern. A line plot was generated to show the mean free area ratio over time for each condition, with error bars representing the standard error of the mean. Statistic analysis was performed in R using wilcox test.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblot

Cells were lysed with buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, EDTA 1 mM and 0.5% NP40) and supplemented with protease inhibitors, phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and phosphatase inhibitors. After lysate clarification (13,000 rpm, 20 min), the lysates were incubated overnight with the indicated antibodies (Flag antibody, Sigma-Aldrich) for the immunoprecipitation. Resin-associated proteins were washed six times with standard lysis buffer, eluted with Laemmli sample buffer and separated by SDS–PAGE. Filter was blocked with 5% milk in TBS Tween 0.1%, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies and finally proteins were detected with ECL (EuroClone).

Nuclear and cytosolic protein extraction

Cells were processed for subcellular fractionation to obtain cytosolic and nuclear protein extracts. Cell pellets were resuspended in Buffer A. Mechanical disruption was performed by passing the suspension through an insulin syringe needle approximately 15 times to ensure efficient plasma membrane lysis while preserving nuclear integrity. Samples were then incubated on ice for 15 min, with intermittent gentle mixing. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 2000 rpm. The resulting supernatant, corresponding to the cytosolic fraction, was carefully collected and stored appropriately. The nuclear pellet was washed by resuspension with 200 µL of Buffer A, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 5 min at 2000 rpm. The supernatant was discarded to minimize cytosolic contamination. The washed nuclear pellet was then resuspended in nuclear extraction buffer (Buffer B) using a volume equal to one-quarter of the initial Buffer A volume. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 min at 13,000 rpm. The final supernatant, containing the nuclear protein extract, was collected for downstream analyses. Buffers: Buffer A: 10mM HEPES, 1,5mM KCl, 0,5mM DTT, NP40 0,5%; Nuclear Buffer: 50mM TrisHCl pH 7.9, 0,3 M Sucrose, 0,42 M KCl, 5mM MgCl2, 0,1 M EDTA, 2 mM DTT.

Generation of ARPE-19 AKAP2 KO + AKAP2-GFP

HEK293T cells were grown in a humidified incubator, at 37 °C, with 5% CO 2. The cells were cultured using DMEM (41966-029; Gibco) with 10% FBS (10270-106; Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (25030-024; Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P0781; Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were dissociated with trypsin-EDTA solution (T4049, Sigma-Aldrich) when the cultures reached ~ 90% confluency. Cell cultures were regularly tested by PCR to assure that cell lines were free from Mycoplasma contamination. For transient transfection, HEK293T cells (at ~ 70% confluency) were transfected with 10 µg DNA plasmids using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (06365779001; Sigma-Aldrich) diluted with serum-free DMEM (41966-029; Gibco), to a concentration transfection reagent to the diluted DNA (3:1 ratio of reagent to DNA). Transient transfections were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Results

AKAP2 is a novel ciliary protein

To elucidate the role of AKAP2 in cellular processes, we conducted a proteomic analysis of AKAP2-Flag immunopurified from HEK293T (Supplementary Fig. 1A). As shown by principal component analysis (PCA), we observed high reproducibility between three biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Volcano plot analysis shows peptide intensity of AKAP2 interacting proteins vs. empty vector (EV) and the statistically significant interactors and ciliary proteins that specifically copurified with AKAP2 immunocomplexes (Fig. 1A). Notably, the identified interactors encompassed proteins involved in ciliary processes, actin filament remodeling, organelle localization, membrane trafficking, and cell cycle regulation (Fig. 1A and B). STRING network analysis of AKAP2 interactome revealed a pronounced enrichment of proteins implicated in cytoskeletal organization and dynamics (Fig. 1C). Consistent with the proteomic data, AKAP2 was found to bind several kinesin motor proteins, including KIF11, KIF5B, and KIF2A, along with IFT172, a key component of the intra-flagellar transport subcomplex IFT-B essential for ciliary assembly and cargo transport (Fig. 1C). Co-immunoprecipitation assays in HEK293 cells confirmed the interaction between exogenous AKAP2 and IFT172 (Fig. 1D). Additionally, AKAP2 interacted with BBS9, a component of the BBSome complex that controls intraciliary trafficking and signaling [31–33], and also with KIF2A, a ciliary-associated kinesin motor protein with microtubule-depolymerizing activity that is required for primary cilium disassembly in growth-stimulated cells [34] (Fig. 1D and E). The mitotic kinase Aurora A (AURKA) and Angiomotin (AMOT), a member of angiostatin binding proteins, were also found to interact with AKAP2 (Fig. 1D) [35–37].

Next, we sought to analyze if AKAP2 localizes within- or close to- primary cilium. To this end, we used retinal ARPE-19 cells that were growth-arrested by serum deprivation, to promote primary cilia assembly. These can be visualized by immunostaining analysis of ciliary proteins, such as Arl13b or acetylated tubulin, a modified form of tubulin that selectively accumulates within the primary cilium. As shown in Fig. 1F and G and Supplementary Fig. 2A, endogenous AKAP2 signal localizes at primary cilia, as confirmed by the co-localization with Arl13b and cilia-APEX [38], a GFP-tagged, cilia-targeted proximity labeling enzyme. Moreover, Flag- or GFP-tagged exogenous AKAP2 proteins are also localized at primary cilium (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Molecular and functional analysis of AKAP2 knockout ARPE-19 cells

To get insight into the role of AKAP2 in cilium biology and related cellular processes, we generated AKAP2 KO ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 3). Previous work suggested a critical role of AKAP2 in cytoskeleton events underlying cancer cell growth and motility [39, 40]. Using AKAP2 KO ARPE-19 cells, we first analyzed the impact of AKAP2 deletion on cell adhesion and migration by measuring the area, circularity and aspect ratio of focal adhesions (FAs) [41, 42], which were stained using anti-p-Paxillin antibody. In AKAP2 KO cells, we observed FAs that were larger, less circular and more elongated, indicating a significant increase of cell adhesion (Fig. 2B and C). Accordingly, while AKAP2 KO cells were overly attached to the plate surface, their migration efficiency was impaired, exhibiting slower invasion rate of a free area (wound) created in a confluent cell monolayer (Fig. 2D and E and Movie). Next, we evaluated the impact of AKAP2 deletion on cell growth by monitoring three-dimensional (3D) cell cultures formation (spheroids) of ARPE-19 cells. As shown in Fig. 2F and G downregulation of AKAP2 markedly reduced the growth of ARPE-19 spheroids, compared to controls.

To better dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of AKAP2 deletion to the retinal cell phenotype, we carried out a comparative transcriptomic analysis in control and AKAP2 KO cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, the analysis revealed 30 transcripts differentially expressed in AKAP2 KO cells, compared to wild-type cells. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed a clear separation between KO and WT samples (Fig. 3B), indicating distinct gene expression profiles driven by AKAP2 loss. Functional enrichment analysis identified pathways central to ciliary activity and retinal cell physiology, including sperm motility, glutaminergic and serotonin receptor signaling and Gap junction pathway (Fig. 3C). These pathways are known to influence retinal cell survival and neuroprotection in pathological conditions such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [43, 44]. The involvement of pathways like sperm motility, typically associated with cytoskeletal organization of ciliary axoneme and ATP-dependent transport, suggests that AKAP2 may influence intracellular trafficking and ARPE dynamics [45]. Interestingly, in AKAP2 KO cells, we observed downregulation of gene transcripts encoding for regulators of ciliogenesis and cilium stability, including lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) [46–48] (Fig. 3B). Additionally, AKAP2 KO cells display upregulation of transcripts encoding for regulators of primary cilium, such as neuronal regeneration-related protein (NREP) [49], serum and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) [50], Bcl-2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3) [51] and carbonic anhydrase gene (CA9) [52] (Fig. 3B). The analysis of upstream regulators revealed key transcription factors and signaling molecules that may significantly impact cellular processes in AKAP2 KO ARPE-19 cells. In particular, tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα), interleukin 1beta (IL1 β) and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) are associated with inflammatory signaling and stress responses. AKAP2 loss not only disrupts ciliary architecture, epithelial integrity, metabolic adaptation and cellular stress responses but may also trigger compensatory inflammatory pathways (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, most of identified pathways were strictly correlated with an alteration of Shh signaling [53–56].

AKAP2 is required for cilium biogenesis and Shh signaling

The data above indicates that AKAP2 localizes at primary cilium. Since AKAP2 is a PKAII specific anchor protein, we investigated the requirement of AKAP2 for PKA targeting to the cilium. In agreement with previous findings [57], immunostaining analysis of serum-deprived ARPE-19 cells confirmed that RI subunit is localized at primary cilium (Fig. 4A and B). We also found a fraction of RII subunits at ciliary compartment (Fig. 4C and D; Supplementary Fig. 4A, 4B). Interestingly, in AKAP2 KO cells, the ciliary staining of RI subunit was mostly unaffected, compared to control cells (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, ciliary localization of RII subunits was markedly reduced by AKAP2 KO (Fig. 4C and D; Supplementary Fig. 4A, 4B). Analogous results were obtained by knockdown experiments using specific siRNAs directed against AKAP2 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, AKAP2 KO cells also exhibit a reduced phosphorylation of PKA substrates within the primary cilium (Fig. 4E and F). Under these conditions, we did not observe major changes in the expression levels and localization of AKAP150 and GPR161, two ciliary PKA anchor proteins [21, 57], in AKAP2 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings indicate that AKAP2, indeed, contributes to ciliary localization and activity of PKA.

Next, we evaluated the role of AKAP2 in primary ciliogenesis. Figure 5A and B show that the number of ciliated cells was significantly reduced in AKAP2 KO cells, compared to wild type cells. Similar findings were obtained by AKAP2 knockdown using specific siRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Next, we evaluated the impact of AKAP2 deletion on ciliary trafficking. Activation of Shh pathway induces a rapid export out of cilium of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), including the orphan receptor GPR161 [58], through a mechanism involving the BBSome complex [59, 60]. We monitored the ciliary localization of GPR161, both in control cells and in AKAP2 KO cells, under basal conditions or following Shh stimulation. As shown in Fig. 5C and D under basal conditions, AKAP2 deletion had no major effects on ciliary GPR161 staining. In wild type cells, stimulation of Shh with purmorphamine, a small agonist of the ciliary receptor Smoothened, markedly decreased the number of GPR161-positive cilia. In contrast, ligand-induced activation of Shh in AKAP2 KO cells had no major effects on the number of GPR161-positive cilia. We also analyzed the localization and trafficking of another neuronal GPCR, somatostatin receptor 3 (SSTR3), within the cilium. Following serum deprivation, cells were left untreated or stimulated with somatostatin (SST). Figure 5E and F show that, under basal conditions, no major effects in ciliary localization of SSTR3 were evident in AKAP2 KO cells, compared to wild-type cells. SST treatment induced a rapid exit of its receptor out of cilium in wild-type cells, whereas in AKAP2 KO cells the same treatment had no effects on ciliary staining of SSTR3. The effects of AKAP2 downregulation on SSTR3 and GPR161 ciliary trafficking, under basal conditions or following agonist stimulation, were replicated by genetic silencing experiments of AKAP2 (Supplementary Fig. 7A-F). Rescue experiments confirmed the role of AKAP2 in ciliary trafficking of cargo receptors. Thus, re-expressing exogenous AKAP2 (AKAP2-GFP) in AKAP2 KO cells restored the exit of endogenous GPR161 from cilium in response to Shh pathway activation, at comparable levels of wild type cells (Fig. 6A-C). Next, we analyzed whether Shh pathway activation regulates the ciliary localization of AKAP2 and PKA. As shown in Fig. 7A and B, stimulation with purmorphamine induced a rapid exit of AKAP2 out of cilium, indicating a dynamic, reversible recruitment of the anchor protein to the ciliary compartment. The exit of AKAP2 from cilium paralleled a marked decrease of ciliary PKA-RII staining (Fig. 7C and D) and a significant decrease of PKA phosphorylation of ciliary substrates (Fig. 7E and F), indicating that AKAP2 dynamically controls PKA signaling at ciliary compartment.

AKAP2 controls cilium development in Medaka fish

To further investigate the role of AKAP2 in ciliogenesis, we conducted an in vivo analysis using the Medaka fish (Oryzias latipes, ol). We generated a GFP-tagged wild-type AKAP2 construct and two AKAP2-GFP mutants carrying alanine substitutions of key residues within the actin-binding domain that destroy the interaction to actin cytoskeleton (AKAP2 F-Mutant) [40] or substitutions of two alanine residues to proline within the RII binding domain (AKAP2 RII-Mutant) [61] that affects PKAII binding to AKAP2 (Fig. 9A). First, we analyzed the expression and ciliary distribution of olAKAP2 in Medaka fish. As shown in Fig. 9B, we confirmed the localization of endogenous olAKAP2 at primary cilium in vivo. Next, we assessed the ciliary localization of GFP-tagged AKAP2 constructs. Figure 9B shows that both AKAP2 F-Mut and AKAP2 RII mutants were not localized at the cilia, compared to the wild-type AKAP2, indicating that the binding to PKA and/or actin is a prerequisite for AKAP2 targeting to ciliary compartment. Next, we evaluated the impact of olAkap2 downregulation on Medaka fish development and ciliogenesis by performing genetic silencing experiments using a morpholino (Mo, Gene Tools) targeting the olAkap2 transcript. The efficiency and specificity of AKAP2 knock-down were verified using appropriate controls [62]. The depletion of olAkap2 caused a delay in embryonic development, resulting in a significant increased embryonic death rate (~ 60%) (Fig. 9C and D). As expected, the injection of Mo-AKAP2 along with AKAP2 mRNA was able to rescue the observed phenotype, confirming the role of AKAP2 in Medaka fish development (Fig. 9C and D). To verify the specificity of AKAP2 mRNA, we also tested the effects of a single mRNA injection on Medaka fish phenotype. As highlighted, injecting AKAP2 mRNA alone did not change the embryo phenotype. Next, we evaluated whether overexpression of mutant forms of AKAP2 mRNAs may induce possible changes in cilia development. Indeed, injections of AKAP2 F-Mut and AKAP2 RII-Mut resulted in alterations of embryo development and embryonic death rate (Fig. 9C and D). Consistently, co-injections of mutant forms of AKAP2 mRNAs with MoAkap2 could not rescue morphological embryo defects (Fig. 9C). However, we observed an amelioration of the embryo survival (Fig. 9D). To evaluate if the observed MoAKAP2 phenotype was related to ciliogenesis defects, we measured the cilia length on cell surface of neural tube of Medaka embryos at stage 24 through whole-mount immunofluorescence with anti-acetylated α-tubulin. As shown in Fig. 9E and G and Supplementary Fig. 9, we detected a significant reduction in cilia length in MoAKAP2 morphant embryos. Consistently, the co-injection of AKAP2 mRNA with MoAKAP2 significantly rescued cilia development (Fig. 9F and G). In contrast, injections of AKAP2 F-Mut and AKAP2-RII Mut resulted in a significant defect in cilia growth (Fig. 9F). Moreover, co-injections of the mutated forms of AKAP2 gene with MoAkap2 could not rescue ciliogenesis (Fig. 9F and G). These data strongly support that the binding of AKAP2 to PKA and actin are both required for cilium biogenesis, signaling and Medaka fish development. To further assess the role of ol-Akap2, we also analyzed the localization and trafficking of both SSTR3 and GPR161 receptors in the cilium. In agreement with the in vitro data, under basal conditions, both receptors had a ciliary localization in wild-type and Mo-Akap2 embryos. Following agonist stimulation, we observed a rapid exit of both receptors from the ciliary compartment only in wild-type embryos, whereas no major effects were observed in Mo-Akap2 embryos. (Fig. 10A-D). Consistently, rescue experiments consolidated the requirement of AKAP2 for proper ciliary trafficking of the cargo receptors. Thus, reintroduction of exogenous AKAP2 into AKAP2‑depleted embryos reinstated the ability of both endogenous SSTR3 and GPR161 to exit the cilium under stimulations with SST and purmorphamine, respectively (Fig. 10A-D).

Discussion

Here, we provide evidence of the existence of a scaffold complex assembled by AKAP2 at primary cilium that controls different aspects of cilium biology. Specifically, we found that AKAP2 expression and localization at primary cilium are required for ciliogenesis, ciliary trafficking of G protein-coupled receptors, and Shh signaling (Fig. 11). Genetic downregulation of AKAP2 in retinal epithelial cells markedly affected ciliogenesis and signaling. The role of AKAP2 in primary cilium activities has been confirmed by manipulating olAKAP2 expression in Medaka fish. Here, genetic knockdown of AKAP2 causes ciliary defects associated with a significant delay in embryonic development and an increase rate of embryonic lethality. Rescue experiments demonstrated the requirement of PKA- and actin-binding properties of AKAP2 in mediating the physiological events underlying embryos development and survival.

AKAP2 regulates Sonic hedgehog signaling cascade at the ciliary compartment. In absence of Shh ligand (Shh off), the receptor Patched1 (Ptch1) inhibits Smoothened (SMO) signaling. AKAP2 binds to the IFT complex and localizes PKAII at the ciliary compartment. GPR161 activates adenylyl cyclase (AC), thus increasing cAMP levels that induce activation of AKAP2-bound PKAII that, in turns, represses Gli transcription factor. The binding of Shh ligand (Shh on), induces the exit of Ptch1 from the ciliary compartment and the consequent translocation of SMO within the cilium, that represses AC. The inhibition of AC, results in the export of AKAP2, PKAII and GPR161 from the cilium and in the translocation of Gli2 to the nucleus, promoting the Shh-dependent gene transcription

Primary cilium acts as a central hub that coordinates distinct signaling events underlying many cellular activities regulating differentiation, growth, metabolism, and survival. cAMP mediates the biological responses of cells to stimulation of specific GPCRs by dedicated hormones and neurotransmitters. The rise of cAMP levels within the primary cilium by a GPR161-mediated activation of ciliary adenylate cyclase maintains an inhibitory loop on Shh signaling pathway, preventing basal activation of GLI2 transcription factor and downstream GLI2-dependent gene transcription [16, 19, 57, 63]. GPR161 acts as an AKAP that recruits PKAI to primary cilium and regulates PKA-dependent Shh pathway [57]. Evidence indicates that Smoothened uses a decoy substrate sequence that interacts with catalytic subunit of PKA [64], thereby restraining its activity and promoting the release of GLI2 inhibition and consequent activation of downstream Shh pathway [64]. The novelty of our study relies on the identification of AKAP2 as a ciliary protein with important roles in targeting PKAII to the ciliary compartment and regulating Shh pathway, contributing to a significant share of total PKA activity at ciliary compartment. Thus, genetic deletion of AKAP2 markedly reduced the localization of RII subunits of PKA at ciliary compartment, with no major effects on ciliary RI subunits. This is consistent with previous findings indicating that AKAP2 is a PKAII-specific AKAP [65]. Interestingly, we also found that the localization of AKAP2 and PKAII at ciliary compartment is highly dynamic. Thus, following activation of Shh pathway, both AKAP2 and PKA-RII are rapidly exported out of cilium, and this event is linked to a significant downregulation of PKA phosphorylation of ciliary substrates. This represents a unique mechanism to dynamically and reversibly convey cAMP signals to a discrete compartment, with major biological effects on downstream PKA targets. However, the identity and functional role of ciliary PKA substrates, as well as the contribution of additional AKAPs AKAPs to ciliary PKA activity, await further characterization.

The role of AKAP2 in cilium biogenesis and ciliary activities is supported by the identification of a de novo translocation t(7;9)(p14.1;q31.3) on chromosome 9 that disrupts the AKAP2 gene in a patient affected by Kallmann syndrome (KS) [66]. This is a ciliopathy disorder characterized by congenital bone anomalies, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) due to gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) deficiency, anosmia or hyposmia, involuntary mirror movements, dental irregularities, kidney-related complications, midline facial anomalies, hearing impairment, and defects in eye movement [67, 68]. However, the pathogenic role of AKAP2 deletion in KS needs further investigation.

AKAP2 controls several aspects of actin cytoskeleton, being implicated in cell motility, cancer invasion and mitogenic pathways [40, 69–71]. AKAP2 is overexpressed in a variety of epithelial cancer cells and its levels positively correlate with the malignant phenotype and invasiveness potential of cancer cells [39, 40, 72, 73]. AKAP2 undergoes to mitotic degradation through a PLK1/βTrCP-signaling pathway and its proteolysis is required for proper cell cycle progression [74]. Here, we added another layer of complexity in the biological functions of this anchor protein by demonstrating that ciliary localization of AKAP2 in growth-arrested cells is a prerequisite for cilium biogenesis and trafficking. Accordingly, downregulation of AKAP2 in retinal cells impaired the sorting out of GPCRs from cilium in response to Shh pathway activation, indicating that AKAP2 is a sensor and regulator of Shh pathway. Given the role of Shh in different aspects of cancer cell growth and progression, by modulating GLI2-dependent gene transcription, AKAP2 may act as a positive regulator of cancer development [75].

Studies in Medaka fish model confirmed the localization and the role of AKAP2 at primary cilium and implicated the actin- and PKA-binding domains for ciliary localization of the protein. In this model system, AKAP2 silencing significantly affected ciliogenesis, embryo development and survival. Importantly, AKAP2 depletion in the medaka model leads to a clear defect in cilia development, manifested as shorter cilia development rather than a complete loss of cilia. The apparent divergence between the in vivo phenotype and the in vitro observations might reflect biological differences between the two systems rather than a discrepancy in AKAP2 function. In the in vivo medaka context, cilia assemble within a fully polarized, three‑dimensional epithelium, where AKAP2 loss impacts both the initiation of ciliogenesis and the subsequent elongation and maintenance. Conversely, the cell‑based assays are performed in two‑dimensional culture, where cilia extend into an unconstrained environment and exhibit greater intrinsic variability in length. In this setting, AKAP2 depletion primarily compromises ciliogenesis initiation, resulting in a reduced proportion of ciliated cells rather than a consistent shortening of those cilia that do form.

The developmental defects observed in AKAP2-silenced Medaka fish embryos were fully rescued by re-expressing AKAP2 wild type, but not AKAP2 mutants lacking the PKA- or actin-binding activity. Interestingly, both AKAP2 mutants failed to localize at ciliary compartment. The binding of AKAP2 to actin is required to localize the protein at the actin cytoskeleton [40, 76]. Given the actin-based structure of the ciliary axoneme, the actin-binding property of AKAP2 is, indeed, a prerequisite for its localization at primary cilium. We also demonstrate that the binding of PKA to AKAP2 regulates ciliary localization of the protein. This most likely involves the interaction of AKAP2-bound PKA to components of the ciliary compartment, as previously reported [64]. These data suggest that AKAP2 by interacting with PKA and with components of the ciliary cytoskeleton acts as a key signaling node for ciliary trafficking and Shh pathway, regulating both the formation and elongation of primary cilium. The identification of additional AKAP2 partners and the mechanisms underlying the recruitment of AKAP2 at primary cilium will further contribute to the dynamic interplay between compartmentalized cAMP signaling and cilium biology.

In summary, our findings reveal a key role of AKAP2 in cilium biology. The dynamic localization of AKAP2 and PKAII at primary cilium, and its role in ciliary trafficking and signaling represent important steps forward our understanding of the mechanisms regulating ciliary-based PKA pathways. Manipulating this regulatory circuitry may, thus, subserve as a therapeutic option for the treatment of proliferative and developmental disorders.
