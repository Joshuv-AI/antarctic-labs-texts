Exploration of the factors affecting different delirium subtypes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Xinrui Zhang, Wei Zhu, Cong Wang, Xuhua Zhou, Qian Chen, Yan Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, delirium became a major complication that worsened patient outcomes. However, the factors influencing the severity of delirium in patients with COVID-19 have not been determined. We conducted this study to detect influencing factors associated with subtypes of delirium in patients with COVID-19. We included 1774 adult inpatients with COVID-19 from January to February 2023 at 7 sites in China. And used the 3 min Confusion Assessment Method and the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale for site assessment to identify and classify subtypes of delirium. Laboratory data were obtained from the Hospital Information System. After multivariate analysis, hypoactive delirium was significantly associated with age, the serum albumin concentration, frailty and sarcopenia, and health and nutritional status. Mixed delirium was significantly associated with age, D-dimer level, sarcopenia, health status and nutritional status. Additionally, hyperactive delirium was significantly associated with age, procalcitonin levels, frailty status and health status. Our findings suggest that poor nutritional status and low serum albumin concentration can help detect patients at high risk of developing hypoactive and mixed delirium. Additionally, clinical staff should pay more attention to patients with inflammatory conditions to assess and detect delirium because many influencing factors are involved in the common pathological mechanism of inflammation.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

At the end of 2019, novel coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was first reported in Wuhan, China. Since then, it has spread rapidly and become a global public health event that threatens people’s lives, health, and social development1. By May 2023, the cumulative death toll had reached more than 6.92 million2. Although COVID-19 mainly manifests with respiratory symptoms, studies have reported the severe impact of COVID-19 on the neurological system3. COVID-19 can cause different neurological conditions, such as hypoanosmia, myelitis, encephalitis, stroke (ischaemic and haemorrhagic), and autoimmune parainfectious encephalitis3. Among the psychiatric and neuropsychiatric features of COVID-19, delirium is a common manifestation of brain dysfunction during the acute stage4. According to the DSM-5, delirium is defined as a sudden and severe disturbance of attention and consciousness with a fluctuating trend5. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of delirium among hospitalized patients with COVID-19 is 20–30%6, with a higher prevalence among elderly patients and critically ill patients4,7,8. Although delirium has become a major complication in patients with COVID-19, its clinical diagnosis is still insufficient, with less than half of the cases being diagnosed9.

Moreover, the variations in delirium motor subtypes increase the difficulty of diagnosis. Delirium is divided into three subtypes according to its motor activity profile: hypoactive (sedation, lethargy, inattention, and slow movement), hyperactive (agitation, auditory hallucinations, aggression), and mixed (fluctuating between hyperactive and hypoactive)5. Despite their defining motor differences, motor subtypes also differ in terms of detection rates, treatment, and outcomes. For example, hyperactive delirium, as the most common clinical motor subtype10,11, has a poorer diagnostic rate and prognosis and greater mortality than the other two subtypes10,12,13. Underdiagnosis of delirium subtypes can result in patients not receiving timely intervention and management, which in turn leads to more serious consequences, such as prolonged hospital stays, increased mortality and readmission rates, and long-term functional decline14.

Since the importance of considering subtypes has been emphasized in recent studies13, few studies have evaluated the subtypes of delirium in patients with COVID-19. However, it is crucial to explore the exact subtype of delirium in patients and to correct for reversible factors as soon as possible. Therefore, this study aimed to detect the incidence and influencing factors related to the subtypes of delirium in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 to guide clinical staff to identify and manage delirium more accurately.

Method

Study design and participants

This was a multicentre cross-sectional study. This study was conducted at seven tertiary hospitals in four cities of Sichuan Province. The Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Sichuan University approved this study (Code: 2023-171). The Chinese Trial Registry code was ChiCTR2300067933. This study strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in this study.

We used a logistic model with a minimum of 10 outcome events per predictor variable (EPV)15. We included a total of 28 predictors, and the incidence of COVID-19 combined with delirium was 32.4%8. Considering a loss to follow-up rate of 10%, the sample size was calculated to be 951. The inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: admitted to the hospital with COVID-19 from January to February 2023; adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years); had a positive SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid/antigen test (or a history of COVID-19 within 1 month); and voluntary participation in this study. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: had communication barriers; were diagnosed with mental disorders; were diagnosed with delirium but failed to classify the exact subtypes; or whose whole laboratory examinations were missing.

Data collection

In this study, all assessors were given unified training to ensure the homogeneity of the assessments before data collection. To comprehensively evaluate the influencing factors and account for their effects, we incorporated demographic variables (sex, age, visual disorders, dysaudia), behavioural factors (smoking history, alcohol consumption, sleep quality, pain, frailty, sarcopenia), nutritional status (MNA-SF score, albumin) and laboratory test results (blood cell count, biochemical parameters, myocardial markers, D-dimer level, etc.). We used the 3D-CAM, RASS, FRAIL, SAEC-F, MNA-SF, and EQ-5D-3L scales to evaluate patients’ behavioural factors on site and extracted demographic data and laboratory test results from the electronic medical records system.

The assessment scales used in this study and their usage are as follows.

3 min confusion assessment method (3D-CAM)

3D-CAM is a quick, simple, structured tool for delirium assessment that was designed by Marcantonio16 based on CAM. In 2018, translation and cultural adaptation were carried out to construct a Chinese version of the 3D-CAM17. The assessments include the following: Feature 1: acute changes or repeated fluctuations in mental state; Feature 2: attention disorder; Feature 3: change in consciousness level; and Feature 4: disordered thinking. If the patient has Feature 1 and Feature 2 and Feature 3 or Feature 4 at the same time, he or she is considered to have delirium. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of 3D-CAM were high. Thus, it has good reliability and validity and has been widely used in general wards, anaesthesia recovery rooms and other departments18.

The Richmond agitation-sedation scale (RASS)

The RASS, which was proposed by Sessler in 200219, is a 10-point scale with four levels of anxiety or agitation (+ 1 to + 4[combative]), one level of calm and alert state (0), five levels of sedation (− 1 to − 5), and inability to wake up (− 5). Each point corresponds to a state of consciousness. The RASS can be used to evaluate the conditions of patients accurately through simple observation, communication, and stimulation and is used to classify delirium into hyperactive (score ≥ − 1), hypoactive (score ≤ − 1) and mixed (score fluctuates between positive and negative) subtypes19,20.

Fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness and loss of weight (FRAIL) scale

The FRAIL scale, developed by Morley and colleagues21, is a simple five-point scale based on the Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short Form Survey and questions about weight loss and disease. The scale includes 5 components: fatigue, resistance (ability to climb a single flight of stairs), ambulation (ability to walk 100 m), loss of weight of more than 5% and more than 5 of the following diseases: hypertension, diabetes, stroke, cancer, acute cardiovascular disease, congestive heart failure, asthma, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, angina pectoris, and arthritis22. The score ranges from 0 (not frail) to 5 (most frail). Patients are scored 1 for each of these deficits. Patients are considered frail if they score ≥ 3 points on the scale. After translation and cross-cultural adjustment, the scale was translated to Chinese23. The Cronbach’s α coefficient, kappa coefficient, item level of content validity index (I-CVI), and total scale level (S-CVI/Ave) were high. The Chinese version of the FRAIL scale has good reliability and validity.

The strength, assistance with walking, rise from a chair, climb stairs and falls (SARC-F)

The SARC-F is a consensus-recommended tool for screening and identifying sarcopenia patients. It has 5 components: strength, assistance with walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls. Each component is scored from 0 to 2 points, and the total score ranges from 0 to 10. A score equal to or greater than 4 is defined as a predictive sign of sarcopenia24. In 2020, the scale was translated into Chinese and assessed for reliability and validity25. The results showed that the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.803, the CVI was 0.820, and the factor analysis also showed that it had good construct validity.

Mini nutritional assessment short form (MNA-SF)

The MNA-SF, a short form comprising six questions from the full MNA, is used to screen for and diagnose nutritional deficiency26. The questions include those on weight loss, body mass index, stress/acute disease, dementia/depression, mobility, and appetite loss. Each question is 0 to 3 points, and the highest score is 14.12 to 14 points, indicating normal nutrition; 8–11 points indicates the risk of malnutrition; and 0–7 points indicates malnutrition27. The MNA-SF has good sensitivity and accuracy and is recommended for nutritional screening28.

Three-level version of the Euroqol five dimensions questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L)

The EQ-5D-3L is a generic preference-based measure of health. This approach is helpful in making health aid decisions29. This scale comprises 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has three levels, corresponding to different scores. The official Chinese version of the scale was released and has passed reliability and validity tests30. The equivalence of the measurement results with the English version has also been preliminarily proven31.

We used the 3D-CAM and RASS results as the primary outcomes. For the secondary outcomes, we chose the MNA-SF and SARC-F scores and the serum albumin concentration.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described by the means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) according to their distribution. Categorical variables are described by numbers and percentages. ANOVA was used for independent group continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used for variables with a nonnormal distribution. Categorical variables were tested by the chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test was used when the frequency was < 5. The Bonferroni procedure was used for post hoc analysis. After univariate analysis, we included significant factors for further multivariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was subsequently performed to test which factors were independently associated with the occurrence of delirium subtypes in hospitalized patients. For the missing data, we performed 20 multiple imputations and then carried out reliability analysis on these 20 imputation results. Finally, we selected the group with the highest Cronbach’s coefficient. The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethics declaration

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Code: 2023-171). The Chinese Trial Registry code is ChiCTR2300067933. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in this study.

Results of univariate analysis

There were fewer elderly patients (aged ≥ 65) in the nondelirium subgroup than in the delirium subgroup. The incidences of frailty and sarcopenia in the nondelirium group were lower than those in the delirium subgroup. Moreover, the health and nutritional status of patients in the delirium subgroup were poorer than those in the nondelirium subgroup. Significant differences among the four groups were also observed in laboratory findings, including WBC, haemoglobin, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte percentage, urea nitrogen, creatinine, BCR, potassium, calcium, ALT, albumin, glucose, blood uric acid, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, myoglobin and D-dimer. These significant variables were subjected to a post hoc test, and the outcomes are also shown in Tables 1, 2.

Results of multivariate analysis

In the multivariate logistic regression model, the hypoactive type was significantly associated with age (OR = 22.615, 95% CI 13.787–37.097, p < 0.001), the incidence of frailty (OR = 3.504, 95% CI 2.028–6.054, p < 0.001) and sarcopenia (OR = 2.219, 95% CI 1.133–4.001, p = 0.019), health status (OR = 0.025, 95% CI 0.010–0.062, p < 0.001) and nutritional status (OR = 0.915, 95% CI 0.854–0.981, p = 0.013) and albumin concentration (OR = 0.953, 95% CI 0.924–0.983, p = 0.002) compared with the patients without delirium. For the mixed-type and nondelirium groups, the significant factors were age (OR = 8.733, 95% CI 4.263–17.888, p < 0.001), the incidence of sarcopenia (OR = 5.801, 95% CI 1.242–27.098, p < 0.025), health status (OR = 0.024, 95% CI 0.006–0.095, p < 0.001), nutritional status (OR = 0.833, 95% CI 0.743–0.934, p = 0.002) and D-dimer level (OR = 1.044, 95% CI 1.001–1.089, p = 0.043). The hyperactive type was significantly associated with age (OR = 16.748, 95% CI 5.440–51.558, p < 0.001), the incidence of frailty (OR = 3.634, 95% CI 1.021–12.930, p = 0.046), health status (OR = 0.044, 95% CI 0.006–0.351, p = 0.003) and procalcitonin levels (OR = 1.057, 95% CI 1.003–1.114, p = 0.037). Discussion

In this observational study, after adjusting for confounding factors, we found that hypoactive delirium was significantly associated with age, the serum albumin concentration, frailty and sarcopenia, and health and nutritional status. Mixed delirium was significantly associated with age, D-dimer level, sarcopenia, health status and nutritional status. Additionally, hyperactive delirium was significantly associated with age, procalcitonin levels, frailty status and health status.

In our study, we reported an incidence of 15.3% delirium among hospitalized COVID-19 patients, which was lower than that reported in previous studies6,8,32,33. As our study was performed during the COVID-19 pandemic, the complexity of the multiple medical morbidities made delirium assessment difficult34. Although fluctuations in motor performance have been demonstrated to be a unique sign of delirium35, fluctuations in motor performance are difficult to diagnose35. Delirious phenomena also overlap with differential diagnoses, such as pain, dementia, and depression. Moreover, systematic assessments of delirium are absent from current COVID-19 management guidelines, and clinical staff lack the ability to recognize delirium as an atypical presentation of the disease6. Failure to reach clinical agreement regarding the subtype of delirium also leads to a missed diagnosis. These factors may increase the risk of delirium not being detected and explain why the incidence in our study was inconsistent with that in previous studies.

Advanced age is a predisposing factor for developing delirium36. This study showed that age has an impact on the subtypes of delirium as well. Elderly people are more susceptible to both SARS-CoV-2 infection and delirium, which leads to higher mortality rates, increased length of stay and long-term dysfunction37,38. Older adults who have COVID-19 are more likely to present with atypical symptoms, notably delirium38. Whereas delirium has been proven to be an independent factor of adverse outcomes and is thought to be a prognostic marker in COVID-19 patients39, delirium assessment should be regularly performed for older people to prevent poor outcomes.

Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome or health state that leaves individuals vulnerable to adverse health outcomes, including dependence and mortality40,41. Our multivariate model showed a significant association between frailty and at least a threefold increase in the odds of developing different types of delirium. This finding suggested that frail patients are inherently vulnerable to COVID-19 and that patients may have poor tolerance of physiologic stressors41. Because we assessed only patients older than 60 years, it is possible to infer that frailty is a triggering factor for hypoactive and hyperactive delirium onset in aged people infected with COVID-19 because they may have a blunted inflammatory response to the infection42.

Sarcopenia, which refers to the loss of muscle mass, is considered an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment in the general population43. The ratio of urea nitrogen to creatinine (BCR) reflects chronic muscle catabolism and is a promising biomarker44,45. A BCR > 18 was reported to be an independent risk factor for delirium46,47. A previous study also revealed that a high BCR was associated with the development of hypoactive delirium in critically ill patients48. However, after we adjusted for confounding factors, we detected no significant differences in the incidence of BCR or any of the other subtypes of delirium in our study. The most intuitive reason was that we included participants from general wards, and chronic muscle breakdown was not as severe as that in intensive care unit (ICU) inpatients. In addition, inflammation is an etiopathogenesis of sarcopenia and shares common pathophysiological mediators with pneumonia and delirium49. Thus, it can be hypothesized that sarcopenic patients have high levels of interleukin (IL)-6 and C-reactive protein50 and an increased risk of developing delirium. Furthermore, hyperactive delirium patients are usually able to exhibit overt signs of hyperactivity and have higher detection rates, which will lead to more aggressive treatment, thus prompting better conditions51,52.

Notably, in this study, nutritional status, especially among patients with hypoactive and mixed delirium, was significantly associated with delirium in COVID-19 patients. The MNA-SF also supported this conclusion. To our knowledge, previous studies have usually focused on the potential relationship between delirium and nutritional status in postoperative patients and have shown correlations between them53,54. However, studies on the associations between nutritional status and different subtypes of delirium are still lacking. Thus, our research may fill this gap in the related field. Nutrition is considered an essential factor for all body functions, and the serum albumin concentration has been extensively used as a biomarker of nutritional status55. The brain is an organ that has extreme nutritional demands and high metabolic activity; therefore, it is reasonable that nutrients influence the occurrence of delirium56. Patients with COVID-19 and delirium usually exhibit malnutrition and a low serum albumin concentration. SARS-CoV-2 attaches to the ACE2 receptor, which is expressed on intestinal epithelial cells57. This receptor can control the uptake of nutrients by binding to amino acid transporters57. Therefore, it can be inferred that SARS-CoV-2 may compete with protein nutrients and destroy absorption through the ACE2 receptor57. Because of inflammation, COVID-19 patients often suffer from fever, loss of appetite, increased catabolism, and abnormal intestinal absorption, which in turn leads to malnutrition57. Given that albumin is a negative acute phase protein, its synthesis rate is affected by nutrition and inflammation58. When the protein intake decreases, the rate of albumin synthesis decreases, and the serum albumin concentration decreases. However, the pathological process of malnutrition and low serum albumin concentrations has not been fully elucidated. It can be hypothesized that patients with low albumin levels are more vulnerable to developing hypoactive and mixed delirium because their nutritional status is poorer than that of patients with nondelirium or hyperactive delirium. Poor nutritional status inhibits the ability to mount an effective response to infection59. It also shows an increased risk of developing adverse health events. In this study, we investigated the occurrence of delirium. While hypoactive delirium is associated with a sense of apathy and inattention60, patients are more likely to experience somnolence, which may result in a loss of appetite and decreased nutritional intake. Moreover, patients with the hypoactive type are more likely to have neuropathology51, which leads to severe low albumin levels. There are few studies about mixed delirium. We speculate that due to the fluctuation between low and high activity in patients with mixed-type delirium, symptoms such as drowsiness, agitation, and aggression may occur. This may cause decreased appetite, reduced nutritional intake, and increased self-consumption, which ultimately lead to decreased albumin levels and malnutrition. Poor nutritional status, low albumin levels and the occurrence of delirium can lead to a vicious cycle and ultimately lead to poor outcomes. Another assumption is that acute inflammation increases vascular permeability and worsens hepatic protein synthesis, causing a reduction in the albumin concentration61. In addition, the risk of delirium, particularly of the hypoactive and mixed types, increases. However, it is not possible to establish causality in a cross-sectional study, and the mechanism and influencing factors of the three motor subtypes remain unclear51. Thus, further analytical and interventional studies are needed to explore nutrients that can alleviate delirium in COVID-19 patients.

D-dimer is a marker of fibrin formation and degradation62. Elevated D-dimer levels associated with an increased incidence of delirium might suggest that deranged coagulation contributes to the pathogenesis of delirium. It can be inferred that the greater the D-dimer level is, the greater the possibility of developing mixed delirium. However, studies focused on the underlying mechanism between mixed delirium and hypercoagulability are urgently needed.

Many of the significant factors mentioned above share a common pathology inflammation49,61. Given that the distinct pathophysiologies of motor subtypes are unknown, inflammation may be a good entry point. Regular assessment of hypoactive and mixed delirium in patients with low albumin levels and poor nutritional status should be emphasized, as early detection and examination may reverse poor outcomes.

Our study had several strengths. First, we aimed to investigate the influence of the subtypes of delirium on COVID-19 patients, not only compare delirium and nondelirium groups. Second, we used validated tools to assess delirium, whereas some studies have used self-reports and medical records to determine the cause of delirium. However, this study also has several limitations. First, as this was a cross-sectional study, we failed to establish a causal relationship between nutritional status and hypoactive and mixed delirium. Second, we did not consider some specific biomarkers associated with delirium, such as the S100β protein or neuron-specific enolase (NSE). These findings may provide good inspiration for our future research. Third, we used Bonferroni correction for post hoc comparisons according to our research design and requirements. However, the characteristics of this method might lead to an increase in the probability of type II errors. We failed to power a priori to detect differences in any of these outcomes. In a subsequent study, we will also choose a method that can control the overall error rate and better balance test efficacy and fidelity. Fourth, because of the low clinical prevalence of hyperactive delirium and the limited overall sample size, the sample size may have been too small for multivariate analysis. The next step could be to obtain more reliable results by expanding the sample size.

Conclusion

In summary, age, the serum albumin concentration, procalcitonin and D-dimer levels, frailty and sarcopenia, and health and nutritional status are significantly associated with the development of different delirium subtypes. Meanwhile, the present study provides further evidence that poor nutritional status and low serum albumin concentrations could help detect patients at high risk of developing hypoactive and mixed delirium. Additionally, clinical staff should pay more attention to inflammation in patients to assess and diagnose delirium because many influencing factors are involved in the common pathological mechanism of inflammation. However, further research is warranted to confirm these associations.
