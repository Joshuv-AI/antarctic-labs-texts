Line‐field confocal optical coherence tomography discloses an unexpected traumatic tattoo confirmed by histopathology following a car accident
Authors: Anna Elisa Verzì, Alessia Villani, Rosario Caltabiano, Giuseppe Micali, Francesco Lacarrubba
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

A 61‐year‐old female was referred to our Clinic for the presence of multiple, asymptomatic pigmented lesions localized on the left elbow. She reported about 3 months earlier a road accident with a car overturning. She was sitting in the driver's seat with her seatbelt on, and the impact caused several bruises and abrasions, especially to the upper limbs, but no fractures. The patient noticed the onset of the pigmented lesions once the wounds healed. On physical examination, several non‐palpable dark blue to black, irregular macules were detected (Figure 1A), and polarized light dermoscopy showed a blue‐grayish pigmentation associated with structureless‐whitish areas (Figure 1B). Line‐field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC‐OCT) revealed a normal epidermis and, in the dermis, the presence of several ovalar or irregular hyperreflecting structures of different sizes (Figures 2A,2B). Based on personal history and clinical and instrumental evaluation, the diagnosis of the traumatic tattoo was suspected. Histopathology of one lesion confirmed the diagnosis by showing multiple, dermal‐based, foci of chronic granulomatous inflammation surrounding dark‐colored amorphous material (Figure 2C).

Dark blue to black, irregular macules on the left elbow. (A) Polarized light dermoscopy shows blue‐grayish pigmentation and structureless‐whitish areas (B).

Vertical (A) and horizontal (B) LC‐OCT shows a normal epidermis and, in the dermis, the presence of several ovalar or irregular hyperreflecting structures of different sizes (arrows). Histopathology (C) shows multiple, dermal‐based, foci of chronic granulomatous inflammation surrounding dark‐colored amorphous material (hematoxylin and eosin; original magnification 150 ×). LC‐OCT, line‐field confocal optical coherence tomography.

As regards the possible causes, the lesions could clinically resemble an asphalt tattoo, but the patient insisted she remained inside the car during the impact. We believe that the violent rubbing of the left elbow on the lining of the car side door, which was made of plastic material, caused the penetration of foreign bodies into the skin as confirmed by LC‐OCT and histopathology.

Traumatic tattoos arise from foreign particles that penetrate and then deposit in the more superficial cutaneous layers at the time of an accident and are often more visible after wound healing. The use of some noninvasive diagnostic techniques, including dermoscopy and reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM), in their evaluation has been seldom described. 1 , 2

LC‐OCT is an emerging noninvasive tool that has been demonstrated to be useful in several neoplastic, inflammatory, and infectious skin disorders. 3 , 4 One case only has been reported so far with LC‐OCT of a traumatic graphite tattoo resulting from an accidental injury with a pencil tip. 5 Similarly to our case, the authors observed the presence of roundish homogeneous hyper‐reflective structures in the mid dermis suggestive of a foreign body, but no histopathology confirmation was provided. In our case, the hyperreflecting structures of different sizes seen with LC‐OCT perfectly corresponded to the dark‐colored amorphous material observed in histopathology. Compared to RCM, the LC‐OCT vertical viewing allows the exact location of the foreign material, thus providing important management clues.

In conclusion, although the diagnosis of traumatic tattoos is generally based on a patient's personal history and clinical examination, LC‐OCT may represent a valuable technique in doubtful cases.
