Pathogenic glycosyltransferase genes and potential therapeutic drugs in pressure overload‐induced heart failure
Authors: Jiahe Wu, Yi Lu, Xinchen Gao, Xiaorong Hu, Zhibing Lu, Chenze Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsProtein glycosylation regulated by glycosyltransferases is an important type of post‐translational modification. The role of the glycosyltransferase genes (GTGs) in heart failure (HF) remains unclear and requires further investigation.MethodsDifferential expression analysis was performed on the transverse aortic constriction (TAC)‐related dataset GSE36074 to screen out the differentially expressed GTGs. Enrichment and protein–protein interaction analyses explored their functional mechanisms and interconnections. Pearson correlation analysis revealed the relationship between GTGs and pathological cardiac remodelling. The upstream miRNAs of GTGs were predicted using corresponding online databases, and the downstream target genes were identified by weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA). Computer virtual screening and molecular docking predicted potential therapeutic drugs. The identified GTGs were validated in vivo, in vitro and in the human HF‐related dataset GSE57338.ResultsTwenty‐one differentially expressed GTGs were identified, and these genes were significantly up‐regulated in the TAC model except for C1galt1, Extl2 and Pigh. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that 11 GTGs were significantly associated with pathological cardiac remodelling. Fifty‐six miRNAs and 31 drugs were predicted to target these GTGs. WGCNA indicated that these GTGs were associated with lipid metabolism‐related genes and pathways. Up‐regulation of B3gnt9, C1galt1, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b was observed in the TAC model. GXYLT2 is up‐regulated and has high disease‐predictive value in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy and ischaemic cardiomyopathy. Knockdown of GXYLT2 in human AC16 cardiomyocytes significantly attenuated angiotensin II (AngII)‐induced hypertrophy.ConclusionsDysregulation of GTG expression may affect TAC‐induced HF through metabolic pathways, and GXYLT2 may be a new potential therapeutic target for HF.Keywords: bioinformatics, glycosyltransferase genes, heart failure, therapeutic drugs, transverse aortic constriction

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a serious threat to human life and health. Despite significant progress in treatment, CVD still kills more than 20 million people every year, accounting for one third of all deaths worldwide. 1 , 2 Heart failure (HF) is the final stage of various CVDs. Myocardial infarction, cardiac overload, cardiomyopathy, inflammation and other factors can cause changes in myocardial structure and function, eventually leading to cardiac systolic or diastolic dysfunction. Studies have confirmed that when the myocardium is injured, the adaptive compensation mechanism is activated to maintain the function of the heart, which is mainly manifested by the hypertrophy of cardiomyocytes, the increase of ventricular thickness and volume, and the aggravation of myocardial fibrosis. 3 , 4 When the damage continues to worsen, decompensation occurs, eventually causing HF.

Protein glycosylation regulated by glycosyltransferases is an important type of post‐translational modification. Under the catalysis of glycosyltransferase, the glycosyl group forms covalent links with some residues on proteins, such as glycosidic bonds, which affect the molecular structure and function of proteins. 5 In recent years, with the innovation of transcriptomics and glycoproteomics research technology, the role of glycosylation in CVDs has been increasingly recognized. 6 It has been reported that the level of protein O‐linked N‐acetylglucosamin (O‐GlcNAc) modification is significantly increased in human diabetic myocardium, and targeted regulation of O‐GlcNAc transferase can save left ventricular diastolic function and prevent poor cardiac remodelling in diabetic mice. 7 It has also been found that glycosylation induced by continuous hypoxic acclimation can play an antioxidant and cardiac protective role in alleviating myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion injury (MI/RI). 8 However, the exact mechanism by which the glycosyltransferase gene (GTG) affects pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF is still far from elucidated and requires further investigation.

The construction of animal models is a crucial method to study the pathology and mechanism of CVDs. Transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery in mice is a common method to study pathological cardiac hypertrophy. TAC increases the afterload of the heart by constricting the aorta and can be used to simulate cardiac hypertrophy and chronic HF caused by hypertension or increased intraventricular pressure. In this study, we systematically searched the TAC‐related datasets (TAC surgery; the sample source was myocardial tissue; the species was Mus musculus) in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database, performed data mining to identify key GTGs and conducted preliminary experimental verification. Enrichment, protein–protein interaction (PPI) and weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) methods explored their mechanisms and interconnections. Therapeutic drugs were identified using computer virtual screening and molecular docking. This study will provide new clues for the mechanism research and treatment strategy of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and chronic HF.

Methods

Data resource

The transcriptome datasets GSE36074, GSE225067 and GSE57338 were obtained from the GEO database. 9 , 10 , 11 GSE36074 is a TAC‐related mRNA microarray dataset with five sham‐operated mice and seven TAC‐induced HF mice. GSE225067 contains complete miRNA expression profile data of heart samples from four sham‐operated mice and five TAC‐induced HF mice. GSE57338 enrolled 136 healthy donors, 82 patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and 95 patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM). Table 1 presents the details of these three datasets.

Identification of differentially expressed GTGs

Differential expression analysis was performed on GSE36074 using the ‘Limma’ package of R software (Version 4.1.0). 12 The analysis process included background correction, normalization, log transformation and screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The P value was corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. DEGs were defined as |logFC| ≥ 0.5 [fold change (FC)] and an adjusted P value <0.05. The ‘ggplot2’ package and ‘pheatmap’ package were used to generate the corresponding volcano plots and heat maps. 13 , 14

The previously published literature was integrated, and a list of 240 GTGs was obtained (Data S1). 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 These GTGs were intersected with DEGs using the Venn diagram online drawing website (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to get the differentially expressed GTGs.

Enrichment analysis

The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp) is a widely used tool for gene function annotation. 19 This database was used to carry out Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on the identified genes. GO annotation consists of three parts: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF). P < 0.05 was set as the filtering threshold for meaningful pathways.

PPI analysis

PPI analysis of these identified GTGs was performed by the STRING database (Version 12.0, https://cn.string‐db.org/) to explore the correlation between the proteins expressed by these genes. 20 Based on the predicted protein interactions, Cytoscape software (Version 3.10.0) was used to visualize the results and draw the network diagram.

Correlation analysis

Nppb, Nppa, Myh7 and Ctgf are widely recognized biomarkers for pathological cardiac hypertrophy. 21 To further explore the relationship between the identified GTGs and HF, Pearson correlation analysis of these pathological cardiac hypertrophy biomarkers and differentially expressed GTGs was performed based on their original gene expression data in GSE36074. P < 0.05 was defined as the presence of a correlation between two genes.

miRNA prediction and construction of miRNA–mRNA regulatory network

Dataset GSE225067 contains complete miRNA expression profile data for TAC‐induced HF in mice. GSE225067 comes from ‘ORB MiRBase 16 Multispecies MicroRNA Microarray’ of the GPL33116 Platform and contains heart samples from four sham‐operated mice and five TAC‐induced HF mice. The differential expression analysis of GSE225067 was carried out using the same method as the DEGs, and the differentially expressed miRNAs were obtained. Online databases TargetScan (Version 7.2, https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) and miRDB (https://mirdb.org/) predicted miRNAs targeting the identified GTGs. The predicted molecular regulatory relationships and the information of differentially expressed miRNAs in GSE225067 were integrated, and the corresponding miRNA–mRNA regulatory network was plotted by Cytoscape software (Version 3.10.0).

WGCNA

To further explore the molecular mechanism of the identified GTGs, we used the ‘WGCNA package’ of R software to identify gene modules that were significantly associated with GTGs. 22 The detailed method was as follows: (1) Outlier genes and samples were removed using the goodSamplesGenes method of the ‘WGCNA package’; (2) construction of the Pearson correlation matrix; (3) the optimal soft threshold was selected, and the Pearson correlation matrix was converted to a weighted adjacency matrix (the scale‐free topology fit index for different values of β was observed; when the index reached 0.9, the β value was determined to be the optimal soft threshold); (4) the adjacency was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM); (5) to classify genes with similar expression profiles into gene modules, the average linkage hierarchical clustering was conducted according to the TOM‐based dissimilarity measure; and (6) the module that significantly correlated with the largest number of GTGs was selected as the key module for subsequent analysis.

Drug prediction and molecular docking

Drug SIGnatures DataBase (DSigDB, https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) and Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/) are two well‐known drug‐related databases that provide drugs or small molecules that target specific genes. 23 , 24 These two databases were used to predict potential therapeutic drugs targeting GTGs and construct the corresponding drug–GTG regulatory network.

To further explore the binding sites and binding strength of drugs and proteins, molecular docking was performed by the Cavity‐detection guided Blind Docking Software (CB‐DOCK2, https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb‐dock2/php/blinddock.php#job_list_load). 25 Based on the results of molecular docking, a smaller value of Vina score indicates stronger intermolecular binding.

Animal acquisition and model construction

All animal experiments met the standards of ‘the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85‐23, revised 1985)’ and were ethically reviewed and approved by ‘the Animal Experiment Center of Wuhan University’ (Approval No. ZN2022235). Male C57BL/6 mice (wild‐type, 8‐week‐old and specific pathogen‐free) were purchased from the Shanghai Animal Laboratory Center. Mice were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 30 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium followed by TAC surgery, and the control group was treated with sham surgery. 26 Four weeks after TAC, mice were euthanized (intraperitoneal injection of 200 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium), and myocardial tissue was harvested for subsequent detection and validation.

Cell culture and processing

The human myocardial cell line AC16 (Bena Culture Collection, Beijing, China) 27 was stimulated with angiotensin II (AngII) (MCE, Shanghai, China) to establish the cell hypertrophy model to simulate cardiac remodelling. Detailed methods were as follows: Cells were cultured to the appropriate density (50%–60%) with complete medium [Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) + 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) + 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA)]. Subsequently, cells were stimulated with medium with a final concentration of 10 μM AngII, 28 and samples were harvested for subsequent detection after continuing to culture the cells for 48 h.

siRNA transfection experiments

The cells were seeded into six‐well plates and cultured to an appropriate confluence (50%–60%). According to the manufacturer's instructions, Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was used as the transfection reagent and co‐incubated with siRNA for 20 min. The mixed transfection reagent was added to the culture medium, and the final transfection concentration of siRNA was 50 nM. After 8 h, the old medium was replaced with fresh medium containing AngII. Samples were harvested for subsequent detection after continuing to culture the cells for 48 h. siRNAs were designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Detailed sequence information of the siRNAs used in this study was presented in Data S2.

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Freshly isolated hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by tissue dehydration, paraffin embedding and transverse sectioning (5 μm). Subsequently, H&E staining was performed on the tissue sections to observe the morphological characteristics of myocardial tissue. The Leica DMI3000B microscope was used to obtain the corresponding image.

Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining

Freshly isolated hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by tissue dehydration, paraffin embedding and transverse sectioning (5 μm). Subsequently, WGA staining was performed on the tissue sections to observe the size of the cardiomyocytes. The inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to obtain the corresponding image, and the cell membrane was shown in green.

For cultured cardiomyocytes, cell slides were prepared, followed by WGA staining using a similar method to that for tissue. Cell area was calculated using the ImageJ software.

Echocardiographic measurement

Four weeks after TAC, M‐mode echocardiography was performed by ‘small animal ultrasound imaging system VINNO D860 LAB (YEERAN, Beijing, China)’ to evaluate cardiac structure and function. Through echocardiographic monitoring, six characteristic indexes [interventricular septal thickness at systole (IVSs), interventricular septal thickness at diastole (IVSd), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at systole (LVPWs), left ventricular posterior wall thickness at diastole (LVPWd), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS)] were obtained. In addition, the body weight (BW), heart weight (HW) and tibia length (TL) of the mice were recorded, and the two indexes, HW/BW and HW/TL, were calculated at the time of harvesting.

Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR)

In vivo (TAC) and in vitro (AngII‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy) models were constructed to mimic HF and pathological myocardial remodelling. The expression differences of the identified GTGs were further verified by RT‐qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA by the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China), and RT‐qPCR was carried out with the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Relative mRNA levels were normalized to that of Gapdh using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The mouse primer sequences in this study are listed in Data S2.

Further validation of identified GTGs in human HF‐related dataset

GSE57338 enrolled 136 healthy donors, 82 patients with DCM and 95 patients with ICM. The samples in GSE57338 were all from human myocardial tissue. 11 The original gene expression data in GSE57338 were downloaded to further verify the expression differences of the identified GTGs in DCM and ICM. Independent sample t test was performed to compare gene expression differences between the two groups, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The R software (Version 4.1.0) was used for bioinformatics analysis and the drawing of relevant statistical maps. The bioinformatics images are validated and optimized by the Sangerbox (Version 3.0, http://vip.sangerbox.com/login.html) online platform. 29 The analysis and drawing of experimental data were completed by GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0.0). All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. Independent sample t test was used to compare the differences in gene expression between the two groups. One‐way ANOVA was used to compare differences between three or more groups. All data are expressed as mean values ± standard error of mean (SEM), and a value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GTG regulation patterns in TAC‐induced HF

Differential expression analysis was performed on TAC‐induced HF‐related dataset GSE36074, and a total of 1896 DEGs were screened out, of which 1135 were up‐regulated and 761 were down‐regulated (Figure 1A,B). Data S3 shows the details of these DEGs. A total of 240 GTGs were obtained from the literature in previous studies. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 The intersection of these DEGs and GTGs obtained 21 differentially expressed GTGs, of which 18 were up‐regulated (B3gnt2, B3gnt8, B3gnt9, B4galnt1, B4galt5, Chpf, Colgalt1, Galc, Galnt10, Gcnt1, Gxylt2, Mgat5b, Pigm, Plod3, Pofut2, Pygl, St3gal2 and St6galnac4) and 3 were down‐regulated (C1galt1, Extl2 and Pigh) (Figure 1C). Figure 1D presents the heat map of the top 10 up‐regulated DEGs and the top 10 down‐regulated DEGs. Figure 1E presents the heat map of the identified 21 differentially expressed GTGs. The expression changes of these genes are obvious in these two heat maps.

GO annotation indicated that the main enriched items for these differentially expressed GTGs were ‘protein glycosylation’, ‘protein O‐linked glycosylation’, ‘poly‐N‐acetyllactosamine biosynthetic process’, ‘glycoprotein biosynthetic process’, ‘sphingolipid metabolic process’, ‘membrane’, ‘Golgi apparatus’ and ‘transferase activity, transferring glycosyl groups’ (Figure 1F). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these genes were mainly involved in ‘metabolic pathways’, ‘mucin‐type O‐glycan biosynthesis’, ‘sphingolipid metabolism’ and ‘glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis’ signalling pathways (Figure 1G). Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between the 21 identified differentially expressed GTGs in GSE36074 (Figure 1H). In the correlation heat map, blue indicates a negative correlation between two genes, while red indicates a positive correlation. Darker colours indicate larger absolute values of the correlation coefficients. The results showed that Colgalt1 and Pofut2 had a strong positive correlation (correlation coefficient = 0.96), Chpf and St6galnac4 had a strong positive correlation (correlation coefficient = 0.85), but B3gnt8 and Extl2 had a strong negative correlation (correlation coefficient = −0.93). The proteins expressed by these GTGs were further subjected to PPI analysis to evaluate their intrinsic association. Finally, a PPI network with 19 nodes and 23 edges was constructed (Figure 1I).

Identification of GTGs significantly associated with pathological cardiac hypertrophy

Nppb is a well‐recognized neurohormonal indicator for evaluating left ventricular function. Nppa and Myh7 are widely used as biomarkers in the detection of cardiac hypertrophy. Ctgf is a key gene that affects the degree of myocardial fibrosis. These four genes are widely involved in the process of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF. 30 , 31 , 32 As shown in Figure 2A–D, Nppb, Nppa, Myh7 and Ctgf are significantly increased in TAC‐induced HF. Pearson correlation analysis was further performed to evaluate the correlation of the identified GTGs with these pathological cardiac hypertrophy biomarkers (Figure 2E). Finally, 11 GTGs (B3gnt9, C1galt1, Extl2, Galc, Galnt10, Gcnt1, Gxylt2, Mgat5b, Pigm, Plod3 and St6galnac4) were found to be significantly associated with all the biomarkers. Of these 11 GTGs, Galnt10 is most significantly correlated with Nppb (correlation coefficient = 0.85), and St6galnac4 is most significantly correlated with Nppa (correlation coefficient = 0.95), Myh7 (correlation coefficient = 0.93) and Ctgf (correlation coefficient = 0.95) (Figure 2F–I).

Prediction of GTG‐related miRNAs and construction of the miRNA–mRNA regulatory network

Differential expression analysis was performed on the TAC‐induced HF‐related miRNA dataset GSE225067, and a total of 247 differentially expressed miRNAs were screened out, of which 38 were up‐regulated and 209 were down‐regulated (Figure 3A,B). Two online websites (TargetScan and miRDB) were used to predict the miRNAs targeting GTGs. The prediction results and differentially expressed miRNAs were integrated to construct a miRNA–mRNA regulatory network (Figure 3C). This regulatory network incorporates 57 miRNAs and 10 GTGs, consisting of 65 nodes and 71 edges. Notably, 13 miRNAs (mmu‐miR‐3106‐5p, mmu‐miR‐3094‐3p, mmu‐miR‐335‐3p, mmu‐miR‐466l‐5p, mmu‐miR‐182‐5p, mmu‐miR‐684, mmu‐miR‐544‐5p, mmu‐miR‐3475‐3p, mmu‐miR‐26a‐5p, mmu‐miR‐325‐3p, mmu‐miR‐669b‐5p, mmu‐miR‐1193‐3p and mmu‐miR‐137‐3p) simultaneously targeted two or more GTGs.

WGCNA revealed gene modules significantly associated with GTGs

To further explore the downstream mechanisms, WGCNA was performed to identify the gene modules most associated with GTGs (Figure 3D,E). The results showed that the ‘lightcyan1’ gene module was significantly correlated with all 11 GTGs, and their absolute correlation coefficients were greater than 0.7. A total of 2761 genes in the ‘lightcyan1’ gene module were intersected with DEGs, and 1002 differentially expressed module genes were obtained (Figure 3F). GO annotation indicated that these differentially expressed module genes were mainly involved in ‘cell adhesion’, ‘lipid metabolic process’, ‘apoptotic process’, ‘positive regulation of apoptotic process’ and ‘extracellular matrix organization’ BPs (Figure 3G). The results in Figures 1G and 3G all showed that the GTGs were significantly related to metabolism or lipid metabolism, so we further focused on lipid metabolism‐related genes (LMRGs). We obtained 102 differentially expressed LMRGs by intersecting the 1591 LMRGs in the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) with the differentially expressed module genes (Figure 3H). The proteins expressed by these differentially expressed LMRGs were further subjected to PPI analysis to evaluate their intrinsic association. Finally, a PPI network with 76 nodes and 180 edges was constructed (Figure 3I). The maximum clique centrality (MCC) and Degree algorithms were further used to identify the key genes in this PPI network (Figure 3J,K). The results showed that Acaa2, Acacb, Cpt2, Dgat2, Eci1, Ehhadh, Pdgfrb, Plcg2, Retsat, Slc27a1 and Tgfb1 ranked top 15 in the results calculated by both algorithms and were considered as key LMRGs.

Association between key LMRGs and GTGs

Further observation of the expression trends of key LMRGs in GSE36074 showed that all of them were significantly down‐regulated except for Pdgfrb, Plcg2 and Tgfb1 (Figure 4A). Pearson correlation analysis was further performed to evaluate the correlation of the identified GTGs with these key LMRGs (Figure 4B). The top 4 significantly positively correlated GTG–LMRG pairs were C1galt1–Retsat (correlation coefficient = 0.92), St6galnac4–Pdgfrb (correlation coefficient = 0.91), St6galnac4–Plcg2 (correlation coefficient = 0.91) and Plod3–Plcg2 (correlation coefficient = 0.90) (Figure 4C–F). The top 4 significantly negatively correlated GTG–LMRG pairs were Pigm–Cpt2 (correlation coefficient = −0.96), Gcnt1–Acacb (correlation coefficient = −0.95), Pigm–Eci1 (correlation coefficient = −0.95) and Pigm–Acacb (correlation coefficient = −0.93) (Figure 4G–J).

Drug prediction and molecular docking

Online databases DSigDB and CTD predicted 31 potential therapeutic drugs that might target the GTGs. Based on the predicted correspondence, a drug–GTG regulatory network with 42 nodes and 54 edges was plotted (Figure 5A). Notably, 10 drugs (acetaminophen, copper sulfate, cyclophosphamide, oestradiol, formaldehyde, hydrogen peroxide, quercetin, resveratrol, trichostatin A and valproic acid) simultaneously targeted two or more GTGs.

Further molecular docking was performed by the CB‐DOCK software, and then the binding strength was evaluated. It was generally considered that Vina score <−7 indicates that the drug and protein have a strong binding ability. The top 6 tightly bound drug–GTG pairs were Plod3–puerarin (Vina score = −11.0), Plod3–chlorogenic acid (Vina score = −10.1), Plod3–doxorubicin (Vina score = −10.1), Gxylt2–oestradiol (Vina score = −9.9), Plod3–genistein (Vina score = −9.7), C1galt1–vincristine (Vina score = −9.6) (Figure 5B–G). Notably, high binding affinity was also observed between Plod3 and trichostatin A (Vina score = −8.9), as well as between Extl2 and quercetin (Vina score = −9.4).

Validation of HF‐related GTGs in the TAC‐induced HF model

To further validate the analysis results, the mouse TAC model was constructed (the aortic arch of the mice was narrowed and subsequently tested 4 weeks after surgery) (Figure 6A). H&E staining, WGA staining and echocardiography were carried out to evaluate whether the TAC model was successfully established (Figure 6B–D). The results showed that the myocardial cell area (Figure 6B,C) and myocardial thickness (Figure 6D–J) of TAC group mice were significantly increased, while the cardiac function was significantly decreased (Figure 6K,L). These results suggested that the pressure overload (TAC)‐induced HF model was successfully constructed.

The expression of the predicted GTGs was examined by RT‐qPCR. As shown in Figure 6M, the expression of B3gnt9, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b was significantly up‐regulated, while the expression of C1galt1 was significantly down‐regulated, and the expression of Extl2, Galc, Galnt10, Pigm, Plod3 and St6galnac4 was not significantly different in the TAC‐induced HF mouse model compared with the sham‐operated group. The expression trend of B3gnt9, C1galt1, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b was consistent with the previous prediction results. Together, B3gnt9, C1galt1, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b were significantly different in the analysis dataset and the TAC‐induced HF mouse experimental model. Their expression levels were significantly correlated with the expression levels of pathological cardiac hypertrophy biomarkers. These five GTGs may be new potential therapeutic targets for HF.

Validation of HF‐related GTGs in the human HF‐related dataset GSE57338

The expression trends of B3GNT9, C1GALT1, GCNT1, GXYLT2 and MGAT5B were further evaluated in the external dataset GSE57338 (Figure 7A–E). The results showed that B3GNT9 and GXYLT2 increased significantly in DCM, while MGAT5B decreased significantly. B3GNT9, GCNT1 and GXYLT2 increased significantly in ICM. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic value of these genes (Figure 7F–J). The results showed that GXYLT2 had a high diagnostic value for both DCM [area under the curve (AUC) = 0.7036] and ICM (AUC = 0.7002).

Expression validation and functional validation of GTGs in the AngII‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy model

We further constructed an in vitro model of AngII‐stimulated human cardiac cell line AC16 to validate the expression trends and functions of the identified GTGs (B3GNT9, C1GALT1, GCNT1, GXYLT2 and MGAT5B). RT‐qPCR revealed that after 48 h of AngII treatment in AC16 cardiomyocytes, the myocardial hypertrophy markers BNP and atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) significantly increased (Figure 8A). Among the five GTGs, only GXYLT2 was significantly up‐regulated (Figure 8A). Pearson correlation analysis revealed that in the human HF‐related dataset GSE57338, GXYLT2 was significantly positively correlated with the myocardial hypertrophy marker ANP (Figure 8B). Cells were transfected with three kinds of siRNAs, respectively. The results showed that Si‐GXYLT2‐2 had the highest inhibitory efficiency (Figure 8C). Inhibition of GXYLT2 with Si‐GXYLT2‐2 significantly reversed the BNP and ANP elevation induced by AngII (Figure 8D,E). WGA staining suggested that inhibition of GXYLT2 significantly reversed AngII‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Figure 8F,G). These results indicate that GXYLT2 is a key gene promoting myocardial cell hypertrophy. Intervention in GXYLT2 may be a new therapeutic strategy for HF.

Discussion

HF is the end stage of various CVDs, which seriously threatens human life and health. The role of glycosylation in the occurrence and development of CVDs is increasingly recognized, and the intervention of glycosyltransferases is expected to become a new treatment strategy. 33 , 34 This study conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis and data mining of GTGs in the TAC‐induced HF, aiming to discover new therapeutic targets.

In this study, we performed differential expression analysis on the TAC‐related gene expression dataset GSE36074 and finally found 18 DEGs and 21 differentially expressed GTGs. Notably, all of the identified differentially expressed GTGs were significantly up‐regulated, except for C1galt1, Extl2 and Pigh. Enrichment analysis suggested that these identified GTGs were mainly related to ‘protein O‐linked glycosylation’, ‘glycoprotein biosynthetic process’, ‘sphingolipid metabolic process’, ‘transferase activity’, ‘metabolic pathways’, ‘mucin‐type O‐glycan biosynthesis’, ‘glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis’ and other signalling pathways or BPs. These identified pathways have been partially investigated in the direction of HF. It has been reported that enhanced O‐glycosylation signal transduction can promote the glycosylation modification of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator 1‐alpha (PGC‐1α), inhibit the expression of PGC‐1α and downstream fatty acid oxidation/mitochondrial genes, and further promote cardiac hypertrophy and HF. 35 Another study reported that the endoplasmic retinal protein Nogo‐B is expressed transiently under cardiac stress and down‐regulates sphingoid biosynthesis through serine palmitoyl transferase, thereby inhibiting the progression of stress‐induced HF. 36 Chondroitin sulfate glycosaminoglycans may play a biphasic role in the development of HF, that is, a protective role in the acute phase and a promoting role in the chronic phase. 37 These studies revealed the potential role of the identified pathways in HF; however, the exact mechanisms need to be further explored.

PPI analysis was performed on the identified differentially expressed GTGs, and a PPI network with 19 nodes and 23 edges was constructed. The Pearson correlation analysis assessed the correlation between the proteins expressed by the identified GTGs and also between the identified GTGs and pathological cardiac hypertrophy biomarkers. The results showed that B3gnt9, C1galt1, Extl2, Galc, Galnt10, Gcnt1, Gxylt2, Mgat5b, Pigm, Plod3 and St6galnac4 were significantly associated with pathological cardiac hypertrophy. Among these genes, only Plod3 has been reported to be significantly associated with HF. 38 These newly identified genes may be new targets for the treatment of HF.

miRNAs targeting HF‐associated GTGs were predicted using online sites TargetScan and miRDB. The predicted miRNAs were integrated with the differentially expressed miRNAs in dataset GSE225067, and a miRNA–mRNA regulatory network was constructed. This regulatory network incorporates 57 miRNAs and 10 GTGs. Thirteen of these identified miRNAs (mmu‐miR‐3106‐5p, mmu‐miR‐3094‐3p, mmu‐miR‐335‐3p, mmu‐miR‐466l‐5p, mmu‐miR‐182‐5p, mmu‐miR‐684, mmu‐miR‐544‐5p, mmu‐miR‐3475‐3p, mmu‐miR‐26a‐5p, mmu‐miR‐325‐3p, mmu‐miR‐669b‐5p, mmu‐miR‐1193‐3p and mmu‐miR‐137‐3p) simultaneously target two or more GTGs. Down‐regulation of circular RNA 00091761 has been reported to protect HF after myocardial infarction via the microRNA‐335‐3p/ASCL4 axis. 39 Inhibiting the expression of miR‐182‐5p in AngII‐treated cardiomyocytes will exacerbate cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. 40 MiR‐26a‐5p can alleviate cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction by targeting ADAM17. 41 Therefore, these identified miRNAs may be novel biomarkers or therapeutic targets for HF. To further explore the downstream mechanism, we performed WGCNA and screened the ‘lightcyan1’ gene module. Enrichment analysis showed that the ‘lightcyan1’ gene module was significantly associated with the lipid metabolism process. Further, 102 differentially expressed LMRGs and 11 core LMRGs (Acaa2, Acacb, Cpt2, Dgat2, Eci1, Ehhadh, Pdgfrb, Plcg2, Retsat, Slc27a1 and Tgfb1) were screened in the ‘lightcyan1’ gene module. Genes Acaa2, Cpt2, Dgat2 and Tgfb1 have been reported to be associated with HF and pathological myocardial remodelling. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 Whether the other seven genes affect HF in a similar manner requires further investigation.

Online databases DSigDB and CTD predicted 31 potential therapeutic drugs. Acetaminophen, copper sulfate, cyclophosphamide, oestradiol, formaldehyde, hydrogen peroxide, quercetin, resveratrol, trichostatin A and valproic acid simultaneously targeted two or more GTGs. Further molecular docking predicted the binding sites and strength of these drugs to GTGs. Some of these identified drugs (oestradiol and resveratrol) have been shown to protect against HF. 46 , 47 Whether these identified drugs can treat HF by affecting glycosylation modification has not been reported and requires further investigation.

Further verification found that B3gnt9, C1galt1, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b were significantly different in the TAC‐induced HF mouse experimental model. B3gnt9 has UDP‐glycosyltransferase activity, which is predicted to participate in the biosynthesis process of poly‐N‐acetyllactosamine. 48 C1galt1 forms the precursor of extended O‐glycan and plays an important role in angiogenesis, thrombosis and kidney inflammation. 49 , 50 Gcnt1 is involved in the synthesis of two O‐glycans of the branched mucin‐type core. Gcnt1‐mediated O‐glycosylation of sialomucin CD43 is a sensitive indicator of Notch signalling in activated T cells. 51 Gxylt2 encodes the glycosyltransferase that elongates the O‐linked glucose attached to epidermal growth factor (EGF)‐like repeats in the extracellular domain of Notch proteins by catalysing the addition of xylose. Gxylt2 accelerates cell growth and migration by regulating the Notch pathway. 52 Mgat5b has acetylglucosamine transferase activity and is involved in the glycosylation of protein O‐linked through serine. 53 The roles and mechanisms of these GTGs in HF remain unexplored and therefore represent new directions. The expression trends of B3GNT9, C1GALT1, GCNT1, GXYLT2 and MGAT5B were further evaluated in the human HF‐related dataset GSE57338. The results showed that GXYLT2 was significantly up‐regulated in both DCM and ICM and had a high diagnostic value. Because the role of GXYLT2 in HF has not been reported, we performed deletion experiments in vitro to verify its function. GXYLT2 was significantly up‐regulated in the model of cardiac hypertrophy established by AngII‐stimulated AC16 cardiomyocytes. Inhibition of GXYLT2 significantly attenuated the pro‐cardiomyocyte hypertrophy effect of AngII. Therefore, targeting GXYLT2 may be a novel therapeutic strategy for HF.

This study is based on existing transcriptome dataset mining and preliminary experimental verification, which has certain limitations. (1) The sequencing data itself has certain system errors, which are determined by technology. (2) The sample size for sequencing is limited. (3) Batch effects may have prevented the detection of differential expression in some genes. (4) The TAC mouse model simulating pressure overload‐induced HF is a commonly used HF model, but it cannot fully represent the human HF situation. (5) The feasibility and safety of the identified drugs also need to be further studied. Further experiments are needed to explore the exact role and mechanism of these GTGs involved in the progression of HF. In addition, further exploration of the targets of these GTGs using glycoproteomics will lead to more discoveries.

Conclusions

In summary, the expression pattern of GTGs in pressure overload‐induced HF was explored based on transcriptome data analysis. Most of these GTGs showed a trend of up‐regulation under pressure overload. B3gnt9, C1galt1, Gcnt1, Gxylt2 and Mgat5b were identified as HF‐related GTGs and may be potential biomarkers. The in vitro loss‐of‐function assay confirmed the pro‐hypertrophic effect of GXYLT2. The relationship between these identified GTGs and lipid metabolism was also explored by WGCNA. In addition, computer virtual screening and molecular docking identified a series of potential drugs (acetaminophen, copper sulfate, cyclophosphamide, oestradiol, formaldehyde, etc.) targeting GTGs. This study will provide new insights into the exploration of novel therapeutic targets and strategies for HF.
