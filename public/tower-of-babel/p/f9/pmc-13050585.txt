Patellar luxation causes abnormal mechanical loading and joint destruction in the femoral-tibial joint
Authors: Qinwen Ge, Zhenyu Shi, Xuefeng Li, Congzi Wu, Qi Sun, Weidong Wang, Luwei Xiao, Di Chen, Peijian Tong, Ping-er Wang, Hongting Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsPatellar luxation can cause abnormal mechanical loading and is a leading cause of patellofemoral joint damage, however its contribution to femoral-tibial joint damage is poorly elucidated. In this study, we sought to investigate the role of patellar luxation in the femoral-tibial joint in both mice and humans.MethodsIn this study, we generated a patellar luxation mouse model based on the standard operating procedure of destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery which mimics osteoarthritis (OA). Then phenotypes including gait analysis, osteophyte formation, aberrant subchondral bone remodelling, cartilage degeneration, and synovitis of femoral-tibial joint were evaluated in vivo at eight weeks after surgery. Additionally, we also performed a retrospective study to investigate femoral-tibial joint characteristics in 18 patients with post-traumatic OA or recurrent patellar luxation.ResultsWe found that patellar luxation caused abnormal gait and augmented osteophyte outgrowth around the tibial plateau and patella. Furthermore, micro-CT analysis showed that mice treated with patellar luxation operation exhibited aberrant subchondral bone remodelling. Intriguingly, according to Safranin-O/Fast Green staining, mice with patellar luxation showed better articular cartilage integrity and less cartilage proteoglycan damage in the medial compartment of the femoral-tibial joint. Moreover, either patellar luxation operation or DMM surgery promoted the expression of TNF-α and F4/80 in synovium tissue. However, mice subjected to patellar luxation exhibited aggressive cartilage erosion in the lateral compartment of the knee joint. In addition, the clinical study showed that patellar luxation was associated with lateral cartilage degeneration of the femoral-tibial joint.ConclusionThis study confirmed that lateral patellar luxation induced subchondral bone osteopenia, promoted osteophyte formation as well as synovial inflammation, and caused lateral cartilage rather than medial cartilage degeneration in the femoral-tibial joint.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(4):363–374.

Introduction

Lateral patellar luxation is a common disease of the knee joint, which primarily occurs in young and active individuals.1,2 It has been reported that the annual incidence of a first-time patellar dislocation is 23.2 per 100,000 person-years in adolescents aged between 14 and 18 years.2 Once patellar luxation occurs, the collision between the patella and femoral condyle often leads to patellofemoral cartilage lesion, acute pain, and even femoral trochlear dysplasia in the knee joint,3,4 leading to an increased episodic instability and limited athletic performance.5,6 Patellofemoral cartilage deterioration and osteoarthritis (OA) are the most common characteristics observed in patients with recurrent patellar dislocation.1,7 It is reported that up to 97% of patients develop concomitant cartilage lesions after patellar dislocation.8

The instability of the patellofemoral joint generally affects the knee function. In most conditions, lateral patellar dislocation is capable of changing the mechanical force distribution of the knee joint, and results in mechanical underloading in the medial compartment and overloading in the lateral compartment of femoral-tibial joint.9,10 It has been shown that excessive mechanical force on load-bearing joints tends to result in OA development associated with articular cartilage degradation, synovial hyperplasia, subchondral sclerosis, and osteophyte formation.11-13 Specifically, articular cartilage subjected to excessive mechanical stimuli often leads to impaired cartilage metabolism and cell death in chondrocytes.14-16 Moreover, it is well established that mechanical loading is involved in regulating bone homeostasis, especially in bone formation.17,18 Previous studies have reported that a deficiency in mechanical sensing protein Piezo1 in osteoblastic cells leads to osteopenia.19,20 Furthermore, altered mechanical loading triggers cytokine release from subchondral bone matrix and induces excessive osteoblastic bone formation that ultimately causes subchondral bone sclerosis.21-24 These recent findings have identified that subchondral bone remodelling is a crucial regulator in OA pathogenesis.25,26

Despite increasing attention being paid to the effects of patellar dislocation on the patellofemoral joint, the contribution of patella luxation to the femoral-tibial joint is poorly elucidated. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of patellar luxation on femoral-tibial joint in mice and human patients with recurrent patellar dislocation.

Methods

Mice

In the present study, to rule out sex differences, 40 ten-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were used. The mice were treated with a 12-hour light and 12-hour darkness circle and obtained food and water ad libitum. We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and have included the ARRIVE checklist in the Supplementary Material.

DMM and patellar luxation surgery

As previously described, destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery was employed for mice OA induction, which resulted in articular cartilage lesions primarily on the central weightbearing region of the medial tibial plateau and medial femoral condyles.27 In brief, the mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane and a capsulotomy was performed in the right knee. Then, the patella was dislocated laterally to give greater exposure of the femoral-tibial joint and the medial meniscotibial ligament (MMTL), which anchors the anterior horn of the medial meniscus to the tibial plateau, of which only the tibial plateau was dissected completely to cause instability of medial meniscus. The patella needs to be restored back in its original position after DMM surgery.27 In this study, however, we did not put the patella back in place, in order to produce a permanent patellar luxation model. Meanwhile, in the sham-operated group, mice were anaesthetized and only subjected to capsulotomy without any other operation.

Gait analysis

In this study, we used the DigiGait imaging system (Mouse Specifics, Inc., USA) to analyze mouse gait. In brief, the transparent conveyor belt on the treadmill moves at a speed of 15 cm/s, allowing mice to run on the treadmill, while capturing relevant data using ventral plane imaging technology. Mice were recorded running for at least five seconds and ten full strides. Before the formal experiment, all mice underwent gait training three times. Here, according to previous studies,13 we mainly evaluated the right hind limb, in which the mice underwent a DMM or patellar luxation operation, as the observed subject, and gait parameters including swing duration time, stance duration time, stride length, and paw area of the right hind limb.

Micro-CT analysis

All the knee samples were scanned by micro-CT scanner (SkyScan, 1276; Bruker, USA) with parameters at 55-kV, 200-μA, and a 421 ms exposure time. According to previous studies, the region of interest (ROI) was drawn from subchondral bone of medial tibial plateau compartment and five consecutive sagittal plane images of ROI were used for 3D reconstruction by visualization software (SkyScan, CTVolx, v 3.0).21,28 In addition, quantitation of bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) of ROI were analyzed using SkyScan, CTAn, v 1.15. Additionally, osteophyte formations were estimated by 3D reconstruction with visualization software (SkyScan, CTVolx, v 3.0).

Histology

For histological analyses, knee joint samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for three days and then decalcified in 14% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for two weeks. Subsequently, all the samples were dehydrated by an automatic dehydrator (Tissue-Tek VIP 5Jr; Sakura Seiki, Japan) following paraffin embedding. Knee joint samples were sectioned sagittally into 3 μm thickness slices for staining. Following deparaffinage and rehydration, paraffin sections were subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining to evaluate the osteoclast activity. In addition, we also performed Safranin-O/Fast-Green (SO/FG) and alcian blue haematoxylin/orange G (ABH/OG) staining for morphological observation on cartilage tissue or synovium tissue. We adopted the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI)29 score to appraise articular cartilage degeneration, while osteophyte scores and synovitis scores described by previous studies were employed for assessments.30,31

Immunohistochemistry

After deparaffinization and dehydration, for antigen retrieval, slices were soaked in sodium citrate solution and heated at 60°C for four hours. Subsequently, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and incubated for secondary antibodies for 20 minutes on the next day. The positive signal was visualized with DAB reagents (ZSGB-BIO) and slices were counterstained with haematoxylin. Primary antibodies including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (ARG57422, 1:300, Arigo, Taiwan), collagen type II alpha 1 (COL2A1) (ab34712, 1:500, Abcam, UK), tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (ARG56080, 1:300, Arigo), F4/80 (ab100790, 1:100, Abcam), collagen type X alpha 1 (COL10A1) (260040, 1:100, Abcam), and matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13) (ab39012, 1:200, Abcam) were employed for immunohistochemistry in the current study. Quantitative analysis was performed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Clinical data

We retrospectively analyzed nine patients, between January 2017 and December 2022, who were diagnosed with patellar dislocation using radiological parameters including lateral patellofemoral angle (LPFA), congruence angle, intercondylar angle, and patellofemoral index.32 The control group consisted of age-matched individuals diagnosed with post-traumatic OA by radiograph or MRI using American College of Rheumatology criteria.33,34 The medial and lateral cartilage thickness of tibia was measured in MRI. Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as mean (SD), and the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v. 7.0; GraphPad, USA). For two group comparison, independent two-samples t-test was applied while comparisons among groups employed two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison’s test. Additionally, Fisher’s exact tests were carried out for categorical variables. p-values less than 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Patellar luxation surgery caused aberrant gait in the hind limbs of mice

Initially, we investigated the mice’s gait after patellar luxation or DMM surgery. As expected, dynamic gait analyses at eight weeks postoperatively showed that mice treated with patellar luxation or DMM experienced abnormality in stride length compared with Sham mice (Figures 1a and 1b). Additionally, we found that, compared to Sham operation, both patellar luxation and DMM operation caused a prolonged swing duration time of the right lower limb (Figure 1c). Conversely, the stance duration time was decreased after the operation (Figure 1d). Furthermore, Sham-operated mice exhibited a large pawprint of right hind limb, indicating more contact with the ground and willingness to increase load, while a reduced pawprint area indicates less contact with the ground and reduced willingness to load, mainly due to joint pain and dysfunction in luxation, DMM, and luxation + DMM group (Figure 1e). Hence, these gait analyses reflected that patellar luxation caused serious gait abnormalities, leading to aberrant mechanical loading.

Patellar luxation promoted osteophyte formation but caused osteopenia in subchondral bone

Next, we established the patellar dislocation murine model and examined whether patellar luxation affects osteophyte formation and subchondral bone microarchitecture using micro-CT analysis. As shown in micro-CT images and 3D reconstruction images of the knee joint, we found that patellar luxation, either with or without DMM surgery, caused notable osteophyte outgrowth around the tibial plateau, and mice that only received DMM surgery exhibited moderate marginal osteophyte formations on medial tibial plateau eight weeks after surgery (Figures 2a to 2c). Notably, 3D reconstruction images of subchondral bone revealed that patellar luxation caused significant bone loss, while mice subjected to DMM developed obvious subchondral bone sclerosis (Figure 2b). Quantification of subchondral bone microstructure revealed significant lower bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in mice that underwent patellar luxation surgery (Figures 2d and 2e). Conversely, subchondral trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) was dramatically increased in DMM mice that underwent patellar luxation operation (Figure 2f). Taken together, these data demonstrate that patellar luxation promotes osteophyte formation around the joint but caused visible osteopenia in subchondral bone, which may be explained by the fact that patellar luxation leads to mechanical underloading and subsequently causes bone loss.

Patellar luxation impaired force-induced bone formation in subchondral bone

Given the solid osteopenia phenotype in subchondral bone caused by patellar luxation, we carried out immunohistochemistry of ALP as well as TRAP staining to gain insight into the subchondral bone remodelling response to abnormal mechanical loading. Our data showed a significant reduction of ALP-positive area in subchondral bone after patellar luxation surgery. By contrast, ALP expression in subchondral bone was visibly higher in DMM-treated mice than in sham-operated mice. Interestingly, increased anabolic bone response elicited by DMM surgery was diminished by the patella luxation operation (Figures 3a and 3b), indicating that patellar luxation could severely impair the bone formation mediated by mechanical loading. Furthermore, according to the results from the TRAP staining, which indicated the osteoclastic bone resorption in subchondral bone, we found that more pronounced effects were apparent in the DMM mice rather than patellar luxation (Figures 3c and 3d). Thus, these results revealed that the distinct subchondral bone loss resulted from patellar luxation was mainly due to the weakening of loading-induced bone formation.

Patellar luxation preserved the integrity of underloading articular cartilage

To further determine whether patellar luxation give rise to femoral-tibial cartilage destruction, we performed SO/FG staining on knee joint sections. Intriguingly, in contrast to DMM-induced mice, which exhibited severe cartilage degeneration, mice with patellar luxation showed better articular cartilage integrity and less cartilage proteoglycan damage than DMM-operated mice (Figure 4a). Furthermore, histological scores were comparable between mice that underwent the sham operation and mice the underwent patellar luxation (Figure 4b). In addition, we also performed immunohistochemistry and found that COL2A1 expression in articular cartilage of mice with patellar luxation was similar to that of the sham-operated mice. In contrast, DMM surgery significantly reduced COL2A1 expression (Figure 4c). Quantification results indicated that there was no significant difference between the sham group and patellar luxation group (Figure 4d). Meanwhile, we also evaluated the cartilage catabolism phenotype with immunohistochemistry of COL10A1 as well as MMP-13. Our data showed a notable increase in both COL10A1 and MMP-13 protein levels in the articular cartilage of DMM-induced mice. Conversely, mice receiving patellar luxation showed mildly increased cartilage catabolism compare to the sham group (Figures 4e to 4h). Thus, these investigations confirmed that patellar luxation, to a certain extent, protected the medial compartment of the femoral-tibial joint against cartilage damage and demonstrated an uncoupling phenomenon between osteophyte formation and cartilage degeneration, suggesting that massive osteophyte outgrowth does not necessarily correlate with severe cartilage damage.

Patellar luxation caused synovial inflammation in femoral-tibial joint

We also evaluated synovial inflammation in anteromedial tibia at eight weeks postoperatively. As demonstrated by the result of ABH/OG staining, we observed that either patellar luxation operation or DMM surgery could lead to aggressive synovitis phenotypes in anteromedial tibia (Figures 5a and 5b), which was supported by immunohistochemical staining for TNF-α in synovium tissue (Figure 5c). Likewise, both patellar luxation and DMM operation were found to augment the expression of F4/80, a specific marker of macrophages, in the synovium of anteromedial tibia (Figures 5d and 5e). These findings also revealed that subchondral bone sclerosis is more likely associated with articular cartilage destruction, while osteophyte formation is closely related to synovial inflammation mediated by aberrant mechanical loading.

Patellar luxation exacerbated lateral cartilage damage degeneration

Considering that patellar luxation may disrupt mechanical loading distribution of the knee joint, we performed a comparative analysis between medial cartilage and lateral cartilage of the femoral-tibial joint. Additionally, in line with micro-CT results, ABH/OG staining confirmed that patellar luxation caused osteophyte formation around the tibia plateau in mice, which was similar to the DMM operation (Figure 6a). As determined by the coronal section of knee joint, the medial cartilage remained virtually unchanged in response to patellar luxation, while DMM operation caused severe articular cartilage degeneration (Figures 6a and 6b). Conversely, we found that patellar luxation, whether accompanied by DMM operation or not, would give rise to aggressive abrasion in lateral cartilage (Figures 6a and 6c). To determine whether this phenomenon was also present in human clinical cases, we performed a retrospective comparative study in OA patients and recurrent patellar luxation patients. In this study, patients with patellar luxation were diagnosed mainly by radiological parameters including lateral patellofemoral angle (LPFA), congruence angle, intercondylar angle, and patellofemoral index, while the control group individuals were selected from age-matched OA patients (Table I). Importantly, MRI data exhibited a reduced tibia lateral cartilage thickness in patellar luxation patients when compared to medial cartilage (Figures 6d and 6e). By contrast, OA patients showed thinner medial cartilage (Figures 6d and 6e). Additionally, we found that compared to the OA group, the patellar luxation group exhibited decreased lateral cartilage thickness (Figures 6d and 6e). Furthermore, patellar luxation patients were more prone to bone marrow oedema in the lateral distal femur (Table I). These results suggest that patellar luxation may cause articular cartilage degeneration in the lateral, but not the medial, compartment of the femoral-tibial joint.

Discussion

Despite previous research having identified that patellar luxation contributes to articular cartilage lesions of the patella and lateral femoral condyle,25 the role of patellar luxation in the medial as well as the lateral compartment of the femoral-tibial joint still remains largely unexplored.35 An increasing number of studies have revealed the important roles of mechanical loading in regulating cartilage homeostasis and bone remodelling.12,19,36 In this study, we employed a lateral patellar luxation and DMM mice model to induce mechanical underloading and overloading, respectively, on the medial compartment of the knee joint.

Current research on patellar dislocation animal models primarily focuses on larger animals such as dogs and rabbits.37,38 A previous study has shown that trochlear dysplasia can be caused by patellar dislocation in growing rabbits, accompanied by significant subchondral bone loss and patellofemoral joint cartilage injury.39 However, mouse models offer notable advantages in the following aspects: as a model organism, mice benefit from mature gene-editing technologies, enabling the construction of transgenic or knockout models, which facilitates precise investigation into the role of specific genes in the pathogenesis of patellar luxation. Compared to larger animals, mice exhibit lower housing costs and shorter reproductive cycles, reducing experimental expenses and accelerating research progress.

In the present study, we have, for the first time, established the role of lateral patellar luxation which caused abnormal mechanical loading on the femoral-tibial joint in mice. We provided the evidence that patellar luxation is able to cause subchondral bone osteopenia but promote osteophyte formation around a knee joint. Additionally, we found the integrity of medial articular cartilage was not significantly affected by the lack of stress loading, even in the presence of local severe inflammation.

OA is regarded as a whole-joint disease that is primarily characterized by articular cartilage degeneration, while also involving aberrant subchondral bone remodelling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation. However, the causal relationship of these features remains inconclusive.40-42 In OA, especially at the late stage, subchondral osteosclerosis is often accompanied by increased osteophyte formation.43,44 Nevertheless, in this study, we discovered that patellar luxation contributed to subchondral bone osteopenia and osteophyte outgrowth around the femoral-tibial joint. Interestingly, either mechanical underloading mediated by patellar luxation or mechanical overloading induced by DMM operation boosted osteophyte formation around the medial tibial plateau. Consistent with our results, a recent clinical study has revealed that patellar dislocation patients had lower bone mineral density in the femur and tibia compared to patients with anterior cruciate ligament ruptures.45 These observations support the idea that subchondral osteosclerosis and osteophyte formation are not always the concomitant events in the femoral-tibial joint.

Synovitis is correlated with the severity of OA clinical symptoms and cartilage degradation.42,46 Here, we found that both patellar luxation and DMM operation elevated the expression of TNF-α and F4/80 in synovium tissue and led to obvious hyperplasia of the synovial lining at the anteromedial tibia. Together with previous findings,47,48 it is possible that synovial inflammation in mice with patellar luxation might be secondary to aberrant mechanical loading. Strikingly, unlike mice treated with DMM surgery that caused mechanical overloading, we did not observe surface cartilage lesion or apparent loss of cartilage matrix in the medial femoral-tibial joint of mice with patellar luxation. In line with experimental results, our retrospective study from clinical patients with patellar dislocation also observed a decreased cartilage thickness in the lateral compartment. Previous studies have generally indicated that mechanical force is one of the primary risk factors for OA in the synovial joint, and induces important signalling that influences chondrocyte behaviour like catabolic enzyme production and ferroptosis.49-52 These results revealed that patellar luxation would cause overt cartilage degeneration in the lateral compartment, rather than the medial compartment, largely due to the increased mechanical loading in lateral cartilage, and indicated that excessive mechanical loading is perhaps a bigger contributor than inflammation to articular cartilage degeneration.

A number of previous studies have posited that osteophyte formation is related to the degeneration of articular cartilage and OA progression.53-55 However, according to our findings, mice that received patellar luxation exhibited an uncoupling phenomenon between osteophyte formation and cartilage degeneration. There were increased osteophytes in the joint, but the medial articular cartilage was still intact. In other words, osteophyte formation is not necessarily secondary to cartilage degeneration, and is closely related to synovial inflammation mediated by aberrant mechanical loading. Similarly, it has been confirmed that articular cartilage does not contribute to osteophyte formation, whereas skeletal progenitors from the periosteum and synovium cooperate to participate in osteophyte formation during OA progression.53,56 These findings provide new insights into the concomitant relationship of OA pathological features. Specifically, subchondral bone sclerosis is more likely associated with articular cartilage destruction, while osteophyte formation is closely related to synovial inflammation.

In addition, we observed that when combined with patellar luxation surgery, the DMM-induced OA progression in the medial cartilage was interrupted. This was largely because that patellar luxation reduced weight bearing in the medial compartment. A 2018 study reported that there was still a small probability of patellar dislocation even if the patella is immediately reset into trochlear notch during DMM operation.57 Therefore, it is worth noting that knee joint samples with patellar luxation should be excluded in femoral-tibial OA investigations in animal models.

Our study had some limitations: first, only a single timepoint was selected for observation in the present study, whereas relevant phenotypes at other timepoints (either earlier or later) were not evaluated. Second,the underlying molecular regulatory mechanism of the impaired force-induced bone formation, excessive osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation in the femoral-tibial joint caused by patellar dislocation is still in need of further exploration. Finally, the sample size of clinical observation of OA and patellar luxation patients should be enlarged in future studies to consolidate our findings.

To a certain extent, this work provides new insights into the role of patellar luxation in femoral-tibial joint, as well as the concomitant relationship among OA pathological features, and highlights that abnormal mechanical loading may deserve more attention than synovitis in the clinical diagnosis and treatment for OA as well as patellar luxation.
