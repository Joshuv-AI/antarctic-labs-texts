Lung organoids and organ-on-a-chip models in respiratory disease research: advances, applications, and challenges
Authors: Xiaojing Wang, Yue Wang, Changyong Wang, Tianyi Zhang, Sheng Yang, Geyu Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung organoids are human-derived in vitro models that can form three-dimensional structures in culture. They provide useful systems for respiratory disease research. This review first outlines the main cell sources and construction methods for lung organoids. It then discusses how these models have been used to study pulmonary fibrosis, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and respiratory infections. The review also explains how organ-on-a-chip technology can add flow, stretch, and other physical cues to organoid models. These cues help researchers build more dynamic systems, but they do not remove the need for careful validation. Finally, this review summarizes key challenges in standardization, model complexity, and clinical translation. It also describes future directions for personalized and precision respiratory medicine.Keywords: Lung organoid, Organ-on-a-chip, Disease modeling, Drug screening, Personalized medicine

Introduction

Respiratory diseases place a major burden on public health and health-care systems. These diseases include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary fibrosis, lung cancer, and infections caused by emerging respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 [1]. Researchers have long used two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures and animal models to study these diseases and to test new treatments. These models remain valuable, but they have clear limits. A 2D culture does not fully show cell polarity, three-dimensional (3D) tissue structure, or communication between epithelial, stromal, and immune cells. Animal models, especially rodent models, differ from humans in airway anatomy, mucus behavior, and immune responses [2]. These differences can make it hard to model chronic human lung disease. They can also affect risk assessment for new environmental pollutants, such as nanoplastics [3–5]. These limits have led researchers to develop in vitro models that capture selected features of the human lung more closely.

Lung organoids (LOs) and lung-on-a-chip (LoC) models can help address some of these limits. Researchers can generate LOs from patient tissue, adult stem cells, or pluripotent stem cells. These models can retain parts of the cellular diversity and genetic background of the source tissue [6–9]. LoC models add controlled physical cues. These cues include cyclic stretch, an air-liquid interface (ALI), and epithelial-endothelial co-culture in microfluidic devices [10–13]. When researchers combine organoids with chip systems, they can study human-derived cells in a more controlled 3D environment. This approach can support studies of inflammation, tissue repair, and drug response [14–17].

Regulatory and funding changes have also shaped this field. In the United States, the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 allows organoids and organ-on-a-chip models to contribute to non-animal drug testing strategies [18]. In Europe, funded programs have supported organoid and organ bioengineering projects related to regenerative and precision medicine [19]. In China, the 14th Five-Year National Key R&D Program includes stem cell research and organ repair as priority areas [20]. These programs show growing interest in organoid and chip-based models. They also highlight the need to define where these models are useful and where they still need validation.

This review summarizes recent work on lung organoids and organ-on-a-chip models. It focuses on COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, lung cancer, and respiratory infections. It also discusses how these systems can model selected parts of the tumor microenvironment and host-pathogen interactions. The review then considers current barriers and future directions. To keep the review balanced, we also include foundational organoid studies, lung epithelial progenitor studies, and broader microphysiological-system research. These studies show both the strengths and the limits of these models [6–9, 11, 12, 21–26].

Cellular sources and engineering strategies for lung organoids and organs-on-chips

Building useful in vitro lung models requires two basic steps. First, researchers need cell sources that match the biological question. Second, they need culture and engineering methods that guide cell organization and approximate the lung microenvironment. This section describes the main cell sources for lung organoids. Cellular sources of lung organoids

Researchers mainly generate lung organoids from three cell sources. These sources are pluripotent stem cells, adult lung stem or progenitor cells, and lung tumor tissue [21–24, 27–30]. Each source has different strengths and limits. Foundational studies of organoids, airway basal cells, AT2 cells, and directed differentiation provide the basis for these approaches [6–9, 21–24].

Pluripotent Stem Cells (PSCs): iPSCs can model selected steps of embryonic lung development in vitro. Researchers guide these cells through definitive endoderm and anterior foregut endoderm stages. This process produces NKX2-1 + lung progenitors. Signals such as WNT, FGF, BMP, and TGF-β then guide these progenitors toward alveolar epithelial cells, ciliated cells, goblet cells, and other lung lineages [23, 24, 27, 31, 32]. Recent studies have generated NKX2-1 + bud tip-like progenitor organoids that can form airway and alveolar cell types [27]. Other studies have produced airway epithelium and alveolar epithelial type 2-like cells from human pluripotent stem cells [23, 24]. In many protocols, NKX2-1 + precursors appear within about 2–3 weeks. More mature airway or alveolar organoids often require another 4–8 weeks of culture. iPSC-derived organoids are useful for personalized modeling and drug testing. However, ESC-based work still raises ethical and sourcing issues, as noted in the ISSCR 2021 guidelines [33].

Adult Lung Stem/Progenitor Cells: Adult stem or progenitor cell-derived organoids can reflect features of the mature lung. Airway basal cells can self-renew and form luminal airway lineages. AT2 cells can also act as stem cells in the adult alveolus [21, 22]. Sachs et al. developed a method to expand basal cells from surgical samples or bronchial brushings into airway organoids [28]. These organoids contain a pseudostratified epithelium with ciliated, goblet, Club, and basal cells. Researchers now use this system for studies of infection, genetic disease, and toxicology [28]. AT2 cells can also be sorted with HTII-280 and grown into alveolar organoids. These organoids can shift toward an AT1-like fate and can support fibrosis and drug-response studies [29]. A main limitation is that these models depend on clinical samples. Donor-to-donor variation can also reduce reproducibility.

Patient-Derived Tumor Organoids (PDOs): PDOs are useful models for lung cancer research. Researchers usually establish them from fresh tumor tissue. These models can retain parts of the genomic profile, histology, and drug sensitivity of the original tumor [30]. ALI culture and immune-cell co-culture can help PDOs retain some features of the tumor microenvironment [34, 35]. These methods may support studies of therapy response, drug resistance, and combination treatment. Hu et al. showed that microwell-array PDO testing could predict patient drug responses within one week in a lung cancer setting [36]. Even so, PDO results should be interpreted with care because immune, stromal, and vascular components are often incomplete.

Construction strategies: from static 3D culture to dynamic chip integration

Lung organoid engineering follows two main principles. Researchers first provide a 3D scaffold that supports cell growth and self-organization. They then apply timed biochemical signals that guide cell fate. This approach is consistent with broader organoid engineering work on matrix composition, soluble factors, physical cues, and quantitative validation [6–9]. Matrigel and collagen provide common 3D extracellular matrices (ECMs) [37, 38]. Synthetic or chemically defined hydrogels, including PEG-based materials, can give researchers more control over stiffness and biochemical signals [37, 39]. Within these scaffolds, WNT, BMP, and FGF signaling help guide lineage choice and branching [31, 38].

Different construction methods use these principles in different ways. iPSC-derived models use stepwise small-molecule treatment to mimic developmental signals [31, 32]. Adult stem cell-derived models use tissue progenitors to study repair and homeostasis in the adult lung [28]. Fetal stem cells can help model early lung formation [40]. Patient-derived cells can help build disease-specific models that reflect selected disease features [41].

ALI culture can improve mucociliary differentiation and barrier function in airway models [42–44]. Static culture still has a key limit: it does not reproduce lung motion or blood flow. Researchers address this problem by combining lung organoids with microfluidic LoC systems. These lung-organoid-on-a-chip (LOoC) systems can provide continuous flow, drug delivery, and mechanical stimulation [2, 13, 45]. Many devices use PDMS and multilayer microchannels made by soft lithography [13]. A common design has two channels separated by a porous flexible membrane. Researchers culture epithelial cells or organoids in the upper channel, often at an ALI. They perfuse the lower channel to model the pulmonary microvasculature [2]. Some chips use vacuum chambers to stretch the membrane and mimic breathing motion [45]. This stretch can affect cell function, viral response, and immune signaling. Some devices also include sensors, such as TEER sensors, to monitor barrier function over time [46]. These systems can add useful dynamic features, but each device still needs validation for its intended use.

Applications of lung organoids and organ-on-a-chip models in disease modeling

Traditional 2D cultures and animal models have provided important information. However, they do not fully capture human airway structure, multicellular interaction, or lung biomechanics [47, 48]. Lung organoids and lung-on-a-chip platforms add human-derived cells, 3D organization, and controlled physical cues [14–17]. This section reviews how these tools have been used in four disease areas: infectious disease, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer. Fig. 3 summarizes the main applications.

Lung organoid and organ-on-a-chip platforms for modeling major pulmonary diseases. Lung organoids and lung-on-a-chip platforms can model selected features of major pulmonary diseases. These diseases include infectious disease, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer. Microfluidic systems can add perfusion, ALI culture, and mechanical cues to 3D organoid models. In infection studies, these models can support work on host-pathogen interaction and antiviral screening. In COPD and exposure studies, they can support toxicity testing and inflammation analysis under defined conditions. In pulmonary fibrosis, these models can show selected features of epithelial injury, fibroblast activation, ECM remodeling, and drug response. In lung cancer, PDOs and chip systems can help model parts of the tumor microenvironment. Overall, these platforms are useful human-relevant research tools. They still require validation against clinical and in-vivo evidence

Researchers should interpret organoid and chip results in the context of each model. Many systems remain simplified. They can show batch-to-batch and donor-to-donor variation. They also do not fully reproduce immune, vascular, neural, or whole-body interactions. For this reason, researchers should combine their results with evidence from clinical samples, animal studies, and multi-omics datasets [9, 11, 12, 25, 26].

Infectious diseases

LOs and LoCs are useful tools for studying host-pathogen interactions in the respiratory tract. Airway and alveolar organoids maintained at an ALI can show cilia-mucus barriers, epithelial polarity, and viral receptor patterns. LoC systems can add cyclic stretch and continuous flow to model airway ventilation and microcirculation [49–51]. Human AT2- and airway-derived organoids have been used to model SARS-CoV-2 infection. These studies support the relevance of ACE2/TMPRSS2-dependent entry. They also show NF-kappaB-associated inflammatory programs, interferon signaling, and barrier disruption [43, 65]. These models can also support medium-throughput antiviral screening. Remdesivir and molnupiravir show dose-dependent inhibition in these systems. TMPRSS2 inhibitors, including nafamostat and camostat, reduce viral invasion and replication in airway organoids [66–68].

Human airway-on-chip and alveolus-on-chip models add controlled viral exposure, drug delivery, and immune-cell perfusion. For example, an Airway-on-a-Chip model captures parts of clinical influenza replication, epithelial injury, and oseltamivir response windows [45]. An Alveolus-on-a-Chip model showed that breathing-like motion can alter innate immune responses and contribute to endothelial inflammation during SARS-CoV-2 infection [52]. Other lung microphysiological systems have used automated or deep-learning readouts to monitor inflammatory responses in controlled lung-related settings [54].

Researchers have also used organoids and chip systems to study bacterial infection, tuberculosis, and broader host-pathogen interaction [69–71]. These models can support studies of epithelial barrier injury and host-directed therapy. They can also help researchers track signaling pathways and screen compounds that strengthen epithelial integrity or reduce excess cytokine responses [70–72].

Together, these studies show that LOs and LoCs can model selected stages of respiratory infection. These stages include pathogen entry, epithelial injury, and early host responses. The models may also support early antiviral screening and pandemic preparedness. Their value depends on cell composition, immune-cell integration, exposure conditions, viral dose, and clinical validation. They should be used as complementary infection models, not as full replacements for in-vivo or patient-based evidence.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

COPD causes persistent airflow limitation, chronic inflammation, and airway remodeling [73]. It remains a major cause of illness and death worldwide. Key features include small-airway fibrosis, goblet-cell hyperplasia, mucus overproduction, ciliary dysfunction, and alveolar destruction [74–76]. Long-term exposure to cigarette smoke, air pollutants, and workplace particles are established risk factors. Recent evidence also suggests that inhaled polystyrene nanoplastics may induce COPD-like lung injury [77].

Several mechanisms contribute to COPD. These mechanisms include inflammatory-cell infiltration, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, protease-antiprotease imbalance, and abnormal epithelial-mesenchymal communication [74, 76, 78]. Patient-derived or pluripotent-stem-cell-based COPD organoids can model some of these features. Reported phenotypes include strong inflammatory responses, altered differentiation paths, and emphysema-like alveolar injury [79, 80].

Small-airway chips can model selected COPD features under ALI conditions. These features include goblet-cell metaplasia and impaired ciliary function. The chips also allow controlled cigarette-smoke exposure and gene-expression analysis of smoke-induced epithelial injury [2]. The newer CFAX12 platform combines cyclic stretch with continuous smoke delivery. This design may improve the study of cigarette smoke, e-cigarette aerosols, and particulate toxicity [55].

Chip and organoid studies have also examined smoke- and infection-related epithelial injury, neutrophil recruitment, host serine-protease activity, and inflammatory responses [2, 81, 82]. PM2.5 or diesel exhaust exposure can increase pro-inflammatory cytokines, apoptosis, and impaired epithelial repair in organoid or lung-on-chip models [15, 83]. Inhaled nanoplastics can cause oxidative-stress-related mitochondrial injury and ferritinophagy-dependent ferroptosis in lung injury models [84]. Non-coding RNA biomarkers have also been used to track lung injury and repair after nanoplastic exposure [85].

LOoC platforms can add useful physiological cues to COPD models. However, long-term immune and stromal co-cultures remain difficult. Standard media, matrices, exposure protocols, and chip designs are also needed to improve reproducibility. Future models may include immune cells, multi-organ links, and multi-omics readouts. Current systems should still be viewed mainly as controlled exposure or mechanism-oriented models. Their use in precision medicine will require comparison with patient samples, long-term clinical phenotypes, and treatment outcomes.

Pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis, especially idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), involves abnormal epithelial repair, fibroblast activation, and ECM deposition. These changes lead to irreversible tissue remodeling. The disease process often forms a cycle. Epithelial injury triggers fibrotic signaling and tissue stiffening. Tissue stiffening then increases mechanical stress and further epithelial dysfunction.

Lung organoids can help researchers study parts of this cycle. TGF-β1 exposure can induce fibrogenesis and ECM accumulation in iPSC-derived alveolar organoids. These models also show epithelial senescence markers, including p16 and p21, and a senescence-associated secretory phenotype [86, 87]. Other organoid models use pro-fibrotic cytokines to generate KRT5+/Trp63 + basal-like cells. This response resembles an abnormal alveolar regeneration axis linked to IPF progression [88].

Co-culture models that pair AT2 cells with fibroblasts have helped researchers study fibroblast-derived STAT3 signaling and AT2 differentiation [89]. Microengineered stromal mimetics and organ-on-a-chip models add mechanical features to fibrosis studies. Matera et al. built ECM-degradation-dependent interstitial scaffolds that model IPF-related fibrotic networks [58]. Papamichail et al. added cyclic strain to organoid-on-chip systems. Their results suggest that mechanical stress can increase fibroblast activation and ECM deposition [57]. Mechanical cues can also affect innate immune responses in infection models [52]. These findings show that mechanics can shape several lung disease processes.

These models may offer more informative settings for anti-fibrotic drug testing than simple monocultures. Pirfenidone and nintedanib can reduce ECM accumulation, but they may not fully reverse abnormal epithelial states [88]. Other systems have tested anti-fibrotic responses and matrix- or basal-cell-related fibrosis phenotypes in selected models [86, 90, 91]. These findings remain model-based evidence. They do not prove clinical efficacy. Organoid and chip systems can help researchers study multicellular crosstalk and rank candidate therapies. Translation will still require confirmation in primary human tissues, clinical datasets, and well-designed preclinical studies.

Lung cancer

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. It causes more than 1.8 million deaths each year. Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for about 85% of cases [92]. Targeted therapies and immune-checkpoint inhibitors have improved outcomes for some patients. However, drug resistance and patient-to-patient variation remain major barriers [92, 93]. Conventional 2D cell lines can change over time in culture. Patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) are useful but slow to establish and lack functional human immunity [93, 94].

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) can retain parts of the genomic and phenotypic diversity of primary tumors within 3–6 weeks [95]. PDO-based drug screening has shown clinically relevant responses to targeted therapies in lung adenocarcinoma. Broader lung cancer organoid studies also suggest that genomic and drug-response profiling may help identify resistance mechanisms and candidate drug combinations [95, 96]. These applications still need clinical validation.

New sampling methods may make PDO generation more practical. For example, researchers have established PDOs from bronchoscopic biopsies [97]. PDO-immune-cell co-culture models can support studies of checkpoint-inhibitor response and T-cell cytotoxicity [35]. CRISPR/Cas9-edited PDOs can also help identify and test resistance genes [96].

Organoid-on-chip and tumor-on-chip systems can add stromal and vascular components to cancer models [59, 60]. These systems can help researchers reconstruct selected features of the tumor microenvironment. LoC platforms can also control matrix stiffness, cyclic strain, and shear stress. These physical cues can affect immune-cell entry and drug response. Increased tumor stiffness can reduce CD8 + T-cell infiltration and weaken PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor responses [98, 99]. Cancer-on-chip models remain question-specific tools. Researchers should choose and validate their immune, vascular, stromal, and pharmacokinetic components according to the study aim [100].

Future studies may combine PDOs, immune and stromal co-cultures, and single-cell or spatial omics in dynamic microfluidic systems. This design could help map biomechanical and immune interactions in the tumor microenvironment. Vascularized tumor-on-a-chip platforms can also help study anti-angiogenic drug response, metastasis-related processes, and drug transport through dense matrices [60]. LOoC systems may become useful preclinical tools for individualized lung cancer therapy research. They should not be used as fixed predictors of patient response unless larger prospective studies show analytical validity, reproducibility, and clinical predictive performance.

Lung organoid and organ-on-a-chip technologies have helped close some gaps between simple in-vitro assays and complex in-vivo systems. In infection, fibrosis, inflammation, and cancer studies, these platforms can support mechanism research, drug testing, and host-environment interaction studies. Researchers should link each conclusion to the cell source, culture time, device material, endpoint, and validation dataset used. Stem-cell biology, microengineering, and multi-omics methods may further improve these models. For now, patient-derived microphysiological systems should remain complementary research tools until reproducibility and clinical predictive value are better established.

Other respiratory disorders

Researchers are also applying lung organoid and organ-on-a-chip models to other respiratory disorders. These disorders include asthma, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and cystic fibrosis (CF). Each disease has distinct inflammatory or genetic features. Table 1 summarizes representative studies across these disease contexts.

Asthma-related organ-on-chip models can capture selected functional features of airway disease. These features include bronchoconstriction, bronchodilation, and epithelial barrier responses. One human airway musculature-on-chip model simulated healthy and asthmatic bronchoconstriction and bronchodilation in vitro [61]. High-throughput bronchus-on-chip systems have also modeled human bronchial structure and inflammation-related responses [62]. These systems may complement airway epithelial organoids and ALI cultures in studies of airway remodeling and drug response.

ARDS and lung-injury models can benefit from controlled mechanical and ECM conditions in organ-on-a-chip systems. ECM hydrogel-based devices have been used to build physiomimetic ARDS models with inflammatory and barrier-related responses [63]. Alveolus-on-a-chip models have also been used to study acute radiation-induced lung injury. These systems provide additional tools for studying epithelial-endothelial injury and candidate protective treatments [64].

Patient nasal epithelial organoids can model CFTR function in cystic fibrosis through swelling responses. High-throughput swelling assays may help assess CFTR-modulating drugs, including ivacaftor and lumacaftor [101]. These assays may support individualized therapy decisions, but they still require careful clinical interpretation.

These examples show that organoid and chip models can address a wider range of respiratory disorders. They are useful for studying selected disease features, environmental toxicology, and drug response. However, each disease area needs its own validation standards. Researchers also need clinically relevant endpoints and clear reporting of negative or non-reproducible results before these systems can guide therapy with confidence.

Challenges and future directions

Lung organoid and organ-on-a-chip technologies have clear potential in respiratory disease research. Their broad use and clinical translation still face several challenges. Future work will need input from stem-cell biology, biomaterials, microengineering, and computational analysis. These fields can help improve reproducibility, model complexity, and clinical relevance. However, more complexity is not always better. The model should match the question being asked. Recent methodological reviews also warn that complex structure does not guarantee better prediction unless the assay has engineering control, qualification, and biological validation [102–104].

Standardization and reproducibility

Reproducibility remains a major challenge. Cell heterogeneity, undefined culture media, and manual handling can create batch-to-batch variation. These factors can change organoid shape, cell composition, and functional readouts. Researchers can reduce this problem by using chemically defined, serum-free media and automated culture systems for more uniform production.

Quality-control standards are also needed. These standards should include transcriptomic profiles, key protein markers, and functional endpoints such as barrier integrity and ciliary-beat frequency. Shared standards would help laboratories compare results. Collaboration among academic groups, industry, and regulatory bodies will be important for preclinical and toxicology validation.

Transparent reporting is another requirement for reproducibility. Researchers should report cell source, passage number, ECM composition, device material, flow conditions, endpoint definitions, and assay acceptance criteria [9, 12, 25, 26]. Device material can also affect results. PDMS is useful for early device design and gas-permeable chips. However, PDMS can absorb small hydrophobic molecules. This absorption can change the effective drug dose and complicate pharmacological assays [105, 106].

Recreating physiological complexity

Most current lung organoids mainly contain epithelial cells. They often lack vascular, stromal, immune, and neural components. This limits their ability to model chronic inflammation, immune escape, fibrosis, and whole-body responses.

Future models may include immune and stromal cells, tunable scaffolds, and multi-organ connections. Hydrogels with adjustable stiffness and ECM remodeling may improve the study of tissue-mechanical feedback. Lung-liver-kidney chip systems could help model inter-organ communication, drug metabolism, and systemic toxicity. These additions may improve biological relevance, but they also add variability, cost, and analysis burden. Researchers should add complexity only when it improves model performance for a defined question.

Improving throughput and data content

High cost, long culture periods, and complex data collection limit the use of organoids in large-scale drug screening. Microfluidic chips can reduce reagent use and offer better control over the culture environment.

LOoC platforms can be combined with high-content imaging, single-cell multi-omics, and real-time sensors. These methods can generate data on cell state, metabolism, and drug response. AI-assisted analysis may help detect subtle phenotypic changes or resistance patterns. Researchers should still confirm that these patterns are biologically meaningful.

Sentinel-sensor LoC systems have shown non-invasive monitoring of nanoplastic-induced lung injury [56]. This example shows that automated culture-to-analysis workflows are feasible for environmental toxicology. Broader use will depend on assay standardization, clear reporting, external validation, and endpoints that match clinical or toxicological outcomes.

Translation from bench to bedside

Clinical translation remains difficult despite technical progress. Key barriers include ethics, regulation, cost, and workflow integration. Patient-specific organoid biobanks may help researchers test therapies using a patient’s own cells. Such testing could help evaluate response to chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy. It should still be viewed as decision support. It should not determine treatment on its own until prospective clinical validation is available.

Clinical use will also require integration with electronic health records and genomic data. This integration could help assess whether organoid systems can predict treatment response. Health-technology assessment will need to examine cost-effectiveness and ethical issues around patient-derived materials. Wider adoption will also require proof that each platform is fit for its intended use. This proof should include reproducibility, robustness, scalability, and clinical relevance [11, 12, 25, 26]. Roadmap and drug-discovery studies also note the need for qualification criteria, stakeholder alignment, manufacturability, and standard performance metrics before organ-on-chip models enter broad regulatory or clinical workflows [107, 108].

These platforms may also help public-health surveillance. For example, they could support rapid assessment of emerging airborne agents or environmental contaminants. Their results should not be used alone for policy or pandemic decisions. They should be combined with epidemiology, toxicology, and clinical evidence.

Outlook

Organoid biology, microfluidics, biomaterials, and computational modeling are making respiratory disease models more precise. Future digital lung-on-a-chip systems may combine continuous culture, automated monitoring, and AI-driven analysis. These systems could help model disease progression and treatment response. This possibility should be interpreted cautiously. Model outputs will depend on data quality, biological interpretation, device reproducibility, and the clinical relevance of training and validation datasets [102–104].

These systems may also support regulatory science and personalized health care. They may improve selected parts of environmental health testing and drug-safety assessment. Their role in formal decisions will depend on reliability, relevance to the intended use, comparison with established methods, and transparent reporting of both successful and unsuccessful validation results [107, 108].

Overall, lung organoid and organ-on-a-chip technologies are useful complementary tools for respiratory disease research. They may contribute to precision respiratory medicine if the field can address standardization, model complexity, validation, scalability, and clinical implementation. These platforms are unlikely to replace existing models in the near term. They may be most valuable when used with clinical data, animal studies, computational models, and multi-omics analyses. In this cautious view, these systems can strengthen human-relevant mechanism studies. Their translational value will depend on rigorous benchmarking and fit-for-purpose validation.
