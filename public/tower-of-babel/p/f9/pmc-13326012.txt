Pathways to sustainable lithium-mediated ammonia electrosynthesis: tuning reaction environment and advancing reactor design
Authors: Ngoc-Trung Nguyen, Thi Kim Cuong Phu, Minh Triet Nguyen, Trang Minh Pham, Phi Long Nguyen, Thi Viet Bac Phung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical lithium-mediated nitrogen reduction (Li-NRR) represents a highly promising, renewable-powered alternative to the energy-intensive Haber–Bosch process for green ammonia synthesis. By electrochemically reducing lithium ions to metallic lithium, which subsequently dissociates the inert N2 molecule, modern Li-NRR systems have achieved unprecedented faradaic efficiencies and remarkable long-term stability. This review critically assesses the recent milestones and fundamental chemistry underpinning Li-NRR. We detail how rational electrolyte engineering—encompassing lithium salts, organic solvents, and proton carriers—can be tailored to actively control the composition of the solid-electrolyte interphase and suppress parasitic side reactions. We further provide insights into the physical operational parameters governing interfacial mass transport, highlighting the kinetic mismatch between rapid lithium deposition and sluggish reactant diffusion. Finally, we discuss the evolution of reactor design, from high-pressure batch cells to gas diffusion electrode flow cells. Concluding with a forward-looking perspective, this review outlines the critical challenges of Li-NRR that must be addressed to realize a sustainable, decentralized ammonia economy.

Introduction

Ammonia (NH3) is a cornerstone of modern society, serving as an indispensable precursor for nitrogen fertilizers and emerging as a highly promising zero-carbon fuel for energy storage. For over a century, global production has been dominated by the Haber–Bosch process, which catalytically combines N2 and H2 under extreme temperatures (400–650 °C) and pressures (200–400 atm).1 Consequently, this energy-intensive process accounts for approximately 2% of global energy consumption and 1.8% of global CO2 emissions, primarily due to the steam-reforming of methane for H2 production.2 Despite its high energy efficiency at large-scale production, the process still relies on centralized, continuous operations, presenting a significant barrier to integrating intermittent renewable energy sources.

In nature, biological N2 fixation, mediated by nitrogenase enzymes in soil microbes, generates ammonia under ambient conditions, serving as the primary natural nitrogen source for ecosystems.3 Inspired by nature and driven by the proposed ammonia economy roadmap,4 electrochemical ammonia synthesis represents a promising alternative route for producing green ammonia from N2 and H2O in small-scale, distributed devices using renewable electricity under ambient conditions (Fig. 1a). This approach offers the potential to reduce both capital and operational costs of ammonia-generating devices while eliminating greenhouse gas emissions.

However, low faradaic efficiency (selectivity) of heterogeneous catalysts for NH3 electrosynthesis in aqueous electrolytes is typically experienced due to more kinetically favorable hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at high overpotentials.6,7 Moreover, the direct nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) is limited by very low NH3 production rates, which generally involves false positive results induced by surrounding N-contaminants. Therefore, redox-mediated nitrogen reduction reaction is conducted in non-aqueous electrolytes to promote the selectivity by suppressing parasitic HER.8,9 Lithium, the lightest alkali metal, is one of the most prominent mediators for N2 fixation to ammonia, in which lithium ions (Li+) can be electrochemically reduced to lithium metal (Li0) that is capable of dissociating the inert NN triple bond via metal nitride formation. To date, the lithium-mediated nitrogen reduction reaction (Li-NRR) represents the one and only genuine electrochemical method to convert N2 to NH3 with high reproducibility and remarkable performance.9–11

The generally accepted concept of Li-NRR includes three key steps: (1) Li+ ions diffusing from bulk non-aqueous solution to the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) are reduced to metallic Li0 on the cathode. (2) The resulting Li0 reacts with N2 to form lithium nitride. (3) The nitrides are subsequently protonated by a proton carrier (e.g. ethanol) to produce NH3 (Fig. 1b). So far, the Li-NRR is able to enable NH3 electrosynthesis at faradaic efficiencies (FE) approaching the target set by U.S. Department of Energy (90%), whereas reported NH3 yield rate (YR) is improved but remain below commercialization benchmarks.11–13

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the history and fundamental principles of Li-NRR under ambient or near-ambient conditions, while highlighting current research status and challenges in this field.

The ability of metallic lithium to chemically reduce N2 was first demonstrated by Fichter et al. in 1930,14 who electrodeposited Li from LiBr/LiCl dissolved in ethanol solvent under 1000 bar N2 atmosphere. Despite the extreme conditions, only 10% of FE toward NH3 was achieved due to competing HER from the excessive reaction between metallic Li0 and the protic solvent. This early work was largely overlooked for six decades until Tsuneto et al. revitalized the approach in the 1990s by replacing ethanol with tetrahydrofuran (THF) – an aprotic solvent with high N2 solubility – while using only small amount of ethanol.15,16 This solvent engineering strategy dramatically suppressed excessive HER, improving FE to ca. 60% under 50 bar N2 with LiClO4 electrolyte.15 Motivated by these prior studies, in 2017, McEnaney et al. demonstrated multi-step NH3 synthesis via high-temperature molten LiOH electrolysis.17 Thereafter, Lazouski et al. reported a continuous Li-NRR system where primary steps occurred in the same batch cell to primarily investigate mechanistic insights.18 At the same time, Andersen et al. successfully validated the Li-NRR for ammonia electrosynthesis by proposing a rigorous protocol of nitrogen purification and quantitative isotope measurements.9 Their crucial tests involved demonstrating the equivalent production of ammonia using 15N2 and 14N2, advocating for the quantification of both ammonia isotopes in a time-resolved manner to definitively verify N2 electroreduction. This work established a rigorous experimental benchmark for validating Li-NRR, addressing the false-positive concerns that have plagued many aqueous NRR reports. Since then, Li-NRR studies have expanded rapidly, motivating the mechanistic and engineering advances discussed in the following sections.

Engineering the reaction environment of lithium-mediated nitrogen reduction reaction

Chemical design: tailoring electrolytes and interfacial chemistry

In Li-NRR systems, overall performances are critically influenced by experimental parameters that control the supply of key chemical species, which correlate to interfacial chemistry at the cathode. This section systematically examines how lithium electrolytes, proton carriers, solvents, and additives dictate Li-NRR performance and stability through their profound influence on the SEI – a key factor determining N2 electroreduction performance.

The SEI represents an interfacial layer that forms spontaneously when electrolyte components undergo reductive reactions at the lithium-coated electrode.19,20 This dynamic, permeable layer consists of both organic and inorganic species and acts as a selective barrier rather than a simple passivation layer.10,21 In Li-NRR, the presence of nitrogen and a proton carrier results in a distinct SEI structure and chemistry compared to those observed in lithium-ion batteries.21–23 An effective SEI layer for Li-NRR must satisfy competing demands: facilitating nitrogen and proton transport to the reactive lithium surface while suppressing parasitic pathways including hydrogen evolution, unproductive lithium accumulation, and continuous electrolyte degradation.24,25 Since N2 electroreduction takes place within the SEI layer, this interfacial region governs the mass transport of key reactants (Li, N2, H+), heterogeneous charge-transfer kinetics, and N2 activation. Insights from lithium battery research reveal that while the SEI is electronically insulating, it conducts lithium ions, creating conditions where mass transport through the layer, rather than surface electrochemistry, often limits overall reaction rates.19 When transport becomes unbalanced – i.e. lithium ion flux exceeds nitrogen supply – an accumulation of detrimental byproducts such as bulk lithium deposits or lithium hydride leads to a deterioration of Li-NRR performance.18,25

Recognizing the critical influence of SEI properties on Li-NRR performance, researchers have developed various strategies to deliberately engineer its composition and structure. These approaches range from electrolyte design (electrolyte and solvent choice, concentration tuning) to the deliberate introduction of reactive additives, and/or modifying electrochemical protocols. Following early demonstrations of Li-mediated nitrogen reduction using simple lithium salts (LiCl, LiBr) in ethanol by Fichter and colleagues,14 systematic investigations into SEI properties began with LiClO4-based systems in aprotic solvents.

Lithium electrolytes

Critically, the chemical nature of the lithium electrolyte directly determines SEI composition and thus performance through its impact on ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability, and nitrogen solubility. Understanding this intricate relationship has driven the evolution from early LiClO4 systems toward high-performance electrolytes. Early Li-NRR research predominantly employed LiClO4 due to its commercial availability and decent solubility in organic solvents.9,25,26 However, systematic investigation by Westhead et al. revealed that FE decreased sharply when LiClO4 concentration was higher than 0.6 M, attributed to reduced nitrogen solubility and diffusivity, shifting the system to a nitrogen-limited regime.27 Post-electrolysis characterization revealed that LiClO4 electrolytes produced SEI layers dominated by chlorinated species (LiClOn, LiCl) and Li2CO3, which provided insufficient selectivity and stability.

Beyond traditional LiClO4 systems, Li et al. significantly advanced the Li-NRR system by utilising highly porous copper electrodes and a LiBF4-based electrolyte to achieve record-high current densities.28 By using electrodes fabricated via the hydrogen bubble template method to increase the electrochemical surface area by 300 times, they achieved an unprecedented ammonia production rate of 2.5 ± 0.1 µmol s−1 cm−2 at a current density of −1.0 A cm−2 with a 71% FE. Furthermore, the system exhibited exceptional selectivity at a current density of −0.1 A cm−2, reaching a FE of 95% ± 3%. They suggested that LiBF4 induces the formation of a compact, uniform, and LiF-enriched SEI, which stabilizes lithium plating and effectively suppresses parasitic electrolyte decomposition. Operando grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering by Deissler et al. confirmed that LiBF4 electrolytes form SEI layers with high concentrations of crystalline LiF along with lithium ethoxide, contrasting sharply with the chlorinated species formed by LiClO4.29 This LiF-rich SEI composition originates from decomposition of BF4− anion at reducing potentials, forming a dense, ionically conductive but electronically insulating layer that effectively suppresses undesired side reactions while maintaining adequate Li+ conductivity.

Despite improvements observed in LiBF4 systems, other fluorinated imide-based lithium electrolytes have proven to provide enhanced Li-NRR performance. Du et al. achieved unprecedented performance metrics of almost 100% FE and stabilized NH3 YR of 150 nmol s−1 cm−2, with stable operation over 4 days, using high-concentration LiTFSI electrolyte in THF at 15 bar N2.11 Post-electrolysis analysis revealed that the high-concentration LiTFSI electrolyte enables the formation of a compact SEI comprising LiF, Li3N and organic components. This dense ionic layer at the electrolyte–electrode interface suppresses reductive degradation of electrolyte and solvent while facilitating the transfer of Li+, N2, and proton carriers. This work suggests that a thin, nanometer-scale SEI layer could provide sufficient ionic transport, while a bulk interface containing LiF, polysulfides, and Li3N formed in the region of the electrolyte–electrode–gas phase boundary resulted in unproductive charge consumption, thereby reducing NH3 FE and YR. Among lithium electrolytes examined in batch cells, LiTFSI electrolyte displays exceptional performance over other traditional lithium electrolytes such as LiPF6,11,28 LiOTf, LiFSI,11 and LiBF4,18,30 demonstrating that electrolyte selection is not only about increased ionic conductivity, but fundamentally about engineering SEI formation.

Proton carrier

In Li-NRR systems, the proton carrier supplies protons to lithium nitride (Li3N), promoting the chemical conversion to ammonia. An ideal proton carrier must deliver protons efficiently while maintaining electrochemical stability at the high anodic potentials. Following the pioneering work by Tsuneto et al., ethanol (C2H5OH) has been widely used in most early Li-NRR studies.16

Early Li-mediated NRR studies generally assumed that an adequate ethanol concentration is required to protonate Li3N to release NH3, while acknowledging that ethanol can also parasitically react with plated Li0 to form lithium ethoxide and evolve H2.26 Rather than acting solely as a reactant in Li3N formation, ethanol can affect nitridation process via the SEI, which controls the mass transfer of chemical species and reactivity at the Li surface. The impact of a proton carrier on SEI formation was understudied until Steinberg et al. unveiled that without ethanol, Li deposition is dominated by metallic lithium accumulation and the formation of a compact, passivating SEI composed largely of anion/solvent decomposition products (e.g., BF4−- and THF-derived species), as detected by cryo-transmission electron microscopy, which likely restricts N2 access to the underlying reactive Li.31 By contrast, introducing ethanol disrupts this passivating layer, leading to a porous and fractured SEI. This permeable layer allows access of N2 and protons to the underlying lithium. In addition, Mygind et al. demonstrated that ethanol must first be converted to ethoxide to effectively mediate protons from the anode.32 To prevent early solvent decomposition, the authors suggest using deprotonated species, such as lithium ethoxide or phenoxide, which provide a more stable pathway to initiate the Li-NRR system (Fig. 2a). This work also indicated that anodic proton generation is fundamentally limited by the cathodic capability to consume electrons through simultaneous reductive reactions, ensuring a charge balance in the electrochemical system. Building on this understanding of alcoholic proton carriers, various studies have systematically screened different types of proton carriers to optimize Li-NRR performance and stability. Krishnamurthy et al. investigated a series of alcohol-based proton carriers to establish relationships between their chemical characteristics and Li-NRR activity.33 They employed Kamlet–Taft parameters and utilized a deep-learning model to predict α (proton-donating) and β (proton-accepting) values for alcohols not yet experimentally examined. Their analysis indicated that successful Li-NRR proton carriers generally exhibit α > 0.78 and β > 0.57. Nevertheless, subsequent studies have questioned whether these KT parameters alone sufficiently explain Li-NRR performance, suggesting that additional chemical factors also contribute, thus requiring further exploration.

To evaluate the effectiveness and stability of various proton carriers, Du et al. performed Li-NRR tests using C1–C5 alcohols, [P6,6,6,14]+, NH4+, H2O and bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (HNTf2) in THF-based LiTFSI electrolyte.34 Among these candidates, alcohols remain the most effective sources of protons for the Li-NRR (Fig. 2b). Notably, iso-propanol and n-propanol delivered Li-NRR performance comparable to that provided by the benchmark ethanol but were more stable against degradation. The latter was discovered to occur through a reaction of ethanol with oxidised THF species to form 2-ethoxytetrahydrofuran and other by-products (Fig. 2a).34

In addition, Lazouski et al. examined different groups of proton shuttles including alcohols, carboxylic acids, phenols, thiols, and esters in LiBF4/THF electrolyte, identifying aliphatic alcohols as the most effective for obtaining high FE.36 Their study revealed a critical concentration threshold: below a minimum proton carrier level, FE remained negligible, but increased sharply once this critical threshold was achieved. This result indicates that insufficient proton availability severely limits N2-to-NH3 conversion and favors HER. Structural variations among alcohols significantly influenced selectivity, with n-butanol achieving the highest FE of ca. 15%. Their work suggests that different alcoholic proton carriers induce distinct SEI structure and properties, which results in specific diffusion of chemical species through SEI to the electrode, thereby providing different N2-to-NH3 conversion under comparable conditions.36 However, optimizing the proton carrier identity must be accompanied by precise concentration control. Oversupply of protons promotes undesirable HER, while undersupply leads to accumulation of unreacted nitride intermediate, both of which decrease the overall FE.

While alcoholic proton carriers enhance Li-NRR performance, their susceptibility to degradation during extended operation has prompted investigations into more stable alternatives. Suryanto et al. introduced the trihexyltetradecyl phosphonium cation ([P6,6,6,14]+) as a recyclable proton shuttle that undergoes continuous deprotonation and regeneration during NH3 electrosynthesis (Fig. 2a).30 This reversible transformation between the phosphonium cation and ylide enables proton shuttling during Li-NRR at the copper cathode and H2 oxidation at a Pt anode in LiBF4/THF electrolyte. Compared to the ethanol-based systems, [P6,6,6,14]+ delivered a 20-fold higher current density, and an improved FE of 69 ± 1% was achieved in 20 h experiments. Although stirring the electrolyte resulted in a lower FE (45%), it enabled an extended stability up to 93 h.

Beyond alcoholic and phosphonium-based proton carriers, researchers have explored structurally diverse proton sources to expand the chemical space of Li-NRR systems. Fu et al. demonstrated that phenol (PhOH) functions as a highly effective proton shuttle, achieving a remarkable FE of 72 ± 3% at 37 mM concentration—surpassing ethanol's performance at its optimal concentration of 50 mM (Fig. 2c).35 Through operando isotope-labeling experiments, they confirmed that the deprotonated phenoxide (PhO−) acts as the primary proton-accepting species, shuttling protons from the anode to lithium nitride at the cathode while exhibiting superior electrochemical stability compared to ethanol. More recently, Banerjee et al. introduced a paradigm-shifting approach by employing ammonia itself as the proton carrier, eliminating the need for external proton shuttles (Fig. 2a).37 Using 0.08 M NH3 in 2 M LiTFSI/THF electrolyte, they achieved a high NH3 YR of 350 ± 40 nmol s−1 cm−2 and an outstanding FE of 93 ± 6% under 15 bar N2, with stable performance maintained over two-day operation. Interestingly, while neutral NH3 promoted Li-NRR, the addition of NH4+ suppressed the reaction, suggesting that the uncharged ammonia molecule is the active proton donor that reacts with Li3N to regenerate both NH3 and Li+. This self-sustaining strategy represents a technological simplification, as the proton source is simultaneously the targeted product, avoiding degradation pathways and side reactions associated with conventional proton carriers. Collectively, these advances signal a paradigm shift from sacrificial, degradation-prone proton donors toward regenerable shuttles that enable sustained, stable Li-NRR operation. The design of effective proton carriers requires balancing proton delivery kinetics, chemical stability under anodic oxidative conditions, and compatibility with the electrolyte solutions to minimize side reactions for high-stability Li-NRR systems.

Solvent choice

The Li-NRR chemistry is essentially incompatible with aqueous systems and requires a high-stability non-aqueous solvent capable of withstanding extreme cathodic polarisation beyond the Li0/+ potentials. Historically, THF has been the most prevalent choice, enabling many of the highest-performing benchmark systems.11,28,38 However, THF suffers from two critical limitations: a relatively low boiling point (66 °C), which leads to unproductive solvent loss into the gas phase, and a low dielectric constant (7.5 at 25 °C), limiting the bulk ionic conductivity. Identifying alternative solvents that improve upon these metrics while maintaining the requisite electrochemical stability has proven challenging. To date, chain ethers such as diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (diglyme; DG) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) represent the most successful advancements.13,39,40 While their dielectric constants are comparable to THF, chain ethers exhibit significantly lower volatility and, crucially, superior resistance to polymerization induced by solvent oxidation—a major degradation pathway that limits the longevity of THF-based electrolytes in continuous operation.

Beyond serving simply as a passive reaction medium, the physicochemical properties of the solvent fundamentally dictate mass-transport dynamics, interfacial chemistry, and overall performance and energy efficiency in Li-NRR.13,39,40 In particular, solvent polarity affects the solubility and diffusivity of dissolved N2, with chain ethers like diglyme (DG) demonstrating superior N2 carrying capacity compared to cyclic ethers like THF.13 Furthermore, the solvent directly determines the Li+ solvation structure. Molecular dynamics simulations have revealed that chain ethers promote anion-rich primary solvation shells, which preferentially undergo reduction to form a robust, inorganic-rich SEI, thereby suppressing excessive solvent decomposition.13 To systematically leverage these structural differences, recent work by Li et al. extended the ‘ring-chain coupling’ empirical law to Li-NRR, demonstrating that judiciously blending cyclic and chain ethers can synergistically optimize both SEI formation and ionic transport.40 Emphasizing these trade-offs, Cai et al. illustrated that while THF achieved a high faradaic efficiency of 59 ± 6% at 1 atm N2 pressure by facilitating a favorable SEI, DME delivered the highest overall energy efficiency due to the inherently superior ionic conductivity of the resulting electrolyte solution.39 Notably, unlike THF, which is susceptible to parasitic ring-opening polymerization under highly reducing conditions, longer-chain ethers like DG exhibit high electrochemical stability and low volatility, ensuring sustained NH3 synthesis and efficient product release (Fig. 3).13

The growth of the solid-electrolyte-interphase (SEI) layer on the cathodic involves multiple concurrent mechanisms: electrolyte–solvent decomposition, hydrogen evolution, reactions with H2O and O2 impurities, and nitrogen-associated side reactions. Finally, solvent selection is severely constrained by its effects on the anodic reaction. Current research majorly focuses on Li-NRR coupled with hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) systems due to the lack of robust membranes that can effectively couple the non-aqueous Li-NRR to the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Therefore, an effective solvent must be stable not only against highly reductive potentials experienced at a Li-NRR cathode but also against oxidative potentials at a HOR anode, while not interfering with half-reactions. If anodic stability is insufficient, the anodic potential becomes excessively positive to overcome kinetic limitations, which might induce unsustainable solvent oxidation. Moreover, the HOR in organic media suffers from intrinsic kinetic limitations and rapid catalyst deactivation caused by solvent-induced surface passivation, with pure platinum deactivating within minutes.41,42 Combining Pt with other metals, for example in the form of platinum–ruthenium42 or platinum–gold12 bimetallic catalysts improves the stability and activity significantly, though it still remains well below the metrics achievable in the conventional aqueous electrolytes. The successful integration of non-aqueous Li-NRR with continuous HOR therefore demands solvents that resist anodic oxidation—a challenge that has driven recent innovations in electrolyser design, discussed in Section 3.

Injection of additives

While the bulk solvent and lithium salt establish the primary matrix of the electrolyte, several research groups have explored whether SEI composition could be further tuned through trace electrolyte additives while maintaining the same traditional electrolyte system. Li et al. found that small amounts of oxygen (0.8 mol% O2) added to LiClO4/THF electrolyte under 20 bar N2, previously believed to induce a detrimental effect, actually improved FE to approximately 78 ± 1%.10 This counterintuitive improvement occurs because O2 molecules activate SEI modification, shifting its composition to inorganic-rich interfaces containing Li2O and LiOH (Fig. 3). This inorganic-dominant layer possesses superior Li+ conductivity and improved mechanical stability, as compared to the organic-rich interface, thus protecting the cathode from side reactions. Subsequent investigations employing gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy indicated that low O2 concentration hindered electrolyte degradation pathways, including poly(tetrahydrofuran) formation, thereby improving the FE of N2 towards NH3.10,43

Spry et al. demonstrated that the controlled introduction of a small concentration of water (36 mM) into LiClO4/THF solution leads to enhanced NH3 FE, contrasting the general belief that the presence of water is purely detrimental to Li-NRR performance (Fig. 3).44 With optimal water concentration, the highest FE of 27.9 ± 2.5% was obtained at ambient pressure without a gas diffusion electrode (GDE). A transformed SEI composition from chlorinated-based species (LiCl, LiClOn) to Li2O-dominant regime, measured by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), resulted in the enhanced performance because this interface with low lithium diffusivity suppresses excessive lithium plating while allowing favourable transport of N2 and protons at efficient rates.44 This work emphasises the extreme sensitivity of the Li-NRR system, in which different experimental parameters result in improved performances through minor variations in electrolyte composition.

Jeon et al. demonstrated that modifying the copper cathode with trace amounts of lithiophilic silver (Ag) significantly enhances ammonia FE.45 With in situ Ag electrodeposition in a LiTFSI-based electrolyte, this system delivered an NH3 yield rate of 163 nmol s−1 cm−2 at a high FE of ca. 85%. This study suggests that Ag facilitates the formation of Li–Ag alloy and a LiF-dominant SEI, which helps reduce overpotential for lithium plating. Importantly, this improvement is dependent on the presence of LiF in the electrolyte, as the addition of AgOTf exhibited unchanged performance in LiClO4-based system.

Another study by Lim et al. demonstrated that incorporating dimethyl sulfide as an additive into LiClO4-based electrolyte solution can tune the SEI morphology and composition, improving lithium plating uniformity and leading to increased process stability and FE.46 They found that the sulfur-derived SEI, containing LiSOx, Li2S, and Li2CO3, enhances the Li+ conductivity and electronic insulation properties. Building on the concept of electrolyte modification, Yun et al. introduced a significant advancement by applying the first localized high concentration electrolyte (LHCE) concept to the Li-NRR system.47 Using 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-propyl ether (TTE) as a non-solvating diluent, they modified the bulk solvation structure to address the limitations of traditional high-concentration electrolytes. The addition of TTE forces anions into the primary Li+ solvation shell even at low salt concentrations, which generates an anion-rich environment at the electrode surface similar to high-concentration systems but without the drawbacks of high viscosity. This structural manipulation proved critical for the SEI composition, as XPS analysis confirms a higher content of LiF in the LHCE-derived layer compared to both low-concentration and standard high-concentration counterparts. The resulting LiF-rich interface provided superior ionic conductivity and suppressed parasitic side reactions, enabling the system to reach a high FE of ca. 73% over 20 hours. Collectively, these studies again highlight that the SEI composition can be deliberately modified through electrolyte additives to tune mass-transport of key species, resulting in improved Li-NRR kinetics and suppressed kinetics of undesirable side reactions.25

Operational parameters: controlling driving force and mass-transport

Nitrogen pressure effects

Beyond the critical role of proton carriers, nitrogen availability represents an equally important factor governing Li-NRR performance. The solubility of N2 in non-aqueous electrolytes is inherently pressure-dependent, and numerous studies have revealed substantial performance improvements under elevated N2 pressures. Specifically, Tsuneto et al. observed increased performance with increasing pressure up to 50 bar N2.15 A similar effect was also experienced by Lazouski et al.18 with linear increase of FE when enhancing N2 from 0 to 1 bar, driven by enhanced N2 solubility. A kinetic model developed by Andersen et al. to correlate the mechanism of Li-NRR and relevant performance proposes two possible mass-transfer limitations: either nitrogen, or proton transfer.25 Based on computational data, they observed that low pressure (1 bar) experiments proceed in the nitrogen-limited regime, while high pressure (up to 20 bar) operate in or near a proton-limited region. This regime transition explains why the sharp FE increase observed between 1 and 10 bar was not experienced when the pressure elevated to 20 bar, as the electrolyte reached nitrogen-saturated plateau. Therefore, various studies were also conducted 15–20 bar condition to overcome mass-transport limitation and achieve the highest performances.5,11,25,34,48

Mass-transport effects

Beyond optimizing the static properties of the electrolyte, dynamic regulation of the hydrodynamic boundary layer—through forced convection and viscosity tuning—represents a critical but underexplored aspect in Li-NRR. While proton and nitrogen supply are optimized in Li-NRR systems,18,25 the diffusion of nitrogen and protons to the Li-deposited cathodic surface can become rate-limiting steps in Li-NRR. This point is particularly relevant for phosphonium-based shuttles, where high viscosity and molecular size introduce a mass-transport limitation that cannot be inferred from interfacial chemistry alone.

Despite their stability advantages, the high viscosity associated with the [P6,6,6,14][NTf2] salt raised the question of how Li-NRR performance changes when the phosphonium cation structure is varied. Following the emergence of high-concentration LiTFSI electrolytes capable of delivering nearly quantitative faradaic efficiency with ethanol, substituting ethanol with the more robust [P6,6,6,14]+ in LiTFSI led to a marked performance decrease, consistent with a hypothesis of insufficiently rapid transport of the bulky proton shuttle and therefore slower effective proton delivery. This motivated a systematic study of phosphonium cations with shorter alkyl chains together with re-optimization of LiTFSI concentration. Counterintuitively, Nguyen et al. found that the bulkiest [P6,6,6,14]+ cation still delivered the highest FE and YR under forced convection, while Li-NRR performance metrics decreased as the phosphonium cation size decreased.49 Post-electrolysis characterizations revealed that short-alkyl-chain proton shuttles induced the formation of organic-rich SEI layers, whereas bulky phosphonium cations favored the deposition of inorganic Li-based species (LiF, Li3N, polysulphides), which suppressed unproductive Li+ electroreduction and electrode passivation. Under the optimized condition of 1.5 M LiTFSI + 0.1 M [P6,6,6,14]+, a long-term operation with FE of ca. 78% was maintained for 72 h. Notably, this study also demonstrated the profound sensitivity of the Li-NRR to fluid dynamics. They emphasise that the effects of changing the length of alkyl chains in the phosphonium cations are not limited to altering the mass-transport characteristics of the proton shuttle, but also influence the kinetics of the Li-NRR through modifications to the composition of the solid electrolyte interphase. Therefore, controlling mass transport in Li-NRR extends beyond just chemical optimization, requiring precise control over fluid dynamics to decouple the kinetics of key processes.

Potential effects

The applied potential serves as a critical parameter in determining the chemical composition and structural morphology of the SEI in Li-NRR.50–53 Izelaar et al. systematically identified three distinct potential regimes using a reliable LixFePO4 reference electrode to correlate specific SEI properties and corresponding FE.51 In the low potential regime (around −3.2 V vs. SHE), the SEI is thin, porous, and predominantly organic, consisting largely of lithium ethoxide. This organic-rich interface is particularly ineffective at regulating the transport of Li+ and H+, leading to high rates of HER and low faradaic efficiency. Their finding aligns with previous works, which indicated that a passivated SEI layer composed of ethanol-derived species under comparable conditions generally induces a poor FE towards NH3.10,11,28,49

As the potential shifts to more negative values (from −3.7 V down to −4.6 V vs. SHE), the SEI transitions to a thicker, denser morphology enriched in inorganic species, particularly LiF originating from LiTFSI anion decomposition. A significant overpotential is required to overcome the kinetic barrier for fluorine-based salt decomposition, explaining why LiF formation is not observed at low overpotentials. Post-mortem XPS analysis by Izelaar et al. revealed a strong correlation between the LiF concentration on the SEI and the resulting FE directly supporting the mechanistic hypothesis advanced by Chorkendorff and coworkers via first-principles simulations.28,51 Their calculations suggest that Li+ transport rate through LiF is significantly lower than through Li2CO3, while the N2 and H+ transfer rates are less sensitive to the SEI composition. This effective SEI layer slows lithium plating rate, extending the residence time of freshly deposited Li0 and thereby facilitating N2 dissociation before further electron consumption occurs. Notably, operando Raman spectroscopy has further confirmed that the SEI is not static but dynamically restructures during Li-NRR, with ethanol-driven formation of LiOEt and Li3N playing an important role in enabling N2 transport and activation at the electrode surface.50

At extreme potentials exceeding −4.0 V, they found that while ammonia FE reaches a maximum, the system suffers from significant current instabilities, which are attributed to a dynamic cycle of continuous SEI thickening and mechanical breakdown.51 This dynamic degradation undermines long-term operational stability and highlights the delicate balance between driving sufficient LiF formation and maintaining a mechanically coherent interface. To mitigate this issue, Andersen et al. proposed a potential cycling protocol for Li-NRR that improved FE and stability of the system to 37 ± 1% and 125 h, respectively.25 The mechanism behind this improvement was further elucidated by Blair and colleagues using in situ neutron reflectometry. They observed that resting the cell at open-circuit voltage (OCV) decreases thickness of lithium/SEI layer, confirming that partial consumption of lithium metals for either ammonia production or solvent decomposition occurs during relaxation periods.21

Collectively, these studies establish that applied potential functions not only as a thermodynamic driving force, but as a precise tuning parameter for interfacial chemistry, where achieving high Li-NRR performance metrics requires careful optimization of applied potential to control SEI properties.

Reactor engineering for sustainable system integration

Advancing from batch to continuous-flow cell

Electrolyser design plays a crucial role in determining the efficiency, stability, and scalability of electrochemical systems. Over the past decade, Li-NRR research has transitioned from simple batch configurations—ideal for optimizations of electrolyte composition—toward advanced flow reactors engineered to improve performance and stability while facilitating continuous production. Early Li-NRR works predominantly relied on single-compartment batch cells, which enabled some of the most significant advances in the Li-NRR performance.11,25,30,38 A standard setup typically includes the working, auxiliary, and reference electrodes within a single compartment (Fig. 4a). While effective at preserving the product, the separator can impede proton transport, decrease overall stability, and increase cell voltage.34 The trade-off between product protection and ionic transport resistance is thus a defining constraint of the two-compartment batch architecture. Furthermore, in such batch configurations, electrolyte oxidation at the anode must eventually be replaced by hydrogen oxidation to move toward practical operation; however, introducing HOR in batch cells is severely constrained by mass-transport limitations of H2.

While high-pressure batch cells excel at benchmarking Li-NRR chemistry and maximizing performance metrics, a fundamentally different approach is required to address mass-transport limitations and operate at ambient N2 pressure. The solution lies in employing GDE, in which gas, electrolyte, and catalytic sites are brought into intimate contact. This triple-phase boundary is a unique architectural advantage of GDE-based flow cells over conventional flooded batch reactors: it decouples gas supply from electrolyte circulation, allowing independent optimization of gas and electrolyte transfer. This GDE configuration necessitates a dedicated continuous-flow electrolyser design that enables uninterrupted ammonia electrosynthesis at 1 bar N2 and more closely resembles industrially relevant operation (Fig. 4b). In terms of scalability, flow cells offer additional advantages including electrode area scale-up through modular stacking that is largely inaccessible in gas-tight batch configurations.

Lazouski and colleagues pioneered the implementation of GDE for Li-NRR using a stainless steel cloth (SSC) support to address mass transport limitations of nitrogen and hydrogen molecules in flooded systems.26 By coupling this cathodic SSC with a hydrogen oxidation reaction at the anodic Pt-coated SSC in the LiBF4/THF solution, they demonstrated a continuous Li-NRR process capable of producing ammonia directly from N2 and H2 at 8.8 mA cm−2 under ambient pressure. Nevertheless, the practical viability of this flow configuration was severely hindered by short operational stability, with operation limited to ca. 8 minutes at 20 mA cm−2 due to high cell voltages (20–30 V), poor energy efficiency (1.4–2.8%) and subsequent electrolyte degradation. Furthermore, this early flow cell demonstrated the feasibility of ambient-pressure GDE-based Li-NRR but exposed a critical architectural trade-off: while physical separation between the cathodic and anodic compartments was maintained, the use of a bare Pt anode in organic media inevitably drove solvent oxidation and decomposition at the high anodic potentials.

To resolve the instability of conventional platinum anodes in organic media within previous systems,26,42 Fu et al. advanced the GDE flow cell concept for NH3 electrosynthesis by coupling the Li-NRR with HOR occurring on a platinum–gold (PtAu) coated anode.12 This catalytic improvement significantly lowered the anode potential (∼0.6 V vs. Pt), effectively preventing the oxidative decomposition and polymerization of the THF solvent typically triggered at higher potentials of the Pt anode. The GDE system with LiBF4/THF solution achieved a faradaic efficiency of 61 ± 1% and an energy efficiency of 13 ± 1% at 1 bar, representing a major step toward sustainable ammonia electrosynthesis under ambient conditions. Interestingly, by using this GDE flow electrolyser to examine the effects of different electrolytes on Li-NRR performance, they revealed that LiTFSI, while demonstrating high-performance in batch systems, consistently showed poor ammonia FE (18%) because sulfur-based decomposition products poisoned the PtAu HOR catalyst.56 By contrast, LiBF4 still demonstrated the highest performance among tested electrolytes, which is correlated to compact SEI formation. Taken together, their work provides valuable insights for designing an effective electrolyte for specific Li-NRR systems. Critically, this work also underscores a reactor–electrolyte coupling effect: the choice of flow cell architecture directly determines which electrolyte chemistry is viable, an important insight that was not explored in traditional batch cells. A remarkable milestone towards long-term operation of ammonia electrosynthesis was achieved by Li et al.13 They demonstrated 300 hours of continuous operation by using a DG solvent, LiBF4 electrolyte and ethanol proton carrier in a GDE flow electrolyser. This electrolyser design produced a total of 4.6 g of ammonia, with 98% of the product released directly into the gas phase. This is highly desirable for convenient, cost-effective separation compared with ammonia accumulating in liquid electrolysed solutions, which is a commonly observed scenario in the field. The gas-phase product release herein is a direct consequence of the GDE flow cell geometry—wherein the ammonia formed at the gas–liquid–solid interface preferentially partitions into the N2 gas stream rather than dissolving into the bulk electrolyte—and represents a significant practical advantage over both batch cells. This first demonstration of gram-scale ammonia production through Li-NRR marked a significant advance linking advanced reactor design and solvent choice with practical scalability.

Despite the successes of liquid-based GDEs, eliminating liquid solvent degradation entirely remains a primary goal, making the transition from liquid to solid-state electrolytes the next frontier. Cai et al. explored a novel membrane electrode assembly (MEA) reactor for NH3 electrosynthesis, using a lithium-deposited stainless-steel cloth cathode, a lithium-doped polyethylene oxide (PEO) polymer electrolyte, and carbon paper anode loaded with Pt/C catalyst (Fig. 4c).54 Flowing N2 and H2 from opposite sides of the MEA, this solid-state system enabled NH3 formation at a modest FE of ca. 9% and YR of ca. 0.7 nmol s−1 cm−2. Unlike traditional flow cells, the MEA configuration employs an integrated polymer electrolyte that serves as both the ionic conductor and structural separator. Although this architecture reduces system complexity by removing flow-control components, it introduces transport bottlenecks due to the lower Li+ mobility within the polymer matrix. While long-term stability was yet demonstrated in their work, solid-state Li-NRR offers advantages of reduced solvent decomposition, favorable gas transfer, and compact cell configuration, as compared to liquid electrolyte-based systems, and brings substantial opportunities for future developments through advanced polymer electrolytes membrane.

Coupling Li-NRR with OER

While Li-NRR coupled with HOR process presents an important interim solution for the distributed green ammonia production, the most economically and technologically desirable anode process for ammonia electrosynthesis is the oxidation of water to protons. Transitioning toward the direct use of water as a sustainable proton source, Hyung Kim et al. developed a biphasic electrolyte system that separates an organic solvent catholyte from an aqueous anolyte.57 From a reactor engineering perspective, this hybrid architecture utilizes the AEM as a selective interfacial barrier to mediate proton transport while mitigating the crossover flux of water and ions into the anhydrous Li-NRR environment. Although a cross-linked AEM significantly suppressed water permeability compared to cation-exchange membranes, enabling Li-NRR at an FE of ca. 60%, it proved insufficient for long-term stability because of significant water accumulation over the 50-hour test. The unavoidable permeation of water during extended operation triggered the accumulation of LiOH within the SEI, which passivated the cathode surface and limited stable operation to 50 hours, during which the ammonia FE declined progressively. In another work, Miao et al. utilized polyoxometalates (POMs) as electron-coupled proton buffers to link water electrolysis directly with Li-NRR.58 POMs serve as proton shuttles that receive protons from an aqueous anode and deliver them to the cathode while suppressing the HER. This approach achieved an ammonia YR of ca. 9 nmol s−1 cm−2 and a FE of 54.2% under ambient conditions. However, such approaches using batch cells are unlikely to become practical due to the unavoidable and continuous loss of the lithium electrolyte anion to the aqueous anodic phase and cross-over of water into the organic cathodic electrolyte solution. These crossover phenomena are intrinsic to the use of polymer exchange membrane, which cannot fully block ionic or solvent species driven by concentration and osmotic gradients.

Metal membrane designs represent a more elegant solution by leveraging the unique property of palladium (Pd) to completely avoid liquid electrolyte crossover. In contrast to polymer exchange membranes, Pd membranes exhibit exclusive selectivity for atomic hydrogen transfer while providing a robust physical barrier to both organic and aqueous electrolytes.59 This effectively prevents electrolyte crossover, thereby offering higher reusability, compared to polymer membranes. Specifically, this unique permeation mechanism operates via a sequence of steps: protons are reduced at the aqueous surface to form palladium hydride (PdHx), then diffuse across the lattice structure, and are oxidised at the organic-facing Pd surface to release protons to the organic electrolyte.59,60 Bemana et al. employed a single Pd foil as both palladium hydride (PdHx) cathode coupled to aqueous OER on the front side and Li-NRR cathode coupled to organic solvent oxidation on the back side.61 Their batch system produced ammonia at a modest level of 5% FE and 0.6 nmol s−1 cm−2. These modest performance metrics are more likely correlated to mass-transport limitations in traditional batch cells. Building on this foundation, Stephens and colleagues advanced the PdHx concept to a two-electrode flow cell where PdHx serves as a passive hydrogen-permeable separator rather than an active electrode.55 This configuration achieved a FE of 36 ± 4% at a current density of −6 mA cm−2 over 6 h, representing the most advanced demonstration of Li-NRR|OER coupling in a continuous flow cell to date. The transition from static batch10,11,18,25,62 to continuous-flow configurations12,13,35,63 highlights a fundamental design principle in Li-NRR innovation: the efficacy of a specific membrane chemistry is inseparably correlated to the hydrodynamic conditions of reactors. This reinforces the critical importance of integrated cell design over the sole optimization of membrane materials. Ultimately, leveraging the selective proton transfer properties of Pd membranes within a flow-cell configuration establishes a robust strategy for advanced Li-NRR, providing a viable pathway toward scalable green ammonia synthesis.

Current challenges of Li-NRR

Despite the significant progress made in optimizing electrolyte composition and SEI engineering, the Li-NRR process faces both fundamental and technological challenges that must be addressed for industrial-scale viability.

Energy efficiency limitation

The most pressing concern is the inherently low energy efficiency (EE) of Li-NRR systems (Table 1).13,63,64 Currently reported EE ranges from 1–17% under realistic operating conditions, with a theoretical thermodynamic maximum ceiling of only 27.8%—a fundamental limitation set by the electrochemistry of lithium electrodeposition.13 This poor energy efficiency can be attributed to three factors: (1) the extreme negative potentials required for lithium electrodeposition (typically −3.2 to −4.6 V vs. SHE), leading to enormous overpotentials; (2) energy losses across the dynamic SEI; and (3) additional potential drop required to drive Li0/+ electroreduction via SEI. In comparison, the Haber–Bosch process achieves high energy efficiency of ca. 60% under optimized conditions. To make Li-NRR economically competitive for distributed ammonia electrosynthesis, the EE must be improved to above 40%, which requires new approaches beyond electrolyte optimization.

To circumvent the thermodynamic limitations of lithium, alternative redox mediators possessing lower negative reduction potentials have been investigated via experimental and simulation-based approaches.65–67 Computational screening studies employing DFT have identified key thermodynamic descriptors for a viable mediator: metal nitride formation energy, standard reduction potential, stability of surface nitrogen vacancies, and adequate salt solubility in non-aqueous electrolytes.67 Based on these criteria, calcium and magnesium are the most promising candidates among the alkaline earth metal group, as both are capable of electrodeposition in organic electrolytes and satisfy the thermodynamic requirements for N2 activation.65,67 While Na and K offer similarly favourable reduction potentials on paper, both are impractical mediators due to their extreme reactivity with organic solvents and the absence of stable electrodeposition conditions in non-aqueous media.67 Nonetheless, despite their thermodynamic promise, Ca- and Mg-mediated systems currently lag behind the optimised Li-NRR in terms of ammonia yield rate and long-term stability, underscoring that the shift to alternative mediators requires systematic optimization of reaction environments including electrolyte composition, SEI chemistry, and proton donor selection.66,68

Electrolyte|solvent decomposition

Understanding solvent and electrolyte degradation in Li-NRR requires distinguishing between cathodic and anodic processes, as each proceeds through different chemistry and influences the system stability under different conditions. At the cathode, the highly negative potentials required for Li deposition drive partial reductive decomposition of the lithium salt anion and, to a lesser extent, THF. However, owing to its wide cathodic electrochemical stability window, THF itself undergoes only minimal reductive decomposition under standard Li-NRR operating conditions—its kinetic stability against cathodic reduction is in fact higher than that of common anions such as TFSI−. Rather than being purely detrimental, this controlled partial decomposition of the salt anion and solvent at the onset of operation is mechanistically beneficial via the SEI layer (see Section 2). At the anode, in contrast, degradation is far more severe and represents a stability issue in Li-NRR systems. Specifically, THF—the standard solvent due to its high N2 solubility—undergoes oxidative polymerisation at high positive anodic potentials (>3 V vs. Li0/+).43,49 This anodic polymerisation generates byproducts that increase electrolyte viscosity, reduce ionic conductivity, and hinder ion transport.49 The cumulative result is system degradation and performance loss after prolonged operation, which is commonly experienced in high-pressure batch cells.27,34 Recent advances using diglyme continuous-flow cells have achieved longer operation (300 h).13 The ether backbone of diglyme lacks the strained ring structure of THF and thus exhibits markedly superior resistance to anodic ring-opening polymerisation, though it presents challenges including higher viscosity and cost relative to THF.13

Despite its status as a “redox mediator,” lithium is not completely recovered after each reaction cycle. Multiple degradation pathways of lithium ions could occur simultaneously: (1) side reactions between electrodeposited Li0 and the organic electrolyte/solvent,31 forming stable lithium compounds (LiH, Li2O, Li2CO3, etc.) that are not electrochemically regenerated; (2) precipitation and encapsulation of lithium within thick, poorly conducting organic-rich SEI layers;31 and (3) mechanical loss through electrode fracturing and particle detachment during cell cycling.69 Over extended operation, these cumulative losses necessitate periodic electrolyte replacement, complicating system design.

Current density limitations

High current densities up to 1 A cm−2 have been demonstrated in small-area batch cells (<1 cm2) using high-surface-area porous electrodes.28 However, even in state-of-the-art continuous-flow reactors with gas-diffusion electrodes (GDE), mass transport of N2 and proton carriers remains insufficient for N2 electroreduction operated at high current densities.12,26 Achieving the industrial target of >−300 mA cm−2 current density with large-area electrodes therefore represents a fundamental kinetic challenge. Advanced continuous flow systems still operate at a current density below −60 mA cm−2 when using large-area electrodes (25 cm2 geometric area), far below the −300 mA cm−2 target.12,13,26 This scaling limitation arises from the rate mismatch between electrochemical Li+ reduction and chemical N2/proton transport to the freshly deposited lithium surface.

According to Faraday's law, the Li electrodeposition rate is directly proportional to applied current,18 whereas N2 and proton transport are governed by convection in bulk solutions and diffusion through the SEI with certain mass-transfer coefficients that are independent of current density.25 At low current densities (<10 mA cm−2), the Li0 deposition rate is slow enough that dissolved N2 molecules can diffuse through the SEI and react with freshly deposited lithium to form Li3N before subsequent protonation. However, at high current densities (>100 mA cm−2), lithium is deposited at a much faster rate than those of N2 and proton diffusion. This rate mismatch at high current densities creates several detrimental effects. First, excessive Li0 that cannot completely react with N2 owing to insufficient N2 mass transport instead undergoes unproductive side-reactions with the electrolyte/solvent molecules, thereby thickening the SEI with organic decomposition products. Second, the protonation step is limited by sluggish proton diffusion through a thick SEI, despite prior Li3N formation. Additionally, the rapid Li electrodeposition at high current densities generates dendritic lithium deposits that fracture the SEI layer, inducing fluctuating current density and non-selective electron consumption, thereby suppressing the ammonia FE. Achieving uniform current distribution, SEI composition, and N2/proton supply simultaneously across large-area electrodes therefore remains an unsolved engineering challenge for practical Li-NRR scale-up.

Product separation

Unlike the Haber–Bosch process, which produces gaseous ammonia at high pressure,1 electrochemical systems struggle to efficiently isolate NH3. In most batch systems, ammonia remains dissolved in the organic electrolyte, which requires complicated post-electrolysis distillation or gas-stripping procedures to obtain ammonia product. Recent breakthroughs using diglyme solvent and optimized GDE flow reactor achieved ca. 98% ammonia release to the gas phase, representing a major step forward. Although GDE flow cells remain feasible for scale-up, these systems require sophisticated control of experimental parameters to prevent typical electrolyte flooding at the electrode–electrolyte–gas interface.26 Precise balance of gas flow rates and flow dynamics of electrolytes, electrode wettability through optimal electrode area, and cell architecture should be optimized to achieve stable operation in specific systems.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Li-NRR has progressed rapidly in the last five years, emerging as a promising route for distributed green ammonia electrosynthesis. This review highlights how electrolyte engineering—through proton carriers, N2 pressure, lithium salts, additives, and applied potentials—controls the SEI to deliver unprecedented metrics. These include FE approaching 100% and continuous operation reaching 300 hours. Furthermore, the deployment of advanced in situ and ex situ analytical techniques to characterize the cathodic SEI has profoundly expanded our fundamental understanding of these dynamic electrochemical systems.

Despite these remarkable achievements, translating outstanding lab-scale performances to industrial application and meeting U.S. Department of Energy targets requires overcoming significant engineering hurdles. As discussed, the extreme negative potentials inevitably drive solvent decomposition, which coupled with severe kinetic mass-transport mismatches, currently prevent stable operation at high current densities. Furthermore, the low energy efficiency, prohibitive electrolyte costs, and the complexities of scaling up flow reactors represent steep fundamental barriers to practical applications.

To bridge the gap between fundamental research and practical application, future efforts must prioritize cross-disciplinary solutions. Key research directions for high-performance Li-NRR should include the screening of more robust, cost-effective solvent/electrolyte systems, the design of advanced flow cell capable of delivering sufficient mass-transport across large-area electrodes, and the development of efficient product separation protocols. Importantly, to address kinetic mismatches in these systems, future mechanistic studies should transition beyond traditional batch cells to utilize hydrodynamic electrodes that provide a precisely-control and uniform mass transport of key chemical species (viz. Li+, N2, H+) for decoupling kinetics of the key processes in a Li-NRR system. Moreover, exploring other alternative mediators with less negative reduction potential represents a great importance of improving overall energy efficiency of the redox-mediated ammonia electrosynthesis. Last but not least, integrating Li-NRR with OER via proton-selective membranes remains a critical frontier for achieving truly sustainable ammonia production.

As global interest in decarbonization drives a growing demand for sustainable chemical manufacturing, the rapidly advancing field of Li-NRR offers ample scope for future breakthroughs. By systematically summarizing the evolution of Li-NRR from early proof-of-concept works to state-of-the-art innovations, we anticipate that this review could provide strategic guidance for the development of robust, scalable devices. With continued advancements, Li-NRR is expected to reduce global reliance on the centralized Haber–Bosch process, establishing a strong foundation for decentralized, renewable-powered green ammonia production.
