O-SNAP uncovers nanoscale chromatin remodeling in dedifferentiation and stress responses
Authors: Hannah H Kim, Ellen Y Zhang, Flavio R Palma, Aayush Kant, Jose Angel Martinez-Sarmiento, Zixian Guo, Robert L Mauck, Vivek Shenoy, Marcelo G Bonini, Su Chin Heo, Melike Lakadamyali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe multi-scale organization of chromatin underlies gene regulation and cell identity, yet how nuclear architecture remodels during cell state transitions remains poorly understood. Here, we use single-molecule localization microscopy and a comprehensive analytical framework we call O-SNAP to reveal distinct chromatin remodeling trajectories in two biological contexts: dedifferentiation in chondrocytes and nuclear oxidative stress-induced remodeling in mammary epithelial cells. Conventional analyses of single-molecule localization microscopy chromatin images based on qualitative inspection or simple metrics, such as chromatin domain size, fail to capture the subtle chromatin transitions in both contexts. In contrast, O-SNAP quantitatively integrates and compares 144 spatial features extracted from single-molecule localization microscopy data, allowing machine-learning based classification of nuclear states and systematic downstream analyses such as feature selection, volcano plots, and feature set enrichment analysis to determine which spatial features most strongly drive classification results. Our analysis shows that in chondrocytes, in vitro passaging drives heterochromatin formation at late passages, whereas intermediate passages exhibit heterogeneous chromatin remodeling. In contrast, in mammary epithelial cells, nuclear oxidative stress leads to chromatin decompaction specifically in cells overexpressing the oxidation-sensitive histone H3.1 variant. Together, these findings demonstrate that integrated, multiscale spatial features of chromatin are sufficient to robustly discriminate distinct cellular states.Subject terms: Optical imaging, Single-molecule biophysics, Chromatin remodelling, Super-resolution microscopy

Introduction

The eukaryotic genome is hierarchically organized across multiple spatial and genomic scales, ranging from nanoscale chromatin structures to the global architecture of the nucleus1,2. At the most fundamental level, 146 base pairs of DNA are wrapped around histone octamers to form nucleosomes (10 nm)3, which compact further into higher-order structures including nucleosome clutches (20–100 nm)3, chromatin nanodomains (hundreds of nm)4,5, topologically associating domains (TADs)6,7, A/B compartments8, and ultimately chromosome territories (several micrometers)9. This multiscale organization is dynamically regulated by chemical modifications to DNA, such as methylation, and to histone tails, including site-specific methylation or acetylation, which modulate chromatin compaction and accessibility to transcriptional machinery10–13. Architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin further shape the genome by mediating long-range interactions and organizing it into domains such as TADs14,15. Additionally, associations with nuclear bodies, including the nuclear lamina16 and nuclear speckles17,18, contribute to functional compartmentalization, segregating the genome into transcriptionally repressive regions like lamina-associated domains (LADs) and active regions near nuclear speckles. This layered spatial compartmentalization is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of cell identity and function19,20. Understanding the relationship between the spatial organization of chromatin and cell state is therefore central to elucidating the molecular basis of developmental programming, cellular reprogramming, and disease-associated state transitions.

Recent advances in genomic and imaging technologies provide an unprecedented view into 3D genome organization21. Among imaging methods, single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) has been particularly powerful, enabling nanoscale visualization of sub-nuclear structures3,22–24 and high-resolution tracing of chromatin folding25–27, thereby revealing previously inaccessible aspects of chromatin organization1,28,29. For example, SMLM has revealed that chromatin is a disordered fiber composed of heterogeneous clusters of nucleosomes or nucleosome clutches (also referred to as chromatin packing domains)3,5,30. Despite this heterogeneity, clutch size has been shown to correlate with the pluripotency status of both mouse embryonic stem cells and human induced pluripotent stem cells3. SMLM imaging further revealed progressive changes to chromatin organization and epigenetic modifications at different time points during somatic cell reprogramming31 as well as fragmentation of heterochromatin in cancer cells32. More recently, SMLM has demonstrated that chromatin architecture in mesenchymal stem cells is sensitive to mechanical cues, and that such remodeling is recapitulated in tissue-resident cells exposed to altered mechanical environments33.

To gain a quantitative understanding of how chromatin architecture responds to distinct environmental cues, we used SMLM to examine nuclear remodeling in two distinct biological contexts: during in vitro expansion of human chondrocyte cells, which has been shown to lead to transcriptional dysregulation34–36 and chondrocyte dedifferentiation37,38 and oxidative stress in mammary epithelial cells, which has been shown to induce activation of mesenchymal to epithelial transition (EMT)-associated genes and the acquisition of more plastic phenotypes39. In both contexts, chromatin reorganization was unexpectedly subtle, and conventional analytical approaches, focused on simple parameters such as chromatin domain size or global compaction, failed to detect meaningful state transitions. We reasoned that the nucleus’s inherently multiscale, hierarchical, and heterogeneous organization cannot be captured by a handful of global descriptors and that a more comprehensive approach is required to reveal nuanced remodeling events in SMLM data4,33,39.

Deep learning-based methods are having a growing impact in the SMLM field, especially in facilitating tasks centered on super-resolution image data acquisition40–47. Machine learning approaches have also been developed for post-imaging analysis to characterize structures from localization data48,49, but these approaches focus on organization at the level of proteins or simple organelles. For the complex organization of the nucleus, image-based deep learning architectures have been applied for accurate classification of cell states based on SMLM images of chromatin-associated components such as histones, DNA, or RNA polymerase II50. However, these methods rely on rasterizing the raw localization data into pixelated intensity maps, which can obscure fine-grained spatial information inherent to SMLM48. Additionally, the image-based neural networks suffer from limited interpretability, making it difficult to pinpoint which spatial features drive classification decisions and impeding biological insight. Hence, the SMLM field lacks an integrated and comprehensive analytical framework for quantitatively characterizing alterations in chromatin architecture. The increasing complexity of SMLM datasets of nuclear targets51–54 demand appropriate analysis tools to fully leverage the wealth of information captured in such data.

To address this gap, we developed Objective, Single-molecule Nucleus Architecture Profiler (O-SNAP) as a tool to automatically and holistically perform data exploration in SMLM data of nuclear targets, including chromatin. O-SNAP begins by extracting a rich set of multiscale nuclear features from localization coordinates, preserving the resolution and spatial complexity of the original data. We leveraged previously established and robust methods, including Voronoi tessellation55–57 and DBSCAN-based clustering58, to extract these spatial features. These spatial features are then interrogated as an integrated feature set using a suite of analytical tools largely adapted from sequencing-based analyses of gene sets, including volcano plots, feature set enrichment analysis (FSEA), and pseudotime trajectory reconstruction. In addition, machine-learning models are trained on a subset of the features for cell state classification. Overall, O-SNAP translates complex localization data into actionable hypotheses and the multivariate analyses contextualize the extent and magnitude of nuclear architectural changes.

Applying O-SNAP to chondrocyte in vitro expansion not only enabled accurate classification of distinct cell states but also revealed an increase in compact heterochromatin formation at both the nuclear interior and periphery in late passages, whereas intermediate stages displayed heterogeneous remodeling. In contrast, mammary epithelial cells overexpressing the oxidation-sensitive histone variant H3.1 exhibited chromatin decompaction in response to elevated oxidative stress, consistent with previous electron microscopy findings, while cells expressing the oxidation-insensitive variant H3.2 showed no such changes.

Together, SMLM imaging and integrated O-SNAP analysis uncovered subtle, nanoscale chromatin remodeling events across distinct biological contexts that are functionally relevant as validated through ATAC-Seq and RNA-Seq analysis of in vitro expanded chondrocytes. O-SNAP also highlighted which spatial features strongly distinguish cell states, enabling users to trace classification outcomes back to specific spatial changes in chromatin architecture. Therefore, the integrated analysis guides hypothesis generation and mechanistic understanding by linking nuclear organization to cellular identity and chromatin remodeling across development, reprogramming, and disease. While the biological meaning of individual features depends on the nuclear target being visualized, we propose putative biological interpretations for chromatin-based features. Beyond chromatin, O-SNAP can be readily extended to analyze other nuclear components, including epigenetic marks, nuclear bodies, and the spatial organization of transcriptional machinery.

Results

Simplistic SMLM analysis fails to resolve nuclear remodeling across distinct biological contexts

To determine how chromatin is remodeled in response to diverse external cues, we carried out super-resolution imaging of histone H2B to visualize global chromatin structure in two model systems: mammary epithelial cells subjected to nuclear oxidative stress and chondrocytes cultured in vitro.

Nuclear oxidative stress has recently emerged as a key regulator of chromatin organization and gene expression. Chromatin spatial organization and transcriptional regulation are modulated in part by the incorporation of distinct histone variants, isoforms of canonical histones that differ slightly in amino acid sequence59. Aberrant deposition of these variants has been linked to pathological states, including tumor initiation, therapy resistance, and metastasis60,61. Notably, H3.1 is distinguished from H3.2 and H3.3 by a single unique cysteine residue at position 96 (Cys96)62 and is enriched in transcriptionally silent chromatin63. This cysteine makes H3.1 susceptible to oxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a property not shared by the other H3 variants64,65. Recent work leveraged a nuclear-localized D-amino acid oxidase (NLS-DAO) system to selectively elevate nuclear ROS (nROS) levels upon addition of D-alanine (D-Ala), enabling chromatin-specific redox manipulation without inducing significant direct oxidative DNA damage39. In breast cancer cells, oxidation of H3.1Cys96 under these conditions was shown to trigger its eviction from chromatin, thereby promoting decompaction of silenced domains and activation of EMT-associated genes, as revealed by chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) and electron microscopy analyses39. However, the low throughput of electron microscopy limits systematic analysis of chromatin remodeling under diverse stress conditions.

To assess the impact of oxidative stress on the spatial distribution of chromatin in a more high throughput manner, we used STORM imaging to visualize chromatin organization in MCF10A mammary epithelial cells co-expressing nuclear-targeted D-amino acid oxidase (NLS-DAO) and either the oxidation-sensitive histone variant H3.1 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1A) or the oxidation-insensitive variant H3.2 (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Cells were imaged under baseline conditions and following treatment with 10 nM D-Ala for 4 h (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Visual inspection of the H2B super-resolution images of treated and untreated cells did not reveal obvious large-scale chromatin reorganization for either histone variant (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Fig. 1A), suggesting that any changes in spatial architecture are likely to be subtle, not visually apparent, especially considering the cell-to-cell variability in chromatin organization (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

In a second model, we investigated the impact of disrupted mechanical environments on chondrocyte chromatin organization during in vitro expansion of human chondrocytes. Chondrocytes are the resident cells of articular cartilage, responsible for synthesizing and maintaining the extracellular matrix (ECM) that supports cartilage integrity and function. Injuries to articular cartilage are common and can lead to progressive erosion of joint surfaces and persistent inflammation, ultimately resulting in osteoarthritis66–68. One therapeutic approach for early-stage disease involves autologous cell-based repair, in which chondrocytes are harvested from the patient, expanded in vitro, and then re-implanted into the defect site using a biomaterial scaffold69,70. A major limitation of this strategy is the poor quality of the ECM produced by the re-implanted cells, a problem linked to the loss of chondrocyte phenotype during in vitro expansion37,38. RNA-sequencing studies have revealed widespread transcriptional dysregulation associated with passaging resulting from culture-induced chondrocyte dedifferentiation34–36. However, subsequent studies reported a poor correlation between chondrogenic gene expression and ECM protein abundance, indicating that transcriptional changes alone cannot fully explain the loss of matrix production and chondrocyte phenotype71. Because chromatin organization lies upstream of gene expression, and because chromatin spatial organization and compaction can be modulated by epigenetic drugs, we asked whether transcriptional dysregulation in passaged chondrocytes is accompanied by aberrant remodeling of chromatin architecture, and whether chromatin represents a potential target for rescuing chondrocyte identity during in vitro expansion. To address this, we performed super-resolution imaging of H2B in primary human chondrocytes (hChon) isolated from two independent donors (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1B). Cells were imaged at three timepoints during in vitro culture: Passage 0 (P0; baseline, no passaging), Passage 3 (P3; approximately 3 weeks of passaging), and Passage 6 (P6; approximately 6 weeks of passaging; Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1B). As with the MCF10A histone variant datasets, the images of chondrocyte chromatin exhibited cell-to-cell variability (Supplementary Fig. 1B) and visual inspection of the super-resolution images did not reveal obvious large-scale chromatin remodeling.

Because visual inspection did not provide clear guidance for downstream analysis, we next quantified established chromatin parameters that have previously revealed nanoscale remodeling in systems exhibiting more pronounced changes in chromatin spatial organization, including human fibroblast cells (hFbs) treated with the histone deacetylase inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA; Fig. 1D), tenocytes (hTen) derived from tendinosis patients (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1C), and hFbs undergoing reprogramming (Fig. 1F). Specifically, we measured the ratio of chromatin density at the nuclear periphery versus the interior (Fig. 1G), compared global chromatin compaction (Fig. 1H) and applied segmentation to define chromatin packing domains and quantify their size (Fig. 1I).

While some of these metrics clearly distinguished conditions associated with large-scale remodeling, such as the ratio of chromatin density at the nuclear periphery relative to the nuclear interior increased dramatically in tendinosis cells (Fig. 1G), they revealed only subtle and difficult-to-interpret changes when applied to chondrocytes and mammary epithelial cells (Fig. 1G–I), where remodeling was more modest. The specific parameters that changed varied across biological systems, precluding generalizable interpretation (Fig. 1G–I). Although all three parameters exhibited small but statistically significant shifts in D-Ala–treated mammary epithelial cells (Fig. 1G–I), the effect sizes were small. Moreover, these subtle changes were observed in cells expressing both the oxidation-sensitive and insensitive histone variants, making it difficult to ascribe clear biological significance to the results (Fig. 1G–I). While global chromatin compaction and domain radius increased in chondrocytes at Passage 6, cells at Passage 3 exhibited high heterogeneity and only subtle or negligible changes in both parameters, making it difficult to assess the biological significance of these differences (Fig. 1H, I). These observations emphasize that with increasing numbers of features, conventional analyses quickly reach their interpretive limits, underscoring the need for an integrated, data-driven framework to capture chromatin remodeling across diverse systems.

O-SNAP for automated, multiscale chromatin organization profiling

To perform a more comprehensive comparison of nuclear states in SMLM data, we developed O-SNAP, a pipeline that uses high-dimensional chromatin features to systematically compare cell states. O-SNAP begins by processing point cloud data (i.e., the raw x-y coordinates) of fluorophores detected via SMLM for nuclear targets such as chromatin, histone modifications, or nuclear bodies, corresponding to high-resolution spatial organization of these targets within the nucleus (Fig. 2). O-SNAP extracts 144 spatial features encompassing nuclear morphology, global and radial chromatin organization, and multiscale domain architecture (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Tables 1–3). While individual features build on established metrics used to describe SMLM-resolved structures, no existing framework, to our knowledge, integrates these parameters across scales to quantitatively characterize an organelle as complex and heterogeneous as the nucleus. The key strength of O-SNAP lies in its capacity to simultaneously evaluate and integrate multiple high-dimensional spatial features through tailored downstream analyses adapted from sequencing-based data exploration, including volcano plots, FSEA, and pseudotime trajectory inference. In addition, O-SNAP performs machine learning based classification (described in detail below) using a subset of spatial features to assess whether chromatin organization alone can robustly discriminate cellular states. This multi-level integration enables O-SNAP to extract information inaccessible to traditional, single-feature analyses. Detailed descriptions of feature extraction are provided in the O-SNAP feature generation section under the “Methods” and the underlying algorithms rely on established segmentation approaches for SMLM data, including Voronoi tessellation and DBSCAN clustering.

The O-SNAP workflow.

The input data for O-SNAP consists of 2-D SMLM localization data of targets visualized in the nucleus. Following data acquisition and segmentation of the nucleus, O-SNAP calculates 144 features from the coordinate information of the SMLM data. Four types of analyses can then be independently conducted. (i) A volcano analysis identifies which features experience the greatest value change between two phenotypes. (ii) Feature set enrichment analysis determines how categories of features compare between phenotypes. (iii) When relevant, pseudotime analysis builds a trajectory based on O-SNAP features to describe feature value changes as cells transition through multiple cell states over time. (iv) The final analysis in the pipeline identifies O-SNAP features with the highest potential to discriminate between different phenotypes, implements a principal component analysis (PCA) on these features, and generates a classification model to evaluate how sufficient these features are in distinguishing the conditions. Once multiscale, high-dimensional spatial features are extracted, O-SNAP offers multiple downstream analytical modalities for comparing chromatin architecture across distinct cell states (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 3–5). Drawing inspiration from transcriptomic workflows such as RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), these include: (i) volcano plots to identify the most differentially changing spatial features between two groups; (ii) FSEA to reveal coordinated shifts within functionally related feature families (Supplementary Fig. 3); (iii) pseudotime trajectory analysis to temporally order cells based on progressive changes in chromatin features (Supplementary Fig. 4); and (iv) feature selection and classification using machine learning models trained to distinguish between cell states based on spatial patterns of chromatin organization (Supplementary Fig. 5). These complementary analysis modules enable both hypothesis-driven and data-driven interrogation of nuclear architecture across cellular transitions, thereby facilitating exploration of chromatin architecture changes presented by information-dense SMLM data. Further details on the implementation of each analysis are in the “Methods” section. Runtimes for the full analysis pipeline are shown for select datasets in Supplementary Table 3. The most time-consuming step in the pipeline is the generation of Voronoi cells and DBSCAN clusters, whereas feature extraction and downstream analysis require only a few minutes, depending on the dataset size.

O-SNAP recapitulates the impact of histone deacetylase inhibition and tendinosis on chromatin spatial organization

To first validate O-SNAP, we applied the pipeline to two well-characterized systems known to exhibit distinct, drastic, and opposing changes in chromatin spatial organization: (1) hFbs treated with the histone deacetylase inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA; Figs. 1D and 3), and (2) primary tenocytes isolated from healthy individuals versus individuals with tendinosis (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 1C, and Fig. 4). TSA treatment induces global hyperacetylation of histone tails, weakening nucleosome-nucleosome interactions and leading to chromatin decompaction due to increased electrostatic repulsion between nucleosomes72. In prior work using SMLM imaging, we showed that TSA treatment results in the fragmentation of large, dense chromatin packing domains, which we referred to as nucleosome clutches, into smaller, less compact, and more spatially dispersed domains3,72. In contrast, tenocytes from tendinosis patients exhibit increased heterochromatin formation, with chromatin becoming sequestered at the nuclear lamina, a repressive nuclear compartment enriched for constitutive heterochromatin33. These two systems provide contrasting benchmarks with visually clear, well-characterized differences, making them ideal test cases to evaluate whether O-SNAP can recapitulate known trends and uncover additional, previously overlooked changes.

We applied O-SNAP to compare the global chromatin organization in wild-type and TSA-treated hFbs (Fig. 1D) using volcano analysis to identify the most significantly altered spatial features (Fig. 3A). Applying fold-change and significance cutoffs revealed three prominent feature changes, particularly within the nuclear interior (I) and periphery (P; Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Fig. 6A–C). At the interior, DBSCAN clustering detected a significant increase in the number of chromatin packing domains following TSA treatment (Feature I.3; Fig. 3A, B, Supplementary Fig. 6A, and Supplementary Table 4), consistent with prior findings that large, compact domains fragment into smaller, more numerous units upon chromatin decompaction72.

At the nuclear periphery, the two most significantly changing features related to the asymmetry in the distributions of chromatin domain compaction (Feature P.6) and radius of gyration (Feature P.9; Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 6A). Closer examination of the DBSCAN-segmented chromatin packing domains revealed a general decrease in both density (Fig. 3B) and radius of gyration (Supplementary Fig. 6B) at the periphery, consistent with chromatin decompaction. However, a subset of peripheral domains remained highly compact or spatially extended (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 6B), leading to a heavy right tail in these distributions and contributing to increased asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 6C). These findings suggest that TSA treatment affects peripheral heterochromatin heterogeneously, inducing widespread decompaction while a minority of domains remain resistant. These results validate O-SNAP’s ability to recapitulate known chromatin remodeling events (more numerous, less compact chromatin packing domains) and highlight its potential to uncover spatial features that inform hypothesis generation (heterogeneous impact of TSA on peripheral heterochromatin compaction).

FSEA further supported these findings, revealing an increased number of chromatin domains globally, at the periphery, and in the interior (Fig. 3C). Nuclear morphology features such as increased nuclear size and roundness also emerged, consistent with chromatin decompaction driving nuclear expansion73,74. Hence, FSEA analysis provides both overlapping and complementary information to volcano plot analysis, highlighting the added value of distinct analysis modalities.

Finally, we applied MRMR feature selection and classification (Fig. 3D). O-SNAP achieved 91.76 ± 5.22% accuracy in distinguishing TSA-treated from wild-type cells (Fig. 3E), which decreased to 57.99 ± 9.04% upon random shuffling of the data labels (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Details on how the confusion matrix accuracies and the overall accuracy are computed can be found in the Classification section under the “Methods”. The most predictive features involved changes in the compaction of interior chromatin and heterogeneity of histone abundance within the peripheral domains (Fig. 3E), aligning with the volcano analysis results on asymmetry (Fig. 3B). Plotting the three selected features revealed clear separation between wild-type and treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 6E).

We next applied the O-SNAP pipeline to primary tenocytes isolated from healthy individuals and from patients with tendinosis33, where loss of tensional homeostasis negatively impacts the function of tenocytes (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1C)75. Volcano analysis revealed 23 significantly altered spatial features (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table 5). Notably, among these changes was the increased ratio of chromatin at the nuclear periphery relative to the interior (Feature P.17; Supplementary Fig. 7A), recapitulating our prior observation that chromatin becomes sequestered at the nuclear lamina in tendinosis (also see Fig. 1G)33. This peripheral accumulation was accompanied by a reduction in the number of chromatin domains at the nuclear interior (Feature I.2; Fig. 4A, B), reflecting a shift in chromatin organization away from the interior toward the periphery. FSEA corroborated these findings, revealing increased chromatin enrichment and greater domain heterogeneity at the nuclear periphery, along with a trend toward rounder nuclear morphology (Fig. 4C). These results provide an unbiased and comprehensive analysis of large-scale nuclear remodeling in disease. Finally, classification analysis distinguished healthy and tendinosis-derived tenocytes with an accuracy of 95.00 ± 6.85% (Supplementary Fig. 7B, C). The most discriminative features included the number of chromatin domains at the periphery and the spatial distribution (spacing) of domains within the nuclear interior (Supplementary Fig. 7B), again reflecting the redistribution of chromatin from interior to peripheral compartments. The increase in compact chromatin at the nucleus periphery in tendinosis cells is consistent with prior ATAC-Seq results where loss of cytoskeletal tension in tendon fibroblasts lead to an overall decrease in chromatin accessibility76.

Together, these analyses across two previously validated systems demonstrate that O-SNAP can reliably detect biologically meaningful changes in chromatin architecture and provide complementary insights across multiple analytical modalities. The pipeline also enables users to identify major feature changes in an unbiased and highly quantitative manner.

O-SNAP reveals a detailed overview of how global chromatin and specific epigenetic marks remodel in early reprogramming

We next evaluated O-SNAP on a more complex and heterogeneous system: heterokaryon-mediated reprogramming of somatic human fibroblasts (hFbs) via fusion with mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). Initiated by diffusible mESC factors acting on the hFb nucleus, the fusion induces reprogramming of the somatic nucleus77–80. We previously showed that chromatin undergoes progressive decompaction over time following fusion31. Unlike TSA treatment or tendinosis, which induce relatively uniform and drastic chromatin changes, heterokaryon reprogramming generates more subtle changes, particularly at early time points after cell fusion and a large amount of cell-to-cell heterogeneity31. These aspects of the heterokaryon dataset pose a more challenging case for O-SNAP.

We first analyzed three cell types using SMLM images of histone H2B, corresponding to global chromatin organization (Fig. 1F): control hFbs prior to fusion, control mESCs prior to fusion, and partially reprogrammed hFbs imaged 48 h post-fusion31. Despite the inherent heterogeneity, O-SNAP achieved an average of 80.94 ± 5.28% classification accuracy in distinguishing these states, dropping to 41.08 ± 6.71% upon random label permutation (Fig. 5A, B), demonstrating the pipeline’s robustness in complex biological settings.

To assess how chromatin remodeling correlated with histone modifications, we additionally analyzed two marks, H3K27me3 representing a repressive mark (Fig. 5C) and H3K9ac representing an active mark (Fig. 5D), which we previously showed to be differentially regulated during reprogramming31. Volcano analysis revealed that 36 spatial features significantly decreased and one increased for H3K27me3 in hFb nuclei after 48 h of reprogramming (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Table 6), while only 5 features changed for H3K9ac (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Table 7), consistent with our previous findings that the repressive heterochromatin is selectively depleted during early heterokaryon reprogramming while the levels of active euchromatin are maintained31. Accordingly, classification accuracy decreased from 97.40 ± 1.46% with H3K27me3 data to 83.87 ± 3.95% with H3K9ac data when distinguishing between two cell states: the somatic hFb nucleus of the heterokaryon at 48-h post-fusion and the control hFbs prior to fusion (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Thus, O-SNAP confirms previous observations that changes in the repressive H3K27me3 mark correlate more strongly with reprogramming while also revealing subtle alterations in active histone marks.

To further probe dynamic chromatin remodeling, we applied pseudotime trajectory analysis using the R package, dynverse81, to the H3K27me3 images of hFb nuclei at 0-, 6-, 24-, and 48-h post-fusion (Fig. 5G). The resulting trajectory captured a continuous transition, with control hFbs prior to fusion (0 h) clustering at the beginning and hFbs at 48-h post-fusion clustering at the end of the timeline (Fig. 5G). In contrast, applying the same pseudotime analysis to global chromatin organization visualized via H2B yielded a less distinct trajectory, with cells from different time points more evenly distributed and inter-mixed along the pseudotime axis (Supplementary Fig. 8C). These results indicate that O-SNAP-extracted spatial features captures temporal dynamics in chromatin architecture, and that repressive H3K27me3-marked chromatin provides greater sensitivity for resolving these transitions than global chromatin structure alone. Examining the trajectory further revealed that H3K27me3 abundance declined early, followed by global decompaction of H3K27me3 marked chromatin (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 8D). Intriguingly, at the end of the trajectory, chromatin packing domains (or clutches) exhibited increased compaction (Feature I.5 in Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 8D), suggesting the possible emergence of H3K27me3-marked highly compact heterochromatin packing domains that may be resistant to remodeling, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation in future studies. While these trends were also present in the H2B data, evidenced both along the pseudotime trajectory as well as the distributions according to the ground-truth labels, the magnitude of change for all features was much more modest compared to the trends in the H3K27me3 data (Supplementary Fig. 8C, D). These results further highlight that repressive chromatin marked by H3K27me3 provides a more sensitive readout of remodeling dynamics during reprogramming than global chromatin organization alone.

Overall, O-SNAP not only provides a comprehensive analysis of specific nuclear features that change in heterokaryon reprogramming and the temporal dynamics of these changes but also reveals epigenetic features such as highly compact H3K27me3-marked packing domains, which may be potentially linked to incomplete reprogramming.

Oxidative stress impacts chromatin spatial organization in human mammary epithelial cells

Having validated O-SNAP on previously characterized model systems exhibiting overt chromatin remodeling, we next applied it to determine whether MCF10A mammary epithelial cells undergo consistent spatial chromatin remodeling in response to elevated nROS levels, and whether these changes depend on the expressed histone variant. Volcano plot analysis revealed that eight chromatin features significantly changed upon D-Ala treatment in H3.1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 8), whereas only one feature passed the significance and fold-change thresholds in H3.2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table 9). These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that H3.1, due to its unique Cys96 residue, is selectively sensitive to nROS-induced oxidation39. Supporting this distinction, feature selection and classification analysis yielded a 90.19 ± 3.67% accuracy in distinguishing D-Ala-treated from untreated cells in the H3.1-overexpressing group (Fig. 6C), compared to just 69.62 ± 3.92% accuracy in the H3.2-overexpressing group (Fig. 6D).

Together, these results suggest that when H3.1 is the predominantly expressed histone variant, elevated nROS levels elicit detectable spatial remodeling of chromatin nanostructure, while H3.2-rich chromatin remains largely unaffected. Further analysis revealed that the features most impacted in the H3.1-overexpressing condition reflected decreased chromatin density in both internal and peripheral chromatin packing domains (nucleosome clutches; Fig. 6E, Supplementary Fig. 9A, and Supplementary Table 8), consistent with a shift toward chromatin decompaction. By contrast, the H3.2-overexpressing nuclei did not exhibit a decrease in compaction and the magnitude of the change that was present was less pronounced (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Fig. 9B). This observation aligns with earlier electron microscopy and ChIP-Seq studies reporting similar chromatin opening in response to ROS-induced H3.1 eviction39.

These findings demonstrate that O-SNAP can sensitively detect subtle, histone variant-dependent changes in chromatin nanoscale architecture under redox conditions. The spatial reorganization of nucleosomes within dense chromatin packing domains to a more open conformation under H3.1 overexpression but not H3.2 characterized by O-SNAP reinforces the emerging view that histone variant composition modulates chromatin’s structural plasticity in response to oxidative stress39 with important implications for understanding gene regulation in cancer, therapy resistance, and metastasis82,83. Importantly, the comprehensive, multidimensional analysis enabled by O-SNAP was essential to uncover these histone variant–specific differences in chromatin remodeling, which were not captured by conventional low-dimensional metrics (Fig. 1G–I). Both the volcano plot analysis and the multi-feature classification revealed that chromatin remodeling was substantially more pronounced in H3.1-overexpressing cells than in those expressing H3.2.

Primary human chondrocytes cultured in vitro undergo chromatin spatial remodeling

We next applied the O-SNAP pipeline to better analyze human chondrocytes from two distinct donors cultured in vitro for extended periods. Classification analysis using O-SNAP features achieved 82.14 ± 6.56% accuracy for Donor A and 82.89 ± 8.66% for Donor B (Fig. 7A) in distinguishing cells across the three passage conditions (P0, P3, and P6), indicating that chromatin spatial organization undergoes measurable and reproducible changes during in vitro expansion. To identify the specific features driving these changes, we performed volcano plot analyses comparing P0 and P6 cells (Fig. 7B). Six features were significantly altered in Donor A and seven in Donor B, with five overlapping feature changes between the two donors, suggesting remarkably consistent patterns of chromatin remodeling associated with extended passaging (Fig. 7C and Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). These shared features reflected increases in the size of chromatin packing domains and in the number of histones within both peripheral and interior chromatin packing domains (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11), pointing toward the formation of larger chromatin domains indicative of increased heterochromatinization. These results were further validated using Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-Seq), which showed a decrease in chromatin accessibility at the P6 timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 10A). In contrast, features that changed between P0 and P3 were donor-specific (Supplementary Fig. 10B–E and Supplementary Tables 12–15), suggesting that the intermediate P3 state may represent a more heterogeneous phase of chromatin reorganization, whereas the structural changes observed by P6 are more stable and convergent.

These observations were further consistent with bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, which revealed a greater number of downregulated than upregulated genes at the P6 time point (613 downregulated versus 456 upregulated). Venn diagram analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the P0–P3, P3–P6, and P0–P6 comparisons showed that the majority of shared DEGs occurred between the P0–P3 and P0–P6 transitions, whereas very few DEGs were common to all three comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 10F). To better compare O-SNAP data to these RNA-Seq results, we combined the data from the two donors, which revealed a similar pattern for differentially changing O-SNAP features. Venn diagram analysis revealed a high degree of overlap between features shared in the P0–P3 and P0–P6 comparisons, with no features common to all three transitions (Supplementary Fig. 10G). The P3–P6 transition exhibited the smallest number of differential O-SNAP features (Supplementary Fig. 10G), mirroring the RNA-seq results, which also showed the fewest DEGs during this interval (Supplementary Fig. 10F).

Together, these findings demonstrate strong concordance between chromatin remodeling events captured by O-SNAP, chromatin accessibility changes measured by ATAC-seq, and transcriptional changes detected by RNA-seq. At the same time, they highlight the single-cell nature of O-SNAP analysis, which enables the detection of cellular heterogeneity while requiring substantially fewer cells than bulk sequencing approaches. They also demonstrate that the O-SNAP pipeline can uncover subtle but persistent alterations to chromatin organization in chondrocytes during in vitro expansion, remaining robust to differences arising from donor variability. These insights provide a foundation for strategies aimed at preserving native chromatin states to support improved chondrocyte function for better therapeutic efficacy. More broadly, these findings highlight the power of O-SNAP to compare complex patterns of chromatin organization across diverse conditions, a task that would be prohibitively difficult using single features analyzed individually.

Discussion

Here, we introduce O-SNAP, a comprehensive, multi-scale, and high dimensional analysis pipeline for profiling nuclear architecture from SMLM data. Unlike previous machine learning approaches such as ASAP84 and AiNU50 that rely on rasterized image renderings, O-SNAP directly processes point cloud data, preserving the high-resolution spatial information critical for accurately characterizing chromatin organization. Our prior work with ECLiPSE applied to simple organelles like mitochondria and lysosomes demonstrated that the parameters used for rendering SMLM images in pipelines such as ASAP influences classification outcomes and can lead to information loss48. O-SNAP therefore builds on this foundation of using point cloud rather than rendered data specifically for analyzing the complex, multi-scale spatial features of the nucleus. The framework incorporates downstream statistical tools inspired by RNA-Seq differential expression analysis to simultaneously examine multiple feature trends. The ability to link classification outcomes to specific spatial features is a key advantage over black-box deep learning methods, as it allows for better insight into how specific chromatin features contribute to cell state identity and may guide targeted manipulation of chromatin states for therapeutic purposes.

Using O-SNAP, we validated known chromatin remodeling events in response to epigenetic perturbations (e.g., TSA treatment) and disease conditions (e.g., tendinosis) while also uncovering more subtle and heterogeneous alterations in chromatin spatial features. Despite TSA generally being considered to promote global decompaction, our super-resolution imaging results reveal that TSA treatment in fibroblasts leads to non-uniform decompaction of chromatin domains, particularly at the nuclear periphery, suggesting selective sensitivity of Lamina-Associated Domains (LADs). TSA has been used in several contexts to prime cells to induce greater efficiency in cellular reprogramming upon additional stimuli such as mechanoactivation85 or developmental cues86. The decompaction-resistant domains we identify may potentially limit the efficiency of epigenetic priming provided by TSA, although further studies are needed to validate this hypothesis.

In heterokaryon reprogramming, O-SNAP revealed the persistence of a subpopulation of highly compact chromatin domains enriched in H3K27me3, indicative of facultative heterochromatin that may resist reprogramming. These observations open avenues for future studies using genomic methods such as ChIP-Seq to map LADs (via Lamin B1) or H3K27me3-enriched regions, enabling the translation of insights obtained from spatial chromatin architecture into genomic context to test specific hypothesis. The robustness of O-SNAP to cell-to-cell heterogeneity in chromatin states was consistently demonstrated across systems by randomly permuting the ground-truth labels, which resulted in a drastic reduction in classification accuracy. Thus, O-SNAP distinguishes cell states based on genuine differences in chromatin spatial organization, remaining robust to the inherent variability among individual cells.

We applied O-SNAP to extract insights from SMLM datasets examining global chromatin organization in response to environmental cues in two distinct biological contexts. In the first, O-SNAP revealed differential sensitivity of chromatin to oxidative stress based on histone variant composition highlighting the utility of O-SNAP in detecting subtle metabolism-driven chromatin remodeling. Specifically, we found that chromatin containing the H3.1 variant was more susceptible to decompaction in response to elevated ROS compared to H3.2-enriched chromatin, consistent with previous reports that used ChIP-Seq and electron microscopy39. Although the chromatin compaction changes detected by super-resolution imaging and O-SNAP analysis were subtle, they carry important implications for cell phenotype. Notably, prior work has shown that oxidative stress–induced chromatin opening at regions enriched for the histone variant H3.1 leads to activation of EMT-associated genes, resulting in altered metastatic potential and therapy resistance in breast cancer cells39. Taken together, these findings suggest that even modest spatial remodeling of chromatin can have substantial functional consequences. Accordingly, the ability to extract such subtle spatial changes through high-dimensional analysis has important implications for understanding how environmental cues influence cell identity and phenotypic state.

We next applied O-SNAP to study in vitro expansion of chondrocytes and observed progressive chromatin remodeling and heterochromatin formation with increasing passage number. Persistent chromatin alterations emerged at Passage 6 in data acquired from two independent donors, revealing the robustness of O-SNAP to donor variability. Changes at earlier passages (P3) were donor dependent, suggesting a more heterogeneous cell state at this timepoint.

These observations were consistent with reduced chromatin accessibility at the P6 time point, as revealed by ATAC-seq, and with bulk RNA-seq data showing similar trends in DEGs across the three passages. Moreover, they align with prior transcriptomic studies indicating substantial heterogeneity during early chondrocyte expansion. For example, single-cell RNA-sequencing of mouse chondrocytes expanded through early (P2) and late (P8) passages showed that early-passage cells occupied multiple transcriptional clusters, retaining partial similarity to freshly isolated chondrocytes, whereas late-passage cells largely collapsed into a single dominant cluster34. At the chromatin level, ATAC-seq analyses in the same study revealed a global reduction in chromatin accessibility at late passages, in agreement with our O-SNAP analysis and ATAC-seq data from late-passage human chondrocytes. Single-cell RNA-sequencing of human chondrocytes at early passages (14 days in culture, corresponding to approximately P2) further demonstrated pronounced transcriptional heterogeneity without clear sub-cluster structure36. The heterogeneous and donor-dependent chromatin remodeling we observed at early passages likely reflects this underlying transcriptional diversity.

Given the poor correlation between expression of chondrogenic genes and collagen abundance71 and the broad, non-specific nature of transcriptional misregulation during early expansion, it remains difficult to identify targeted interventions capable of preserving or restoring the native chondrogenic phenotype based on transcriptomic data alone. Our observations on chromatin remodeling suggest a critical temporal window prior to Passage 6 during which epigenetic interventions, such as treatments promoting chromatin decompaction (e.g., TSA), may be effective in preventing unproductive heterochromatin formation. Future studies that systematically apply such interventions at defined time points, combined with super-resolution imaging and O-SNAP analysis, will be instrumental in testing this hypothesis.

Beyond the biological applications explored here, O-SNAP analysis of STORM images has recently revealed that acute disruption of nuclear speckles, through targeted degradation of the core speckle proteins SON and SRRM2, leads to enrichment of chromatin at the nuclear lamina87. Complementary genomic analyses further identified a class of genomic regions, termed SWING domains, that, upon speckle disruption, relocalize to the nuclear periphery and acquire repressive histone marks such as H3K9me3, concomitant with transcriptional downregulation, particularly of genes involved in developmental pathways. Together, these findings illustrate how O-SNAP can facilitate the discovery of links between nuclear organization, chromatin state, and gene regulation, highlighting its utility for studying nuclear architecture across diverse biological contexts.

Importantly, O-SNAP is not limited to global chromatin spatial organization analysis. As demonstrated in the heterokaryon reprogramming model, the pipeline can be readily adapted to study other nuclear features, including histone modifications. Its compatibility with different nuclear targets opens the door to analyzing the spatial organization of diverse nuclear structures, such as transcriptional machinery or nuclear bodies like speckles and nucleoli87. Future work applying O-SNAP to multiplexed datasets53 will facilitate the assessment of how multiple nuclear features are remodeled in concert during cell state transitions and to identify a minimal yet informative set of nuclear markers for robust cell state classification. Such analyses could yield insights into the coordination of nuclear architecture in development, disease, and reprogramming contexts. Given its broad applicability, the interpretation of the specific O-SNAP spatial features also depends on the target that is being visualized, and not all spatial features may be immediately biologically interpretable. In the context of chromatin organization, we have provided putative biological definitions for the 144 spatial features in Supplementary Data 1 but these biological definitions should in the future be adapted to the visualized target.

Users should also be familiar with the mathematical basis of the extracted features provided in Supplementary Data 1 for careful interpretation of the feature outputs. For example, features based on distribution skewness may show disproportionately large fold changes in volcano plots, since a skewness value near zero represents a normal distribution, thereby amplifying deviations from this normality. Consulting both the feature definitions in Supplementary Data 1 and associated violin plots is advised for accurate interpretation. Finally, depending on experimental requirements, a user may wish to make additional modifications to the default feature set. For instance, a phenotype may present substantial nucleus wrinkling, in which case the default radial analysis scheme may not appropriately subdivide the nucleus. Although the pipeline provides some consideration for this by directly using the boundary shape of the nucleus to discriminate the nuclear periphery from the nuclear interior, a user may wish to adjust the radial analysis, which assumes nuclei can be reasonably modeled by ellipses, and instead have the regions modeled by a series of polygons that match the shape of the nucleus. The scripts that define feature extraction can be modified should a user wish to modify or add their own features.

O-SNAP incorporates strategies to address limited sample size and multiple hypothesis testing, such as cross-validation, dimensionality reduction, and statistical corrections. However, as for other supervised analysis methodologies, its performance relies on the quality of the input localization data, which is impacted by labeling density, segmentation accuracy, and the number of imaged nuclei for each phenotype. Inadequate quality of the input data may lead to false positives that do not reflect true biological patterns. Therefore, careful optimization of sample preparation and image acquisition is essential, as it underpins the reliability of any quantitative SMLM measurement. Both classification and pseudotime trajectory inference benefit from larger, balanced datasets, in which each phenotype is similarly represented. While SMLM imaging is currently time-intensive and low throughput, future advances in field-of-view expansion88 and automation, combined with O-SNAP analysis, may enable the detection of rare phenotypes. Nevertheless, users should exercise caution in such applications: although O-SNAP offers parameter adjustments to improve sensitivity in unbalanced datasets containing rare phenotypes, achieving balanced sampling is strongly recommended for robust results.

In summary, O-SNAP offers a versatile framework for analyzing both physiological and pathological transitions in nuclear architecture, providing insights into the underlying chromatin remodeling dynamics in response to diverse environmental cues and potentially enabling the rational design of interventions to modulate cell state transitions.

Human fibroblast sample preparation

Human BJ fibroblasts (ATCC, CRL-2522) were grown in media comprised of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich, #11965-084) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (HyClone, #SH30910.03), 1% v/v sodium pyruvate (100 mM; #11360-070, Gibco), 1% v/v antibiotic–antimycotic (Gibco, #15240-062), and 1% v/v GlutaMax (Gibco, #35050-061). Media was replaced every other day. For imaging, cells were seeded in Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ II 8-well chambered coverglass (ThermoFisher, #155409PK). For hyperacetylation STORM experiments, cells were treated with 300 nM of Trichostatin (TSA; Sigma-Aldrich, #T8552) in complete growth medium for 24-h prior to experiments.

Cells were fixed in PBS with 4% v/v paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #15710) warmed to 37 °C for 10 min at 25 °C. Fixed cells were washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton ×-100 (Fisher Scientific, #BP151-100) in PBS. Cells were incubated in blocking buffer containing 10% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fisher Scientific, #BP1600-100) and Triton ×-100 at 0.2% v/v for 1 h at 25 °C. Rabbit anti-histone H2B primary antibodies (Invitrogen, #PA5-115361) targeting were diluted in the blocking buffer at 1:50 for 15–18 h at 4 °C. Cells were rinsed three times in a wash buffer of 2% w/v BSA in 0.025% v/v Triton ×-100 in PBS with gentle agitation, followed by incubation with anti-Rabbit secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, #A31573) diluted in blocking buffer at 1:50 for one hour at 25 °C. Cells were washed again three times in wash buffer at 25 °C.

Control cells received fresh medium only.

Cells were fixed in PBS 1× with 4% v/v paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #15710) warmed to 37 °C for 10 min at 25 °C. Primary human articular chondrocytes were isolated from cartilage tissue debris obtained from two male donors (ages 46 and 55; LifeLink Tissue Bank). The tissues were obtained with informed consent and provided as de-identified donor material under protocols approved by the procurement organization.

STORM imaging and preprocessing

All images were acquired on an ONI Nanoimager (Oxford Nanoimaging microscope) using NimOS Nanoimager Software v1.19.4. The microscope is equipped with a 100×, NA 1.45, oil immersion lens (Olympus) and four lasers: 405, 488, 561, and 640 nm lasers, two channels: the first with 498–551 nm and 576–620 nm band-pass filters (Channel 0) and the second with 666–705–839 nm band-pass filters (Channel 1), and a Hamamatsu Flash 4 V3 scientific complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (sCMOS) camera with a pixel size of 117 nm and field of view of 50 × 80 µm. Nuclei were randomly selected and imaged using Highly Inclined and Laminated Optical sheet illumination (HILO).

Cells were imaged in an imaging buffer of 0.1 M cysteamine (MEA; stock, 77 mg/mL #30070-10 G, Sigma-Aldrich in 360 mM HCl; #A508-P500 Fisher), 5% w/v glucose (#A16828, Alfa Aesar), and 1% glucose oxidase (GLOX) solution. The GLOX solution was made using 14 mg Glucose Oxidase (#G2133, Sigma-Aldrich) in 50 µL of catalase (20 mg/mL; #106810, Roche Applied Science) in 200 µL of 10 mM Tris (#15568025, Invitrogen) at pH 8.0 with 50 mM NaCl (#S217-500 Fisher). The imaging buffer was replaced every 90 minutes. Images were acquired with a 15 ms exposure time for 30,000 frames at 30 °C with constant, high intensity laser power excited at 647 nm. To account for the high density of H2B signal in the nucleus, the sample was first pre-bleached for 1 min with high laser power prior to image acquisition.

Following acquisition of the raw images, STORM localizations were generated and drift-corrected using the NimOS software with a localization precision of 30 nm. Additional filtering thresholds are listed in Supplementary Table 16. Localization data was exported from the NimOS software as CSV files. Using a custom-made MATLAB analysis software57, localizations were manually cropped to the shape of the nucleus. The segmented localization coordinate data of chromatin targets were then used as input for the O-SNAP analysis pipeline.

O-SNAP analysis

Data requirements

O-SNAP was implemented in MATLAB (MathWorks). It accepts an array of 2-D coordinates and was developed to study localizations with variable spatial distribution about the nucleus. The data should be already segmented to the boundary of the nucleus. O-SNAP assumes the raw data is in pixels and prompts users for a conversion factor to nm. Beyond characterization of the nucleus features, the user must label each nucleus localization set with its associated class (e.g., treatment, timepoint, cell type) if they aim to utilize the classification features.

O-SNAP feature extraction

Nucleus morphometrics

From SMLM image data, features describing the 2-D shape of the nucleus can be calculated and these morphology-related features are prefixed in the O-SNAP shorthand system with “M.” The nucleus boundary is calculated on the localizations by using the MATLAB boundary function to identify the outermost localizations that form the outline of the nucleus and using the MATLAB smoothdata function to smooth the boundary with a moving average.

With the nucleus boundary defined, several standard morphometric features can be calculated, such as the nucleus perimeter (M.26). The nucleus area is calculated by using the MATLAB polyarea function to measure the area defined by the boundary. The nucleus radius is calculated by measuring the radius of a circle with the same area as the nucleus (M.25). The major and minor axes of the nucleus are determined by performing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the boundary localizations, which rotates the point cloud so that the axis with the largest variation in localization position is aligned along the x-axis. The major axis (M.21) is the longest length along the first component, the minor axis (M.23) is the length along the second component, and the aspect ratio (M.1) is the ratio of the major to minor axis lengths. Features, such as boundary bending energy (M.3) and boundary elastic energy (M.12), are also derived by quantifying the curvature of the boundary. The boundary can be used to create a convex hull, the smallest convex polygon that contains the original shape that then provides additional features that compare the properties of the original shape to those of the convex hull, such as convexity (M.8). A detailed explanation for all morphometric features is described in Rows 1–28 of Supplementary Data 1.

Voronoi localization segmentation

To quantitatively measure both local and global spatial distribution of localizations, a previously described method of Voronoi tessellation was used to segment the coordinate data56,89. The Voronoi analysis was conducted using the custom MATLAB software (R2024). This procedure assigns a polygonal cell for each localization whose area can then be quantified and enables clustering. Briefly, a Delaunay triangulation was generated using the built-in delauneyTriangulation function, which serves as a basis for the built-in voronoiDiagram function to segment the coordinate space into Voronoi cells, returning a list of vertices and regions corresponding to one polygon for each localization. The built-in MATLAB polyarea function then determined the area for every localization’s Voronoi cell. To quantify chromatin compaction, the Voronoi density was then defined as the inverse of the Voronoi area, where higher values of Voronoi density correspond to localizations in regions of higher chromatin compaction. As a normalization to account for variation in labeling between nuclei, the reduced Voronoi density was calculated, where for each nucleus, the Voronoi area distribution is divided by the mean Voronoi area value, resulting in the reduced Voronoi area distribution. The inverse of the reduced Voronoi areas is the reduced Voronoi density distribution. This information was visually represented by color-coding the localization renderings from blue to red based on the value of the log value of the Voronoi density.

Features related to global localization density are prefixed in the O-SNAP shorthand system with “G”. The mean, standard deviation, and skewness were derived from the distribution of both the Voronoi density and reduced Voronoi density to include as O-SNAP features (G.5, G.7, and G.6, respectively). Only the mean of the reduced Voronoi density is included as a feature (G.10), as the standard deviation and skewness produce identical values to that of the non-normalized Voronoi density distribution. The 40th percentile value of the Voronoi (G.3) and reduced Voronoi density (G.8) was used as an O-SNAP feature to represent sparse chromatin (euchromatin) while the 70th percentile (G.4 and G.9, respectively) were used as a feature to represent dense chromatin (heterochromatin). In addition to these values, the total number of localizations (G.1) and the number of localizations per unit area (G.2) are also included as features. These features are also described in Rows 29–38 of Supplementary Data 1.

Nucleus periphery and interior segmentation

In addition to features that describe changes occurring to chromatin organization globally throughout the nucleus, given the importance of nuclear envelope to chromatin organization and function16, it was of interest to understand how chromatin organization at the periphery of the nucleus differed from the nucleus interior. It was thus necessary to divide these two regions and designate separate O-SNAP features for each region. Features related to the nucleus interior are prefixed in the shorthand system with “I”, and features related to the nucleus periphery are prefixed with “P”.

There are two ways in which the distinction between nucleus periphery and interior are implemented. One approach, based on ref. 33, defines the nucleus periphery as the outer 15% of the nucleus area and the nucleus interior as the inner 85% (Supplementary Fig. 2A). This cutoff is used for features I.1 (localization density per unit area at the nucleus interior), I.2 (packing domain density per unit area at the nucleus interior), P.1 (localization density per unit area at the nucleus periphery), P.2 (packing domain density per unit area at the nucleus interior), P.16 (the ratio of P.1:I.1), and P.17 (the ratio of P.2:I.2). These features are also described in Rows 39–40, 57–58, and 72–73 of Supplementary Data 1. Localizations and high-density packing domains are characterized separately for these two regions (see Clustering analysis: DBSCAN clustering analysis under “Methods” for details on analysis of high-density packing domains).

A second, more stringent cutoff that defines the periphery as the outermost 5% of the nucleus area was used to characterize the thickness and length of putative Lamin Associated Domains (LADs) and is based on the analysis pipeline developed for ref. 90, which is further described in Model LAD analysis under “Methods” (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

Clustering analysis. O-SNAP leverages two distinct clustering approaches to characterize multiple facets of chromatin organization throughout the nucleus: (1) DBSCAN58,91–93 (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is used to detect high-density, nanoscale chromatin packing domains (what we previously termed nucleosome clutches; Supplementary Fig. 2A), and (2) contiguity of the Voronoi localization segmentation is applied in a multiscale fashion to segment chromatin domains ranging from nanoscale to microscale (Supplementary Fig. 2B)55,56,72,89. Both approaches ultimately generate a set of domains that segment the localization data. The domains are analyzed for key morphological properties including size, density (reflecting compaction), and radius of gyration (reflecting spatial dispersion). In addition to the average values, O-SNAP computes distributional statistics such as standard deviation (quantifying heterogeneity) and skewness (capturing asymmetry and deviations from normality).

DBSCAN clustering analysis

DBSCAN clustering segments localizations into groups to characterize dense regions of chromatin, or packing domains, based on a previously developed analysis pipeline90. First, localizations were filtered for those with Voronoi densities above the 70th percentile value from all nuclei within a system. The DBSCAN algorithm segmented the filtered localizations into packing domains (or clutches) representing the highest density regions of chromatin. DBSCAN was implemented with two parameters: (1) ε, the threshold for the neighborhood search radius, here used with a value of 20 nm, and (2) the minimum number of neighbors in a cluster for a given core point, here set to 3. The resulting clusters are then filtered for those with greater than 35 localizations per cluster.

The segmented clusters were further separated into two categories: peripheral and interior clusters, where the peripheral clusters are within 5% of the nucleus radius from the nucleus boundary and the remaining clusters are considered as interior packing domains. The distribution and quantity of these domains are described with feature I.3 (number of localizations attributed to packing domains at the nucleus interior), I.4 (number of packing domains at the nucleus interior), P.3 (number of localizations attributed to packing domains at the nucleus periphery), and P.4 (number of packing domains at the nucleus periphery), which can be found in Rows 41–42 and 59–60 of Supplementary Data 1.

The properties of the domains at the periphery and interior were then separately characterized, where domains were measured for their size (i.e., equivalent radius), compactness (i.e., localization density per unit area), dispersion (i.e., radius of gyration), and number of localizations per domain. Additionally, for packing domains at the nucleus interior, the spacing between neighboring packing domains were calculated. For each property, the domains generated a distribution of values, which were log-transformed to obtain a distribution more closely resembling a normal distribution. The mean value, standard deviation, and skewness across these distributions for each were used as O-SNAP features for each nucleus. Features I.5-I.18 and P.5-P.15 represent these values, which can be found in Rows 43–56 and 61–71 of Supplementary Data 1.

Voronoi clustering analysis

The Voronoi clustering analysis achieves characterization of clusters at multiple length scales. The algorithm takes a parameter that defines the upper threshold for localizations to cluster based on their Voronoi area. The algorithm then clusters these filtered localizations by forming groups of contiguous Voronoi cells. Clusters with fewer than a minimum number of localizations (default = 5) are discarded. For this paper, we selected four area thresholds ranging from 17.78 to 100.0 nm2 evenly across a log scale to achieve broad coverage of cluster sets at different length scales (17.78, 32.62, 56.23, and 100.0 nm2), where smaller area thresholds lead to smaller, more dispersed (i.e., are more frequent and, generally, more evenly spaced out throughout the nucleus) clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Increasing the area threshold leads to segmentation of more contiguous chromatin domains at larger length scales. Features derived from these clusters are prefixed in the shorthand system with “V#”, where “#” ranges from 1 to 4 that correspond to the Voronoi clustering thresholds. Clusters were measured for size (i.e., equivalent radius), compactness (i.e., localization density per unit area), and dispersion (i.e., radius of gyration). The mean value, standard deviation, and skewness across these distributions for each were used as O-SNAP features for each nucleus. The number of clusters per unit area was also measured as a feature. These features are listed in Rows 105–144 of Supplementary Data 1.

Model LAD analysis

The packing domains at the nuclear periphery (within 5% of nuclear boundary) produced by DBSCAN were used to create models of the unique chromatin pattern at the nucleus periphery as previously described in ref. 90. Briefly, about fifty quadrilaterals, or “model LAD domains”, were created such that one side ran tangent to the nucleus boundary, creating a rough perimeter of the nucleus. The height of each quadrilateral perpendicular to this tangent was dependent on the size of the packing domain at the periphery. This set of quadrilaterals could then be assessed for additional features describing the organization of chromatin at the nucleus periphery. The mean, standard deviation, and skewness of the distribution of the quadrilateral side lengths both tangent and perpendicular to the nucleus boundary, as well as the value of the total model LAD area divided by the total tangent side lengths, were used as O-SNAP features. These features have the prefix “L” in the shorthand system and appear in Rows 74–80 of Supplementary Data 1.

Radial density analysis. To evaluate radial organization, O-SNAP divides the nucleus into ten concentric zones from the interior to the periphery and quantifies chromatin domain abundance and feature gradients across these zones (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The zones are defined by evenly partitioning the major and minor axes of the nucleus into ten segments, which are then used to generate elliptical boundaries for each radial bin (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Because of the irregular shape of nuclei, localizations may lie beyond the outermost ellipse. To ensure these edge-localizations are not excluded, the outermost zone is defined as including all localizations or domains that lie beyond the ninth ellipse (formed from 90% of the length of the major and minor axis, Supplementary Fig. 2C). While this approach results in ring areas varying on the nucleus area, this guarantees the same number of rings, and thus features, across all nuclei.

For each ring, features are obtained by measuring either the localization density (features with prefix “R” in the shorthand system, features R.1–R.10) or the number of packing domains per unit area (features with prefix “RD” in the shorthand system, features RD.1–RD.10; Supplementary Fig. 2C). Following this, the average change in these densities over the innermost eight rings was calculated, normalized over the length of the major or minor axis. For localization density, this corresponds to features R.11 (the change in localization density radially along the major axis) and R.12 (the change in localization density radially along the minor axis). Similarly, for the number of packing domains per unit area, this corresponds to features RD.11 (the change in packing domain density radially along the major axis) and RD.12 (the change in packing domain density radially along the minor axis). These features appear in Rows 81–104 in Supplementary Data 1.

Volcano analysis

The feature distributions were compared between each pair of conditions available for a given SMLM data set. The difference in mean values was compared between each pair, without outlier removal by default. The two-sample t-test was used to determine the difference between the distributions. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, p-values were then adjusted, where the default is set to the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure94,95. Here, features with an absolute fold change greater than 2 and an adjusted p-value satisfying α < 0.05 were considered significant, although these thresholds are user-configurable and can be modified based on specific experimental expectations.

Feature set enrichment analysis

FSEA adapts the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) method introduced in ref. 96. Here, FSEA aims to achieve a similar goal with O-SNAP features rather than gene expression values. Features are ranked with an established ranking metric. For a given feature set, S, the Enrichment Score (ES) quantifies the degree to which a given feature set differs from a uniform, null distribution. A test statistic describes the level of significance of the feature set enrichment, which is adjusted to account for multiple hypothesis testing and differences in the number of features between sets.

Input

FSEA operates on a set of samples with N features (formerly N genes in GSEA). In O-SNAP, each sample represents a nucleus that is one of two possible phenotypes of interest, A or B. All N features are ranked according to their correlation with the phenotype label to form L=f1,…fN based on a metric, r(fj)=rj. In O-SNAP, there are 8 possible ranking metrics (Supplementary Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 17). Here, the default metric is “Signal-to-noise” (“S2N”), which ranks features based on the difference between the means of each class and divides this by the sum of their standard deviations. FSEA also requires a group of M feature sets, S, that represent the categorization of features into related trends, where each feature should appear exactly once across all the sets (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

Calculation of enrichment score (ES)

For each feature set S containing NH features, the calculation of its enrichment score, ES(S), begins with the normalization of the full dataset into z-scores (Supplementary Fig. 3A). ES a cumulative score calculated by traversing the ranking L=f1,…fN. At each position along L, the fraction of features in S(“hits”) increases the score by a value of Phit (see Eq. (1)), which is weighted by the correlation metric to phenotype. The fraction not contained in the set of interest, in other words, features contained in S(“misses”) decrease the score by Pmiss (Eq. (2); Supplementary Fig. 3B). The exponent p controls the weight of the step.

ES is the maximum deviation of Phit−Pmiss from zero over all values of i (Supplementary Fig. 3C). The value of ES(S) will be higher for sets whose features concentrate either at the top or bottom of L.

O-SNAP incorporates a correction absent from traditional GSEA that modifies the position of features in L whose value increase opposes that of the trend described in the feature set. Unlike in sequencing data, where higher expression always indicates positive enrichment, an increase in an O-SNAP feature value may reflect a change in the opposite direction of its associated feature set. For example, the feature “M.1” (nucleus aspect ratio, the ratio between the major axis to the minor axis) has a value near 1 when the nucleus shape approximates a circle and increases in value the more elongated the nucleus becomes. M.1 is grouped under the “rounder nucleus” set, but an increase in aspect ratio corresponds to a more elongated, less round nucleus. To avoid redundancy in feature sets (e.g., including both “rounder nucleus” and “less round nucleus” as separate sets), a correction is applied to the ranking metric of the features whose increase opposes the direction of their respective feature set. For instance, with the “S2N” metric, the additive inverse is used in place of the original value when ranking the features whose increase opposes the described feature set. Thus, the relationship between ES(S) and the distribution of features along L still holds. The correction for each ranking metric is detailed in Supplementary Table 17.

For p=0, ES(S) reduces to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, and when p=1 (the default value used in O-SNAP), the features in S are weighted by their correlation to phenotype A or B, normalized by the total sum of correlations over all genes in S. The latter option, according to ref. 96, results in more gene sets with less-than-perfect coherence to score well.

Estimating significance

To test for statistical significance, a permutation method is used to compare ES, obtained from the original feature data, against ESNULL, a set of scores derived from a null distribution of randomly assigned phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 3D). The feature data undergoes 1000 permutations of the phenotype labels, which preserves the feature correlation structure within the data. For each permutation, π, ranking L is created using the shuffled data with a corresponding result for ES(S,π). The 1000 iterations of permutation and recalculation of ESNULL forms the distribution of ESNULL scores (Supplementary Fig. 3D). A nominal p-value for S is determined by calculating the proportion of ESNULL scores less than the calculated ES of the original data set, if ES(S) is positive, and the proportion greater than ES, if ES(S) is negative.

Multiple hypothesis testing: adjusting for variation in gene set size

To compare ES between multiple feature sets, the scores must be further normalized, and the significance testing should be corrected for multiple hypothesis testing. For each S and 1000 fixed permutations π of the phenotype labels, a new ranking L and associated ES(S,π) are calculated. To adjust for differences in feature set size (i.e., the number of features within each set), the positive and negative values of ES(S) and ES(S,π) are rescaled separately, where the positive and negative scores are divided by the respective mean of ES(S,π) to obtain the normalized scores, NES(S) and NES(S,π) (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Further detail regarding the reasoning behind this approach is discussed in more detail in ref. 96.

After normalized scores are obtained, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) is computed. The ratio of false positives to the total number of sets is controlled by fixing the level of significance separately for the set of NES(S) and NES(S,π) with either positive or negative values. To do this, a null distribution is formed by considering all values of NES(S,π) for all S and π. Then, for a given S, in the case where NES(S)=NES*≥0 (i.e., the positive NES case), the FDR is calculated as the ratio of the percentage of all (S,π) with NES (S, π) ≥ NES* divided by the percentage of observed S with NES(S)≥0 whose NES(S)≥NES*. This procedure was performed similarly for NES(S)=NES*≤0, where the FDR is instead the ratio of the percentage of all (S,π) with NES(S,π)≤NES* divided by the percentage of observed S with NES(S)≤0 whose NES(S)≤NES*. The final plot displays feature sets with largest NES values, where the color of the markers indicates the FDR q-values and the size of the marker is the number of features contained in the set (Supplementary Fig. 3E).

Pseudotimeline analysis

Pseudotimeline analysis was performed by utilizing the R package collection, dynverse, which enables the exploration and implementation of trajectory inference (TI) methods81. A basic understanding of R is required to modify and run scripts for dynverse, which provides its own tutorial; an example script is provided as Pseudotime_Example_Heterokrayon.R. Analysis was run in RStudio v2024.12.1.

O-SNAP feature sets derived from SMLM data originally produced in ref. 31 were adapted as input for trajectory inference analysis. The feature data was exported from MATLAB as a CSV and imported into R. Images from the hFb nuclei at 0-, 6-, 24-, and 48-h post-fusion were considered for the pseudotime analysis, and the control mESC nucleus was excluded from the pseudotime analysis. The feature data was then z-score normalized and used as the expression data. Because the dynverse wrapper requires sequencing count data in the input dataset, this array was assigned to a constant dummy value of 0.5. This value does not influence the trajectory outcome and serves only to meet the software’s input format expectations.

A limited amount of prior information was provided to guide the inference and to improve the stability and simplicity of the inferred trajectory. Prior information consisted of the sample phenotype labels, expected start and end phenotypes, and expected transitions between phenotypes (e.g., 0- to 6-h, 6-h to 24-h, and 24-h to 48-h). Given our expectation that the reprogramming behavior of the somatic heterokaryon nucleus is continuous and non-branching transition between cell states, the trajectory was constrained to a linear topology with defined start and end phenotypes. In scenarios where the transition might be more complex, the dynverse package offers alternative topologies and methods that can accommodate branching systems, provided the dataset is sufficiently large for each cell state.

A shortlist of trajectory methods was obtained from dynverse based on dataset-dependent parameters, such as cell count and the expected trajectory topology (see Supplementary Table 18). Each of these candidate methods analyzed the processed feature data to create an initial trajectory, where samples are placed along a topology and assigned a value that quantifies the progression between cell transitions. If the samples form distinct subpopulations with time, the trajectory’s topology may feature branches that represent cell state decisions. To facilitate interpretation, the pseudotime calculated from the trajectory analysis was used to project the samples onto a linear trajectory and samples on extraneous branches deviating from the main progression were omitted in subsequent analysis. The pseudotimes for every cell state were binned to evaluate to degree each trajectory method could separate the samples across pseudotime in a manner that reflected their ground truth labels.

Once the best performing method was determined, in this case Slingshot97, the most important features informing the trajectory were identified from the H3K27me3 data using functions provided in dynverse. With a heatmap, the values of the top three important features were plotted against pseudotime and compared between the H3K27me3 signal and global chromatin.

Feature selection and principal component analysis

In this work, O-SNAP data is used to train and test classification models designed to distinguish different cell states of interest to (1) identify which features and (2) evaluate the extent these features are sufficient to distinguish between phenotypes.

To rigorously evaluate the classification performance from a limited sample size, the data is split into fivefolds where a random 80% of the sample are used for model training and the remaining 20% is reserved to test for model performance. The data is split such that each fold contains a different subset of the training data with at least one member of each class. Within each fold, the training data is z-score normalized (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Feature selection is then performed, using the Minimum Redundancy—Maximum Relevance (MRMR) algorithm98, which was introduced to identify features of interest from gene expression data. This approach aims to identify a subset of the original 144 features that best discriminates between cell states while also minimizing the redundancy of the set that represents information shared between the selected features (e.g., features that have a strong correlation). Details on the MRMR procedure are fully described both in the original publication98 as well as the MATLAB documentation. Once features are ranked on the training set within each fold, a decision must be made on the number of features to include in the final set. This cutoff was automatically determined by examining the top 12 MRMR-ranked features and locating either the “knee” point in the MRMR scores of the ranked features or the feature at which there was the largest drop in MRMR scores occurs. Whichever of these options results in the smaller set of features is taken as the final selected set for that fold (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Although the feature selection occurs separately for each fold, it is difficult to interpret five separate feature sets. To address this, O-SNAP aggregates the feature sets together by summing the MRMR scores together from each fold to obtain an aggregated ranking. The same approach for finding a cutoff number of features is used on the aggregate feature selection (Supplementary Fig. 5C). The final set of feature values are then normalized and undergo principal component analysis (PCA). The PCA serves both as dimensionality reduction step, reducing correlation within training values for classification, and as a visualization tool of the underlying structure in the data independent of class labels to assess the data. By default, the number of components retained from the PCA-transformation is the smallest set of PCA components such that at least 75% of the variance in the feature set is explained. The PCA-transformed training data is then used to train a suite of models for classification (Supplementary Fig. 5D). In this way, the models are generally formed independently from each other except for the aggregated feature selection, for the sake of interpretability.

Classification

A suite of different, standard classification models built into MATLAB were trained, including architectures such as decision trees, logistic regression classifiers, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and k-nearest neighbor (KNN) classifiers on the normalized, PCA-transformed, selected O-SNAP features as described under Feature selection and principal component analysis under “Methods”. Five replicates of the same model are trained. To evaluate the performance of the models, the 20% reserved data is independently normalized, followed by filtering for the selected features and undergoes a PCA-transformation applied to the respective training data. The trained models then predict on the test data, and the ground truth labels are compared to the predicted labels.

For each individual trained model, the overall accuracy is simply obtained from the proportion of samples correctly classified compared to the total test sample count. For each model architecture and within each fold, the number of correctly classified and misclassified samples for the five model replicates are averaged, also providing a standard deviation on the model training within the fold. These mean counts are then averaged again across the five folds to obtain statistics on the overall accuracy of the model architecture for the full dataset. The averaged counts are row-normalized to report the proportion of correct or incorrect classifications. The overall accuracies across different models are compared, and the model with the highest mean accuracy was considered the best model for a given O-SNAP classification and was assessed further to better understand its performance.

Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curves were one approach to evaluate the classification performance. ROC curves are obtained by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate at different thresholds. These curves depict how two rates change depending on a threshold value that alters the sensitivity of the classifier to a specific phenotype at the cost of correctly identifying the others. The results shown represent the distribution of the ROC curves across the fivefolds for a given model architecture.

Confusion matrices offered another means to understand classifier performance. They display the number or proportion of correct identifications along the diagonal and misclassifications in the off-diagonal positions of the matrix. To derive these summary plots, confusion matrices are first generated for each of the fivefolds and the raw sample counts are used to populate the matrix, which are averaged within each fold over the five replicates of the same model type. The confusion matrices are then row-normalized so that the confusion matrix cells represent the proportion of true labels that were correctly or incorrectly classified. To obtain statistics of the classification results across the five folds, the weighted average and standard deviation were calculated for each normalized row, where the weights are the total number of test samples in the cell state the given row represents. This approach accounts for differences in the number of test sample counts of a given phenotype that may have been present for separate samples, as only the total test sample size was held constant but not the proportion of test samples from each class. The final values shown are these weighted means and standard deviations.

O-SNAP also performs a similar classification pipeline, but in place of the aggregated feature selection, maintains the individual fold-wise feature selections so that the full process of classification is fully independent between folds. The results of these are not shown but are available in the analysis. Another classification pipeline is also available, where normalization and feature selection occur on the entire data set and is followed by the fivefold cross-validation approach. However, this approach is prone to data leakage, where the trained classification models are indirectly informed about the test data set since the feature selection is performed on the entire data set. Hence, to achieve a robust approach while maintaining interpretability, O-SNAP implements the aggregated feature selection approach instead.

To determine whether O-SNAP was identifying true differences, as opposed to random variance, in chromatin organization between phenotypes, the classification performances for the TSA and reprogramming O-SNAP results were compared to that of models trained on the same dataset with shuffled phenotype labels (Supplementary Fig. 6D and Fig. 4C). In the shuffled dataset, only the class labels are permuted such that correlations between features in a sample are still preserved. The shuffling only occurs once prior to all subsequent O-SNAP analysis. Thus, normalization, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and classification are applied to the shuffled data in the same fashion as the ground truth data.

Chromatin accessibility profiling (snMultiome-ATAC-Seq)

To complement nanoscale chromatin organization assessed by STORM imaging, aggregated chromatin accessibility profiles were generated and analyzed at the pseudo-bulk level to assess global accessibility trends associated with human chondrocyte dedifferentiation during in vitro expansion. Human chondrocytes at passage 0 (P0) and passage 6 (P6) were processed using the 10× Genomics Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression platform following the manufacturer’s nuclei isolation protocol. Approximately 10,000 nuclei per sample were loaded for library preparation and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (~25,000 reads per nucleus). Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using Cell Ranger ARC, and high-quality nuclei were filtered and analyzed in Seurat v5/Signac v1.13. The snMultiome-ATAC-Seq data can be found in Reference99. Starting from this data, pseudo-bulk ATAC-Seq profiles were generated by aggregating reads from all chondrocyte nuclei within each passage group (P0 or P6) identified in the single-nuclei ATAC dataset. Differentially accessible regions (DARs) were identified between passages, and chromatin accessibility signal was summarized across all DARs for each passage. For visualization, ATAC-Seq signal was centered on the midpoint of each DAR and aggregated across a ± 5 kb window. Median accessibility signal across all regions was calculated for each condition and plotted to compare global chromatin accessibility profiles across passages.

Gene expression profiling (RNA-Seq)

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed to assess transcriptional changes associated with chondrocyte dedifferentiation during in vitro expansion. Primary human articular chondrocytes derived from multiple donors were cultured and expanded in vitro to passages 0, 3, and 6 (P0, P3, and P6) prior to RNA isolation. Total RNA was extracted and purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA integrity and concentration were assessed prior to library preparation and sequencing. RNA-Seq libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform using paired-end reads. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in R using the DESeq2 package100. Genes were considered differentially expressed based on a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold-change cutoff of 1. Summary-level analyses, including counts of up and downregulated genes across passage comparisons, were used to characterize global transcriptional trends associated with passage.
