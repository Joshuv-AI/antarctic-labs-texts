Simultaneous activation and late-stage inhibition of autophagy induces autophagy-dependent cell death in leukemia
Authors: Yie Wei Chua, Sek Chuen Chow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResistance to apoptosis is a major challenge in cancer therapy, especially in relapsed or refractory leukemia. Autophagy, a conserved lysosomal degradation pathway, plays a complex dual role: it can promote tumor survival under stress, yet its excessive activation can trigger autophagy-dependent cell death (ACD). This paradox has inspired a novel therapeutic strategy that simultaneously activates and inhibits specific stages of autophagy to push cells toward death. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this approach remain poorly understood, particularly in non-solid tumors like leukemia.MethodsThis study investigated the effects of simultaneous autophagy activation and stage-specific inhibition on cell death in human Jurkat E6.1 leukemic T cells. Cells were treated with rapamycin (RAPA) to induce autophagy, combined with either chloroquine (CQ) to block late-stage degradation or 3-methyladenine (3-MA) to inhibit early-stage autophagosome formation. Cell viability was measured by MTS assay, while autophagy flux was evaluated using a combination of MDC staining, mCherry-eGFP-LC3 fluorescence imaging and Western blot analysis for LC3. To delineate the mode of cell death, we accessed apoptosis via caspase-3 cleavage and employed pharmacological inhibitors of apoptosis (z-VAD-FMK) and necrosis (Necrostatin-1).ResultsSimultaneous autophagy induction with RAPA and late-stage inhibition with CQ (RAPA + CQ) triggered massive autophagosome accumulation and significantly enhanced cell death. In contrast, early-stage inhibition with 3-MA (RAPA + 3-MA) prevented autophagic vesicle formation and abrogated the cytotoxic effect. Importantly, this cell death occurred independently of both apoptosis and necroptosis, suggesting a direct result of excessive and unprocessed autophagosomes, consistent with ACD.ConclusionIn conclusion, our findings demonstrate that trapping cells in a state of high autophagic flux through simultaneous induction and late stage inhibition, is a potent strategy to trigger non-apoptotic cell death in leukemia. This approach represents a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome apoptosis-resistant leukemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16102-2.

Background

Apoptosis has been the primary cell death mechanism in cancer treatments, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy [1]. However, many tumors frequently develop apoptotic resistance, a key feature of relapsed or refractory cancers, severely undermines treatment efficacy [2]. In leukemia, for example, treatment options for refractory cases are often limited, leaving allogeneic stem cell transplantation as the only viable option, highlighting the critical need for alternative strategies [3, 4]. This has led to a growing focus on non-apoptotic cell death pathways such as pyroptosis, necroptosis and ferroptosis [5, 6]. Autophagy is especially compelling due to its dual role in cancer biology and its potential to be therapeutically harnessed.

Autophagy is a conserved lysosomal degradation pathway that maintains cellular homeostasis by recycling damaged or toxic cytoplasmic components. The process begins with the initiation and cargo sequestration of autophagosomes, and complete with their fusion to lysosomes, forming degradative autolysosomes [7]. In normal cells, autophagy is tightly regulated by nutrient-sensing pathways such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA), remaining low under nutrient-rich conditions but upregulated during stress. Pharmacological modulators target these pathways: mTOR inhibitors like rapamycin (RAPA) activate autophagy, whereas early-stage inhibitors such as 3-methyladenine (3-MA) block autophagosome initiation by inhibiting class III phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) [8]. Conversely, late-stage inhibitors including chloroquine (CQ) and bafilomycin A1 disrupt lysosomal degradation, preventing autophagosome clearance [9, 10].

In cancer, autophagy exhibits a context-dependent dual role. It initially acts as a tumor suppressor during tumorigenesis by maintaining genomic stability, removing damaged mitochondria to prevent reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and clearing toxic protein aggregates [11, 12]. However, as tumors progress, an “autophagy switch” occurs, repurposing the pathway to promote cancer survival under stressors like nutrient deprivation and oxidative stress. [13]. Cancer cells also exploit autophagy to evade chemotherapy, with elevated autophagic flux reported in chemoresistant leukemia and breast cancer models [14, 15]. Consequently, autophagy inhibition is a promising therapeutic strategy to resensitize resistant cancers, as demonstrated in cases of arabinoside-resistant leukemia and epirubicin-resistant breast cancer [14–18].

Paradoxically, autophagy can also induce cancer cell death through autophagy-dependent cell death (ACD), a form of cell death characterized by excessive autophagic flux and extensive cytoplasmic vacuolization that is distinct from apoptosis [19]. Several anticancer agents, including tamoxifen and temozolomide, can trigger this ACD [20–26]. This dual role of autophagy, both protective and lethal, has inspired a therapeutic strategy that combines autophagy inducers with inhibitors [25, 27–35]. Although the underlying mechanism is not fully understood, one proposed explanation is that certain anticancer agents exert autophagy-independent cytotoxicity while simultaneously inducing a protective autophagy response. In this scenario, autophagy functions as a survival mechanism that partially offsets drug-induced cytotoxicity; therefore, inhibiting autophagy removes this protective response and reveals the drug’s full cytotoxic potency [36]. However, this explanation is only partially true, as several studies have demonstrated that the impact of autophagy inhibition is highly dependent on timing. For instance, late-stage inhibition enhances imatinib-induced cell death, whereas early-stage inhibition reduces its cytotoxicity, suggesting that the induction of autophagy itself can be part of the drug’s efficacy [37]. These findings indicate that the strategic combination of autophagy activation and inhibition can trigger distinct cytotoxic mechanisms and may represent a key strategy to induce ACD.

The molecular mechanisms of combined autophagy activation and inhibition, however, remain poorly understood, particularly in leukemia. To address this, we investigated the effects of the autophagy activator RAPA combined with late-stage CQ or early-stage 3-MA inhibitor in human Jurkat E6.1 leukemic T cells. Our results demonstrate that the RAPA/CQ combination induced autophagosome accumulation and significantly increased cell death via ACD. In contrast, RAPA/3-MA blocked autophagosome formation and did not enhance cytotoxicity. This RAPA/CQ-induced cell death occurred independently of apoptosis and necroptosis, indicating that excessive autophagosome accumulation is sufficient to drive ACD. Together, these findings not only clarify the role of autophagy modulation in leukemia, but also validate the paradoxical strategy of concurrent autophagy induction and late-stage inhibition as a potent treatment, particularly for apoptosis-resistant cancers.

Methods

Reagents

Rapamycin (RAPA), benzyloxycarbonyl-valine-alanine-aspartic acid-(O-methyl)-fluoromethylketone (z-VAD-FMK) and necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) were purchased from MedChem Express (USA). Chloroquine (CQ), monodansylcadaverine (MDC) fluorescent dye, and 3-methyladenine (3-MA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), while staurosporine (STS) was sourced from i-DNA Biotechnology (USA). For immunoblotting, rabbit monoclonal antibodies against caspase-3 and microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 isotype B (LC3b) were acquired from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA), and mouse monoclonal antibodies against beta-actin were purchased from Sigma (USA). Secondary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse IgG and anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP), were obtained from Dako (Japan).

Jurkat E6.1 cell culture and treatments

The T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) cells, Jurkat E6.1 cells (ATCC, Rockville, MD) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin; Sigma, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere. For experiments, cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL and treated with specified drug concentrations for designated time periods. In prolonged treatments (> 24 h), culture media was replenished with fresh medium at double the original volume to maintain optimal nutrient levels and pH stability throughout the experimental duration.

MTS cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay as described previously [38]. Following 24 or 48 h of treatment, freshly prepared MTS/PMS solution (5:1 v/v) was added (20 µL per well) to a final MTS concentration of 0.33 mg/mL. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, absorbance was recorded at 490 nm using a Tecan Infinite 200 Pro microplate reader.

MDC fluorescence imaging and quantification

Autophagy was evaluated using monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining for both qualitative and quantitative assessment. Treated Jurkat cells were collected, washed with PBS, and incubated with 100 µM MDC for 30 min at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. After washing, cells were processed for either microscopy or fluorometric analysis. For imaging, cells were suspended in PBS/glycerol (1:1 v/v), mounted on slides, and examined using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope equipped with a UV filter (1000 × oil immersion). Images were acquired with a Nikon DS-U2 camera and analyzed using NIS-Elements software (v4.20). For quantification, cell suspensions in PBS were transferred to 96-well plates, and fluorescence was measured (excitation 335 nm/emission 512 nm) using a Tecan Infinite 200 Pro microplate reader.

Generation of mCherry-eGFP-LC3b reporter cell line

The mCherry-eGFP-LC3b lentivirus was generated as previously described [39]. Jurkat cells (1 × 105) were transduced via spinoculation (1,200 × g, 90 min, 25 °C) with concentrated virus in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL), followed by 48 h incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Stable transduced cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL) for 7 days, with daily media changes. For experiments, transduced cells were treated, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS/glycerol (1:1 v/v) for fluorescence microscopy. Imaging was performed using an Olympus BX51 microscope equipped with GFP and mCherry filters (1000 × oil immersion) for eGFP and mCherry visualisation, respectively. For visual representation in figures, the acquired single-channel images were merged to demonstrate the colocalization of autophagosomes.

To quantify autophagic flux, the colocalization of mCherry and eGFP signals was analyzed using ImageJ, adapting previously described methods [40, 41]. Acquired images were split into distinct green and red 8-bit channels. To exclude background noise, thresholds were established relative to negative controls and consistently applied across all treatment groups (set to 60 for the mCherry channel and 40 for the eGFP channel). Colocalization analysis was then performed using the Just Another Colocalization Plugin (JACoP) to calculate Manders’ colocalization coefficients (M) [42]. Specifically, the fraction of the mCherry signal overlapping with eGFP (M1) was measured. Results are presented as the % mCherry colocalized with eGFP to determine the proportion of autophagosomes that had not yet undergone lysosomal acidification. Data represent the mean ± SEM from three independent experiments (n = 3), with at least 15 cells counted in total for each treatment group.

Western blot analysis

Treated Jurkat cells (2 × 10⁶) were lysed in RIPA buffer (100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.6], 1 mM EDTA [pH 7.4], 1% Triton-X) containing PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad), and 30 µg of protein per sample was resolved by 15% SDS-PAGE after mixing with Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad). Separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and immunoblotted with primary antibodies against caspase-3, LC3 and β-actin, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using ECL chemiluminescence (Amersham) and imaged with a G:BOX system. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis of the data

Experimental data were analysed using GraphPad PRISM version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey’s test, as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data in this paper represent the mean ± SEM from three independent experiments (n = 3).

Results

Dose-dependent effect of autophagy modulators on cell viability and autophagic flux in Jurkat cells

To evaluate the cytotoxicity and autophagy-modulating effects of RAPA, 3-MA and CQ, Jurkat cells were treated with varying concentrations of these compounds for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the MTS assay, and autophagic vesicle formation was assessed via MDC staining. As shown in Fig. 1A, all RAPA treatments did not significantly affect Jurkat cell viability but increased MDC fluorescence by approximately 1.3-fold relative to the untreated control, consistent with autophagy activation. Similarly, CQ (50 µM and 100 µM, Fig. 1B) had no significant impact on cell viability but caused substantial autophagic vesicle accumulation, with MDC fluorescence rising to 2.5- and 2.7-fold as compared to control, respectively. This is consistent with its role as a late-stage autophagy inhibitor blocking autophagosome degradation. In contrast, 3-MA (Fig. 1C) exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity, causing approximately 40% cell death at 10 mM. Furthermore, treatment with 3-MA at concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 10 mM progressively reduced the MDC fold-change to 0.8 or lower relative to the control, confirming the inhibition of early-stage autophagosome formation. Effect of combined autophagy activation and inhibition at different stages on cell viability in Jurkat cells

Cells were treated with RAPA, CQ or both, and viability was assessed after 24 and 48 h. As shown in Fig. 2A-B, no significant changes in cell viability were observed at 24 h. By 48 h, however, cotreatment with RAPA and CQ induced significantly higher cytotoxicity (55%) compared to either compound alone (RAPA: 21%; CQ: 20%). Given the enhanced cell death observed with simultaneous autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition (RAPA + CQ), we next examined whether combining autophagy activation (RAPA) with early-stage inhibition (3-MA) would further reduce cell viability. Jurkat cells were treated with RAPA, 3-MA or both, and cell viability was assessed at 24 and 48 h. As shown in Fig. 2C-D, cotreatment with RAPA and 3-MA significantly decreased cell viability at both time points. However, this reduction was not significantly different from that induced by 3-MA alone, suggesting that the cytotoxic effect was primarily driven by 3-MA, with no additive contribution from RAPA.

Effect of combined autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition on the autophagosomes formation in Jurkat cells

To assess the effects of combined autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition, we treated Jurkat cells with RAPA, CQ or both and monitored autophagic vesicle formation using MDC staining and fluorescence microscopy at 24 and 48 h. At the 24 h (Fig. 3A), both RAPA and CQ alone induced an increase in MDC-stained autophagic vesicles (green puncta), but their combination resulted in a more pronounced accumulation. Further quantification revealed a 3.1-fold increase in MDC fluorescence in co-treated cells compared to untreated control, significantly exceeding the increases seen with RAPA (1.4-fold) or CQ (2.3-fold) alone (Fig. 3C). This trend persisted at 48 h time point (Fig. 3B), indicating sustained autophagic flux disruption. To further corroborate these findings, we determined the LC3b-II/LC3b-I ratio, a key autophagy marker reflecting autophagosome formation. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4A-C) confirmed that RAPA + CQ cotreatment significantly elevated the LC3b-II/LC3b-I ratio compared to single treatments at both time points, aligning with the MDC data. Together, these results indicate that combined autophagy induction and late-stage inhibition synergistically enhance autophagic vesicles accumulation. To determine whether the accumulated vesicles were autophagosomes or autolysosomes, we transduced Jurkat cells with a mCherry-eGFP-LC3b reporter. In this system, autophagosomes appear as yellow puncta (mCherry +/eGFP +), while autolysosomes appear as red-only puncta (mCherry +/eGFP-) due to the quenching of eGFP in acidic lysosomes [43]. As expected, RAPA-treated cells showed predominantly red puncta (autolysosomes), whereas cells treated with CQ alone or RAPA + CQ cotreatment exhibited yellow puncta (autophagosomes) (Fig. 5A-B). These observations were further quantified by using ImageJ and reported as % mCherry colocalized with eGFP. The % colocalization was significantly higher in cells treated with CQ and RAPA + CQ cotreatment, confirming that CQ successfully inhibited autophagosome-lysosome fusion (Fig. 5C-D). These observations were consistent at 24 and 48 h. Altogether, these results support the conclusion that RAPA and CQ synergistically drive autophagosome accumulation.

Effect of combined autophagy activation and early-stage inhibition on the formation of autophagosomes in Jurkat cells

Following our investigation of combined autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition, we next examined the effects of pairing autophagy activation (RAPA) with early-stage inhibition (3-MA) in Jurkat cells. MDC staining revealed that while RAPA significantly increased formation of autophagic vesicle (green puncta) at 24 h, whereas cotreatment with 3-MA substantially attenuated this effect to levels statistically indistinguishable from the untreated control (Fig. 6A, C). This inhibitory effect was sustained through 48 h (Fig. 6B), confirming that 3-MA effectively counteracts RAPA-induced autophagosome formation in Jurkat cells.

Mechanisms of cell death following combined autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition in Jurkat cells

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced cell death following combined autophagy activation (RAPA) and late-stage inhibition (CQ) in Jurkat cells, we systematically investigated two major programmed cell death pathways. First, we examined apoptosis, which is mediated by caspase activation and proteolytic cleavage events [38]. Western blot analysis revealed cleaved caspase-3 only in staurosporine (STS)-treated cells (positive controls) but not in RAPA/CQ-treated cells (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, the pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-FMK failed to attenuate RAPA/CQ-induced cell death (Fig. 8B), effectively excluding apoptosis as the underlying mechanism. We next evaluated necroptosis, a RIP1-RIP3-dependent pathway inhibitable by necrostatin-1 (Nec-1) [44]. However, Nec-1 treatment did not rescue cell viability in RAPA/CQ-co-treated cultures (Fig. 8C), ruling out necroptosis involvement. Discussion

This study demonstrates that simultaneous activation and late-stage inhibition of autophagy significantly enhances cell death in Jurkat leukemic cells, accompanied by robust autophagosome accumulation. In contrast, early-stage inhibition with 3-MA suppressed autophagy initiation and abrogated this effect. These findings highlight that autophagy can act not only as a survival pathway but also as a driver of cell death, depending on the stage at which it is interrupted. The results align with the concept of ACD, where excessive autophagosome build-up becomes cytotoxic, a mechanism that is distinct from and independent of classical apoptosis or necroptosis as shown in this study.

Simultaneous activation and late-stage inhibition of autophagy represents a novel strategy to enhance cell death in leukemia. In this study, we provide the first evidence that such dual modulation can drive cytotoxicity in a T cell leukemia model. This finding contrasts with earlier work in chronic lymphocytic leukemia-derived B cells, where RAPA + CQ showed no significant cytotoxic effect [32]. The discrepancy likely reflects cell-type specificity, as Jurkat E6.1 T cells may respond differently to autophagy modulation. Similar findings have been reported in solid tumors, such as breast cancer, glioblastoma, and lung cancer, where combined autophagy modulation using RAPA + CQ or other modulators such as everolimus, temsirolimus, torin-1 or bafilomycin A1 enhanced cytotoxicity [27, 29, 30, 45–47]. In vivo studies support these observations, showing that mTOR inhibition and late-stage autophagy blockade significantly reduces tumor size [48–50]. Additionally, genetic ablation of key autophagy regulators such as Rab7, STX-17 and SNAP29 has produced similar outcomes, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this approach [27, 29].

Moreover, this study further confirmed that RAPA + CQ-induced cell death in Jurkat cells was independent of classical cell death pathways. The pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-FMK had no effect, and cleaved caspase-3 was undetectable, ruling out apoptosis. Necroptosis, mediated by RIPK1 signalling, was also excluded, as the inhibitor Nec-1 failed to rescue the cells. These findings are in line with previous studies showing that simultaneous autophagy activation and late-stage inhibition induces a form of cell death that is neither apoptotic nor necroptotic and cannot be reversed by z-VAD-FMK or Nec-1 [29, 47, 51]. Collectively, these data strongly support ACD as the underlying mode of death in this context.

The stage-specific outcomes observed here emphasize the paradoxical nature of autophagy in cancer. When blocked early (RAPA + 3-MA), autophagy cannot progress to a level sufficient to trigger ACD, explaining the lack of cytotoxicity. In contrast, late-stage inhibition (RAPA + CQ) causes continuous initiation without completion results in lethal autophagosome accumulation. In line with these observations, LC3 dynamics provided additional mechanistic insight. The combination of RAPA and CQ markedly increased LC3-II accumulation, consistent with autophagosome build-up due to impaired degradation. By contrast, 3-MA did not fully block LC3-I to LC3-II conversion but instead depleted the LC3-I pool, thereby limiting new autophagosome formation, as reported previously that RAPA + 3-MA treatment failed to reverse LC3-II accumulation despite successfully impairing autophagic flux [52]. Together, these findings support the view that autophagosome accumulation, rather than autophagy initiation alone, is the critical determinant of cell death in this model.

Mechanistically, the prolonged accumulation of autophagosomes induces cytotoxicity through multiple pathways distinct from classical apoptosis and necroptosis [29]. One major proposed mechanism is lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP). For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma models, the simultaneous activation of autophagy combined with late-stage blockade of autophagosome-lysosome fusion using agents such as CQ triggers LMP, as evidenced by the release of procathepsin D into the cytoplasm [53]. This subsequent release of cathepsins is directly linked to lysosomal-dependent cell death and can indirectly trigger mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization [54]. Furthermore, recent studies highlight elevated ROS and subsequent energy deprivation as critical drivers of autophagosome accumulation cytotoxicity [29]. The autophagosome accumulation-mediated cell death can be significantly rescued by the replenishment of reduced L-glutathione or ATP [53, 55]. The profound disruption of cellular recycling machinery has been correlated with elevated endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response, which may be associated with severe ATP deficits [56]. Although the mechanism remains elusive, it is not surprising that disruption of such recycling and degradation machinery in the cells may contribute, at least in part, to the cytotoxicity observed.

Given the potent cytotoxicity associated with profound autophagosome accumulation, the dual-treatment strategy in this study holds significant therapeutic potential. Both RAPA and CQ are FDA-approved agents with established clinical safety profiles. While the primary clinical indication for RAPA is the prevention of organ transplant rejection, and CQ is classically utilized as an antimalarial agent, their ability to modulate the autophagy pathway has garnered substantial interest in cancer research [57, 58]. Importantly, our demonstration that apoptosis and necroptosis are dispensable in this context underscores the potential of ACD as an alternative cytotoxic route. Consequently, repurposing these clinically established drugs represents a highly feasible and promising strategy for the treatment of apoptosis-resistant leukemia, which currently face limited therapeutic options.

Notably, this study refines the idea that autophagy inhibition is universally beneficial; rather, the timing and stage of intervention determine whether autophagy blockade undermines or enhances cancer cell killing. Most conventional cancer therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy, trigger apoptosis as their primary cytotoxic mechanism. However, many of these agents also induce autophagy as a stress response. The impact of autophagy inhibition in this context depends strongly on the stage at which the pathway is blocked. Early inhibition prevents autophagy initiation, thereby abolishing any drug-induced autophagic activity. By contrast, late inhibition blocks lysosomal degradation but still allows autophagosome formation. This leads to autophagosome accumulation, which can itself become cytotoxic and contribute to ACD. Thus, when a cancer drug induces autophagy, late-stage inhibition may have an additive or synergistic effect, enhancing overall cell killing rather than merely removing a survival pathway. Indeed, it has been previously reported in glioma models, that in autophagy-independent cytotoxic contexts (e.g., with cisplatin or NVP-BEZ235), inhibition at any stage enhances cell death, suggesting a protective role for autophagy [35, 59]. In contrast, late-stage autophagy inhibition but not early-stage augments the cytotoxicity of autophagy activators like quercetin and arsenic trioxide [27]. These findings thus provide a rationale for carefully designed combinatorial regimens that exploit the “autophagy switch” in leukemia therapy.

Nevertheless, several limitations within the current study should be acknowledged. While our findings demonstrate that dual-treatment-induced cell death occurs independently of apoptosis and necroptosis, strongly supporting the role of ACD, the potential concurrent activation of other programmed cell death pathways, such as pyroptosis and ferroptosis, cannot be entirely ruled out. For example, basal autophagy negatively regulates the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key driver of pyroptosis; therefore, the profound disruption of autophagic flux observed in our model could potentially alleviate this suppression and trigger pyroptosis [60]. Furthermore, while our findings provide robust mechanistic proof-of-concept within a T-ALL model, leukemia is a highly heterogeneous disease. Future studies must validate this dual-targeting strategy across diverse hematological malignancies, including myeloid leukemia models such as THP-1, to determine whether the observed autophagy-dependent cytotoxicity is broadly applicable or lineage-specific. Additionally, while pharmacological modulators provide valuable insights, 3-MA, commonly used at high concentrations (mM), may non-specifically affect cellular functions beyond autophagy inhibition [61]. Future studies may employ genetic approaches, such as RNA silencing to knock out autophagy-related genes essential for autophagosome formation (e.g., ATG5, ATG7 or STX17) to provide more specificity and reinforce the ACD finding [62]. Despite these caveats, the present work provides proof-of-concept that dual modulation of autophagy, activation coupled with late-stage inhibition, can trigger ACD in leukemia cells, positioning this strategy as a promising therapeutic avenue for apoptosis-resistant malignancies. Further research is needed to optimize this dual-targeting approach for integration into current leukemia treatment strategies.
