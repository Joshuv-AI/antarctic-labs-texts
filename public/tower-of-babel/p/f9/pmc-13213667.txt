Topical delivery of a PPARγ agonist enables adipogenic repair of irradiated skin
Authors: Ashley Siegel, Samerender Hanumantharao, Pratik Koirala, Viren Sehgal, Chaoqi He, Menfang Wu, Soheila Ali Akbari Ghavimi, Ziyu Chen, Zachary Williams, Xi Xu, Chang Liu, David Kozono, Shailesh Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of cancer treatment, yet it frequently results in radiation-induced tissue injury (RITI), causing long-term morbidity such as chronic wounds, impaired mobility, joint dysfunction, and wound healing complications following oncologic resection. RITI is characterized by fibrosis, diminished adipogenesis, and reduced vascularity, ultimately compromising reconstructive outcomes. Current topical treatments are limited to moisturizers and corticosteroids, the latter of which may delay wound healing. Surgical interventions—including adipose grafting and complex tissue transfer—remain the mainstay of treatment but are associated with high morbidity. There is a critical need for a low-risk, accessible strategy for primary prevention of RITI. We developed a novel topical formulation of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) agonist rosiglitazone to enhance regenerative healing in irradiated skin. In murine models, topical rosiglitazone increased dermal adipocyte presence, restored regenerative capacity in irradiated skin by reprogramming mesenchymal progenitors, and reduced fibrotic collagen deposition. These changes were associated with accelerated wound healing in irradiated fields. Our findings demonstrate that local PPARγ activation promotes adipogenic and vascular regeneration while limiting fibrosis, thereby improving tissue resilience to radiation injury. This non-invasive, easily administered topical therapy offers a promising strategy for preventing RITI in the nearly one million patients who undergo radiation therapy annually in the United States.

Introduction

Radiation-induced tissue injury (RITI) presents substantial morbidity for patients undergoing radiotherapy for cancer treatment. In the United States alone, nearly 1 million individuals receive radiation therapy annually. RITI often leads to skin fibrosis, resulting in skin tightness and impaired joint mobility, particularly in areas like the axilla (after breast cancer radiation)1,2 or neck (after head and neck cancer radiation).3,4 When neoadjuvant (e.g., sarcoma) or adjuvant (e.g., breast cancer) radiation is necessary, subsequent surgical resection may be complicated by impaired incisional healing and chronic wounds.5 Even in the absence of surgery, RITI can cause long-term wound healing complications.6,7

Patients affected by RITI often require complex reconstructive operations, including free tissue transfer, to restore form and function. These procedures demand specialized surgical expertise, substantial postoperative recovery, and carry risks of donor and recipient site complications. Moreover, some patients, particularly older individuals or those with radiation-associated cardiopulmonary injury, may not be candidates for these invasive interventions.

The underlying mechanisms of RITI include excessive collagen deposition8,9 and impaired adipogenesis10 and angiogenesis11 in affected tissues. Current cutaneous delivery strategies, such as hydrogel scaffolds applied over dermal defects,12,13,14 are technically challenging to apply over large, irradiated areas already at risk for chronic wounds.

Adipocytes are known to exert anti-fibrotic effects on tissues.15,16,17 In clinical practice, autologous adipose tissue grafting is often employed to improve skin quality following radiation. Recent studies demonstrate that adipocytes not only reduce fibrosis but also orchestrate tissue healing, positioning them as potential therapeutic targets for addressing both fibrosis and impaired wound healing. However, adipose grafting remains an invasive, multi-stage procedure requiring general anesthesia—an option not feasible for all patients. Given the inability to reliably predict which patients will develop severe RITI, an effective preventative approach would require a widely accessible, low-morbidity strategy.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is a master transcriptional regulator of adipogenesis. Clinically available PPARγ agonists, developed primarily for the treatment of diabetes, have demonstrated systemic anti-fibrotic effects.18,19 Among these, rosiglitazone has been extensively studied and exhibits potent anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, and pro-adipogenic effects in multiple preclinical models of tissue injury compared to other PPARγ agonists, including skin, lung, liver, and gastrointestinal tract.20,21,22,23 Its robust pharmacologic profile made it a compelling candidate for repurposing in this context.24 In support of this, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of murine whole skin reveals that rosiglitazone strongly upregulates adipogenic transcriptional programs while suppressing fibrotic gene expression, further justifying its investigation as a targeted therapy for radiation-induced fibrosis. However, systemic administration is limited by serious adverse effects, including osteoporosis and cardiac failure25,26—concerns particularly relevant for cancer survivors who have undergone radiation therapy. To date, a strategy for localized PPARγ agonist delivery to skin has not been reported.

Here, we report the development of a topical PPARγ agonist formulation using rosiglitazone for the prevention of RITI. We demonstrate that topical rosiglitazone (TR) enhances cutaneous adipogenesis in mouse and human tissue, and improves wound healing and fibrosis in irradiated skin, without promoting pathogenic collagen deposition. In addition, TR significantly increased vascularity in irradiated wounds, as evidenced by elevated CD31 expression, suggesting improved angiogenic potential. These findings offer strong support for advancing TR as a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate radiation-associated morbidity, improving outcomes for cancer survivors.

Results

Computational analysis reveals PPARγ agonists as drivers of adipogenic rather than fibrogenic differentiation in irradiated skin

To identify compounds that promote adipogenic over fibrogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) in irradiated skin, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from murine whole skin (GSE201447).27 Following Harmony integration and Leiden clustering, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) projections demonstrated effective alignment across naive and irradiated samples (Figure 1A) and revealed distinct cellular populations (Figure 1B). Based on top marker genes, we identified fibrogenic (cluster 4; Dcn and Col1a1) and adipogenic clusters (cluster 20; Plin2 and Cidea). Differential expression analysis of cluster-averaged profiles confirmed the expected enrichment of fibrogenic (FC > 0) and adipogenic (FC < 0) markers (Figure 1C). Using the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb),28 we scored these gene signatures to rank candidate drugs predicted to promote adipogenesis. Notably, multiple PPARγ agonists, including rosiglitazone, emerged among the top hits (Figure 1D). In non-irradiated skin, rosiglitazone significantly increased adipose layer thickness compared to control-treated samples after 1 week (418.9 μm vs. 326.1 μm, p = 0.019; n = 2/group, 3 sections/mouse, 3 high-power fields (hpfs)/section; Figures 2B and 2D). This increase was driven by both adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia, as indicated by larger adipocyte size (167.1 μm vs. 155.5 μm, p = 0.047; Figures 2C and 2E) and higher adipocyte number per hpf (43.3 vs. 35.5, p = 0.005; Figures 2C and 2F).

In irradiated skin, daily TR also led to a modest increase in adipose layer thickness (327.1 μm vs. 247.7 μm, p = 0.0496; Figures 2G and 2I), but there was no significant change in average adipocyte size (187.0 μm vs. 192.2 μm, p = 0.57; Figures 2H and 2J) or adipocyte number per hpf (39.3 vs. 39.2, p = 0.99; Figures 2H and 2K). This suggests that the observed increase in adipose content may reflect changes in tissue organization, lipid accumulation, or extracellular matrix expansion rather than true changes in cell size or number. Together, these ex vivo experiments provide foundational evidence for the adipogenic potential of rosiglitazone in both healthy and radiation-injured skin.

Rosiglitazone enhances adipogenic signaling and suppresses fibrotic gene expression in ADSCs

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are multipotent mesenchymal progenitors capable of differentiating into adipocytes and other mesenchymal lineages in response to environmental cues. To further investigate the molecular effects of rosiglitazone, we cultured ADSCs exposed to either 0 or 15 Gy of ionizing radiation and subsequently treated them with vehicle control or rosiglitazone (10 μM) for 7 days.

Quantitative PCR revealed significantly increased expression of adipogenic markers (Adipoq and Pparg) in non-irradiated ADSCs treated with rosiglitazone, consistent with our ex vivo findings (Figure 2L). In contrast, irradiated ADSCs without rosiglitazone showed upregulated expression of fibrotic genes (Col1a1 and Pdgfra), indicative of radiation-induced profibrotic signaling (Figure 2L). Notably, rosiglitazone treatment restored fibrotic gene expression to near-baseline levels in irradiated ADSCs, suggesting a reversal of radiation-induced transcriptional dysregulation. These findings support the hypothesis that rosiglitazone promotes adipogenic differentiation while mitigating fibrotic programming in radiation-damaged mesenchymal cells.

Topical rosiglitazone enhances adipogenesis and improves chronic fibrosis in irradiated skin

To evaluate whether TR could mitigate radiation-induced skin injury, we administered daily treatment with either rosiglitazone or vehicle control to the irradiated dorsal skin of mice for 4 weeks (Figure 4A). In prior experiments using uninjured in vivo skin, we demonstrated that TR significantly enhanced adipogenesis through both adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia compared to untreated (NT) and topical control (TC) littermates, similar to our ex vivo results (Figure 1). Specifically, TR-treated skin exhibited increased adipose layer thickness (TR: 162.2 μm vs. NT: 109.4 μm, TC: 114.9 μm; ∗p < 0.05), higher average adipocyte count per high-powered field (TR: 74.3 vs. NT: 46.0, TC: 54.9; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01), and larger average adipocyte size (TR: 161.2 μm vs. NT: 104.4 μm, TC: 124.7 μm; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01; n = 3–4/group, 3 sections/mouse, 3 hpf/section; Figures S3A–S3C), highlighting the therapeutic potential of our formulation. Rosiglitazone-treated skin demonstrated significantly increased adipose thickness (202.8 μm in rosiglitazone-treated vs. 137.1 μm in control-treated, p = 0.002, n = 6–8/group, 3 sections/mouse, 3 hpf/section; Figures 4B and 4D), a higher average number of adipocytes (82.3 adipocytes/hpf in rosiglitazone-treated vs. 58.7 adipocytes/hpf in control-treated, p = 0.005; Figures 4C and 4E), and larger average adipocyte size (144.1 μm average adipocyte/hpf in rosiglitazone-treated vs. 115.5 μm average adipocyte in control-treated, p = 0.008; Figures 4C and 4F). We then performed immunofluorescence (IF) staining to assess the functional state of newly formed adipocytes in the subdermal fat layer following radiation and rosiglitazone treatment. Rosiglitazone-treated skin exhibited significantly higher perilipin expression—a marker of mature, healthy adipocytes—compared to controls (results expressed as relative intensity in arbitrary units: 9.8 a.u. rosiglitazone-treated vs. 4.8 a.u. control-treated, p = 0.022, n = 5–6/group; 1 section/mouse, 4 hpf/section; Figures 4I and 4K), confirming enhanced adipocyte differentiation. We also examined adiponectin, an anti-fibrotic adipokine secreted by adipocytes, but found no significant difference in expression between treatment groups (8.7 a.u. rosiglitazone-treated vs. 7.6 a.u. control treated, p = 0.64; Figures 4J and 4L). Hair follicles and epidermal immunofluorescent signals were excluded from analysis via appropriate thresholding (see materials and methods for more details).

Topical rosiglitazone enhances adipogenic signaling and suppresses fibrotic gene expression in irradiated mouse skin

To complement our IF findings and assess transcriptional changes in vivo, we performed qPCR analysis of irradiated dorsal skin treated daily with TR or vehicle control for 4 weeks following 15 Gy radiation.

Rosiglitazone treatment significantly increased expression of adipogenic genes (Adipoq and Pparg) compared to irradiated skin treated with control, and even surpassed levels seen in non-irradiated skin treated with rosiglitazone (Figure 4M). Conversely, irradiated skin treated with TC showed marked upregulation of fibrotic genes (Col1a1 and Pdgfra) relative to all other groups. Importantly, rosiglitazone treatment normalized fibrotic gene expression to levels comparable to uninjured controls, indicating that rosiglitazone can reverse radiation-induced fibrosis at the transcriptional level within tissue-resident cells. These results extend our in vitro and ex vivo observations and suggest that rosiglitazone promotes adipogenic repair while suppressing fibrosis in vivo following radiation injury.

To investigate whether PDGFRα+ FAPs contribute to the adipocyte pool in response to rosiglitazone, we performed dual IF staining for perilipin (a mature adipocyte marker) and PDGFRα or PDGFRβ in irradiated skin (Figures 5D and 5E). Quantitative colocalization analysis using Manders’ coefficient revealed significantly increased colocalization of perilipin with PDGFRα in rosiglitazone-treated samples compared to controls, supporting the direct adipogenic differentiation of PDGFRα+ FAPs (Figure 5G). Together, these findings suggest that rosiglitazone promotes in situ reprogramming of fibrogenic PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cells into adipocytes, consistent with our ADSC data and broader model of rosiglitazone-mediated adipogenic repair. This mechanism may underlie rosiglitazone’s ability to restore adipose homeostasis and mitigate fibrosis in irradiated tissue.

To test this, we created excisional wounds on dorsally irradiated mouse skin and compared wound closure kinetics between animals treated daily with TR versus vehicle control (Figure 6A). Rosiglitazone-treated mice exhibited faster healing, with a shorter median time to complete wound closure compared to controls (12 vs. 15 days, p = 0.26 by log-rank test; n = 7/group; Figures 6B and 6C). These findings suggest that rosiglitazone does not promote wound resolution by increasing tissue mass or cellular proliferation, but rather through qualitative improvements in tissue remodeling.

We next examined vascularity and collagen organization within the resulting scars. On IF staining for CD31, rosiglitazone-treated wounds exhibited significantly increased angiogenesis, as measured by mean fluorescence intensity (TC: 0.13 a.u. vs. TR: 0.52 a.u., p = 0.03; n = 6–7/group, 1 section/mouse, 4 hpf/section; Figures 6D and 6E), regardless of day of wound closure. To assess collagen ultrastructure, we performed Picrosirius red staining viewed under polarized light on the day of wound closure, which distinguishes between collagen fiber types: immature, loosely organized type 3 collagen appears green/yellow, while mature, densely packed type 1 collagen appears orange/red.31,32 Rosiglitazone-treated wounds showed a significantly higher proportion of green-staining collagen fibers (TC: 4.40% vs. TR: 8.71%, p = 0.0025; n = 7/group), and a lower proportion of red-staining fibers (TC: 78.26% vs. TR: 71.13%, p = 0.045; Figures 6F and 6G), indicating a shift toward a more regenerative extracellular matrix and reduced collagen maturation.

Rosiglitazone restores adipogenic programming in irradiated human adipocytes

To extend our findings to human tissue, we next investigated the effects of rosiglitazone on irradiated human adipocytes. Human adipocytes were isolated and exposed to varying doses of ionizing radiation, followed by treatment with either rosiglitazone-containing media or standard culture media for 7 days (Figure 7A). As expected, radiation led to a dose-dependent decrease in expression of adipogenic genes ADIPOQ and PPARG, alongside an increase in fibrotic marker PDGFRA (Figure 7B). Like in our mouse model, when irradiated adipocytes were treated with rosiglitazone for 7 days, gene expression profiles shifted back toward a homeostatic state—adipogenic gene expression increased, and fibrotic gene expression decreased—closely resembling levels observed in unirradiated adipocytes (Figure 7C). Discussion

Radiation induced tissue injury (RITI) remains a major source of morbidity for cancer survivors,6,33,34,35,36,37 yet current therapeutic options are limited. Here, we demonstrate that topical application of a PPARγ agonist, rosiglitazone, enhances adipogenesis by promoting the formation of functional adipocytes in irradiated skin, and accelerates wound healing through the attenuation of fibrosis and improvement in skin angiogenesis. These findings establish topical rosiglitazone (TR) as a promising, non-invasive strategy to mitigate the long-term cutaneous sequelae of radiation therapy.

Our data show that radiation exposure suppresses adipogenic differentiation while promoting a fibrotic transcriptional program in mesenchymal precursors, consistent with prior reports implicating adipocyte loss as a key driver of radiation-associated fibrosis.16,38 Importantly, we observed that TR not only restored adipose tissue thickness but also increased both adipocyte number and size, accompanied by a marked reduction in collagen deposition. Rosiglitazone treatment also accelerated wound healing in irradiated skin—a compelling finding given that prior studies have shown this agent reduces cellular proliferation.39,40,41 In our model, improved healing was not mediated by fibroblast expansion, but rather by decreased fibrosis and favorable remodeling of the collagen matrix. Specifically, rosiglitazone reduced dense, red birefringent collagen fibers and increased finer, green collagen fibers, suggesting a shift toward more regenerative extracellular matrix architecture. Polarized light imaging was used in this wound healing model to assess fiber organization and maturity, whereas in non-wounded irradiated skin, total collagen deposition was quantified under bright field conditions to evaluate global fibrosis. Furthermore, we found that rosiglitazone enhanced angiogenesis in irradiated wounds, as evidenced by increased CD31 expression, addressing the well-documented vascular deficiency of irradiated skin.11 Lastly, the upregulation of perilipin expression in treated tissues indicates that rosiglitazone promotes the maturation of lipid-rich, functional adipocytes, supporting restoration of the cutaneous adipose compartment.

A major strength of our approach lies in the use of a topical delivery strategy. Systemic PPARγ agonists have shown anti-fibrotic efficacy in other models,42,43 but their clinical use is constrained by serious adverse effects, including osteoporosis and congestive heart failure.25,26 By contrast, topical application offers localized therapeutic benefit while minimizing systemic exposure, a particularly important consideration for cancer survivors who may have heightened cardiopulmonary vulnerability following radiation. Furthermore, unlike hydrogel-based delivery platforms,12,13,14 our topical formulation is readily applicable over broad surface areas and may be adapted for prophylactic use in patients at high risk for RITI.

Our lineage tracing experiments suggest that the expansion of the adipose compartment after TR treatment arises predominantly from local tissue-resident mesenchymal progenitors rather than recruitment of circulating bone marrow-derived stem cells. This finding aligns with previous studies identifying PDGFRα+ fibroblast-like precursors as key contributors to adipocyte regeneration during wound healing.44 In support of this, we also observed that TR augmented adipogenesis in ex vivo human skin models: rosiglitazone-treated irradiated human skin exhibited the appearance of new adipocytes and increased perilipin expression compared to non-radiated controls. These findings suggest that local PPARγ activation can directly reprogram resident progenitors toward an adipogenic fate, even in fibrotic or damaged human tissue.

While these results are encouraging, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although we observed a clear benefit in a moderate-dose (15 Gy) radiation model, rosiglitazone’s efficacy was diminished at higher radiation doses (30 Gy), suggesting a threshold of tissue injury beyond which regenerative responses may be insufficient. However, prior studies have shown that high single-fraction doses such as 30 Gy often result in severe acute tissue necrosis and systemic toxicity,45,46 limiting their translational relevance. In contrast, moderate doses in the 15–20 Gy range more faithfully recapitulate the sublethal, progressive fibrosis observed in clinical radiation injury.47,48 Future studies should explore dose-dependent effects and evaluate whether combination therapies, such as rosiglitazone with anti-inflammatory agents or pro-angiogenic factors, can extend therapeutic efficacy in more severe injury models. Second, while we demonstrated improved fibrosis and adipogenesis at histologic and molecular levels, functional outcomes such as skin elasticity and tensile strength were not assessed in this study. Such endpoints will be critical in future translational work. Lastly, our experiments focused primarily on preventative or early intervention strategies; whether TR can reverse established, chronic fibrosis remains an open question and will be essential for eventual clinical translation.

In conclusion, our findings establish topical PPARγ activation as a novel therapeutic avenue for preventing and mitigating radiation-induced skin fibrosis. Given the simplicity, accessibility, and safety profile of topical delivery, this approach holds substantial potential for improving reconstructive outcomes and quality of life for cancer survivors. Future studies will be needed to optimize dosing strategies, expand efficacy across diverse radiation regimens, and evaluate long-term functional benefits.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether TR mitigates radiation-induced skin fibrosis by promoting adipogenesis in both mouse and human tissue models. To evaluate this, a total of 95 mice underwent dorsal irradiation at 15 Gy to induce chronic skin fibrosis, which was allowed to develop over a 4-week period. Mice were then randomly assigned in a blinded fashion to receive either a novel TR formulation or a vehicle control, applied 5 days per week. Each treatment group included at least five animals with comparable baseline characteristics. A separate cohort of mice underwent radiation followed by full-thickness excisional wounding to assess wound healing in irradiated tissue; these mice also received either TR or control treatment. All mice were 6–8 weeks old at the start of experiments. Female mice were used for radiation-only studies, while male mice were used in the radiation plus wounding cohort. At 4 weeks, irradiated dorsal tissue was harvested for histological analysis, IF, and flow cytometry. Histology was used to assess dermal fibrosis and adipose regeneration, while IF and lineage tracing were employed to characterize the differentiation of tissue-resident PDGFRα+ mesenchymal cells into adipocytes. In parallel, healthy human skin samples obtained intraoperatively were treated ex vivo with TR to evaluate its direct effect on adipogenic and fibrotic gene expression. In vitro assays and histological measurements were performed with three and two technical replicates per sample, respectively. The number of animals per group was based on prior experience with similar models to ensure adequate power to detect biologically relevant differences; no formal statistical power calculation was performed. All analyses were conducted in a blinded manner, and outliers were identified using Grubb’s test. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH). Human tissue use was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board at BWH, with informed consent obtained from all donors.

RNA-seq

scRNA-seq data were obtained from GEO (GSE201447; whole skin from irradiated [15 Gy, day 14] and naive mice, n = 4/group). Drug-gene interactions were sourced from DGIdb (September 2025). Raw 10× matrices were filtered for doublets (Scrublet, rate 0.06), cells (≥200 genes, ≥500 counts, ≤15% mito reads, and total counts ≤99.5th percentile), and genes (≥3 cells). Data were normalized (target sum 1e4), log1p-transformed, and 3,000 HVGs selected (Seurat v.3; batched by condition). Principal-component analysis (PCA) (50 PCs), harmony integration, neighbor graph construction, UMAP, and Leiden clustering (res. 0.8) were performed. Differential expression (clusters 4 vs. 20) used mean expression, log2FC, and Welch’s t test (|log2FC| ≥1.5, p < 0.01). Drug effects were signed by interaction type, scaled by score, aggregated into reversal scores, and filtered to FDA-approved non-immuno/oncologic agents with ≥10 gene targets. More detailed methods of our process can be found under supplemental information.

Human tissue harvest

Human tissue skin samples were obtained intraoperatively during surgical reconstruction procedures from healthy female donors, with informed patient consent obtained prior to surgery. The removal of human tissues and their use in experiments were approved by the Institutional Review Board of BWH (protocol number 2013P001982). All experiments were conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and its revisions.

Animals

For 1 week, 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory; Bar Harbor, ME, US; weight: 23 ± 2 g) were acclimatized in the BWH vivarium. The experiments were carried out according to the protocol approved by the IACUC at BWH (protocol number 2020N000036). Human skin samples were prepared by surgically removing all visible subcutaneous fat prior to placement in 12-well plates.

Tissue procurement

At the conclusion of experiments, all mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation. The dorsally irradiated skin was harvested and divided for processing: half was fixed in 10% formalin for 48 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at 5 μm, while the other half was fresh frozen in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) for cryosectioning at 5 μm. Human skin samples were obtained intraoperatively during surgical reconstruction procedures from healthy female donors. Samples were processed for either paraffin embedding or cryopreservation in OCT and were sectioned at 5 μm for subsequent histological analysis.

Histology

Mouse and human tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin (Avantik), eosin (Sigma), and Picrosirius Red (ScyTek Labs) using standard protocols to assess adipose thickness and dermal fibrosis. Images were acquired using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

Adipose thickness

Sections were imaged at 4× magnification. Measurements were taken from three locations per image, across three images per section and three sections per mouse (27 total measurements per mouse).

Fibrosis quantification

Picrosirius red-stained sections were analyzed in ImageJ. A consistent region of interest (ROI) was manually defined from the epidermal-dermal junction to the panniculus carnosus. Fibrotic area within the ROI was quantified using a fixed color threshold (min: 80, max: 121). Nine non-overlapping fields per mouse were averaged.

Adipocyte size and count

Images were captured at 20× magnification, with five fields analyzed per section (five measurements per mouse). Human sections were similarly analyzed. All images were processed using ImageJ (v.1.52a) under identical acquisition settings.

Reagents

Rosiglitazone stock solution (20 mM) was prepared by dissolving rosiglitazone (AdipoGen Life Sciences, San Diego, CA, US) in DMSO at a concentration of 7.15 mg/mL. For cell culture experiments, 100 μL of the stock solution was added to 100 mL of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin to achieve the final working concentration (10 μM). TR formulation was prepared by dissolving rosiglitazone at a concentration of 10 mg/mL in a solution comprising 10% dimethyl sulfoxide ([DMSO] cellular grade; Sigma-Aldrich), 40% polyethylene glycol 300 (PEG300; Sigma-Aldrich), 5% Tween 20 (Fischer, Hampton, NH, US), and 45% normal saline. The control formulation contained the same proportions of DMSO, PEG300, Tween 20, and normal saline without rosiglitazone. Starting on the day of radiation, mice were treated with 50 μL of either the TR or control solution applied to the dorsum, 5 days per week, for the duration of the study.

Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR

Mouse bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs) and ADSCs were isolated from C57BL/6 mice and cultured under standard conditions (n = 3 technical replicates/group). Upon reaching confluence, cells underwent adipogenic differentiation for 7 days. On day 7, cells were irradiated with 0, 15, or 30 Gy (n = 3 technical replicates/group) and cultured for an additional 7 days prior to RNA extraction. RNA isolation and qPCR: Total RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research), and cDNA synthesis was performed using the qMax cDNA Synthesis Kit (Accuris). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using gene-specific primers. Primer sequences can be found under supplemental methods.

Ex vivo radiation and skin culture

Dorsal skin samples from mice were collected following hair clipping and depilation with Nair, then excised into 1 × 1 cm2 sections. All samples were washed in PBS and placed in transwell inserts within 12-well plates. Each well was filled with 500 μL of complete culture media (DMEM-F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin). Skin samples were positioned tangentially on the inserts, with the epidermis facing upward and exposed to air, while the dermis remained in contact with the media to facilitate nutrient diffusion and prevent dehydration. Experimental plates were exposed to a single 15 Gy radiation dose (Rad Source, model RS 2000, serial #3259), while control plates remained unirradiated. A single dose of 15 Gy was chosen based on prior studies demonstrating that this dose induces consistent sublethal skin injury and chronic fibrosis in murine models without causing excessive acute toxicity or lethality.45,46 Skin samples were assigned to one of two treatment groups as follows: (1) TC—media with topical vehicle (40% PEG300, 5% Tween 20, 45% saline, and 10% DMSO), or (2) TR—media with TR (10 mg/mL in vehicle preparation). Topical formulations were applied daily to the epidermis, and media was refreshed every other day for 14 days. Following treatment, samples were either cryopreserved or paraffin-embedded for histological analysis.

For histologic quantification, we analyzed n = 2 biological replicates (mice) per group, with two technical replicates (skin samples) per mouse. For both 4× and 20× magnification images, three sections per sample were evaluated, with 3 hpfs per section, for a total of 9 hpf. Within each hpf, three independent measurements were taken and averaged to generate a single value per field.

In vivo dorsal radiation

Animals were anesthetized with 100 μL intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of ketamine (87.5 mg/kg, Zoetis)/xylazine (12.5 mg/kg, Dechra) mixture for a final concentration of 17.5 mg/mL ketamine/2.5 mg/mL xylazine. A 1 cm × 1 cm area of skin on the back of each mouse underwent hair removal using clippers and Nair lotion. Eye lubricant was applied to prevent excessive drying while mice were immobile. Mice were placed inside a custom-made lead cylinder (opening circumference: 2.5 cm2, overall circumference: 12.5 cm2, and height: 12 cm; Figure S2) to prevent whole body radiation, with a 1.5 cm2 opening through which the shaved area was exposed. This was the only part of the mouse that was subjected to radiation. Mice received radiation (15 Gy in one session or 30 Gy in two 15 Gy sessions) in an enclosed low-dose rate research irradiator (Best Theratronics, Canada; model: GC40 C1610X2, serial number 0268).

Bone marrow ablation and transplantation

Mice underwent bone marrow ablation via i.p. injections of busulfan (10 mg/kg; Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, US) for 4 consecutive days (n = 5/group). On day 5, same-sex, isogenic tdT+ bone marrow was isolated for transplantation into the ablated mice. Donor mice were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, and both hindlimbs were harvested with femurs, tibias, and fibulas intact. To isolate bone marrow, the soft tissue surrounding the femur was removed and the proximal end of the femur was excised. A 10 cc syringe fitted with a 21 gauge needle and filled with ice-cold PBS was inserted into the distal end of the femur, flushing the bone marrow through the proximal end onto a 70 μm nylon cell strainer. The cells were collected into a 50 mL Falcon tube and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in ice-cold PBS, and cells were counted using a hemocytometer. The suspension was diluted to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 104 cells/μL. A total of 1 × 106 cells in 100 μL were transplanted into recipient mice via retro-orbital (RO) injection using a 30 gauge needle. Recipient mice were rested for 3 weeks post-transplantation to allow for bone marrow engraftment. Reconstitution of bone marrow was confirmed by flow cytometry, quantifying tdTomato+ cells in recipient mice at the time of sacrifice. Animals with <65% reconstitution were excluded from the study.

Dorsal wounding

In wounding experiments, mice (n = 7/group) underwent one 8 mm full-thickness dorsal skin excision, including the panniculus carnosus, immediately following radiation. The dorsal skin was cleansed with 70% ethanol before excision. TC or rosiglitazone was applied daily to the wound and surrounding skin edges. Wound size was measured on the day of tissue harvest, which occurred either upon wound closure or on post-operative day 15 for non-healing wounds. Collected tissues were processed for both paraffin embedding and cryopreservation for IF. For histologic analysis of skin thickness, 5 μm sections were obtained from the scar on the day of wound closure and stained with H&E. Images were captured at 4× magnification, and three measurements each of the epidermis, dermis, and total thickness were averaged to yield one value per animal. Layer thicknesses were compared between control- and rosiglitazone-treated groups. For IF imaging using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated CD31, cryosectioned tissues were incubated overnight at a concentration of 1:100 (Invitrogen, catalog #2373735). Samples were imaged as 20× magnification and the FITC laser was set to 638 nm. For fluorescence imaging quantification, the thresholding value for CD31 detection was set to 31/255 (minimum/maximum). One measurement per image was obtained and averaged across four images per sample. Detailed methodology describing IF protocol, confocal settings, and intensity measurement quantification can be found under supplemental information.

IF staining and quantification

Cryosections were blocked with SuperBlock (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following antibodies: rabbit anti-perilipin (Cell Signaling, #3467S, 1:50), rabbit anti-adiponectin (Cell Signaling, #2789S, 1:100), antigen-presenting cell (APC)-conjugated anti-CD140a (PDGFRα; BioLegend, #135908, 1:100), and APC-conjugated anti-CD140b (PDGFRβ; BioLegend, #136008, 1:100). Sections stained with unconjugated antibodies were incubated for 2 h with Alexa Fluor 488 or 647 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Invitrogen #A32731, 1:500; AAT Bioquest #16837, 1:300), and counterstained with DAPI (BioLegend, #422801, 1:500).

Images were acquired at 40× using an Olympus Fluoview confocal microscope with standardized laser settings (405 nm for 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole [DAPI], 488 nm for FITC, 561 nm for tdTomato, and 640 nm for APC). Emission detection ranges and photomultiplier tube (PMT) sensitivity settings (high voltage [HV]) were adjusted per fluorophore but kept consistent across samples.

For quantification, images were converted to 8-bit in ImageJ and thresholded to detect antibody-positive cells. Threshold values were as follows: mouse—perilipin (22/27), adiponectin (24/32), tdTomato (33/86); human—perilipin and adiponectin (30/50). Only subdermal tissue was outlined for percent area analysis, excluding epidermal and hair follicle artifacts. Four images per sample were averaged. Sensitivity settings were standardized across all samples probed with the same antibody.

Colocalization

Colocalization analysis was performed on skin sections co-stained with anti-perilipin (rabbit, Alexa Fluor 488 secondary) and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD140a (PDGFRα) or CD140b (PDGFRβ). In ImageJ, channels were split and converted to 8-bit. Thresholds were applied as follows: perilipin (16–45), PDGFRα (41–120), and PDGFRβ (41–120). Tissue regions were outlined using the freehand tool, and colocalization was quantified using the “Coloc2” plugin with Manders’ coefficient. Imaging and analysis were performed blinded. GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analysis.

Flow cytometry

Radiated skin and bone marrow were processed into single-cell suspensions. Skin was minced and digested in 0.1% collagenase at 37°C for 1 h, filtered through a 75 μm mesh, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde on ice. Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% goat serum, and stained with anti-perilipin-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3467S; 1:5,000) followed by Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit secondary (Invitrogen, #WF322198, 1:2,000).

For bone marrow reconstitution, cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD45 and anti-PDGFRα, then gated for tdT+ populations. Data acquisition was performed on a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo v.10.10. For bone marrow reconstitution analysis, cells were incubated with APC-conjugated anti-CD45 and APC-conjugated anti-PDGFRα antibodies to identify hematopoetic stem cell (HSC)- and mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-derived populations. After staining, all samples were washed to remove unbound antibodies and analyzed on a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer equipped with appropriate filters. APC-positive cells were further gated for tdTomato expression. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v.10.10).

Polarized light microscopy

Picrosirius red-stained skin samples from the dorsal wounding and radiation cohorts were harvested individually on the day of complete wound closure, which varied between animals. For each sample, tissue was sectioned at the center of the healed wound, and the resulting scar region was analyzed under polarized light to assess collagen fiber maturity and organization. Birefringence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Wetzlar, Germany). Under polarized light, collagen fibers appear in distinct colors based on their thickness and maturity: thin, immature fibers stain green/yellow, while thick, mature fibers stain orange/red.31,32 Image analysis was performed using an ImageJ plugin developed by Gerull et al.49 to quantify the proportion of collagen fibers by color and type.

Additional details on materials and methods are provided in supplemental information.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation. Statistically significant differences between two groups were established using two-tailed Student’s t test and between multiple groups using one-way ANOVA. Day until wound closure was represented with a Kaplan-Meir curve with statistical comparison using a log-rank test. Statistical significance set at a p value <0.05. Data were analyzed and graphically presented using GraphPad Prism (v.9, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).
