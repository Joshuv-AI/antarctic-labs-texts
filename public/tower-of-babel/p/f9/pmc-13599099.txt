Single-session agreement of ChatGPT and Gemini treatment recommendations with multidisciplinary tumor board decisions in thoracic oncology
Authors: Kubra Hasimoglu Gurun, Fatmagul Cikladilmez, Guzin Demirag, Bahiddin Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the single-session agreement between treatment recommendations generated by ChatGPT and Gemini and real-world decisions made by a thoracic oncology multidisciplinary tumor board (MDTB).MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 102 thoracic oncology cases discussed at a university hospital MDTB between June and December 2025. Cases were categorized as primary lung cancer, pulmonary mass without pathological diagnosis, esophageal cancer, or pulmonary metastasis. Standardized anonymized case summaries, including clinical, radiological, pathological, and laboratory data, were presented to ChatGPT and Gemini. Each model was asked to provide a single tumor board–like management recommendation. Each case was submitted once to each model in a separate new chat session, and the initial output was used for analysis. Recommendations were classified into predefined categories and compared with final MDTB decisions. Agreement was assessed using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient.ResultsIn this single-session evaluation, overall agreement with MDTB decisions was 52.0% for ChatGPT and 56.9% for Gemini. Both models showed moderate agreement, with slightly higher agreement for Gemini than ChatGPT (κ = 0.487 vs. κ = 0.432; p < 0.001). Agreement was higher in standardized scenarios, such as primary lung cancer and esophageal cancer, but lower in pulmonary masses without pathological diagnosis and pulmonary metastases. No significant difference was found between the models in overall concordance with MDTB decisions (p = 0.359).ConclusionThe initial outputs of ChatGPT and Gemini showed moderate agreement with MDTB decisions in this single-session evaluation. LLMs may support guideline-based thoracic oncology decision-making but remain limited in complex, patient-specific scenarios and should not replace multidisciplinary clinical judgment. These findings should be interpreted as concordance with real-world MDTB decisions rather than evidence of stable model-level performance or clinical accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16458-5.Keywords: Large language models, ChatGPT, Gemini, Multidisciplinary tumor board, Thoracic oncology, Lung cancer, Artificial intelligence, Clinical decision support

Introduction

The thoracic oncology multidisciplinary tumor board (MDTB) represents the standard decision-making platform for individualized management of primary lung malignancies, esophageal cancers, pulmonary metastases from other solid tumors, mediastinal lymphadenopathies, and rarer thoracic tumors such as thymoma.

According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN data, lung cancer is among the most common malignancies worldwide, accounting for 12.4% of all new cancer cases and 18.7% of cancer-related deaths and remains the leading cause of cancer mortality [1]. When all solid tumors are considered together, the lung is one of the most common visceral sites of metastatic involvement. Indeed, several autopsy series have reported the lung as the most frequent visceral site of distant metastasis from solid tumors [2]. The high frequency of pulmonary metastases highlights the critical role of the thoracic region not only in primary lung cancer but also in the dissemination of various extrathoracic malignancies [3]. Esophageal cancer, although ranking 15th in incidence among all cancers according to 2022 GLOBOCAN data, ranks 8th in cancer-related mortality. Despite its relatively lower incidence, its high mortality rate and frequent clinical problems such as dysphagia and associated nutritional impairment further increase the comorbidity burden and complicate treatment planning [4].

In recent years, substantial advances have been made in the diagnosis and treatment of thoracic cancers, particularly lung cancer. Frequent updates in staging systems, evolving surgical strategies, advances in radiotherapy techniques, the identification of new driver mutations, and the increasing use of targeted therapies have made individualized, patient-specific treatment decisions essential, thereby reinforcing the need for MDTB evaluation [5]. This multidimensional and complex decision-making process remains a major challenge for clinicians, particularly in centers where access to multidisciplinary evaluation is limited [6]. In this context, interest in clinical decision-support systems has continued to increase.

Rapid advances in artificial intelligence, particularly the emergence of large language models (LLMs), have created new opportunities for their use in medicine. LLMs are artificial intelligence systems capable of contextual natural language processing, synthesizing unstructured clinical data, and generating guideline-based recommendations. In oncology practice, these models are increasingly regarded as potential supportive tools for rapidly reviewing current guidelines and literature, summarizing clinical scenarios, and structuring treatment options [7].

Among the most widely used LLM-based platforms today are ChatGPT (OpenAI) and Gemini (Google). However, in diseases such as lung cancer, where treatment decisions are highly individualized and require multidisciplinary evaluation, the extent to which these models align with real-world MDTB decisions remains unclear. Although data are available on the use of artificial intelligence in oncology, including breast cancer screening, radiation oncology applications, and selected systemic treatment decision-support systems, studies directly comparing LLMs with MDTB decisions remain limited [7].

In the present study, two current and widely used LLMs, ChatGPT-5.2 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) and Gemini Pro (Google DeepMind, Mountain View, CA, USA), were evaluated. These models were selected because they are increasingly used in clinical decision-support scenarios and are based on different artificial intelligence architectures. The aim of this study was to comparatively evaluate the agreement between treatment recommendations generated by ChatGPT-5.2 and Gemini Pro and the decisions made by MDTB for the same thoracic oncology cases. By doing so, we aimed to clarify the potential contribution of LLMs to clinicians, particularly in centers where MDTB evaluation is not routinely available, as well as the limitations of these technologies as clinical decision-support tools.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

In this retrospective observational study, board presentation records and final board decisions were retrospectively collected from the decision registry of the MDTB held at a university hospital between June 2025 and December 2025. A total of 126 cases discussed during this period were screened. All data were anonymized before analysis. Local ethics committee approval was obtained for the study (decision no: 2026/78).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients aged 18 years or older, (ii) oncologic cases evaluated within the scope of the thoracic oncology tumor board, and (iii) availability of a written final decision made during the board meeting. Cases presented to the board for non-oncologic thoracic diseases and patients younger than 18 years were excluded.

Of the 126 cases, 6 were excluded because of insufficient subgroup sample size for rare tumor entities or non-malignant conditions, such as thymoma and mediastinal lymphadenopathies without a malignant diagnosis. In addition, 18 cases were excluded because of incomplete clinical, radiological, or pathological data. Consequently, the final analysis was performed on 102 cases.

The MDTB included core representatives from medical oncology, radiation oncology, thoracic surgery, pulmonology, radiology, pathology, and nuclear medicine. Each case was discussed after review of the available clinical history, imaging findings, pathological results, laboratory data, disease stage, performance status, comorbidities, and treatment feasibility. Decision-making was guided by current international oncology guidelines, including European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations where applicable, together with national practice conditions, treatment availability, reimbursement policies, and local institutional standards. Final recommendations were generated through multidisciplinary consensus and recorded in the tumor board decision registry. The same core specialties were represented throughout the study period, although the individual physicians attending each meeting could vary according to clinical schedules.

The same instruction was used for all cases to ensure that the model generated a single decision. Radiological and pathological data were provided to the LLMs in text format. The complete prompt template, instruction text, and an anonymized example case demonstrating the standardized case presentation format are provided in Supplementary File.

Large language model evaluation process

Each case discussed by the tumor board was presented in the same format to ChatGPT-5.2 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) and Gemini Pro (Google DeepMind, Mountain View, CA, USA). A separate new chat session was initiated for each case to prevent any carryover of information or contextual learning from previous cases. Each case was submitted once to each model, and the initial output generated under the standardized prompt conditions was used for the primary analysis. Repeated prompting of the same cases was not performed, because the study was designed to evaluate single-session, real-world concordance between LLM-generated tumor board–like recommendations and MDTB decisions. The LLMs were instructed to evaluate the cases in accordance with current clinical guidelines, including ESMO and NCCN, and to provide a recommendation equivalent to a tumor board decision. However, the full texts of the NCCN and ESMO guidelines were not provided to the models as part of the prompt, and no external guideline documents were uploaded or retrieved during the evaluation process.

LLM evaluations were performed between January 1, 2026, and February 1, 2026, through the web-based interfaces of both models. Default system settings were used for both models, and no model parameters were modified. No custom system-level instructions were used beyond the user-entered prompt template described in Supplementary File. No leading statements were used during case presentation; no discussion of the case was conducted, and no additional information or feedback was provided. The outputs generated by the LLMs were recorded as “decisions.” These outputs were saved verbatim and subsequently classified. In cases where more than one recommendation was provided, the first and most strongly emphasized recommendation was accepted as the primary decision.

LLM decisions were compared with both the entries in the tumor board decision registry and the final MDTB decisions verified through patient records. All decisions were evaluated by two independent clinicians. In cases of disagreement, the final decision was reached by consensus.

The cases were presented to the LLMs in Turkish, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary analysis in this retrospective study was defined as the overall decision agreement between the MDTB and the LLMs. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages.

The level of categorical agreement between MDTB and LLM decisions was calculated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ), and the results were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch classification. Unweighted Cohen’s kappa was used because the management categories were nominal and did not have a natural ordinal structure that would allow clinically objective weighting of discordant categories. Kappa values were classified as follows: 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement.

In secondary analyses, agreement rates were evaluated separately according to decision categories, such as surgery and systemic therapy, and according to subgroups including case type, age, and sex. These subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and descriptive and were not intended to establish definitive subgroup-specific performance differences. In subgroup analyses, MDTB–LLM agreement was reported using both Cohen’s kappa values and percentage agreement. Because subgroup analyses were exploratory and some subgroup sample sizes were small, subgroup-level p values and kappa estimates were interpreted descriptively and with caution.

The McNemar test was used to compare ChatGPT and Gemini in terms of concordance with tumor board decisions. Pearson’s chi-square test was used for subgroup analyses according to age groups.

When subgroup sample sizes were limited, the results were reported descriptively. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 102 cases were included in the study. Of these, 77.5% (n = 79) were male, and the mean age was 65.77 ± 10.38 years (range, 21–83 years). Regarding case classification, 52.0% (n = 53) were primary lung cancer, 16.7% (n = 17) were pulmonary masses without pathological diagnosis, 9.8% (n = 10) were esophageal cancer, and 21.6% (n = 22) were pulmonary metastases.

The McNemar test showed no statistically significant difference between ChatGPT and Gemini in terms of treatment recommendation concordance with the MDTB (p = 0.359). This finding indicates that the overall single-session concordance of the two models with MDTB decisions was comparable.

Sex-based subgroup analyses included small numbers of cases in several diagnostic categories; therefore, these findings should be interpreted descriptively. In the sex-based agreement analysis between MDTB and ChatGPT treatment recommendations, moderate and statistically significant agreement was observed among male patients with primary lung cancer (κ = 0.570; p < 0.001; n = 45), whereas agreement was lower and not statistically significant among female patients (κ = 0.333; p = 0.063; n = 8). In the pulmonary mass without pathological diagnosis group, agreement was slight among male patients (κ = 0.217; p = 0.140; n = 14), while assessment in female patients was limited by the small number of cases (κ = 0.000; n = 3). In esophageal cancer, moderate and statistically significant agreement was found among male patients (κ = 0.579; p = 0.001; n = 8), whereas statistical evaluation in female patients was not reliable because of the limited number of cases (κ = 0.000; n = 2). In the pulmonary metastasis group, fair-to-moderate and statistically significant agreement was observed among male patients (κ = 0.390; p = 0.002; n = 12), whereas agreement was low and not statistically significant among female patients (κ = −0.136; p = 0.158; n = 10). When all cases were evaluated together, moderate and statistically significant agreement was found among male patients (κ = 0.516; p < 0.001; n = 79), while agreement among female patients was slight and not statistically significant (κ = 0.115; p = 0.168; n = 23).

In the sex-based agreement analysis between MDTB and Gemini treatment recommendations, moderate and statistically significant agreement was observed among male patients with primary lung cancer (κ = 0.512; p < 0.001; n = 45), whereas higher agreement was observed among female patients (κ = 0.680; p < 0.001; n = 8). In the pulmonary mass without pathological diagnosis group, moderate agreement was found among male patients (κ = 0.413; p = 0.005; n = 14), while statistical assessment in female patients was limited by the small number of cases (κ = 0.000; n = 3). In esophageal cancer, substantial and statistically significant agreement was observed among male patients (κ = 0.784; p < 0.001; n = 8). Among female patients, almost perfect agreement was observed (κ = 1.000; n = 2), although statistical significance could not be demonstrated because of the very small sample size (p = 0.157). In the pulmonary metastasis group, moderate and statistically significant agreement was found among male patients (κ = 0.424; p < 0.001; n = 12), whereas agreement was low and not statistically significant among female patients (κ = −0.099; p = 0.230; n = 10). When all cases were evaluated together, moderate and statistically significant agreement was observed among male patients (κ = 0.527; p < 0.001; n = 79), while fair-to-moderate but statistically significant agreement was found among female patients (κ = 0.306; p = 0.001; n = 23).

In the age-based agreement analysis, stratified as patients younger than 65 years and those aged 65 years or older, MDTB and ChatGPT treatment recommendations showed fair-to-moderate agreement in the younger age group (κ = 0.308; p < 0.001; n = 41). In contrast, agreement was higher in patients aged 65 years or older and reached a moderate level (κ = 0.502; p < 0.001; n = 61).

Similarly, in the age-based agreement analysis between MDTB and Gemini treatment recommendations, fair-to-moderate agreement was observed in patients younger than 65 years (κ = 0.380; p < 0.001; n = 41). Agreement was higher in patients aged 65 years or older and reached a moderate level (κ = 0.553; p < 0.001; n = 61).

Discussion

In this single-session evaluation, the initial outputs of both LLMs showed moderate agreement with MDTB decisions (ChatGPT, κ = 0.432; Gemini, κ = 0.487), consistent with previous literature [8]. However, agreement rates varied substantially according to diagnostic subgroup and decision category. The highest levels of agreement were observed in clinical scenarios with clearer guideline-based algorithms, such as primary lung cancer and esophageal cancer, whereas agreement decreased markedly in more complex situations requiring multidisciplinary interpretation, such as lesions without pathological diagnosis and metastatic disease. In the study by Longwell et al., which evaluated the performance of LLMs on medical oncology examination questions, LLMs performed well on standardized knowledge-based questions but showed limitations in questions requiring clinical reasoning [9]. These findings suggest that LLMs may perform better in clinical settings with well-defined algorithmic pathways but remain limited in scenarios requiring patient-specific interpretation [10, 11].

When the distribution of decisions was examined, a clear difference in approach was observed between the MDTB and the LLMs. Surgery was the most frequently recommended option by the MDTB (24.5%), reflecting real-world clinical practice in which clinical status, staging findings, and patient-specific factors are evaluated together. In contrast, the lower frequency of surgical recommendations by the LLMs may be related to the fact that surgical decision-making depends strongly on additional clinical variables, including operability, comorbidities, performance status, and patient preference. Similarly, in the study by Kim et al., which compared Watson for Oncology with MDTB decisions in patients with lung cancer, agreement was very high in metastatic-stage disease but lower in non-metastatic stages, with discordance particularly evident in surgery and chemoradiotherapy decisions. In that study, the main reasons for discordance included detailed clinical factors such as advanced age, poor performance status, comorbidities, the need for pneumonectomy, and patient preference [12]. In this context, the tendency of ChatGPT to more frequently recommend systemic therapy and Gemini to more frequently recommend biopsy in the present study may indicate that, under conditions of uncertainty, these models tend to shift toward more generalized treatment options or toward diagnostic clarification. The higher frequency of systemic therapy recommendations by ChatGPT (26.5%) may reflect a preference for treatment-based algorithms, whereas Gemini’s more frequent recommendation of biopsy (27.5%) may reflect a diagnostic clarification-oriented approach. Overall, the higher agreement in algorithmically clearer scenarios and lower agreement in more complex multidisciplinary situations are consistent with the existing literature.

When evaluated according to decision type, the low agreement in surgical decisions was particularly noteworthy (ChatGPT, 24.0%; Gemini, 40.0%). In contrast, higher agreement in more standardized decision areas such as chemoradiotherapy, biopsy, and follow-up suggests that these recommendations may be more readily reproduced by LLMs. The tendency of the MDTB to favor local curative approaches more frequently in oligometastatic and recurrent disease, whereas LLMs more often recommended systemic therapy, is clinically meaningful. This finding is consistent with the SABR-COMET trial, which demonstrated that local ablative treatment may improve survival in selected patients with oligometastatic disease, as well as with ASTRO/ESTRO guidelines recommending surgery- or radiotherapy-based definitive local treatment in appropriately selected patients with oligometastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [12–14]. Therefore, one possible explanation for the low agreement in surgical decisions is that LLMs may be insufficiently able to distinguish, on a patient-specific basis, oligometastatic disease amenable to curative treatment from disseminated metastatic disease, instead relying primarily on more generalized treatment algorithms.

In the diagnosis-based analysis, both models showed moderate-to-substantial agreement in primary lung cancer, whereas agreement decreased considerably in pulmonary nodules or masses without pathological diagnosis and in pulmonary metastases. In the approach to pulmonary masses without pathological diagnosis, Gemini achieved statistically significant agreement, whereas ChatGPT did not. Case-level review indicated that this difference was particularly evident among patients for whom the MDTB recommended surgery for lesions without pathological confirmation (n = 9). In three such patients, ChatGPT recommended systemic therapy despite the absence of pathological diagnosis and followed an algorithm consistent with primary lung cancer treatment. This suggests that ChatGPT may have gone beyond the available data and generated decisions through inferential or speculative reasoning [15, 16]. By contrast, Gemini appeared to prioritize the need for pathological confirmation in these cases, recommending biopsy or surgery and thereby following a more guideline-based and algorithmic approach. This difference is consistent with recent literature showing that different LLMs may exhibit distinct decision-making patterns in the same clinical scenarios [17, 18].

Among cases of pulmonary metastases from other malignancies, most were presented to the MDTB as metachronous or synchronous oligometastatic disease. The MDTB generally recommended curative treatment options such as surgery or radiotherapy in these cases, whereas the LLMs more frequently recommended systemic therapy and did not sufficiently adopt a curative-intent approach [12]. In some cases, pulmonary nodules considered metastatic by the MDTB were not interpreted as metastatic by the LLMs; instead, they were approached using algorithms similar to those applied to pulmonary nodules in individuals without known malignancy, resulting in follow-up recommendations. Li et al. reported that the accuracy of artificial intelligence systems for lung lesion risk prediction was higher in primary lung cancers but substantially lower in metastases and benign lesions. This finding is in line with the results of the present study [19].

In esophageal cancer, Gemini showed higher agreement than ChatGPT (κ = 0.836 vs. κ = 0.464), which was mainly related to better agreement in decisions other than chemoradiotherapy. This difference is consistent with recent studies indicating that LLMs may show variable performance in the same clinical scenarios because of differences in model architecture, training data, and clinical reasoning patterns [17, 18]. The fact that both LLMs showed 100% agreement for chemoradiotherapy decisions in esophageal cancer may be related to the central role of chemoradiotherapy in the treatment algorithm for this disease. In the present study, neoadjuvant and definitive chemoradiotherapy were evaluated under the same decision category; therefore, a recommendation of chemoradiotherapy by both the MDTB and the LLMs was considered a basic indicator of agreement. In clinical practice, the initial treatment strategy for esophageal cancer is often structured around chemoradiotherapy, with subsequent management determined according to treatment response. This may explain why the treatment algorithm for esophageal cancer is more standardized and predictable than that for other thoracic malignancies. Indeed, ASCO guidelines strongly recommend preoperative chemoradiotherapy or definitive chemoradiotherapy for locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and ESMO guidelines also define chemoradiotherapy as a key component of curative treatment in locally advanced disease [20, 21].

In the age-based analysis, agreement rates were lower in patients younger than 65 years for both models. In younger patients, the MDTB more frequently favored surgical approaches, whereas Gemini more often recommended biopsy and follow-up, and ChatGPT more often recommended systemic therapy and follow-up. Case-level evaluation suggested that this may be related to the MDTB’s tendency to pursue more aggressive local curative approaches in younger patients with oligometastatic or recurrent disease. Guidelines for oligometastatic NSCLC emphasize that pulmonary resection may be a valuable option in appropriately selected patients and that treatment decisions should be made through multidisciplinary evaluation [22]. In contrast, in patients aged 65 years or older, treatment decisions are recommended to be individualized more cautiously according to performance status, comorbidities, and physiological reserve [23]. Therefore, the relatively higher LLM–MDTB agreement in older patients may be explained by the fact that the MDTB itself recommended surgery less frequently in this group.

Among older patients, the most prominent discrepancy was observed in radiotherapy decisions. Among the 10 patients for whom the MDTB recommended radiotherapy, Gemini made the same recommendation in only 2 cases and ChatGPT in 4 cases. Case-level review showed that, in older patients, the MDTB tended to choose curative radiotherapy modalities instead of surgery as a curative treatment option, whereas the LLMs did not adequately reproduce this clinical decision. This discrepancy may reflect not simply a failure in recommending radiotherapy, but rather a broader limitation of LLMs in approaching recurrent and oligometastatic disease [17, 18].

In the sex-based analysis, agreement with ChatGPT was higher in male patients than in female patients, whereas this difference was less pronounced for Gemini. Particularly among female patients, surgical decisions made by the MDTB were more frequently converted into follow-up recommendations by the LLMs. One possible explanation is that subsolid nodule patterns, which may show a more indolent course and are more frequently observed in women, may have led the LLMs toward more conservative recommendations [24]. In addition, it is known that the performance of artificial intelligence models may vary across demographic subgroups [25]. Overall, these findings suggest that observed LLM concordance may depend not only on disease type but also on the demographic distribution and clinical complexity of the cases. However, the small number of cases in female subgroups is an important limitation, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. The subgroup analyses should also be interpreted with caution. Several subgroup strata, particularly sex-based analyses within specific diagnostic categories, included small numbers of cases. Kappa estimates derived from small samples may be unstable and may not reliably reflect true subgroup-level agreement. In addition, multiple subgroup comparisons were performed without formal adjustment for multiplicity because these analyses were exploratory and intended to identify descriptive patterns rather than confirm statistically independent subgroup effects.

The absence of a statistically significant difference between the two models in the McNemar test (p = 0.359) indicates that ChatGPT and Gemini had comparable overall concordance with MDTB decisions. However, this does not mean that the two models generated the same decisions. Detailed analyses showed that ChatGPT tended to recommend systemic therapy more frequently, whereas Gemini tended to recommend biopsy and surgery more often. Thus, although their overall concordance with MDTB decisions was similar, their decision-making patterns differed. Therefore, when evaluating artificial intelligence systems in this context, it is important to consider not only overall accuracy but also the nature of the decisions generated. This interpretation is consistent with recent literature showing that different LLMs may achieve similar overall accuracy in the same clinical scenarios while differing in clinical reasoning and treatment recommendations. Recent studies have also demonstrated substantial performance differences between models at different stages of the clinical decision-making process. Accordingly, LLMs should be evaluated not only by whether their final recommendations are correct, but also by the clinical quality and reasoning pattern of the decisions they generate [17, 18].

In the present study, radiological findings were provided to the LLMs through radiology reports rather than direct image input. Although image-based analysis is theoretically possible, this approach was preferred because LLMs still have important limitations in medical imaging, particularly with respect to hallucination, reliability, and interpretive consistency [26, 27]. Another limitation is that the cases were presented in Turkish, whereas the reference guidelines are primarily in English, and LLM performance has been shown to vary across languages [28]. This language-related issue may have influenced how guideline-based information was retrieved, interpreted, or translated into final management recommendations by the models. However, Turkish-language case presentation was intentionally used to reflect a realistic local workflow, because clinicians in non-English-speaking settings may interact with LLM-based tools in their native language. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of Turkish-language, text-based prompting. Future studies should directly compare Turkish and English prompts using identical case information to determine the effect of input language on LLM-based oncology recommendations.

A further important limitation is that within-model reproducibility was not assessed. LLMs are stochastic systems, and identical prompts may generate different outputs across repeated sessions. In the present study, each case was submitted once to each model in an independent new chat session, and the initial recommendation was used to simulate a single real-world clinical query. Therefore, the reported agreement estimates reflect single-session outputs generated under predefined prompt conditions and should not be interpreted as fixed model-level performance. Future studies should evaluate repeated prompting, within-model consistency, majority-vote strategies, and model-version effects when assessing LLMs as clinical decision-support tools.

Another limitation is that although the models were instructed to follow NCCN and ESMO guidelines, the guideline texts themselves were not provided as input. Therefore, the recommendations depended on the models’ internally represented guideline knowledge, which may have been incomplete, outdated, or unverifiable at the time of prompting. As a result, some discordance between LLM recommendations and MDTB decisions may reflect limitations in guideline recall or model knowledge cutoffs rather than deficiencies in clinical reasoning alone. Future studies should evaluate LLM performance using explicitly provided guideline versions or retrieval-augmented approaches to distinguish guideline recall from clinical reasoning.

In this study, LLM decisions were compared according to the broad main management categories defined in the Methods section. This approach allowed comparison across heterogeneous thoracic oncology scenarios, but it may obscure clinically meaningful differences within the same category, such as surgery alone versus neoadjuvant treatment followed by surgery, or different systemic treatment regimens. More detailed comparisons, such as the specific content of systemic therapy in patients for whom systemic treatment was recommended, were not included in the statistical analysis. In addition, when LLMs provided multiple or conditional recommendations, only the first or most strongly emphasized recommendation was used for agreement assessment. This approach improved consistency of classification but may have simplified the conditional nature of LLM-generated clinical reasoning and may have influenced the measured agreement rates. Similarly, because the decision categories were nominal, unweighted kappa was used; however, this approach assigns equal importance to all discordant categories and may not fully reflect the clinical severity of different types of disagreement. An important consideration is that the present study evaluated concordance with MDTB decisions rather than diagnostic or therapeutic accuracy. MDTB recommendations represent a real-world multidisciplinary reference comparator, but they should not be interpreted as an absolute gold standard. Differences between MDTB and LLM recommendations may therefore reflect not only limitations in LLM reasoning, but also local institutional practice, treatment availability, reimbursement conditions, patient preference, operability assessments, and clinical information not fully captured in text-based prompts. We acknowledge that guideline-derived recommendations, country-specific approval and reimbursement conditions, and real-world treatment availability may lead to differences between MDTB and LLM decisions [29]. This may be one factor contributing to lower agreement. However, we believe that the impact of this issue was relatively limited in the present cohort, because the cases presented to the board were generally borderline cases requiring distinction between surgery and chemoradiotherapy, or cases of oligometastatic disease. Patients with clear stage IV disease for whom systemic therapy was already the obvious treatment option were less frequently presented to the board. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported limitations of LLMs in selecting specific systemic therapy regimens [15]. Larger studies focusing specifically on the content of systemic treatment decisions are therefore warranted.

Conclusion

In this single-session concordance study, the initial outputs of ChatGPT and Gemini demonstrated moderate agreement with real-world MDTB decisions, particularly in standardized and guideline-based clinical scenarios. However, they show clear limitations in complex areas requiring patient-specific decision-making, such as recurrent and oligometastatic disease. In clinical settings where MDTB evaluation is not routinely available, LLMs may serve as supportive second-opinion or decision-support tools during case assessment. However, these models should not be regarded as clinical decision-making authorities; rather, they should be considered systems that summarize literature and guideline-based information, with the final decision always remaining the responsibility of the clinician. These findings should be interpreted as single-session concordance with real-world MDTB decisions rather than evidence of stable model-level performance or clinical accuracy.
