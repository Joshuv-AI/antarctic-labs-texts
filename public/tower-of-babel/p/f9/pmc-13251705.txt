Recent advances in clinical neurophysiology of myoclonus☆
Authors: Talyta Grippe, Benedetta Angeloni, Amitabh Bhattacharya, Robert Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in clinical neurophysiology have transformed the understanding and assessment of myoclonus. Neurophysiological techniques have made it possible to identify the neural generators of myoclonus and have provided the foundation for modern classification systems that integrate clinical phenomenology with physiological mechanisms. Many of these developments were shaped by the work of several research groups worldwide, with major influence from the contributions of Professor Mark Hallett. His studies helped to establish the central role of neurophysiology in the investigation of myoclonus. Through careful neurophysiological experiments and clinical observations, his work clarified the mechanisms underlying cortical and brainstem myoclonus and contributed substantially to the characterization of functional jerks. This review summarizes current approaches to the clinical neurophysiological evaluation of myoclonus and outlines the evolution of classification systems leading to the recent consensus framework proposed by the International Association of Parkinsonism and Related Disorders. Key neurophysiological techniques used in clinical practice are discussed, and emerging quantitative approaches, including machine-learning of neurophysiological signals, are also highlighted as potential tools to improve diagnostic precision and standardization in the evaluation of myoclonus.

Introduction

Myoclonus is an involuntary hyperkinetic movement characterized by sudden, brief jerks that can occur either in isolation or as part of a broader neurological disorder. Although myoclonus is typically described as a brief, shock-like movement, its clinical presentation can vary considerably in terms of distribution, triggers, and underlying mechanisms. From a clinical perspective, myoclonus can be difficult to diagnose and classify. Similar jerky movements may arise from different anatomical generators and pathophysiological mechanisms, and clinical observation alone is usually not sufficient to determine their origin. Neurophysiological evaluation has therefore become an essential component of the diagnostic workup. Over the past decades, neurophysiological studies have provided key insights into the mechanisms underlying myoclonus and have identified distinct physiological subtypes based on their anatomical generators. These advances have also shaped modern classification systems, which now integrate clinical phenomenology with objective neurophysiological findings.

In this review, we outline the historical foundations and recent advances in the clinical neurophysiology of myoclonus, and discuss how they have refined its diagnosis and classification.

Definition of myoclonus

Prof. Hallett often remarked in his lectures that if myoclonus had to be summarized in a single word, the most appropriate would be simple. [1] Indeed, together with the terms “brief,” “shock-like,” and “jerky,” this term captures most descriptions of myoclonus from expert groups [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. Myoclonus is defined as an involuntary movement caused by a sudden muscle contraction (positive myoclonus) or by an abrupt interruption of ongoing muscle activity (negative myoclonus) [2], [4]. It can occur spontaneously, with action, or as a reflex produced by sensory stimulation [2], [4].

Myoclonus is typically irregular, although some rhythmicity may be observed in specific conditions such as cortical tremor or peripheral myoclonus [6]. The clinical phenotype may also vary in terms of body distribution. Myoclonus may affect a few adjacent muscles (focal), involve several muscle groups in different regions (multifocal), or become generalized, affecting large parts of the body. [1], [2]

These clinical features of myoclonus may be used during the initial clinical evaluation to identify the abnormal movements as myoclonus and distinguish them from other jerky movements that may mimic it. [7] These include simple tics, which are typically preceded by a premonitory urge and are partially suppressible; fragments of chorea, which are more random and complex in appearance; tremor, which is generally rhythmic; and dystonia, which may occasionally appear jerky but is characterized by more sustained muscle contractions with overflow muscle activation [6], [8].

A wide range of etiologies, pathophysiological mechanisms, and anatomical generators can contribute to the sudden neuronal discharges that produce myoclonic jerks [9]. The following sections discusses the historical evolution of the concept of myoclonus and how advances in neurophysiology helped define its major subtypes.

Historical review of myoclonus

The history of myoclonus as a movement disorder is closely intertwined with the development of neurophysiological techniques used for its assessment (Fig. 1, [10]). This phenomenology was described in 1881 by Friedreich, who used the term “paramyoclonus multiplex” [11]. Based on these early observations, Lundborg proposed a classification system in 1903 that distinguished essential, symptomatic and epilepsy-associated forms of myoclonus. In 1938, Grinker, Serota, and Stein reported EEG spike and wave discharges preceding myoclonus in two patients with familial myoclonus epilepsy [12]. A major advance occurred in 1946, when neurophysiological evaluation showed increased amplitude of somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) following electrical stimulation of the median nerve in patients with progressive myoclonic epilepsy. This feature, later termed “giant SEPs”, was recognized as a marker of myoclonus generated in the cortex [13].Fig. 1Key historical milestones in myoclonus.Fig. 1

Subsequently, the development of EEG-EMG back-averaging technique enabled the identification of cortical transients preceding myoclonic jerks [14], and the presence of the cortical (C) reflex after a peripheral nerve stimulation [15]. Building on these findings, Hallett and Shibasaki independently proposed that cortical myoclonus is secondary to hyperexcitability in the cortex and represents a “cortical loop reflex”. Their work integrated several key observations, including the presence of the C-reflex, a cortical EEG transient, and enlarged SEPs, analyzed the temporal relationship between the myoclonic jerk and motor and sensory components of EEG and SEP. Their publications appeared within a few months of each other [16], [17].

Further advances expanded the understanding of other anatomical generators. In 1977, Hallett, Chadwick, Adam and Marsden demonstrated that the brainstem could act as a generator in patients with post-anoxic myoclonus [18]. In 1991, a subtype of subcortical myoclonus known as brainstem myoclonus (reticular myoclonus), was further localized to the brainstem based on a characteristic pattern of muscle activation that spread rostrally and caudally from muscles innervated by cranial nerves [19]. In the same year, the startle response, which also has its origin in the brainstem, was recognized as pathological when it failed to habituate in patients with hyperekplexia [20].

The identification of spinal generators of myoclonus was described earlier in 1974, based on cases showing similar neurophysiological patterns [21]. Shortly afterward, peripheral myoclonus was reported in 1977 and later confirmed by additional studies that associated it with distinct neurophysiological features [22], [23].

A more recent addition to the classification of myoclonus is functional myoclonus, or functional jerks [2]. The Bereitschaftspotential (BP), a slow, negative EEG marker of volitional action obtained using EEG-EMG back-averaging technique was originally described as preceding volitional movements. [24] Although the precise physiological significance of its components remains under investigation [25], this potential has been shown to precede functional jerky movements, thereby serving as a neurophysiological marker of functional muscle jerks or myoclonus [26].

Over the years, the role of clinical neurophysiology in the diagnosis of myoclonus has been extensively discussed. Much of this debate has focused on the neurophysiological characterization of the cortical subtype [27]. Although questions remain regarding the most sensitive techniques, clinical neurophysiology is widely regarded as the gold standard for myoclonus assessment. [28], [29] As a result, neurophysiological findings have been incorporated as a dedicated axis in the recent myoclonus classification [2] that will be discussed in the following section.

Classifications of myoclonus

Historically, the classification proposed by Marsden, Hallett and Fahn is the most widely recognized and clinically adopted framework for myoclonus [30]. Introduced in 1981, this etiological classification emerged from an effort to organize the heterogeneous historical descriptions of myoclonus into well-defined categories sharing common etiological features and separate myoclonus from epilepsy classification [31]. Based on a comprehensive review of the literature at the time, it aimed to minimize ambiguity from previous classifications. The authors proposed a framework that, in many ways, reflects the evolution of our understanding in neurology from the 19th century onwards. This classification has remained in use for more than four decades and encompasses four main groups: (1) physiological, or myoclonus occurring in normal physiological contexts; (2) essential, referring to isolated myoclonus without additional neurological deficits or identifiable secondary causes; (3) epileptic, in which myoclonus represents a major seizure manifestation; and (4) symptomatic, the broadest category, where myoclonus is identified as secondary to an underlying neurological condition [30].

This framework emphasized etiology and clinical context rather than the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms. However, this approach has several limitations that became increasingly evident with advances in clinical neurophysiology and molecular genetics. Growing recognition of the role of anatomical generators demonstrated that similar clinical phenotypes may arise from distinct neural networks, while identical etiologies may produce heterogeneous manifestations of myoclonus [32]. The development of advanced genetic sequencing techniques has since enabled the identification of specific molecular etiologies, promoting a more genetically informed classification and updated nomenclature [33]. Furthermore, the task force responsible for revising the classification identified key issues requiring reconsideration. Terms such as “essential” or “primary” were increasingly viewed as broad categories encompassing poorly defined clinical entities [2]. Additional priorities included clearer delineation between epileptic and non-epileptic myoclonus [34], as well as recognition of functional myoclonic movements, with the aim of establishing more homogeneous terminology and achieving consensus regarding their phenomenological classification [35].

In response to these developments, the revised consensus classification proposed by the International Association of Parkinsonism and Related Disorders (IAPRD) introduced a multidimensional, axis-based framework analogous to current dystonia classifications [2]. The new system is structured along two complementary axes. Axis I focuses on clinical characterization and is subdivided into two components. Axis Ia, which includes clinical features, historical elements, phenomenology, and diagnostic evaluations. Axis Ib, incorporates clinical neurophysiology, classifying myoclonus according to presumed anatomical generators, including cortical, cortical-subcortical, subcortical, brainstem, spinal, propriospinal, and peripheral forms, as well as functional jerks. Integration of clinical and neurophysiological data within Axis I allows identification of defined clinical syndromes, including myoclonus-dystonia, progressive myoclonus epilepsy, progressive myoclonus ataxia, cortical myoclonus tremor, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome, and functional jerks. This novel approach aims to highlight the most common entities seen in clinical practice. Each syndrome can have more than one etiology, and the syndromes do not encompass all possible presentations of myoclonus. However, this framework helps clinicians to tailor etiological investigations in Axis II.

Axis II addresses etiology and includes genetic, acquired, functional neurological disorder, idiopathic, and physiological causes. Unlike earlier classifications, etiology is considered alongside, rather than prior to, phenomenology and neurophysiology. This approach acknowledges that accurate classification of myoclonus requires simultaneous consideration of clinical phenotype, neurophysiological mechanisms, and underlying cause, thereby facilitating diagnostic precision, research standardization, and therapeutic intervention.

Clinical neurophysiology of myoclonus

To localize the generator of myoclonus, neurophysiological tools such as surface polymyography (multichannel surface EMG), accelerometry, electrical stimulation, and EEG are combined to identify specific responses that characterize myoclonus generators.

The initial step in the neurophysiological evaluation is the use of surface polymyography, often combined with accelerometry. Surface EMG electrodes are placed over the muscles most prominently involved in the abnormal movements, while accelerometers may be positioned to capture the associated limb or segmental displacement. These recordings allow characterization of key neurophysiological features of the jerks, including EMG burst duration, recruitment pattern, and the co-contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles. Such measures help distinguish myoclonus from other jerky hyperkinetic movements and provide important clues regarding the anatomical level of the generator [6], [29], [36], [37]. Occasionally, short myoclonic bursts (e.g., less than 50 ms) on surface EMG may be difficult to differentiate from fasciculations or myokymia, particularly when the movement amplitude is low. In such cases, needle EMG may help clarify the underlying discharge pattern and support the differential diagnosis [7].

To investigate the cortical origin of myoclonus, EEG is combined with EMG using jerk-locked back-averaging (JLBA). In this technique, EMG discharges corresponding to myoclonic jerks are identified and used as temporal triggers to average the preceding EEG activities across multiple events. When short time windows preceding the EMG discharge are analyzed—within approximately 100 ms—JLBA may reveal cortical spikes or sharp transients time-locked to the EMG discharge, which are characteristic of cortical myoclonus [6], as illustrated in Fig. 2. JLBA facilitates detection of this relationship when it is not evident on routine EEG studies. SEPs are obtained by electrical stimulation of a peripheral nerve such as the median nerve at the wrist, typically with stimuli delivered at an intensity slightly above motor threshold and at frequency of approximately 3 Hz. Cortical responses are recorded over the contralateral centroparietal EEG electrodes. In patients with cortical myoclonus, SEPs are frequently enlarged, producing “giant SEPs,” most commonly defined as when the first two components (N20-P25, P25-N30) have an amplitude higher than 10 μV [29] (Fig. 3). The similar latencies of giant SEP and normal SEP suggest that its generator source lies in the primary somatosensory cortex, as well as the primary motor cortex and supplementary motor areas [38]. Neurophysiological studies using transcranial magnetic stimulation and SEP recovery cycle paradigms suggest that the presence of giant SEPs reflect hyperexcitability (lack of inhibition) of the sensorimotor cortex [28].Fig. 3Example of median nerve somatosensory potential with amplitude >10 μV of the segment N20/P25 component.Fig. 3

In cortical myoclonus, the C-reflex can be elicited through electrical stimulation of a peripheral nerve (most commonly the median nerve at the wrist) while recording EMG activities from hand or forearm muscles. In addition to the short-latency spinal reflex responses (M-wave and H-reflex), a later EMG response occurring at approximately 40–50 ms in upper limb muscles may be observed [16]. The latency between the myoclonus-related cortical spike and the onset of the spontaneous jerk closely matches the latency between the N33 SEP component and the C-reflex response. This temporal concordance suggests that the C-reflex represents the neurophysiological correlate of cortical myoclonus and reflects abnormal sensorimotor cortical processing [4]. Overall, these findings support a key role of the sensorimotor cortex in the generation of the C-reflex. However, the precise anatomical pathway remains uncertain. It is still unclear whether the hyperexcitability underlying cortical myoclonus primarily affects the afferent, efferent, or combined afferent-efferent components of the transcortical reflex circuit [28].

Taken together, JLBA cortical spikes, giant SEPs, and C-reflex responses represent the most widely used neurophysiological markers supporting the diagnosis of cortical myoclonus, combined with a short EMG burst duration (<50 ms) [36] and co-contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles (Fig. 4). Corticomuscular coherence analysis has also gained attention, particularly in cases with high-frequency myoclonic jerks [6]. In contrast, the evaluation of non-cortical myoclonus relies heavily on EMG features, including EMG burst duration and the pattern of muscle activation spread. Along with the latency between sensory stimuli and the resulting jerk, these parameters provide important clues regarding the anatomical level of the generator in subcortical and spinal forms of myoclonus [29], [32].Fig. 4Surface EMG recording of cortical myoclonus showing very brief muscle activities with EMG burst durations of ∼30 ms, and co-contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles in the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles.Fig. 4

It is often stimulus sensitive and may include positive and negative components. Individual patients may exhibit multiple forms of cortical myoclonus simultaneously, with different activation patterns, amplitude, and localization [29]. It is not disease-specific, and it may occur in both focal cortical and diffuse brain disorders [6]. It can be present in multiple conditions, for example, progressive myoclonus epilepsy with ataxia [34], corticobasal syndrome [40], and post-anoxic myoclonus (Lance-Adams syndrome) [41]. In contrast, cortical-subcortical myoclonus refers to jerks arising from diffuse bilateral hyperexcitability involving both cortical and subcortical structures. It is typically spontaneous and diffuse, and clinically manifests as generalized seizures with a myoclonic component. Neurophysiological features include EMG bursts with a duration of approximately 50–150 ms, generalized polyspike-and-wave activity on EEG, and generalized EMG discharges that are time-locked to the EEG abnormalities [2].

Subcortical myoclonus arises from generators located below the cerebral cortex. Myoclonus-dystonia is classical example of subcortical myoclonus, possibly generated in the basal ganglia. It may present as focal or multifocal, affecting mostly the axial muscles and proximal limbs. It is usually spontaneous or action-induced, and may be associated to genetic disorders as mutations in the SGCE gene encoding ε-sarcoglycan (DYT-SCGE), Anoctamin-3 (ANO3) gene mutations, and ADCY5 gene mutations [33]. Features suggesting a subcortical origin of myoclonus include EMG burst durations typically ranging from 50 to 150 ms, absence of a cortical transient preceding the EMG burst on EEG–EMG back-averaging, and normal amplitude SEPs [29], [42].

Brainstem myoclonus (reticular myoclonus) is rare, and can also be considered as one subtypes of subcortical origin [18], [19]. It has a typical muscle activation spreading through contiguous brainstem innervated structures. It may occur spontaneously, and the EMG burst duration varies from 10 to 100 ms [29]. Exaggerated startle also has the brainstem as generator, it is always externally triggered, most commonly by sound. Neurophysiological features include a blink response preceding the muscles activation, and a EMG burst duration longer than 100 ms [20], [43].

Spinal myoclonus can be divided into propriospinal and spinal focal myoclonus. Spinal myoclonus, renamed segmental myoclonus12 [2], has focal body distribution and is thought to be caused by a loss of inhibition in spinal interneurons leading to hyperexcitability of anterior horn cells [44], affecting one to three contiguous levels of the spinal cord. It is associated with spinal lesions or spinal anesthesia. The myoclonus can be spontaneous or action induced, and stimulus sensitivity is uncommon [2], [44]. Neurophysiological features include EMG discharges localized to contiguous spinal levels, long EMG burst durations typically exceeding 100 ms (often ranging from 500 to 1000 ms), co-contraction of agonist-antagonist (most commonly rectus abdominis and paraspinal muscles), and semi-rhythmic jerks occurring at a frequency of approximately 2 Hz [2], [6].

Propriospinal myoclonus is usually spontaneous and arrhythmic. It has been proposed that it arises from a spinal generator that transmits activity slowly up and down the spinal cord via the propriospinal pathways [45]. Secondary etiologies include structural lesions that can be found in a small fraction of cases, whilst functional disorders are usually considered the most frequent underlying cause [44], [45], [46]. It predominantly involves axial muscles, and patients typically have slow-flexion truncal jerks that worsen when lying down. It may be stimulus-sensitive but with longer latency than cortical myoclonus [44]. Neurophysiological features include EMG bursts typically exceeding 100 ms in duration, a characteristic spreading pattern arising from truncal muscles with subsequent rostral and caudal propagation to adjacent segments, [46] and presence of BP in cases with functional etiology.

Peripheral myoclonus originates from lesions affecting cranial, peripheral nerves or spinal roots, such as in amputation or hemifacial spasm (facial nerve) [29]. It is usually spontaneous, irregular and does not have stimulus sensitivity. Neurophysiological features suggesting a peripheral origin of myoclonus include EMG discharges localized to muscles innervated by a specific peripheral nerve or root, variable EMG burst durations typically ranging from 50 to 100 ms, absence of stimulus sensitivity, and (semi) rhythmic muscle contractions [2].

Functional myoclonus consists of jerky movements resembling myoclonus that arise from a functional neurological disorder. Clinically, these movements tend to be slower and more complex than organic myoclonus, and when they are stimulus-sensitive, there is typically a long and variable delay between the sensory stimulus and the motor response. A useful clinical maneuver to evaluate stimulus sensitivity is to perform a series of tendon taps and occasionally stop the reflex hammer just before it contacts the tendon. In many cases, a movement may still occur despite the absence of actual contact. This phenomenon is known as the “Hallett sign.” [47], [48]. Neurophysiological features suggesting functional myoclonus include prolonged EMG burst durations (typically exceeding 200 ms), non-physiological or highly variable stimulus latencies, and variable or inconsistent muscle recruitment patterns. The presence of a BP on EEG–EMG JLBA or event-related desynchronization (ERD) (characterized by a reduction in beta-band activity preceding the movement), provides additional support for a functional origin [2].

Although different subtypes of myoclonus have distinct corresponding neurophysiological findings, each finding is not always present. Therefore, a battery of tests is needed to improve their diagnostic sensitivity. For cortical myoclonus, a recent review showed that SEPs were studied in 144 of the patients reported in the literature (63% of the whole sample), and they were enlarged in 67 subjects (46%). C-reflex or long-latency reflexes were studied in 68 subjects (29%), and abnormal responses were present in 49 subjects (72%). JLBA was studied in 138 subjects (60%) and a cortical transient was present in 70 subjects (50%) [29]. A single center retrospective study showed up to 97% of the 64 patients with cortical myoclonus evaluated showed a positive EEG correlate [49], highlighting possible heterogeneity related to the technical differences. In patients with subcortical myoclonus presenting with a myoclonus–dystonia phenotype, SEP, JLBA, and LLR studies did not show abnormal findings in most cases, and only one patient had enlarged SEPs. [29] In patients with brainstem myoclonus, a small number of recordings (eight patients) available for pooled analysis showed a typical pattern of muscle activation and the presence of a positive C-reflex in two patients. [29].

New methods to analyze myoclonus

Recently, there was a collective effort to bring more objective tools into the neurophysiological analysis of myoclonus. Two groups developed and tested two different tools to differentiate cortical myoclonus from tremor.

One group explored the use of machine learning to separate essential tremor and cortical myoclonus using standardized protocols with accelerometer recordings [50]. The model demonstrated excellent classification performance across all tested postures and dynamic tasks. The highest accuracy was achieved during the pronated outstretched arms posture with relaxed wrists, as well as with the four-finger tapping task.

Another group investigated the use of surface EMG and the power spectral analysis to differentiate cortical myoclonus from tremor disorders, including essential tremor, dystonic tremor, and orthostatic tremor. By combining five EMG-derived variables from power spectrum analysis, the authors demonstrated that these movement disorders can be distinguished with high diagnostic accuracy [51].

These analyses provide important refinements into the neurophysiological diagnosis of myoclonus. However, they are not yet ready in routine application and require further validation in broader clinical settings. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that quantitative accelerometers and EMG-based metrics may facilitate the dissemination of the use of neurophysiological tools for diagnosis of jerky movement disorders. With further refinement, such approaches have the potential to increase diagnostic reliability and feasibility, particularly by reducing the time required for interpretation and leading to more standardized assessments.

Conclusions

The study of myoclonus is an evolving field in which foundations were laid in the 19th century and have continued to develop through the coordinated efforts of multiple research groups worldwide. Although myoclonus represents a relatively uncommon phenomenology within movement disorders, it encompasses great clinical and pathophysiological complexity. This complexity has fueled ongoing scientific discussion and contributes to a broader understanding of human motor physiology.

Prof. Mark Hallett has played a pivotal role in advancing the neurophysiological understanding of myoclonus. Early in his career, working alongside Prof. C. David Marsden, he contributed to the study of post-hypoxic patients and produced seminal work describing reflex myoclonus and the brainstem origin of certain myoclonic jerks using neurophysiological approaches. These early investigations helped establish neurophysiology as a central tool for the characterization and localization of myoclonus generators.

Another major focus of Prof. Hallett's research has been functional movement disorders. As one of the founders of the Functional Neurological Disorder Society, he made profound contributions to the current understanding of these conditions. Furthermore, his work has been fundamental for the clinical and neurophysiological characterization of functional jerks, and through careful clinical observation he described key diagnostic features, including what became known as the “Hallett sign [47].” In addition, neurophysiological studies from his group [25], [52], [53] clarified the role of premotor potentials and demonstrated how tools such as EEG-EMG JLBA can reveal characteristic features of functional jerks, alongside within consistency, entrainment, and distractibility demonstrated by polymyography recordings.

His extensive body of work, in the study of myoclonus, illustrates not only the dissemination of innovative ideas but also his dedication to training fellows and collaborators who continue to advance the field. Through this legacy, the scientific narrative of myoclonus will continue to develop in the years ahead.
