Kidney transplantation and statins effects on dyslipidemia in kidney transplant recipients
Authors: Ziad Arabi, Mohammed Tawhari, Tarek Arabi, Abdulaziz Alaklabi, Waleed Fallatah, Belal Sabbah, Abdullah Alghamdi, Ahmad Alnasrullah, Areez Shafqat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Dyslipidemia is very common after kidney transplantation (KTx), and it has been reported in up to 75% of KTx recipients (KTRs). Hence, statins are commonly used to control lipid levels post-KTx. Studies examining dyslipidemia and the effects of statins in KTRs receiving tacrolimus-based protocol remain limited particularly in the Middle East. This retrospective study examined the impact of KTx on lipids in Saudi KTRs.Methods:Data from 287 KTRs from 2017 to 2020 were analyzed, comparing lipid profiles and HbA1c levels before and 12 months after KTx in those who received statins versus those who did not. The immunosuppression protocol consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and prednisolone.Results:195 (67.9%) of patients received statins during the first year post-KTx. In the present study, KTx alone led to an increase in total cholesterol (TC) by 0.31 mmol/L (P = 0.02) and low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) by 0.40 mmol/L (P < 0.01). However, despite weight gain and elevated HbA1c levels after KTx, KTx also resulted in an increase in high-density lipoproteins (HDLs) by 0.17 mmol/L (P < 0.01). Additionally, triglyceride (TG) levels decreased post-KTx. However, this change was not statistically significant. The use of statins alongside KTx improved HDL levels by 0.17 mmol/L (P < 0.01) and reduced TG by 0.34 mmol/L (P < 0.01). Statin users did not exhibit significant changes in TC or LDL levels. The mean changes in TC and LDL levels (from baseline to 12 months) were significantly different between statin users and non-users despite comparable weight gain and elevated HbA1c levels after KTx.Conclusion:In conclusion, KTx was associated with improved HDL levels despite adverse effects on weight and HbA1c levels. The addition of statins to the treatment regimen not only maintained the positive effects of KTx on HDL and TG but also protected against increases in TC and LDL levels. These findings emphasize the potential benefits of KTx on HDL and TG levels in KTRs who received a tacrolimus-based immunosuppression protocol. Statin therapy modulates the LDL and TC increase seen post KTx.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is very common after kidney transplantation (KTx), and it has been reported in 74% of KTx recipients (KTRs)[1]. The incidence of dyslipidemia peaks within the first year after KTx but remains a long-term complication in a significant proportion of patients[2]. Dyslipidemia post KTx plays a key role in the increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD)[3,4], and it may negatively impact graft function and survival[5,6]. Modification of cardiovascu-lar risk factors remains crucial to reducing the risk of CVD[7,8,9,10]. Data regarding the effects of dyslipidemia and lipid-lowering agents on CVD and graft survival remain limited. The ALERT study, a multi-center randomized controlled trial, assessed the effects of fluvastatin on cardiac outcomes in 2102 KTRs who were >30 years old[11]. KTRs in the fluvastatin group suffered significantly less cardiac deaths and non-fatal myocardial infarctions than those receiving a placebo[12]. Based on the ALERT study, KDIGO recommends the universal use of moderate-intensity statin in all KTRs age >30 years. KTRs ≤30 years should also be prescribed statins if they have multiple CV risk factors[13].

Due to the universal use of statins among eligible KTRs as per the current international guidelines[13,14], there are only scant data about the effect of KTx itself on lipid profiles in the absence of statin therapy. Additionally, since calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) are the backbone of immunosuppression protocols in KTx (except for CNI-sparing experimental protocols), the effects of KTx on dyslipidemia cannot be dissected from the effects of CNIs.

Studies examining dyslipidemia and the effects of statins in KTRs receiving tacrolimus-based protocol remain limited particularly in the Middle East. Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed the effects of KTx and statins on the lipid levels of our center.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of all adult KTRs at our center from 2017 to 2020. We included all adult (≥18 years) kidney transplant recipients at our center between 2017 and 2020 who had at least 1 year of post-transplant follow-up data. Pediatric recipients and any patients who experienced graft loss or death within 12 months post-transplant were excluded from analysis. Baseline patient demographics were collected, along with serum low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and total cholesterol (TC) levels at baseline (during the pre-KTx assessment, typically 3–6 months before KTx), and at 1 month, 6 months, and 12 months post KTx. Furthermore, data regarding baseline CVD and cardiovascular risk factors, such as blood pressure (BP), HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus (DM), and weight changes, were recorded.

The induction protocol at our center includes methylprednisolone all KTRs. Thymoglobulin or basiliximab are added based on the immunological risk. The maintenance immunosuppression protocol consists of tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and prednisolone[14]. BP is obtained by standardized clinic blood pressure measurement. For the purpose of this study, BP at the pre-KTx evaluation is considered the baseline BP and hypertension was defined as ≥140/90 mmHg as per the relevant guidelines during the defined time period of this study[15–17].

At our center, transplant nephrologists were also advised to follow relevant guidelines of dyslipidemia management of the study period[12,13,18] In line with the ALERT study[11], KTRs age ≥30 were prescribed statins and KTRs <30 with CVD risk factors were prescribed statins. Eligible KTRs are typically prescribed atorvastatin 10 mg prior to discharge if they have not been prescribed pre-KTx statin[14]. Furthermore, we halve the maximum dose of statin in KTRs to avoid drug-to-drug interactions with CNIs, such as tacrolimus; however, any changes to the statin prescriptions were ultimately at the managing nephrologists’ discretion.

Patients were categorized into two groups according to their statin usage during the first year post-KTx. The aim of this study was to assess the effects of KTx and statin usage on lipid levels during the first 12 months post-transplant. We recorded the statin dosage used by KTRs before and after transplantation, including any changes made to the prescribed dose.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (approval # RC20/382/R). The KAIMRC IRB waived the requirement of patient consent due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study was reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines[19].

Data analysis was carried out using the SPSS software version 26.0. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation while categorical variables were described as number and frequency. The independent-samples t-test, paired-samples t-test, repeated measures ANOVA, chi squared, or Fisher’s Exact tests were used. All reported P values are two-sided and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As expected, statin users were significantly older than non-statin users (48.7 ± 14.1 vs. 31.7 ± 12.3, P < 0.001). DM was also significantly more prevalent in the statin group than the non-statin group (47.2% vs. 7.6%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, coronary artery disease and cerebral vascular disease were significantly more common in statin users (P < 0.001 and P < 0.043, respectively). Baseline weight, baseline BMI, weight at 12 months, and BMI at 12 months were all significantly higher in KTRs receiving statins (P < 0.001).

When comparing the two patient groups, baseline weight and HbA1c were higher in the statins group. However, both groups gained weight and had HbA1c increase at comparable magnitudes (P = 0.227 and P = 0.603, respectively). The changes of HDL and TG were also comparable between the two groups. However, statins significantly reversed post-KTx increases in TC levels (P = 0.015) and blunted changes in LDL levels (P = 0.002).

Of KTRs 30 years or older, 80% received statins by 12 months post-KTx. Regarding the time of statin initiation, statin has already been prescribed before transplant in 79 (36.9%), statin was started by the transplant team before discharge. 60 (28.0%), statin was not started by the transplant team before discharge but during the first-year post-transplant. 33 (15.4%), and no statin was initiated during the study duration 43 (20%).

Prior to KTx, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin constituted 78% and 12% of the statin prescriptions, respectively. The pre-KTx dose was 40 mg daily for 46% and 20 mg for 33% of pre-KTx atorvastatin users. After KTx, the majority (53%) of patients maintain their pre-KTx dose, and 24% witnessed a dose reduction.

By 12 months, the used statins were atorvastatin in 186 (95.5%) at 10 mg in 111 (60%), 20 mg in 21 (33%), and 40 mg in 27 (14%); whereas rosuvastatin was used in 8 (4%), and only one patient (0.5%) received simvastatin. Statins were discontinued in seven (2.5%) patients due to elevated liver functions in four patients and other causes in threes.

Discussion

KTRs are at increased risk of dyslipidemia due to enhanced appetite, post-KTx weight gain, new onset diabetes after transplantation, and immunosuppressive agents specifically steroids and CNIs[20,21]. CNIs inhibit enzymes involved in lipid metabolism and activate lipid-producing enzymes[22] resulting in increase of TC, LDL, and TG levels and decrease of HDL levels[21]. KTx using a cyclosporine based immunosuppression protocol is associated with a characteristic pattern of dyslipidemia, with increased TC, LDL, and HDL, and a decrease in TG[2].

Tacrolimus, on the other hand, has a less adverse impact on lipids[23,24]. In fact, conversion from cyclosporine to tacrolimus can result in a −1.4 mmol/L (−16%) change in TC and a 25% reduction in LDL, as seen in a study of 65 KTRs by McCune et al[25] Consistent with these differences, our tacrolimus-based cohort showed a more favorable lipid profile than historically seen with cyclosporine. In the present study, we demonstrate comparable findings that KTx (using a tacrolimus-based protocol) increases TC, LDL, and HDL levels 1-year post-KTx. Additionally, KTRs demonstrate a numerical decrease in TG levels by 0.09 mmol/L on average, but that did not reach a statistical significance.

Our findings align with a meta-analysis of eight randomized controlled trials (mostly in cyclosporine-era KTRs) which found that statin therapy did not significantly affect HDL or TG levels, even though it reduced LDL[26]. It is worth noting that several trials in that meta-analysis used only low-intensity statins, which may partly explain the modest impact on HDL/TG.

In the current study (tacrolimus-based), statin users witnessed significant improvements in HDL and TG levels despite weight gain and increased HbA1c. Similarly, Thompson et al reported in an Australian population that KTx (mostly tacrolimus-based) increased HDL (P < 0.0001) and decreased triglyceride (P = 0.007) concentration, whereas there was no significant change in LDL and total cholesterol[27]. Interestingly these effects were dependent on maintenance of graft function (and statin therapy). If graft failure occurred then HDL again decreased, and triglycerides increased. Thompson et al concluded that KTx ameliorated alterations in plasma lipoprotein profile associated with kidney impairment, an effect that was dependent on the maintenance of graft function[27]. Of note our KTRs had an excellent graft function with creatinine of 98.9 μmol/L at 12 months post-KTx.

It is estimated that about 10% of patients receiving statins in the general population stop taking a statin because of subjective complaints, most commonly muscle symptoms without raised creatine kinase[28]. However, clinical trials have shown much less discontinuation rate. For example, the discontinuation rate in the ALERT study (cyclosporine based) was only 5% and it was comparable in both treatment arm (fluvastatin) and placebo[12]. In this study, the discontinuation rate was about 2.5%. However, that discontinuation rate might be higher since the adherence of patients to the prescribed therapy was assessed only through prescription refills.

The current KDIGO guidelines suggest usage of moderate intensity statins in all adult KTRs[13]. The recommendation is largely due to the fact that the only available randomized controlled trial studying statins in KTRs is the ALERT study[11]. In the current study, 80% of KTRs 30 years or older received statins during their first year after KTx. These findings are largely consistent with a study by Rønning et al, which assessed the patterns of statin usage in Norway[29] and 72% of Norwegian KTRs were receiving statins during their first-year post-KTx[29].

This study examined the “physiological” effects of KTx on lipids profile in KTRs receiving tacrolimus-based protocol. It also reviewed the pharmacological effect of statin on KTRs. The present study seems to be the first of its kind in the Middle Eastern population.

However, there are several limitations to the study. First, the single center nature of this study limits patient heterogeneity and generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the effects of KTx on lipids profile in patients who were not candidates for statins, may or may not be applicable to the high-risk group (who were eligible and received statins). However, the effect of KTx cannot be ethically assessed without the use of statins since the use of statins in this high-risk group is considered standard of care[13]. Due to the short follow-up time, the study was also not intended to examine the cardiovascular outcomes of the two patient groups, rather only serum lipid profiles. However, studies have revealed that statin use is associated with reduced all-cause mortality in KTRs; hence we hypothesize that a similar effect would be present in our population with prolonged statin use[30]. We also did not assess the adherence of patients to the prescribed therapy, except through prescription refills. Low adherence to statin treatments has been associated with higher LDL levels and higher risk of mortality in the general population[31]. Hence, low adherence to therapy could undermine the effects statin in our patient population. Although all our KTRs received moderate-intensity statins as per KDIGO guidelines, the use of higher intensity statin especially in a tacrolimus-based protocol could also have resulted in more aggressive control of dyslipidemia post-KTx[32]. Finally, the retrospective nature of the study may have resulted in selection bias when prescribing statins. Prospective studies would be needed to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

In our study, KTx was associated with improved HDL levels despite adverse effects on weight and HbA1c, and it did not significantly change TG levels. The addition of statins to the regimen maintained the favorable HDL changes and produced a significant reduction in TG levels, while also protecting against rises in TC and LDL.
