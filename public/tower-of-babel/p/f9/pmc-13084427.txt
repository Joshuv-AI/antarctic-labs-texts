First responder airway and compression rate cluster randomized controlled trial: rational and design

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is a leading cause of mortality, accounting for millions of deaths globally each year.1, 2, 3 Successful resuscitation is possible and requires timely response and care involving community, emergency communications, public safety, emergency medical services (EMS), and hospital providers. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is the cornerstone of resuscitation. Consequently, variation in CPR can affect the likelihood of clinical outcomes (heart resuscitation and neurological recovery).4

Among professional first responders, CPR typically incorporates chest compressions and positive pressure ventilation to support circulation and help treat hypoxia and acidosis. Current evidence underpinning guidelines underscore the understanding that the composition of CPR – including specific metrics involving chest compression rate, depth, release, and interruption – can influence its effectiveness.4 Precise compression rate in particular can be achieved using a metronome, an inexpensive and accessible means to direct CPR.5, 6 Guidelines recommend a rate range of 100–120 compressions per minute. Yet, differences in this compression rate range can affect other compression metrics. For example, higher rates increase the absolute number of compressions but may impede guideline-directed depth and release goals.7, 8 Whether there is a more targeted rate that optimizes overall compression performance is uncertain, though observational and experimental data suggests that compression metrics may cluster or interact to affect hemodynamic support.9, 10

Likewise, there is variation in ventilation, and this variation may influence prognosis, though less is known about ventilation characteristics and outcome.11, 12, 13 Guidelines recommend that rescuers provide sufficient ventilation to initiate chest rise.4 Research suggests that professional rescuers are sometimes challenged to achieve this goal, which is adversely associated with survival outcomes.11, 12 Previous randomized investigations have focused on tools used by advanced providers (paramedics and physicians), comparing tracheal intubation with supraglottic airways (SGA).14

Less is known about first responder airway tools and OHCA outcome. Conventional airway management by professional first responders (emergency medical technicians [EMTs]) often employs positive pressure mask (also described as bag-valve-mask or BVM) ventilation. Although this technique can achieve oxygenation and ventilation, mask ventilation can also produce complications related to hypo- or hyper-ventilation.15, 16, 17 These complications may occur because of patient factors related to facial and upper airway anatomy, body habitus, patient positioning, or operator issues related to inadequate skill or person-power.18

Because of such challenges with mask ventilation, SGAs are increasingly considered for first-responder airway management during OHCA. An SGA device bypasses the anatomy of the face and oral cavity, seats in the posterior pharynx where the pliable tip can potentially occlude the esophagus to decrease aspiration risk, and directs ventilation toward the trachea. Indeed, some first responder agencies use an SGA instead of a mask, observing that the SGA can be correctly placed in the majority of patients.19 There is however, no definitive evidence comparing outcomes between patients ventilated with mask versus SGA implemented by first responders, providing clinical equipoise for a randomized comparison

In summary, chest compressions and ventilations both influence arrest physiology and are associated with outcomes, yet randomized evaluation of first responder interventions involving these fundamental components of CPR are generally lacking. The First responder Airway and Compression rate Trial (FACT) will compare mask vs supraglottic ventilation for first responder airway management, and compare first responder chest compression rates (100 vs 110 vs 120 per minute) during OHCA resuscitation. In this way, the interventions are incorporated in “standard practice”, so that the study compares guideline-supported strategies of EMS CPR.

Methods

Design and hypotheses

The FACT study uses a factorial, cluster randomized cross-over adaptive trial design where each of the 28 first responder EMS agencies are randomly assigned to control (mask) or intervention (SGA) and one of the three compression rates (100 vs 110 vs 120) at the outset and then switch intervention assignments (for rate and airway) every 4 months.

Hypothesis first responder airway: We hypothesize that the proportion of patients who survive to hospital discharge will be greater for patients randomized to SGA compared to mask ventilation.

Hypothesis first responder chest compression rate: We hypothesize that proportion of patients who survive to hospital discharge will be greater for patients randomized to compression rate of 100 per minute compared to 110 or 120 per minute.

Trial registration

The FACT study is registered with ClinicalTrials.org (NCT05969028). The SPIRIT checklist is included in Appendix A.

Study setting and EMS system

The study occurs in King County Washington. King County encompasses 2000 square miles and has a population of 2.3 million persons who reside in urban, suburban, and rural settings. There are 11 cities with a population >50,000 persons, the largest being the City of Seattle.

EMS is activated by calling 9-1-1. Emergency dispatch prioritizes identification of OHCA and provides telephone CPR instructions. The EMS response is two-tier consisting of 28 first responder Fire Departments and 5 second-tier paramedic agencies. The first-tier “first responders” are EMT-firefighters trained in basic life support (BLS) and equipped with automated external defibrillators (AEDs). Prior to the FACT study, first responders used mask ventilation as the standard approach for ventilation during OHCA. The second-tier consists of paramedics trained in advanced life support (ALS) (e.g., manual defibrillation, vascular access, drug administration, tracheal intubation). Average EMS response interval from dispatch to scene arrival is 5 min for first tier and 11 min for second tier.20

The EMS system follows the American Heart Association resuscitation guidelines.21 Specifically, the first-responder agencies in greater King County (outside Seattle) provide sequenced compression to ventilation ratio of 30:2 while first responders in the City of Seattle use a compression to ventilation ratio of 10:1 using uninterrupted compressions. After intubation, EMS throughout King County use continuous compressions with a compression to ventilation ratio of 10:1. EMS providers activate a preset metronome attached to their AED to guide compression rate.

Study population

The FACT study enrolls consecutive adults who experience OHCA in Seattle and greater King County and receive attempted resuscitation by first responders. The study excludes (1) those with an advanced directive indicating no attempted resuscitation, (2) traumatic arrest, (3) those with an “opt out” bracelet, and (4) protected populations to include pediatric patients (<18 years), known prisoner, and known pregnancy.

Additional patients are specifically excluded from the airway portion of the trial. These patients include (1) those with a pre-existing tracheostomy and/or mechanical ventilation as these patients typically already have an established airway, (2) those where paramedics arrive simultaneously or before the first responder crew, and (3) those where the patient arrests after EMS arrival. In these latter two circumstances, paramedics have primary responsibility for the airway from the outset, foregoing the first responder airway intervention. These patients are still eligible for the compression rate intervention.

Trial interventions and clinical care

First responder ventilation strategy

The FACT study compares first responder mask ventilation with ventilation via an SGA. As part of study training, first responders underwent training in both mask and SGA ventilation. First responders in King County have routinely used mask ventilation as core treatment of OHCA. Mask ventilation uses a 2-hand technique whereby the provider applies the mask by tilting the chin and lifting the head with a hand on each side of the mask to support a patent airway and good seal (Fig. 1).

Mask ventilation and i-gel supraglottic airway ventilation.

Because use of an SGA was a new skill for first responders in King County, each EMT underwent specific training to perform SGA use for OHCA. The training included didactic explanation, video review of the process of placement and use in the context of EMS resuscitation, and hands-on evaluation during simulated OHCA that required successful SGA placement and ventilation using a high-fidelity manikin that supports ventilation measurement. If the SGA cannot be placed after two attempts, the mask is deployed as a rescue airway. In a subset of subjects, ventilation rate and inspiratory and expiratory ventilation volumes will be assessed using the EOLife device.

Choice of supraglottic airway. The study uses the i-gel SGA (Fig. 1). The device features a soft, gel-like non-inflatable cuff that provides an anatomically contoured fit that self-molds to the laryngeal inlet and can be rapidly placed in a manner analogous to an oral-pharyngeal device. First-responder i-gel use is appealing for practical considerations; it is simple to operate as it does not require providers inflate a cuff, has a “built-in” bite block, and is straightforward to size based on an adult patient’s sex (women size 4, men size 5).22 The i-gel can be inserted quickly and achieve adequate ventilation at least as often as other common supraglottic devices.23, 24

Compression to ventilation ratio. The compression to ventilation ratio is either synchronous 30:2 or asynchronous 10:1 before a paramedic airway and is determined by the standing practice of the BLS agency. Thus, the randomized airway assignment does not affect the compression to ventilation ratio.

Paramedic tracheal intubation. The primary approach used by paramedics for advanced airway is tracheal intubation. The study system monitors paramedic advanced airway care demonstrating a generally high level of performance involving first-pass and overall intubation success.25, 26, 27 Paramedics have protocol authorization to perform tracheal intubation regardless of first responder airway, with the goals to support compressions and limit interruptions in CPR.

Compression rate

As part of the FACT study, each OHCA is assigned a compression rate of 100, 110, or 120 compressions per minute as guided by the preset metronome attached to the first-responder AED. Upon establishing pulselessness, the first-responder team starts chest compressions and activates the metronome preset at the study rate. These three rates were chosen given they are endorsed by international experts based on review of evidence.4 The assigned compression rate is maintained throughout resuscitation to return of spontaneous circulation or death. Prior experience from the study system indicates that the metronome can successfully guide rate.5 Compression rates will be assessed through review of the defibrillator recording.

Other EMS and hospital care

FACT does not dictate other resuscitation treatments (i.e. medication administration) or hospital care though such information is collected per the Utstein template.

Randomization

Eligible adults are enrolled by first-arriving fire department personnel under emergency exception to informed consent using a cluster-randomized cross-over trial design. A cluster is comprised of a fire department agency, randomized at the agency level to perform ventilation using the mask or i-gel and compressions at 100, 110, or 120 compressions per minute for a pre-specified calendar period of 4 months, and subsequently switched to an alternate treatment for a comparable 4-month period. An EMS agency will use both airway tools over the course of an 8-month intervention period, after which they will be re-randomized to mask versus i-gel and repeat the transition to the alternate airway after 4 months of the initial airway tool. Chest compression rate will transition every 4 months such that an agency is assigned to each of the 3 compression arms over the course of 12 months. Over the course of 2 years, each agency will achieve balanced allocation by using all 6 possible combinations of compression rate and first responder airway (Fig. 2). The planned study duration is 4 years, providing for a total of twelve 4-month randomization blocks to achieve balanced intervention allocation.

Sample randomization with allocation of the 6 airway and rate combinations over 2 years.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is survival to hospital discharge, an outcome captured in nearly all patients.20 Secondary outcomes will be spontaneous circulation at the end of EMS care and survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological status as measured by the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) based on hospital record review. Spontaneous circulation at the end of EMS care provides an intermediate outcome that more directly measures heart resuscitation and provides for mechanistic understanding. The CPC requires successful heart resuscitation and additionally incorporates brain preservation and recovery. The CPC has good inter-reviewer reliability and strong prognostic association with long-term survival.28, 29 Consistent with guidelines, we classify CPC of 1 or 2 as favorable functional status.30

Data sources and data elements

The study system maintains an OHCA registry that uses the Utstein template.30 The registry collects demographic, circumstance, care, and outcome information from the dispatch recording, EMS reports, the defibrillator to include ECG, impedance, and audio recording, hospital record, paramedic airway report, and death certificates. Compression rate is assessed through review of the defibrillator recording. First responders verbalize care (captured by the defibrillator audio) to include the initial ventilations using the study airway. In a subset of cases, insufflation and exhaled volumes are directly measured using a ventilation monitoring device in black-box mode (EOLife, Archeon Inc). First responders complete an online form that reports which airway was used and any challenges. The paramedic airway report provides information about the process of tracheal intubation including the grade view, number of attempts, and challenges to intubation to help understand if first responder airway affects downstream airway management.27

Analysis plan

The detailed analysis plan is provided in Appendix B. Primary evaluation will be based on the intent-to-treat principle. A secondary analysis will compare outcomes using “as-treated” status. The primary outcome comparisons for each factor (airway and compression rate) will be assessed using generalized estimating equations to compare absolute differences in survival rates between the i-gel and mask groups and between compression rate groups while accounting for correlation within a cluster. Planned subgroup analyses include intervention comparisons according to sex, initial rhythm (shockable and non-shockable), and BLS CPR strategy (synchronous 30:2 and 10:1 asynchronous continuous).

Sample size calculations and power

The FACT study plans to enroll ∼4200 patients over the course of 4 years, ∼2100 patients in each airway arm. A prior randomized trial of advanced airways observed outcome differences of 2% (CPC) to 4% (3-day survival).31 We considered that the early, initial use of the supraglottic airway may achieve similar or more substantial outcome effects compared to mask ventilation. The study has at least 85% power to detect a 3.7% difference in survival to hospital discharge between i-gel (18.7%) and mask (15.0%) using a two-sided alpha of 0.05. For chest compression rate comparisons, we anticipate ∼1400 patients in each rate group. The study has 85% power to detect differences of 2.6% and 5.2% in survival between compression rate of 100 versus 110 and 100 versus 120 using an alpha level of 0.05 for a two-degree of freedom test, assuming a survival rate of 15% in the 110-mask group.

Formal interim monitoring/potential adaptation

The study is reviewed by an independent Data Safety Monitoring Board before study onset and annually thereafter. We plan a single formal interim analysis after about half of the patients have been enrolled. The interim analysis will use prespecified formal stopping rules for superiority and futility. Based on the interim results, the DSMB may recommend dropping one of the compression rate arms.

Ethics, regulatory oversight, and safety

FACT is conducted as an exception from informed consent trial. FACT was approved by the oversight review board on 11/14/2022 (Study ID 00014110). An investigational device exemption was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration on 10/13/2023 (Application G230254). The independent DSMB convened to review the study prior to onset on 12/14/2023. Because the study compares resuscitation strategies supported by guidelines, the primary safety assessment will evaluate airway complications using the established process of airway review.27 The study is currently enrolling subjects with the DSMB scheduled to convene the planned interim midway analysis later in 2026.

Discussion

The FACT study aims to inform CPR best practices by professional EMT first responders. Using a factorial, cluster randomized design, the trial will evaluate if the i-gel SGA improves survival compared to mask ventilation and whether compression rate of 100 per minute improves survival compared to 110 or 120 per minute. Ventilation and chest compressions are fundamental interventions that support circulation and oxygen delivery, yet the optimal strategy for professional first responders is determined in large part by observational inference.

The study interventions were chosen in part because they are fundamental and relevant to many OHCA resuscitations. There is evidence that these basic strategies are associated with potentially important differential physiological effects.4 The interventions have clinical equipoise given their current-day use and lack of definitive comparative evidence. Moreover, the airway and compression rate interventions are supported by resuscitation guidelines so that none of the arms is considered experimental.21 Hence the FACT study uses a rigorous prospective design to compare the relative effectiveness of standard-of-care practices. The standard-of-care comparisons helped to support ethics and regulatory review and approval, though the study is still being conducted under exception from informed consent.

One interesting opportunity offered by FACT is the ability to evaluate these formative interventions using two distinct BLS-phase CPR strategies as the City of Seattle uses continuous compressions with interposed breaths at 10:1 and greater King County uses synchronous 30:2 during the BLS phase. A planned secondary analysis will examine intervention outcomes for 10:1 versus 30:2 strategies. FACT will also work to advance mechanistic understanding by assessing insufflation and exhaled volumes in a subset of patients. Such information may be able to help explain any observed outcome differences, especially those attributable to excessive leak. For both airway and compression rate, there are efforts to assess intervention compliance through provider attestation and review of the defibrillator audio and impedance channel, the latter providing objective assessment of compression rate.

The FACT study has limitations. EMS providers cannot be blinded to intervention assignments, though hospital providers and study coordinators abstracting clinical outcomes do not know intervention status. Although the study captures some mechanistic information, we will only achieve this for ventilation volumes in a modest subset given cost and operational limitations. We did not direct specific changes to paramedic practices around intubation, a skill generally practiced at a high level in the study system. There may be variation in use of intubation that depends upon the randomized airway strategy, patient characteristics, or paramedic preference. The study will be able to objectively assess compression rate but generally will not be able to measure other compression characteristics (release, depth) and has no ready means to measure blood flow during CPR. The study occurs in a large metropolitan EMS system with a record of prehospital research. The circumstance enables efficient operational implementation and trial conduct though potentially limits generalizability.

The FACT study evaluates strategies of first responder CPR ventilation and chest compressions to help inform formative resuscitation care. The results may help refine optimal strategies to improve OHCA survival and provide understanding that can advance resuscitation science.
