Nanoscale Structures of Tough Microparticle-Based Films Investigated by Synchrotron X-Ray Scattering and All-Atom Molecular-Dynamics Simulation
Authors: Keita Namba, Yuma Sasaki, Yuto Kawamura, Shotaro Yoshida, Yoshiki Hieda, Kazushi Fujimoto, Natsuki Watanabe, Yuichiro Nishizawa, Takayuki Uchihashi, Daisuke Suzuki, Takuma Kureha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, the nanoscale structures of microparticle-based films are revealed by synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and all-atom molecular-dynamics (AA-MD) simulations. The microparticle-based films consisting of the simplest acrylate polymer microparticles are applied as a model because the films are formed without additives and organic solvents and exhibit high toughness properties. The characteristic interfacial thickness (tinter) obtained from the SAXS analysis reflects the mixing degree of polymer chains on the microparticle surface in the film. The cross-linking density of inner microparticles is found to be strongly correlated to not only several properties of individual microparticles, such as swelling ratio and radius of gyration, but also the tinter and toughness of the corresponding films. Therefore, the tinter and toughness values follow a linear relationship because the cross-linking restricts the mixing of polymer chains between their surfaces in the film, which is a unique feature of microparticle-based films. This characteristic also affects their deformation behavior observed by in situ SAXS during tensile testing and their density profiles calculated by AA-MD simulations. This work provides a general strategy for material design to control the physical properties and structures of their films for advanced applications, including volatile organic compound-free sustainable coatings and adhesives.

Introduction

During evaporation of the solvent from polymer-microparticle dispersions, microparticle-based films are obtained due to microparticle deformation and interpenetration.1−4 Such microparticle-based films are widely used in diverse applications, including paints, coatings, and adhesives.5−7 In particular, waterborne microparticle-based films attract considerable attention because the film-forming process has a low environmental impact and is safe for humans when the solvent of the microparticle dispersion is water.8−10 For example, the microparticles are usually synthesized in an aqueous system, even when using hydrophobic polymers, which are often synthesized by solution polymerization using organic solvents or bulk polymerization where heat of polymerization is a concern. Thus, microparticles are generally regarded as environmentally friendly materials and are of crucial importance in the polymer industry. However, polymer microparticles with a low glass-transition temperature (Tg) usually afford films with poor mechanical strength, which hinders their practical applications.11 Several strategies can be adopted to improve the mechanical properties of such films, including the addition of additives, postpolymerization modifications, hot pressing, or annealing. Unfortunately, these approaches are usually accompanied by complicated fabrication steps and reduced chemical safety.11−14

Our groups have previously developed mechanically tough microparticle-based films that are formed by merely evaporating water from dispersions.15−20 Especially, poly(methyl acrylate) (pMA)-microparticle-based films with high toughness properties were obtained by controlling the interparticle physical cross-linking.19 In all cases, the key factor to achieving high toughness is the interpenetration at the interfaces between the microparticles, where the polymer chains on the particle surface diffuse to neighboring particle surfaces. To characterize the interpenetration between microparticles, synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) has been used as a powerful tool for the characterization of nanoscale structures with colloidal dimensions (1–100 nm). For microparticle-based films, SAXS measurements have been applied to obtain qualitative information about the microparticle packing structures within the films in colloidal crystalline structures.21−26 In this context, we have developed a universal analytical method to qualitatively calculate the degree of mixing of polymer chains on the microparticle surface, i.e., the characteristic interfacial thickness (tinter).17−20 This method has already been applied to several microparticle-based films, demonstrating that their mechanical properties can be effectively interpreted in terms of tinter.

In one of our previous studies, the SAXS profiles of pMA-microparticle films did not exhibit Bragg peaks derived from face-centered cubic (fcc) colloidal crystals, which are usually observed in SAXS profiles of conventional colloidal films,21−26 suggesting that the pMA microparticles were highly deformed and coalesced with each other in the films. Moreover, the tinter of the pMA microparticle films was higher than those of other microparticle films,19 such as poly(butyl acrylate-co-methyl methacrylate) and poly(ethyl acrylate-co-methyl methacrylate).17,18,20 These results suggested that the pMA chains on the microparticle surfaces are deeply interpenetrated with each other in these films. However, the pMA microparticles were slightly cross-linked with a cross-linker molar ratio of 0.1 mol %, indicating that the pMA chains easily interpenetrate. In order to examine the origin and mechanism of tough microparticle-based films in detail, a systematical and comprehensive study is required for a series of pMA-microparticle films as a model system. We hypothesized that the mechanical properties of pMA-microparticle films could be tuned by adjusting the cross-linking density due to the promotion or suppression of interpenetration between microparticles. Cross-linking is essential in many polymeric materials. In fact, since the discovery of the vulcanization of natural rubber,27,28 the cross-linking of polymers has been extensively studied and has greatly contributed to the development of materials science and industrial areas pertaining to rubber, tires, gels, and resins.29−31

So far, the cross-linking effect on the microparticle films consisting of poly(butyl methacrylate-co-butyl acrylate) was investigated by tensile test and fluorescent energy-transfer measurements, which reveal the polymer diffusion between the particles.32 Even when the films were annealed to increase the mechanical properties, the cross-linking caused the limitation of the extent of polymer diffusion, which suppressed improving mechanical properties. In another work, poly(butyl(meth)acrylate) microparticle films were also investigated by tensile tests and small-angle neutron scattering (SANS), which calculate the penetration depth of the coalescence between polymer chains of adjacent particles.33 Although the SANS experiment was applied to only the non-cross-linked microparticle films, they concluded that the highly cross-linked particles cannot perform any significant interdiffusion. Based on these studies, we assume that the cross-linking of pMA microparticles can be expected to affect their physicochemical properties, such as deformation and interpenetration behavior. In addition to these previous studies in the 90s, in the present study, combining the pure and tough model system (i.e., homo pMA microparticle-based films) with our bespoke techniques could provide design guidelines and insight into the origin of toughness.

To this aim, we have synthesized in this study a series of pMA microparticles with different cross-linking densities and performed SAXS measurements to determine the degree of mixing of the pMA chains on the microparticles in the waterborne films. In addition to typical SAXS measurements and mechanical tests, we developed a novel all-atom molecular-dynamic [all-atom molecular-dynamics (AA-MD)] simulation for microparticle systems. The AA-MD simulation allows obtaining the dynamics and density profiles on the atomic scale from the particle center to the surface to reveal the cross-linking effects in detail. Furthermore, we measured stretched pMA-microparticle films by in situ SAXS. Although SAXS experiments have been applied to the other particle films, such as poly(styrene-co-butyl acrylate),34,35 there are, to the best of our knowledge, no reports on the relationship between the nanoscale structures and mechanical properties of the microparticle films during deformation. Herein, we can control the mechanical properties and nanoscale structures of microparticle-based films by adjusting the cross-linking density. The cross-linking effects are discussed systematically for the first time from the viewpoint of not only the physical properties of individual microparticles, but also the unique characteristics of their films, including the interpenetration behavior, molecular dynamics (MD), mechanical properties, and deformation mechanism.

Experimental Procedures

Materials

Methyl acrylate (MA, >98.0%), potassium peroxodisulfate (KPS, 95%), 1,6-hexanediol dimethacrylate (HDD, >98%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, >99.5%), and ethanol (99.5%) were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Japan) and used as received. Sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (DBS, >95.0%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Japan) and used as received. Deionized water was prepared using an EYELA, SA-2100E1 system (Japan).

Synthesis of pMA Microparticles

pMA microparticles with different cross-linking densities were prepared via emulsion polymerization. A mixture of DBS (0.07 g) and MA and the HDD cross-linker in various proportions (for details, see Table 1) was dissolved in 195 mL water and poured into a three-neck round-bottom flask (300 mL) equipped with a mechanical stirrer, a condenser, and a N2 gas inlet. The resulting emulsion was stirred (300 rpm; 70 °C) in a temperature-controlled oil bath and sparged (N2; 30 min) to remove any oxygen. Thereafter, the N2 sparging was changed to a N2 flow, and a solution of the KPS initiator (0.11 g) in water (5 mL) was added to the suspension to start the polymerization. After 4 h, the obtained pMA-microparticle dispersion was cooled to room temperature and purified by dialysis for 1 week, whereby the water was changed on a daily basis. It should be noted here that the pMA-H0.1, pMA-H1, and pMA-H5 microparticles displayed in Table 1 had already been synthesized and used in a previous study.19

Preparation and Conditioning of pMA-Microparticle-Based Films

To prepare the microparticle-based films for the mechanical and scattering experiments, a pMA-microparticle dispersion (ca. 10 wt %) was poured into a silicone-rubber mold and dried at 25 °C for 24 h in a temperature-controlled incubator (LTI-2100, EYELA, Japan). The solid weight of the microparticle-based films was fixed at 24 mg/cm2 (for mechanical tests: 3.5 cm × 3.5 cm; for scattering tests: 1 cm × 1 cm). Samples for the tensile tests were cut from a microparticle-based film using a dumbbell-shaped cutter (ISO37-4).

Characterization of pMA Microparticles and Films

Dynamic-light-scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a DLS-8000 instrument (Otsuka Electronics Co., Ltd., Japan) with a He–Ne laser (633 nm) at a scattering angle (θ) of 90°. The time-average intensity correlation function was obtained with a time duration of 180 s. For the DLS measurements, the concentration of the tested microparticles was 0.01 wt %. We have previously reported that the pMA microparticles can be assembled and disassembled with the aid of a suitable solvent, such as DMF or aqueous ethanol, because the microparticles are solvated and swollen by good solvents.19 Thus, to evaluate the cross-linking density in terms of the swelling ratio and morphology, DMF was selected. The swelling ratio was determined using the following equation, where the hydrodynamic radius (Rh) was measured in DMF or water, respectively.1

Static-light-scattering (SLS) measurements were also performed at 25 °C using the DLS-8000 instrument (Otsuka Electronics Co., Ltd., Japan) with a He–Ne laser (633 nm). The scattering angle was changed in increments of 3° from 40 to 120°. The SLS data are provided as averages of three runs of the intensity autocorrelation (each measurement lasted 120 s). For the SLS analysis of the particles dispersed in DMF, sufficient SLS intensity could not be detected at a concentration of 0.01 wt %. Thus, the microparticle concentrations in the water and DMF dispersions were set to 0.01 and 0.1 wt %, respectively.

The zeta potentials of the microparticles were measured by electrophoretic light scattering using an ELSZ2000 instrument (Otsuka Electrons Co., Ltd., Japan). The electrophoretic mobility of the microparticles was analyzed using the Smoluchowski equation. The dispersions used for the measurements were the same as those used for the DLS measurements. Before the measurements, the dispersions were allowed to stand at 25 °C for 600 s.

The microparticle-based films were subjected to Soxhlet extraction to measure the gel content (GC) of the pMA microparticles. To completely disintegrate the microparticle-based films into single particles, the films were dissolved in toluene for 12 h prior to the extraction. In order to dissolve the non-cross-linked free polymer and calculate the GC, the Soxhlet extraction was allowed to proceed for 10 h at 170 °C. To trap the cross-linked gel fraction, i.e., the insoluble components, of microparticles, a cellulose fiber filter (Thimble Filter No. 84, Advantec, 8 μm in retained particle size) was used. Toluene was removed from the soluble fraction using a Buchi R-300 evaporator (Switzerland) and dried in air. The GC was determined using the following equation, where w1 and w2 represent the weight of the gel (insoluble) fraction and the weight of the original film, respectively.2

The Tg was measured by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; DSC250, TA Instruments, USA) using 25 mg of freeze-dried (FDU-1200, Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Japan) microparticles packed into an aluminum pan. The empty aluminum pan was used as the reference. The measurements were conducted under a flow of N2 gas (60 mL/min) at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min from −60 to 100 °C. The Tg was defined as the crossing point of the intersection of the tangent line at the inflection point and the baseline of the heat flow curve (second heating).

The size and morphology of individual pMA microparticles were evaluated using atomic force microscopy (AFM). For the sample preparation, microparticle-containing aqueous dispersions (0.1 wt %) were mixed with the same volume of ethanol. Then, the resulting mixture (200 μL) was spread on the surface of water (depth: ∼1 cm) in a glass Petri dish (diameter: 5 cm) to adsorb the microparticles at the air/water interface. After standing for 1 min to allow the ethanol to evaporate, a glass substrate (18 mm × 18 mm; Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Japan) was inserted vertically into the water surface and the glass substrate was slowly lifted. Using this method, microparticles are transferred to the substrate in intervals.36 For imaging, a microcantilever (SI-DF20; spring constant: 15 N/m; tip diameter: 20 nm; Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Japan) was used. Prior to the experiment, the glass substrates were washed with detergent and deionized water. To analyze the microparticle size, the full width at half width (fwhm) was measured from the height profile using a NanoNavi Station (Hitachi High-20 Tech Science Corp., Japan).

For the characterization of the mechanical properties of the microparticle-based films, tensile tests were performed at 25 °C using a testing machine (tensilon RTC1250A, A&D Company, Japan) equipped with a 50 N load cell at an elongation rate of 10 mm/min. The fracture energy for pMA-microparticle-based films without thermal annealing was calculated from the stress (σ)–strain (ε) curves according to the following equation. The ε (=(l – l0)/12 × 100) was calculated using l and l0, which mean the distance between the displacement from the initial state and the initial length between the crossheads, respectively.3

For the measurements of mechanical properties of pMA-microparticle-based films with thermal annealing, the distance between crosshead was set to 5 mm. Hence, the extension ratio (λ = (l/5) + 1) and fracture energy for annealed films were determined as follows.4

The microparticle-based film surfaces were observed using a laboratory-built tip-scan atomic force microscope (TS-AFM).37 All measurements were performed in tapping mode with a wide scanner (SFS-120XY, Sigmakoki, Japan) and a HQ/NSC14/Al BS cantilever (spring constant: 5 N/m; tip radius <8 nm; resonance frequency: ∼160 kHz; MikroMasch, Estonia), which was oscillated near its resonance frequency by an excitation piezo glued on the cantilever holder. The displacement of the cantilever was detected using the optical lever method (laser wavelength: 680 nm), and its free oscillation amplitude was approximately 20 nm. The microparticle-based films were cut to 4 mm × 8 mm and fixed on a sample stage with a clamp. The imaging was conducted at 150 pixels ×150 pixels, and the obtained height images were subjected to background subtraction using a rolling-ball filter (radius: 100 nm).

The microscale structures of the pMA-microparticle films were measured by SAXS. To determine the interfacial thickness of static film samples, SAXS measurements were performed at the BL6A beamline of the Photon Factory (Japan). The wavelength of the X-rays and the sample-to-detector distance (SDD) were 1.0 Å and 2.5 m, respectively. The films were attached to the sample holder using Kapton tape and exposed to the X-ray beam for 45 s. Since the scattering is isotropic with respect to all radial directions, two-dimensional (2D) images were circularly integrated using FIT2D in order to obtain one-dimensional (1D) SAXS profiles. To characterize the deformation behavior of the pMA-microparticle films, the BL03XU beamline of SPring-8 (Japan) was used with a tensile tester (UNI-156-L, ISLAND INDUSTRY CO., Ltd.). The film sample was mounted onto the tensile tester at the beamline at a SDD of 4.0 m. The samples were stretched with a speed of 10 mm/min at both ends, ensuring that the X-rays (1.0 Å) were exposed to a fixed position in the middle of the sample. Each SAXS pattern was collected within 45 s after the film was stretched to an arbitrary strain. The SAXS data were calibrated for background scattering and normalized with respect to the primary beam intensity.

Molecular Dynamics Calculations

The polymer particles for MD calculations were modeled using 30 of the 580-mer pMA, which is five times longer than the molecular weight between entanglement points. To effectively represent the cross-linking within these polymers, a constraint was imposed on the spatial distance between cross-linked oxygen atoms (Figure 1a). This constraint was implemented as a harmonic function with a fixed length of 0.85 nm. The harmonic constant for this constraint was set to 25,000 kJ/nm2. Four different systems were prepared to explore the effects of varying cross-linking densities, which were set to 0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 5%. For each system, MD calculations were performed at a high temperature of 600 K for a period of 20 ns. Following the calculations, the pMA microparticles were subjected to a controlled cooling process involving a gradual temperature reduction from 600 to 300 K at a rate of 20 K/20 ns.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of pMA Microparticles

For the SAXS measurements, we prepared pMA microparticles using different amounts of HDD cross-linker in the polymerization. The obtained samples are denoted as pMA-HX, where H represents the HDD cross-linker and X refers to the molar feed ratio of the HDD (Table 1 and Figure 1a). Different from the previous studies,32,33 where the cross-linking effects on the particle films were investigated, we also evaluated the physical properties of individual microparticles depending on cross-linking density. The microparticles exhibit similar Rh values in water because they were prepared via emulsion polymerization under the same conditions of monomer and initiator concentrations (Table 1). The coefficient of variation (CV) was below ∼14% according to the AFM images, indicating that uniformly sized pMA microparticles were successfully prepared by changing the HDD molar ratio (Figure 1b). The dried elastomer particles were usually deformed on the substrate, and thus the particle height, which refers to the difference in height between the substrate surface and the top of the particle, was lower than the fwhm values (Figure 1c). The deformation degree was defined as the value obtained by dividing Rh,water by the height of a pMA microparticle adsorbed on the substrate (Figure 1d). The deformation degree of pMA microparticles decreases with increasing HDD amount, suggesting that the cross-linking density increases the stiffness of individual pMA microparticles. This interpretation is supported by other parameters, such as the swelling ratio, Tg, and GC, which were determined by DLS using eq 1, DSC (Figure S1a), and the Soxhlet extraction method, respectively. With increasing molar ratio of HDD, the swelling ratio decreases (Figure 1e) and the Tg and GC values increase (Table 1). It should also be noted here that the GC does not reflect the absolute degree of cross-linking because small cross-linked polymers may also pass through the filter (retained particle size: 8 μm) used in this study during extraction, and thus it should be recognized as a qualitative indicator. The pMA-H0 microparticles, which were prepared without HDD, were dissolved in DMF and became polymer chains with a number-average molecular weight of 905 × 103 g/mol (Figure S1b).

The cross-linking effects on the shape of the pMA microparticles dispersed in water and DMF as poor and good solvents, respectively, were characterized by SLS. As shown in Figure 1g, the SLS profiles, which are described by P(q,rigid) or P(q,fuzzy), differ between water and DMF.42−44 The fitting equations are described in the Supporting Information and the fitting parameters are summarized in Table S1. When the pMA microparticles were dispersed in water, their SLS profiles were fitted by P(q,rigid) and the obtained values for the radius of gyration (Rg) values were similar (Figure 1f). Conversely, in DMF, the SLS profiles were well-fitted by P(q,fuzzy), which is usually applied to microgels swollen in good solvents,42−44 indicating that the pMA microparticles are swollen in DMF and their surfaces became fuzzy and vague. Indeed, the SLS curves of pMA-microparticle dispersions were significantly different for water and DMF in the low HDD region (up to 4 mol %), suggesting that the size and morphology of pMA microparticles differed between water and DMF. In particular, the SLS intensity of the microparticles in DMF was lower than that in water at any q range measured, despite the fact that the microparticle concentration in DMF was 10 times higher than that in water. However, the SLS curves of pMA-microparticle dispersions in water and DMF became closer with increasing HDD molar ratio. The Rg of the pMA microparticles in DMF was also similar to that in water, suggesting that their cross-linking densities increase with increasing HDD molar ratio, suppressing the swelling of pMA microparticles. In the case of the pMA-H8 and pMA-H10 microparticles, these parameters were similar to those of the pMA-H5 microparticles, suggests that the HDD cross-linker does not act as an effective cross-linking point (for details, vide infra). The zeta potentials of the pMA microparticles were also similar (ca. −40 mV), indicating that the microparticle surfaces were negatively charged by the sulfonate-acid residue of the KPS initiator, which causes the electron-density contrast in the SAXS measurements.17

Nanoscale Structures of pMA-Microparticle-Based Films Revealed by SAXS

As shown in Figures 2a and S2, the pMA-microparticle-based films were homogeneous without wrinkles or uneven coloration. Before the SAXS measurements, the morphology of the pMA-microparticle-based films was also examined via TS-AFM (Figures 2b and S3a–j). In the films, the microparticles were ordered and retained their original spherical shape regardless of the cross-linking density. In contrast, the cross-section height profiles of the film surface depended on the cross-linking density of the pMA microparticles. Thus, the depth between adjacent microparticles increased with increasing cross-linker molar ratio up to 5 mol % (Figures 2b and S3k), suggesting that the cross-linking restricted the deformation and fusion between pMA microparticles.

Next, the microscopic structures of the static pMA-microparticle-based films were characterized via SAXS using synchrotron X-rays. The corresponding SAXS profiles are displayed in Figure 2c. In the case of highly cross-linked pMA-microparticle films such as pMA-H5, the SAXS profiles showed peaks in the range of the measured scattering vector (q). Usually, the peaks reflect the ordered structure of the microparticles in a film, and the Bragg peaks are derived from fcc colloidal crystals.17,18,20−26 In fact, the peak positions (q*) of the films were ∼0.009 Å–1. The distance between the microparticles calculated using Bragg’s equation (d = 2π/q*) was ∼70 nm, which is consistent with the Rh (Table 1) and the distance between the microparticles on the film surface observed by AFM (Figure S3l). However, in the case of slightly cross-linked pMA microparticle films, i.e., those containing less than 5 mol % of HDD, the characteristic peak was not observed in the SAXS profiles even when the particle size was similar to that of the pMA microparticles. These results suggest that pMA microparticles with low cross-linking density may be highly deformable and fuse to each other, while pMA microparticles with high cross-linking density were ordered in the films (Figure 2b).

To quantitatively analyze the mixing degree of pMA chains on the microparticles in the films, we applied an analysis method previously used for the tinter of block copolymer films to these microparticle-based films.45−47 We developed a model for the trapezoidal electron-density variation in the domain structure (Figure 2d). We assumed that two different electron densities exist in the microparticle film, which is caused by the surface-localized charged groups due to the residues of the initiator, i.e., the relatively heavy element sulfur with high electron density. According to the mechanism of emulsion polymerization of water-immiscible monomers,48 the residues of water-soluble initiators are localized near the microparticle surfaces. Thus, for the microparticle core, we assumed a low-density matrix, whereas the mixing area between the charged groups localized at the particle surface can be expected to exhibit high electron density. In other words, the delocalization of charged groups occurs upon mixing polymer chains on the microparticle surface during film formation.17−20 According to Ruland’s theory for two-phase materials with diffuse boundaries,49 the domain-boundary thickness can be determined using Porod’s law by analyzing the deviation of the scattering curves at high q, where the intensity is expressed by5where C is a constant and σ represents the diffuseness of the boundary. Negative values were obtained for the slopes of the pMA microparticle films (Figure 2e), indicating the presence of two different electron densities, i.e., a scattering contribution from tinter. It should be noted here that for an ideal two-phase structure, i.e., the presence of a clear interface, a plot with no slope would be expected. Figure 2f shows the tinter of the pMA microparticle films as a function of the HDD molar ratio. The tinter value decreased with increasing HDD molar ratio, which suggests that cross-linking hinders the diffusion of the pMA chains on the microparticle surface during film formation. This trend is consistent with various parameters of an individual microparticle, such as the swelling ratio, Tg, and GC (Figure 1d and Table 1), as well as the AFM images of the film surface (Figure 2b). It should also be noted that tinter increased slightly in the HDD region of 0–0.5 mol %. However, this is within the fitting-error range and thus not considered a significant difference. Indeed, the other parameters, such as the deformation degree and swelling ratio, were also almost identical.

To confirm the cross-linking effect on the mixing of pMA chains between the particle surfaces, tensile tests were conducted, and the mechanical properties were compared with the tinter values. Figure 3a shows the stress–strain curves of the pMA-microparticle films, and the corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 2. As expected, the fracture strain decreased and the Young’s modulus increased upon increasing the HDD molar ratio up to 5 mol %, resulting in a decrease in fracture energy, i.e., toughness. It is well-known that in bulk polymer film systems, the cross-linking makes the polymeric film hard and brittle.50,51 A similar phenomenon has been observed in this microparticle-based film system. However, in contrast to bulk polymer films, the mechanical properties of microparticle-based films were strongly affected by the tinter values. Specifically, the fracture energy increased linearly with increasing tinter (Figure 3b), except for the pMA-H8 and pMA-H10 films (vide infra). In other words, mixing the pMA chains on the microparticle surface considerably affects the mechanical properties of the microparticle-based films. For example, high cross-linking density near the particle surface seems to inhibit interparticle diffusion of the polymer chains, leading to a decrease in the fracture strain (e.g., ∼880% for pMA-H1 and ∼30% for pMA-H5). Notably, various parameters of the pMA-H8 and pMA-H10 microparticles, including the swelling ratio, Rg in DMF, Tg, and GC, are independent of the HDD molar ratio, which suggests that HDD does not act as an effective cross-linker in such microparticles. Even in film systems, the mechanical properties, including the Young’s modulus, fracture strain, and fracture energy, are also independent of the HDD molar ratio. Therefore, there is no correlation with tinter (Figure 3b). There was concern that HDD might not have been properly incorporated into the microparticles during polymerization, however, no aggregates were observed after the polymerization of the pMA-H8 and pMA-H10 microparticles, as in the case of other pMA microparticles. The yield was over 10 wt %, which is similar to that obtained when using a low molar ratio of HDD. Thus, we inferred that the difference in reactivity between methacrylate and acrylate52,53 causes no correlation between the molar ratio of HDD and the several parameters obtained from the microparticles and their films. Especially, the fuzzy layer (σsurf), which represents the width of the smeared particle surface, of pMA-H8 and pMA-H10 in DMF was thicker than that of the pMA-H5 microparticles (Table S1), suggesting that HDD molecules tend to cluster around the particle core during polymerization. In addition, the acrylate moiety is more water-soluble than the methacrylate moiety, which further enhances this trend. As observed in this study, pMA-rich shells are more likely to form good films.

So far, SANS and fluorescence techniques have been used to investigate the diffusion of polymer molecules across the interface between adjacent microparticles in films. In fluorescence studies, fluorescence-energy-transfer experiments reveal the polymer diffusion across the particle–particle interface, where one particle is labeled with a donor dye and the other with an acceptor. The effective length of interfacial contacts between polymers, where energy transfer occurs (δET), can thus be calculated. Even though the microparticle-based films were different from those in the present study (i.e., polymer species, drying conditions, and the presence of a dye), δET was of the same order of magnitude as tinter (∼3 nm).32,54−56 In SANS experiments, the interdiffusion behavior is studied by measuring particle blends made from deuterated and protonated particles.33,57−61 The penetration depth of the coalescence between polymer chains of adjacent particles can be calculated using the Rg of the particles in the film. The tinter value was also close to the penetration depth (e.g., 2–5 nm), which should be at least 3–3.5 nm to contribute effectively to the tensile strength of polystyrene latex films.59 In the pMA-microparticle films, the tinter value seems to be effective for the mechanical strength only when tinter values become ∼3.5 nm or more, resulting in fracture-energy values over ∼50 MJ/m3.

We found that the cross-linking remarkably affected the penetration between pMA-microparticle interfaces in films and their mechanical properties for a HDD cross-linker content of up to 5 mol %. To further explore these cross-linking effects, we developed an AA-MD simulation for particle systems. Figure 3c shows a representative snapshot of a pMA microparticle calculated by AA-MD. The cross-linker molar ratio was changed to 0, 0.1, 1, 5 mol % in order to reproduce the pMA-H0, pMA-H0.1, pMA-H1, and pMA-H5 microparticles. The cross-linker-content dependence of Tg determined by the controlled cooling process showed a trend similar to that obtained from DSC (Supporting Information Figure 5a). Meanwhile, the MD-calculated fluctuations also depended on the cross-linker molar ratio above the Tg (480 K), suggesting that the cross-linking inside the pMA microparticles worked effectively in the MD calculation system ((Supporting Information Figure 5b). In particular, the density profiles of the pMA microparticles above Tg, where the corresponding films were prepared and characterized in this study, exhibited two characteristic features (Figure 3d). First, the density near the pMA-microparticle surface gradually decreased. Similar to the SAXS results, two density regions were observed, i.e., the particle core with a constant density and the particle surface, where the density is decreased compared to that of the core. Second, the particle-surface region became narrower in the case of the pMA-H5 microparticles. The cross-linking increased the pMA density on the particle surface and hindered the pMA-chain diffusion. This would explain why the mixing of pMA chains on the particle surface is suppressed with increasing cross-linking density up to 5 mol %, resulting in a decrease in tinter values and mechanical properties.

Thermal Annealing Effect on the pMA-Microparticle-Based Films

The present highly cross-linked pMA-microparticle films exhibit hard and brittle properties. Considering that annealing usually increases the fracture energy of microparticle-based films even without additives because it promotes the interpenetration between the microparticle surfaces,17,18,33,60−62 the pMA-H5-microparticle films, which showed low tinter and toughness values due to the high cross-linking, were annealed at 70 °C for arbitrary periods of time. Figure 4a shows the SAXS profiles of pMA-H5-microparticle films annealed for different periods of time. Similar to slightly cross-linked pMA-microparticle films such as pMA-H0.1, the interference peak between microparticles disappear with increasing annealing time, suggesting that annealing promotes the fusion of pMA chains on the microparticles. Therefore, the mechanical properties also depend on the annealing time (Figure 4b). Especially, Young’s modulus increases with increasing annealing time, suggesting that the physical cross-linking density, i.e., the mixing of pMA chains between the microparticle surfaces, is increased (Table S2). As a result, the toughness, i.e., fracture energy, increases slightly below an annealing time of 24 h. In contrast, the toughness increases substantially after 24 h of annealing, and the corresponding peak was not observed in the SAXS profile. In fact, the relationship between tinter and the fracture energy shows an inflection point around an annealing time of 24 h (Figure 4c). The polymer diffusion in entangled melts usually occurs via reptation, which means that a chain molecule diffuses curvilinearly along its contour.63,64 The microparticle surface loses its memory concerning its position in space upon reptation, which affords a pseudotwo-phase system in a manner similar to the junction diffusion of block copolymers (Figure S4b).65,66 In the case of the AA-MD simulation, with increasing annealing temperature, the density and fluctuation of pMA microparticles decreases and increases, respectively (Figure S5). Thus, annealing enhances the mixing of pMA chains on the microparticles, and the annealing effect can be expressed in terms of the SAXS profiles related to tinter. In particular, the stress–extension ratio curves of pMA-H5-microparticle films annealed for more than 24 h exhibited a clear yield point. Thus, the polymer mixing promoted by annealing may influence the yield stress.

Nanoscale Structures of Stretched pMA-Microparticle-Based Films Revealed by in situ SAXS

Finally, to investigate the cross-linking effects on the deformation behavior of pMA-microparticle films, in situ SAXS measurements were performed on slightly and highly cross-linked pMA-microparticle films under stretching. In the previous studies, the deformation behavior of the microparticle films was evaluated only by tensile tests32,33 or by SAXS,34,35 however, our study is the first to carry out both mechanical testing and in situ SAXS for their deformation behavior. In particular, since the effect of cross-linking on the deformation behavior just before film fracture was unclear, it was revealed using the model pMA system. In the pMA-H0.1-microparticle films, the diffraction rings in the 2D SAXS patterns gradually became ellipses with increasing strain up to 800% with the short axis in the stretching direction (Figure 5a). Conversely, in the highly cross-linked pMA-H5-microparticle films, which exhibited high Young’s modulus and low fracture strain compared to films of pMA-H0.1 (Figure 3a), the diffraction rings were highly deformed to elliptical shapes, even upon applying only 30% strain, before the film fractured at a strain above 30% (Figure 5b). To quantitatively determine the changes in the q values along and perpendicular to the stretching direction, the absolute SAXS intensities of the microparticle films were obtained by integrating the intensity within a thin rectangular box along the respective directions (Figure 5a,b). As mentioned above, the Bragg peak was observed in the SAXS profile of nonstretched pMA-H5 films at q* = ∼0.009 Å–1. The shift in q* was observed in the SAXS intensity along directions perpendicular to the stretching direction (q* ∼ 0.014 Å–1) after deformation, even at 10% strain. This result indicates that in perpendicular direction, adjacent particles were closer than before stretching. This peak became broad, resulting in a shoulder shape, before disappearing completely, indicating that the microparticle arrangement is greatly disrupted just before the film breaks.

The low-q intensities (I(q=0)), which stem from the film surface, of pMA-H0.1 and pMA-H5 in directions perpendicular (I(0,pend)) and parallel (I(0,para)) to the stretching are also plotted in Figure 5c,d. In the pMA-H0.1-microparticle films, I(0,pend) increases and I(0,para) decreases in two stages. A similar phenomenon is usually observed in elastomer and hydrogel films.67−71 The stretched pMA chains are concentrated in the perpendicular direction, and the density of pMA chains decreases in the parallel direction. In addition, the changes in I(0) in two stages may be explained by the stress–strain curves (Figure 3a). In the first stage (0 ≤ ε/% ≤ 100), the stress related to the Young’s modulus rapidly increases, which is accompanied by an increase in I(0,pend), suggesting that pMA-H0.1 microparticles are substantially deformed in the film. In the second stage (100 < ε/%), where the stress and I(0,pend) slightly increase, the mixing region of interfaces, i.e., the interpenetration, between the microparticles is expanded and the pMA chains slip, leading to film fracture. Meanwhile, in the pMA-H5-microparticle film, both I(0,pend) and I(0,para) decrease with increasing deformation, and the difference between I(0,pend) and I(0,para) increases considerably and is larger than that of the pMA-H0.1-microparticle film, even in the low-strain region (Figure 5e). In addition to the forward scattering, according to Guinier’s law,72 the Guinier plots, i.e., ln[I(q)] as a function of q2, were calculated from the SAXS intensities along parallel directions (Figure S6). The obtained Rg,parallel value of the microparticles increased with increasing strain, similar to the way I(q=0) changes. Although it is difficult to separate the scattering contributions into the lattice factor or form factor of the microparticles during deformation, they seem to reflect the deformation of the particles despite the relatively rough calculations.

In previous studies, the deformation of particle films revealed by SAXS has been attributed to the interdiffusion of polymeric chains between adjacent particles.34,35 Accordingly, the pMA-H5 microparticles can be expected to be difficult to deform in the film, and the slipping of physically interdiffused near-surface pMA chains on the microparticles can be expected to occur preferentially in the low-strain region. Thus, cross-linking reduces the deformability of individual particles, inhibits the mixing of pMA chains on the particle surfaces, and promotes fracture by slipping the interpenetrated polymers between the particle surfaces.

Conclusions

We performed SAXS experiments and AA-MD simulations on the microparticle-based films consisting of the pure and simplest acrylate polymer (pMA) microparticles to reveal the nanoscale structures related to their toughness. In order to control their toughness, a series of pMA microparticles with different cross-linking densities were synthesized as model systems and then characterized systematically using SAXS and other characterization techniques, such as AFM, Soxhlet extraction, DSC, visible light scattering, tensile tests, and AA-MD simulation. The inner cross-linking suppresses the deformation and swelling in good solvents of individual particles, resulting in changes in their fundamental physical properties, such as the radius of gyration (Rg), the glass-transition temperature (Tg), and the deformation degree. In the microparticle-based films, the characteristic interfacial thickness (tinter), which reflects the mixing degree between the pMA chains on their surface, depends on the cross-linking density because the cross-linking restricts the mixing of the pMA chains. The thermal annealing causes the mechanical properties by increasing tinter value. These phenomena were corroborated by AA-MD simulations, which were performed for the first time on a microparticle system and revealed a linear relationship between tinter and the toughness, i.e., the fracture energy. In addition, in situ SAXS measurements were carried out during tensile testing in order to evaluate the stretched microparticle films, which revealed that the deformation behavior of pMA-microparticle films was also affected by cross-linking. Cross-linking suppresses the deformation of individual microparticles in the film and the mixing of pMA chains on the particle surface, which easily slip through upon stretching. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that describes the structural physical properties of microparticles and their films using static and dynamic techniques from the Å-to-mm scale, such as AFM, DLS, SLS, tensile tests, AA-MD, and in situ SAXS measurements. We are convinced that a deeper understanding of the nanoscale structures and physical properties of tough microparticle-based films will promote the development of advanced applications, including volatile organic compound-free coatings, adhesives, and cosmetics.
