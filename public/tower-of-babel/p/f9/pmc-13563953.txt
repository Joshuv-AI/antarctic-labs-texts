Prevalence and characteristics of persistent symptoms following hospitalization for COVID-19: a cross-sectional follow-up study
Authors: Huseyin Dogus Okan, Gulsum Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) primarily presents as an acute respiratory infection, many patients continue to experience symptoms after the acute phase of the disease. Early persistent symptoms during the post-acute recovery period may adversely affect functional recovery and quality of life. This study aimed to evaluate persistent symptoms in hospitalized COVID-19 patients following completion of the quarantine period.MethodsThis retrospective longitudinal follow-up study evaluated hospitalized COVID-19 patients after completion of quarantine/isolation to determine the prevalence and characteristics of persistent symptoms. Descriptive statistics and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 180 patients were included, with a mean age of 50.63 ± 16.90 years, and 52.8% were female. At initial presentation, the most common symptoms were weakness (53.9%), diffuse body/muscle/joint pain (48.9%), cough (48.3%), fever (41.1%), loss of taste and smell (30.0%), headache (28.3%), and dyspnea (24.4%). Persistent symptoms after the quarantine period were observed in 64.9% of patients. The most common persistent symptoms were cough (28.9%), weakness (28.9%), diffuse body/muscle/joint pain (20.0%), loss of taste and smell (13.3%), dyspnea (12.8%), fatigue/exhaustion (12.8%), and headache (11.1%). Significant reductions were observed in several symptoms after the acute phase, including fever, sore throat, headache, diarrhea, and abdominal pain (p < 0.05).ConclusionsPersistent symptoms were frequently observed during the early post-acute recovery period in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Respiratory complaints, fatigue, weakness, and musculoskeletal symptoms remained common after the acute infectious period. These findings emphasize the importance of post-discharge follow-up and supportive care strategies in patients recovering from COVID-19.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Although most patients recover from acute COVID-19, a substantial proportion continue to experience symptoms beyond the acute phase of infection. Persistent symptoms such as fatigue, dyspnea, cough, myalgia, weakness, and loss of taste or smell may negatively affect physical recovery, daily functioning, and quality of life [1, 2].

Recent studies have primarily focused on long COVID or post-COVID condition, which generally refers to symptoms persisting for several months after acute infection [3]. However, less attention has been paid to symptoms occurring during the early post-acute recovery period immediately following completion of quarantine or isolation. Evaluation of this period may provide important clinical information regarding patient recovery, follow-up requirements, and supportive care needs.

Hospitalized patients may be particularly vulnerable to persistent symptoms because of greater disease severity, prolonged inflammation, and multisystem involvement during acute infection. Therefore, early recognition of persistent symptoms may facilitate appropriate post-discharge management and follow-up strategies [2].

The present study aimed to evaluate persistent symptoms among hospitalized COVID-19 patients during the early post-acute recovery period following completion of quarantine and to compare symptoms reported at hospital admission with those observed during follow-up. Unlike most later studies performed after the introduction of vaccination programs and evolving SARS-CoV-2 variants, this cohort represents hospitalized patients from the first year of the pandemic, providing an opportunity to characterize symptom persistence during the earliest stages of recovery in a relatively homogeneous population of unvaccinated individuals experiencing their first documented infection.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective longitudinal follow-up study was conducted at the COVID-19 outpatient follow-up clinic of a tertiary pandemic training and research hospital in Türkiye between November 30 and December 14, 2020.

Study population

The study included adult patients who were hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed or clinically/radiologically suspected COVID-19 infection in dedicated COVID-19 inpatient clinics and subsequently attended post-discharge outpatient follow-up visits after completion of the quarantine/isolation period. COVID-19 diagnosis and treatment procedures were performed according to the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health COVID-19 guidelines valid during the study period. Patients with positive real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test results for SARS-CoV-2 were included in the study. Clinical evaluation, laboratory findings, and thoracic computed tomography findings were assessed by treating physicians during hospitalization. Hospitalization decisions were made according to national COVID-19 hospitalization criteria valid during the study period [4, 5]. Recovery was defined according to the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health COVID-19 guidelines in effect during the study period and was based on clinical improvement and completion of the recommended quarantine/isolation period. Repeat RT-PCR testing to confirm viral clearance was not routinely performed before post-discharge follow-up evaluation.

Patients younger than 18 years of age and those with incomplete clinical records were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected using standardized outpatient follow-up assessments performed by physicians working in the COVID-19 outpatient clinic. After recovery from acute COVID-19 and hospital discharge, patients were invited for routine post-COVID outpatient follow-up. During these visits, physicians assessed and recorded persistent symptoms reported by the patients. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical findings, symptoms at initial hospital admission, and persistent symptoms during the early post-acute recovery period were recorded. Smoking status was categorized as current smoker, former smoker, or non-smoker. Current smokers were defined as individuals who were actively smoking at the time of hospitalization. Former smokers were defined as individuals who had quit smoking before hospitalization. For statistical analyses, the smoking variable referred exclusively to current smokers, whereas former smokers were analyzed separately and were not included in the current smoker category. Symptoms assessed during the acute phase included fever, cough, dyspnea, weakness, diffuse body/muscle/joint pain, headache, loss of taste and smell, gastrointestinal complaints, sleep disturbances, and other systemic symptoms. Persistent symptoms reported after completion of quarantine/isolation were similarly evaluated and compared with symptoms present during the acute phase of infection. Physical examination findings were documented during outpatient evaluation, and laboratory test results were retrieved from the hospital electronic information system. Symptoms present during the acute hospitalization period were obtained retrospectively from hospital medical records and electronic health records. In contrast, persistent symptoms during the post-acute recovery period were assessed prospectively during scheduled outpatient follow-up visits through patient interviews and physician clinical evaluation.

A total of 180 patients were included in the study. Ten patients were asymptomatic during the acute phase of COVID-19 and therefore did not report any symptoms at hospital admission. During post-discharge follow-up, 125 patients reported at least one persistent symptom, whereas 55 patients reported complete resolution of symptoms and no ongoing complaints. Symptom frequencies presented in Table 2 reflect the number of patients reporting each symptom at the respective time point.

Definition and assessment of persistent symptoms

Symptom onset was defined as the date of the first COVID-19–compatible symptom reported by the patient and documented in the medical records. When the exact symptom onset date was unavailable, the date of the first positive SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR test or the hospital admission date was considered the index date.

During the study period, all patients were managed according to the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health COVID-19 guidelines and the institutional COVID-19 follow-up protocol. Following clinical recovery and hospital discharge, patients were instructed to continue home isolation according to national recommendations. In routine institutional practice, hospitalized COVID-19 patients were generally scheduled for outpatient follow-up approximately 14 days after discharge and completion of the recommended isolation period.

The interval between hospital discharge, completion of isolation, and follow-up assessment was based on the actual outpatient visit date. Because of pandemic-related scheduling constraints and outpatient clinic workload, follow-up timing was not identical for all patients. Although the target follow-up time in routine institutional practice was approximately 14 days after discharge and completion of isolation, actual follow-up intervals varied among patients according to clinical and logistical circumstances. Persistent symptoms were assessed during this early post-acute recovery period at the scheduled outpatient follow-up visit.

Persistent symptoms were defined as symptoms that were absent before COVID-19 and either newly developed during acute infection or persisted beyond the acute phase, continuing until the post-discharge follow-up visit. Symptom assessment was based on patient self-report and physician clinical evaluation during the early post-acute recovery period. Symptoms were recorded if they were reported by the patient during follow-up and considered clinically relevant in the context of recent COVID-19 by the evaluating physician. Pre-existing chronic symptoms present before COVID-19 were not classified as persistent post-COVID symptoms unless patients reported a clear worsening following infection. All participants underwent physician evaluation during follow-up, and no alternative clinical diagnosis was identified that could better explain the reported symptoms.

Given that the study was conducted during the first year of the pandemic, prior to the publication of current WHO and CDC definitions of post-COVID condition, persistent symptoms were classified according to clinical follow-up findings rather than formal long COVID diagnostic criteria. Similar approaches have been used in previous post-acute COVID-19 follow-up studies evaluating persistent symptoms after hospital discharge [6]. Symptom severity was not graded using a standardized scale, and symptoms were analyzed as present or absent. Continuity of symptoms was assessed based on patient report; however, symptoms were not required to be continuously present every day between hospital discharge and follow-up evaluation.

Symptoms were recorded as binary variables (present or absent). No validated symptom severity scales or quantitative symptom burden assessments were available during routine clinical follow-up; therefore, symptom severity and symptom burden could not be evaluated.

Fatigue was defined as a patient-reported complaint of persistent physical tiredness, reduced energy, or decreased ability to perform usual daily activities during follow-up. Fatigue was evaluated as a clinical symptom based on patient self-report and physician assessment and was not classified according to diagnostic criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) or other fatigue syndromes. Furthermore, no validated fatigue scales or specific psychological or psychiatric assessment tools were used. Therefore, fatigue in the present study should be interpreted as self-reported physical fatigue rather than a psychosomatic symptom or a criteria-based fatigue syndrome.

Myalgia was defined as patient-reported muscle pain or aching occurring during the post-acute follow-up period and was recorded based on clinical history obtained during outpatient assessment.

COVID-19 diagnosis

All patients included in the study had laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 infection based on a positive real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test for SARS-CoV-2. No clinically or radiologically suspected cases without RT-PCR confirmation were included. COVID-19 diagnosis was established according to the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health guidelines in effect during the study period.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Approval No: 71522473/050.01.04/604; Date: 01 December 2020). As this study was conducted retrospectively using routinely collected clinical follow-up data, informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Epi Info version 7 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons of symptom frequencies between hospital admission and post-discharge follow-up were performed using McNemar’s test for paired binary data. Comparisons between independent groups were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with persistent symptoms. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 180 patients hospitalized for COVID-19 infection and evaluated during the early post-acute recovery period were included in the study. Of the patients, 95 (52.8%) were female and 85 (47.2%) were male. The mean age was 50.63 ± 16.90 years (range: 19–96 years). Overall, 86 patients (47.8%) had at least one chronic comorbidity. The most common comorbid diseases were hypertension (22.2%), diabetes mellitus (13.3%), and cardiovascular disease (11.1%). Continuous medication use was reported by 72 patients (40.0%). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 27.93 ± 3.93 kg/m² (range: 17.85–43.34 kg/m²), and the majority of patients were classified as pre-obese or obese. At the time of hospital admission for suspected COVID-19 infection, 170 patients (94.4%) had at least one symptom, while only 10 patients (5.5%) were asymptomatic. The most frequently reported symptoms during the acute phase were weakness (53.9%), diffuse body/muscle/joint pain (48.9%), cough (48.3%), fever (41.1%), loss of taste and smell (30.0%), headache (28.3%), dyspnea (24.4%), and fatigue/exhaustion (24.4%) (Table 2).

Persistent symptoms during the early post-acute recovery period were observed in 125 patients (64.9%), whereas 55 patients (30.5%) reported complete symptom resolution after the quarantine/isolation period. The most common persistent symptoms were cough (28.9%), weakness (28.9%), diffuse body/muscle/joint pain (20.0%), loss of taste and smell (13.3%), dyspnea (12.8%), fatigue/exhaustion (12.8%), headache (11.1%), and malaise (8.3%) (Table 2).

Comparison of symptoms at hospital admission and during the early post-acute recovery period demonstrated significant reductions in the prevalence of several symptoms. Fever (41.1% vs. 3.3%, p < 0.001), diffuse body/muscle/joint pain (48.9% vs. 20.0%, p < 0.001), weakness (53.9% vs. 28.9%, p < 0.001), sore throat (16.7% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.001), headache (28.3% vs. 11.1%, p = 0.001), rhinorrhea (8.9% vs. 1.1%, p = 0.001), loss of taste and smell (30.0% vs. 13.3%, p = 0.001), malaise (20.0% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.001), abdominal pain (11.1% vs. 1.7%, p < 0.001), diarrhea (15.0% vs. 3.3%, p < 0.001), fatigue/exhaustion (24.4% vs. 12.8%, p = 0.006), insomnia (13.9% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.009), restlessness (11.1% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.019), and palpitations (8.9% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.004) decreased significantly during follow-up. Although cough (48.3% vs. 28.9%, p = 0.001) and dyspnea (24.4% vs. 12.8%, p = 0.001) also declined significantly, these symptoms remained among the most frequently reported persistent manifestations during the recovery period. Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated persistent symptoms during the early post-acute recovery period in hospitalized COVID-19 patients following completion of the quarantine/isolation period. Our findings demonstrated that persistent symptoms remained common after the acute infectious phase, with approximately two-thirds of patients reporting at least one ongoing symptom during follow-up. The most frequent persistent manifestations were cough, weakness, diffuse body/muscle/joint pain, loss of taste and smell, dyspnea, fatigue, and headache. These findings suggest that recovery from acute COVID-19 infection may continue beyond virological recovery and hospital discharge.

Persistent symptoms during the early post-acute recovery period were observed in approximately two-thirds of hospitalized patients. This prevalence is consistent with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrating that symptom persistence is common following acute COVID-19 infection. Lopez-Leon et al. reported more than 50 long-term effects of COVID-19, with fatigue, dyspnea, cough, headache, and musculoskeletal complaints among the most frequently reported manifestations [7].

COVID-19 is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disease with both acute and post-acute clinical consequences. Previous studies have shown that a considerable proportion of patients continue to experience symptoms weeks or months after acute infection [6, 7]. Although the concept of “Long COVID” generally refers to symptoms persisting for at least several months [3, 12], persistent symptoms may also emerge during the early recovery period immediately after the acute phase. Our findings contribute to the growing literature demonstrating that clinically significant symptom persistence may begin shortly after completion of quarantine or isolation [8, 9, 11].

A distinctive aspect of the present study is its timing during the first year of the pandemic, before the introduction of COVID-19 vaccination programs and before the emergence of current long COVID definitions. Consequently, our findings provide a unique description of symptom persistence during the early post-acute recovery phase in a relatively homogeneous cohort of unvaccinated patients experiencing their first documented SARS-CoV-2 infection. Unlike many later studies that evaluated symptoms several months after infection, our study focused on the period immediately following completion of quarantine, thereby capturing the earliest stages of post-acute recovery. This design allowed us to characterize symptom persistence before potential modification by vaccination, reinfection, or evolving viral variants, factors that may complicate interpretation of findings in later cohorts. Moreover, the study provides data from a hospitalized Turkish cohort evaluated immediately after completion of quarantine, a period that has been less frequently investigated than the later phases of post-COVID recovery.

In our cohort, weakness and diffuse body/muscle/joint pain were among the most common symptoms both during acute infection and during the post-acute recovery period. Similarly, several international studies have identified fatigue and musculoskeletal complaints as the predominant persistent symptoms after COVID-19 infection. Carfì et al. reported that fatigue and dyspnea remained common approximately two months after symptom onset in hospitalized patients recovering from COVID-19 [9]. Similarly, Goërtz et al. reported that fatigue, muscle weakness, and other physical complaints remained highly prevalent approximately three months after SARS-CoV-2 infection, indicating that persistent symptoms may continue well beyond the acute phase of the disease [10]. Townsend et al. also reported that persistent fatigue was common following SARS-CoV-2 infection regardless of the severity of the initial infection, suggesting that post-COVID fatigue may occur even after clinical recovery [14].

Respiratory symptoms were also prominent in our study population. Persistent cough and dyspnea remained among the most frequently reported symptoms after the quarantine period, despite significant improvement in many acute-phase manifestations. Similar findings were reported by Halpin et al., who demonstrated that fatigue, breathlessness, and rehabilitation needs remained common among patients following hospital discharge after COVID-19 infection [11]. Persistent respiratory sequelae have also been associated with residual airway inflammation, pulmonary parenchymal injury, impaired diffusion capacity, and prolonged inflammatory responses following acute SARS-CoV-2 infection [6].

Loss of taste and smell also remained relatively common during follow-up in our study. Olfactory and gustatory dysfunctions are characteristic manifestations of COVID-19 and may persist after acute infection because of direct viral effects on the olfactory epithelium and neural pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that sensory dysfunction may continue for weeks or months after viral clearance [12]. Similarly, Boscolo-Rizzo et al. reported that olfactory and gustatory dysfunctions may persist long after acute infection, although gradual recovery was observed in most patients during long-term follow-up [13]. Neurological and neuropsychiatric symptoms were also observed during the early post-acute recovery period in our cohort, including headache, sleep disturbances, restlessness, and drowsiness. Similar findings have been reported in large cohort studies evaluating post-COVID neurological outcomes. Taquet et al. demonstrated that neurological and psychiatric manifestations may persist for months after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, emphasizing the prolonged multisystem effects of COVID-19 [14]. In addition, increasing evidence suggests that persistent neurological symptoms may represent an important component of post-acute COVID-19 recovery [12].

An important finding of the present study was the significant decline in the prevalence of most acute COVID-19 symptoms during follow-up. Nevertheless, cough, dyspnea, weakness, fatigue, diffuse body/muscle/joint pain, and loss of taste and smell remained common despite significant reductions compared with the acute phase. These findings suggest that although acute inflammatory manifestations generally resolve after recovery, respiratory, musculoskeletal, and constitutional symptoms may persist into the early post-acute period and represent a substantial component of the post-COVID symptom burden.

The finding that nearly two-thirds of hospitalized patients continued to experience symptoms shortly after the acute phase has important clinical implications. Although several acute manifestations resolved rapidly, respiratory symptoms, fatigue-related complaints, and musculoskeletal symptoms frequently persisted. These findings suggest that symptom burden remains substantial even in the early recovery period and support the need for structured post-discharge follow-up programs.

An important finding of the present study was that increasing age was independently associated with persistent symptoms during follow-up. In addition, diabetes mellitus was associated with persistent symptoms in the univariable analysis, although this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for potential confounders. These findings suggest that older age may be an important determinant of delayed recovery following acute COVID-19 infection. Older individuals may experience slower recovery because of reduced physiological reserve, greater comorbidity burden, and prolonged inflammatory responses following acute SARS-CoV-2 infection. Therefore, age may be useful for identifying patients who could benefit from closer post-discharge monitoring and supportive care.

The high prevalence of persistent symptoms observed in our cohort highlights the importance of systematic post-discharge follow-up even during the earliest stages of recovery. Although many acute inflammatory manifestations resolved after completion of quarantine, respiratory complaints, fatigue-related symptoms, and musculoskeletal symptoms frequently persisted. These findings suggest that symptom assessment should not be limited to the acute infectious period and support the integration of structured follow-up and rehabilitation strategies into routine care for hospitalized COVID-19 survivors. Early recognition and management of persistent symptoms may facilitate recovery, improve functional outcomes, and reduce the long-term burden of post-COVID morbidity [15, 16].

The exact mechanisms underlying persistent post-acute symptoms remain incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include persistent immune activation, chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, autonomic nervous system dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and prolonged tissue injury. Additionally, physical deconditioning, psychological stress, sleep disturbances, and prolonged hospitalization may contribute to delayed recovery in hospitalized patients. Similar mechanisms and clinical manifestations have been emphasized in previous comprehensive reviews evaluating post-acute COVID-19 recovery and symptom persistence [15, 16].

Recent evidence continues to support the clinical relevance of persistent post-COVID symptoms across different phases of the pandemic. In 2024, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) proposed a consensus definition of Long COVID, emphasizing that post-COVID conditions may affect multiple organ systems and occur following both mild and severe SARS-CoV-2 infections [12]. Furthermore, contemporary studies have demonstrated that persistent respiratory symptoms, fatigue, musculoskeletal complaints, cognitive impairment, and reduced quality of life remain among the most frequently reported post-COVID manifestations despite changes in viral variants, vaccination status, and clinical management strategies [17–19]. Although our study focused on the early post-acute recovery period during the first year of the pandemic, the symptom profile observed in our cohort is broadly consistent with the spectrum of persistent symptoms described in more recent studies.

The present study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single tertiary center with a relatively limited sample size, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings. Second, symptom assessment was primarily based on patient self-report and outpatient clinical evaluation, potentially introducing recall bias. Third, long-term follow-up beyond the early post-acute recovery period was not available. In addition, the exact interval between hospital discharge and outpatient follow-up evaluation was not systematically recorded. Although patients were routinely scheduled for outpatient evaluation after completion of the recommended quarantine/isolation period according to institutional practice, the exact timing of follow-up varied among patients because of pandemic-related logistical constraints, outpatient clinic workload, and individual scheduling factors. Consequently, some patients may have been assessed earlier or later in their recovery course, which could have influenced the prevalence and pattern of persistent symptoms observed at follow-up. Furthermore, detailed indicators of acute disease severity, including length of hospital stay, intensive care unit admission, oxygen requirement, mechanical ventilation, chest CT severity, and inflammatory laboratory markers, were not systematically available and therefore could not be evaluated as potential predictors of persistent symptoms. Because disease severity is likely associated with the development and persistence of post-COVID symptoms, the absence of these variables may have resulted in residual confounding in the regression analyses. Consequently, the independent associations observed in the present study should be interpreted with caution. Objective assessments such as pulmonary function tests, radiological imaging, inflammatory biomarkers, and quality-of-life measurements were also not systematically evaluated. Persistent symptoms were assessed only as present or absent and without the use of validated symptom questionnaires or standardized severity scales. Consequently, symptom burden, symptom intensity, and their impact on daily functioning could not be quantified objectively. This limitation may have reduced the ability to distinguish between mild and severe persistent symptoms and should be considered when interpreting the clinical significance of the reported findings. In addition, information regarding menopausal status was not available. Furthermore, all patients represented primary COVID-19 infections and none had received COVID-19 vaccination prior to infection, as the study was conducted before the initiation of national vaccination programs. Therefore, the potential effects of vaccination status, reinfection, and menopausal status on persistent symptoms could not be evaluated. Finally, the data were collected during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic and several years before manuscript submission. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to contemporary COVID-19 populations, which may differ because of widespread vaccination, reinfections, evolving SARS-CoV-2 variants, and changes in clinical management. Nevertheless, the study provides a unique description of persistent symptoms during the early post-acute recovery period in a relatively homogeneous cohort of unvaccinated patients experiencing their first documented SARS-CoV-2 infection, thereby offering valuable insight into symptom persistence before the potential influence of vaccination, reinfection, and later viral variants.

Conclusion

Persistent symptoms were frequently observed during the early post-acute recovery period in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Respiratory complaints, weakness, fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and loss of taste and smell remained common after the acute infectious phase. These findings highlight the importance of structured post-discharge follow-up, symptom monitoring, rehabilitation programs, and supportive care strategies in patients recovering from COVID-19. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as functional status, quality of life, and objective clinical outcome measures were not evaluated in the present study. Further prospective multicenter studies with larger patient populations and longer follow-up durations are needed to better understand the clinical course and long-term consequences of persistent post-COVID symptoms.
