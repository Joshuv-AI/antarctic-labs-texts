Antiparasitic activity and molecular docking of canthin-6-one derivatives from LED- modulated Eurycoma longifolia roots against Blastocystis ST3 and ST7
Authors: Mahmoud Ali Khalaf Abushattal, Sani Sale, Muhammad Tahir Muhammad, Sreeramanan Subramaniam, Mohamad Nurul Azmi Mohamad Taib, Arutchelvan Rajamanikam, Farah Haziqa Meor Termizi, Nor Hasnida Hassan, Mohamad Fadhli Mad’ Atari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eurycoma longifolia (tongkat ali) is a widely recognized medicinal plant in Southeast Asia, valued for its bioactive alkaloids and quassinoids. Building on previous findings that light-emitting diode (LED) modulation enhances the antiproliferative activity of E. longifolia hairy root cultures (ELHRCs) crude extracts, this study aimed to isolate the specific active constituents, characterize their subtype-specific efficacy, and perform in silico analysis to evaluate potential interaction with proteases against Blastocystis ST3 and ST7. Through chromatographic purification and spectroscopic analysis (NMR, FT-IR, and GC-MS), two compounds canthin-6-one (1) and 9-methoxycanthin-6-one (2) were isolated from ELHRCs grown under various LED spectra for 10 weeks. Quantitative analysis revealed that compound 1 was specifically isolated from Red LED treatments. In contrast, compound 2 was successfully isolated across all treatments, with the highest yield (1.00%) obtained under Mint Green LED illumination. In vitro assays demonstrated that both compounds exhibited dose-dependent inhibitory activity. Using nonlinear sigmoidal dose-response modelling, the values for compound 2 were determined to be 0.0427 mg/mL (ST3) and 0.0433 mg/mL (ST7), while compound 1 exhibited values of 0.0482 mg/mL (ST3) and 0.0603 mg/mL (ST7). Molecular docking studies targeting a cysteine protease surrogate revealed binding affinities of − 6.9 kcal/mol and − 7.3 kcal/mol for compounds 1 and 2, respectively. Although the standard drug metronidazole exhibited higher potency in vitro, these results indicate that LED-stimulated ELHRCs are a viable source for the production of canthin-6-one alkaloids with promising antiparasitic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53733-8.

Introduction

Eurycoma longifolia (commonly known as tongkat ali) is a well-known medicinal plant native to Southeast Asia and has been traditionally used in Malaysia for over 2000 years to treat various infectious and inflammatory conditions1. Belonging to the Simaroubaceae family, E. longifolia is widely recognized for its aphrodisiac effects. However, it also demonstrates diverse pharmacological properties attributed to its rich composition of bioactive compounds. Among these, alkaloids with a canthin-6-one skeleton are of particular interest due to their documented antibacterial and antiparasitic2–4, anti-inflammatory5, antihyperglycemic6 and anticancer activities7.

Blastocystis sp. is a common intestinal protozoan parasite found in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals8. There are 44 recognized subtypes of Blastocystis sp9, among it ST1 to ST9 are the most frequently reported in human infections10 and have been associated with gastrointestinal disorders such as abdominal pain, bloating, and diarrhea, particularly in immunocompromised individuals11. Although Blastocystis sp. is increasingly regarded as commensal due to its frequent detection in asymptomatic colonization, its interaction with gut microbiota and potential association with gastrointestinal diseases remain insufficiently understood. Hence, elimination of this protozoan specifically in symptomatic infections continues to be considered clinically relevant.

Currently, metronidazole (MTZ), the first line treatment administered, demonstrate inconsistent efficacy across Blastocystis sp. subtypes and geographical regions, with growing evidence of resistance12,13. Moreover, MTZ therapy may inadvertently trigger cellular mechanisms that enhance survival and proliferation of Blastocystis sp cells12,14,15. These observations emphasize urgent need for alternative therapeutics with a more consistent efficacy.

Natural products from medicinal plants are gaining traction as promising sources of new antiparasitic agents. Recent advances in plant biotechnology have enabled the use of hairy root cultures (HRCs), generated via Agrobacterium rhizogenic transformation, as stable, scalable platforms for the production of valuable secondary metabolites16,17. Optimizing environmental factors, such as light spectrum, can significantly influence the biosynthetic pathways of these metabolites particularly E. longifolia hairy root cultures18.

Previous research investigated4 the exposure of E. longifolia hairy root cultures (ELHRCs) to various LED light spectra including white, blue, red, blue/red combination, mint green, and a dark control, to assess their impact on alkaloid synthesis. The dichloromethane (DCM) extracts from these cultures exhibited significant antiparasitic activity against Blastocystis sp, with efficacy comparable to MTZ. However, the specific active compounds responsible for this effect are yet to be identified, and their mechanism of action remained unclear.

In recent years, in silico computational approaches have become essential tools in drug discovery and natural product research, particularly for predicting molecular interactions and identifying potential biological targets. Among these approaches, molecular docking is widely used to evaluate the interaction patterns between bioactive compounds and specific protein targets19, thereby providing mechanistic insights at the molecular level. In the context of Blastocystis sp cysteine proteases have been proposed as key virulence factors involved in host tissue invasion, nutrient acquisition, and immune modulation20. These enzymes are therefore considered potential therapeutic targets for antiparasitic intervention21–23. Although experimentally validated protein structures for Blastocystis sp cysteine proteases remain limited, the use of homologous protein models provides a valuable exploratory framework for assessing ligand–protein interactions. Accordingly, molecular docking in this study was employed as a hypothesis-generating approach to investigate the potential interaction of isolated alkaloids with cysteine protease, thereby complementing the in vitro findings.

Building upon these findings, the present study seeks to isolate, purify, and structurally characterize the key bioactive compounds responsible for the anti-Blastocystis sp activity of E. longifolia. To explore the potential mechanism of action, molecular docking simulations were performed targeting parasite cysteine protease, a critical enzyme implicated in virulence and host immune evasion20. Through the integration of experimental and in silico approaches, this study provides molecular-level insights into the therapeutic potential ELHRC-derived alkaloids and lays the groundwork for the future development as antiparasitic agents.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and analytical instruments

All chemicals and solvents utilized in this study were procured from Merck (Germany) and Qrec (Asia). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed using 4 × 10 cm aluminum-backed plates coated with silica gel 60F254 (Merck, Germany) at a thickness of 0.25 mm. Plates were visualized under ultraviolet light at wavelengths of 254 nm and 365 nm. The mobile phases for TLC comprised varying ratios of dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol (MeOH): 49:1, 48:2, 47:3, 46:4, and 45:5. Detection of alkaloids was carried out using Dragendorff’s reagent. Column chromatography (CC) was conducted on silica gel 60 (mesh size 230–400, Merck, Germany).

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired using a Bruker AVANCE III HD 500 MHz spectrometer. Data analysis was completed with TopSpin 3.6.2 software. Calibration was done using tetramethylsilane (TMS), deuterated chloroform (CDCl₃), or deuterated methanol (CD₃OD) as internal standards. The ¹H NMR spectra included data on chemical shifts, integration values, multiplicities (e.g., singlets [s], doublets [d], and doublet of doublets [dd]), and spin-spin coupling constants (J, in Hz). The purity of the isolated compounds 1 and 2 was assessed via 1D (1H,13C) and 2D (COSY, HSQC, HMBC) NMR analysis; both compounds with no significant structural impurities detected (spectra provided in Supplementary Files).

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was performed on a Shimadzu GC-MS-QP 2010 Ultra system (Kyoto, Japan), equipped with an HP5MS (5975 MSD) column, to determine the molecular ion peaks (m/z) of the compounds. Infrared (IR) spectral data were obtained using a PerkinElmer Universal ATR FT-IR spectrometer, using DCM as the solvent. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were recorded with a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Kyoto, Japan), also using DCM as the solvent.

Plant material

The Eurycoma longifolia plant (FRI 105599, Maran, Pahang, Malaysia) were used as material to induce the hairy root cultures by24. The hairy root culture stocks were obtained from the Forest Research Institute Malaysia (FRIM) under Material Transfer Agreement (MTA) between FRIM and the School of Biological Sciences, Universiti Sains Malaysia (SBSUSM). Cultures were grown in Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium (pH 5.8), sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min. Cultivation was carried out at 22 ± 2 °C in the dark with orbital shaking at 110 rpm under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle, as previously described4,25.

Growth conditions

The ELHRCs were subjected to various LED treatments to assess their effects on alkaloid synthesis. The spectral qualities included white (400–700 nm), blue (440 nm), red (660 nm), combined blue and red (1:1; 440 nm + 660 nm), and mint green (530 nm) light, with a dark condition serving as the control. Light intensities (PPFD) were measured using a PLA-20 plant lighting analyzer and were as follows: W (44.38 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), R (14.24 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), B (6.40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), BR (17.63 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), and MG (7.90 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Samples were collected at 10 weeks post-treatment and dried at 37 °C until constant weight4.

Extraction and isolation of compounds in ELHRCs

The extractions were performed using a maceration technique. After 10 weeks of culture, the harvested roots from various LED treatments—dark (3.78 g), white (6.0 g), blue (4.2 g), red (6.72 g), blue plus red (1:1) (2.0 g), and mint green (4.5 g)—were dried at room temperature until a constant weight was achieved. The whole dried roots from each treatment were extracted by soaking in dichloromethane (DCM) in 250-mL conical flasks at room temperature for three days. This process was repeated three times and filtered through filter paper. The combined extracts were concentrated at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator. Solvent evaporation was performed using a Heidolph Hei-VAP rotary evaporator equipped with a 1300 W heating bath (P/N 517–61000-00-0, Heidolph Instruments GmbH & Co. KG, Germany), operated within a temperature range of 20 °C to 210.°C. Following the DCM extraction, the root residue was further soaked in methanol (MeOH) for three days, following the same procedure.

The crude DCM-soluble extracts were fractionated via column chromatography (CC; 1.5 cm × 70.0 cm, 0.15 g SiO2, 0.063–0.200 mm, 70–230 mesh) using an n-hexane/DCM/MeOH step gradient to yield seven major fractions (Fr. 1–Fr. 7). Based on the purification results (Table 1), canthine-6-one (3.00 mg, 0.45% yield) was isolated from Fr. 7 of the red-light treatment. The alkaloid 9-methoxycanthin-6-one was isolated from the following fractions: Fr. 3 (Dark), Fr. 6 (White), Fr. 4 (Blue), Fr. 2 (Red), Fr. 1 (Blue plus Red 1:1), and Fr. 5 (Mint green).

Antiparasitic effect of isolated compounds against Blastocystis sp

Source of Blastocystis sp isolate

This in vitro investigation was conducted at the Emerging Pathogen Laboratory 1, Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Medicine, University of Malaya, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. Two Blastocystis sp subtypes were used: ST3, which was isolated from symptomatic patients exhibiting clinical signs such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, abdominal distension, and diarrhoea; and ST7, obtained from a patient diagnosed with schizophrenia but presenting no gastrointestinal symptoms26. Both isolates were originally collected from hospitals affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine, University of Malaya. The cultures were maintained in vitro through routine sub-culturing every 3–4 days in Jones medium enriched with 10% horse serum and incubated at 37 °C, following previously described protocols4.

Preparation of isolated compounds and metronidazole

The isolated alkaloids, canthin-6-one (1) and 9-methoxycanthin-6-one (2), obtained from the 10-week ELHRC extracts, were dissolved in 1 mL of 0.1% DMSO (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). These were evaluated against Blastocystis sp at a range of concentrations (0.0001; 0.001;0.01; and 0.1 mg/mL). was used as a reference anti-protozoal drug for comparative benchmarking. Following the method described by Dhurga et al.14, the stock solution prepared at 10 mg/mL was used to prepare various MTZ concentrations (0.0001; 0.001; 0.01 and 0.1 mg/mL).

Drug susceptibility assays

A growth profile study was conducted to evaluate the antiparasitic efficacy of isolated compounds 1 and 2 against Blastocystis sp. ST3 and ST7 isolates. Metronidazole (MTZ) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used as a reference drug. The study employed four concentrations of the compounds and MTZ: 0.0001; 0.001; 0.01 and 0.1 mg/mL. The assay was performed using 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Each tube contained Blastocystis sp cells at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, pooled from day 3 of culture. A 100 µL aliquot of the isolated compounds or drug at the desired concentration was added to the culture tubes, followed by the addition of fresh Jones’ medium supplemented with 10% horse serum, to a final volume of 1 mL. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C. A tube containing the same volume of parasites in Jones’ medium with 10% horse serum and 100 µL of 0.1% DMSO was also prepared as a control. All experiments were performed in triplicates.

The viability of Blastocystis sp cells was assessed daily for 10 days. The Trypan Blue exclusion method was utilized to determine cell counts via a haemocytometer chamber (Improved Neubauer, Hausser Scientific). While this method primarily assesses membrane integrity, it served as a standard measure for longitudinal growth profiling in this study.

Determination of growth inhibition and IC5₀ values

The antiparasitic activity of purified compounds from E. longifolia hairy root cultures and metronidazole (MTZ) was evaluated against Blastocystis subtypes ST3 and ST7. Parasite growth inhibition (%) was calculated using: (A − B/A) × 100, where A is the mean number of viable cells in untreated controls, and B is the mean in treated samples. Each concentration was tested in triplicate. Tested concentrations included 0.0001; 0.001; 0.01; and 0.1 mg/mL, assessed at 48, 72, and 96 h. Rather than relying on operational inhibitory thresholds, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and (IC90) values were determined through nonlinear sigmoidal dose-response modelling (variable slope). These parameters were estimated based on the 72-hour data points to ensure a robust calculation of potency. Parasites were harvested from day-3 cultures for all assays. The study focused on the total viable population as determined by Trypan Blue exclusion. While various morphological states common to Blastocystis sp (including vacuolar, granular, and cyst-like forms) were observed during the experiments, a differential count of these specific morphological forms was not performed, and results reflect the overall population response.

Molecular docking study

A molecular docking study was conducted as a hypothesis-generating approach to assess the potential inhibitory interactions between the isolated compounds (1 and 2) and cysteine protease, a key virulence factor in Blastocystis sp pathogenesis. In the absence of a resolved three-dimensional structure for Blastocystis sp cysteine protease, the cysteine protease from Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense (PDB ID: 2P7U) was utilized as a homologous structural surrogate. This exploratory framework was chosen based on functional similarities and conserved active site residues common to parasitic cysteine proteases. This approach offers a preliminary yet meaningful insight into the possible inhibitory mechanisms of these compounds against Blastocystis sp. The molecular docking workflow followed a structure-based design strategy, an essential tool in current drug discovery for evaluating natural product interactions at the atomic level27. The chemical structures of the test compounds were initially drawn using ChemDraw and saved in PDB format. Protein preparation was performed in UCSF Chimera 1.14, where water molecules and non-essential heteroatoms were removed using the Dock Prep tool. The receptor and ligands were separated, polar hydrogens were added, Gasteiger charges were assigned, and energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm.

Docking simulations were executed using AutoDock Vina. The docking grid was centered on the active site of the receptor with coordinates set at X: 40 Å, Y: 44 Å, Z: 46 Å and dimensions of −8 Å × 2 Å × 10 Å, with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å28. Analysis focused exclusively on the binding affinities and interaction profiles of compounds 1 and 2. Post-docking analyses were conducted using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 21.1.0) to visualize binding poses, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic contacts.

Statistical analysis

All values were determined using nonlinear sigmoidal dose-response modelling (variable slope) performed in SPSS. Growth inhibition data were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s post hoc test to determine significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) between treatment groups. The results are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests were employed to compare the growth profiles and susceptibility between Blastocystis sp subtypes ST3 and ST7. All statistical computations were performed using SPSS version 28.0.

Results

Structural elucidation of isolated compounds

The phytochemical constituents of E. longifolia hairy root cultures (ELHRCs) were analysed to determine the presence and variation of alkaloids under different light-emitting diode (LED) treatments. The dichloromethane (DCM) extracts of ELHRCs were subjected to chromatographic separation using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and column chromatography (CC), followed by structural characterisation through various spectroscopic techniques. Among the treated samples, two known alkaloids canthine-6-one (1) and 9-methoxycanthin-6-one (2), were successfully isolated and identified, based on comparison with previously reported literature (Table 1; Supporting Information S1–S14). Quantitative analysis of the isolation yields revealed a differential influence of the light spectrum on alkaloid accumulation. Compound 2 was successfully isolated from all treatments, with the highest yield (1.00%) obtained under MG LED illumination, followed by W (0.92%) and B (0.83%) treatments. In contrast, compound (1) was uniquely isolated from the R LED treatment (0.45% yield; Table 1). These findings suggest that specific LED spectra, particularly Mint Green and Red, play a distinct role in modulating the biosynthesis and accumulation of canthin-6-one derivatives in ELHRCs.

Antiparasitic activities of isolated compounds on Blastocystis sp

The growth dynamics of Blastocystis sp were analysed to evaluate the inhibitory efficacy of the isolated compounds 1 and 2 against subtypes ST3 and ST7. As shown in Fig. 1, both compounds and the reference drug MTZ demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition across a concentration range of 0.0001 to 0.1 mg/mL. The most consistent suppression of parasite growth was observed at the 0.1 mg/mL concentration.

Based on the growth profiles in Fig. 1, the untreated ST3 control peaked at Day 3 with a viable cell count of 4.24 × 105 cells/mL. At a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, compound1 reduced the viable cell count to 0.036 × 105 cells/mL, while compound2 and MTZ resulted in counts of 0.58 × 105 and 0.81 × 105 cells/mL, respectively. For subtype ST7, the control population reached cells/mL at Day 3. Treatment with 0.1 mg/mL of compounds 1, 2, and MTZ resulted in viable counts of 5.66 × 105, 6.84 × 105, and 1.61 × 105 cells/mL, respectively.

To quantify the inhibitory effect, the percentage of growth inhibition was calculated at 48, 72, and 96 h (Fig. 2). Statistical comparison using Duncan’s post-hoc test revealed significant differences between treatments at the 0.1 mg/mL concentration. Against ST3, both isolated alkaloids exhibited significantly higher growth inhibition at 48 h and 72 h compared to MTZ (P < 0.05). Specifically, at 72 h, 1 and 2 achieved 91.50% and 88.01% inhibition, respectively, compared to 80.78% for MTZ. However, by 96 h, the inhibitory effects against ST3 were statistically comparable across all three treatments (P < 0.05), as indicated by the shared statistical groupings in Fig. 3..

In contrast, the sensitivity of ST7 followed a different pattern. While compound 1 reached a peak inhibition of 92.88% at 72 h, its efficacy declined to 75.35% by 96 h. Metronidazole demonstrated the most consistent activity against ST7, maintaining significantly higher inhibition (94.01% at 72 h and 92.95% at 96 h) compared to both isolated alkaloids (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the calculated IC50 values (Table 2) confirmed that while the isolated alkaloids possess substantial antiparasitic activity, MTZ remains the more potent agent overall, with significantly lower IC50 values compared to compounds 1 and 2.

Molecular docking (exploratory analysis)

Molecular docking was conducted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach to investigate the potential interactions between the isolated alkaloids and a parasitic cysteine protease surrogate (PDB ID: 2P7U). This analysis provides preliminary insights into the possible binding modes of these compounds within the enzymatic active site. Comprehensive docking scores and interaction details are presented in Tables 3 and 4.

The models illustrate predicted connectivity to conserved residues such as Met145 and Cys22, serving as a theoretical basis for potential enzymatic inhibition.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, both isolated compounds exhibited interactions with amino acid residues in close proximity to the enzymatic active site. Compound 1 exhibited a binding affinity of − 6.9 kcal/mol (Table 3), indicating a stable interaction with the surrogate enzyme’s active site. This interaction was supported by multiple non-covalent forces, including π–sulfur interactions with Met145 involving the compound’s pyrrolidine and phenyl rings. Additional stabilizing forces included a T-shaped π– π interaction with Trp188 and four π– π stacking interactions involving the aromatic framework with Trp184 (Table 4).

Discussion

The isolation of compounds 1 and 2 from E. longifolia hairy root cultures (ELHRCs) (Table 1) represent a significant advancement in exploring plant-derived antiparasitic agents. These alkaloids are known for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial and antiparasitic properties, attributed to their ability to interfere with essential cellular processes in protozoan parasites29. In particular, 9-methoxycanthin-6-one has shown promising activity against Blastocystis sp, extending previous findings that emphasized the efficacy of E. longifolia constituents such as eurycomanone15. The identification of these compounds from ELHRCs by NMR analysis (spectra provided in supplementary files) underscores the importance of a sustainable source of bioactive molecules with potential pharmacological applications17.

Recent studies on Blastocystis sp have highlighted subtype-specific behaviour and clinical relevance30,31. Subtype 3 (ST3) is the most prevalent human Blastocystis sp and is often detected in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals [11]. This subtype have been associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) and possess greater protease activity32–34. Subtype 7 (ST7), although primarily considered a non-human subtype of Blastocystis sp has been implicated in pathogenic outcomes, including elevated cysteine protease activity, disruption of gut epithelial barriers and alterations of host microbiota33. Additionally, ST7 infections have been linked to gut dysbiosis, with a higher prevalence of Proteobacteria, suggesting complex interactions between the parasite and host microbial communities35. Although recent work has emphasized the role of bacteria in shaping pathogenic outcome in a single subtype of Blastocystis sp36, understanding subtype-level variation in treatment responses remains essential. Accordingly, this study has focused on ST3 and ST7, two subtypes that have been consistently associated with pathogenic outcomes.

Over the years, a growing body of research has recognized E. longifolia as a rich source of pharmacologically active compounds. Numerous studies have highlighted its anticancer7,37, anti-inflammatory38, and antibacterial effects39. Among its major bioactive constituents, alkaloids such as canthin-6-one, 9-methoxycanthin-6-one, and their N-oxide or hydroxy derivatives have demonstrated broad-spectrum bioactivity, including antimicrobial and cytotoxic properties6,40,41.

Building upon previous work with LED-modulated ELHRCs extracts4, the present study successfully isolated and identified compounds 1 and 2. When tested against Blastocystis ST3 and ST7, both compounds achieved high growth inhibition at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. At specific time points, their inhibitory effects demonstrated comparable in vitro activity to metronidazole (MTZ). However, as demonstrated by the significantly lower IC50 values in Table 2, MTZ remains the more potent agent overall. Furthermore, while the alkaloids showed promising activity at 72 h, MTZ maintained significantly higher efficacy against ST7 at the 96-hour time point, reflecting the inherent challenges in treating this specifically virulent subtype.

This study provides the first exploratory investigation into the binding behaviour of ELHRC-derived alkaloids against a parasitic cysteine protease surrogate (Fig. 3). Molecular docking analysis indicated that compounds 1 and 2 exhibited stable binding affinities (− 6.9 and − 7.3 kcal/mol, respectively). In contrast to the broadly genotoxic mechanism of MTZ, which targets DNA via reductive activation42, these docking results suggest that canthin-6-one alkaloids may interact with the active site of papain-family cysteine proteases.

Although various crude plant extracts, including garlic, ginger, and turmeric, have been shown to inhibit Blastocystis sp growth to varying degrees, these treatments often suffer from inconsistent results and lack subtype specificity43. Our findings offer a more refined and targeted alternative. By isolating and characterizing specific active compounds from ELHRCs, we move beyond the limitations of crude extracts and establish a foundation for standardized, plant-derived antiparasitic agents.

The inhibition of parasite growth (Fig. 3) and binding affinity to cysteine protease suggest promising potential of ELHRC-derived alkaloids as a more targeted treatment not only for Blastocystis sp but also other protozoan parasite. The integration of in vitro efficacy data with molecular docking insights strengthens the case for canthin-6-one and 9-methoxycanthin-6-one as lead compounds for future drug development. Furthermore, the use of hairy root cultures under controlled LED treatments represents a sustainable and scalable strategy for producing these valuable bioactive.

These in silico findings serve as a preliminary exploration and must be interpreted with caution. Due to the use of a homologous protein model (PDB ID: 2P7U) and the inherent limitations of computational docking, these data do not confirm direct enzyme inhibition but rather provide a theoretical basis for future biochemical validation. Differences in sequence and subsite residues between the surrogate and the native Blastocystis sp protease may alter actual ligand binding and potency.

Regarding experimental methodology, parasite viability was assessed using the Trypan Blue exclusion method. While this remains a widely used technique for assessing membrane integrity, its inherent limitations are recognized. Specifically, Trypan Blue exclusion may not fully capture metabolic viability or subtle physiological shifts. As noted by Mirza et al.44, resazurin- or XTT-based assays can provide more sensitive quantitative data regarding the metabolic state. Furthermore, this study acknowledges the morphological heterogeneity of Blastocystis sp cultures. The results reflect the response of a pooled, heterogeneous population; the lack of characterization of specific life stages, such as the amoeboid form associated with increased resistance, remains a limitation of the current work.

At lower concentrations, the compounds 1 and 2 showed limited inhibitory activity against Blastocystis sp., which may reflect suboptimal cellular uptake or the need to reach a critical threshold for effective activity. Their comparatively reduced efficacy against ST7, relative to MTZ, suggest the presence of subtype-specific tolerance mechanisms, possibly associated with elevated cysteine protease activity. The absence of cytotoxicity data against normal human cell lines represents an important limitation and should be addressed in future studies to establish the selectivity index and safety profile of these alkaloids. While these findings underscore considerations relating to and subtype variability, they also indicate avenues for future optimization, including structural modification and combination therapy approaches, supporting the continued evaluation of these compounds as antiparasitic leads.

Conclusions

Our study reports the isolation of compounds 1 and 2 from LED-modulated E. longifolia hairy roots with demonstrated inhibitory effect against Blastocystis subtypes ST3 and ST7. Quantitative isolation data revealed that Mint Green and Red LED spectra were associated with increased the yields of these bioactive alkaloids. At the 0.1 mg/mL concentration, the compounds exhibited inhibitory effect comparable to MTZ, however, IC50 estimates indicate that MTZ remains more potent overall. Preliminary molecular docking suggests that these alkaloids interacts with parasitic cysteine proteases, providing a putative basis for their mechanism of action. Collectively, these findings supports the potentials of canthin-6-one derivatives as scaffolds for antiparasitic development, while highlighting the need for further studies evaluating efficacy across morphological forms and to establish their safety through comprehensive cytotoxicity testing.
