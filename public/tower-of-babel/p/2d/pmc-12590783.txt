Association between non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio and sarcopenia in US adults: a population-based study
Authors: Xin Li, Xinyue Duan, Jinjiang Li, Zhaoxiong Lin, Chunyan Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio (NHHR) serves as a lipid biomarker indicating the balance of pro-atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoproteins in the bloodstream. However, research exploring the effects of NHHR on sarcopenia remains limited. Consequently, this investigation aims to examine the potential correlation between NHHR and sarcopenia.MethodsThis nationwide study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2011 and 2018. We looked into the correlation of serum NHHR levels with the risk of sarcopenia through multivariable logistic regression. Furthermore, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess the robustness of the findings, and restricted cubic splines were utilized to investigate potential nonlinearities.ResultsThe study encompassed 5,491 participants, of whom 367 (6.68%) were diagnosed with sarcopenia. Elevated NHHR was found to have a significant positive correlation with the incidence of sarcopenia in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.06–1.21, P < 0.001). The occurrence of sarcopenia was 2.11 times higher in participants in the highest NHHR quartile than in the lowest quartile (95% CI: 1.13–3.92, P = 0.023). RCS analysis further demonstrated a nonlinear association of NHHR with sarcopenia (P for nonlinear < 0.001). Extensive subgroup and sensitivity analysis produced consistent outcomes, supported the validity of our findings.ConclusionStudies indicate that NHHR is linked to an elevated risk of sarcopenia among Americans. In an aging society, NHHR might be a useful biomarker for managing and forecasting sarcopenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01111-x.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a degenerative disorder characterized by a decline of muscle mass, strength, or physical performance, commonly linked to age and a variety of chronic illnesses [1]. It is estimated that 10–16% of the global elderly population is currently affected, and by 2050, over 200 million people worldwide are projected to suffer from this condition [2, 3]. Sarcopenia markedly increases the risk of falls, fractures, and mortality, thereby severely impairing quality of life. Additionally, the prolonged hospitalization and healthcare costs associated with sarcopenia impose a significant financial burden on healthcare systems [4]. Consequently, it is imperative that sarcopenia be identified early and prevented for everyone.

According to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), low muscle mass is the core diagnostic criterion for diagnosing sarcopenia, as it usually appears before declines in muscle strength and physical performance [5]. Research has shown that muscle mass begins to decline around the age of 30, with an accelerated loss after approximately 50 years, whereas muscle strength starts to decrease around the age of 40, accelerates after 60 years, and declines nearly three times faster than muscle mass [6–10]. This suggests that structural deterioration often precedes functional impairment. The process is accompanied by selective atrophy of type II muscle fibers and multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including inflammation, insulin resistance (IR), mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuromuscular junction degeneration, and hormonal changes [11–16]. Given the incomplete availability of strength and performance data, we operationally defined sarcopenia using muscle mass to ensure consistency and comparability in this study.

Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) includes various plasma lipoproteins, such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and lipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)], with the exception of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [17]. It is deemed a more accurate indicator of atherosclerotic risk than any single lipoprotein component and has been designated as a primary therapeutic target by the National Lipid Association [18]. HDL-C is recognized as a protective lipid due to its roles in reverse cholesterol transport as well as its anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties [19]. The non-HDL-C to HDL-C ratio (NHHR) is considered a novel composite lipid parameter, reflecting the equilibrium across atherogenic and protective lipoproteins in the blood. Compared to individual lipid markers, NHHR delivers a more comprehensive evaluation of lipid metabolism. Literature indicates that NHHR is significantly associated with various metabolic and inflammatory conditions, including type 2 diabetes mellitus [20], hypertension [21], hyperuricemia [22], and depression [23].

Despite this, most existing research on lipid metabolism and sarcopenia has concentrated on individual lipid parameters such as total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, and HDL-C, with limited investigation into composite indices like NHHR—particularly within non-oncologic populations. Considering the shared pathophysiological mechanisms between dyslipidemia and sarcopenia—such as systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and mitochondrial dysfunction [24, 25]—NHHR may serve as a relevant biomarker for sarcopenia risk assessment.

This study aims to evaluate the association between NHHR and sarcopenia utilizing data from a nationally representative U.S. cohort. We hypothesize that elevated NHHR levels are positively correlated with increased prevalence of sarcopenia and that NHHR could function as a potential predictive marker for sarcopenia, thereby informing clinical strategies for disease prevention and management.

Materials and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a nationwide study aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional status of the US population. This survey is conducted biennially and utilizes a complex stratified sampling design to ensure a representative sample. Detailed information is available on the official website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes). The research protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed permission was acquired from all participants.

This research included NHANES data obtained over four cycles spanning from 2011 to 2018, encompassing the sum of 39,156 individuals. initially, those under the age of 20 (n = 16,539) and those with self-reported cancer (n = 2,173) were not included. Subsequently, participants with missing data on sarcopenia components (n = 10,028) and NHHR components (n = 451) were eliminated. Furthermore, participants lacking covariate data (n = 4,474) were also removed. Ultimately, our research included 5,491 participants. Figure 1 depicts the detailed selection procedure.

The calculation of NHHR

NHHR was calculated using biomarkers obtained from blood samples. The formula for NHHR is defined as non-HDL-C divided by HDL-C. Non-HDL-C is obtained by deducting HDL-C from total cholesterol (TC).

Sarcopenia

In accordance with the guidelines published by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH), the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in this study were established using sarcopenia index thresholds of < 0.789 for males and < 0.512 for females [26]. The sarcopenia index is computed as appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) divided by body mass index (BMI). ASM is obtained through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and reflects the total lean body mass of the arms and legs, whereas BMI is derived through dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2).

Covariates

In this study, the covariates included age, gender, race, educational level, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), sedentary time, physical activity, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Physical activity was assessed based on the duration of moderate-intensity activities performed weekly, encompassing both occupational and recreational pursuits. Smoking status was assessed based on the criterion of having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life. Drinking status was defined as having consumed a minimum of 12 alcoholic beverages in the past year. Diabetes diagnosis was established through self-reporting, the use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications, fasting blood glucose levels exceeding 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels greater than 6.5% [27]. Hypertension was diagnosed according to a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications [28]. CVD included conditions such as coronary heart disease, angina, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke, all identified through self-reported questionnaires.

Statistical analyses

Due to the specific characteristics of NHANES, we employed one-quarter of the examination weights as new sample weights in our statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics of baseline features were performed based on whether sarcopenia was present or not. Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test or Fisher’s exact probability test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and analyzed using the chi-squared test.

Firstly, we performed weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis to assess the relationship between NHHR and sarcopenia across three distinct models. Model 1 represented an unadjusted crude model; Model 2 was adjusted for variables including age, gender, race, education level, and PIR; and Model 3 included additional adjustments for smoking status, drinking status, sedentary time, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, and CVD. Additionally, we transformed NHHR from a continuous into a categorical variable. Participants were stratified into four groups based on NHHR quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4), with the Q1 quartile acting as the reference group, to further investigate the linear trend between NHHR and sarcopenia. Subsequently, we then investigated potential nonlinear interactions between NHHR and sarcopenia using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis and threshold effect analysis.

In addition, we stratified the data by gender, race, education level, marital status, PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore the association of NHHR with sarcopenia across these different stratifications. Interaction tests were used to evaluate the consistency of associations across different subgroups, with results presented in forest plots. Finally, sensitivity analyses were applied to assess the robustness of the results.

Data was analyzed using R software (version 4.3.3), with a p-value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Association between NHHR and sarcopenia

All three models consistently demonstrated a positive association between every unit increase in NHHR and an increased risk of sarcopenia (P < 0.001). A linear trend analysis demonstrated a noteworthy correlation linking NHHR to sarcopenia incidence across three multivariate regression models (P for trend < 0.001).

Nonlinear association between the NHHR and sarcopenia

Figure 2 illustrates the nonlinear relationship between NHHR and sarcopenia based on RCS analysis. A nonlinear, non-significant inverse L-shaped correlation of NHHR with sarcopenia was found in the fully adjusted model (P for nonlinear < 0.001). Threshold effect analysis using segmented regression pinpointed a breakpoint at an NHHR value of 3.63, as shown in Table 3. Below this breakpoint, NHHR was substantially linked with an elevated incidence of sarcopenia (OR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.47–2.18, P < 0.001). However, beyond this breakpoint, the link lost significance (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.87–1.12, P = 0.863).

Subgroup analysis

Figure 3 illustrates the positive correlation of NHHR with sarcopenia across various subgroups. Nevertheless, this relationship was notablely influenced by drinking status and diabetes (all P for interaction < 0.05). Among drinkers, NHHR was more strongly associated with the risk of sarcopenia compared to non-drinkers (OR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.18–1.36). In participants without diabetes, NHHR showed a significant positive relationship with sarcopenia (OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.15–1.33).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the stability of the relationship of NHHR relative to sarcopenia, we performed an analysis of sensitivity. In the fully adjusted model, NHHR was still substantially linked to a higher risk of sarcopenia even after it was divided into tertiles (OR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.20–2.90, P = 0.007), as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Additionally, the association between NHHR and sarcopenia remained unchanged when those with a history of diabetes were excluded, as shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Discussion

To investigate the correlation of NHHR relative to sarcopenia, we performed a cross-sectional study with 5,491 adults from the NHANES dataset. This research indicated a positive connection between elevated NHHR and the risk of developing sarcopenia, which was consistent across three models. RCS curve further confirmed a nonlinear trend between NHHR and sarcopenia.

Currently, research on NHHR and sarcopenia has been limited to cancer populations; our study addresses this gap in non-cancer populations, indicating NHHR may be a potential factor in sarcopenia development. Although direct studies linking NHHR and sarcopenia are lacking, the role of various lipid metabolism indicators in sarcopenia has been widely discussed. Increasing evidence suggests that non-HDL-C is a better predictor of microvascular and endothelial dysfunction due to atherosclerosis than LDL-C, thus becoming a primary target for lipid management [17, 29]. Meta-analyses have reported that, compared with non-sarcopenic populations, individuals with sarcopenia had elevated serum TC, TG, and LDL levels and reduced HDL-C levels, which is consistent with the findings of our study [30].

However, there is conflicting evidence regarding the relationship between lipoproteins and sarcopenia. A cross-sectional study in elderly individuals from Eastern China found that sarcopenic patients had lower serum non-HDL-C and TC levels, but higher HDL-C levels [31]. Similarly, a four-year longitudinal study reported that elevated HDL levels were associated with heightened sarcopenia risk [32, 33]. Two mechanisms may explain this: extremely high HDL-C levels can render HDL particles dysfunctional, weakening their anti-inflammatory capacity or even exerting pro-inflammatory effects, which over time can promote muscle protein breakdown and inhibit protein synthesis [34, 35]; genetic variants such as CETP or LCAT deficiencies can lead to abnormally high HDL-C, often with other metabolic abnormalities, increasing muscle loss risk [36, 37].Furthermore, another study in 303 hospitalized elderly patients found no statistically significant association between HDL levels and sarcopenia, while demonstrating that elevated serum TC, TG, and LDL levels within normal ranges may exert protective effects against sarcopenia [38]. These differences suggest potential threshold effects and population specificity in the relationship between lipid metabolism and sarcopenia.

Lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle cells plays a key role in energy production, with approximately two-thirds of resting skeletal muscle energy derived from lipid oxidation [39]. Fatty acids and their lipid metabolites regulate muscle mass and function by modulating the growth, proliferation, and differentiation of muscle cells [40]. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms connecting sarcopenia to dysregulated lipid metabolism remain insufficiently explored.

To further elucidate this connection, several mechanisms may underlie the association between NHHR and sarcopenia. First, chronic inflammation may mediate this association, as elevated 7-ketocholesterol and 7β-hydroxycholesterol levels have been linked to increased TNF-α and IL-8 in patients with sarcopenia [41]. Beneficial lipids, such as omega-3 fatty acids, may exert protective effects by mitigating low-grade inflammation [42]. Second, lipid droplet accumulation in muscle cells can impair mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, leading to cellular damage and muscle atrophy [43]. Third, dysregulated lipolysis and excess reactive oxygen species may disrupt protein synthesis through oxidative stress and protein misfolding [44]. Finally, lipid-induced insulin resistance may reduce glucose utilization and energy production, further compromising muscle mass and strength [45, 46]. Taken together, these pathways suggest that elevated NHHR may reflect cumulative inflammatory, oxidative, and metabolic stress, offering insight into its link with sarcopenia.

Our study found a stronger association of NHHR in relation to sarcopenia for drinkers compared to non-drinkers. This finding suggests that managing both lipid levels and alcohol intake is essential in the prevention or intervention of sarcopenia. Drinking disrupts lipid and protein metabolism, leading to reduced muscle protein synthesis or increased degradation, which may accelerate sarcopenia progression. Furthermore, drinking heightens oxidative stress and inflammation, synergizing with elevated NHHR to increase the risk of sarcopenia.

Sarcopenia is not only a natural consequence of aging but also secondary to various chronic conditions. Diabetes plays a significant role in the relationship between lipid metabolism and sarcopenia. It is often accompanied by complex metabolic disturbances including IR, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, abnormal protein metabolism, and vascular disorders [47], all of which contribute to impaired muscle energy metabolism and muscle mass decline. These mechanisms partly overlap with NHHR’s pathways in sarcopenia development, exerting both direct and indirect effects on sarcopenia risk. Our results presented a stronger association of NHHR and sarcopenia in non-diabetic individuals, possibly because diabetes management typically involves medications that indirectly protect muscle mass, thereby weakening NHHR’s impact on sarcopenia. As a result, the influence of diabetes complicates the link between NHHR and sarcopenia.

In this study, NHHR is proposed as a potential prognostic biomarker for the early detection and management of sarcopenia, thereby informing clinical strategies for disease prevention and intervention. NHHR can be derived from routine lipid profile assessments, offering a cost-effective and widely applicable tool in clinical practice. Furthermore, as a composite index, NHHR integrates atherogenic and cardioprotective lipoprotein components, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of lipid metabolism. Regular monitoring of lipid status may facilitate the early identification of individuals at high risk for sarcopenia and enable timely intervention, presenting new avenues for public health management targeting this population.

Our research presents certain advantages. Firstly, it utilizes an extensive, nationally representative sample of people in the US, enhancing the credibility and generalizability of the findings. Secondly, all analyses were rigorously adjusted for potential confounders, and the use of subgroup and sensitivity analyses strengthens the stability of the results.

Nevertheless, some limitations must be considered. First, as a cross-sectional study, causality between NHHR and sarcopenia cannot be established. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are crucial to establishing causality. Second, while we observed a significant positive association between NHHR and sarcopenia, it remains unclear whether NHHR is a contributing risk factor or merely a byproduct of the condition. Despite adjustments for confounders, unmeasured variables may still have influenced the results. Third, this study concentrated on middle-aged U.S. adults, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other ethnic groups or older individuals. Finally, the study results may be influenced by variations in measurement methods and diagnostic criteria. On the one hand, different muscle mass assessment techniques, such as DXA and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), can introduce systematic biases into evaluation results. On the other hand, there is no unified definition of sarcopenia, with threshold differences across guidelines such as EWGSOP, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) and FNIH. These factors can affect both the accuracy of the results and their comparability across studies. In this study, sarcopenia was defined based on low muscle mass, which is suitable for large-scale epidemiological research but may overestimate the true prevalence. Future studies should incorporate multiple diagnostic indicators to further validate and expand upon our findings.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that NHHR might be a useful biomarker for forecasting and managing sarcopenia in Americans. By regularly monitoring NHHR, clinicians could more effectively identify individuals at high risk for sarcopenia, allowing for timely and targeted interventions. These findings offer new perspectives on the function of lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle in the context of an aging population; however, additional research is necessary to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
