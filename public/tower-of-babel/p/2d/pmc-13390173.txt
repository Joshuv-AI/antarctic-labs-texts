Association of serum spermidine level with vascular calcification in peritoneal dialysis patients
Authors: Dan Luo, Xueqi Liu, Jinzhong Chen, Qimei Luo, Shuting Li, Zhanjun Jia, Mi Liu, Xianrui Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular calcification (VC) is a major predictor of mortality in patients receiving peritoneal dialysis (PD), yet reliable tools for early risk stratification remain limited. We investigated whether serum spermidine, a naturally occurring polyamine, is associated with VC in PD patients and developed an exploratory model incorporating spermidine and routine clinical variables. In a cohort of 193 PD patients, VC (n = 51) was assessed by abdominal X-ray. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with VC, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection and nomogram construction. Serum spermidine levels were significantly higher in patients with VC than in those without VC (median 7.02 vs. 5.63 nmol/L, p = 0.006) and remained independently associated with VC in multivariate analysis (odds ratio 1.16, 95% confidence interval 1.02–1.32, p = 0.026). The final nomogram, which included serum spermidine, age, diabetes, serum albumin, statins use, and peritonitis history, demonstrated good discrimination (area under the curve 0.795) and internal validity (C-index 0.751). These findings suggest that serum spermidine is associated with VC in PD patients and may have value as a candidate biomarker. The proposed nomogram should be considered exploratory, and its clinical utility requires validation in prospective multicenter studies.Keywords: Serum spermidine, vascular calcification, peritoneal dialysis, nomogram, associated factors

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) serves as a vital renal replacement therapy for end-stage renal disease and is widely employed globally [1]. With advancements in dialysis technology and extended patient survival, the management of long-term complications in PD patients has become an increasing clinical concern. Among these, vascular calcification (VC) is particularly critical due to its high prevalence and direct impact on mortality [2,3]. A Chinese cohort study observed accelerated VC progression in 86.5% of 1,489 adult dialysis patients [4]. Furthermore, abdominal aortic calcification has been established as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality, especially in the PD population [5]. While traditional risk factors, including age, diabetes, and disorders of mineral metabolism, are established contributors [6], they also highlight the need to identify additional biomarkers that may improve VC risk stratification.

Spermidine, a naturally occurring polyamine, is an essential metabolite for maintaining cellular homeostasis [7], participating in key processes such as autophagy, oxidative stress response, and immune modulation [8,9]. Under physiological conditions, spermidine levels in the human body are maintained through a dynamic balance involving endogenous biosynthesis, exogenous dietary intake, and gut microbial metabolism [10]. The progression of renal impairment is paralleled by a gradual decline in plasma spermidine levels in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [11]. Compared to non-dialysis CKD patients, PD patients experience unique metabolic alterations. The persistent stimulation from glucose-containing dialysate easily induces disorders in glucose and lipid metabolism along with bioincompatibility reactions, triggering peritoneal microinflammation and ultimately leading to dysregulation of the intraperitoneal environment [12]. High susceptibility to peritonitis significantly alters the gut microbiota composition in PD patients [13]. These factors collectively contribute to the distinct metabolic profile of PD patients, which may consequently disrupt spermidine metabolic homeostasis. More importantly, dietary spermidine intake shows an inverse correlation with cardiovascular disease risk [14]. Mechanistically, research indicates that spermidine could significantly alleviate CKD-related VC by upregulating SIRT1 expression and inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress [15]. While fundamental studies suggest a protective role of spermidine in vascular health, its association with VC in PD patients remains unexplored. Clarifying this relationship is clinically important for understanding the role of spermidine and other candidate biomarkers in VC.

Based on the KDIGO guidelines, the assessment of VC should be incorporated into routine follow-up for CKD patients [16,17]. However, clinical diagnosis and monitoring of VC remain challenging due to its frequently asymptomatic nature during early stages. While the guidelines recommend abdominal lateral X-ray and echocardiography as practical alternatives to CT for detecting VC and cardiac valve calcification [16], reliance on imaging biomarkers alone has limitations, particularly in identifying early-phase VC, which is often not readily apparent on conventional imaging studies. Given the challenges in diagnosing and managing VC, circulating biomarkers may provide useful information for future studies aimed at improving VC assessment in high-risk populations.

This study aims to investigate the association between serum spermidine and VC in PD patients and to explore whether spermidine, integrated with clinical parameters, may have exploratory value for VC risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This was a cross-sectional study of continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) patients from a single center in China. Participants who were treated at the Division of Nephrology in The Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University (The First People’s Hospital of Shunde, Foshan) were enrolled. Eligible participants were men or women older than 18 years and who had received CAPD for at least 3 months and were regularly followed up at the PD center. The major exclusion criteria included (1) active peritonitis or other systemic/localized infections at enrollment; (2) within 4 weeks after completion of antibiotic therapy for peritonitis; (3) acute exacerbation of major comorbidities; (4) major surgery/trauma within 3 months prior to enrollment; and (5) severe gastrointestinal disorders that might affect dietary intake, nutrient absorption, or spermidine metabolism. The period of enrollment was February 2025 to August 2025, during which a total of 193 patients were recruited. The biological samples and clinical data of patients were obtained by the way of de-labeling personal information of the participants in order to ensure complete protection of privacy in this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion.

Measurement of serum spermidine

The blood sample was obtained within 24 h of admission when they attended the PD follow-up clinic. Serum was stored at −80 °C in the Biobank of The Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University (The First People’s Hospital of Shunde, Foshan) after secondary centrifugation for 10 min at 3,000 rpm, until assayed in a single batch for blinded determination of serum spermidine.

Serum concentrations of spermidine were quantified using a commercial sandwich ELISA kit (Cat#LV10880, Animalunion Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) per the manufacturer’s protocol. The target protein was captured by a pre-coated antibody and detected by a biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by streptavidin–HRP conjugation. Color was developed using TMB substrate, and the reaction was stopped with acid. The optical density was measured at 450 nm, and sample concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve. All samples were assayed directly without dilution, with each measurement performed in duplicate. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, it specifically detects human spermidine with no significant cross-reactivity with analogous polyamines. Both intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were confirmed to be below 10%. The lower limit of quantification is 0.312 ng/mL. The assay demonstrates a linear range from 1.56 to 100 ng/mL (R2 > 0.99). The manufacturer’s manual can be accessed online through this website (http://www.animaluni.com/index.php?m=home&c=View&a=index&aid=5481).

Assessment of vascular calcification

Vascular calcification was assessed using a validated semi‑quantitative scoring method based on abdominal lateral radiography, as described by Kauppila et al. [18]. For predicting mortality in PD patients, abdominal aortic calcification demonstrates superior predictive value compared to calcification in other arterial beds [5]. Briefly, the abdominal aorta adjacent to the first four lumbar vertebrae was divided into four segments, with the midpoint of each intervertebral space serving as the boundary. Both the anterior and posterior wall segments were evaluated separately. Calcified deposits were graded on a scale from 0 to 3: grade 0 indicated no calcification; grade 1, scattered deposits involving less than one‑third of the aortic wall; grade 2, calcification extending over one‑third to two‑thirds of the wall; and grade 3, calcification affecting at least two‑thirds of the wall. The scores from all eight segments were summed to yield a total Kauppila calcification score ranging from 0 to 24, where 0 represented no calcification and any score >0 indicated the presence of calcification [19]. All radiographic images were independently reviewed by two experienced clinical radiologists who were blinded to the patients’ clinical data. In cases of disagreement between the two readers, the images were re‑examined jointly, discussed, and a consensus score was assigned.

Data collection and definitions

All the biochemical and hematological tests were examined in our hospital. Medication use was ascertained from prescription records during the preceding three months to minimize underestimation of exposure. Residual renal function (RRF) and total Kt/V were calculated using PD Adequest software 2.0 (Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Chicago, IL). RRF was determined as the average of 24-h urinary urea and creatinine clearances adjusted for body surface area [20]. The adequacy of PD is commonly assessed by total solute clearance, quantified as the total weekly Kt/V urea. In this metric, ‘K’ represents the urea clearance (in mL/min), ‘t’ is the dialysis time, and ‘V’ denotes the volume of urea distribution, which approximates total body water. The total Kt/V encompasses clearance from both PD (Kt/V PD) and any RRF (Kt/V renal).

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic characteristics were stratified by VC. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normal data were presented as median (interquartile ranges). Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze the categorical variables. In contrast, the Welch two sample t-test or rank-sum test was used to examine the continuous variables. The associations between serum spermidine levels and clinical variables were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis. To select variables for model construction, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was applied. Multivariable logistic regression was then used to estimate the independent association of selected variables (including serum spermidine) with VC. A nomogram was developed based on the final logistic model. Its discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with internal validation performed via 1,000-cycle bootstrap resampling to obtain a bias-corrected concordance index (C-index). Calibration was assessed with a calibration plot comparing predicted probabilities against observed outcomes. Decision-curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical net benefit across a range of probability thresholds. To evaluate the incremental value of serum spermidine, we constructed three models. Model 1 included serum spermidine alone. Model 2 incorporated serum spermidine and the variables selected by LASSO regression. Model 3 combined serum spermidine with established traditional risk factors for VC, including age, diabetes, serum phosphorus, serum corrected calcium, history of peritonitis, statin use, and phosphate binder use. These variables were selected a priori based on prior literature and clinical relevance in VC related research. Specifically, age and diabetes have been repeatedly associated with VC in ESRD populations [21]; serum phosphorus and corrected calcium were included because mineral metabolism abnormalities are central to CKD-MBD and VC [22]; phosphate-binder exposure was included because calcium-based phosphate-lowering therapy may influence calcification burden [23]. In addition, peritonitis history was included as a PD-specific marker of cumulative inflammatory burden, given evidence that systemic inflammatory responses are associated with VC in patients with ESRD [24]. Statin use was included as a treatment-related covariate reflecting baseline cardiovascular risk and lipid-lowering therapy exposure [25]. Results with a two-sided p value of <0.05 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.2, Vienna, Austria) and MSTATA software (www.mstata.com).

Results

Baseline characteristics stratified by vascular calcification

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population, stratified by VC, are presented in Table 1. This study of 193 participants (51 with VC) revealed significantly higher serum spermidine levels in the VC group (7.02 vs. 5.63 ng/mL, p = 0.006). Key demographic disparities included a substantially older age in VC patients (58 vs. 47 years, p < 0.001) and a longer dialysis vintage (50 vs. 38 months, p = 0.040). The VC group also exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes (47.1% vs. 17.6%, p < 0.001) and peritonitis history (52.9% vs. 31.0%, p = 0.005), along with elevated fasting glucose, CRP, and eGFR, but lower serum albumin, magnesium, and total cholesterol (all p < 0.05). The use of statins (56.9% vs. 28.2%, p < 0.001) was significantly more common in the VC group. Conversely, the use of phosphate binders (43.1% vs. 65.5%, p = 0.005) and calcimimetics (68.6% vs. 83.1%, p = 0.029) was significantly less frequent in the calcification group. Differences in the use of vitamin D analogues (p = 0.631) did not reach statistical significance. These comprehensive findings highlight the multifactorial nature of VC risk, which involves metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, pharmacological, and dialysis duration factors.

Factors associated with vascular calcification

Univariate analysis identified several variables associated with VC, including serum spermidine, age, diabetes, eGFR, serum albumin, magnesium, peritonitis history, statin use, calcimimetic use, and phosphate binder use. After adjustment for traditional risk factors (serum spermidine, age, diabetes, serum phosphorus, corrected serum calcium, history of peritonitis, statin use, and phosphate binder use), the multivariate model identified independent associations with VC for both serum spermidine (adjusted OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32, p = 0.024) and diabetes (adjusted OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.39–7.01, p = 0.006) (Table S1). A restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis revealed a significant, linear dose–response relationship between serum spermidine and VC risk (p-overall = 0.030; p for nonlinearity = 0.855) (Figure S1). Furthermore, serum spermidine exhibited a weak negative correlation only with total Kt/V (Spearman’s r = −0.22, p = 0.008), suggesting relative independence from other clinical parameters (Table S2).

Variable selection via LASSO regression

To identify the variables most strongly associated with VC, we performed LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation. The coefficient profiles (Figure 1) illustrate the regularization path, demonstrating how variable coefficients were shrunk toward zero as the penalty parameter increased. At the optimal tuning parameter selected by cross-validation, six variables with non-zero coefficients were retained from an initial set of 19 variables. These variables included serum spermidine (coefficient = 0.011), age (coefficient = 0.002), diabetes (coefficient = 0.487), statins use (coefficient = 0.152), and history of peritonitis (coefficient = 0.057), all of which had positive coefficients in the selected model, as well as serum albumin (coefficient = −0.056), which had a negative coefficient. The intercept of the selected model was 0.479. The selected variables were used for subsequent nomogram construction (Table S3).

Development of a nomogram for vascular calcification risk assessment

A nomogram for VC risk assessment was constructed using the six variables identified by LASSO regression: serum spermidine, age, diabetes status, serum albumin, statins use, and history of peritonitis (Figure 2). Multivariable logistic regression showed that serum spermidine (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32, p = 0.026) was independently associated with higher odds of VC, whereas serum albumin (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96, p = 0.004) was associated with lower odds of VC. Diabetes (OR 3.05, 95% CI 1.35–6.87, p = 0.007) and history of peritonitis (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.04–4.96, p = 0.039) were associated with higher odds of VC. Age and statin use were not significantly associated with VC (Table 2).

Nomogram prediction model. The nomogram is utilized by first locating a patient’s value for each variable on the corresponding top scale (‘Points’) and drawing a line upward to the ‘Points’ axis to obtain an individual score for each parameter. The sum of these scores yields the ‘Total Points,’ which is then projected downward to the ‘Linear Predictor’ and finally to the ‘Risk of Outcome’ scale. This final scale provides the estimated probability of the outcome occurring, expressed as a percentage.

Discriminative ability of models incorporating serum spermidine

The discriminatory ability of different models for identifying VC was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Figure 3). As summarized in Table 3, the univariate model, which included serum spermidine alone, yielded an AUC of 0.629 (95% CI 0.541–0.718, p = 0.004). Subsequently, two multivariate models were constructed and evaluated. Model 2, which incorporated variables selected by Lasso regression – including age, diabetes status, statins use, history of peritonitis, serum spermidine, and serum albumin – achieved an AUC of 0.795 (95% CI 0.720–0.870, p < 0.001). An internal validation was performed using bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples, yielding a bias-corrected Harrell’s C-index of 0.751. Decision curve analysis confirmed the model’s clinical value, demonstrating a positive net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Figure S2). For comparative purposes, a second multivariate model (model 3) was also developed by integrating serum spermidine with conventional clinical risk factors (age, diabetes, peritonitis history, serum phosphorus, corrected calcium, and use of statins or phosphate binders), resulting in an AUC of 0.774 (95% CI 0.693–0.855, p < 0.001). DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves showed no statistically significant difference between the two multivariate models (test statistic = 1.369, p = 0.171) (Table S4). Discussion

This study provides preliminary evidence of an association between serum spermidine levels and VC in PD patients. We developed an exploratory nomogram integrating serum spermidine and clinical variables (age, diabetes status, statins use, history of peritonitis, and serum albumin) to examine their combined relationship with VC in this cohort. In the model, serum spermidine was independently associated with VC (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32, p = 0.026), and the nomogram showed acceptable discrimination (AUC 0.795) and internal consistency (corrected C-index 0.751). These findings suggest that a combined biomarker-clinical factor approach may be useful for understanding VC-related patterns in PD patients, but external validation and prospective studies are needed before any clinical application.

During the progression of CKD, the accumulation of uremic metabolites and circulating toxins perpetually stresses the vascular system, thereby promoting VC through multiple interconnected pathways [26,27]. These established mechanisms include the induction of inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of calcium-phosphate metabolism. In addition, emerging processes such as DNA damage, cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming may also contribute to VC by promoting the osteogenic transformation of VSMCs [28–30]. Against this complex pathological background, spermidine may represent a biologically relevant mediator in VC pathogenesis.

The observed association between serum spermidine and VC provides preliminary evidence that merits further investigation, but it does not establish causality. This finding may be interpreted from two non-mutually exclusive perspectives, each with potential pathophysiological implications.

The first perspective posits that elevated serum spermidine may reflect alterations in polyamine metabolism within the status of VC within the uremic milieu. Renal failure disrupts the polyamine metabolic pathways of synthesis and degradation [11,31], while PD-associated factors, such as dialysis solutions and peritonitis, further alter intestinal absorption and gut microbiota composition [32], potentially contributing to aberrant spermidine accumulation. This interpretation is supported by the observed weak negative correlation between serum spermidine and dialysis adequacy (Kt/V), suggesting that poor solute clearance might contribute to its systemic accumulation. Furthermore, impaired cellular uptake or utilization of spermidine may contribute to its accumulation in serum and may be accompanied by altered cellular function. A study using cultured intestinal cells has shown that elevated spermidine concentrations induce dose-dependent cytotoxicity, characterized by reduced cell viability, morphological abnormalities, and other toxicological responses [33]. Another study showed that elevated whole blood spermidine levels, resulting in a decreased spermine to spermidine ratio, were positively associated with sarcopenia in hemodialysis patients [34]. Taken together, these findings suggest that elevated serum spermidine may be a marker of disturbed metabolic homeostasis in uremia.

Alternatively, elevated serum spermidine may reflect a compensatory and protective response to VC-associated stress. This perspective is biologically plausible, given that spermidine has been linked to cardiorenal protection through mechanisms such as enhanced autophagy, inhibition of oxidative stress, immune modulation, and promotion of cellular repair [14,35,36]. In this context, increased serum spermidine may reflect an adaptive response to vascular injury or metabolic stress rather than a direct pathogenic driver. This interpretation is consistent with animal studies showing that spermidine supplementation suppressed VC [15], and increased spermidine production by gut microbiota alleviated atherosclerosis [37]. However, a detailed dietary assessment was not performed in this study; therefore, the potential influence of dietary intake and gastrointestinal factors on serum spermidine levels cannot be fully excluded.

Together, these interpretations highlight the context-dependent biology of polyamines in uremia. Future longitudinal studies that track temporal changes in serum spermidine and VC progression will be required to clarify the directionality of this association. Such studies may also help determine whether modulation of spermidine could have therapeutic relevance in this high-risk population. Although spermidine supplementation represents a promising frontier [8,38], evidence remains limited regarding its long-term safety and efficacy. Therefore, routine unsupervised use cannot be recommended at present.

This study is strengthened by a rigorous statistical approach to model development. We employed LASSO regression, a method that reduces overfitting and selects the most relevant predictors from a set of candidate variables. The resulting nomogram integrates age, serum spermidine, serum albumin, diabetes status, and history of peritonitis, and may help summarize the combined association of these variables with VC in this cohort. The model demonstrated good discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.795, which was superior to a model based on spermidine alone (AUC = 0.629). Beyond its statistical performance, the selected variables highlight several clinically relevant correlates. First, the inverse association between serum albumin and VC underscores the importance of nutritional status and inflammation in PD patients, consistent with its established role in the malnutrition–inflammation–atherosclerosis (MIA) syndrome and as a predictor of cardiovascular outcomes in CKD [39,40]. Second, the association between a history of peritonitis and VC supports the notion that prior inflammatory burden may be linked to VC, as peritonitis can induce systemic inflammatory responses [41,42]. Third, diabetes is a well-documented risk factor for vascular pathology and calcification in CKD [43,44]. Overall, this nomogram should be interpreted as an exploratory model that reflects the combined relationships between clinical variables and VC in this study, and its clinical utility requires confirmation in prospective external validation studies.

On the other hand, our study showed that the absence of significant associations between VC and several traditional risk factors, such as serum calcium, phosphorus, and iPTH, warrants careful consideration. Although elevated levels of these parameters are established risk factors for accelerated VC progression [4], they are subject to close monitoring and targeted pharmacological intervention (e.g., phosphate binders, vitamin D analogues) in clinical practice to maintain target ranges. As a result, their circulating levels may not reliably reflect the extent or progression of VC, which could explain the lack of statistically significant correlations observed in some clinical cohorts. Nevertheless, the performance of the model incorporating these parameters (model 3: AUC = 0.774) remains informative. This suggests that even when maintained within a relatively narrow range through management, these mineral metabolism parameters continue to contribute meaningfully to VC risk. In this context, models 2 and 3, which integrate serum spermidine with clinical variables, showed better discriminatory performance than spermidine alone in this cohort. Although model 2 (LASSO-derived) and model 3 (clinically guided) performed comparably, model 2 offers a more parsimonious variable set without compromising discriminative ability, suggesting that it may be useful as an exploratory summary model for future validation rather than a clinical risk stratification tool.

Several limitations should be considered. First, its cross-sectional, single-center design and the lack of external validation limit causal inference and generalizability. Second, the relatively small sample size may have affected the statistical stability of the analyses. Third, VC assessment by abdominal radiography may underestimate prevalence due to lower sensitivity than computed tomography (CT). Fourth, while practical, the ELISA method for spermidine is less specific than LC–MS/MS. Future studies require longitudinal, multicenter designs, advanced imaging, and mass spectrometry validation.

Nevertheless, our findings may still have exploratory value. Serum spermidine should not be viewed as a direct therapeutic target at this stage, but rather as a candidate biomarker for further investigation. In this context, its combination with the widely available abdominal X-ray may provide a useful framework for future methodological studies examining VC-related patterns in PD patients. More broadly, this study highlights a potentially complex and context-dependent role of spermidine in uremic vascular disease, warranting future longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify its pathophysiological significance.

Conclusions

Our study provides preliminary evidence that serum spermidine is associated with VC in PD patients and presents an exploratory nomogram integrating spermidine with readily available clinical variables. These findings offer new insights into the potential role of spermidine in VC and warrant further longitudinal and external validation studies.
