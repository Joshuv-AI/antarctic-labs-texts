Gut microbiota profiles in reactive and metastatic lymphadenopathy in colorectal cancer: a tumor location–stratified analysis
Authors: Toru Miyake, Masatsugu Kojima, Soichiro Tani, Keiji Muramoto, Hiromitsu Maehira, Sachiko Kaida, Tomoharu Shimizu, Masaji Tani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePreoperative lymphadenopathy in colorectal cancer may represent nodal metastasis or reactive change. We aimed to compare gut microbiota profiles between reactive and metastatic lymphadenopathy and to explore microbial features associated with nonmetastatic lymph node enlargement.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study of colorectal cancer patients with radiological lymphadenopathy who underwent resection at Shiga University of Medical Science Hospital between 2018 and 2021. For the primary analysis, we included patients with radiological lymphadenopathy on preoperative CT. An additional analysis restricted to right-sided colon cancer compared no lymphadenopathy without pathological lymph node metastasis (NN), lymphadenopathy without pathological lymph node metastasis (PN), and lymphadenopathy with pathological lymph node metastasis (PP).ResultsPathological nodal metastasis was identified in 34 patients, whereas 29 had reactive lymphadenopathy. Fecal samples showed higher alpha diversity than tumor tissues. In left-sided colorectal cancer, no notable bacterial taxa exceeded the predefined LDA threshold. In right-sided colon cancer, Proteobacteria were enriched in metastatic cases, whereas Firmicutes were more abundant in reactive lymphadenopathy. Predicted pathway analysis suggested distinct metabolic profiles between the two groups. In the additional right-sided analysis, alpha and beta diversity did not significantly differ among NN, PN, and PP, although taxon-level differences were observed between NN and PN.ConclusionGut microbiota profiles differed between reactive and metastatic lymphadenopathy in colorectal cancer, with more distinct findings in right-sided tumors. These findings suggest an association between microbial patterns and reactive lymphadenopathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05154-2.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Microbiome, Lymph node metastasis, Lymphadenopathy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer globally, and recent increases in advanced cancers, along with a rising incidence of colorectal cancer in patients under 50 years old, highlight the necessity for social interventions [1, 2]. Lymph node metastasis is a critical prognostic factor in colorectal cancer [3, 4], and accurate preoperative diagnosis of lymph node involvement is essential. When lymphadenopathy is identified near the tumor in preoperative imaging, such as CT scans, lymphadenopathy is detected as lymph node metastasis and clinically diagnosed as stage III. Nonetheless, even in cases of significant preoperative lymphadenopathy, histological analysis occasionally shows no evidence of cancer metastasis to the lymph nodes. In such cases, reactive lymphadenopathy might result in overdiagnosis. Currently, postoperative histopathological evaluation is the most reliable method for diagnosing lymph node metastasis, as preoperative lymph node size does not reliably indicate metastasis [5], and PET imaging has high specificity but low sensitivity [6]. The mechanisms and precise methods used for distinguishing between reactive lymphadenopathy and lymph node metastases in colorectal cancer remain insufficient.

Gut bacteria have been shown to affect not only gastrointestinal diseases such as periodontal disease and inflammatory bowel disease but also metabolic diseases like type 2 diabetes and obesity, as well as neurological disorders [7]. Culturing has been the principal technique for identifying gut bacteria; despite this, numerous such bacteria are anaerobic and challenging to culture. However, next-generation sequencing (NGS) now enables detailed analyses of the gut microbiota [8]. The role of the gut microbiota in colorectal cancer, particularly in tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy, has garnered attention [9]. Fusobacterium is implicated in colorectal cancer development [10], and Bacteroides fragilis is abundant in colorectal cancer tissue and associated with tumorigenesis [11]. Colorectal tumors differ in origin and gene expression profiles depending on their location (right vs left colon) [12]. Emerging evidence also suggests variation in tumor-associated microbiota by tumor site [13]. For example, previous studies have reported enrichment of Fusobacterium in left-sided colorectal cancer, whereas genera such as Blautia, Faecalibacterium, and Subdoligranulum have been reported more frequently in right-sided tumors [14].

We hypothesized that differences in the gut microbiota might contribute to reactive lymphadenopathy without metastasis. Although lymph node status in colon cancer does not usually alter the preoperative surgical plan or indicate neoadjuvant therapy, distinguishing reactive from metastatic lymphadenopathy may improve biological understanding of radiologically enlarged lymph nodes and may help identify microbiome-associated markers for more accurate preoperative interpretation in future studies. This study aimed to compare the gut microbiota in reactive lymphadenopathy and metastatic lymphadenopathy cases in colorectal cancer, using fecal and tumor-associated microbiota profiles.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 130 colorectal cancer patients who underwent resection at Shiga University of Medical Science Hospital between August 2018 and August 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. For the primary analysis, 63 patients with radiological lymphadenopathy on preoperative CT were included. Clinical staging was determined preoperatively based on imaging findings, in accordance with the UICC TNM Classification, eighth edition. Lymphadenopathy was defined as lymph nodes with a short-axis diameter of ≥ 5 mm on CT. Reactive lymphadenopathy was defined as lymph node enlargement identified on preoperative imaging and initially interpreted as metastatic but with no lymph node metastasis detected on postoperative pathological examination. Pathological staging was based on postoperative histological examination. For the additional analysis, we focused on right-sided colon cancer and identified 41 cases from the study cohort. These cases were classified according to radiological lymphadenopathy and pathological lymph node status into four groups: patients without lymphadenopathy and without pathological lymph node metastasis (NN, n = 13), patients without lymphadenopathy but with pathological lymph node metastasis (NP, n = 2), patients with lymphadenopathy but without pathological lymph node metastasis (PN, n = 14), and patients with lymphadenopathy and pathological lymph node metastasis (PP, n = 12). Because the predefined additional three-group framework consisted of NN, PN, and PP, the two NP cases were excluded from the additional three-group analysis. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiga University of Medical Science (No. R2018-037). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Preoperative fecal samples were collected from colorectal cancer patients. Fecal samples were collected before bowel preparation and before administration of prophylactic antibiotics. As preoperative preparation, mechanical bowel preparation was performed and 750 mg of metronidazole was administered twice on the day before surgery. In addition, 1 g of cefmetazole was administered prior to surgery. Bacterial specimens were obtained from a piece of the resected tumor and stored at −80 °C. DNA was extracted using the MPure Bacterial DNA Extraction Kit (MP Bio).

Analysis of microbiome

DNA libraries were generated using a two-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) method. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using a mixed primer set (5′-ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-NNNNN-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ and 5′-GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT-NNNNN-GACTACHVGGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). PCR products were purified with AMPure XP (BECKMAN COULTER). Paired-end sequencing was performed on the V3–V4 region using the MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, CA, USA). DNA preparation and 16S rRNA gene sequencing were outsourced to Seibutugiken. Sequence data were analyzed using the Qiime2 package. After chimera and noise sequences were removed with Qiime2’s dada2 plugin, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were classified using the Silva 138 database (99% OTU full-length sequences). Mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were excluded.

Functional prediction analysis

PICRUSt2 software (version 2024.5) was used to predict gene function based on 16S rRNA marker gene sequences [15]. Statistical Analysis of Metabolic Profiles (STAMP, version 2.1.3) was employed to examine differences in predicted metabolic pathways between groups. Welch’s t-test was used for comparing the dominance of each metabolic pathway, with multiple testing correction applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A q value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were visualized using bar plots, displaying confidence intervals for each metabolic pathway.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Qiime2 package. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, while permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was employed for beta diversity analysis. Biomarker identification was carried out using the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size algorithm (LEfSe). Pathway analysis was conducted using PICRUSt2 in Qiime2, utilizing the Metabolic Pathway Database, and subsequently assessed through LEfSe.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 130 consecutive colorectal cancer patients who underwent resection during the study period were retrospectively reviewed. For the primary analysis, 63 patients included in the study had radiologically diagnosed lymphadenopathy based on preoperative imaging. Of these, 34 patients (54%) were confirmed to have pathological lymph node metastases, while 29 patients (46%) had no pathological metastasis and were classified as having reactive lymphadenopathy (Fig. 1). Gut flora

The bacterial flora within the tumor mucosa differs from the bacterial flora in feces [16]. Therefore, we first compared the bacterial flora between tumor tissue and feces samples. The alpha diversity, indicating the diversity within a sample, was higher in feces samples compared to tumor tissue. Specifically, the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) median for feces samples was 105 (interquartile range 84–124, p value < 0.001), while that for tumor tissue was 21.5 (interquartile range 14.75–39.0, p value < 0.001). The Shannon index showed similar results, with feces samples having a higher index (5.093 [4.555–5.460], p < 0.001) than tumor tissue (2.743 [1.866–3.862], p value < 0.001) (Fig. 2a–c). Beta diversity, measured by the Bray–Curtis index (p value = 0.001) and the Jaccard index (p value = 0.001), also showed significant differences between the two sample types (Fig. 2d, e). In tumor tissue, Proteobacteria were significantly increased (LDA score 4.903, p value < 0.001), whereas feces samples had a higher abundance of Firmicutes (LDA score 4.825, p < 0.001) and Bacteroidota (LDA score 4.396, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2f–i).

Lymph node metastasis and gut microbiota

Pathological lymph node metastasis was observed in 34 patients (54%). Right-sided and left-sided colorectal cancers have been shown to differ in origin and gene expression profiling [12]. In right-sided colon cancer, methylation and genetic mutations are known to vary [17]. We analyzed tumor tissue microbiota separately for right-sided and left-sided colorectal cancer, comparing cases with lymph node metastasis to those with reactive lymphadenopathy without metastasis. In left-sided colorectal cancer, LEfSe analysis detected only limited differences in microbial profiles between cases with and without lymph node metastasis. The identified discriminative features were confined to Eukaryota-related signals in the metastatic group, and no bacterial taxa exceeded the predefined LDA threshold. In right-sided colorectal cancer, LEfSe analysis at the phylum level revealed that Proteobacteria (LDA score 4.773, p = 0.033) were increased in patients with lymph node metastasis, while Firmicutes (LDA score 4.810, p = 0.012) were predominant in patients without lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3a). At the genus level, Streptococcus (LDA score 4.958, p = 0.0002), Gemella (LDA score 4.455, p = 0.0027), Parvimonas (LDA score 3.921, p = 0.0040), and Peptostreptococcus (LDA score 3.663, p = 0.0409) were more abundant in patients without lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3b–f).

Group comparisons for relative abundance were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test

Functional prediction in right-sided colon cancer

Differences in metabolic pathways between the two groups were investigated using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2). Seventy-one pathways were identified (Fig. 4). In patients with lymph node metastasis, the TCA cycle (TCA) and acetate production pathways (PWY-5188) were enriched. In contrast, anaerobic glycolysis (ANAGLYCOLYSIS-PWY) and related metabolic pathways (GLYCOLYSIS) were enhanced in patients without lymph node metastasis. Additionally, pathways related to cell wall and membrane synthesis, such as peptidoglycan synthesis (PEPTIDOGLYCANSYN-PWY) and phosphatidyl lipid synthesis (PHOSLIPSYN-PWY), were more active in the group without lymph node metastasis. O-antigen synthesis (OANTIGEN-PWY) and lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis (PWY-7229, PWY-6396) were also more abundant in this group. Coenzyme A synthesis (COA-PWY) was also increased in the group without lymph node metastasis, indicating differences in predicted pathway profiles between the two groups.

Subgroup analysis of right-sided colon cancer

In the additional analysis restricted to right-sided colon cancer, cases were reclassified into three groups after exclusion of the NP subgroup (NN, n = 13; PN, n = 14; PP, n = 12) (Supplementary Figure 1). To further examine microbial features associated with reactive lymphadenopathy, fecal microbiome profiles were compared between the NN and PN groups at the genus level using LEfSe. The alpha and beta diversity did not significantly differ among NN, PN, and PP. This analysis identified several discriminative taxa between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 2). Compared with the NN group, the PN group was characterized by enrichment of several taxa, including a Lachnospiraceae-related feature, Fusobacterium, and Peptostreptococcus, whereas Blautia and Subdoligranulum were enriched in the NN group.

Discussion and conclusions

In this study, we collected gut microbiota samples from feces and tumors in colorectal cancer patients and analyzed them using NGS. We compared the gut microbiota in feces and tumors and found that the diversity was higher in feces, while tumor tissue exhibited a distinct bacterial profile. Focusing on the differences between reactive lymphadenopathy and metastatic lymphadenopathy, we observed that Firmicutes were predominant in cases of reactive lymphadenopathy in right-sided colon cancer, whereas Proteobacteria were more abundant in metastatic lymphadenopathy. Additionally, in cases of reactive lymphadenopathy without metastasis, the metabolic pathways of the gut microbiota were highly active.

The gut microbiota in colorectal cancer differs according to the tumor location, and we previously reported a predominance of Fusobacterium in left-sided colorectal cancer in feces [14, 18, 19]. In our cohort, we found that Proteobacteria were significantly enriched in right-sided tumors with lymph node metastasis, consistent with prior reports of Proteobacteria dominance in colorectal and even breast cancer tissues [16, 20, 21]. Moreover, the increased abundance of Firmicutes in nonmetastatic, reactive lymphadenopathy cases may reflect differences in the tumor-associated microbial context between the two groups. These findings suggest an association between local microbiota and reactive lymphadenopathy. This pattern of Proteobacteria enrichment aligns with previous findings in colorectal and breast cancer, suggesting a broader role for this phylum in tumor-associated microbiota across diverse populations.

Colorectal cancer differs in origin and gene expression profiles between the right and left sides of the colon [12, 17], and the tumor-associated microbiota also varies by location [13]. In this study, we highlighted the differences in gut microbiota based on the presence or absence of lymph node metastasis in right-sided colon cancer, with active bacterial metabolic pathways observed in cases of reactive lymphadenopathy without metastasis. The lack of clear microbiome differences in left-sided tumors may reflect limited statistical power, biological differences between right- and left-sided colorectal cancers, and methodological factors related to this exploratory subgroup analysis. Previous studies have noted the association between gut microbiota and cancer development and progression [9, 22], but our study is unique in that it focuses on the specific pathology of reactive lymphadenopathy in colorectal cancer. Moreover, the data were obtained from a Japanese population, which has a distinct diet culture that includes fermented foods, unlike previous studies conducted in Western populations. To further address the interpretation of reactive lymphadenopathy, we performed an additional subgroup analysis restricted to right-sided colon cancer and compared the NN and PN groups. This analysis showed that the PN group was characterized by enrichment of a Lachnospiraceae-related feature, Fusobacterium, and Peptostreptococcus, whereas Blautia and Subdoligranulum were enriched in the NN group. These findings suggest that reactive lymphadenopathy may reflect a distinct intestinal microbial context rather than simply the absence of pathological lymph node metastasis. However, because this analysis was exploratory and based on a limited number of cases, the results should be interpreted cautiously.

The analysis of metabolic pathways based on the presence or absence of lymph node metastasis revealed that, in the metastatic group, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production pathways were activated. The activation of the TCA cycle indicates efficient oxygen-dependent energy production, a characteristic pattern of highly metabolically active cancer cells [23, 24]. The metastatic group showed increased activity in pathways related to SCFA production, including acetate, as well as cofactor biosynthesis such as biotin and vitamin B6. These pathways may reflect differences in predicted metabolic profiles between the two groups, although their biological significance in lymph node metastasis remains uncertain. However, further studies are needed to clarify their precise roles in lymph node involvement. In contrast, the nonmetastatic group exhibited enhanced anaerobic glycolysis (ANAGLYCOLYSIS-PWY) and glycolysis (GLYCOLYSIS) pathways. The activation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis pathways (e.g., OANTIGEN-PWY and PWY-7229) may be consistent with an inflammatory microbial milieu in the tumor microenvironment [25]. Additionally, pathways related to cell wall and membrane synthesis (e.g., PEPTIDOGLYCANSYN-PWY) were activated, suggesting that increased bacterial activity may be associated with reactive lymphadenopathy [26]. This study utilized 16S rRNA gene sequencing for amplicon-based microbiota profiling and functional prediction using PICRUSt2. While this method enables broad taxonomic coverage and cost-effective inference of microbial pathways, it is limited by genus-level resolution and relies on predicted, not directly measured, gene functions. The PICRUSt2-based pathway analysis in this study reflects predicted functional profiles inferred from 16S rRNA gene sequencing rather than direct measurements of microbial metabolic activity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The strengths of this study include a comprehensive comparison of gut microbiota in reactive versus metastatic lymphadenopathy, specifically in right-sided colon cancer. Through the analysis of fecal and tumor-associated microbiota, we explored the relationship between microorganisms and the tumor microenvironment. The application of 16S rRNA sequencing for bacterial profiling and metabolic pathway prediction supports the conclusions of this investigation.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, and the subgroup analyses according to tumor location and lymph node status resulted in small effective group sizes that may have limited statistical power. Second, potential clinical confounding factors, including tumor stage, tumor size, inflammatory status, and T stage, were not adjusted for in a formal multivariable model because of the limited sample size and risk of overfitting. Third, although fecal samples were collected before bowel preparation and antibiotic administration, tumor specimens were obtained at the time of surgery after these interventions, and the local tumor-associated microbial environment may therefore have been influenced by preoperative bowel preparation and prophylactic antibiotics. Fourth, we did not directly analyze microbiota within lymph node tissue, and the relationship between microbiome profiles and reactive lymphadenopathy therefore remains indirect. Finally, 16S rRNA-based analysis and PICRUSt2 provide limited taxonomic resolution and predicted rather than directly measured functional information. In addition, the radiological definition of lymphadenopathy using a short-axis diameter of ≥ 5 mm was relatively inclusive. However, a recent CT-based study in colon cancer reported optimal short-axis cutoffs of 5.9 mm for right-sided colon cancer and 5.2 mm for left-sided colon cancer, suggesting that a 5-mm threshold may be clinically plausible in exploratory analyses [27]. Future studies should examine whether the observed findings remain consistent when alternative size thresholds are applied.
