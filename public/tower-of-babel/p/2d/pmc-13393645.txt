How Can Healthcare Professionals Treat Dependence on New Tobacco and Nicotine Products in Their Patients?
Authors: Carlos Rábade-Castedo, José Ignacio de Granda Orive, Carlos A. Jiménez-Ruiz
Published: 2026

The use of emerging tobacco and nicotine products (e-cigarettes, heated tobacco, hookahs, nicotine pouches, etc.) is increasing worldwide. The accessibility of these devices, which are outside the regulations governing conventional tobacco, and the false claims that they are effective methods for reducing tobacco harm or quitting smoking have led to their unstoppable growth.1, 2

Their high addictive potential has made it very difficult for many users to quit. On the one hand, recent studies have shown that a high percentage of smokers who start using these products continue indefinitely or become dual users. On the other hand, current Eurobarometer surveys observe that a quarter of consumers attempt to quit by seeking support from a healthcare professional.3, 4

However, these professionals are inadequately trained to provide the support patients need to quit. Thus, therapists have limited scientific information available to treat nicotine addiction to these products in their patients. Most documents and clinical practice guidelines focus on the diagnosis and treatment of conventional smoking but do not include information on how to measure consumption intensity or nicotine dependence, nor do they recommend specific interventions for users of these products.5

Therefore, the Smoking Cessation Group of the Spanish Society of Pulmonology and Thoracic Surgery (SEPAR), in conjunction with other scientific societies in Latin America, developed a pioneering consensus document to advise healthcare professionals on diagnosing and treating patients addicted to these new forms of nicotine and tobacco (electronic cigarettes, heated tobacco, and water pipes).6

Among the contributions of this document, the following stand out:a)The introduction of new scales and questionnaires to determine the intensity of nicotine use and dependence in users of these devices. Thus, this expert consensus proposes different scales depending on the type of nicotine product used. In the case of electronic cigarette users, vaping intensity is defined by cotinine levels in bodily fluids (plasma, urine, saliva). Cotinine levels above 200 ng/ml indicate severe vaping intensity. In these types of users, other biomarkers such as co-oximetry have low yield. This reflects the need for this quantitative measurement of cotinine in smoking cessation units. However, for primary care settings, cotinine determination is not feasible due to a lack of material or human resources. Therefore, in these settings, vaping intensity can be estimated by the number of times or hours per day these devices are used, as these correlate strongly with blood cotinine levels.7

Furthermore, nicotine dependence is quantified using two questionnaires: the EDS-4 (E-Cigarette Dependence Scale) and the PS-ECDI (Penn State E-Cigarette Dependence Index). Both have been validated for measuring e-cigarette dependence and have the highest internal consistency. The PS-ECDI is a validated scale for measuring electronic cigarette dependence, consisting of 10 items. It contains questions about various dependence traits such as: time to first cigarette, number of times used per day, craving assessment, irritability, and waking up at night. A PS-ECDI score of 13 or higher indicates high nicotine dependence. However, the EDS-4 scale consists of 4 items. A score greater than 3 on any of the items indicates high nicotine dependence. The EDS-4 is simpler than the PS-ECDI, which facilitates its implementation.8, 9 In the case of users of other emerging tobacco products (heated tobacco and waterpipes), other scales are used, such as the modified Fagerstrom Test (FTCD) and the Lebanese Waterpipe Dependence Scale (LWDS-11), to measure nicotine dependence. An LWDS score of 10 or higher in water pipe users indicates a high dependence.10

All these characteristics measurable by these scales (intensity and dependence) allow for the identification of users of these products with higher scores and, therefore, those for whom intensified psychological and pharmacological treatment is necessary.b)Inclusion of other variables of the consumer of these emerging products, such as the degree of motivation, self-efficacy, or analysis of previous attempts to quit these products, allows for individualized treatments. Thus, the level of motivation or self-efficacy to quit these products allows for the selection of the necessary type of psychological treatment. In patients with low motivation and self-efficacy, defined by a visual analog scale score of less than 8, the priority intervention will be motivational interviewing. In water pipe users, there is only scientific evidence of the efficacy of varenicline and bupropion in cessation of these products. Currently, there are no studies analyzing the efficacy of conventional smoking cessation medications for heated tobacco products.

In short, any healthcare professional encountering a patient who wishes to quit products such as electronic nicotine delivery devices (ENDS) will have to consider two questions: (a) Is the patient a dual user (electronic cigarette and conventional tobacco user)? (b) Does the patient exhibit a high dependence on these products, as determined by the EDS-4 > 3 and PS-ECDI ≥ 13, or a high degree of intensity of consumption? Thus, in patients with high nicotine dependence or dual users (conventional tobacco users combined with ENDS) intensification of pharmacological treatment is recommended by combining different nicotine dependence medications (combined patches with rapid-release forms of nicotine with partial nicotinic receptor agonists).

Therefore, this document, based on the limited existing scientific evidence and expert opinions, aims to provide tools to facilitate the treatment of nicotine dependence in users of new tobacco products. However, none of the questionnaires and scales proposed in this document have been validated in Spanish, so further studies are needed to analyze their validity in our setting. Furthermore, this consensus includes recommendations from smoking cessation experts on the treatment of these products, but these recommendations need to be evaluated through observational studies and clinical trials that analyze the effectiveness of psychological interventions and pharmacological treatments in our healthcare settings.
