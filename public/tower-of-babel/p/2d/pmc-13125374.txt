Women with PCOS have a later menopausal transition and a lower prevalence of menopausal symptoms at age 46: A population‐based birth cohort study
Authors: Johanna Lavi, Susanna Savukoski, Elisa Hurskainen, Meri‐Maija Ollila, Laure Morin‐Papunen, Maarit Niinimäki, Terhi T Piltonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWomen with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) have been suggested to experience later menopause, yet population‐based studies are lacking. The present study aimed to assess, in a population‐based birth cohort, whether women with PCOS are more likely to experience late peri−/postmenopause or to have menopausal symptoms in their mid‐40s than women without the syndrome.Material and MethodsThe present study is a long‐term, population‐based prospective cohort study from the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1966 (NFBC1966). The PCOS group (n = 380) was identified based on the Rotterdam criteria (oligomenorrhea, hyperandrogenism, or polycystic ovarian morphology according to anti‐Müllerian hormone levels). Women who did not meet the Rotterdam criteria for PCOS were considered as controls (n = 1469). Late peri−/postmenopause was defined as at least 60 days of amenorrhea accompanied by elevated follicle‐stimulating hormone levels (>25 IU/L) or the use of menopausal hormone therapy. In women who had undergone hysterectomy or were using progestins, menopausal status was defined as elevated follicle‐stimulating hormone levels (>25 IU/L). Menopausal symptoms were self‐reported via questionnaire during a follow‐up visit at age 46.ResultsWomen with PCOS had a significantly lower likelihood of being late peri−/postmenopausal in their mid‐40s compared with controls (PCOS: 3.1% vs. Controls: 18.4%; adjusted odds ratio 0.15, 95% confidence interval 0.07–0.32). To support this result, women with PCOS more often reported having regular menstrual cycles and had a lower risk of menopausal symptoms at age 46, even after adjusting for body mass index, education, and smoking status (adjusted odds ratio 0.68, 95% confidence interval 0.51–0.92). Hot flashes and sleep disturbances were less frequent among women with PCOS, but there were no differences in other menopause‐related symptoms, such as vaginal dryness, affective symptoms, memory disturbances, or sexual dysfunction between the study groups.ConclusionsFrom the nonselective population data, it was shown that late peri−/postmenopausal status and symptoms were less frequent in women with PCOS at age 46, supporting the idea of prolonged ovarian function in affected women. This should be considered in patient counseling and future studies of fertility, contraception, and menopausal hormone therapy.Keywords: anti‐Müllerian hormone, hot flashes, menopausal symptoms, menopausal transition, menopause, perimenopause, polycystic ovary syndrome

Key message

This population‐based birth cohort study shows that women in their mid‐40s with PCOS were less often at late peri‐/postmenopausal and had fewer menopausal symptoms than women without the syndrome. There were no differences between the study groups regarding vaginal dryness, affective symptoms, memory disturbances, or sexual dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most prevalent endocrine disorder among women, affecting approximately 8%–15% of the female population worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 Key diagnostic features of PCOS include ovulatory dysfunction or irregular cycles, polycystic ovarian morphology, and clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism (HA). 4 Reproductive features aside, PCOS also involves metabolic and psychological disturbances that contribute to increased risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, as well as mood disorders. 5 The clinical phenotype of women with PCOS changes over time as their ovarian reserve and related hormonal activity diminish. 6 Due to this new balance in the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, women with PCOS begin having regular cycles and ovulations around the age of 35, whereas HA seems to prevail beyond menopause. 7 , 8 On the other hand, metabolic disturbances are aggravated by the increased adiposity and loss of lean mass that often accompany aging. 8 , 9

The depletion time of ovarian reserve and the ultimate timepoint at which menopause occurs exhibit significant individual genetic variation and are also linked to lifestyle factors, such as smoking and environmental factors. 10 Nevertheless, a dominant pattern of reproductive aging has been established as the basis for the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW) criteria recommended by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM). 11 The onset of menopausal transition typically occurs around the age of 46, with a range from 34 to 54 years. 12 , 13 When menstrual cycles become irregular, antral follicle count and subsequent anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels are already markedly low. 11 Prolonged intervals of amenorrhea (>60 days) and follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) levels >25 IU/L are indicative of late menopausal transition, which typically lasts 1–3 years, often accompanied by menopausal symptoms. 11 Final menstrual bleeding, defined after 1 year of amenorrhea, occurs at a mean age of 50.5 years, with a range from 40 to 60 years. 14 , 15 , 16 Interestingly, a higher antral follicle count in PCOS is associated with greater ovarian reserve, a possible prolongation of the fertile period, and a delay in the onset of natural menopausal transition. 6 , 17 , 18

Menopausal symptoms are a consequence of hypoestrogenism, which results from the depletion of antral follicles and may significantly impact women's well‐being. 19 , 20 Symptoms that are most associated with menopausal transition are vasomotor and genitourinary. 21 During menopause, approximately 50%–75% of women experience vasomotor symptoms, such as hot flashes and night sweats, while more than 50% report genitourinary symptoms. 21 Additional menopause‐related symptoms may include insomnia, musculoskeletal and joint pain, difficulties in cognitive functions, sexual dysfunction, and emotional instability. 22 Individual factors, including perceived stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and symptom sensitivity, are associated with the duration of menopause‐related symptoms, as frequent vasomotor symptoms can persist for over 7 years, with 50% of women experiencing them for more than 4 years. 23

Limited research has been conducted on the natural progression of PCOS in women beyond their fertile years. 24 Previous studies have indicated that the onset of menopause may occur 1–3 years later in women with PCOS compared to non‐PCOS women. 25 , 26 , 27 However, the studies have been heterogeneous, 24 and they have lacked a population‐based approach, yielding in inconsistent findings. In the present study, which drew from the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1966 (NFBC1966), the prevalence of menopausal symptoms and the phase at which menopausal transition occurs among women with PCOS in their mid‐40s were compared to their non‐PCOS counterparts. This work represents the largest population‐based cohort study conducted to assess the perimenopausal period in women with PCOS. We hypothesized that women with PCOS are less likely to reach late peri‐/postmenopause by their mid‐40s and, consequently, experience fewer menopause‐related symptoms at this age.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and setting

The study population was drawn from the longitudinal NFBC1966 28 (Figure 1). The cohort center has established several predefined data collection points, and a detailed cohort description and follow‐up protocol have previously been published. 29 In 1997, during the 31‐year follow‐up, questionnaires were mailed to all living female cohort members with a known address in Finland (n = 5608), with an 81% response rate (n = 4523). Additionally, women living in Northern Finland or the Helsinki metropolitan area (n = 4074) were invited to a clinical examination, with 77% (n = 3127) participation. In 2012, at the 46‐year follow‐up, questionnaires regarding health—including menopausal symptoms, aspects of menstrual cyclicity, behavior, work, and social background—were mailed to all living female cohort members (n = 5123), along with an invitation to a clinical examination. Of these women, 72% (n = 3706) responded to the questionnaire, and 64% (n = 3280) participated in the clinical examination. Study population

The identification of women with PCOS in the NFBC1966 has previously been described in detail. 30 Briefly, women with and without PCOS were categorized based on the 31‐year follow‐up and according to the Rotterdam criteria. Women exhibiting at least two of the following three features—oligomenorrhea, HA, or AMH levels ≥3.2 ng/mL—were defined as having PCOS. AMH was utilized to identify antral follicle excess, serving as a surrogate for the ultrasonographic detection of polycystic ovarian morphology, according to the 2023 International Evidence‐based Guidelines for the Assessment and Management of PCOS. 5 The cut‐off for polycystic ovarian morphology was set at 3.2 ng/mL, as defined in previous studies. 18 , 31 , 32 Women who were using hormonal contraceptives or who were pregnant at age 31 (n = 2640) were excluded from the study population so that a more accurate diagnosis of PCOS could be obtained concerning menstrual status and AMH levels. Combined hormonal contraceptives can mask menstrual cyclicity by inducing regular bleeding patterns and reducing hyperandrogenemia, resulting in lower testosterone (T) levels. Oligomenorrhea was considered when the intermenstrual interval exceeded 35 days or when amenorrhea was present. Clinical HA was defined by the presence of hirsutism, which was determined based on questions regarding bothersome or excessive hair growth. Previous studies, including the authors' own, have demonstrated that self‐reported oligomenorrhea and hirsutism can accurately identify women with PCOS. 33 , 34 , 35 Biochemical HA was defined as elevated T (>2.3 nmol/L) or free androgen index levels (>5.6) according to laboratory reference ranges. In total, 380 women met the Rotterdam criteria for PCOS; those who did not (n = 1469) were considered as controls. Women who self‐reported having been diagnosed with PCOS or polycystic ovarian morphology at age 46 (n = 27) but who did not meet the Rotterdam criteria at age 31 were excluded from the study.

Late peri‐/postmenopausal status

A total of 144 women were identified as late peri‐/postmenopausal, while 827 women were considered reproductive or in early perimenopause according to the STRAW +10 criteria. 11 Late peri‐/postmenopausal status was defined as at least 60 days of amenorrhea and elevated FSH levels (>25 IU/L) (n = 40). The use of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) was considered as an indication of entry into the late peri‐/postmenopausal group regardless of other criteria. Women receiving MHT (n = 24) were identified by the national medicine reimbursement register (if they had received MHT 1 year prior to the 46‐year study). Women who had undergone hysterectomy or who were using progestin‐only treatment (peroral, subdermal implant, intrauterine device), FSH level >25 IU/L, at age 46, were considered to be late peri‐/postmenopausal, regardless of their menstrual history (n = 80).

The control group consisted of reproductive women and women in early perimenopause. The selection criteria were an FSH value ≤25 IU/L and a last menstrual period occurring <60 days before the clinical examination. Women who had undergone hysterectomy or who were using progestin‐only treatment (peroral, subdermal implant, intrauterine device), FSH level <25 IU/L, at age 46 were selected for the control group, regardless of their menstrual history.

Women who did not give permission to use their medication register data were excluded from the analysis (n = 4). Women who reported using tamoxifen (n = 3) or combined contraceptives (n = 55) were also excluded, as were women with conflicting (n = 94) or missing data (n = 722). Conflicting data were noted when, based on FSH levels, women were placed in a different group than the one indicated by the duration of amenorrhea, and vice versa. Women who had undergone bilateral oophorectomy prior to the study period, identified using data from the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare's Care Registry for Healthcare regarding healthcare procedures, were excluded from the study (n = 12).

Menopausal symptoms

Menopausal symptoms were assessed using a questionnaire, which was completed by 1345 women from the study population (288 women with PCOS, 1057 controls). The specific types of symptoms—hot flashes, vaginal dryness, sleep disturbances, affective symptoms, memory disturbances, sexual dysfunction, and muscle or joint soreness—were assessed by asking respondents to rate the frequency of each symptom on a scale from 1 to 5 (1, not at all; 2, infrequent; 3, somewhat; 4, quite a lot; 5, very often) when applicable. Women indicating no symptoms or infrequent symptoms were considered asymptomatic, while those reporting symptoms more frequently (somewhat, quite a lot, very often) were categorized as symptomatic. Cycle regularity was assessed via a single question that asked whether the menstrual cycle was regular or irregular.

Hormonal assays

Serum levels of T, sex hormone‐binding globulin, luteinizing hormone, and FSH were analyzed at NordLab Oulu, a testing laboratory (T113) accredited by the Finnish Accreditation Service (FINAS) (EN ISO 15189). Briefly, serum T levels were assayed using liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA), which offers higher accuracy compared with immunoassays, particularly at female concentration ranges. The relatively high cut‐offs used for free androgen index and total T improve specificity and reduce false positivity. Sex hormone‐binding globulin levels were determined using fluoroimmunoassay (Wallac Inc. Ltd., Turku, Finland). Free androgen index levels were calculated using the formula FAI = 100 × T (nmol/L)/SHBG (nmol/L). Luteinizing hormone and FSH values were determined by immunochemiluminometric methods (Advia Centaur XP, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Tarrytown, NY). Serum AMH levels were analyzed using the Elecsys® AMH Plus electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics, Germany), as previously described. 30 The blood samples were drawn on a random day during the menstrual cycle.

Descriptives

In the clinical examinations, weight (kg) was measured using a regularly calibrated digital scale. Height (cm) was assessed with a standard calibrated stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated in kg/m2. In cases where measurements were missing, self‐reported values were utilized. No statistical difference was observed between the measured and self‐reported BMIs. Education levels were classified as basic, secondary, and tertiary. Smoking habits were classified into three categories: never smoker, former smoker, and current smoker. Alcohol consumption was categorized as no use, light use (≤20 g/day), and heavy use (>20 g/day). Physical activity was assessed based on metabolic equivalent of task hours per week, where one metabolic equivalent of task was defined as energy expenditure at rest, allowing for the comparison of different activities based on their intensity. Marital status was categorized as either single or in a relationship.

Statistical analyses

Normality of the variables was assessed visually using histograms. As none of the continuous variables were normally distributed, the differences in variables among the study groups were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. The differences in categorical parameters were analyzed with a χ 2 test. The results are reported as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles. Binary logistic regression analyses were also performed. The results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York). The adjusting factors (BMI, education, and smoking) were selected for the analyses based on observed differences between the groups or on previously published evidence.

Multiple imputation was performed to address missing data in the covariates used for adjustment. Sensitivity analyses were then conducted for the logistic regression models that included these covariates as adjusting factors. Multiple imputation was performed in SPSS using fully conditional specification. A total of 20 datasets were imputed (imputation = 20), with up to 50 iterations per dataset (maxiter = 50). Predictive mean matching (20 nearest‐neighbor donors) was used as the imputation method. The variables subjected to imputation were BMI at age 46 (continuous), education at age 46 (categorical), and smoking status at age 46 (categorical). The following variables were included as predictors in imputation regressions from age 31: BMI, smoking status, alcohol usage g/day, education, waist circumference, pelvic circumference, metabolic equivalent of task hours per week, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol. The variables included as predictors in imputation regressions from age 46 were skeletal muscle mass, visceral fat area, percent body fat, waist circumference, pelvic circumference, self‐rated health, alcohol usage g/day, and metabolic equivalent of task hours per week. These predictor variables were selected based on non‐parametric correlations (and data availability). Results were pooled across the imputed datasets.

RESULTS

The characteristics of the study population at age 46 are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Women with PCOS were more often obese according to BMI, with a median BMI of 26.7 kg/m2 (25th to 75th quartile: 23.9–30.9) than the controls, who had a median BMI of 25.3 kg/m2 (22.6–29.3). There were no significant differences in education, smoking habits, alcohol intake, physical activity, or marital status between the groups. AMH levels were 4–5 times higher in women with PCOS at age 46 compared to the controls, with a median of 0.86 (0.39–1.69) ng/mL versus 0.19 (0.04–0.53) ng/mL, respectively. Hormonal levels differed significantly between the groups, with the PCOS group having higher levels of T and free androgen index, and lower levels of sex hormone‐binding globulin, luteinizing hormone (LH), and FSH. Additionally, the LH/FSH ratio was higher in women with PCOS compared to the controls.

Of the study population, 71.3% (n = 1318) responded to the question regarding cycle regularity. Of those women with PCOS, 65% reported having a regular menstrual cycle, compared with 52.7% of the controls (p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). Conversely, a smaller proportion of women with PCOS reported irregular cycles compared to the controls (9.9% vs. 14.2%, respectively; p < 0.001). Fourteen women reported having amenorrhea for more than 2 years since their final menstrual bleeding (1.9% of 726 women with available information, missing data 31.7%), excluding those using combined oral contraceptives or progestins and those who had undergone hysterectomy.

Hormonal intrauterine devices were the most common progestin used: 26.7% (n = 60) of women with PCOS and 34.6% (n = 258) of controls used them, whereas only 0.9% of women with PCOS and 1.9% of controls used oral or subcutaneous progestins. All women receiving MHT were in the control group, whereas none of the women with PCOS used MHT. Among controls, 7.6% (n = 80) and among women with PCOS, 9.0% (n = 26) used medications known to affect menopausal symptoms (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, gabapentin, pregabalin, clonidine, and oxybutynin), all of which are alternative treatments for menopausal symptoms. 21 , 36

The menopausal status of 255 (67.1%) women with PCOS and 746 (50.8%) controls could be determined. Fewer of the women with PCOS were late peri‐/postmenopausal at age 46 compared to the controls (3.1% vs. 18.4%, respectively, p < 0.001) (Figure 2B). Menopausal symptoms at age 46 were significantly less prevalent among women with PCOS (26.7%) compared to the controls (34.3%) (p = 0.02) (Table 3). Hot flashes and sleep disturbances were less common in women with PCOS compared to the controls: 11.5% versus 17.2% (p = 0.02) for hot flashes, and 12.8% versus 17.9% (p = 0.04) for sleep disturbances. No differences were observed between the study groups concerning other menopausal symptoms (affective symptoms, memory disturbances, sexual dysfunction, vaginal dryness, muscle or joint pain). In the binary logistic regression model, the likelihood of being late peri‐/postmenopausal at age 46 was lower in women with PCOS (OR [crude] 0.14, 95% CI 0.07–0.31) compared to the controls, with adjustments for BMI, education, and smoking having no effect on these results (Figure 3). The likelihood of experiencing any menopausal symptoms was lower in women with PCOS (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.93) compared to the controls, with adjustments for BMI, education, and smoking having no effect on the result. PCOS was associated with a lower risk of hot flashes (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.42–0.93) and sleep disturbances (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.46–0.99). In subanalyses of the robustness of the results—conducted by excluding women using medications known as having an effect on menopausal symptoms (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, gabapentin, pregabalin, clonidine, and oxybutynin), and by excluding women using MHT—the findings remained consistent, with the exception of sleep disturbances, which no longer differed between the groups under either exclusion. In addition, when excluding medications known to affect menopausal symptoms, affective symptoms were less frequent in women with PCOS compared to the controls (14.1% vs. 19.5%, respectively; p = 0.05), and the risk of experiencing affective symptoms was lower (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.46–1.00; p = 0.05; adjusted OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.97; p = 0.03). In sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation for BMI, smoking, and education to address missing data, the impact of adjustments on symptoms remained unchanged, except for vaginal dryness. After imputation, vaginal dryness was significantly less frequent in women with PCOS (adjusted OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.97; p = 0.04).

DISCUSSION

In this population‐based cohort study, women with PCOS had entered late peri‐/postmenopause less often and had reported fewer menopausal symptoms at age 46 compared to women without PCOS. To the authors' knowledge, this research was the first to examine menopausal symptoms in women with PCOS. Previous research has shown, in relatively small populations, that the onset of menopause occurs later in women with PCOS. 25 , 26 , 27 , 37 In a cross‐sectional retrospective follow‐up study by Dahlgren et al., 37 among women aged 49–58 years, 56.4% of those in the control group had reached menopause (FSH > 50 IU/L) at the time of evaluation compared to 30.4% of women with PCOS (PCOS: n = 33, 50.0 ± 1.3 years [mean ± SD]; controls: n = 132, 51.7 ± 5.3 years). Li et al. 25 examined multiple factors that could influence menopausal age, revealing that PCOS was one factor associated with a later onset of menopause (n = 61 936). The authors estimated that women with PCOS experience natural menopause at an average age of 56, compared to 52 years of age for the general population. Forslund et al. 26 reported that women with PCOS experience menopause 2 years later than women without PCOS (n = 27). A more recent population‐based cohort study, including 348 women with PCOS and 1348 non‐PCOS controls (115 women with PCOS and 692 controls had reached menopause), found that women with PCOS reach menopause approximately 1.5 years later than women without PCOS. 27 On the other hand, Schmidt et al. 38 found no significant difference between women with PCOS and controls in terms of age at menopause onset (n = 93). Nevertheless, a recent meta‐analysis 24 involving eight studies and 405 women with PCOS found no significant difference between women with PCOS and non‐PCOS controls with respect to age at onset of menopause. This finding, however, was classified as “very low evidence” due to the poor quality and heterogeneity of the included studies. Other studies on the natural age of menopause in women with PCOS have reported inconclusive results, which may be due to methodological issues. For example, using AMH as a predictive marker for determining menopausal age or the onset of menopausal transition may be problematic due to limited understanding of how AMH levels decline across different patient groups, including PCOS patients.

The results of the present study showed that PCOS was associated with a later onset of late perimenopause by age 46. It was also observed that women with PCOS reported fewer menopausal symptoms. Given this observation, it could be expected that women with PCOS also experience fewer affective symptoms or memory problems, and less sexual dysfunction, muscle or joint pain, and vaginal dryness, all of which are generally related to menopause. 20 However, the results did not demonstrate this effect. One possible explanation for this outcome is that women with PCOS are well known to experience multimorbidity, with overlapping symptoms that could mimic menopausal symptoms. 39 , 40 , 41 Women with PCOS have been reported to experience more severe depression, anxiety, obstructive sleep apnea, body image difficulties, and psychosexual dysfunction. 5 Vaginal dryness, on the other hand, is more characteristic of later postmenopausal stages; therefore, differences would not be expected at age 46, when the majority of women have likely not yet reached postmenopause. Only in the sensitivity analyses did vaginal dryness differ slightly between women with and without PCOS, which likely reflects both the small number of cases available to detect differences in this cohort and the influence of confounding factors on the symptom profile. Overall, the fact that women with PCOS reported fewer menopausal symptoms than the controls supports the hypothesis of a later onset of menopausal transition in these women.

AMH levels seem to be predictive when estimating when a woman will undergo menopause. 42 It has been shown that women with PCOS have AMH levels 2–3 times higher than women without PCOS, suggesting that women with PCOS have a larger ovarian reserve than non‐PCOS counterparts. 6 Consistent with previous research, the present study found AMH levels to be 4–5 times higher in women with PCOS who were in their mid‐40s. Although hormonal contraceptive use at age 46 was not controlled for in the descriptive analyses, higher AMH levels could explain why women with PCOS were less likely to be menopausal compared to the controls. It must be noted, however, that as high AMH levels are one of the criteria for PCOS at age 31, this study could not determine whether the later onset of menopause in women with PCOS is linked to a higher number of ovarian antral follicles or to a slower rate of follicular atresia.

This study had several strengths. It was population‐based and included an unselected population, which eliminated the potential selection bias associated with hospital referral‐based studies or studies referring to patients experiencing fertility problems. Further, the study included a wide range of measurements that allowed for adjustments with relevant confounding factors. T concentrations were determined by mass spectrometry, thereby improving the specificity of this measure. Women diagnosed with PCOS were included in the study based on the Rotterdam criteria, which are endorsed in the latest international PCOS guidelines. 5 Moreover, cohort members were born within a one‐year window of time, thereby minimizing age‐related bias. Additionally, environmental and societal factors, like socioeconomic indicators and marital and smoking status, were comparable between the study groups. Overall, this research was the largest population‐based effort to assess menopausal symptoms among an unselected population of women in their mid‐40s who have PCOS.

The study had some limitations. As a significant number of participants had not yet reached late perimenopause, the final age at which these women ultimately entered this phase of menopausal transition could not be determined. However, this information will be collected in future studies from this cohort. The female population of Finland has been shown to have a mean age at menopause similar to that reported for other populations of European ancestry 43 and to have a prevalence of PCOS similar to that in other parts of the world. 4 , 34 Although women from the NFBC1966 have a slightly lower body weight than women born 10–20 years later, Finland has a comparatively high rate of obesity and insulin resistance among European nations, on par with North and Latin America, the Middle East, and Australia. The NFBC1966 population is also ethnically homogenous, complicating the generalizability of the findings to women with other ethnic backgrounds.

Moreover, the authors acknowledge that reliance on a single randomly timed FSH measurement (outside the early follicular phase) is suboptimal. As FSH levels vary substantially across the menstrual cycle and especially during perimenopause, defining late peri‐/postmenopause based solely on FSH measurement is problematic. That said, only those who were using progestin or had undergone hysterectomy were defined solely based on FSH levels in this study. The authors also acknowledge the potential bias related to progestin use and FSH levels, as progestins—especially oral and subcutaneous progestins—may lower FSH levels by downregulating gonadotropin release. However, most progestin users in the study were using hormonal intrauterine devices, which have a minimal impact on hormone levels. 43 Although the cohort had a generally good rate of participation, the multiple data collection methods and time points, as well as missing data and exclusions, reduced the final study population by a non‐insignificant margin. Multiple imputation was carried out to evaluate this effect.

CONCLUSION

Women with PCOS are less likely to reach late peri−/postmenopause by their mid‐40s than those without the syndrome, indicating larger ovarian reserve in women with PCOS sustaining their ovarian function longer. This was evidenced in the present study by the lower incidence of menopausal symptoms, hot flashes, and sleep disturbances among women with PCOS at age 46. However, other, more general symptoms, such as affective symptoms, memory disturbances, or sexual dysfunction, did not differ from the controls, possibly due to overlap with common symptoms of PCOS. The results suggest that women with PCOS experience delayed menopausal transition with longer exposure to natural estrogens, which could confer health benefits to these women. Future studies should therefore investigate the health consequences of PCOS beyond menopause.
