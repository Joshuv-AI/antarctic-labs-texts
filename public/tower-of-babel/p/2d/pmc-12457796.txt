Radiofrequency ablation for symptomatic, non-functioning thyroid nodules: a single-center retrospective observational study
Authors: Huiyin Zhu, Lifen Bai, Ming Zhou, Ling Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study was designed to investigate the efficacy and safety of radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for treating thyroid nodules (TNs).MethodsWe retrospectively included the patients who underwent RFA for TNs in our hospital between January 2020 and December 2023. Preoperative and postoperative data including age, sex, body mass index, and thyroid hormone levels were collected from each patient. The efficacy of RFA on TNs was analyzed by evaluating the volume reduction (VR) and volume reduction rate (VRR) at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12, respectively. In addition, the efficacy of RFA on nodule composition and postoperative complications was evaluated. Finally, a subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential risks.ResultsVR at postoperative month 12 was significantly higher than those at postoperative months 1, 3, and 6, respectively (all P < 0.001). VRR at month 12 was significantly higher than that of months 1, 3, and 6, respectively (all P < 0.001).ConclusionRFA is an effective option for treating TNs with good safety. VR and VRR showed significant increase after RFA compared with the pretreatment levels.Keywords: Benign nodule, Radiofrequency ablation, Retrospective study, Thyroid nodule, Ultrasonography

INTRODUCTION

Thyroid nodules (TNs), with an estimated incidence of 4%–7% in the general population, are considered one of the most frequently reported thyroid diseases [1]. Fortunately, most of the TNs are benign, which requires no further treatment; however, appropriate treatment should be given to some individuals with compression symptoms [2,3].

Conventional surgery and radioiodine therapy have been utilized for treating TNs [4]. As a minimally invasive technique, radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is feasible with good safety for TNs. To date, RFA is considered a relatively newer treatment option for benign TNs. It is reported to show a 50%–80% reduction in TNs volume at 1 year [5] and 2 years [6], respectively. Nevertheless, these experiences are merely provided by a few institutions and some case series, due to a lack of patients undergoing RFA. In a comprehensive literature review, Muhammad et al. [7] reported that 21 retrospective, 17 prospective, and 6 randomized controlled trials had focused on the volume reduction rate (VRR) after RFA, and the majority of the studies were performed on solid nodules. Our study was designed to investigate the efficiency of RFA on the TNs in solid and/or cystic types among Chinese patients. Our study provides further evidence of the efficacy and safety of RFA in treating TNs.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee of Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital (No. 2021109–001). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before enrolling in this study.

Patients and study design

In this retrospective analysis, we included patients who underwent RFA for benign TNs that were cytologically proven between January 2020 and December 2023. The following patients were excluded from the study: those with giant substernal goiter or the majority of TNs in the position behind the chest bone; those who could not tolerate the surgery; those with contralateral vocal cord dysfunction; severe coagulation disorder; organ dysfunction; those with a history of thyroid cancer.

For the study design, we first collected the preoperative and postoperative data of each patient, including sex, age, body mass index, total triiodothyronine (T3), total thyroxine, free thyroxine, thyroglobulin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, reverse T3, and calcitonin. In addition, we investigated the nodule nature (cystic, cystic and solid, solid patterns), volume, and power and time of ablation. The composition of the nodule was classified as solid, cystic and solid, or cystic according to the proportion of fluids [8]. Then we evaluated the efficiency of RFA for treating TNs by comparing the postoperative volume at months 1, 3, 6, and 12 with the pretreatment volume.

Procedures

RFA was conducted using a multichannel RFA system (STARmed), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For patients with larger nodules (a diameter of more than 4 cm), a longer operation time (possibly more than 1 hour), or those who are anxious, nervous, and unable to tolerate the procedure under awake conditions, we choose general anesthesia. The patient was in the supine position with the neck extended, followed by ultrasonography to fix the tumor margin. For the determination of the volume of TNs, the left-right diameter and the anteroposterior diameter of the thyroid nodule on the largest transverse section were determined under ultrasound, together with the upper-lower diameter of the thyroid nodule on the largest longitudinal section. The volume was calculated based on the following formula: V = 6/π × (left–right diameter) × (anterior–posterior diameter) × (upper–lower diameter). The RFA procedures were performed according to the previous description [9,10]. The utilized electrodes were Electrode Ref RFTP 0710 (RF Medical), with a length of 7 cm, an exposure of 10 mm, a size of 18 gauge, and a power of 45 W. The electrode was inserted until reaching the deepest part of the nodule, and then was moved towards the center and surface of the nodule, in order to divide the nodule. Afterwards, it was withdrawn and repositioned in a different area of the nodule. The nodule was divided into small, multiple, and conceptual ablation units. Cystic nodules were aspirated first before the ablation. For the solid/cystic nodules with a cystic fluid ratio between 50% and 90%, fluid aspiration was performed, followed by injection of 1% polidocanol equal to 1/3 of the aspirated cystic fluid volume. After RFA, the patients were observed for 6–8 hours in the hospital, followed by discharge according to the patients’ will. The patients received no antibiotics before or after ablation.

Comparison of nodule volume

For each nodule, we determined the anteroposterior, vertical, and transverse diameters at the baseline and postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12. The nodule volume was calculated using the formula of V = πabc/6, where a, b, and c represented the transverse, vertical, and anteroposterior diameters [11]. Finally, we compared the nodule volume at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12 with the pretreatment levels, together with the comparison among the postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12.

Comparison of volume reduction and volume reduction rate

The VRR was determined by the US imaging and was calculated with the equation according to the previous description: VRR (%) = [initial volume (mL)–final volume (mL)]/initial volume (mL) [12]. The VRR comparison was performed by comparing the postoperative VRR at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12 with the baseline levels. Neck ultrasonography was conducted in the same manner as the determination of nodule volume.

Postoperative evaluation

Patients were discharged after the 6–8-hour observation period. During follow-up, patients were subject to postoperative evaluation, including assessment of symptoms and complications.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 27.0 (IBM Corp.) was used for the statistical analysis. The last value carried forward approach was used for the imputation of missing repeated measurement data, and the random forest method was used for the imputation of missing non-repeated measurement data. Categorical variables were shown as sample size (proportion), while continuous variables were shown as mean ± standard deviation. The median values (interquartile range) were used to represent the data with serious deviations from normality. For the thyroid volume that was not normally distributed, the Friedman method was used to analyze the changes before and after ablation, as well as the VRR. The natural logarithm of the nodule volume was transformed, and the difference between the natural logarithm of the nodule volume before ablation and the natural logarithm of the nodule volume at each time point after ablation (designated as D.lnV) was used as the dependent variable. The generalized estimating equations were utilized to determine the nodular nature and number, and their effects on volume changes. The results were adjusted for variables that were unbalanced between subgroups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline patient characteristics

Before ablation, all nodules were subjected to fine-needle aspiration biopsy, and the pathological diagnosis confirmed that they were all benign. Reduction in thyroid nodule volume after radiofrequency ablation

The median of the nodule volume showed a significant decrease at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12 (all P < 0.001) compared with the pretreatment. Compared with the median nodule volume at month 1, there was a significant decrease at postoperative months 3, 6, and 12 (all P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis

Taking D.lnV (ln.V0–ln.V) as the dependent variable, the interference factor was corrected in this section, and then the repeated measurement analysis was performed. After RFA, the nodules in the patients showing solid/cystic nodules and solid nodules all decreased. The nodules in the patients with solid nodules were still larger than those with solid and cystic nodules, but the statistical difference was not significant (adjusted P = 0.063). Compared with the patients with a TIRADS (Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System) classification of IVa, those with a classification of IVb showed no statistical differences (adjusted P = 0.779). Also, those with a high risk showed no statistical differences compared with those with an extremely high risk (adjusted P = 0.228) (Table 2).

Radiofrequency ablation induced no additional complications

In total, 18 patients (27.7%) showed pain immediately after RFA, and merely 4 (6.2%) reported pain at 12 hours after RFA. The risk of 12-hour pain was significantly reduced compared with the pain immediately after RFA (odds ratio, 0.171; 95% confidence interval, 0.054–0.540; P = 0.001). No patients showed complications of voice change or hematoma.

DISCUSSION

Recently, image-guided ablation techniques (e.g., RFA) have been utilized as an alternative to surgery for treating TNs. RFA is being widely used as it yields better outcomes, especially when used for solid and predominantly solid nodules [13]. In this study, we focused on the short-term efficiency of RFA in treating different TNs, including solid and/or cystic types. Our data showed that RFA yielded a significant increase in the VRR at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12 compared with the baseline level.

Our understanding of the efficiency and safety of RFA is still limited. To date, there is no definite relation between nodular composition and VRR of TNs. In a previous study, Jeong et al. reported that the size of cystic nodules showed a significant decrease in the 1- and 3-month follow-up compared with the solid and solid/cystic types, respectively [14]. In contrast, there were no statistical differences in the size of the cystic nodules compared with the solid and solid/cystic types 6 months after RFA. In a study designed to evaluate the efficacy of RFA on benign TNs, Li et al. [15] collected patients who underwent RFA with solid nodules (n = 262) or cystic-solid nodules (n = 1,027), which indicated that nodule maximal diameter reduction ratio and VRR in the cystic/solid nodule patients showed significant improvement compared with that of the solid nodule group at months 3 and 6 after RFA. In this study, the median of the nodule volume showed a significant decrease at postoperative months 1, 3, 6, and 12 compared with the baseline. In addition, the VR and VRR at postoperative month 12 were significantly higher than those at postoperative months 1, 3, and 6, respectively. In addition, the solid nodules were more likely to present a significant reduction in volume compared with the other 2 types. In the future, more studies are required to illustrate the potential mechanisms.

RFA is superior to conventional surgery for treating TNs as it can preserve thyroid gland function and minimize postoperative complications. RFA is an effective and safe alternative to surgery [16]. In a 4-year follow-up, RFA was reported to be effective in treating patients with no life-threatening complications, which was featured by shrinking benign TNs and controlling nodule-related problems [17]. Indeed, RFA has also been reported to be associated with various complications including voice change, pain, skin burn at the puncture site, hematoma, hypothyroidism, thyrotoxicosis, fever, and edema. Fortunately, most complications recovered spontaneously without sequelae. To our best knowledge, pain is the most common complication in those who underwent RFA [18]. Occasionally, the pain could radiate to some body sites such as the shoulder, ear, jaw, and chest [19]. However, it is usually self-limiting and resolved immediately after switching off the RFA. It is well-controlled after oral analgesic, and only 5.5% of patients require analgesic for >2 days [14,20]. As one of the most serious complications, voice changes were induced primarily by thermal injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve [21]. However, it may also be related to vagus nerve injury. In this study, the postoperative complication in our patients was pain after RFA, and 27.69% of the patients showed pain immediately after RFA, which was 6.15% at 12 hours after RFA. No patients showed voice changes. This further indicated the safety of RFA for treating the TNs.

In this study, there are some limitations indeed. We could not set a control group due to ethical issues. In addition, the follow-up duration is not long enough, but according to the previous descriptions [7], most studies have consistently reported followup every 3–6 months within 1 year, with rare studies of 2 years. Additionally, the vascular scores were not evaluated before or after RFA. Moreover, this study was a retrospective design that was carried out in a single center. Furthermore, there were many radiologists involved, and the interobserver variability of ultrasound may not be avoidable. In the future, the acquisition of clinical practice requires a more careful and critical appraisal of the data.

In conclusion, RFA is an alternative modality for treating TNs. It is an effective method in reducing the volume of TNs and in controlling nodule-related compressive symptoms. Our data indicate that RFA is a safe procedure for TNs.
