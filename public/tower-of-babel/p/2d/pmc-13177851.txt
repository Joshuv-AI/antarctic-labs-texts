Role of Micronutrients in Perioperative Care: A Systematic Review
Authors: Debasrita Banerjee, Ravi Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicronutrient adequacy is essential for wound healing, immune competence, antioxidant defense, and erythropoiesis, all critical to perioperative recovery. Deficiencies in iron, vitamin C, vitamin D, selenium, and zinc are common in surgical patients and may worsen outcomes. This review evaluated evidence linking micronutrient status or supplementation with surgical outcomes.MethodsA PRISMA-based systematic review of major databases and trial registries (through August 2025) included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies assessing endpoints such as wound healing, surgical-site infection, transfusion, organ dysfunction, pain, and length of stay. Risk of bias was assessed using standard tools. Narrative summarization of data was done because the outcome measures and study designs were different in eligible studies.ResultsTwenty-two studies met the inclusion criteria. Intravenous iron consistently increased hemoglobin and iron stores but showed variable transfusion effects. Vitamin C supplementation reduced perioperative pain and modestly improved wound healing. Selenium normalization did not translate into outcome benefits. Low vitamin D and zinc levels were associated with higher infection rates and delayed healing, though interventional evidence remains limited. The results of individual studies were put into perspective using the previously published systematic reviews.ConclusionsRoutine high-dose supplementation is not justified. Targeted preoperative screening and correction of iron, vitamin D, zinc, and selenium deficiencies may improve recovery in high-risk surgical patients. Further large, standardized trials are needed to define optimal timing, dosing, and clinical benefits.How to cite this articleBanerjee D, Yadav R. Highlights

This review on the use of PRISMA-based on 22 clinical studies demonstrates that negative effects on iron, vitamin C and D, zinc, and selenium in the perioperative period are predictive factors of worsening surgical outcomes. Specifically, correction enhances the recovery. The review has not found any evidence supporting the universal use of high-dose supplementation and instead, highlights individualized, phenotype-specific screening and treatment within the perioperative care.

Introduction

Surgical injury triggers a complex physiological response characterized by inflammation, oxidative stress, immune activation, and increased metabolic demands for tissue repair. Adequate micronutrient status is fundamental to these perioperative processes.1,2 Iron is essential for erythropoiesis and oxygen transport; vitamin C supports collagen synthesis, immune defense, and antioxidant protection; vitamin D modulates immune and inflammatory pathways; while selenium and zinc are critical cofactors for antioxidant enzymes, cellular immunity, and wound healing. Disruption of these micronutrient-dependent pathways can impair recovery and increase susceptibility to postoperative complications.3

Micronutrient deficiencies are common in surgical populations, particularly among older adults, patients with chronic disease, cancer, malnutrition, or inflammatory states. Observational evidence consistently links deficiencies of iron, vitamin D, zinc, and selenium with adverse perioperative outcomes, including anemia, Surgical Site Infections (SSIs), delayed wound healing, organ dysfunction, and prolonged hospital stay.4,5 However, interventional trials evaluating perioperative supplementation have yielded heterogeneous results, with benefits often confined to selected patient groups or surrogate outcomes.

The purpose of this systematic review is to synthesize contemporary clinical evidence from randomized trials and observational studies to clarify the role of key micronutrients in perioperative care, evaluate their impact on surgical outcomes, and inform targeted, evidence-based approaches for perioperative screening, supplementation, and future research priorities.

This systematic review has been prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251158060.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

Searches were conducted up to August 31, 2025. The vitamins and trace elements of interest were made clear in the registered scope registered on PROSPERO by the review protocol. Due to the heterogeneity of study findings and the lack of comparable quantitative data, we synthesized our findings using a narrative approach based on the SWIM framework.

We omitted conditionally imperative amino acids like arginine as well as wider immunonutrients in this review. The established eligibility criteria were limited to vitamins and trace elements; thus, immunonutrition that was based on arginine was not within the range.

Databases Searched

The literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases to ensure comprehensive coverage of published and ongoing studies, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Google Scholar, and the clinical trial registry ClinicalTrials.gov.

Timeframe: Searches were conducted up to August 31, 2025.

Search terms: Search strings combined three domains:

The search strategy employed Boolean operators to combine three concept domains. Nutrient-related terms (“iron” OR “vitamin C” OR “vitamin D” OR “selenium” OR “zinc” OR “trace elements”) were combined with perioperative terms (“perioperative” OR “preoperative” OR “postoperative” OR “surgery”) using the AND operator, and further linked to outcome-related terms (“wound healing” OR “surgical site infection” OR “transfusion” OR “acute kidney injury” OR “length of stay”).

Screening Process

The screening process was conducted in a stepwise manner, with titles and abstracts independently screened for relevance, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles to confirm inclusion criteria. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies, and high-quality observational designs. In addition, more than 30 relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses were consulted to support discussion and provide contextual interpretation of the primary evidence (Fig. 1).

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were clinical investigations conducted in surgical patients and assessed preoperative or perioperative micronutrient status or supplementation, specifically involving iron, vitamin C, vitamin D, selenium, zinc, or other trace elements. To be included, studies were required to report at least one clinically relevant outcome, such as wound healing, SSI, transfusion requirement, organ dysfunction (including acute kidney injury), length of hospital stay, or perioperative pain and opioid use. Eligible study designs comprised RCTs, prospective cohort studies, and high-quality observational studies.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they were animal or in vitro experiments, involved pediatric or obstetric surgical populations, evaluated interventions unrelated to the micronutrients of interest, or lacked extractable clinical outcome data. Case reports, narrative reviews, conference abstracts without full-text availability, and studies with major methodological flaws or insufficient reporting were also excluded.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using established tools appropriate to study design: Randomized controlled trials were evaluated with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool, while observational studies were appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Any discrepancies in study selection or quality assessment were resolved through consensus to maintain methodological rigor.

Out of the 22 studies that were included, 16 were randomized controlled trials that were divided by micronutrient. Four of these RCTs for iron included the PREVENTT, Spahn, Froessler, and Song trials; nine RCTs for vitamin C, including LOVIT plus dental and orthopedic trials; two RCTs for selenium, including SUSTAIN CSX and Schmidt; one RCT for zinc mainly comprising the historical Sedlacek trial; and zero RCTs for vitamin D with only observational evidence from Quraishi and Birinci cohorts. Six to eight observational studies per nutrient set were available to supplement RCT evidence. The uneven distribution of RCTs across the nutrients—with iron and vitamin C having the strongest evidence base, while vitamin D had no randomized trials—was the main factor that determined the GRADE certainty levels and led to conditional or strong recommendations for each micronutrient intervention (Fig. 2).

Primary Clinical Studies Used in this Review and Findings

Summary of the Selected Studies

The evidence base primarily comprises randomized controlled trials and well-characterized cohorts evaluating perioperative iron therapy, particularly intravenous ferric preparations, in major abdominal and cardiac surgery. Large multicenter trials consistently demonstrate correction of hemoglobin and iron indices, but these improvements do not reliably translate into reductions in transfusion, short-term mortality, or LOS in unselected populations.3,8 In contrast, smaller single-center studies and trials enriched with iron-deficient patients report more favorable transfusion outcomes, underscoring the importance of patient selection and timing (Table 1 and Fig. 3).7,11,12

Proportional representation of study types in the systematic review on micronutrients and perioperative outcomes

Antioxidant and trace element interventions show mixed results. Perioperative vitamin C has been associated with reduced inflammation, postoperative pain, and opioid use in elective surgery,9,10 whereas large intensive care unit (ICU) trials report heterogeneous outcomes and raise safety concerns in critically ill patients.13 Selenium supplementation prevents perioperative selenium decline but has not shown consistent benefits for organ dysfunction or mortality in large RCTs,4,14 despite observational links between low selenium and adverse outcomes.15,16 Observational evidence also associates low preoperative zinc and vitamin D levels with delayed wound healing, increased infection risk, and prolonged hospital stay,1,2,17,18 while interventional data remain limited and inconsistent outside deficiency states.19,20

Overall, substantial heterogeneity in study design, timing, dosing, and outcomes limits pooled inference. The findings support targeted screening and correction of documented micronutrient deficiencies rather than routine high-dose supplementation in unselected surgical populations (Table 1 and Fig. 3).

Appraisal Checklist

Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist covering eight criteria, including inclusion clarity, exposure and outcome measurement, confounder identification/control, and statistical validity (Supplementary Table 2). Most studies met core criteria, but several observational studies showed inadequate confounding control and inconsistent assessments, weakening their evidence. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews generally met all quality standards.

Overall, 19 of 22 studies (86.4%) met acceptable quality (≥6/8), with 11 (50.0%) scoring ≥7/8. RCTs showed higher quality (mean 7.12/8) than observational studies (6.61/8). Poor confounding control was the main limitation (31.8% of studies), particularly in observational cohorts (1/9), contributing to Very Low GRADE certainty for vitamin D and zinc despite positive associations.

GRADE Certainty-to-Clinical Decision Translation

GRADE findings indicate high certainty for iron supplementation in correcting hemoglobin, supporting routine use in preoperative anemia. Moderate–low certainty exists for iron in reducing transfusion, vitamin C in pain and wound healing, and selenium in organ dysfunction and mortality, warranting selective use. Low certainty persists for zinc in wound healing and vitamin D in infection prevention due to limited or inconsistent RCT evidence (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Risk of Bias and Quality

Larger multicenter RCTs (PREVENTT, Spahn et al., SUSTAIN CSX) were well randomized and methodologically robust, whereas smaller single-center RCTs, particularly vitamin C trials in dental and orthopedic settings, were often underpowered and occasionally single-blinded (Table 3; Fig. 4). Among observational studies, some adjusted for confounders, but residual confounding remains likely. Overall, RCT evidence supports micronutrient effects on biological markers (e.g., hemoglobin, ferritin) and intermediate outcomes such as pain, but not on hard clinical endpoints, including mortality or organ dysfunction.

Synthesis by Nutrient

IV iron rapidly corrects iron deficiency and improves hemoglobin and iron indices, particularly in anemic surgical patients. Small RCTs and observational studies suggest benefits in selected surgeries and when combined with ESAs, but the large PREVENTT trial showed no reduction in transfusion or mortality despite Hb correction. Evidence indicates benefits mainly for biological endpoints, with effectiveness influenced by timing, surgical context, inflammation, and iron phenotype.

Perioperative vitamin C supplementation reduces postoperative pain and opioid use across several surgical settings. However, high-dose IV vitamin C shows no mortality benefit and may increase adverse effects in critically ill patients. Moderate-dose supplementation is conditionally supported for pain relief in stable elective surgeries, with further research needed on optimal dosing and patient selection.

Low selenium levels are associated with increased perioperative complications and organ dysfunction. While supplementation reliably corrects biochemical deficiency, RCTs have not demonstrated consistent benefits for mortality or major clinical outcomes. Selenium may be useful in deficient or high oxidative-stress patients, but is not supported for routine perioperative use.

Zinc deficiency is strongly associated with impaired wound healing and higher infection risk. Observational studies consistently show worse outcomes with low zinc, but RCT evidence supports supplementation only in deficient or malnourished patients. Routine zinc supplementation in well-nourished surgical populations is not justified.

Low preoperative vitamin D is consistently linked to higher infection rates and perioperative complications. Current evidence supports screening and correcting deficiencies in high-risk patients, but the absence of confirmatory RCTs limits recommendations to targeted treatment rather than universal supplementation.

For clinical translation, a concise, evidence-informed summary algorithm outlining when to screen, test, supplement, or avoid routine micronutrient use in perioperative care is provided in Appendix 1.

Discussion

This synthesis of 22 human clinical studies demonstrates that inadequate micronutrient status—particularly involving iron, vitamin D, zinc, and selenium—is consistently associated with poorer perioperative outcomes, including SSIs, delayed wound healing, organ dysfunction, and prolonged recovery.1,2,19 These associations are biologically plausible, given the central role of micronutrients in erythropoiesis, immune competence, antioxidant defense, and tissue repair during the perioperative stress response.

Among the evaluated nutrients, iron supplementation shows the most consistent evidence at the biological level. Randomized controlled trials confirm that intravenous iron effectively corrects anemia and replenishes iron stores, even within short preoperative timeframes.3,6 However, large pragmatic trials such as PREVENTT have not demonstrated consistent reductions in transfusion requirements or short-term mortality, underscoring that correction of laboratory deficits does not automatically translate into improved hard clinical outcomes across unselected surgical populations.3 These findings suggest that the clinical benefit of iron depends on baseline iron phenotype, inflammatory status, and timing relative to surgery, rather than universal administration.

Vitamin C supplementation demonstrates a different evidence profile. Across several surgical settings, perioperative vitamin C has been associated with reductions in postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and improvements in wound-related parameters.5,10 These effects are most apparent in short-term, symptom-focused outcomes rather than survival or organ failure. Importantly, evidence from critical care populations cautions against indiscriminate high-dose intravenous use, highlighting the need for careful dose selection and patient stratification.13 Collectively, vitamin C appears promising as an adjunct for analgesia and recovery in stable elective surgery, but not as a universal perioperative intervention.21–26

Selenium deficiency is consistently associated with adverse perioperative outcomes in observational cohorts, reflecting its role in redox balance and immune modulation. Interventional trials show that supplementation reliably restores biochemical selenium levels and modulates inflammatory markers, but have not demonstrated consistent improvements in mortality or organ dysfunction.4,14,27 These findings indicate that selenium supplementation is most justifiable in patients with confirmed deficiency or high oxidative stress rather than as routine perioperative therapy.

Zinc follows a similar pattern. Low zinc status is strongly linked to impaired wound healing, increased infection risk, and postoperative complications across multiple surgical contexts.28,29 Interventional evidence supports supplementation primarily in zinc-deficient or malnourished patients, with limited justification for routine use in well-nourished populations.20,30 Zinc, therefore, functions best as a corrective therapy rather than a preventive supplement.

Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent and consistently associated with increased postoperative infections and complications.1,18,31–33 While randomized trials confirming outcome benefits are limited, the strength and consistency of observational evidence support screening and correction of deficiency in high-risk patients, aligning with current perioperative nutrition guidance.

Taken together, these findings support a shift away from indiscriminate supplementation toward precision perioperative nutrition, where micronutrient interventions are aligned with documented deficiencies, surgical risk profiles, and realistic biological targets. Benefits are most clearly demonstrated for surrogate and intermediate outcomes—such as hemoglobin correction, pain reduction, and biochemical normalization—rather than for universal reductions in mortality or organ dysfunction.

From a translational perspective, perioperative micronutrient optimization fits naturally within broader prehabilitation frameworks that integrate nutrition, anemia management, and functional conditioning. Iron correction may be embedded within elective surgical prehabilitation timelines, while vitamin C and zinc may be selectively incorporated into perioperative nutrition plans for malnourished or high-risk individuals. Such targeted deployment enhances both clinical efficiency and cost-effectiveness.

Future research should focus on deficiency-enriched trial designs that recruit only patients with confirmed micronutrient deficits, alongside optimization of timing and dosing strategies, particularly for iron and vitamin C. Studies should incorporate biomarker-guided approaches using inflammatory and metabolic indicators to identify responders and should extend beyond short-term surrogate outcomes to evaluate long-term endpoints such as functional recovery, quality of life, and hospital readmissions.

Routine perioperative micronutrient supplementation is not supported by current evidence; instead, targeted, deficiency-based correction—particularly for iron and vitamin D—offers the greatest potential clinical value.

Integration with ERAS Pathways

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols provide a practical platform for implementing targeted micronutrient strategies. Screening for iron and vitamin D deficiency may be incorporated into preoperative checklists, while perioperative vitamin C could be evaluated as part of opioid-sparing analgesia bundles. Selenium and zinc may be selectively used in deficient subgroups. Embedding micronutrient optimization within ERAS pathways allows standardized delivery, improved compliance, and systematic outcome evaluation without expanding protocol complexity.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that perioperative micronutrient status has a meaningful influence on surgical recovery, with deficiencies in iron, vitamin D, zinc, and selenium consistently associated with adverse outcomes. Robust randomized controlled trials confirm that intravenous iron effectively corrects anemia and improves hemoglobin levels; however, consistent reductions in transfusion and mortality have not been demonstrated. Vitamin C shows potential benefits in reducing postoperative pain and supporting wound healing in selected surgical settings. In contrast, evidence for vitamin D and zinc is largely observational, and high-quality interventional data remain limited. Selenium supplementation has not shown clear clinical benefit in large trials, underscoring the importance of appropriate patient selection rather than routine use.

Clinically, these findings support the integration of targeted biochemical screening into perioperative pathways, with correction of documented micronutrient deficiencies rather than indiscriminate supplementation. Vitamin C may be considered as an adjunct to enhance recovery in specific patient groups, while routine high-dose micronutrient use is not justified. Current evidence supports a precision-based perioperative nutrition approach focused on deficiency correction, selective supplementation, and alignment with prehabilitation and ERAS frameworks to optimize perioperative outcomes.
