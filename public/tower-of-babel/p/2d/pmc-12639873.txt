Overcoming barriers in primary bone cancer: Nanomaterial-enabled immunotherapy
Authors: Zhanchen Liu, Liwen Fan, Ying Wang, Xiqian Zhuo, Duo Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary bone tumors, which occur in bones or their affiliated tissues, have a low incidence rate, accounting for less than 1 % of all human malignant tumors. Seriously, the early symptoms of primary bone tumors are undetectable, and they easily metastasize, 20 % of cases are already in the advanced stage at the time of initial diagnosis. For most patients with primary bone tumors, surgery is the first choice of treatment, but its therapeutic effect is often limited. Meanwhile, primary bone tumors are generally not sensitive to other traditional tumor treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy, and may develop resistance to them, which often leads to a poor prognosis for patients. Crucially, immunotherapy, an important tumor treatment strategy, has been developing rapidly in recent years. It aims to improve the outcome of primary bone tumor treatment through mechanisms like remodeling immune microenvironment. Furthermore, as a new type of therapeutic carrier, nanomedicine has been widely used to improve the effects of immunotherapy, which can increase bioavailability and drug stability, reduce toxic side effects and so on, and has shown unique advantages such as high tumor targeting and high tissue permeability, making it essential for immunotherapy of primary bone tumors. In this review, we discussed the current understanding of primary bone tumors and immunotherapeutic methods, and comprehensively analyzed the application of nanomaterials in primary bone tumor immunotherapy to provide a theoretical basis and new ideas for the further development of new materials in the field of immunotherapy for primary bone tumors.Keywords: Primary bone neoplasms, Immunotherapy, Nanomaterial, Multifunctional nanoparticles, Combination therapy

Introduction

Primary bone tumors originate from bones or their accessory tissues, they account for 0.62 % of the total incidence of all kinds of malignant tumors, while their mortality rate is 0.77 %, which occurs most frequently in young people and elderly individuals [1]. Primary bone tumors occur rarely, and several cases are already in advanced stages when it is detected, which seriously affects the prognosis and survival of patients. In primary bone tumor treatment, surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy are the four classic methods. However, it is difficult to precisely control the tumor resection boundary in anatomically complex areas, which often leads to failure of surgical treatment. Meanwhile, primary bone tumors are less sensitive to radiotherapy, and they are also easily resistant to chemotherapy and targeted therapy. The combination of these methods not only increases the burden on the patient's body but also causes harm to normal tissues [2,3]. Therefore, investigating new, effective, and safe therapeutic strategies has become a key point to solve the issue of primary bone tumor treatment.

In recent years, immunotherapy has received increasing attention because of its strong specificity, high efficiency, and ability to kill tumors with chemotherapy resistance, which has been a popular topic of primary bone tumor treatment research [4]. Immunotherapy achieves the clearance of tumors by activating the patient's immune system, with low toxicity and side effects, and can be used to treat primary bone tumors through multiple mechanisms, such as stimulating immune cells to increase their activity, increasing the contact between immune cells and antigens, and regulating the tumor immunosuppressive environment, which has a wide range of application prospects. However, immunotherapy for primary bone tumors also has certain limitations, such as poor drug delivery efficiency, difficulty in allowing immune cells to fully infiltrate tumor tissues, and immunotoxic side effects [5]. Finding novel strategies to improve therapeutic outcomes has been urgently needed.

Nanomaterials have been utilized widely in tumor therapy research, which refer to a class of special materials whose sizes range from 1 to 100 nm. Nanomaterials have several excellent properties, such as large specific surface areas, easy magnetic resonance imaging, easy chemical modification, high biocompatibility, high photothermal conversion efficiency [6]. These advantages have led to considerable progress in their application in primary bone tumor immunotherapy, which usually used to improve drug transport systems and activate immune responses by acting on tumor cells or immune cells. Nanomaterials can target the bone tumor region by exerting enhanced permeability and retention effects (EPR) and binding to specific groups [7], and control drug release by external stimuli such as ultrasonic waves, heat, light, and magnetism, or by internal stimuli such as low pH, hypoxia, and specific enzyme activities [8]. Some drug-loaded nanomaterials can directly promote immune cell maturation and activation and increase the release of corresponding cytokines, or induce the death of tumor cells and indirectly stimulate immune cells via antigens released from lysis. Other nanomaterials can regulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) by improving vascular permeability through inflammatory mediators, normalizing the tumor vasculature, and responding to physical stimuli, then reverse immunosuppression, so that improve the immunotherapeutic effect [9]. In addition, several metallic nanomaterials can efficiently convert light into heat and induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production when exposed to near-infrared (NIR) light [10], which has phototherapeutic potential, and can be utilized for combining immunotherapy with other types of therapy.

In this review, we comprehensively discussed the current research progress on primary bone tumor immunotherapy, focused on the application of nanomaterials in the field of primary bone tumor immunotherapy, described their therapeutic mechanisms and application examples in detail, and finally summarized the advantages and limitations of nanomaterials in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors, providing insights and suggestions for the future development of new materials that can be used for the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors.

Progress in primary bone tumor immunotherapy

Bone tumors can be classified into primary bone tumors and secondary bone tumors. Secondary bone tumors refer to tumors originating from other organs or tissues that metastasize to the skeleton, often arising from highly invasive cancers such as breast cancer, prostate cancer, and lung cancer [11]. The physiological characteristics of secondary bone tumors vary considerably, highly influenced by the primary tumor. Their treatment poses significant challenges, and currently, palliative care is often the main approach taken [12]. Primary bone tumors can be further classified into various types according to the site of tumor tissue prevalence and cell morphology and structure, which exists significant heterogeneity. As a new approach to primary bone tumor treatment, many feasible treatment options have been investigated in immunotherapy. The research status of different primary bone tumor immunotherapy methods is comprehensively discussed below.

Research progress and treatment strategies for primary bone tumors

According to the fifth edition of the classification system published by the World Health Organization (WHO), primary malignant bone tumors can be categorized by site of origin into chondrogenic tumors, osteogenic tumors, fibrogenic tumors, notochordal tumors, vascular tumors of bone, hematopoietic neoplasms of bone, and other mesenchymal tumors of bone. They can also be classified by tumor cell composition into osteoclastic giant cell-rich tumors. Each category encompasses one or more specific types of bone tumors (Table 1) [13]. Among these types of primary malignant bone tumors, osteogenic osteosarcoma (OS), Ewing sarcoma (ES) belonging to undifferentiated sarcomas within other mesenchymal tumors, and chondrogenic chondrosarcoma (CS) have been studied more extensively. The most common type of primary malignant bone tumor is OS, which constitutes nearly 70 % of total cases and is triggered by gene mutations in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) or osteoblasts in mature bone tissue. OS is usually found in the metaphyses of long bones such as tibia, femur, and humerus in adolescents, whose five-year survival rate is 70 %. Meanwhile, OS is highly metastatic, it can easily spread to organs such as lungs, significantly reducing the survival rate to 20 % [14]. The occurrence of OS is related to the low expression of the tumor suppressor genes p53 and RB1, whereas the excessive activation of a few pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, WNT/β-catenin, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, and Hedgehog signaling pathways, is also a major mechanism leading to the development and metastasis of OS [15]. Another primary bone tumor that is also prevalent in adolescents is ES, which originates from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), is commonly found in pelvis and long bones, has the second highest incidence of adolescent primary bone tumors [16]. ES is characterized by chromosomal translocations, which is usually triggered by fusion proteins generated by the translocation of EWSR1 on chromosome 22 to FLI1on chromosome 11. ES is also highly aggressive, with metastasis occurring in about 25 % of patients and a survival rate of only about 50 % [17]. Unlike OS and ES, CS is a primary bone tumor that occurs in chondrocytes or mesenchymal cells that differentiated toward chondrocytes, and is most often concentrated in the long bones, pelvis, and scapula of adults aged 30–60 years, comprising 20–30 % of all primary malignant bone tumor cases. Owing to the low percentage of dividing cells and poor vascular distribution, although the growth rate and metastasis of CS are more controllable than those of other primary bone tumors, it also leads to frequent resistance to conventional therapeutic modalities like radiotherapy and chemotherapy [18].

In the occurrence and development of primary bone tumors, osteoclasts play an important role. When tumor cells proliferate to a certain extent after dormancy in the bone, they secrete cytokines that can cause bone destruction or bone-related events directly or indirectly through osteoclasts. Some osteoclast-stimulating factors directly activate osteoclasts, whereas others indirectly promote bone resorption by osteoclasts through stimulating the nuclear factor ligand RANK-L to increase the levels of IL-6 and IL-8 released, triggering bone destruction [19]. Additionally, certain tumor cells can induce osteoclasts to secrete specific cytokines, which in turn act on the tumor cells to enhance their proliferation, forming a vicious cycle that destroys the original physiological bone metabolism [20], then finally causes severe bone-related events, including spinal cord compression, hypercalcemia, pain, and pathological fracture [21]. Therefore, the development of primary bone tumors is extremely detrimental to the quality of life and health of affected individuals and requires greater attention and treatment.

Classical primary bone tumor treatment strategies include radiotherapy, chemotherapy, surgery, and targeted therapy. Surgery is the preferred option for most cases, which is extremely demanding for the operator. When tumors grow in special anatomical structures, the positive rate of surgical margins is high, and complete resection is difficult. Besides, primary bone tumors have poor sensitivity to radiation therapy due to their biological characteristics and TME, and are also resistant to chemotherapy and targeted therapy, which often leads to distant metastasis, further limiting the effectiveness of traditional treatment options [2]. New treatment approaches that are safer, more effective, and more convenient for primary bone tumors need to be developed.

Mechanism of primary bone tumor immunotherapy

Tumor immunotherapy can inhibit and kill tumor cells by actively or passively inducing tumor-specific immune responses, which has the advantages of being specific and efficient and sparing the body from injurious treatment. In the treatment of primary bone tumors, immunotherapy is different from traditional treatments fundamentally, it killstumors through various mechanisms. It can activate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), B cells, and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) through direct action on peripheral immune organs such as spleen and lymph nodes, to activate immune response [22,23]. It can also reverse the immunosuppressive effects of the TME by converting M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which promote tumor growth, into M1-type TAMs, which possess the ability to engulf tumor cells and present antigens, secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, thereby enhancing the cytotoxicity of other immune cells and inhibiting immunosuppressive cells. [24]. Furthermore, immunotherapy can cause immunogenic cell death (ICD) by directly targeting bone tumors, resulting in a massive release of antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn activate APCs and accelerate their antigenic processing, eliciting a strong immune response and producing a strong tumor killing effect [25].

Several indicators can be used to measure the outcomes of immunotherapy. Calreticulin (CRT) and high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), two proteins that can exert antitumor activity by stimulating the immune system, are often used as markers to initially determine whether primary bone tumors undergo lysis and the immune response is activated, as their levels increase considerably after the lysis and death of tumor cells [26]. Besides, in terms of cytokines, an increase in the levels of certain immunoreactive cytokines, such as IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, and a decrease in the levels of the immunosuppressive cytokine TGF-β, favor the infiltration and activation of immune cells [27]. Detecting changes in the levels of these cytokines is one of the criteria used in most studies to determine whether the immune system is functioning. These strategies may also serve as key parts of primary bone tumor immunotherapy in the future, possibly by increasing the expression of relevant targets to improve the microenvironment of primary bone tumors to achieve a greater immunotherapeutic effect.

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)

Immune checkpoints are substances that can suppress the body's immune-killing function and prevent cells from being attacked by the immune system. The immune checkpoint that has been studied the most is PD-1, which can be produced by various immune cells. After binding with PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, PD-1 can terminate the tumor-killing function of immune cells and inhibit immune responses [28]. Another immune checkpoint is CTLA-4, which can be expressed in Tregs and memory T cells, competes with CD28 for the B7 ligand, promotes IDO expression, inhibits the proliferation and activation of T cells through the interference with the cell cycle and the disruption of cytokine levels [29]. Because of its ability to inhibit the function of immune checkpoints in tumor tissues, ICB can maintain the normal killing activity of T cells, thus clear tumor cells and preventing immune escape, which is the most widely used immunotherapy. Common immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) mainly target PD-1/PD-L1.

In the treatment of primary bone tumors, ICBs have good antitumor effects when used alone or in combination with other drugs. Similar to other tumors, elevated PD-1 expression is observed in primary bone tumors compared to normal bone tissue. Blocking PD-1/PD-L1 releases its suppression of CD8+ T-cells, enhances immune responses, and prevents immune escape. Development of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors is now relatively well-established, with monoclonal antibodies, peptides, and small molecules all available for application [30]. In addition to anti-PD-L1 antibody, another major ICI is the anti-CTLA-4 antibody, which can induce OS cell apoptosis in patients with various types of OS, effectively inhibiting the progression and metastasis of OS. When used in combination with other ICIs, the therapeutic effect is even more pronounced [31]. Along with these two major immune checkpoints, several studies have investigated other immune checkpoints with similarly good efficacy. For instance, CD47 is a type of transmembrane protein that suppresses the phagocytosis of macrophages after binding with SIRPα on their surface, thereby allowing tumor survival. Blocking this process with anti-CD47 antibodies significantly inhibits OS growth and prolongs mice survival, with notable therapeutic effects, which can also be used in combination with STAT-3 inhibitors [32].

ICB is a very important means of immunotherapy that can maintain the normal function of immune cells and significantly inhibit tumors. It can not only be used along with chemotherapy but also be combined with other types of immunotherapies to significantly enhance the therapeutic effect, which has the advantages of simple principles, safety, reliability, and few side effects. However, in clinical trials, because of the immunosuppressive effects of the TME, such as the suppression of the immune response by TAMs and MDSCs, the impaired function of dendritic cells (DCs), and the joint suppression of immune activity by multiple immune checkpoints [33], ICB need to be further improved for the treatment of primary bone tumors to achieve better therapeutic effects.

Tumor vaccines

Tumor vaccines are a technique used to stimulate the body's immune system, trigger a systemic immune response and eliminate tumors via the use of tumor-specific proteins, peptides, DNA, or other lysates as antigens. Tumor vaccines can be classified into peptide vaccines and DC vaccines. Peptide vaccines activate specific immune responses by synthesizing peptide fragments corresponding to certain antigens expressed by tumor cells and inoculating them into patients [34], whereas DC vaccines are cultured and matured in vitro and then activated with tumor cell lysates or tumor-specific peptides, which are sufficiently activated and proliferate before being injected back into patients to directly participate in the immune response [35]. Peptide vaccines have demonstrated promising results in the treatment of primary bone tumors [36,37]. Studies applying peptide vaccines to treat OS have demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects against OS cells and suppressed metastasis. Peptide vaccines exhibit high therapeutic efficacy and safety, avoiding off-target effects, but require HLA-I-mediated activation to function [5]. Besides, DC vaccines can act in conjunction with immune activators and ICIs to enhance therapeutic effects. Anti-glucocorticoid-induced tumor necrosis factor receptor (GITR) antibody is an immune activator that inhibits the proliferation of Tregs with high expression of GITR, which promotes the activation of CTLs and enhances the immune response [38]. Co-injection of the anti-GITR antibody and DC vaccine prepared from tumor lysates into the mouse OS LM8 model increased the IFN-γ level and decreased the IL-10 level in serum, and promoted CD8+ T-cell infiltration, with a tumor volume of only about 270 mm3 after 42 days, compared to more than 1200 mm3 in the control group, nearly doubling the survival period of the mice, whose therapeutic effect was significantly better than that of the DC vaccine alone [39]. Zhou et al. showed that a DC vaccine made from mouse CD103+ cDC1 can trigger systemic and long-lasting cytotoxicity mediated by tumor antigen-specific T cells, induce a Th1 response, activate CD8+ T-cells and induce them to secrete IFN-γ, thus inhibiting the growth of the OS K7M3 cell line, increasing the survival rate of mice to over 50 %, preventing secondary attack, and resulting in complete tumor clearance within two weeks if combined with CTLA-4 or PD-1 antibodies [40].

In summary, the in vivo mechanism of action of tumor vaccines is primarily based on increasing the levels of cytokines that regulate the immune system, such as IFN-γ, and promoting CD8+ T-cell infiltration, which exerts a powerful tumor-killing effect. Besides, tumor vaccines can also be combined with precision ablation techniques such as thermotherapy, surgery, and electroporation to enhance treatment outcomes [41].

Cytokine therapy

Cytokines belong to a class of small-molecule proteins secreted by immune cells or non-immune cells that can regulate cell growth and differentiation and modulate immune functions, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL), interferon (IFN), and colony-stimulating factor (CSF), as well as growth factors and chemokines. Cytokine therapy inhibits or promotes the expression of several cytokine-related genes through the use of drugs or certain treatments, increasing the number or the area of action of cytokines that increase immunity and decreasing the number of cytokines that inhibit immunity, thus increasing the immune response and killing tumors (Table 4).

Cytokine therapy currently applied in primary bone tumor treatment.

Some cytokines have inhibitory effects on the development of primary bone tumors; for example, type I IFN has anti-angiogenic ability, and being modified by polyethylene glycol (PEG) will significantly prolong its half-life, decrease the frequency of administration, and increase the effective dosage, which is effective in treating primary bone tumors [46]. Besides, some IL family members, like IL-2, IL-12, and IL-15, can activate and promote natural killer (NK) cell and T lymphocyte expansion, which are the most common class of cytokines [47]. However, some other cytokines have dual effects on tumor development, such as IL-10, a cytokine released by immature DCs and other immune cells, can inhibit DC maturation, T-cell activation, and the body's antitumor capacity [48]. But IL-10 also enhances the reactive activity of IL-2 and prevents the apoptosis of CD8+ T-cells, which favors the immune response [49]. The same as granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF); although GM-CSF promotes the proliferation of cells such as neutrophils and macrophages, it also helps tumor cells survive and expand [50]. Nevertheless, some cytokines promote tumor growth, such as the pro-inflammatory factor, TNF-α, which not only increases the expression of PD-1 in tumor cells, but also induces the apoptosis of CD8+ T-cells. These changes hinder their infiltration and function and help tumor cells achieve immune escape [51]. Another cytokine is TGF-β; although it truly inhibits early tumor development, its inhibitory effect changes to a promotional effect as the tumor progresses. Additionally, TGF-β can inhibit most immune cells, for example, by inhibiting T-cell proliferation, inducing B-cell apoptosis, and exerting a pro-inflammatory effect [52]. Therefore, TGF-β is usually regarded as an immunosuppressive cytokine.

In cytokine therapy, because of the multifaceted nature of cytokines in the development of primary bone tumors, both high levels of immunoreactive cytokines and low levels of immunosuppressive cytokines are desirable. The most important feature of cytokine therapy is its rich variety and diverse mechanisms of action. However, as high concentrations or doses of cytokines often harm the body in unpredictable ways, the application of cytokine therapy is quite limited and tightly controlled, and the scope of application is restricted.

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT)

ACT is a method of extracting one's lymphocytes or allogeneic lymphocytes, activating and expanding them in vitro, sometimes through gene editing, and then killing the tumor cells directly by inserting these lymphocytes into the body of the affected individual [53]. ACT can be divided into adoptive NK cell therapy and adoptive T-cell therapy, and the latter can be further classified into T-cell receptor-engineered T-cell therapy (TCR-T-cell therapy) and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T-cell therapy). NK cells have the advantages of no need for activation, high lethality, a rich mechanism of action, reliability, and safety, which are excellent for treating primary bone tumors [54]. TCR-T-cell therapy involves the modification of receptors on activated T-cell surfaces to enhance their functionality, making it easier to recognize tumor antigens and clear tumors. In contrast, CAR-T-cell therapy involves the direct implantation of synthetic antigens on the surface of T cells without the restriction of MHC [55].

Adoptive NK cell therapy is often useful when other immunotherapeutic techniques are ineffective. For example, ES is a low-immunogenic tumor, and most ES cells have low or no expression of MHC class I [56], which makes it difficult for them to be recognized by CTLs and thus survive. However, ES lacking MHC class I have no inhibitory effect on NK cells and so that they are more susceptible to NK cell-mediated immune responses. A study used the K562-mbIL21 system to expand NK cells in vitro and found that their killing effect on ES TC106 cells reached 80 %. Intravenously injecting these cells into an in vivo xenograft model significantly decreased the number of ES metastatic nodules in the lungs, but their effect on ES primary foci was not prominent; thus, it is considered to be a new treatment for metastatic or recurrent ES [57]. For adoptive T-cell therapy, TCR-T-cell therapy has good tolerability, safety, specificity, and antitumor activity [58]. However, it is still in the research and development stage because it depends on the expression of MHC and HLA to perform its therapeutic function and is less effective in primary bone tumors with low expression of MHC and in patients with large HLA differences [59]. Unlike TCR-T-cell therapy, studies on CAR-T-cell therapy have reported favorable therapeutic outcomes. CAR-T cells that target EphA2 can kill OS and ES cells in vitro by releasing IL-2 and IFN-γ, eradicate tumors, greatly prolong mouse survival, and inhibit tumor metastasis in an in vivo model [60]. B7-H3-targeted CAR-T cells can secrete IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, completely eradicate MG63.3 and ES xenograft models, and have a obvious survival advantage [61]. However, there is a risk that CAR-T-cell therapy may trigger cytokine release syndrome, which manifests as neurologic inflammation, fever, hypotension, and dyspnea [62].

To sum up, ACT has advantages that function independently of the immunosuppressive environment in vivo, which is effective in removing bone tumor cells that express the corresponding targets. Especially, adoptive NK-cell therapy, which exerts killing effects without relying on antigen recognition, is an important option for refractory primary bone tumor treatment with low immunogenicity. As the selection of antigens and targets greatly influences the outcomes of ACT, and the management of the side effects of ACT is complicated and harmful, ACT is in the preliminary design and experimental research stages.

Current challenges in primary bone tumor immunotherapy

Although immunotherapy has several advantages for primary bone tumors, it also has clinical limitations. Owing to the special physicochemical properties of the bone microenvironment (BME) in which primary bone tumors are located, the high vascular density and the low oxygen content, they lead to the expression of hypoxia-inducible factors, which promote tumor growth through multiple pathways, for example, promoting angiogenesis, stimulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of tumor cells, proliferation of tumor stem cells, and immune escape [63]. Besides the BME, the TME is also one of the major causes of a decrease in immunotherapeutic effects. Immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs, MDSCs, and Tregs, as well as immunosuppressive cytokines including IL-10 and TGF-β, can be found in the TME, and the overactivation of a few pathways such as PD-1, IDO, and STAT-3 also contributes to the suppression of the immune response [64]. Failure to effectively relieve the multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms of TME can significantly affect immunotherapy. Additionally, some primary bone tumors such as OS are low-immunogenic tumors with low sensitivity to the immune response [65], which further increases the difficulty of immunotherapy (Scheme 1). However, the application of nanomaterials offers a breakthrough in addressing these biological barriers. Due to their smaller size compared to conventional materials, nanomaterials inherently penetrate denser bone matrix more readily to reach tumor tissues. Their large specific surface area enables binding to multiple ligands, further enhancing penetration into bone tissue and precision targeting of tumors. Furthermore, specific types of nanomaterials, such as metallic nanoparticles, can act on immune cells within the TME. They promote the activation and infiltration of DCs and CTLs, while also facilitating M1 polarization of TAMs, significantly reversing the immunosuppressive environment. Certain nanoparticles can also modulate the expression of tumor chemokines, altering the TME to create conditions more conducive to immune responses [66].

Mechanisms and influencing factors of immunotherapy for primary bone tumors.

In addition to its limited efficacy, immunotherapy for primary bone tumors also faces widespread concerns about its potential systemic toxicity. Published literature has documented immune-related adverse events (irAEs) triggered by the use of ICIs such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, affecting vital organs and systems including the blood, heart, intestines, nerves, and lungs, and in some cases resulting in fatal outcomes, with an incidence rate exceeding 50 % [[67], [68], [69]]. To detect whether irAE has occurred and respond as early as possible, several biomarkers have been identified, including blood cell counts, cytokine levels, antibody levels, and HLA expression levels [70]. To ensure the efficacy of immunotherapy in eliminating primary bone tumors, enhancing drug targeting to tumor tissues is crucial. Primary bone tumors express numerous specific ligands at high levels, including mTOR, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), TP53 mutations, MYC, insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R), vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), and so on. The excessive activation of these targets is a key factor promoting the proliferation and metastasis of primary bone tumors. They can serve as targets for delivering drugs to primary bone tumors, playing a vital role in drug accumulation within tumor tissues and mitigating off-target effects. Numerous studies have demonstrated significant therapeutic effects on primary bone tumors by targeting these pathways [4]. These targets also hold tremendous promise in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors involving nanomaterials.

Progress in the use of nanomaterials in primary bone tumor immunotherapy

In the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors, nanomaterials can not only be used as carriers of nanomedicines but also improve the immune microenvironment or achieve multiple treatments by combining them with other medicines, offering functions such as immune stimulation and convenient active regulation. Furthermore, it significantly enhances drug targeting by responding to endogenous characteristics of the TME, actively applying exogenous stimuli, binding to receptors, and delivering multi-stimuli, which significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy for primary bone tumors [71]. Additionally, nanomaterials can significantly address the inherent high drug resistance of primary bone tumors, providing crucial support for the efficacy of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, phototherapy, and other treatments [72,73]. Nanomaterials generally have the advantages of a large specific surface area, good permeability, and special thermal, electromagnetic, and biological properties [74]. They can be divided into three categories: organic nanomaterials, inorganic nanomaterials, and hybrid nanomaterials. Furthermore, organic nanomaterials include polymeric nanomaterials, lipid-based nanomaterials, nanohydrogels, and natural cell membranes; inorganic nanomaterials consist of metallic nanomaterials and non-metallic inorganic nanomaterials, while the latter include carbon nanomaterials and silicon nanomaterials; hybrid nanomaterials are mainly metal-organic frameworks. Organic nanomaterials

The greatest advantages of organic nanomaterials are that they are biocompatible, modifiable, easily degradable, and non-residual, and they can also bind to drugs in various ways, which makes them ideal for use as drug carriers [75]. Meanwhile, given the chiral nature of organics, some studies have focused on the development of chiral nanomaterials with special electronic, magnetic, and optical properties, covering a wide range of organic, inorganic, and hybrid materials [76].

Polymeric nanomaterials

Polymeric nanomaterials have high stability, no cytotoxicity, easy degradation, and no residue. They can efficiently carry various substances, such as antigens, immune activators, cytokines, and siRNAs, which are the best choice as transport carriers. Polymer nanoparticles can not only deliver drugs to tumor cells or APCs through various active or passive targeting mechanisms but also protect drugs and extend drug circulation time in the body while reducing systemic toxicity because of their stable chemical properties. Additionally, polymers have good immunostimulatory activity, and polymer nanoparticles of specific diameters can stimulate an increase in the levels of several cytokines, which are more readily absorbed by the immune system, further enhancing the immunostimulatory effect [77]. Based on these properties, polymeric nanomaterials are widely used in primary bone tumor immunotherapy.

The polymeric materials used widely are chitosan, polymeric micelles, and PEG. Among them, chitosan has significantly better bio-adhesion properties compared to other polymers, and its surface is positively charged in an acidic environment, which maintains its stability and effectively prolongs the retention time of the drug [78]. Cao et al. [79] used H6R6 peptide-modified chitosan encapsulated with Fe2+, siRad18, and DOX (Fig. 1a) and reported that it could prevent DNA damage repair and inhibit the development of DOX resistance. Moreover, ROS generated by the Fe2+-induced Fenton reaction caused ICD in K7M2 cells, and then reduced cell viability to about 40 %, and released tumor-associated antigen (TAA), which in turn effectively activated and matured DCs. The chitosan significantly inhibited OS growth and attenuated lung metastasis in mice, and increased the survival rate from 80 % to 100 % in combination with PD-L1 blockers (Fig. 1b). Compared to chitosan, polymeric micelles, which are formed from amphiphilic block copolymers self-assembled in water, are a type of stable colloidal solution that has various advantages, such as better drug dissolution, more controlled drug release, the ability to target tumors, low rejection reactions, and ease of surface modification [80]. Jin et al. [81] designed a polymeric micelle (OPDEA-PDCA) that could target the mitochondria of OS cells. It inhibited PDHK1 activity and induced mitochondrial oxidative stress (Fig. 1c), which further led to cellular pyroptosis and was more than 100-fold more toxic than the dichloroacetic acid monomer. In the K7M2, MG63, and MNNG-HOS cell lines, it significantly triggered ICD, upregulated the expression of HMGB1 and CRT, and increased IFN-γ levels and CD8+ T-cell number, obviously improving the survival rate of OS tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1d). However, cellular pyroptosis can also increase extracellular PD-L1 expression, which limits the therapeutic effect somewhat. The combination of PD-L1 inhibitors further inhibits OS development and ensures the survival of the mice within 30 days (Fig. 1d). Similar to polymeric micelles, PEG can circulate and accumulate in bone tumor tissues via EPR with good biocompatibility, drug encapsulation efficiency, and low cytotoxicity [82]. Xiao et al. [83] fabricated a dual pH-sensitive CDM-PEG-PMDA composite nanocarrier loaded with the photosensitizers, Ce6, and aCD47, which induced ICD under laser irradiation, amplified the signals of HGMB1 and CRT, exhibited higher DC, CD4+ T-cell, and CD8+ T-cell infiltration and increased the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α, enabling the synergistic treatment of photodynamic therapy (PDT) and immunotherapy in K7M2-bearing mice, nearly doubling their survival time (Fig. 1e).

Except to the above materials, poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is also a common polymer nanomaterial that can be used as a drug carrier to regulate the release rate to achieve slow release, with good encapsulation and targeting effects [84]. PLGA can also be used to deliver appropriate concentrations of tumor lysate antigens, which have stronger stimulatory and maturation-promoting effects on DCs and do not affect T-cell activation or the secretion of cytokines, such as IL-4 and IFN-γ, which can be used to develop tumor vaccines [85]. These features may be applied in the future.

Polymers have been used by most of the researchers in this field as the basis for designing vectors, which is an important part of immunotherapy for primary bone tumors and the best option for achieving multimodal combination therapy. The advantage of the ease of modification of polymers allows them to meet the individual needs of various studies, and their combination with specific drugs can improve targeting or cell penetration capabilities. Besides, the high loading efficiency of polymers facilitates the simultaneous delivery of multiple drugs, allowing multiple therapeutic techniques to work synergistically to significantly improve therapeutic efficacy.

Lipid-based nanomaterials

Lipid-based nanomaterials primarily consist of lipids, whose composition is similar to that of the biofilm system of cells, have the outstanding advantages of low immunogenicity, good permeability, and high biosafety, can deliver diverse substances, including peptides, inorganic biomaterials, and nucleic acids, and can effectively control the release of drugs. Surface modification by various ligands and inducers can endow lipid-based nanomaterials with the ability to bind to various targeting agents or materials, exhibit different electrical properties, and be stored at different temperatures based on different uses, but it also increases the difficulty of fabrication and production cost, which limits the range of applications to some extent [86].

Liposomes and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are two main types of lipid-based nanomaterials. Liposomes are double-layered, closed spherical vesicles with an aqueous core formed by the dissolution of phospholipids in aqueous solution. These vesicles exhibit amphiphilic properties and drug diffusivity and are often used to trigger ICDs [87]. Wang et al. [88] encapsulated DOX with liposomes and then co-transported it with the cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) inhibitory factor Nox 4i and reported that they effectively inhibited CAFs and induced K7M2 cells to undergo ICD, amplified CRT levels, stimulated DC, CD3+, and CD8+ T-cell maturation, and secreted IL-2 and IFN-γ, which stagnated OS growth and suppressed lung metastasis in K7M2/NIH3T3-bearing mice. This strategy could be used in combination with αPD-1 (Fig. 2a–c). Unlike liposomes, LNPs usually consist of bilateral shells composed of aqueous nuclei and multiple lipids, which can be used to transport metal ion-containing drugs. A research group designed a LNP formed by self-assembly of lipid tails loaded with oxaliplatin (OXA)-based PtIV (Lipo-OXA-ALN) combined with alendronate (ALN), which efficiently targeted K7M2 cells and inhibited the acquisition of Ca2+ by bone tumor cells. This not only directly inhibited the exchange of essential substances by bone tumor cells, but also increased their permeability and improved the uptake of nanoparticles, thus allowing OXA to exert a stronger killing effect. This LNP induced 33.1 % apoptosis in vitro, and in vivo experiments demonstrated significant inhibition of tumor growth (Fig. 2d). Increased expression of the DC maturation markers CD86 and CD80, increased percentage of CD3+ and CD8+ T-cells, decreased Tregs content, increased ratio of M1/M2 TAMs, and increased levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were also reported, which reversed the immunosuppressive microenvironment (Fig. 2e) [89]. Additionally, LNPs can be used to transport mRNA, which may be applied to the development of mRNA-based bone tumor vaccines [90,91].

Overall, lipid-based nanomaterials are essential because of their unique properties, significantly higher tissue permeability and safety than other materials, as well as their ease of modification, which can considerably improve their immunostimulatory activity or drug delivery efficiency when combined with different moieties. Meanwhile, lipid-based nanomaterials can be loaded with many types of substances and are among the key materials for the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors. Many experimental studies are being performed to complement and develop lipid-based nanomaterials that can be used for immunotherapy of primary bone tumors.

Other organic nanomaterials

Besides the two main organic nanomaterials mentioned above, other materials of influence for primary bone tumor immunotherapy, such as nanohydrogels and natural cell membranes, which also have very unique properties and play a special role in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors, have been identified.

Hydrogels are three-dimensional hydrophilic networks composed of cross-linked biocompatible polymers whose porous structure can be used to encapsulate and transport drugs. Owing to their physicochemical properties are quite similar to the human extracellular matrix, hydrogels show excellent stability in physiological environments [92]. When doped with nanomaterials, the structural scale of the hydrogel was further expanded, and the drug transport and release capabilities were further enhanced, resulting in satisfactory biodegradability, structural stability, and water retention ability, making it a good choice for killing bone tumors via ICD [93]. A study revealed that an mPEG45-PLV19 thermosensitive hydrogel containing ALN and OXA could target to mouse in situ K7M2 cells, and sustain the slow release of OXA and induce ICD within 40 days, with a CD8+ T-cell infiltration rate of 45.8 % and OS inhibition of 60.4 %, which significantly decreased the degree of tibial bone destruction and inhibited lung metastasis (Fig. 3a–c) [94]. Nanohydrogels can be used not only for immunotherapy but also as transport carriers and dispersants for other nanoformulations to increase their permeability in tissues. Another important function of nanohydrogels is that they can be used for bone damage recovery after bone tumors are resected through surgery, modulating the TME and immune microenvironment, inhibiting the production of osteoclasts and M2-type macrophages, and facilitating the proliferation of BMSCs, which is beneficial for bone regeneration [95,96].

Natural cell membranes are new materials that are being studied and applied. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membranous vesicular structures secreted by cells to the outside; EVs with diameters between 30 and 150 nm are known as exosomes and are under investigation. MSCs are the main source of exosomes, which regulate immune activity and highly target tumor sites [97]. Exosomes carry proteins, RNAs, cytokines, and other substances from MSCs, among which certain miRNAs even promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bone tumors, severely interfering with therapeutic efficacy and safety [98]. Therefore, engineering MSCs or using cell membranes derived from tumor cells are commonly used solutions. Gong et al. [99] prepared a PEG-PCL nano-micelle encapsulated by OS cell membranes to carry RRx-001 and IR780, which are CD47 inhibitors and ultrasound sensitizers respectively (Fig. 3d). They found that it could be activated by ultrasound to cause ICD in K7M2 and 143B cells via sonodynamic therapy (SDT), with an apoptosis rate close to 60 %, increased HMGB1 and CRT expression, promoted macrophage infiltration into OS and M1-type polarization, and raised the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which eliminated in situ development and lung metastasis of the subcutaneous K7M2 tumor model in mice, with a survival rate of 80 % at 70 days. In contrast, other groups of mice died within 50 days (Fig. 3e and f). Despite the higher biocompatibility and permeability of natural cell membranes, their yield is not stable enough, the extraction method is complicated, the impurities carried may lead to adverse reactions or limit therapeutic efficacy, and related experimental studies are very limited, mostly still in the exploration stage.

To conclude, nanohydrogels have high drug encapsulation efficiency and tissue permeability, and can be stable in the body for a long period, which is highly favorable for the slow release of drugs. In addition to transporting drugs, nanohydrogels can also participate in the repair process after treatment and promote bone regeneration. In contrast, the function of natural cell membranes mainly depends on their cell of origin. Cell membranes from immunomodulatory cells, such as MSCs, play complex and multiple roles in tumor growth, whereas those from tumor cells have bone tumor-targeting effects. Although these two organic nanomaterials have gained less attention, their unique properties and roles should be considered important complementary parts of nanomaterials for the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors, which are under further research and development.

Inorganic nanomaterials

Inorganic nanomaterials, with higher stability, more diverse physical properties, and more easily regulated sizes and shapes, are widely used not only in routine examinations and diagnosis, but also in multimodal synergistic treatment of primary bone tumors [100]. However, their high stability may also lead to the accumulation of toxicity. The categorization of inorganic nanomaterials includes metallic nanomaterials and non-metallic nanomaterials. In this part, the role and application of inorganic nanomaterials in primary bone tumor immunotherapy are fully described.

Metallic nanomaterials

Metallic elements and their related proteins are important components of organisms that are involved in key life processes, such as signal transduction, energy transfer, redox regulation, and material metabolism, and they also play important roles in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors [101]. A wide range of metallic nanomaterials, such as iron, manganese, copper, calcium, and magnesium, is available (Table 6).

Role of metal nanomaterials in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors.

Above all, iron and manganese nanomaterials can activate immune cells and kill tumor cells directly. Iron death, a form of programmed cell death caused by the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, is one of the key pathways for iron to treat primary bone tumors. Yu et al. [102] prepared PLGA nanomicrospheres loaded with Fe3O4 and the PDT probe DHJS, and reported that it blocked the expression of the Nrf2 pathway, inhibited the antioxidant activity of OS cells, produced 10.8-fold greater levels of ROS under laser irradiation compared to that recorded in the control group. This triggered intense iron death, reduced HOS cell activity to 7.5 %, and almost completely cured HOS and K7M2-bearing mouse models. Moreover, the nanoparticles promoted DC maturation, induced M1-type polarization of macrophages, and increased the number of CD4+ T-cells and TAMs, which also has potential for ACT.,As important as iron, manganese is present in most metalloenzymes and is required for various physiological processes, which activate cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and the STING pathway to mature and activate DCs, macrophages, NK cells, and CD8+ T-cells [103]. A study using BSA-templated MnO2 nanosheets loaded with the glycosyl-modified Toll-like receptor 7 agonist 1V209 was found to promote the production of ROS by M1-type macrophages via the Fenton-like reaction, increasing their ratio by threefold in vivo through the activation of the NF-kB pathway, and increasing the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells. When these cells were injected into the tumor tissues in situ in mice bearing K7M2, OS growth was largely stopped, systemic immune responses were activated, almost no lung metastasis occurred, and they functioned as the ACT [104].

Same as iron, copper is also an important trace element that plays an important regulatory role in various metabolic processes. In primary bone tumor treatment, copper can be used to achieve immunotherapy by inducing ICD through ROS generated by a Fenton-like reaction. Cheng et al. [105] constructed a mesoporous Cu&Ce-based oxide nanoplatform loaded with indocyanine green (ICG), using an RGD peptide as a targeting agent, and reported that it could destroy the membrane potential of mitochondria of 143B cells under light irradiation at 808 nm via two pathways, exothermic and Fenton-like reaction-generated ROS. Under light irradiation at 808 nm, it disrupted the mitochondrial membrane potential of 143B cells through both exothermic and Fenton-like reactions to generate ROS, thus triggering ICD, with a total mortality rate close to 100 %, which tripled the CRT level on the cell membrane, decreased the level of cytoplasmic HMGB1 to 20 %, increased the proportions of mature DCs and CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells, and significantly increased the levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2. It treats 143B mouse subcutaneous OS model through three modes, photothermal therapy (PTT), chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and immunotherapy, which allowed 90 % of the mice to survive for more than 100 days, whereas all mice in the control group died within 42 days. The NPs also have excellent fluorescence imaging capabilities and can be used as tumor nano-contrast agents. In another study, a multi-synergistic environmentally responsive nanoparticle combining gold nanoparticles, copper peroxide, and borosilicate bioactive glass was designed. In this nanoparticle copper peroxide can be decomposed into oxygen and copper ions, whereas the oxygen can be used by gold nanoparticles to oxidize glucose in the TME to gluconic acid and H2O2, which not only leads to an undersupply of energy to the bone tumor cells but also provides the raw material for the copper ions to undergo a Fenton-like reaction, generating large amounts of ROS while inhibiting mitochondrial autophagy in bone tumor cells. These changes trigger intense oxidative stress and ICD, promote DC maturation and M1-type macrophage polarization, and increase the number of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cells, which significantly inhibits 143B mice model development. After eliminating tumors, these nanoparticles also promote osteogenesis and accelerate bone repair [106].

Unlike other metallic nanomaterials, calcium and magnesium enable immunotherapy through cytokine therapy and promote immune cell activation respectively. Calcium can regulate pH in humans, while calcium carbonate helps maintain acid-base balance in the body and can also be used to regulate the TME. Gao et al. [107] developed the nanosystem A-NPs@(SHK + Ce6) based on CaCO3/PDA. This nanosystem can target the bone tumor region and allow the combined treatment of PDT and immunotherapy, reversing the TME via the neutralization of H+ and the inhibition of lactic acid production, raising the pH from 6.8 to 7.4, increasing the IL-1β and TNF-α contents, decreasing the TGF-β levels, inducing M1-type macrophage polarization, improving the percentage of CD8+ T-cells to 40 %, and inhibiting Tregs activation. These changes resulted in an 81.8 % inhibition rate in the K7M2 in situ mouse model, with a median survival of more than 60 days compared to the control of 31 days, and a significant increase in survival time to more than 180 days after combination with PD-1 blockade, with long-term T-cell immune memory function. In contrast, magnesium is not only an important trace element but also an essential element for leukocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) to activate the immune function of CD8+ T-cells [108]. The metabolic activity of tumors decreases the magnesium concentration in the TME, which suppresses immune activity. A study reported that a composite nanohydrogel simultaneously delivering Mg2+, a hedgehog pathway antagonist, vismodegib, and the anti-PD-L1 antibody, which released biologically active Mg2+ over a long period, increased the ratio of CD8+/CD4+ T-cells to 0.33, and inhibited 90.2 % of the K7M2 tibia model of a mouse in which 90 % of its bone tumor tissue was surgically resected. The median survival increased from 31 days to 42 days, and the expression of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), an osteogenic marker that promotes the osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs and facilitates bone regeneration [109].

Along with their involvement in immunotherapy, metallic nanomaterials can be used to label immune cells and assess the therapeutic effects of immunotherapy. A study used a special microfluidic device to mechanically perforate CAR-T cells capable of targeting CD276, which is highly expressed in OS, and labeled them with iron oxide nanoparticles. This study found that the activity of the treated CAR-T cells was unaffected, cell proliferation was promoted through intracellular IL-2 signaling, and the inhibitory effect on MG63.3 cells was slightly enhanced in vitro. No significant difference was found in the ability to release IFN-γ and clear tumor cells in the OS-bearing mouse model. The labeled CAR-T cells can be effectively detected and localized using various imaging techniques, which provides an important assay for evaluating the effect of CAR-T-cell therapy on primary bone tumors and paves the way for its wider application in primary bone tumor treatment [110].

Metal nanomaterials not only exhibit excellent direct clearance effects on primary bone tumors but also possess dual imaging capabilities. A microsphere nanoparticle primarily composed of iron oxide can deliver various drugs such as immunomodulators while simultaneously enhancing imaging contrast, enabling real-time monitoring of immune responses [111].

The toxicity of metal particles has long been a subject of concern. High doses of metal particles can disrupt normal physiological metabolic processes through multiple mechanisms, exhibiting both short-term and long-term toxicity, which is an aspect that remains significantly understudied in current research. Generally speaking, metallic nanomaterials are the most widely used, richly characterized, and well-researched class of inorganic nanomaterials, and their mechanisms of action are also abundant, including the three major aspects of acting on bone tumor cells, TMEs, and immune cells. Each metal nanomaterial has a unique site of action, and some can assist in postoperative bone repair. Besides the metals mentioned above, other nanoparticles, such as platinum, silver, and gold, also have immunotherapeutic prospects and may be used in the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors. Additionally, metal nanomaterials are well suited for combination with other materials, thus optimizing performance, and have a key position in combination therapy, especially in linking phototherapy with immunotherapy, which is widely favored by researchers. Beyond participating in immunotherapy for primary bone tumors, metallic nanomaterials can also be used to repair bone defects caused by the tumors. Customized metallic prostheses with appropriate pore sizes facilitate muscle ingrowth and attachment while offering high strength and stability [112].

Non-metallic inorganic nanomaterials

Compared to those of organic and metallic nanomaterials, the utilization and design of non-metallic inorganic nanomaterials in primary bone tumor immunotherapy are severely lacking; however, there are still some studies have reported their potential for application in this direction for future development and experimentation. Non-metallic inorganic nanomaterials, mainly carbon nanotubes and silica, are viable options for developing bone tumor vaccines.

Carbon is the most vital element for organisms, and carbon nanomaterials are equally essential in tumor therapy. Certain carbon nanomaterials promote M2-type polarization of macrophages and induce the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, which are beneficial for post-treatment recovery processes, such as angiogenesis and bone repair, which also limit their application in immunotherapy in turn [113,114]. In contrast, some progress has been made in the study of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which are hollow tubular materials formed by a networked arrangement of carbon atoms with an off-domain π-electron cloud and a high specific surface area, making them ideal carriers for transporting drugs and antigens. The use of carboxylated multiwalled CNTs with special surface areas and defects to deliver OVA can enter macrophages within 6 h, resulting in a considerable increase in IL-6 and TNF-α levels, MHC II expression, and CD4+ T-cell proliferation with a duration of action of up to five days, which are ideal platforms for the design of peptide vaccines for bone tumors (Fig. 4a and b) [115].

Another non-metallic inorganic nanomaterial that is widely used in biomedical applications is silicon nanomaterial because of its large specific surface area, high strength, high biocompatibility, and special mechanical, optical, and biological properties [116]. Silica nanoparticles have highly variable sizes, high stability, and high versatility, which favors their use in developing tumor vaccines. An extracellular nanomatrix consisting of silica nanozigzags (NZ) with a specific spacing and number of spacers was found to induce the maturation of DCs by mechanically activating the focal adhesion kinase-induced upregulation of DC maturation markers, such as CD86, XCR1, and DC-SIGN, and an increase in the expression of CCR7, suggesting that the DCs were homing to the lymph nodes (Fig. 4c). These NZ-induced mature bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) promotes IL-12, IFN-γ, and TNF-α expression, which activates CTLs and causes them to proliferate in large numbers, producing memory CTLs, which are more effective than BMDCs whose maturation is promoted by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Fig. 4d), and is a reliable choice for the development of DC vaccines for bone tumors [117].

Inorganic nanomaterials for the immunotherapy of primary bone tumors have been applied in a limited way. First, their ability to activate the immune response is suboptimal, and their main mechanism for treating primary bone tumors is not immunotherapy but phototherapy. These nanomaterials include the graphene family [118], carbon dots [119], and silicene [120]. Second, as inorganic nanomaterials can enhance bone regeneration and angiogenesis by regulating the immune microenvironment, they are conducive to bone reconstruction aftertumor elimination, but this will inhibit immune activity, which is detrimental to the immune response, limits the application of inorganic nanomaterials in primary bone tumor immunotherapy.

Hybrid nanomaterials

Hybrid nanomaterials contain organic groups and inorganic groups and have the characteristics of both materials. The most commonly applied hybrid nanomaterial in primary bone tumor immunotherapy is the metal-organic framework (MOF), a porous crystalline structure formed by connecting metal ions or metal clusters with negatively charged organic ligands. They have the advantages of a large surface area, good adjustability, high porosity, easy surface functionalization, easy degradation, and ease of substance exchange [121].

Zeolitic imidazolate framework 8 (ZIF-8), a crystalline material with a three-dimensional porous structure created by the self-assembly of Zn2+, 2-methylimidazole, and other organic ligands through ligand bonding, promotes ALP expression, stimulates the generation and proliferation of osteoblasts, and accelerates bone regeneration. The roles of ZIF-8 include ICD, reversal of immunosuppression, and induction of cellular autophagy. Ma et al. [122] developed a 3D titanium scaffold integrated with ZIF-8 loaded with DOX and IDO inhibitors (TZDI), which was highly exothermic under microwave irradiation and caused ICD in 60 % of K7M2 cells within 24 h. It significantly increased HMGB1 and CRT expression, activated BMDCs, promoted IFN-γ release, increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and dramatically reduce tumor size in a mouse in situ femoral OS model through heat therapy, chemotherapy, and ICD (Fig. 5a and b). Besides, loading gemcitabine and D-1-methyltryptophan with hyaluronic acid-modified ZIF-8 (ZIF-8@Gem/D-1-MT) interfered with DNA replication in bone tumor cells, inhibited IDO activity and MDSC function, reversed immunosuppression, increased the infiltrating proportion of Th cells and CTLs, upregulated TNF-α and IL-2 levels, blocked the cell cycle of K7M2 and MG63 cells and raised their apoptosis rates to about 70 % and 50 % respectively, and inhibited the metastasis and invasion of OS, ultimately leading to the disappearance of the tumor (Fig. 5c and d) [123]. Ge et al. [124] employed the biocompatible bone tumor-targeting agent PEG-FA and the novel ZIF-8 to deliver curcumin and PD-1 blocker (CBZP), which exhibits pH sensitivity and can spontaneously slowly release curcumin in the TME, induce autophagy in bone tumor cells, and improve the exposure of CRT and the release of HMGB1. These changes can increase IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α secretion, promote DC maturation, increase CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell proliferation and infiltration, decrease the number of Tregs, and significantly reduce the tumor size of the K7M2 mouse model compared to the other groups, which increases the survival rate, and improves long-term immune function (Fig. 5e and f).

It can be seen that MOFs hold the characteristics from both organic and inorganic substances, endowing them with many unique advantages, the most prominent of which is the variety of immunotherapeutic mechanisms, which not only make them suitable for diverse therapeutic purposes with broad applications but also allow various therapeutic pathways to work together to significantly improve therapeutic efficacy and maximize the inhibition oftumor development. Additionally, its framework structure is conducive to drug transport, which further enriches the mechanism of action of MOFs in the treatment of primary bone tumors. MOFs can also meet the needs of certain therapeutic techniques when used along with other substances for surface modification. Additionally, numerous studies have demonstrated the potential of MOFs for delivering tumor vaccines, which could be utilized in the future to develop therapies for primary bone tumors [125]. However, the synthesis of MOFs is more complicated than that of other nanomaterials, and the in vivo degradation pathways of organic ligands and inorganic ions are not clear, which limits the application of MOFs.

Summary and outlook

The findings of this review are as follows. First, immunotherapy for primary bone tumors has diverse mechanisms of action and is regarded as a breakthrough treatment tool, but it also has certain limitations, including the suppression of the immune microenvironment and insensitivity to immunotherapy. Therefore, the effect of immunotherapy alone in treating primary bone tumors is unsatisfactory in some patients, and a common strategy to enhance the therapeutic effect involves combining multiple immunotherapies together or combining immunotherapies with other therapies.

Second, the development of nanomaterials for immunotherapy treatment of primary bone tumors is limited compared to that of other tumor diseases. The relevant experimental studies and clinical trials are not sufficient, especially for the development and testing of inorganic nanomaterials. Under the most common circumstances, the immune pathway is not utilized, although the inorganic nanomaterials are involved in primary bone tumor therapy. For example, graphene oxide nanomaterials can effectively kill DOX-resistant MG63 cells via PTT and PDT [126]. Several organic nanomaterials are also similar, such as exosomes from MSCs can be used to regulate signaling pathways related to primary bone tumor angiogenesis, and they can also target and kill OS cells after loading DOX [127]; Self-assembled polydopamine on calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite scaffolds can load DOX, triggering synergistic chemotherapeutic photothermal therapy against HOS cells upon exposure to NIR light and acidic TME conditions while simultaneously promoting bone regeneration [128]. In addition, some immunotherapeutic functions have also been validated in other types of cancer, including melanoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer, but have not yet been applied in the field of primary bone tumors. Such strategies include PEGylated gold nanoparticles transporting CpG, which can stimulate the maturation of BMDCs, activate T cells, and inhibit the progression of a mouse melanoma model effectively through ACT [129]; DNA vaccines prepared from polyethyleneimine have been used in various tumor models, such as B-cell lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma, and melanoma, all of which produce potent immune responses and have extremely rich mechanisms of action [130]; Hollow black TiO2 nanospheres modified with gold, carbon dots, and hyaluronic acid can reverse the TME via M1-type repolarization of M2-type macrophages, promote the maturation of DCS and the infiltration of effector T cells, activating immune reactions against mammary tumors [131]. Besides, some therapeutic agents have been predicted by preliminary experiments in vivo or in vitro to have promising applications in primary bone tumor immunotherapy, but further experimental validation is lacking; these include CNTs and silica, which were described in this article. Although the development of primary bone tumor immunotherapy has shortcomings, a large amount of information is available, and the prospects for development are really bright.

Third, although nanomaterials have many good properties and immunotherapy has led to the development of relatively complete treatment protocols, the two are not closely integrated. Nanomaterials often function only in transporting drugs, and the corresponding immunotherapeutic mechanisms only involve regulating the TME and inducing ICD. Researchers should continue giving importance to the special properties of nanomaterials so that they can play a unique role in future studies on primary bone tumor immunotherapy.

Finally, nanomaterials in practical applications cannot be simply categorized into one type; many nanotherapeutic formulations are composed of multiple nanomaterials together, such as polymeric materials are often modified by metal particles; hydrogels, and natural cell membranes commonly used to encapsulate other drug-carrying particles (fusion cell membrane-encapsulated nanoparticle technology) [132]. Therapeutic nanoparticles composed of different kinds of nanomaterials have several advantages that may improve the efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

We propose developing a novel porous hybrid biodegradable nanomaterial containing both organic ligands and inorganic molecules. Organic groups such as RGD peptides and lipids enable high targeting and penetration into bone tumor tissues, while inorganic molecules like iron and copper achieve multiple functions including killingtumor cells and imaging. When functioning as a drug carrier, it should exhibit high bone tissue permeability, enable sustained drug release, and degrade naturally. When acting as the therapeutic agent itself, it must ensure complete tumor clearance by targeting multiple signaling pathways. Furthermore, the nanomaterial design can incorporate AI-assisted personalized design, with novel methods like 3D printing employed to construct the ideal nanodrug formulation [133]. This article systematically describes the available information on primary bone tumors and immunotherapy, as well as the functional mechanism underlying primary bone tumor immunotherapy. It also discusses the specific applications of nanomaterials in the context of primary bone tumor immunotherapy. Immunotherapy is a method of treating tumors that has gained widespread attention from researchers in recent years. It has enormous development potential and therapeutic prospects, and the application of nanomaterials is the key to improving the effects of immunotherapy and making qualitative changes in immunotherapy. Therefore, the development of nanomaterials that can be used in primary bone tumor immunotherapy is highly important and urgently needed. In the future, researchers should continue focusing on this topic and further promote the development of primary bone tumor immunotherapy.
