Efficacy of anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament for pain control in abdominal surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hanwei Gong, Zichen Zheng, Li Xu, Zifei Wang, Guangrui Kou, Qianwei Sun, Mengxing Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA critical component of postoperative abdominal surgery recovery is the efficacy of analgesia administered postsurgery. In 2020, a novel approach to the quadratus lumborum block (QLB) was proposed: anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament (QLB-LSAL). A hypothesis indicating that the performance of the block would provide superior analgesia for abdominal surgery was developed. The primary aim of this meta-analysis was to assess the efficacy of the QLB-LSAL in reducing postoperative opioid use. The secondary objective was to assess the effects of this block on postoperative pain-related indicators, including pain scores, time to first patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) activation, and the occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).MethodsA comprehensive search of relevant databases, including PubMed, the Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science, and other relevant sources, was conducted. Studies were screened for broad eligibility by title and abstract, followed by full-text screening. Data were extracted from the included studies and then analyzed via Review Manager software (version 5.4) and STATA (version 18.0 MP-Parallel Edition).ResultsA total of eight studies involving 596 participants were included in the analysis. The QLB-LSAL group demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in postoperative opioid consumption within the first 24 h (mean difference [MD] = − 10.88, 95% confidence interval [CI]: − 12.37 to − 9.39, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0). Additionally, reduced pain scores at rest were observed at various time points postoperatively. The incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in the QLB-LSAL group was significantly lower than that in the control group (risk ratio [RR] = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.69, P < 0.0001, I2 = 41%). Additionally, the QLB-LSAL group exhibited a reduced need for postoperative rescue analgesia (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.96, P = 0.03; I2 = 0).ConclusionsThe anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament significantly provides less 24-h postoperative opioid consumption after abdominal surgery, prolongs the time to first patient-controlled analgesia activation, and lowers the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting.Trial registrationThe protocol of this study was registered with PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD42024614871).Keywords: Quadratus lumborum block, Supra-arcuate ligament, Abdominal surgery, Pain, Opioids

Background

The quadratus lumborum block (QLB) was first presented by Blanco in 2007 as a novel technique based on the transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) [1]. The QLB has been shown to offer several advantages over other techniques, including a longer duration of analgesia and more effective attenuation of visceral pain. The anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament (QLB-LSAL), initially proposed in 2020, involves the injection of medication between the diaphragm and quadratus lumborum above the lateral arcuate ligament, thereby facilitating the entry of the local anesthetic into the lower thoracic paravertebral space [2]. This method is distinguished by its rapid onset, prolonged duration of action, and minimal impact on hemodynamics.

The extant literature indicates that unilateral QLB-LSAL can be used for anesthesia and postoperative analgesia in lateral abdominal and retroperitoneal organ surgeries (e.g., nephrectomy), whereas bilateral QLB-LSAL can provide intraoperative and postoperative analgesia for total abdominal surgeries (e.g., radical resection of rectal cancer, hysterectomy, etc.) [3]. However, the absence of high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) has led to a lack of sufficient data to support its widespread utilization in abdominal surgery. Consequently, many RCTs investigating the use of QLB-LSAL in abdominal surgery have been published recently. These trials contribute to further validation of the safety and efficacy of QLB-LSAL in abdominal surgery.

The present systematic review was conducted with the objective of collating evidence from recent RCTs on the efficacy of QLB-LSAL for postoperative analgesia in adult patients undergoing total abdominal surgery in comparison with a control group receiving no block or another block. The primary aim of the present study was to assess the efficacy of QLB-LSAL in reducing postoperative opioid use. Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment included pain scores both at rest and during exercise, the time to first patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) activation, the utilization of salvage analgesia, and the occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) in both groups as secondary outcomes.

Methods

This meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines [4]. The protocol was registered with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42024614871) prior to the initiation of the study. All analyses are based on previous studies; therefore, no ethical approval was needed.

Search strategy

Owing to the intervention method, the anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament cannot be searched using medical subject headings (MeSH), so we conducted the search using"quadratus lumborum block"and"supra-arcuate ligament"as keywords. Two independent investigators (Gong and Zheng) searched various databases, including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Web of Science, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov, from their respective inception dates to 31 October 2024. To mitigate the risk of publication bias, the reference lists of the studies were also searched, and only RCTs were selected as potential target studies.

Study selection criteria

Two independent reviewers (Gong and Zheng) selected potentially suitable studies on the basis of predetermined inclusion criteria. The identified studies were deemed appropriate for inclusion in the current meta-analysis by two reviewers independently.

The selection criteria for the studies included in our meta-analysis were as follows: First, patients who underwent abdominal surgery under general anesthesia were included. Second, eligible studies had to specify the following intervention criteria: (1) patients with a single-sided abdominal incision received ipsilateral QLB-LSAL; and (2) patients with midline or bilateral incisions received bilateral QLB-LSAL. Third, eligible studies must have included at least two of the following outcome measures: (1) postoperative opioid consumption; (2) pain scores at rest and during movement exercises at multiple postoperative time points; (3) incidence of rescue analgesia; (4) time to first PCA pump press; and (5) occurrence of postoperative adverse events (e.g., nausea and vomiting). Finally, the study must have been considered to be an RCT.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted from each study by two reviewers (Gong and Zheng) using a preestablished data extraction protocol. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved via consultation with a third reviewer (Xu). The data extracted included bibliographic information (author, year, PubMed ID), study design (description of control and intervention), pain-related outcomes (opioid consumption within the first 24 h postoperatively, pain scores at rest and during activity at various time points, time to first PCA press, incidence of rescue analgesia), and other outcome measures (incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and other adverse events). The use of opioids within the first 24 h following surgery was the main outcome of this systematic review; other outcome measures were regarded as secondary.

In this meta-analysis, the means and standard deviations (SDs) were extracted for continuous or ordinal variables (e.g., opioid consumption, time to first PCA press), and the counts (incidence rates) were extracted for dichotomous variables (e.g., postoperative nausea and vomiting, rescue analgesia). The findings of the studies by Hozo [5] and Luo [6] suggest that the numerical values expressed as medians and interquartile ranges can be statistically converted to means and standard deviations. The conversion to intravenous morphine milligram equivalents was facilitated by the use of standard conversion tables [7]. The included studies assessed pain scores at rest or during activity at various time points (6, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively) using the visual analog scale (VAS) or the numerical rating scale (NRS). Notably, the VAS and NRS employed for assessment are recognized as being equivalent [8].

Assessment of methodological quality

The quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two blinded reviewers (Wang and Kou) on the basis of the Cochrane Collaboration's tool for assessing the risk of bias [9]. A"risk of bias"table was created, encompassing the following domains: method of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias details. The overall quality of each study was then categorized as having a"low,"","or"unclear"risk of bias. The modified Jadad 7-point scale was utilized to evaluate the quality of the RCTs. Studies with a quality score of less than 4 were categorized as low-quality articles [10]. The overall quality of evidence in the meta-analysis was then graded according to the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were performed using Review Manager software (version 5.4) and STATA (version 18.0 MP-Parallel Edition). For continuous variables, such as opioid consumption within the first 24 h postoperatively, pain scores, and time to the first PCA pump press, the mean difference (MD) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. For dichotomous variables, such as the incidence of rescue analgesia and postoperative nausea and vomiting, the risk ratio (RR) was calculated with a 95% confidence interval (CI) using the Mantel‒Haenszel method. The selection of either a random-effects model or a fixed-effects model for the consolidation of data was made on the basis of the size of the I2 statistic.

The statistical heterogeneity of each analysis was assessed using Cochran's Q test and I2 statistics. An I2 value of 50% or more was considered to indicate significant heterogeneity [11]. In the meta-analytic approach adopted in this study, if the I2 statistic was ≥ 50%, a random-effects model was employed, accompanied by a sensitivity analysis to identify and exclude studies contributing to heterogeneity and a further examination of the underlying causes. Conversely, if the I2 statistic was < 50%, a fixed-effects model was selected for the analysis.

A funnel plot was constructed for the primary outcome measure. The symmetry of the funnel plot was then tested to assess potential publication bias [12]. The symmetry of the funnel plot was evaluated using Egger's test to determine whether publication bias was present. Subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of pain scores at different time points.

Identification of studies

The preliminary literature search yielded a total of 85 potential journal articles and abstracts. Following a thorough review of these studies and the exclusion of duplicates, eight studies [13–20] were selected for inclusion in this systematic review.

Opioid consumption in the first 24 h after surgery

Five studies [13, 14, 17, 18, 20] included in this review assessed the total consumption of opioids within the first 24 h after surgery. The forest plot of the pooled analysis of opioid consumption in the QLB-LSAL and control groups in these studies is shown in Fig. 3A.

A Forest plot of total opioid consumption within the first 24 h post-surgery. B Sensitivity analysis forest plot derived from five studies. C Forest plot of total opioid consumption within the first 24 h post-surgery.(excluding the study by Wang). D Funnel plot of total opioid consumption within the first 24 h post-surgery

The five studies included in the analysis exhibited significant heterogeneity upon testing, with an I2 value of > 50% and a Q test P value of < 0.1, suggesting a high level of heterogeneity among the studies. A sensitivity analysis was conducted, the results of which are shown in Fig. 3B. The sensitivity analysis indicated that the study by Wang [20] exerted a substantial influence on the overall findings.

Following the exclusion of Wang's study, a re-examination of the heterogeneity was conducted, yielding an I2 value of 0 and a Q test P value of 0.65 > 0.1, indicating an absence of significant heterogeneity among the remaining studies. Consequently, a fixed-effects model was selected for data pooling. Compared with the control, QLB-LSAL significantly reduced opioid consumption in the first 24 h after surgery (mean difference [MD] = − 10.88, 95% confidence interval [CI], − 12.37 − 9.39, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3C).

As the primary outcome measure, a funnel plot was constructed to assess the potential for publication bias, as illustrated in Fig. 3D. The plot was symmetrical (with Egger's test yielding a P value of 0.267 > 0.05), thereby indicating an absence of evidence to suggest publication bias and the accuracy and reliability of the conclusions of this study.

Pain scores at rest or during movement

Five studies [13, 14, 16, 18, 20] evaluated pain scores at rest and during activity at various time points postoperatively. Subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of pain scores at different time points after surgery. The forest plot of the pooled analysis of pain scores at rest and during activity for the QLB-LSAL and control groups at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively is presented in Fig. 4.

Forest plot of pain scores at various time points post-surgery

Compared with the control group, the QLB-LSAL group presented statistically significant differences in the NRS/VAS pain scores at rest at 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively and during movement at 6 h postoperatively. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in the pain scores at rest or during movement for the remaining time periods.

The NRS/VAS scores at rest at 6 h (MD = − 0.79, 95% CI: − 1.51 to − 0.07, P = 0.03, I2 = 90%), 12 h (MD = − 0.75, 95% CI: − 1.47 to − 0.03, P = 0.04, I2 = 95%), and 24 h (MD = − 0.59, 95% CI: − 1.17 to − 0.02, P = 0.04, I2 = 91%). The NRS/VAS score at movement at 6 h (MD = − 0.47, 95% CI: − 0.89 to − 0.04, P = 0.03, I2 = 55%).

Considering the subjective nature of pain score assessments, where the rating of each patient is influenced by their individual perception, this can be a contributing factor to the observed heterogeneity. The efforts did not yield a reduction in heterogeneity despite the attempts to use the sequential exclusion process. With respect to these findings, a random-effects model was employed for the analysis of the data.

Time to first PCA activation

Four studies [14, 16, 18, 19] assessed the time to first PCA activation postoperatively. The results demonstrated that the QLB-LSAL group presented a significantly prolonged time to first PCA activation (MD = 5.10, 95% CI: 4.22 to 5.98, P < 0.00001, I2 = 68%). The corresponding forest plot is presented in Fig. 5.

Forest plot of the time to first patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) activation post-surgery

Given that the observed heterogeneity falls within an acceptable range, a random-effects model was selected for the analysis of the data. These findings indicate that QLB-LSAL can prolong the time required for the first demand of PCA postoperatively.

PONV incidence and rescue analgesia incidence

A total of seven studies [13, 14, 16–20] investigated the occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, whereas four studies [15, 16, 18, 20] examined the issue of postoperative rescue analgesia. The forest plots corresponding to these studies can be found in Fig. 6A. and 6B.

A Forest plot of the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting. B Forest plot of the incidence of postoperative rescue analgesia

The occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in the QLB-LSAL group was considerably lower than that in the control group (RR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.69, P < 0.0001, I2 = 41%). Additionally, the requirement for postoperative rescue analgesia in the QLB-LSAL group was lower (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.96; P = 0.03, I2 = 0).

The I2 value was 41% (< 50%) for the incidence of nausea and vomiting and0 (indicating no heterogeneity) for the incidence of rescue analgesia. The Q test P values for both outcomes were greater than 0.1. Consequently, a fixed-effects model was selected for the data pooling in both cases.

Block-related complications

In the eight trials [13–20] under consideration, no reports of serious adverse events associated with nerve block were found. Such events may include local anesthetic toxicity, infection, and injury to the vasculature, among others.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, direct evidence from eight studies involving 596 patients was used to assess the postoperative analgesic effects of QLB-LSAL for abdominal surgery. Compared with no block or another block (conventional QLB or TAPB), QLB-LSAL demonstrated a significant advantage in reducing the total consumption of opioids within the first 24 h after surgery.

The importance of pain management in abdominal surgery has recently become increasingly prominent. The development of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concepts and ultrasound-guided visualization techniques has resulted in nerve blocks becoming an integral component of intraoperative and postoperative pain management [21]. QLB-LSAL, an extension of the traditional quadratus lumborum block (QLB), has been proposed as a means of injecting medication into the appositional area between the quadratus lumborum muscle and the diaphragm [22]. This study confirms the benefits of QLB-LSAL for abdominal surgical analgesia.

Our study demonstrates that QLB-LSAL significantly reduced 24-h postoperative opioid consumption. However, when comparing the cumulative equivalent morphine consumption between the groups, it is crucial to consider whether the observed difference in morphine consumption is of clinical significance. An oral-morphine difference of 30 mg [23] is considered the minimum clinically important difference; upon conversion to the intravenous equivalent, 10 mg of morphine is considered a significant clinical difference. The observed discrepancy in the equivalent morphine consumption between the QLB-LSAL group and the control group within 24 h postoperatively was 10.88 mg, indicating that the result is not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful.

QLB-LSAL has been demonstrated to reduce pain scores during rest periods following surgical intervention while concomitantly diminishing the prevalence of postoperative nausea and vomiting. With respect to pain scores, QLB-LSAL has been shown to result in reduced scores during rest at 6, 12, and 24 h postsurgery; however, these differences were not statistically significant. Pain scores are inherently more subjective, which may have contributed to the high degree of heterogeneity observed in the analysis. These variations are less objective than the differences in total opioid consumption, which are more robust in demonstrating disparities.

In the context of prolonging the time to the first demand of PCA and reducing the incidence of postoperative rescue analgesia, QLB-LSAL demonstrated significant advantages. With respect to postoperative nausea and vomiting, the incidence was significantly lower in the QLB-LSAL group, which may be associated with the reduced total opioid consumption in this group.

QLB-LSAL may provide superior postoperative analgesia, a hypothesis that is predicated on an understanding of its mechanism of action. The lateral arcuate ligament extends from the transverse process of the L1 vertebra to the middle of the T12 rib, forming the lower edge of the diaphragm along with the medial arcuate ligament. The psoas fascia and the anterior layer of the thoracolumbar fascia run cephalad together until they reach the lateral and medial arcuate ligaments, where they split into two layers. One of these layers persists as the lateral and medial arcuate ligaments, whereas the other becomes connected to the endothoracic fascia [24]. This anatomical characteristic reveals the presence of an appositional area between the quadratus lumborum and the diaphragm above the level of the lateral arcuate ligament. This appositional area is connected to the paravertebral space at the lower thoracic level, which may serve as a potential pathway for the spread of QLB medication across the arcuate ligament to the paravertebral space.

Dam et al. [25] conducted cadaver studies, and after anterior QLB injection with 30 ml of dye solution, dissection revealed paravertebral space diffusion in every block, reaching the T9 level, with the corresponding ventral branches of the thoracic sympathetic trunk and spinal nerves in the paravertebral space also being stained. In addition, Elsharawy et al. [26] reported that medication administered via both the posterior QLB and the subcostal approach can reach the thoracic paravertebral space, with medication administered via the subcostal approach reaching the T6 level. This finding reinforces the notion that local anesthetics entering the paravertebral space and exerting their effects constitute a significant mechanism of QLB. In the context of QLB-LSAL, the procedure involves direct passage of the needle over the arcuate ligament in the appositional area between the quadratus lumborum and the diaphragm for the administration of a local anesthetic. This approach facilitates the dissemination of the medication to the paravertebral space, thereby exerting its blocking effect.

The unique needle insertion method of QLB-LSAL necessitates a certain level of operator expertise, as there is a possibility of puncturing the pleura and kidney during the needle path. This potential complication underscores the importance of the involvement of experienced anesthesiologists. However, none of the included studies reported any serious complications or adverse events related to QLB-LSAL. Despite the demands of the nerve block skills of anesthesiologists, QLB-LSAL is a viable option for widespread adoption. Notably, it is not associated with the increased risks associated with paravertebral blocks.

Although QLB-LSAL has been increasingly adopted in clinical practice, the number of RCTs evaluating this technique remains limited. Systematic searches across multiple databases identified only 8 eligible studies. Although this meta-analysis suggested potential advantages of QLB-LSAL in abdominal surgery, the generalizability of its conclusions was constrained by the following critical limitations. (1) Sparse evidence and heterogeneous patient populations: the limited number of RCTs enrolled diverse abdominal surgical subtypes, resulting in a small overall sample size that lacked sufficient statistical power to support universal conclusions. (2) Variable control group designs: several studies did not employ any analgesic control group, whereas others utilized alternative regional block techniques (e.g., TAPB) or varied local anesthetic agents/doses, potentially introducing methodological bias and compromising interstudy comparability.

Additional limitations of this study include the following: (1) significant heterogeneity was observed in certain outcome measures, which may exacerbate publication bias given the limited RCT pool; (2) continuous outcomes (e.g., opioid consumption, pain scores) required conversion from nonnormal distribution metrics to means and standard deviations (SDs), introducing indirect measurement artifacts despite standardized analytical protocols; and (3) the absence of any study assessing the efficacy of the nerve block [27] could introduce bias due to unsuccessful blocks.

Owing to the differences in the data reported across various literature sources, it was not possible to assess a greater number of outcome indicators (such as patient satisfaction and PACU discharge time). To definitively ascertain the efficacy and safety profile of QLB-LSAL in abdominal surgery, future investigations should prioritize the execution of rigorous multicenter, double-blind, and triple-blind (where feasible) RCTs.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament leads to a substantial reduction in opioid consumption 24 h after abdominal surgery. This reduction is not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. The block also effectively prolonged the time to first patient-controlled analgesia activation and reduced the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting.
