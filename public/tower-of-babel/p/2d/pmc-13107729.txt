The effect of the prone and side lying position by using the positioning bed on the comfort of the preterm baby
Authors: Zerrin Cigdem, Funda Durdu, Melike Yavas Celık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe study was carried out experimentally to determine the effect of prone and side-lying position on the comfort of preterm infants using positioning bed in preterm infants hospitalized in Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU).MethodThe sample of the study consisted of 33 preterm babies. Research data were collected with the Baby Information Form, Newborn Follow-up Form, Neonatal Position Assessment Tool (NPAT) and Premature Infant Comfort Scale (PICS) developed by the researchers.ResultsThere was a significant difference between pre-test and first hour scores and between pre-test and second hour scores in NPAT scores of preterms in prone position.ConclusionIn conclusion, it is thought that the position bed used affects comfort more than the baby’s position in this study. For increasing the comfort of the preterm baby and support its development it is recommended to use a position bed when positioning the baby and that the position to be given is primarily prone position.

Introduction

A baby born before completing the 37th week (259 days) of pregnancy is called a preterm baby. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that preterm birth rates in the world vary between 5-18% according to countries and regions, and the annual number of preterm births is estimated to be 15 million [1, 2].

Preterm babies require technologically advanced medical interventions. In order to survive and continue its healthy development, it needs the support of a team of specialized healthcare professionals. Their lack of regulatory behavior, inability to act in a purposeful manner, and lack of communication skills make them even more vulnerable. The extrauterine environment for a premature baby is the environment in which the baby’s energy and physiological balance for its integrity is disrupted and its social, sensory and behavioral development is negatively affected. These threats to the baby cause short and long-term consequences on the baby’s vital signs, pain score, oxygen need, weight gain, and the development of the nervous, muscular, skeletal and sensory systems. In the adaptation of these babies to extrauterine life, a healing environment should be created in which environmental factors are regulated, care needs are handled baby-centered and implemented in a way that supports the baby’s development [3–5].

Individualized Supportive Developmental Care (ISDC) practices are based on Synactive Theory. ISDC’s Risky newborn has 5 subsystems: state regulation system, motor system, autonomic/physiological system, attention-interaction system and self-regulation system, which are constantly interacting with each other and also with the environment in contact with the baby. ISDC, which is applied in the NICU, aims to organize the care environment and the care provided in a way that supports the development of the risky newborn, taking into account the gestational age and adaptation status of the newborn. ISDC approaches consist of family-centered care, developmental supportive activities (positioning, group care practice, facilitating self-calming), creating a healing environment and ensuring sleep-wake routine [4–6]. Motor skill development, one of the important milestones of a baby’s life, is a process that develops from head to toe. The earliest activity a baby should do is to maintain a stable posture against the influence of gravity. Postural development is affected not only by gravity but also by sensory systems.

Developmental positioning directly affects the amount of energy a baby expends. The baby placed in the appropriate position cries less and shows signs of stress, and is protected from the development of posture disorders, intraventricular bleeding and chronic pain. Additionally, the baby’s neurodevelopmental competence increases. Supportive positioning of the preterm baby in the NICU, which improves stable posture, is considered a key element of care. However, there is no defined and standardized form of optimal positioning practices [5, 7, 8].

The Neonatal Position Assessment Tool (NPAT) as an educational tool to assess positioning was developed by Coughlin et al. between 2007 and 2010 [9]. to evaluate the supportive position practices applied in the NICU. Scale Kahraman et al. It was adapted into Turkish by [10]. A study reported that NPAT is an effective tool in evaluating positioning, and that head, neck and hand positioning is best evaluated [11, 12]. The main purpose of position management in NICU is; The aim of the treatment/care interventions applied to babies is to minimize the pain/suffering caused by stressors originating from the environment, to make it easier for them to cope with stress by protecting their posture, and to increase preterm comfort [13]. This study was planned experimentally to determine the effect of prone and side-lying positions using a positioning bed on the comfort of the preterm baby.

Method

Place, type and time of research

It was planned experimentally to examine the effect of prone and side lying positions using a positioning bed on the comfort of the preterm baby. The crossover design was used in the study. The research was carried out in the NICU of a Research and Application Hospital between 01.12.2017 and 01.07.2018.

Washout period

A sweeping technique was used between the two methods applied in the study. Therefore, to prevent any interaction between the two techniques, the baby was allowed to rest for a period of time (3 h) after each position, and routine care (feeding, diaper changing, etc.) was performed.

Ethical aspect of research

Ethics committee permission was obtained from the Non-Interventional Medical Ethics Committee of a University subject to the research (date: 28.11.2017, protocol number: 2017/12). Permission was obtained from the institution where the research was conducted. Written consent was obtained from the parents of the babies included in the study. The research was conducted in accordance with the Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Population and sample of the research

The sample size for the G*Power analysis was calculated using the G*Power program. The first error type was 0.05, the Cohen’s effect size was 0.3, and the sample size was 33. Based on these inputs, the calculated power was found to be 95%. According to this calculation, 33 babies were included in the sample.

Equipment used in collecting research data

Research data were collected with the Baby Information Form, Newborn Follow-up Form, Neonatal Position Assessment Tool (NPAT) and Premature Infant Comfort Scale (PICS) developed by the researchers.

Baby information form

It consisted of a total of 9 questions, including the gender of the preterm baby, the method of birth, the baby’s gestational age, birth weight, birth head circumference, birth length, Apgar score, diagnosis, and nutritional status.

Newborn follow-up form

It is a form developed by researchers, in which NPAT and PICS scores are recorded at the moment the preterm baby is positioned and at the 1st and 2nd hours.

Neonatal Position Assessment Tool (NPAT)

NPAT includes a total of 6 items (shoulders, hands, hips, knees-ankles-feet, head and neck) and each item is evaluated in the range of 0–2 points (0-1-2). The lowest score from the scale is 0 and the highest score is 12. A high score on the scale is considered an indicator of correct positioning and the total score is desired to be between 10–12.

A total score of 8 or less from NPAT reveals the necessity of ensuring the baby’s flexion posture and implementing supportive positioning interventions. The application time of the tool varies between 2 and 5 min, and the interobserver reliability coefficient is announced as 0.90 [9, 10].

Premature Infant Comfort Scale (PICS)

It is a scale developed by Monique et al. (63) in 2007 to evaluate pain and distress for babies between 28 and 37 weeks. It is a multidimensional scale; A total of 7 parameters that evaluate behavioral (Alertness, Calmness/Agitation, Crying - evaluated only in babies with spontaneous breathing -, Respiratory status - evaluated only in mechanical ventilation support -, Physical movements, Muscle tone, Facial movements) and physiological (Average heart rate) status. is formed. Each parameter was rated on a 5-point Likert type, ranging from 1 to 5 points. The baby’s comfort is evaluated based on the total score obtained from the scale, and a low score indicates that the baby’s comfort level is high. The cut-off score of PICS is ≥ 17, the highest score is 35 and the lowest score is 7. The cut-off score is considered the limit value for the baby’s comfort level and indicates that an attempt should be made to reduce pain/distress [13, 14]. The reliability of PBKS was examined by evaluating Cronbach’s alpha value in the pre-test and post-test and was declared as 0.76 and 0.86, respectively [14].

Pulse oximeter device and probe

In the study, “7 Pulse Oximeter’ monitor was used to determine the Peak Heart Rate of the preterm newborn during positioning.

Stopwatch

In the study, after the prone and side lying positions were given to the preterm baby using a positioning bed, NPAT and PICS scores were made at the 0th, 1st and 2nd hours, while a telephone chronometer was used to determine the times accurately.

Position bed

Tortoise Neo position bed imported by a private company was used in the research. The bed has been specially produced to position the newborn in a similar way to that in the womb, thus calming him down and ensuring the continuation of his neuromotor development. Bed; It protects the baby from posture disorders, breathing and swallowing difficulties, as well as deterioration of skin integrity, by allowing the baby to lie in a midline physiological flexion position within safe limits and to be able to move actively.

Application of research

Data in the study; It was obtained by applying the application in the 16 − 08 (night) shift in order to minimize the effect of environmental stimuli such as sound, light, noise and human density on preterm babies. The positioning bed used in the study was wiped with disinfectant solution (70% Ethyl alcohol, 5% Propane 2-ol, 0.10% Alkyl (C12-18) Dimethylbenzyl Ammonium Chloride) in accordance with the usage recommendations before and after each patient use, and the used cover was washed.

One of the most recommended positions in the NICU is the prone position. This position has been reported to have positive effects on sleep, feeding, residual urine, pain, and residual volüme [15, 16]. Also, the use of the prone position in the NICU is recommended to reduce cardiovascular events such as apnea, bradycardia, and hypoxemia and to support nutrition [17]. It has also been reported that the side-lying position, along with the prone position, is very important for premature babies in terms of weight gain, early discharge, and reducing the negative consequences of neonatal intensive care [18]. Therefore, these two positions were preferred in our practice. We started with the prone position and then tried side-lying positions.

In the study, babies were examined every 3 h, respectively; It was carried out in two stages by giving prone and side lying positions on the position bed. Routine nursing care (feeding, diaper change) was given to each baby before the application. First, the baby was placed in the prone position and 1-minute NPAT and PICS scores were given. This measurement in the first minute was considered as “pretest”. NPAT and PICS scores were given by observing the baby in the 1st and 2nd hours after the prone position was given. After scoring, the baby was given clinical routine care (feeding and diaper change), was given a side-lying position, and 1-minute PICS and NPAT scoring were performed. This measurement in the first minute was considered as “pretest”. The baby was observed in the side-lying position and evaluated with NPAT and PICS at the 1st and 2nd hours (Figs. 1 and 2). During this entire procedure, no interventions that could cause pain or suffering to the baby were performed.

All applications were carried out by the same researcher. In the lying position given using the position bed; The areas where the head, hips and knees would be placed were hollowed out by hand. The knees were kept in a partially flexed position. After the position was given, the bed was supported by hand from the sides and compressed inwards. Thus, the preterm baby was surrounded. In all positions given, care was taken to ensure that the head and body were in the same alignment on the midline and the hands were closed towards the face. The head of the bed is elevated 15–30 degrees. The neck was kept in slight flexion of less than 30 degrees.

Prone position

In this position, which is given with the use of a positioning bed, the head of the preterm baby is turned to the left or right side and the hands are placed on both sides of the head.

Side sleeping position

In this position, which was given with the use of a positioning bed, the extremities of the preterm baby were brought to the midline and the baby was laid on the right side. The hands were placed towards the face to perform a hand-mouth maneuver (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis of data

Study data consists of 33 preterm babies. The analyzes were completed by transferring the data to the IBM SPSS Statistics 21 package program. In the analyses, frequency distributions (n,%) were given for categorical variables (birth type, etc.) and descriptive statistics (mean, SD, minimum, maximum) were given for numerical variables (birth length, etc.). The normality assumptions of the numerical variables to be tested statistically were examined with the Shapiro Wilk test and it was seen that the variables were normally distributed (p < 0.05). Therefore, non-parametric statistical method was used in the study. Differences between two dependent numerical variables were examined with Wilcoxon signed-rank analysis. In the analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Dicsussion

The purpose of giving the preterm baby a therapeutic position is; It facilitates coping with stress, reduces exposure to environmental stressors, prevents the development of posture disorders by maintaining the intrauterine position, protects against pain caused by treatment/care interventions, and accelerates physiological recovery and growth. In order to increase the effectiveness of the position, it is important to flex the baby in the midline position, pay attention to wrapping, ensure comfort and support its maintenance [4, 7–9]. Wrapping the baby; It is the baby’s desire to attach to a stable surface and is a strategy to cope with stress. It should not be restrictive/tight and should not hinder the baby’s movement, and should ensure the normal development of the musculoskeletal system. The best position in the NICU is; To position the neck in a slight flexion of less than 30 degrees, to ensure symmetrical physiological flexion, to support the hands closed towards the face, to ensure that the supported pelvis-trunk and the head, neck and trunk are in the same alignment [3, 8].

A balanced/appropriate lying position; It supports flexion, symmetry, hand-mouth maneuvers, increases oxygenation, reduces heat loss and prolongs the baby’s sleep time. Studies have shown that the prone position has many benefits for babies. In cases where the prone position cannot be given, it is recommended that the baby be placed in a side-lying position [19]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effect of prone and side-lying positions using a positioning bed on the comfort of preterm babies. Our first hypothesis for this purpose is that the baby’s comfort increases when he is placed in the prone position on the position bed. This hypothesis was confirmed as a result of our study. When preterm babies were placed face down on the position bed we used, their comfort changed and increased positively in the first and second hours. In their study, Grenier et al. [20] examined the effects of body positions on the baby’s self-regulatory system and stress behaviors in preterm babies; They evaluated a total of 6 positions: supported (prone, side lying, supine) and unsupported (prone, side lying, supine). Researchers stated that babies showed the lowest stress responses first in the supported prone position and then in the supported side-lying position. Çağlayan S [21]. examined the effects of prone and supine flexion positions given for two hours on the heart rate, oxygen saturation and pain levels of preterm babies in his single-group study. In the study, it was announced that heart rates increased significantly in the prone position and prone flexion positions in the 2nd hour, and average pain scores in the prone flexion position gradually decreased over time. Cândia et al. [22] in the study conducted to determine the changes in salivary cortisol concentration before and after prone positioning and to evaluate physiological and behavioral responses; It has been shown that prone positioning significantly reduces salivary cortisol level, respiratory rate and Brazelton sleep score. Studies have shown that placing the baby in the prone position has a positive effect on the baby.

Another hypothesis we put forward in our study is that infant comfort is expected to increase when placed on their side in a positioning bed. We observed a change and positive increase in comfort in premature infants placed on their sides in the positioning bed we used. In the study, it was assumed that the NTAP 1st and 2nd hour scores of babies in the side-lying position were equivalent. Side lying position is one of the preferred positions in newborns to prevent posture disorders and facilitate flexion, when the extremities remain in the midline [9]. It is recommended by the American Association of Pediatrics as the best option when the prone position is not/cannot be used. There are also studies suggesting that a supported side-lying position can positively affect the baby’s physiological and behavioral characteristics [20].

Limitations

The limitations of the study were that the study was not a randomized controlled trial, meaning it lacked a control group, the blinding method for the researcher collecting the data during the data collection phase could not be applied due to the type of study, and the selection of a sample from a wide age group (babies at 28–32 weeks of gestation were included. However, it is possible that the developmental differences of the babies (muscle tone, mobility, and neurological development) could affect the results of the study).

Conclusion

We can say that positioning in the NICU has an impact on newborn comfort levels, and that a positioning bed can support this positive effect. However, it is recommended that more robust studies be conducted to test the effectiveness of this bed and to test each position individually. It’s also important to remember that using the prone position by parents carries risks. This position should be used under the supervision of a healthcare professional. Sleeping on the stomach outside of a controlled environment increases the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Therefore, the prone position should only be used in a hospital setting under continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring. This position should not be adopted for home care.
