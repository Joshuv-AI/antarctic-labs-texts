Hyperspectral non-destructive detection based on geometrical influence correction and deep learning technology: A case study on early bruise detection in fruits
Authors: Wei Tao, Yixiao Wang, Shan Zeng, Bing Li, Chaoxian Liu, Xiangjun Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperspectral imaging is widely used for non-destructive detection in fruit because it can capture spectral differences arising from changes in tissue structure and chemical composition. However, variations in the curved-surface geometry of fruit often introduce non-structural spectral distortions. These distortions reduce detection accuracy and limit model generalisation. To address the adverse effects of illumination–geometry–spectral coupling, this study proposes a fruit bruise detection framework integrating hyperspectral imaging with three-dimensional (3D) point cloud data through geometrical influence correction based on an intrinsic decomposition. In particular, pixel-level surface normals and lighting features are first extracted from 3D point clouds to jointly acquire spectral and geometric information. A dual-branch encoder–decoder network is then designed to incorporate geometric constraints from the point cloud. This network decouples the observed spectra into intrinsic reflectance and illumination components. Finally, a bruise detection model is constructed using intrinsic reflectance images, enabling pixel-level identification of bruised regions on the fruit surface. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method maintains spectral structural consistency while significantly enhancing the discriminability of bruised areas. Compared with models using raw spectral data, the proposed approach improves detection accuracy and increases the recall rate of bruised regions. The introduced framework offers a novel physics-based decoupling mechanism for hyperspectral detection and demonstrates clear advantages in suppressing non-structural spectral variations arising from varying fruit surface geometry.

Introduction

Mechanical impacts incurred during harvest, handling and transport in agricultural operations routinely degrade fruit quality (Wang et al., 2026). Such impacts not only mar external appearance and flavour but also trigger internal tissue deterioration, thereby shortening shelf life and reducing commercial value (Wan et al., 2025). Accordingly, the prompt and reliable detection of surface bruises has emerged as a critical challenge in modern agrotechnology (Song et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2025). Conventional inspection methods, which rely on manual examination or machine-vision schemes, often prove inadequate owing to limited accuracy, poor environmental robustness and low processing speed (Wang et al., 2021). By delivering continuous spectral information across hundreds of bands, hyperspectral imaging (HSI) sensitively captures subtle structural and biochemical alterations induced by mechanical damage and is therefore increasingly regarded as a promising solution for bruise detection (Shanthini et al., 2025).

Despite its potential, the practical application of HSI for early bruise detection in fruit remains constrained by several factors. A major challenge arises from the naturally curved and heterogeneous surface morphology of most fruits (Li et al., 2024). Under such conditions, the measured spectra are affected not only by tissue properties but also by surface orientation, local curvature, illumination distribution and light scattering paths. As a result, identical or similar tissues may exhibit markedly different spectral responses at different surface locations, producing non-structural spectral variations unrelated to the actual bruise status. These effects are particularly detrimental to early bruise detection, where the spectral differences between bruised and healthy tissues are often weak and easily masked by geometry- and illumination-induced distortions (Saengwong Ngam et al., 2024).

To address these limitations, investigators have increasingly integrated HSI with support vector machines (Wang et al., 2024a), with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to analyse HSI of fruits, achieving encouraging detection rates (Olisah et al., 2024). Deep learning methods can automatically learn joint spatial–spectral representations, thereby outperforming handcrafted features (Yang et al., 2025). CNN-based models learn joint spatial–spectral representations that surpass handcrafted features (Gai et al., 2022). However, these approaches typically require large volumes of data and remain susceptible to variations in surface curvature (Khan et al., 2022). To mitigate radiometric and geometric artefacts, researchers have explored pre-processing and feature-reduction strategies (Li et al., 2024; Yamaguchi et al., 2025). Principal component analysis is frequently used to discard redundant bands while retaining bruise-related information (Wang et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2021). Although such techniques can boost accuracy under controlled conditions, their effectiveness diminishes when confronted with the varying geometry of fruit skins, indicating that robustness and generalizability remain inadequate (Li et al., 2024, 2025; Yamaguchi et al., 2025).

To obtain a more physically meaningful and robust spectral representation that improves bruise-detection accuracy, the fusion of three-dimensional (3D) point clouds with hyperspectral data has attracted increasing attention. A recent geometric–influence–correction framework combined near-infrared HSI with concurrent surface shape acquisition (Li et al., 2025). Pixel-wise corrections for curvature and scattering-path differences were applied across cultivars and fruit sizes, markedly enhancing bruise signatures. Compared with HSI alone, incorporating point-cloud information effectively suppressed distortions, yielding detection models with greater robustness and generalizability (Li et al., 2024). Nevertheless, in most existing studies, geometric cues are used solely as a pre-processing add-on. Under geometric variations or complex shadowing, distortion errors persist and degrade early bruise detection. Therefore, current research remains dominated by spectrum-centric processing, and the deeper coupling between spectral and geometric information remains underexplored.

In summary, the varying curved-surface morphology of fruit leads to varying light scattering and reflection, resulting in spectral distortions. These geometry-induced spectral variations make it difficult to distinguish the spectral signatures of early bruised regions in hyperspectral images. In this study, a geometry-aware bruise detection framework is developed by integrating HSI with 3D point cloud data (see Fig. 1). Instead of directly detecting the original spectra, the proposed method recovers intrinsic reflectance by separating the observed hyperspectral signal into reflectance and shading components under geometric guidance. This approach reduces non-structural spectral distortions caused by curved surfaces and illumination variation while preserving bruise-related spectral characteristics. Using the recovered intrinsic reflectance, pixel-level early bruise detection is performed to improve the reliability of early bruise identification on fruit surfaces. Specific objectives include the following: (1) to characterise the spectral and image features of early bruising under varying fruit surface geometry and illumination, (2) to develop a geometry-aware hyperspectral correction strategy for early bruise identification across various fruits and (3) to establish an efficient pixel-level algorithm for early bruise detection and classification.Fig. 1Core algorithm schematic diagram.

Materials

Materials preparation framework.

Sample preparation

The experimental specimens were Fuji apples and Qiuyue pears. Fuji apples were from Luochuan County, Shaanxi Province, and Qiuyue pears were from Jiaodong, Shandong Province. All fruits selected for the experiment were regular in shape. In total, 200 fruit samples were used, comprising 100 apples and 100 pears. To maintain optimal flavour and quality, the fruits were stored at 1.0°C ± 0.5°C and 90% ± 5% relative humidity. Experiments were conducted at 25°C ± 1°C and 60% ± 5% relative humidity. A vertical guiding device with an inner diameter of 25.0 ± 0.1 mm and a length of 500 ± 1 mm was used. AGCr15 steel ball impacted the apple surface at a speed of 2.5 ± 0.1 m/s from a predetermined height (Wang et al., 2026). Each fruit received impacts at multiple uniform points, with two tests per point for reliability.

Data acquisition

The data acquisition procedure is shown in Step 2 of Fig. 3. A custom acquisition platform was constructed with calibrated sample height and illumination geometry. Hyperspectral images and 3D point cloud data were acquired sequentially while each fruit remained stationary, ensuring spatial consistency across modalities.

Hyperspectral data were acquired using a SPECIM FX10 push-broom system (400–1000 nm, 224 bands, 5.5 nm spectral resolution and 2.7 nm sampling interval). Uniform illumination was provided by a dual-source lighting setup comprising three 50-W halogen lamps on each side, positioned 300 mm from the sample at 45°. Lamp output was stabilised by a power controller. Before HSI, a 3D point cloud of each fruit was captured using an ORBBEC Gemini 2XL depth camera (1280 × 720 pixels, 30 fps). Imaging was initiated immediately after bruise induction. For each fruit, hyperspectral scanning started within 2 min after impact, with a median delay of ∼1 min, and the full hyperspectral cube was acquired within 1–2 min thereafter.

Data pre-processing

All data processing, analysis and visualisation tasks in this study were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 graphics card (24 GB VRAM) and executed in a Python 3.11 software environment. Prior to intrinsic decomposition, a method for matching hyperspectral images with 3D point cloud data was designed. This method primarily comprises data acquisition, pre-processing and spatial registration steps. To obtain reliable spectral data, original images require correction through dark background subtraction and white reference calibration (Baek et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024), as follows:(1)Ihc(i,j)=I(i,j)−D(j)W(i,j)−D(j)where Ihc(i,j) denotes the corrected relative reflectance spectrum at position (i,j), I(i,j) represents the raw spectral data, W(i,j) corresponds to the white reference data and D(j) denotes the dark current data. The variables i and j represent the row and column pixel positions within the spatial dimensions of the image, respectively. The hyperspectral data are then registered with the point cloud data and used as input for subsequent methods.

Registration algorithm

For the hyperspectral data, we iterate through all 273 bands. We calculate the average intensity difference between the black and white squares on the calibration plate for each band image. We select the band with the largest intensity difference as the optimal geometric feature band. This band provides the highest contrast between the black and white squares and offers the most reliable image foundation for subsequent sub-pixel corner detection.

After determining the optimal single-band image, we can use it together with synchronously collected point cloud data to perform high-precision 2D–3D feature point matching. We match N sets of feature point correspondences {pi,Pw,i}i=1N, where pi=(ui,vi)T is the corner coordinate on the image and Pw,i=(Xw,i,Yw,i,Zw,i)T are its 3D coordinates in the LiDAR world coordinate system. Our goal is to solve for a rotation matrix R and a translation vector t, which together form the transformation from the LiDAR coordinate system to the camera coordinate system. This transformation maps a world point Pw to a point Pc in the camera coordinate system:(2)Pc=RPw+t

Combined with the camera pinhole model, the complete projection process can be expressed as follows:(3)s[uv1]=KPc=K(RPw+t)=K[R|t][XwYwZw1]

Thus, we formulate the external parameter calibration as a classical PnP (Perspective-n-Point) problem. The core task of the PnP algorithm is to solve for the camera's position and orientation in the real-world coordinate system, i.e. the rotation matrix R and translation vector t, given the known camera intrinsic matrix K and at least three sets of 2D–3D matched points. Essentially, this involves finding the optimal (R∗,t∗) such that the geometric error between the theoretical projection positions and the actual observed positions of all 3D points when projected back onto the image plane using this set of parameters is minimized. The objective function of this optimisation problem is expressed as follows:(4)(R∗,t∗)=argminR,t∑i=1N∥pi−π(K,R,t,Pw,i)∥22where π(·) denotes the complete projection function. To enhance the robustness of the algorithm against potential feature point matching errors, we adopted the Random Consistent framework to solve this minimization problem.

Methods

To reduce non-structural spectral distortions arising from the coupled effects of fruit surface geometry and illumination variation, this study develops a geometry-aware framework for early bruise detection in fruit by integrating HSI with registered 3D point cloud data. The overall workflow is shown in Fig. 4. The proposed method combines geometric guidance with a physics-consistent deep learning strategy to recover intrinsic reflectance from the observed hyperspectral signal. This approach suppresses spectral interference from surface curvature and shading, while preserving bruise-related spectral characteristics for subsequent pixel-level detection. The following subsections describe the reflectance recovery network, the physical constraints used in model training and the bruise detection procedure.Fig. 4Detection framework of the proposed method.

Detection framework of the proposed method.

Geometry-guided reflectance recovery network

Problem formulation

Let the acquired fruit hyperspectral image be denoted by Il∈RH×W×B, decomposed into two components: the intrinsic reflectance image Rh∈RH×W×B and the environmental shading component Sh∈RH×W×B. The objective of the proposed method is to recover intrinsic reflectance from the observed hyperspectral signal while separating the shading component. The recovered reflectance is expected to provide a more stable, geometry-independent representation of fruit tissue by reducing the effects of surface curvature and illumination variation and is then used for bruise detection. Following the Retinex formulation, a logarithmic transformation is applied to facilitate reflectance–shading separation:(5)I˜=log⁡(Il)(6)R˜=log⁡(Rh)(7)S˜=log(Sh),which yields the following relationship:(8)I˜=R˜+S˜

The overall objective is to predict R˜ and S˜ under physical consistency constraints.

Geometry-guided reflectance recovery network

The proposed geometry-guided reflectance recovery network, based on the intrinsic decomposition method, jointly exploits hyperspectral and 3D geometric information to separate shading from material reflectance before early bruise detection. The network takes as input a log-transformed hyperspectral cube I˜=log⁡(Il)∈RH×W×B and a registered point cloud P∈RH×W×3. A schematic of the network architecture is shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Architecture of the geometry-guided reflectance recovery network.

Architecture of the geometry-guided reflectance recovery network.

The hyperspectral encoder comprises stacked 3D convolutions that operate across spatial and spectral dimensions to capture fine-grained spatial–spectral patterns related to fruit tissue composition. In parallel, the point cloud encoder applies per-pixel multilayer perceptrons to the (X, Y, Z) coordinates and aggregates geometric information through max pooling, thereby encoding local surface shape and overall fruit curvature. The spectral and geometric features are fused through channel-wise concatenation and a shared bottleneck layer, and the joint representation is then fed into two symmetric decoders.

The reflectance decoder uses transposed and refinement convolutions to recover full-resolution reflectance, whereas the shading decoder reconstructs the corresponding shading component. A structure-guided module uses surface normals derived from the point cloud to guide shading reconstruction along the true fruit geometry. In addition, a lightweight graph-based refinement block is introduced in the reflectance branch to smooth reflectance only among spectrally and geometrically similar neighbours, thereby enhancing contrast between bruised and healthy tissues.

Network training is supervised by a composite loss with physically motivated terms. First, a spectral consistency term enforces Retinex reconstruction, ensuring that the sum of reflectance and shading remains close to the input signal. Second, a normal-aware spatial term encourages shading to vary smoothly with surface orientation while preserving reflectance edges at geometric or spectral discontinuities. Third, a graph-based reflectance regularisation term penalises large differences between neighbouring pixels with similar spectral and geometric characteristics. As a result, the recovered reflectance provides a cleaner, more object-related spectral representation for subsequent early bruise detection.

Reflectance-based early bruise detection

Based on the recovered reflectance, a pixel-level early bruise detection method was developed for fruit samples. This method exclusively uses the reflectance cube, which is physically disentangled from illumination. Z-score normalisation is first applied at the band level to each pixel's reflectance spectrum r(x,y)∈RB. The normalisation is computed as follows:(9)rˆ(x,y)=r(x,y)−μσ,μ,σ∈RBwhere μ and σ represent the mean and standard deviation of each spectral band computed from the training set, respectively. During model construction, a random forest classifier is used as the core detection model. The random forest comprises K decision trees {hk}k=1K, each trained on a bootstrap sample drawn with replacement from the training data. During node splitting, each tree searches for the optimal split among a random subset of m spectral bands, promoting diversity across trees. Each individual tree uses Gini impurity IGini as the splitting criterion, defined as follows:(10)IGini(t)=1−∑c∈{D,N}p(c∣t)2where p(c∣t) denotes the proportion of pixels belonging to class c at node t. The final prediction of the entire forest is obtained through majority voting across all decision trees. The dataset is split into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. Formally, the predicted class for an input spectral vector is given by(11)H(rˆ)=arg⁡maxc∈{D,N}∑k=1KI(hk(rˆ)=c)where K is the total number of trees, I(·) is the indicator function, c is a candidate class label and hk(fx,y) is the class prediction made by the k-th tree for the input vector.

To improve generalisation performance, a grid search was used to optimise three key hyperparameters: the number of trees, the maximum tree depth and the minimum number of samples required at a leaf node. This procedure ensured that the classifier achieved high detection accuracy while reducing the risk of overfitting.

Experimental results and discussions

This section presents the experimental results of the proposed geometry-aware framework for early bruise detection in fruit. To evaluate the method's effectiveness, the analysis focuses on three aspects: reflectance recovery performance, spectral discrimination improvement after correction and final bruise detection performance across different fruit samples. All samples were split into training and validation sets at 80%/20%. The model was trained with a batch size of 4, an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a maximum of 2000 epochs. A cosine annealing schedule was used for learning rate adjustment. Model performance was evaluated on the validation set every five epochs, and an early-stopping strategy was used to prevent overfitting. The experimental results were run 20 times under fixed-parameter conditions, and the mean value was selected as the result to fill the experimental table.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that integrating HSI with registered 3D point cloud data effectively improves early bruise detection in fruit. A key advantage of the proposed framework is that it explicitly accounts for the effects of fruit surface geometry during spectral correction, rather than treating them as simple noise. By recovering reflectance under geometric guidance, the method reduces the influence of curved surfaces and illumination variation, leading to improved inter-class separability and stronger intra-class consistency.

Compared with raw hyperspectral data, the recovered reflectance provided a more stable spectral representation for both apples and pears. This improvement was reflected not only in spectral analysis but also in the final detection results, where both accuracy and bruise recall increased. These findings suggest that geometry-aware reflectance recovery can serve as a useful pre-processing and representation strategy for fruit hyperspectral quality inspection.

It should also be noted that the current study was conducted under relatively controlled experimental conditions and involved two fruit types. Although the results are encouraging, broader validation is still required across a wider range of fruit species, surface properties and illumination conditions. In addition, further research is needed to improve the computational efficiency of the framework for practical online or real-time applications.

Conclusions and future work

To address spectral distortions in hyperspectral fruit detection caused by varying surface geometry, this study proposes a geometry-aware framework that integrates HSI with registered 3D point cloud data. By recovering reflectance under geometric guidance, the proposed method reduced non-structural spectral distortions caused by curved surfaces and illumination variation and provided improved spectral features for detection. Early bruise detection experiments on apple and pear samples showed that the recovered intrinsic reflectance outperformed raw hyperspectral data in both test accuracy and bruise recall, demonstrating the potential of the proposed framework for robust fruit quality inspection. Future research should focus on validating the method under more diverse fruit types and imaging conditions, as well as improving its efficiency for practical online applications.
