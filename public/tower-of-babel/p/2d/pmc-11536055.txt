Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins cholesterol, 10-years atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk, and risk of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke
Authors: Yi-Ping Jia, Jia-Min Wang, Jie-Qiong Lyu, Huan-Huan Yang, Meng-Yuan Miao, Xiaowen Wang, Zhong-Xiao Wan, Yan Zheng, Li-Qiang Qin, Fu-Rong Li, Guo-Chong Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriglyceride-rich lipoproteins cholesterol (TRLs-C) has been associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), even among individuals with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol in the targeted range. We assessed the associations of TRLs-C with myocardial infarction (MI) and ischemic stroke (IS) and compared the associations with those for other traditional lipids (i.e., triglycerides and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [non-HDL-C]). Included were 327,899 participants from the UK Biobank who were free of MI or IS and did not receive lipid-lowering treatment at baseline. Ten-year risk for ASCVD was estimated by the Pooled Cohort Equations and was grouped as low (<7.5%), intermediate (7.5% to <20%), and high risk (≥20%). Multivariable Cox regression models were used to examine the associations of TRLs-C, triglycerides, and non-HDL-C with risk of MI and IS, overall and by the 10-years risk categories. During a median of 12.3 years of follow-up, 8,358 incident MI and 4,400 incident IS cases were identified. After multivariable adjustment, higher TRLs-C was associated with a higher risk of MI (p-trend <0.0001) but not IS (p-trend = 0.074), with similar associations for triglycerides and non-HDL-C. There were interactions between TRLs-C and 10-years ASCVD risk on risk of MI (p-interaction <0.0001) and IS (p-interaction = 0.0003). Hazard ratios (95% CIs) of MI comparing the highest with the lowest quartiles of TRLs-C were 2.10 (1.23–1.30) in the low-risk group, 1.52 (1.38–1.69) in the intermediate-risk group, and 1.22 (1.03–1.45) in the high-risk group. The corresponding estimates for IS were 1.24 (1.05–1.45), 0.94 (0.83–1.07), and 0.83 (0.67–1.04), respectively. Similar interactions with the 10-years ASCVD risk were observed for triglycerides and non-HDL-C on risk of MI and for triglycerides on risk of IS. Elevated levels of TRLs-C (or triglycerides or non-HDL-C) are associated with a higher risk of developing MI and IS (except non-HDL-C) predominantly among individuals who are typically classified as being low-risk. These findings may have implications for more detailed risk stratification and early intervention.Supplementary key words: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Lipids, Residual risk, Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins cholesterol

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which mainly includes myocardial infarction (MI) and ischemic stroke (IS), is currently the most common causes of disability, disease burden, and death in high-income countries and expected to become so for worldwide regions by 2030 (1). It is well-documented that dyslipidemia, such as high levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), are causally related to ASCVD (2). In recent years, pharmacological lowering of LDL-C, primarily via statin therapy, has an unambiguous impact on reducing ASCVD (3). However, significant residual cardiovascular risk has been reported among individuals with lipid-lowering treatments, even when achieving the targeted LDL-C levels (4).

Among all the potential residual ASCVD risk factors, triglyceride-rich lipoproteins cholesterol (TRLs-C) (also widely referred as remnant cholesterol) has attracted a great deal of research attentions (5). TRLs-C represents the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, specifically very-low-density lipoproteins and intermediate-density lipoproteins during fasting, in addition to chylomicron remnants during the nonfasting state (6, 7). Upon infiltration into the arterial wall, these particles are phagocytosed by macrophages, resulting in the formation of foam cells (6). Consequently, heightened levels of TRLs-C are likely to intensify cholesterol accumulation within the arterial wall, thereby promoting the advancement of atherosclerosis and subsequently contributing to the occurrence of ASCVD (8).

TRLs-C can be calculated as total cholesterol (TC) minus high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) minus LDL-C (6, 7, 9). An advantage of this calculation is that it can be calculated from a standard lipid profile at no additional cost. In a study which compared calculated and measured TRLs-C study, higher levels of measured and calculated TRLs-C both were associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality, although the association for calculated TRLs-C was stronger than that for measured TRLs-C (10).

Many studies have demonstrated higher TRLs-C levels to be associated with a heavier coronary atherosclerotic burden, even in relatively low-risk individuals who have optimal LDL-C levels (11, 12, 13, 14, 15). However, there are few studies with additional models adjusting for other traditional lipid traits substantially correlating with TRLs-C, such as triglycerides and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) (5, 12). In addition, previous epidemiological evidence has suggested a stronger association of TRLs-C with cardiovascular events in specific subgroups of individuals with a relatively lower cardiovascular risk, such as among individuals without diabetes (11) or hypertension (16) or among relatively leaner individuals (16), as compared with the associations observed for the opposite subgroups with the corresponding risk factor. To this point, no studies have specifically assessed whether the relationship between TRLs-C (or triglycerides or non-HDL-C) and risk of ASCVD may be dependent on an individual’s phenotypes characterized by the burden of traditional ASCVD risk factors.

In this context, we leveraged data from the UK Biobank study to investigate the associations of TRLs-C and the two closely correlated traditional lipids (i.e., triglycerides and non-HDL-C) with risk of MI and IS. We also explored whether the associations of these lipids with MI and IS differed according to traditional ASCVD risk burden (reflected by predicted 10-years risk for ASCVD).

Materials and Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective, longitudinal study established to provide a resource for investigation of the genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors associated with a wide range of diseases (17). From 2006 to 2010, the UK Biobank study recruited around 500,000 men and women with diverse ethnic backgrounds, aged between 37 to 73 years old, from 22 centers throughout England, Wales, and Scotland. During the recruitment phase, participants provided an extensive range of health-related information and underwent multiple measurements to gather data. The UK Biobank study was approved by the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care and the National Health Service North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (REC reference for UK Biobank 11/NW/0382). All participants provided informed consent through electronic signature.

Assessment of TRLs-C levels

Using the blood samples collected at baseline of the UK Biobank, TC levels were measured by CHO-POD analysis on a Beckman Coulter AU5800. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and LDL-C levels were measured by enzymatic protective selection analysis on a Beckman Coulter AU5800. TRLs-C was estimated as TC minus the sum of LDL-C and HDL-C. Even though currently there is no standard method to assess TRLs-C, this equation has been widely used in previous studies because it is available from the standard lipid profile (6, 7).

Ascertainment of MI and IS

Participants self-reported information on history of MI and stroke prior to the study baseline. Incident MI and IS events that occurred during follow-up were identified based on cumulative medical records of hospital diagnoses (18, 19). According to the international classification of diseases, 9th or 10th revision (ICD-9 or ICD-10), algorithmically-defined outcomes, including MI and IS, were available for further research (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/alg_outcome_main.pdf).

Assessment of other covariates

A touchscreen questionnaire and nurse-led interviews were used to gather data at baseline on demographic and socioeconomic factors, lifestyle behaviors, medical histories, and medication usage. The Townsend deprivation index, a composite measure of social deprivation, was derived based on four socioeconomic variables (unemployment, non–car ownership, non–home ownership, and household overcrowding). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using measured weight and height (kg/m2). Blood pressure was measured using the Omron HEM-7015IT digital blood pressure monitor and calculated as the mean of two sitting measures. Diabetes was defined by self-reported history of physician’s diagnosis, self-reported use of hypoglycemic agents, or an HbA1c ≥ 126 mg/dl (6.5%). Multiple blood biomarkers, including serum major lipids and apolipoprotein B (Apo B), were quantified using blood samples collected at baseline (20).

Statistical analysis

For the current analysis, we excluded participants who had self-reported or diagnosed MI or IS (n = 18,150) or reported lipid-lowering treatments (n = 62,445) at baseline. We further excluded participants with missing data on TC, LDL-C, or HDL-C (n = 93,413) and those with unreasonable levels of calculated TRLs-C (≤0, n = 504). After these overlapping exclusions, 327,899 participants (186,876 men and 141,023 women) were included in the final analysis (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Baseline participant characteristics were reported according to quartile of TRLs-C, as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (where appropriate) for continuous variables and number with percentage for categorical variables. Correlations between TRLs-C and traditional cardiometabolic traits were assessed using Spearman partial correlation coefficients with multivariable adjustment.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of MI and IS across quartile of TRLs-C, triglycerides, and non-HDL-C with the lowest quartile of the analyzed lipid as the reference. Person-time of follow-up was calculated from the date of enrollment through the date of diagnosis of MI or IS, death, loss of follow-up, or the most recent follow-up, whichever came first. Two models were constructed to account for potential confounders. The minimal model was adjusted for age (years) and sex. The full model was further adjusted for ethnicity (White, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, other or mixed), Townsend deprivation index (in quintile), smoking status (never, former, current), pack-years of smoking (for current smokers), annual household income (<£18,000, £18,000-£30,999, £31,000-£51,999, ≥£52,000), systolic blood pressure (SBP) (mmHg), and antihypertensive medication use. As TRLs-C levels may be in the causal pathway from obesity or diabetes to ASCVD (5), an exploratory analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of further adjustment for BMI and diabetes. To explore if other lipids may explain the examined associations, we added various exploratory analyses where HDL-C, LDL-C, Apo B, non-HDL-C, triglycerides, and TRLs-C were adjusted individually (in addition to model 2 adjustment).

Many studies have demonstrated higher TRLs-C levels to be associated with a heavier coronary atherosclerotic burden, even in relatively low-risk individuals who have optimal LDL-C levels (16, 21, 22). To explore the potential influence of traditional risk factors on the associations of TRLs-C with risk of MI and IS, we also examined the associations within different population subgroups defined by components of the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) (23, 24). The PCE score has been widely used to predict 10-years ASCVD risk in different populations including UK Biobank participants (25, 26). These components included age (<60 or ≥60 years), sex, ethnicity (black or not), SBP (<140 or ≥140 mmHg), TC (<240 or ≥240 mg/dl), HDL-C (<50 or ≥50 mg/dl), current smoking (yes or no), and diabetes mellitus (yes or no). In addition, we calculated a PCE 10-years risk score for each participant to reflect their overall burden of ASCVD risk factors. The participants were then grouped as having low (<7.5%), intermediate (7.5% to <20%), and high (≥20%) risk for developing ASCVD in the following 10 years (27). Next, we examined the associations of TRLs-C with risk of MI and IS across the 10-years risk categories. TRLs-C had substantial correlations with triglycerides (r = 0.76) and non-HDL-C (r = 0.80), and we implemented the similar analyses to assess the associations of triglycerides and non-HDL-C with risk of MI and IS according to the 10-years risk categories.

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3) or SAS (version 9.4). A two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Median (interquartile range) of TRLs-C among the 327,899 included participants was 25.7 (19.4–33.2) mg/dl. Participants with higher levels of TRLs-C were older, were more likely to be male and ethnically white, had lower household income, and were more likely to smoke, be overweight or obese, and take antihypertensive medications (Table 1). Higher TRLs-C was also associated with higher levels of TC, LDL-C, and SBP and lower HDL-C.

Correlations between TRLs-C and TC (r = 0.67), LDL-C (r = 0.67), Apo B (r = 0.69), and especially triglycerides (r = 0.76) and non-HDL-C (r = 0.80) were substantial. TRLs-C was moderately positively correlated with BMI (r = 0.30) and moderately negatively correlated with HDL-C (r = −0.31) (Supplemental Fig. S2).

Associations of TRLs-C, triglycerides, and non-HDL-C with risk of MI and IS

During a median of 12.3 years of follow-up, 8,358 incident MI and 4,400 incident IS cases were identified. With adjustment for age and ethnicity, TRLs-C was significantly associated with risk of MI (p-trend <0.0001) and IS (p-trend = 0.0003) (Table 2). Further adjustment for sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, and medication usage yielded a similar association for MI (HRQ4 vs. Q1 = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.78–2.04; p-trend <0.0001) but attenuated that for IS (HRQ4 vs. Q1 = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.99–1.19; p-trend = 0.074). In the additional exploratory analyses where specific lipids were further adjusted for (based on model 2), the association of TRLs-C with risk of MI was attenuated but remained significant (Table 2). For example, after further adjusting for triglycerides (model 8), HR of MI comparing the highest with the lowest quartile of TRLs-C was 1.71 (95% CI: 1.58–1.86).

The crude incidence of MI was 95.5, 350.6, and 960.7 per 100,000 person-years across the groups with low, intermediate, and high risk for ASCVD, respectively. The corresponding data for IS were 49.8, 179.6, and 376.5 per 100,000 person-years, respectively. We then examined the associations of TRLs-C (each 10 mg/dl increment) with risk of MI and IS stratified by the traditional ASCVD risk factors (components of the PCE). For both MI and stroke, there was evidence for stronger associations among participants with relatively lower ASCVD risk (Supplemental Fig. S3).

As a result, there were significant interactions between TRLs-C and 10-years ASCVD risk on risk of both MI (p-interaction <0.0001) and IS (p-interaction = 0.0003), with both associations being gradually attenuated (the TRLs-C-IS association was reversed to be inverse in the high-risk group) with increasing 10-years ASCVD risk (Fig. 1). When comparing the highest with the lowest quartile of TRLs-C, HRs of MI were 2.10 (95% CI: 1.23–1.30) in the group with 10-years risk <7.5% (p-trend <0.0001), 1.52 (95% CI: 1.38–1.69) in the intermediate-risk group (p-trend <0.0001), and 1.22 (95% CI: 1.03–1.45) in the group with 10-years risk ≥20% (p-trend = 0.012). The corresponding estimates for IS were 1.24 (95% CI: 1.05–1.45) (p-trend = 0.014), 0.94 (95% CI: 0.83–1.07) (p-trend = 0.64), and 0.83 (95% CI: 0.67–1.04) (p-trend = 0.0062), respectively. The elevated risk of MI associated with TRLs-C in the low-risk group remained after further adjusting for triglycerides or non-HDL-C, yet that for IS was eliminated after further adjusting for triglycerides (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this large prospective study of 327,899 UK participants without lipid-lowering treatments at baseline, we evaluated and compared the associations of TRLs-C, triglycerides, and non-HDL-C with risk of incident MI and IS. Overall, we found that all the three lipids were associated with risk of MI, even after one was adjusted for another, and that only triglycerides and non-HDL-C were associated with IS. We further examined the associations of these lipids with risk of MI and IS according to 10-years ASCVD risk defined by the PCE score. Our findings showed that the associations of these lipids with MI and the associations of TRLs-C and triglycerides with IS were most pronounced among participants with the lowest predicted risk for ASCVD and gradually attenuated with increasing 10-years ASCVD risk.

Several epidemiological studies have shown elevated TRLs-C to be associated with a higher risk of ASCVD (10, 22, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35). A recent meta-analysis combined results from 31 studies and indicated that higher TRLs-C was associated with a higher risk of total cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, and total stroke (36). No previous studies, to the best of our knowledge, have assessed whether the association of TRLs-C with risk of ASCVD is dependent on the accumulative burden of traditional risk factors. Nevertheless, in secondary analyses of some previous studies, TRLs-C seemed to be more strongly associated with the risk of developing cardiovascular events in low-risk subgroups, such as in younger individuals (16, 28), women (28, 37), non-smokers (28), and individuals without hypertension (16, 28) or diabetes (11), when compared with the associations in the opposite subgroups with the corresponding risk factor. In the aforementioned meta-analysis (36), the association of TRLs-C with cardiovascular events also appeared to be stronger in younger than in older individuals (pooled relative risk [RR]: 1.39 vs. 1.20), in normotensive than in hypertensive individuals (RR: 1.45 vs. 1.25), and in leaner than in obese individuals (RR: 1.37 vs. 1.17).

Although no previous studies have specifically focused on TRLs-C in relation to ASCVD risk in low-risk populations, a few studies indeed assessed LDL-C or non-HDL-C. In a prospective study of 36,375 participants, elevated LDL-C (eg, 160–189 mg/dl vs. <100 mg/dl) was associated with a substantially higher risk of cardiovascular deaths among individuals with 10-years ASCVD risk ≤7.5% or ≤5% (38). Similarly, among individuals who were not recommended for statin therapy at baseline (ie, 10-years predicted risk <7.5%, no diabetes mellitus, and LDL-C between 70 and 189 mg/dl) in the Framingham Offspring Cohort, which sustained exposure to moderately elevated non-HDL-C over a decade was associated with an approximately 1.8-fold higher risk of developing coronary heart disease after adjustment for traditional risk factors (39). In the present study, when the predicted ASCVD risk was <7.5%, participants in the highest quartile of TRLs-C had a 2.1-fold risk of developing MI when compared with those in the lowest TRLs-C quartile. Although the association of TRLs-C with MI remained after further adjusting for triglycerides or non-HDL-C, we observed similar differences in the association of triglycerides and non-HDL-C with risk of MI according to the predicted ASCVD risk. These findings suggest that approaches to identifying individuals who fall in the low predicted risk category may further classify the risk of developing ASCVD if their specific lipids levels (e.g., TRLs-C, triglycerides, or non-HDL-C) are took into account. Our findings may have the potential to improve early prevention strategies and facilitate precision therapy for populations who are typically classified as being low-risk.

Potential mechanisms underlying the effect modification of the burden of traditional cardiovascular risk factors on the relationships between these specific lipids and ASCVD remain unknown. Atherosclerosis plaque formation followed by plaque rupture is responsible for ischemic events such as MI and IS (40). Beyond dyslipidemia, the traditional risk factors contribute to the atherosclerotic processes and the pathophysiology of ischemic events through various biological actions such as inductions of inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance (41, 42, 43), some of which may also be involved in the adverse association of lipids with risk of ASCVD (44, 45). High-risk individuals usually have a cluster of multiple risk factors and the lesions of atherosclerosis may already be present in the blood vessels. Thus, the traditional ASCVD risk factors may cause atherosclerosis competitively with dyslipidemia, leading to an attenuated association between specific lipids and risk of ASCVD among high-risk individuals. On the other hand, although participants with lipid-lowering treatments at baseline were excluded from the present analysis, it is expected that high-risk individuals are more likely to be treated for dyslipidemia during the follow up. As such, lipid levels measured at baseline are likely less representative of long-term levels for high-risk individuals, which highlights the importance for repeated measures to better capture the risk of ASCVD in this population group.

According to the 2018 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology cholesterol guidelines, people with a predicted 10-years ASCVD risk lower than 7.5% would typically not be judged suitable for statin treatment (27). Nevertheless, in an individual-participant meta-analysis of 22 trials of statin therapy versus control, reduction of LDL-C with a statin reduced the risk of major vascular events, especially among those with a 5-years risk of major vascular events lower than 5% (46). Given the shared atherosclerotic mechanisms for lipids such as LDL-C, TRLs-C, and triglycerides (6), it is reasonable to assume that those with a lower predicted risk for ASCVD may still be at largely varying risk for developing ASCVD depending on their lipid levels. Thus, specific lipids-lowering interventions may further decrease the risk of ASCVD among the “low-risk” individuals who have elevated levels of these cardiovascular detrimental lipids.

Strengths of our study include the large sample size and the long-term follow-up, leading to sufficient number of incident cases of MI and IS. As a result, we were able to exclude individuals with lipid-lowering therapy at baseline to eliminate the related confounding and to address and compare the relationships of TRLs-C and other traditional lipids with risk of MI and IS across different population subgroups at varying risk for developing ASCVD. Potential limitations to our study should also be acknowledged. Despite the careful control of the traditional risk factors, this observational study cannot make causal inference. TRLs-C was not directly measured but instead was calculated using traditional lipid biomarkers, although both calculated and measured TRLs-C have been associated with future risk of developing cardiovascular events (36). Moreover, only baseline lipid levels were considered in the present study, and future research is needed to address how longitudinal changes in TRLs-C levels or long-term sustained exposure to elevated TRLs-C may be associated with risk of ASCVD. Finally, participants in the UK Biobank are predominantly of White ethnic background who are relatively healthier than the general population in the UK. Thus, generalizability of our findings to other ethnic or regional populations remains unclear.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings showed that elevated levels of TRLs-C, triglycerides, or non-HDL-C are associated with a higher risk of developing MI (for non-HDL-C) or both MI and IS (for TRLs-C and triglycerides) predominantly among individuals with low-risk phenotypes. These findings may have implications for more detailed risk stratification and early intervention. Further studies are needed to ascertain whether lipids-lowering interventions (e.g., lifestyle modification or pharmacological therapy) could favorably influence MI or IS outcomes in low-risk individuals with elevated levels of these lipids.

Perspectives

Competency in medical knowledge

Elevated levels of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins cholesterol or the related lipids (especially triglycerides) are associated with a higher risk of developing myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, predominantly among individuals who are typically classified as being low-risk.

Translational outlook

Participants with a lower predicted risk for ASCVD may still be at largely varying risk for developing ASCVD depending on the levels of TRLs-C or the related lipids. These findings may have implications for more detailed risk stratification and early intervention of ASCVD.
