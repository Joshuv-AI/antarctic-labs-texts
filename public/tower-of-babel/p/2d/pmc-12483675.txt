Protocol for quantifying extracellular and intracellular ATP from macrophages upon inflammasome activation using a luciferin-luciferase technique
Authors: Julieta Schachter, Adriana Guijarro, Pablo J. Schwarzbaum, Pablo Pelegrín
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtracellular ATP triggers several cellular responses, while intracellular ATP depletion primes cells for death. Here, we present a protocol for the quantification of extracellular and intracellular ATP from bone marrow-derived macrophages upon inflammasome activation using a luciferin-luciferase technique. We describe steps for macrophage isolation, differentiation, and inflammasome activation with real-time ATP measurement. The protocol allows quantitative determination of intracellular ATP and extracellular ATP hydrolysis, with applicability to different cell types.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Schachter et al.1

Before you begin

Extracellular ATP (eATP) and its hydrolysis products (ADP and adenosine) activate different purinergic receptors, triggering a wide range of cellular responses, depending on the tissue and type of activated receptor.2 Macrophages are able to release intracellular ATP (iATP) into the extracellular medium in response to different stimuli.1,3,4 The resulting accumulated eATP is dynamic, since its concentration depends not only on cytosolic ATP release, but also on secreted or cell membrane bound ectonucleotidases capable of hydrolyzing eATP on the extracellular milieu or on the cell surface.5

In experiments with viable cell suspensions, cell membranes are impermeant to eATP, so eATP hydrolysis is solely driven by the activity of membrane-bound ectonucleotidases. As a result, the terms “ecto-ATPase activity” and “rate of eATP hydrolysis” can be used interchangeably throughout this manuscript, as any decrease in nucleotide levels, detected by the plate reader, will reflect the activity of these enzymes.

Assessing iATP is crucial for several reasons. First, iATP is the main driver of non-lytic ATP release.6 Second, it serves as a dynamic indicator of intracellular pathway activation, reflecting metabolic activity and stress responses.7 Finally, iATP depletion signals energetic impairments that compromise cell viability.8 Various endogenous and exogenous factors can alter iATP levels,9 making its measurement essential for evaluating both cell viability and metabolic state. However, accurate iATP quantification is challenging, since cell rupture transfers iATP to the extracellular space, where it becomes a substrate for both released intracellular ATPases and ectonucleotidases, leading to its rapid degradation. The protocol facilitates the calculation of the rates of iATP release and eATP hydrolysis. Its application can be easily expanded to assess eATP dynamics in most eukaryotic cells.10,11

The use of pharmacological inhibitors is recommended to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying ATP release, as multiple pathways have been described across different tissues, including channel-mediated release via pannexins and connexins12 and vesicular exocytosis.3 In parallel, ectonucleotidase inhibitors are commonly used to prevent underestimation of eATP levels due to rapid eATP degradation. However, care must be taken to ensure that the selected inhibitors do not interfere with the luciferin–luciferase reaction used to quantify ATP. Innovation

The measurement of nanomolar concentrations of extracellular and intracellular ATP requires a robust and sensitive protocol. This protocol introduces an innovation by enabling real-time quantification of both eATP and iATP, as well as extracellular ATP hydrolysis. Additionally, it offers a significant advantage over traditional kit-based approaches by providing a cost-effective and efficient assay capable of detecting ATP concentrations ranging from nanomolar to low micromolar levels. We also provide a brief guide for the use of specific pharmacological inhibitors that help dissect ATP release and degradation pathways, carefully selected to avoid interference with luciferase activity.

Measurement of ecto-ATPase activity

Timing: 2 h

Timing: 2 h

To obtain the net flux of ATP from the cytosol to the external medium in a given condition, it is also important to measure the hydrolysis rate of extracellular ATP, which will depend on the activity of ectoATPases present in the cell surface. Accordingly, eATP hydrolysis rate will be calculated by adding known concentrations of exogenous ATP.Note: Following the eATP kinetics by registering luminescence values every 2–4 s for 30–40 m, depending on the number of samples being measured and the reading speed of the plate reader employed. Expected outcomes

This protocol outlines the methodology for measuring both eATP and iATP in BMDMs. The data collected with this procedure describes eATP kinetics following stimulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome with nigericin (Figure 5), evidencing the ATP release, as well as eATP hydrolysis by ectonucleotidases, and the iATP content. The transformation of RLU into ATP concentration can be achieved using the calibration curve. The data collected from the calibration curve may be plotted in Microsoft Excel, where linear regression is performed to calculate the slope and evaluate the R2 value. To ensure a good fit of the data to the linear model, the R2 value should be close to 1. The slope derived from this regression will then be used to convert RLU into ATP concentration.

The parameter KATP (an apparent first-order constant), derived from the relationship between ecto-ATPase activity and ATP concentration, can serve as a comparative measure of the kinetic efficiency of ecto-ATPases across different cell types.

Limitations

This method relies on luciferin’s ability to emit light upon oxidation, catalyzed by luciferase in the presence of ATP (Figure 6).Figure 6The firefly luciferase bioluminescent reactionLuciferin is bound by luciferase and, in the presence of ATP, magnesium and oxygen, the enzyme catalyzes the oxidation of luciferin. This oxidation generates an excited state of oxyluciferin, which releases light, AMP and pyrophosphates (PPi).

The firefly luciferase bioluminescent reaction

Luciferin is bound by luciferase and, in the presence of ATP, magnesium and oxygen, the enzyme catalyzes the oxidation of luciferin. This oxidation generates an excited state of oxyluciferin, which releases light, AMP and pyrophosphates (PPi).

The reaction converts ATP, luciferin, and O2 into CO2, PPi, AMP, and oxyluciferin, with light as a byproduct. Luciferase acts as a remarkably efficient ATP sensor due to the extremely high photon yield of the reaction, emitting one photon per 1.1 ATP molecules consumed.26

This high light output minimizes ATP consumption even at nM to low μM ATP concentrations typically found in the extracellular space, enabling continuous detection without altering ATP levels under most experimental conditions. Since luciferase operates at low activity, this also ensures that byproducts such as AMP and PPi are minimally produced. Nevertheless, ATP concentrations below 30 nM may still be challenging to detect because the ATP released must reach the plate reader’s detection threshold. Increasing cell numbers or reducing the assay volume can help concentrate ATP in the reaction medium. Optimizing conditions such as pH, temperature, and cation availability, while minimizing inhibitors and contaminants, is essential for luciferase stability and accurate ATP quantification.

Calibration curves should be performed in the assay medium to assess potential inhibitory substances and compounds that may interfere with luciferase activity. Divalent cations, crucial for luciferase function, must be present, while divalent chelators should be minimized as they can inhibit activity.

Thermostability has been achieved in several luciferase mutants by incorporating various point mutations to stabilize enzyme structure, allowing them to function at 37°C. These variants have been widely used in molecular biology applications where luciferase serves as a reporter gene from various expression vectors in mammalian cell systems. However, when luciferase is used as a tool for ATP detection, many of these mutations result in a significant loss of ATP affinity, making it difficult to detect low ATP levels.

We used a kinetic protocol where ATP is released from cells or exogenously added, and the measurement is taken simultaneously. In contrast, for an end point protocol, it is important to consider that ATP may be unstable in biological samples, and therefore prone to degradation. Therefore, samples should be processed quickly or stored under appropriate conditions to prevent ATP loss and ensure accurate measurements.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

BMDMs samples are contaminated with hematopoietic cells, such as erythrocytes. See “preparation of bone marrow-derived macrophages” step 4.b.

Potential solution

The ability of bone marrow cells to adhere to the plastic of the cell culture plate will make it easy to remove hematopoietic cells that do not stick and remain soluble by removing the culture medium. Another solution could be to use lysis buffer to remove these cells before plating them for experimental use, or to perform flow cytometry to remove unwanted cells.

Problem 2

Variability in bone marrow quality. The quality and quantity of bone marrow obtained can vary significantly depending on the age of the mice or the extraction conditions (for example, if the type of needle used for extraction is very narrow, it can break or damage a large part of the cellular content). See “preparation of bone marrow-derived macrophages” step 4.e.

Potential solution

Work under standardized collection conditions and using needles of the appropriate size (25G). In addition, cell viability can be checked by performing cell counts with a specific stain such as trypan blue, which will stain dead cells.

Problem 3

Luminescence is not detected at low concentrations of the calibration curve/ the values of RLU are too high. See “measurement of extracellular ATP increase upon NLRP3 inflammasome activation” step 6.b.

Potential solution

•If luminescence is not detected at low ATP concentrations or the RLU values are too high, it may indicate issues with the settings of the reader or assay conditions.•Adjust the gain of the plate reader to optimize sensitivity. This can help achieve accurate detection, especially at low concentrations of ATP (please refer to the specific instructions for adjusting the gain on your reader plate. If you cannot find detailed instructions for this step, you may want to consult the user manual or leave the settings as is).•Ensure that the calibration curve was prepared correctly and that all reagents are fresh and properly mixed. If the RLU values are too high, it may indicate saturation of the assay, which can be addressed by reducing the sample volume or diluting the samples.•In cases where the light signal is weak, one potential approach to enhance sensitivity is to slightly increase the concentration of luciferase. This increase can amplify the emitted light, improving the detection of low ATP concentrations. However, it is essential to perform calibration experiments in advance to assess the impact of elevated luciferase concentrations on ATP degradation. If the luciferase activity at higher concentrations leads to significant ATP consumption, this effect must be accounted for to ensure accurate ATP measurements. Calibration experiments should also verify the consistency and linearity of the calibration curve under these adjusted conditions. If luciferase-induced ATP degradation becomes non-negligible, the degradation must be subtracted from the measured signal to accurately determine the real ATP levels in the sample. By carefully balancing luciferase concentration and calibration conditions, both enhanced sensitivity and reliable quantification can be achieved, ensuring that the calibration curve remains linear and consistent.

Problem 4

The RLUs vs ATP concentrations do not show a linear relationship (calibration curve is not linear). See “measurement of extracellular ATP increase upon NLRP3 inflammasome activation”, note in step 6.b.

Potential solution

Check for potential issues such as improper mixing, ATP degradation, buffer contamination or signal saturation at very high nucleotide concentrations. Adjusting the ATP concentration range or optimizing the luciferin-luciferase mix may help improve linearity. If a linear relationship cannot be attained, consider fitting non-linear functions to the data (e.g., hyperbolic curves), even though this may lead to a certain loss in sensitivity.
