Dysphagia is Associated With the Combination of Defective Bolus Transit and Poorly Relaxing Lower Esophageal Sphincter in Patients With Ineffective Esophageal Motility
Authors: Ala’ Abdel-Jalil, Thai Hau Koo, Ronnie Fass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsIneffective esophageal motility (IEM) is the most frequently encountered esophageal manometric abnormality. Nonobstructive dysphagia is frequently associated with severe esophageal peristaltic dysfunction. This study aims to evaluate the presenting symptoms and association between dysphagia and specific manometric findings in patients with IEM.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 228 IEM patients diagnosed on high-resolution manometry at an academic institution (2010-2013). We collected data regarding the main presenting symptoms and manometric findings: bolus transit, distal esophageal pressure amplitude, and lower esophageal sphincter (LES) resting and relaxation pressure.ResultsDysphagia was the main presenting symptom (25%) in IEM patients. Bolus transit was incomplete in either liquid or viscous swallows (30%) and incomplete in both liquid and viscous swallows (59%) in IEM patients. The LES resting pressure and LES relaxation pressure were elevated (9% and 36%, respectively) in IEM patients. There was no significant difference between dysphagia and either poorly relaxing LES (P = 0.725) or defective bolus transit (DBT) to liquid and viscous swallows (P = 0.372) compared to the rest of the IEM patients. However, there was a significant association between dysphagia and the combination of poorly relaxing LES and DBT in liquid and viscous swallows (P = 0.006). Subgroup analysis comparing dysphagia with heartburn/regurgitation demonstrated a similar significant association between dysphagia and the combination of poorly relaxing LES and DBT during viscous and liquid swallows (P = 0.016).ConclusionsDysphagia is a variable symptom associated with complex esophageal motility abnormalities. IEM patients with a combination of poorly relaxing LES and DBT are more likely to have dysphagia.Keywords: Deglutition disorders; Esophageal sphincter, lower; Heartburn; Manometry; Retrospective studies

Introduction

Ineffective esophageal motility (IEM) is the most commonly identified manometric abnormality in esophageal high-resolution manometry (HRM). It has traditionally been defined manometrically as a large proportion of low-amplitude or failed peristaltic contractions. Early criteria classified an IEM diagnosis when more than 30% of distal esophageal swallows had amplitudes < 30 mmHg.1 However, the advent of multichannel intraluminal impedance combined with manometry revealed a key discrepancy: manometrically “ineffective” swallows do not always equate to functional failure of bolus transport.1 In a seminal study by Tutuian and Castell,1 about 68% of liquid and 59% of viscous swallows meeting manometric IEM criteria still achieved complete bolus transit (BT), and nearly one-third of IEM patients had normal overall bolus clearance on impedance. These findings underscored that the true effectiveness of esophageal motility must be evaluated by BT outcome, not pressure metrics alone.1 This recognition prompted a re-examination of IEM diagnostic thresholds and the incorporation of BT assessment into clinical practice.

Subsequent work proposed stricter manometric criteria for IEM to correlate better with clinical impairment. Notably, Blonski et al2 suggested raising the threshold to require ≥ 50% of swallows to be ineffective (low-amplitude) for an IEM diagnosis. In a cohort comparison, patients meeting this “new” IEM definition (≥ 50% ineffective swallows) experienced dysphagia and BT abnormalities more frequently than those who only met the older 30% criterion.2 This provided a rationale for adopting the ≥ 50% cutoff, which was later formalized in the Chicago classification version 3.0 (CC v3.0) for esophageal motility disorders. The most recent CC v4.0 has tightened the definition even further: more than 70% of swallows must be ineffective (weak contractions, distal contractile integral 100-450 mmHg·s·cm) and/or ≥ 50% are failed (DCI < 100) to conclusively diagnose IEM.3 Swallow sequences with large peristatic breaks (> 5 cm) are now considered part of IEM rather than a separate “fragmented” category. Under these new standards, far fewer patients qualify as definite IEM––in one recent analysis, < 10% of those previously labeled IEM by CC v3.0 remained so by CC v4.0 criteria, and notably dysphagia was the dominant symptom among these truly hypomotile cases.4 Cases with 50-70% ineffective swallows are deemed “borderline” in CC v4.0, requiring adjunctive evidence (eg, poor multiple rapid swallow response, abnormal bolus retention on imaging or impedance) to confirm clinically significant hypomotility.3 This evolution reflects an effort to distinguish patients whose motility abnormality has meaningful functional consequences (bolus stasis and symptoms) from those with minor manometric abnormalities.

In parallel, technical advances in impedance monitoring have enabled quantitative BT metrics that go beyond a simple complete/incomplete dichotomy. Standard impedance analysis defines bolus entry and exit at each esophageal segment by a 50% drop and recovery in impedance relative to baseline, allowing classification of each swallow as having complete or incomplete clearance.1 This binary transit outcome, while useful, may overlook partial clearance deficits that contribute to symptoms.1,5,6 Therefore, new impedance-derived measures such as the esophageal impedance integral (EII) and bolus flow time have been developed. The EII (often expressed as an EII ratio) quantifies intraluminal bolus retention by integrating impedance over time, effectively capturing the volume of residual bolus in the esophagus.7 Early studies indicate that these high-resolution impedance manometry (HRIM)-based metrics correlate strongly with patients’ dysphagia severity. For instance, Carlson et al8 found that among patients with non-obstructive dysphagia (and normal or minor motility diagnoses), the EII ratio had the strongest association with dysphagia symptoms, outperforming conventional manometric metrics. In that study, the EII ratio and bolus flow time differentiated dysphagic patients from both healthy controls and those with reflux symptoms, highlighting their potential diagnostic value.8 Moreover, reproducibility of the EII has been demonstrated and abnormal values correlate with patient-reported dysphagia.7 These advances align with the CC v4.0 emphasis on assessing bolus clearance: the latest guidelines explicitly encourage using impedance or barium transit findings to support an IEM diagnosis when manometric results are borderline.3

In summary, the diagnostic framework for IEM is moving toward an integrated evaluation of pressure and BT. Our study was situated in an evolving context. We focused on patients with IEM and dysphagia to examine how manometric weakness and BT failure correlate with each other and with symptoms. By incorporating impedance-based transit analysis, we aim to discern why some patients with IEM experience dysphagia while others do not. We also discuss our findings in light of emerging metrics (EII) and the latest consensus criteria (CC v4.0) to clarify the clinical significance of “ineffective” motility in esophageal symptomatology.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the presenting symptoms and association between dysphagia and specific manometric findings in patients with IEM at a single academic institution. The intervention was conducted between August 2010 and September 2013 at the Esophageal Disorders Clinic of our academic institution. The objectives of this study were to analyze the outcomes and presenting symptoms of patients with IEM according to the severity of the disease (mild = normal BT; moderate = abnormal BT to either liquid or viscous swallows; severe = abnormal BT to both liquid and viscous swallows). It also compared dysphagia with heartburn/regurgitation as the main presenting symptom (subgroup analysis).

We acknowledge that more recent updates, such as CC v4.0, offer more stringent and clinically specific criteria for IEM diagnosis. However, we would like to clarify that the HRM studies in our dataset were performed between 2010 and 2013 before the development and publication of CC v4.0 in 2021. At the time of data collection and initial diagnosis, CC v3.0 was the prevailing gold standard, and all patients were classified accordingly. Although retrospective reclassification using CC v4.0 may be considered, it would be methodologically challenging and potentially introduce bias due to the lack of uniformly collected adjunctive metrics (eg, multiple rapid swallows, positional swallows, and standardized symptom correlation), which are now recommended under CC v4.0. Additionally, attempting to reclassify patients using CC v4.0 may introduce bias, particularly given the incomplete availability of certain protocol-driven data elements required for definitive CC v4.0 diagnosis. For these reasons, while we agree that CC v4.0 provides improved clinical relevance, our goal was to study the symptom–physiology correlation based on the best available standard at the time of testing.

Patient Selection

We retrospectively searched the database of the academic institution’s Esophageal Disorders Clinic for IEM patients according to HRM criteria per then-current CC criteria and collected data from the medical records of each patient about the main presenting symptom and the following manometric findings: BT (impedance), distal contractile integral (DCI), and lower esophageal sphincter (LES) resting and relaxation pressure (integrated relaxation pressure [IRP]).

We included consecutive patients with IEM diagnosed by HRM who were evaluated for various gastroesophageal reflux disease symptoms, such as dysphagia, heartburn, acid regurgitation, chest pain, and/or belching. A detailed review of the patients’ medical, endoscopic, manometric, pH, and histological records was performed to ensure proper inclusion in the study. Patients younger than 18 years of age, those with a known obstructive esophageal disease by endoscopy (ie, cancer and stricture), scleroderma, esophagitis on upper endoscopy, esophagogastric junction (EGJ) outflow obstruction (EGJOO) (mean IRP ≥ 22 mmHg for Sandhill technology), achalasia, absent peristalsis (100% of swallows with failed peristalsis), diffuse esophageal spasm, and jackhammer esophagus, as defined by the CC v3.0,3 and those who had previously undergone esophageal surgery (ie, anti-reflux surgery or myotomy) or endoscopic intervention (ie, transoral fundoplication) were excluded. Patients with atypical (ear, nose, throat, or respiratory) symptoms only and those with oropharyngeal dysphagia without associated esophageal symptoms were also excluded.

All patients had normal upper endoscopy findings and were found to have manometric IEM according to the current CC criteria. (Under CC v3.0, IEM was defined as ≥ 50% ineffective swallows; this corresponds to the “inconclusive” or “definite” IEM range in the updated CC v4.0)

Although patients with a definitive diagnosis of EGJOO were excluded, defined by a mean IRP ≥ 22 mmHg (Sandhill system) with supportive findings (eg, elevated intrabolus pressure, abnormal EGJ morphology, or obstructive symptoms), some patients included in the IEM cohort exhibited isolated IRP elevations without meeting the full criteria for EGJOO under CC v3.0. These individuals were retained to explore the relationship between impaired LES relaxation and BT in the context of IEM, particularly given the heterogeneity in clinical presentation and evolving definitions of EGJOO over time.

All manometry studies were performed using a Sandhill Scientific HRM system. Accordingly, the IRP threshold for abnormal LES relaxation was defined as > 22 mmHg in the supine position based on system-specific normative values. The previously mentioned 15 mmHg threshold referenced general legacy definitions but was not used to determine inclusion or exclusion in this analysis. Patients with sustained IRP values exceeding 22 mmHg, along with other supporting evidence suggestive of EGJOO, were excluded to maintain the diagnostic clarity for IEM.

High-resolution Manometry and Impedance Protocol

The UNI-ESO-WG1A1 High-Resolution Probe (Sandhill Scientific Inc, Highlands Ranch, CO, USA) is a 4-mm diameter solid-state catheter with 32 circumferential pressure channels and 16 multi-channel intraluminal impedance sensors to assess the success or failure of bolus movements through the esophagus. The catheter assembly included at least 6 impedance-measuring segments spaced at 2 cm intervals, spanning the distal 20 cm of the esophagus (impedance pairs located approximately at 3, 5, 9, 15, and 17 cm above the LES, with a reference just proximal to the LES). Manometric studies were performed with patients in the supine position with the patient at 30° incline after at least a 6-hour fast, which is the standard position for esophageal manometry according to the CC protocol. Impedance sensors were positioned to record the end of the proximal esophageal segment through the distal esophagus and into the proximal stomach. Subjects underwent HRM testing with 10 liquid (5 mL normal saline) swallows performed 20-30 seconds apart, and patients refrained from swallowing between test swallows to assess basal EGJ pressure and morphology. This was followed by 10 viscous swallows (5 mL of applesauce-like substance). The data collected using the HRM probe can be displayed in color-coded pressure topography plots. Subsequently, the data were analyzed using Sandhill BioVIEW Analysis Suite 64 software (Sandhill Scientific Inc).

We opted for supine swallows to maintain consistency with normative metrics and diagnostic criteria.9 Notably, body position can influence manometric measurements: prior studies show that upright swallows generally yield lower distal contractile pressure and can even change diagnostic classifications.9 We did not perform upright HRM in this protocol; however, we acknowledge upright assessments may better simulate typical eating conditions and can be valuable in select clinical contexts as an adjunct.3,9

Individual swallows were excluded from the analysis in cases of double or multiple swallows, which could lead to deglutitive inhibition of peristalsis. Impedance sensors were positioned to record from the termination of the proximal esophageal segment through the distal esophagus and into the proximal stomach, with approximately 2 intragastric impedance measurements. Complete bolus clearance was defined as bolus entry, followed by sequential bolus clearance at all impedance recording sites. Conversely, incomplete bolus clearance was defined as bolus entry without bolus clearance at one or more esophageal impedance recording sites. Hence, each swallow was characterized as either complete or incomplete bolus clearance and the total percentage was calculated accordingly.

Manometric Definitions

IEM was defined manometrically as per CC v3.0: ≥ 50% swallows with DCI < 450 mmHg·s·cm (ineffective) or failed (DCI < 100). LES relaxation was assessed by the IRP over 4 seconds. We recorded whether the IRP was elevated (≥ 15 mmHg on supine swallows, the cutoff for impaired EGJ relaxation in our laboratory’s reference) or normal. LES basal pressure was measured, with an elevated resting LES pressure defined as > 40 mmHg (hypertensive LES) per the convention.

Esophageal peristalsis was defined as intact if no break longer than 5 cm was observed within isobaric contour. Fragmented peristalsis was also recorded, defined as ≥ 50% swallows with large (> 5 cm) defects in the 20 mmHg isobaric contour and a DCI ≥ 450 mmHg·s·cm.

Impedance and Bolus Transit Analysis

Impedance signals were analyzed to determine the BT completeness for each swallow. Bolus entry was defined at each impedance segment as a ≥ 50% drop in impedance from baseline, and bolus exit when impedance recovered to ≥ 50% of baseline.1 A given swallow was classified as complete BT if bolus exit was observed at all measured distal impedance sites (down to the 5 cm above LES sensor) for at least 5 seconds.1 Incomplete transit was defined if any distal impedance segment failed to register bolus exit (ie, retention of bolus in that segment).1 This analysis was applied separately for liquid and viscous swallows. For each patient, we calculated the percentage of swallows with incomplete transit for liquids and for viscous; based on prior normative data, we considered > 20% incomplete swallows abnormal, although our primary analyses treated transit as a continuous variable and in categorical groups as defined below.

We also categorized each patient’s overall transit as normal transit (complete clearance in ≥ 80% of liquid swallows and ≥ 70% of viscous swallows, analogous to established impedance norms10), partially impaired transit (incomplete clearance in 1 consistency only), or globally impaired transit (incomplete clearance in both liquid and viscous swallows). These thresholds (> 20-30% abnormal swallows constituting abnormal transit) mirror the criteria used by Tutuian and Castell.6 Impedance analyses were performed manually by 2 investigators, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. We did not perform a separate segment-by-segment transit time analysis; rather, we focused on the presence of incomplete BT per swallow and per patient, as defined above. Advanced impedance metrics such as EII were not measured in this study; our transit assessment remained qualitative (complete/incomplete) for each swallow, consistent with the available software tools.

Data Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics and symptom distribution of the patients were analyzed for IEM patients using descriptive statistics. The definition of IEM according to HRM requires ≥ 5 out of 10 study swallows to show weak or failed DCI. The BT for liquid and viscous swallows and the mean resting LES pressures were compared. A subgroup analysis of patients with IEM who presented with dysphagia, heartburn, and regurgitation was performed, including DCI, IRP, mean resting LES pressure, and BT data for liquid and viscous swallows.

The data were maintained in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet at our academic institution and analyzed using an unpaired t test for normally distributed continuous parameters. Continuous variables were compared non-parametrically using the Mann–Whitney U test (reported as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range] as appropriate). Categorical variables (eg, proportion of incomplete swallows and prevalence of dysphagia) were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, to assess differences in the proportions of patients/measurements.

We specifically evaluated the associations between dysphagia and (1) manometric findings (elevated IRP, hypertensive LES); (2) overall transit impairment category; and (3) combined metrics (eg, co-occurrence of abnormal transit and elevated IRP). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate changes between the DCI of a single swallow and MRS. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 29.0, SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) or R program (version 4.4.1, Vienna, Austria). The results are presented in the tables and bar graphs. The results are presented with corresponding P-values to identify any statistically significant differences across the participant groups.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Symptom Distribution

A total of 228 patients (mean age, 55 ± 14 years; 63% female) met the criteria for IEM and were included in the study (Fig. 1). The primary presenting symptoms were broadly divided into dysphagia vs gastroesophageal reflux-like complaints. Dysphagia was reported in 56 patients (24.6%), whereas the remaining 172 patients (75.4%) did not report dysphagia as a symptom. Among the non-dysphagia group, the most common symptoms were chronic heartburn and/or regurgitation (49 patients, 21.5%), often in the context of refractory gastroesophageal reflux disease evaluation (the remaining patients were referred for atypical chest pain or globus; those groups were too small for meaningful separate analysis). For additional subgroup analysis, we identified 48 patients with dysphagia as the predominant symptom (no significant concomitant heartburn) and 49 patients with predominant heartburn/regurgitation without dysphagia; these 2 groups were used to compare transit and manometric findings in “dysphagia vs reflux” presentations.

Manometric Findings in Ineffective Esophageal Motility

By design, all patients had IEM on HRM. Among these IEM patients, LES relaxation was normal in the majority, although a substantial minority showed borderline outflow abnormalities (Fig. 2). Specifically, IRP was elevated (≥ 15 mmHg supine) in approximately one-third of cases (77/228, 33.8%). Dysphagia and non-dysphagia patients had similar rates of elevated IRP (about 36% vs 33%, respectively, difference not significant, P = 0.725) (Table 1). Median IRP values were 12.8 mmHg (interquartile range, 10-17) in the dysphagia group vs 11.5 mmHg (8-16) in others (P = 0.19).

Bolus Transit Abnormalities on Impedance

Despite uniformly ineffective manometry, BT was not uniformly failed. Many patients exhibited preserved clearance for a portion of swallows, especially liquids. Out of the 228 IEM patients, 25 (11%) had normal overall BT by our criteria (ie, no significant impairment for either liquid or viscous swallows) (Fig. 3). The remaining majority had some degree of transit abnormality; 203 patients showed incomplete BT in at least one consistency, with 135 (59%) exhibiting abnormal transit for both liquids and viscous swallows (global transit impairment) (Table 3). Viscous swallows were more frequently abnormal than liquid swallows (on average 35% vs 27% incomplete, P = 0.03).

When focusing on the subgroup with dysphagia (n = 56), impedance confirmed a high rate of bolus clearance dysfunction. Only 36% of dysphagic IEM patients had an entirely normal transit (ie, complete clearance of all test swallows). The vast majority (64%) of patients showed at least some failed bolus movements. Notably, 59% of dysphagia patients demonstrated incomplete transit for both liquids and viscous swallows (indicating pervasive transit failure), while an additional 5% had incomplete transit with one consistency (usually viscous), but not the other.

However, impaired BT was not exclusive to those with dysphagia. A considerable proportion of IEM patients without dysphagia also had transit abnormalities on HRIM. Among 172 patients who did not report dysphagia, approximately 58% had incomplete transit on impedance for at least one bolus type (99/172). This rate was only slightly lower than the 64% (36/56) observed in the dysphagia group, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.37) (Table 1). Similarly, in the symptom-based subgroup analysis, the proportion of patients with any abnormal BT was virtually the same between those with predominant dysphagia (73%) and those with predominant heartburn/regurgitation (67%), with a non-significant difference (P = 0.54) (Supplemental Table 1).

Interaction of Transit Abnormalities With Esophagogastric Junction Function

Given that dysphagia might arise from a combination of factors, we further examined whether coexisting EGJ relaxation impairment modulates the impact of weak peristalsis on symptoms. We hypothesized that incomplete BT might be more clinically relevant (ie, more likely to cause dysphagia) when accompanied by even subtle outflow obstruction (hypertensive lower esophageal sphincter pressure [LESP]). To test this, we looked at the subset of patients who had both an abnormal BT (for liquid and viscous) and a hypertensive LESP and compared their symptom frequency to those with transit abnormality alone. Because EGJ dysfunction can reflect either impaired relaxation (IRP) or high basal tone (LESP), we performed parallel analyses using both parameters. The outcomes are described below.

Among patients with dysphagia, a greater fraction had a combination of abnormal transit and elevated LESP than those without dysphagia (Fig. 4). In the dysphagia group, 15 of 56 patients (27%) had incomplete transit for both bolus types and elevated LESP, whereas only 25 of 172 (14.5%) non-dysphagia patients showed this combination. This difference was statistically significant (P = 0.036) (Table 4). In contrast, having abnormal transit with a normal LESP was common in both groups (41 dysphagic vs 147 non-dysphagic patients).

We observed a similar trend when comparing the dysphagia vs heartburn-predominant subgroups (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Among the 48 dysphagia-predominant patients, 17 (35%) had both abnormal BT and high IRP, whereas only 7 (14%) of the 49 heartburn-predominant patients had this dual finding. This difference is notable and statistically significant (P = 0.016) (Supplemental Table 1). Meanwhile, the prevalence of abnormal transit without elevated IRP was high in both groups (approximately 65% of dysphagia patients and 86% of heartburn patients had some transit abnormality if one does not require IRP to be elevated) (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

Our study illustrates the nuanced relationship between manometric ineffective motility, BT, and esophageal symptoms. The key finding was that dysphagia in IEM was strongly associated with objective bolus clearance failure, particularly when compounded by subtle LES relaxation abnormalities. In contrast, milder forms of IEM (with preserved transit or normal EGJ relaxation) often do not cause dysphagia. These findings help contextualize IEM within the modern understanding of esophageal function, where mere pressure metrics are insufficient, and functional clearance must be considered.1

Traditionally, IEM was defined liberally, capturing many patients with relatively minor peristaltic weakness.11,12 Our data support prior observations that such patients may have normal BT and minimal symptoms.1 In Tutuian et al’s 2004 study,1 one-third of manometric IEM patients had intact bolus clearance and were arguably overdiagnosed if one relied on pressure criteria alone. This “overdiagnosis” is clinically important. Labeling patients with IEM when their actual transit is adequate could lead to unnecessary concerns or interventions. In our cohort, we likewise found about 11% of IEM patients had normal impedance transit and no dysphagia. This aligns with the move toward stricter IEM definitions. The revised criterion proposed by Blonski et al2 (requiring ≥ 50% ineffective swallows) was an attempt to focus on those truly at risk of dysphagia. Notably, using that ≥ 50% cutoff, Blonski’s group demonstrated more frequent dysphagia and transit abnormalities in “new IEM” patients compared to those who fell short of that threshold.2 Our findings echo this: patients who would have been “borderline” by old criteria (eg, only 30-40% ineffective swallows) often had normal transit and lacked dysphagia, whereas those with more pervasive peristaltic failure were symptomatic.

The CC v4.0 has embraced this paradigm by setting an even higher bar for an unequivocal IEM diagnosis––> 70% ineffective or ≥ 50% failed swallows.3 Consequently, a large proportion of cases that were IEM under CC v3.0 are now considered “inconclusive” motility unless additional evidence of dysfunction is obtained.3 This change appears justified by outcomes: recent analyses found that only about 10% of patients met the definite IEM criteria under CC v4.0, and those were predominantly the ones with dysphagia complaints.4 In other words, the new criteria enrich for clinically relevant IEM. Our study supports this approach – many of our 228 IEM patients might be reclassified as having normal or borderline motility under CC v4.0 (since only one-third had ≥ 70% ineffective swallows); indeed, borderline cases often had normal transit and fewer symptoms. Meanwhile, the minority of our patients who would still qualify as “definite IEM” by CC v4.0 (eg, those with > 70% ineffective or half failed swallows) largely overlapped with the dysphagic, poor-transit subgroup. This suggests that the CC v4.0 criteria are more specific for true motility failure with clinical consequences, whereas the older broader criteria captured many physiologically compensated cases. However, we acknowledge that our study defined IEM by CC v3.0 criteria; a post-hoc reanalysis using v4.0 thresholds would be valuable in future work to confirm how many of our subjects fall into the new “inconclusive” category and whether adjunctive testing (as recommended by CC v4.0) would have stratified them correctly.3

A major implication of our findings is the importance of measuring the BT in conjunction with manometry. We showed that esophageal clearance failure is the proximate cause of dysphagia in patients with IEM, a logical yet previously unproven assumption. Dysphagia arises when swallows do not effectively empty the esophagus; our impedance data confirm that most dysphagic IEM patients indeed had incomplete bolus clearance on multiple swallows. However, an equally critical insight is that manometric peristaltic integrity alone is an imperfect predictor of the bolus clearance. Some patients in our series had poor contractions, yet managed to clear the majority of boluses (perhaps via residual gravity or longitudinal muscle contraction assisting transit). Others seemingly had only moderate hypomotility, but still failed transit and experienced dysphagia, especially if there was any co-existing LES outflow resistance. This underscores the rationale behind complementary transit testing in borderline cases. Current guidelines acknowledge this: for cases with 50–70% ineffective swallows (indeterminate IEM), the CC v4.0 technical review advises pursuing objective evidence of impaired bolus clearance via barium esophagram or impedance to solidify the diagnosis.3 Our results provide clinical backing for that recommendation.

Moreover, they highlighted that a binary assessment (complete vs incomplete transit), while useful, may not capture the full spectrum of functional impairment. All-or-none transit criteria might, for example, classify a swallow as “complete” even if minimal residual bolus remains, or “incomplete” even if transit fails only at a single distal site for a moment. These nuances likely explain why some patients without dysphagia still had what we counted as “incomplete” transit – perhaps those instances of residual bolus were minor or rapidly cleared by secondary peristalsis; hence, they were asymptomatic. Here, emerging quantitative impedance metrics can enhance the discrimination. The EII is one such metric that aggregates the extent and duration of bolus presence across the esophagus.7 A high EII ratio signifies substantial bolus retention, whereas a marginally elevated EII might indicate only partial clearance with minimal clinical effect.1,5,6,8 Recent studies have shown EII and related measures to correlate with symptom severity more robustly than simple transit indices.8 For instance, in non-obstructive dysphagia patients, Carlson et al8 demonstrated that the EII ratio had the strongest association with dysphagia scores, successfully distinguishing symptomatic patients from those with similar manometry who did not report dysphagia. This suggests that not all incomplete transits are equal – a small amount of residual bolus might be well tolerated, whereas larger volume retention causes sensation. In our study, we did not quantify bolus volume, but the finding that dysphagia tracked with the presence of more pervasive transit failure (both liquids and viscous, affecting multiple swallows) hints at a dose-dependent effect of bolus stasis. Incorporating metrics such as EII in future studies (and clinical practice) could refine our ability to predict dysphagia; rather than a yes/no transit metric, a continuous measure of clearance efficacy might better stratify which IEM patients truly need therapeutic intervention.

Taken together, our results and the literature trends point to a few practical conclusions. First, patients meeting only minimal IEM criteria (eg, 30-40% weak swallows) should be evaluated carefully for actual functional impairment before attributing their symptoms to IEM. If such a patient has no dysphagia and normal impedance transit (or EII in the normal range), labeling them with esophageal motility disorder may be misleading; their symptoms (eg, heartburn) might have an alternate cause. This reinforces the stance of CC v4.0 to avoid overdiagnosing IEM in the absence of supportive evidence.3 Our finding that many reflux-predominant patients had manometric IEM but normal clearance and no dysphagia supports the notion that some cases of “IEM” are incidental or clinically unimportant. On the other hand, definite transit abnormalities on impedance should prompt attention, even if the manometric criteria for IEM are not fully met. We encountered patients with relatively preserved manometry (just below the IEM threshold) who nonetheless had significant incomplete BT and dysphagia; these individuals might have been missed by the old classifications. This scenario is captured by the new concept of “inconclusive IEM” in which additional testing can confirm a true disorder. In practice, barium swallow or rapid drink challenge HRIM could reveal poor clearance in such cases, guiding treatment.

Second, our data emphasize the interplay between peristaltic failure and EGJ dysfunction. Neither was alone strongly predictive of symptoms, but together, it was. Clinicians should thus be alert to multifactorial motility impairment, and an IEM patient with even borderline LES hypertension or mildly elevated IRP might be more likely to benefit from therapy (eg, a proton pump inhibitor trial if reflux is present or even LES-relaxing measures) than an IEM patient with completely normal EGJ function. This resonates with prior findings that failed swallows (complete absence of contraction) have more functional impact than just weak swallows,3 and that an elevated IRP, even if not meeting the achalasia criteria, can worsen clearance. Our observation that dysphagia was concentrated in those with both clearance failure and elevated IRP suggests an overlap with what some authors call “EGJOO with ineffective motility.” Whether such patients should be managed differently (for example, with therapies targeting the EGJ vs promotility agents) is an area for future investigation.

We recognize several limitations in our study. It was retrospective and limited to patients who underwent HRM, which may have introduced referral bias (clinicians are more likely to refer patients with significant symptoms). The impedance analysis was qualitative; we did not calculate the EII or bolus clearance time, which might have provided a more granular insight. In addition, we only used supine swallows. It is known that some patients with IEM may clear boluses better upright due to gravity, potentially reducing the sensitivity of supine HRM to correlate with symptoms.9 However, our protocol adhered to standardized diagnostic criteria,9 allowing us to compare our results with published criteria and outcomes. We also did not systematically assess contraction reserve (eg, via multiple rapid swallows), which could differentiate true neuromuscular impairment from volume-induced inhibition in IEM patients.3 Despite these limitations, our study’s strengths include the use of combined HRM-impedance in a sizeable cohort and the focus on clinical correlations that mirror recent guideline shifts.

In conclusion, IEM represents a spectrum, and not all manometric “ineffectiveness” is created equal. Our findings reinforce that BT is the ultimate arbiter of esophageal function; patients whose IEM is accompanied by failed bolus clearance (especially in tandem with slight EGJ outflow resistance) are those with dysphagia. Patients with preserved transit, even if the pressure is low, tend to remain asymptomatic. These insights align with the progressive tightening of the IEM diagnostic criteria over time, with the aim of identifying truly clinically significant motility failures. Moving forward, we advocate the routine incorporation of impedance evaluation (and emerging metrics, such as EII) in esophageal motility testing. This will improve diagnostic accuracy and help tailor management; for instance, distinguishing an IEM patient who might benefit from bolus clearance aids (eg, swallowing therapy, liquid boluses, or possibly medications such as achalasia-type therapies if EGJOO is present) from those who simply have manometric abnormalities but adequate function and may not require intervention. Ultimately, by embracing a more holistic assessment of esophageal motor function and integrating pressure, flow, and clearance, we can better understand and treat patients with IEM and dysphagia in the era of CC v4.0 and beyond.
