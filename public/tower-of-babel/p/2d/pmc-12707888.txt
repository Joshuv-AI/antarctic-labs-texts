Genetic Characterization of 128 Chinese Individuals with Neurodevelopmental Disorders via Whole-Exome Sequencing
Authors: Yayun Qin, Huang Cao, Lijun Liu, Meiqi Yi, Ting Wang, Ling Zeng, Xiaoyan Wang, Runhong Xu, Chengcheng Zhang, Hui Li, Jieping Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are chronic conditions marked by abnormal brain development, presenting with significant clinical heterogeneity. Early diagnosis is crucial but challenging due to the complex symptoms. Genetic factors play a dominant role in NDD etiology. This study was to evaluate the diagnostic utility of dual-dimension whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis in Chinese patients with NDDs and to deepen the understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations.MethodsThis study retrospectively analyzed WES data of 128 Chinese NDD patients from Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital (July 2020–March 2024) for single-nucleotide variants (SNVs)/small insertions-deletions (Indels) and copy number variants (CNVs). Pathway enrichment, tissue-expression analyses, and functional experiments were conducted to interpret pathogenic genes and variants of uncertain significance.ResultsThe overall diagnostic rate for NDDs was 35.9% (46/128), with 28 cases confirmed by SNV/Indel analysis (30 variants in 29 genes) and 18 by CNV analysis (22 variants). Dual-dimension analysis markedly improved the diagnostic rate compared to conventional SNV/Indel analysis (35.9% vs. 21.9%). Patients with multisystem abnormalities had a higher diagnostic rate (63.2% vs. 31.2%). Among the 30 SNV/Indel variants, 86.7% (26) were de novo, and 70.0% (21) were novel. Recurrent pathogenic variants in ASXL3, SHANK3, and EHMT1 genes were identified. Most pathogenic genes were enriched in transcription-regulation pathways and highly expressed in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex. Functional experiments showed that the NLGN3 c.562G>A (p.G188R) hemizygous variant affects protein stability and is deleterious, aiding prenatal diagnosis and the birth of a healthy offspring.ConclusionIntegrating CNV analysis into routine WES workflows effectively clarifies the genetic heterogeneity of NDDs, expands the gene variant spectrum, and provides a basis for NDD prognosis assessment and precision diagnosis and treatment.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) refer to a spectrum of chronic conditions affecting brain development [1]. These disorders affect approximately 3% of children globally [2]. NDDs include, but are not limited to, global developmental delay (GDD), intellectual developmental disorders (IDDs), autism spectrum disorders (ASDs), developmental language disorders (DLDs), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), epilepsy (EP), and cerebral palsy (CP) [3]. GDD and IDD are the most prevalent manifestations of NDDs. Diagnosing NDDs traditionally involves clinical observation and psychological assessments, such as the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems-11 (ICD-11) [4] and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5) [5]. However, the overlapping and multifaceted nature of NDD symptoms often complicates diagnosis. Early diagnosis is critical, as it can significantly improve outcomes through timely educational interventions and therapeutic strategies.

The etiology of NDDs is complex, and influenced by genetic, environmental, immune, metabolic, and other factors. Among these, genetic factors play a pivotal role. Identifying genetic causes is essential for providing precise genetic counseling and effective management of patients, enabling a more accurate assessment of recurrence risk and the potential prediction of future medical issues. Common genetic contributors to NDDs include single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (Indels), and copy number variants (CNVs) [6]. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) has emerged as a valuable technique for uncovering the genetic basis of NDDs [7, 8]. Additionally, chromosomal microarray analysis and copy number variant sequencing (CNV-seq) are effective in detecting CNVs in individuals with intellectual disability (ID), GDD, and/or ASD [9–11]. The combined use of trio-WES and CNV-seq has been shown to elevate the detection rate in children with NDDs [12, 13]. Advances in algorithms that compare coverage depth have facilitated the integration of CNV analysis into WES data, enabling the detection of both large and small CNVs [14–17]. The inclusion of exome-based CNV analysis renders WES a more cost-efficient option for the clinical diagnosis of NDDs [17]. Nevertheless, a thorough evaluation of SNVs/Indels and CNVs derived from raw WES data remains necessary for accurately diagnosing NDDs.

In our study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical features and WES data from 128 patients diagnosed with NDDs. Our findings underscore the genetic heterogeneity inherent in NDDs and affirm the utility of WES as an effective diagnostic tool for these conditions. Moreover, our research sheds light on the application of WES in the diagnostic process for NDDs and broadens the spectrum of pathogenic variants in the NDD-associated genes.

Methods

Recruitment of Participants and DNA Extraction

The study encompassed 128 probands diagnosed with NDDs, who underwent WES between July 2020 and March 2024. This cohort comprised 73 males and 55 females, aged 10 months to 40 years. The enrollment criteria were: (1) completion of neuropsychological development assessment and diagnosis by a clinician, corresponding to NDD typing; (2) the presence of ≥1 of the following NDD types: IDD, ASD, GDD, DLD, ADHD, EP, and CP; and (3) the precise etiology of these individuals with NDDs remains elusive. Neuropsychological assessments and standardized tests primarily include standardized psychological and educational evaluations: cognitive ability tests, such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-V (WISC-V); academic achievement tests, such as the Woodcock-Johnson IV; and behavior rating scales and tests, such as Conners. Medical evaluations include neuroimaging studies, such as magnetic resonance imaging; neurodevelopmental examinations and physical health assessments include sleep monitoring, hearing and vision tests, etc. Environmental and social factor assessments involve family interviews and school observations, among others. Individuals with nonhereditary factors such as trauma and prenatal infection were excluded. Clinical manifestations were defined according to Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms for data analysis [18]. GDD is characterized by significant delays in achieving milestones in areas such as gross and fine motor skills, speech/language, cognitive, and social/emotional development. By the age of five, many children with GDD are diagnosed with IDD, identified by social adaptability challenges and an IQ below 70. ASD is present as difficulties in social communication and repetitive behaviors. DLD hinders language acquisition, comprehension, and usage. ADHD is marked by persistent inattention or hyperactivity-impulsivity, leading to social functioning issues. EP is a common neurological disorder in children, defined by recurrent seizures due to abnormal brain electrical activity. CP involves a spectrum of disorders that affect movement, balance, and posture. Comprehensive clinical data, including age, sex, and specific clinical indications, are detailed in online supplementary materials (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549155).

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples using Qiagen DNA Blood Midi/Mini kits (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

WES Analysis

The study utilized trio-WES to sequence the exomes of affected children and their parents, and quad-WES to include an additional sibling, enhancing our ability to detect de novo and inherited variants associated with NDDs. In cases where parental DNA samples were unavailable, singleton-WES was used to analyze the exome of the affected individual to identify potential pathogenic variants. Short tandem repeat analysis was performed to verify parentage and exclude maternal contamination. Exome capture and subsequent high-throughput sequencing were conducted at Berry Genomics Corporation (Beijing, China) using NanoWES (Berry Genomics, China) and the NovaSeq6000 sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Quality control protocols ensured that all samples achieved an average coverage depth of approximately 100×, with more than 95% of targeted bases (including exons and ± 20 intronic nucleotides flanking exon-intron junctions of all nuclear genes) sequenced at a read depth of 20× or greater (online suppl. Table S1).

Raw image files underwent processing using CASAVA (v1.82) to perform base calling and generate raw data. The raw sequence data were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) utilizing the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (0.7.17) tool. Variant calling was carried out using the Verita Trekker® Variants Detection System by Berry Genomics and GATK (4.1.9.0, https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) [19] (online suppl. Fig. S1). Variant annotation and interpretation were conducted through ANNOVAR [20] and the Enliven® Variants Annotation Interpretation System (v2.10.0), both authorized by Berry Genomics. Enliven® was used for variant calling and Verita Trekker® for annotation and automated American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) classification. The underlying algorithms are described in the following patents: (1) Enliven® – ZL 202110386066.9, ZL 202110184919.0, ZL 202410185961.8; (2) Verita Trekker® – ZL 202210392298.X, ZL 202210392841.6. SNVs/Indels were filtered using human population databases, such as gnomAD (v3.1.1, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) [21], the 1000 Genome Project (phase3, https://browser.1000genomes.org) [22], the Berry big data population database, and dbSNP (156, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp) [23]. Additional annotation databases were consulted, encompassing in silico prediction algorithms like SIFT (https://sift.jcvi.org) [24], FATHMM (https://fathmm.biocompute.org.uk) [25], MutationAssessor (https://mutationassessor.org) [26], CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu) [27], SPIDEX [28], as well as disease and phenotype databases that are updated in real time such as OMIM (https://www.omim.org) [29], ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar) [30], HPO (https://hpo.jax.org/app/) [31], and HGMD (https://www.hgmd.org) [32]. The specific scores or thresholds used for variant classification with each in silico tool are detailed in the current ClinGen recommendations [33]. Variants with minor allele frequencies ≥1% under a recessive model or ≥0.1% under a dominant model were filtered out. Remaining variants were systematically interrogated against population cohorts and curated evidence from PubMed, ClinVar, OMIM, and HGMD. Each variant was classified according to the ACMG and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines [34] into one of five tiers – pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign – by integrating population frequency, in silico predictions, functional assays, segregation, de novo status, and allelic context. Sanger sequencing confirmation and co-segregation analysis were performed for all variants suspected to be clinically significant.

Exome-Based CNV Analysis

A tool named Sprinkle, developed by Berry Genomics, was utilized for CNV calling. “cn” indicates the average copy number of the region to be analyzed. A copy number loss is determined when cn ≤1.3, and a copy number gain is determined when cn ≥2.7. For the sex chromosomes of males, a copy number loss is determined when cn ≤0.3, and a copy number gain is determined when cn ≥1.7. This tool incorporated various components such as the XHMM polymerase chain assembly method for noise reduction in sequencing data, the CNV Kit fix module for GC content and bias correction, and subsequent copy number estimation and CNV identification in both exonic and large segment regions. The interpretation of identified CNVs was guided by factors such as their type, size, frequency, presence of key genes, and recurrent areas, following the guidelines established by the ACMG [35, 36]. To evaluate the pathogenic potential of detected CNVs, databases like the Database of Chromosomal Imbalance and Phenotype in Humans Using Ensembl Resource (DECIPHER, https://decipher.sanger.ac.uk) [37], the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV, updated: February 25, 2020, https://dgv.tcag.ca) [38], and the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) Dosage Sensitivity Map (https://dosage.clinicalgenome.org/) [39] were commonly consulted.

Brain Expression Profiling of Pathogenic Genes

Using the Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [40], query pathogenic genes and identify brain regions with high RNA and protein expression of these genes. Summarize the findings in an Excel spreadsheet, and visualize the results using pie charts.

Plasmid Construction

The open reading frame of NLGN3 was amplified from HeLa cell cDNA and seamlessly cloned into a previously constructed 3xFlag vector (Addgene #208616) using seamless cloning technology (Bio Basic Inc., B632219). For the construction of the NM_018977.4 open reading frame plasmid, Exons 3 and 3/4 were synthesized via polymerase chain assembly as detailed in the literature [41] and seamlessly inserted in place of the corresponding region. The NM_018977.4: c.562G>A (p.188G>R) variant was introduced through point mutagenesis followed by seamless cloning. Modifications and insertions made during the molecular cloning process were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech [Shanghai] Co., Ltd.).

Cell Culture and Transfection

HeLa cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (CellMax, CGM314.05) enriched with 2 mm Ala-Gln (OKA, D10184, 0.2 μm PES filtered) and 10% fetal bovine serum (CellMax, SA211.02), at 37°C in an environment containing 5% CO2. Antibiotics were omitted except when specified. Plasmids were introduced into the cells employing Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) or Lipo8000 (Beyotime, C0533) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturers.

Western Blot Analysis

The Western blot experiments were performed in duplicates. Cells transfected with plasmids were lysed with a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer that included a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). After denaturing the proteins, they were separated on a 10% acrylamide gel and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The Flag-tagged NLGN3 proteins were identified using a mouse anti-Flag antibody produced in-house (Sigma, Cat. No. T6664, diluted 1:1,000), and their expression levels were quantified relative to tubulin, detected using anti-beta-tubulin antibodies (Proteintech, Cat. No. 66240-1-Ig, diluted 1:20,000).

Results

Cohort Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

This study enrolled a total of 333 individuals, including 101 trio-WES, 26 singleton-WES, and one quad-WES. Among the 128 probands with NDDs, there were 73 males and 55 females, aged between 10 months and 40 years, with most being younger than 10 years old (shown in Fig. 1a).

Clinical features of probands in the cohort. a Demographics of the cohort. b NDD subtypes in the cohort. c Affected systems.

Seven types of NDDs were identified among these patients, encompassing both simplex and multiplex NDD types. IDD (67/128, 52.3%) was the most common, followed by DLD (44/128, 34.4%), ASD (42/128, 32.8%), and GDD (30/128, 23.4%) (shown in Fig. 1b). Abnormal phenotypes primarily involved the nervous system but also included craniofacial, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, ocular, auditory, and genitourinary systems (shown in Fig. 1c).

Detection Rate of WES

In our study of 128 cases of NDDs, the overall diagnostic yield was 35.9% (46/128), with SNV/Indel analysis identifying 28 cases and WES-based CNV analysis identifying 18 cases (Table 1). Integrating SNV/Indel and CNV analyses significantly enhanced the diagnostic effectiveness of WES, compared to a conventional WES analysis pipeline that only considers SNV/Indel analysis (35.9% vs. 21.9%) (Table 1). The detection rate was higher in NDD patients analyzed through trio-WES compared to those analyzed through proband-only WES (37.3% vs. 30.8%) (Table 1). Among the 46 cases with positive findings, 34 displayed exclusively neurological abnormalities, whereas 12 cases also showed abnormalities in other systems, mainly affecting the craniofacial, ocular, muscular, auditory, cardiovascular, and genitourinary systems. Moreover, WES revealed a higher detection rate in NDD patients with additional systemic abnormalities compared to those with only neurological abnormalities (12/19, 63.2% vs. 34/109, 31.2%) (Table 1). The WES detection rate varied across different NDDs: GDD showed a 63.3% yield (19/30), IDD yielded 52.2% (35/67), DLD yielded 34.1% (15/44), CP yielded 33.3% (2/6), and ASD in 12 cases (28.6%, 12/42) (Table 2). Notably, all positive cases for ADHD and EP were all in the multiplex group.

Characteristics of the Pathogenic or LP Variants

Through routine SNV/Indel analysis of WES data, we identified 30 pathogenic or LP genetic variants across 29 unique genes in 28 cases. A significant majority of the identified pathogenic genes followed an autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance pattern (24/29, 82.8%), with 6.9% (2/29) being autosomal recessive (AR) and 10.3% (3/29) being X-linked (Table 3). The 30 identified variants included 13 nonsense, 9 frameshift, 5 missense, and 3 splicing variants (Table 3). Notably, 26 variants were classified as de novo, and additional 21 were novel variants not previously documented in the literature. A considerable number of the pathogenic genes were associated with transcription and transcriptional regulation processes (shown in Fig. 2a), with RNA and/or protein expression predominantly observed in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum (shown in Fig. 2b).

We identified 22 pathogenic or LP CNVs in 18 NDD cases through WES-based CNV analysis. These CNVs encompassed 15 deletions and 7 duplications, with sizes varying from a minimal single exon of 546 kb to a substantial segment of 27.92 Mb (Table 4). Among these, 18 CNVs were de novo, one was inherited from mothers diagnosed with ID, one was passed from a phenotypically normal mother, and the origins of two CNVs could not be determined. Analysis of the 46 positive cases highlighted recurrent involvement of ASXL3 (1537, 4740), SHANK3 (1486, 6350), and EHMT1 (1580, 1486), with different pathogenic variants in each gene, emphasizing the wide genetic spectrum of NDDs.

Illustrative Case Studies

In two specific instances, multiple genetic variants were linked to the patients’ clinical symptoms. In case 1748, a de novo nonsense variant in the SYNGAP1 gene, previously associated with mental retardation, autosomal dominant 5 [42], was found. This genetic alteration corresponded with the patient’s seizures and GDD. Additionally, two pathogenic variants in the DUOX2 gene were identified – one inherited from the mother and the other arising de novo – linking to AR thyroid dyshormonogenesis 6 (OMIM# 607200). The presence of hypothyroid symptoms in the patient suggested that the de novo variant was on the paternal chromosome.

In case 1537, a frameshift variant in the ASXL3 gene was identified. The clinical phenotypes of the proband included delayed speech and language development, cleft palate, horseshoe kidney, spastic CP, ear deformity, and feeding difficulties. Pathogenic variants in the ASXL3 gene have been associated with Bainbridge-Ropers syndrome [43], characterized by delayed psychomotor development, severe ID, limited or absent speech, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, poor growth, and distinct facial features. The clinical presentation of this patient closely matched the described syndrome. However, cleft palate and horseshoe kidney were not reported in this syndrome. Thus, we focused on a variant of unknown significance in the CHD7 gene, inherited from his father, who exhibits a horseshoe kidney. This variant may possibly explain the child’s cleft palate and horseshoe kidney, though its pathogenicity remains to be conclusively determined.

Cases Involving Variants Require Further Investigation

Beyond those cases with a definitive diagnosis, there were instances where genetic variations related to the patient’s symptoms were identified, yet lacked comprehensive evidence for pathogenicity or presented unusual inheritance patterns. For example, case 1150 displayed a pathogenic frameshift variant in the KDM5B gene, commonly associated with AR IDD [44]. However, the observed heterozygous de novo variation did not match the expected inheritance pattern for KDM5B, suggesting other undetected complex structural variants. Similarly, cases 1408, 1431, and 1385 revealed variants of uncertain clinical significance that aligned with the clinical presentations of the individuals, necessitating additional investigations to establish their pathogenicity.

Further Functional Validation for Case 9550

Patient 9550, a ten-year-old boy diagnosed with ASD, showed symptoms such as reduced eye contact, limited fine motor skills, repetitive behaviors, and difficulties in social interaction and communication. He was referred to the Eugenic Genetics Department of the Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital for genetic counseling due to his mother’s reproductive concerns. While CNV-seq testing did not reveal any pathogenic CNVs directly explaining the patient’s phenotype, trio-WES analysis identified a hemizygous c.562G>A variant in the NLGN3 gene, inherited from his mother (shown in Fig. 3a). This missense variant, which results in a substitution of glycine for arginine at position 188, has been confirmed by Sanger sequencing (shown in Fig. 3b). It was predicted to be deleterious by multiple bioinformatics tools (REVEL = 0.949) [33, 45], and it is absent in gnomAD v3.1.1 population database. According to the ACMG guidelines [34], it was classified as of unknown clinical significance (PP3_Strong + PM2_Supporting).

a, b The family pedigree affected by autism and the Sanger sequencing confirmed the c.562G>A variant in the NLGN3 gene.

The missense variant affects a glycine residue that is conserved across species, including humans, mice, elephants, and zebrafish (shown in Fig. 4a). Structural predictions using AlphaFold [46] indicate that replacing glycine with the larger arginine side chain will cause structural changes to the NLGN3 protein (shown in Fig. 4b). NLGN3 gene variants are distributed throughout the entire gene, and G188R is located in the extracellular domain of NLGN3 (shown in Fig. 4c).

The impact of the NLGN3 variant on protein function is analyzed using a comparison of the conservation of the mutated amino acids across different species (a), three-dimensional structural representations of both wild-type and mutant proteins (b), a detailed overview of the NLGN3 gene variant (c), and Western blot to examine the expression levels of both wild-type and mutant NLGN3 proteins (d). The glycosylation-modified mature and immature NLGN3 proteins are indicated by the top and bottom arrows, respectively, with MG132 acting as the proteasome inhibitor.

Western blot analysis showed that the G188R variant decreased the production of glycosylated mature NLGN3 proteins compared to the wild-type (shown in Fig. 4d). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 revealed that immature NLGN3 undergoes proteasome-mediated degradation (shown in Fig. 4d). These findings, combined with functional experiment outcomes, suggest the variant’s likely pathogenicity (PP3_Strong + PM2_Supporting + PS3_Supporting). After undergoing amniocentesis at 18 weeks for targeted gene testing at the NLGN3 c.562 locus, the fetus was found to have the wild-type genotype. The family later welcomed a healthy baby girl, who had been monitored for normal growth and development, reaching the age of four.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of WES for diagnosing NDDs when complemented by exome-based CNV analysis. Out of 128 NDD cases analyzed, the overall detection rate achieved was 35.9% (46/128), with 28 cases being identified through SNV/Indel analysis and 18 cases through exome-based CNV analysis. The integration of SNV/Indel and CNV analyses significantly improved the diagnostic capabilities of WES, increasing the detection rate from 21.9% with traditional SNV/Indel analysis alone to 35.9%. This highlights the value of incorporating CNV analysis into standard WES data processing, as it improves diagnostic outcomes and enriches our understanding of the genetic basis of NDDs.

Notably, WES demonstrated a higher diagnostic yield for NDD patients with additional systemic abnormalities compared to those with only neurological symptoms (63.2% vs. 31.2%). This finding is consistent with a meta-analysis that reported a 31% diagnostic yield for isolated NDD [7]. A recent study in China found a diagnostic yield of 50.3% for NDD patients with additional disorders [47], indicating that diagnostic rates can vary due to differences in cohort diversity, family structure, and the number of affected individuals. In our study, the diagnostic rates for different NDD subtypes were varied: 63.3% for GDD and 33.3% for CP. These rates were differential compared to previous studies, which reported 61% for GDD [48] and 24.5% for CP [49]. Our CNV detection rate of 14.1% is slightly lower than the 15%–20% typically seen with CNV-seq or chromosomal microarray analysis, reflecting the relative limitations of WES in detecting CNV. Given the wide range of genetic variations associated with NDDs, including chromosomal rearrangements, CNVs, SNVs/Indels, and other genetic anomalies, identifying an underlying molecular diagnosis remains challenging due to the heterogeneous nature of these disorders. Children with NDDs who test negative on exome sequencing may benefit from genome sequencing to identify intronic or regulatory variants, or from RNA sequencing to detect splicing variants.

The pathogenesis of NDDs is notably complex, marked by patients presenting genetic overlaps that result in diverse clinical manifestations [50, 51], alongside the occurrence of multiple de novo genetic variations within individual patients. This intricate genetic landscape means that NDDs can arise from anomalies in a single gene or through the cumulative effects of variants in several genes. In two cases, multiple genetic variants were linked to complex clinical symptoms. Case 1748 involved a de novo nonsense variant in the SYNGAP1 gene, linked to ID and seizures, and two pathogenic variants in the DUOX2 gene associated with thyroid dyshormonogenesis and hypothyroid symptoms. Case 1537 featured a frameshift variant in the ASXL3 gene, causing Bainbridge-Ropers syndrome, and a variant in the CHD7 gene, possibly explaining a horseshoe kidney. These cases highlight the diagnostic and treatment challenges posed by NDDs with mixed genetic profiles. Moreover, functional analyses are indispensable for assessing the implications of missense variations. The discovery of a novel missense variant in the NLGN3 gene in case 9550 poses challenges in assessing its pathogenicity, particularly as prenatal ultrasound falls short as a screening tool for NDDs. Thus, the evaluation and prenatal diagnosis of newly identified genetic variants are heavily reliant on the insights gained from functional experiments.

A genetic perspective enhances our understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying NDDs. Although many genes linked to NDDs remain unidentified, most known genes affect a few common molecular pathways crucial for brain development. Numerous genes implicated in NDDs function as transcriptional regulators or chromatin remodelers [1, 52]. In this study, genes such as NFIX, HDAC8, AUTS2, TCF4, ASXL3, CSNK2A1, TCF20, SATB2, KMT2A, SETD1B, PHF21A, MN1, ASH1L, FOXG1, ADNP, ZBTB18, and MED13L fall into this category. Given the broad spectrum of targets these proteins influence, disruptions in any one of these genes can lead to varied outcomes. As a result, individuals diagnosed with NDDs often present with a wide array of abnormalities. For instance, HDAC8 variants impact cell proliferation and differentiation, leading to IDs and other distinctive features [53], while AUTS2 variants cause IDD, ASD, and other syndromic features [54]. FOXG1 is vital for neural development, and disruptions in its regulation by SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 result in brain size reduction and impaired neurogenesis [55]. These disruptions contribute to the complex and diverse symptoms seen in NDDs, emphasizing the importance of these genetic pathways in brain development.

De novo variants are crucial in the etiology of NDDs [56, 57], and trio-WES is a highly effective method for identifying these variants in affected patients. In this study, we identified 44 de novo variants across 41 cases, including 26 SNV/Indels and 18 CNVs. Trio-WES facilitated the detection of 43 of these variants, with the remaining one confirmed through Sanger sequencing of parental DNA. The identification of de novo variants through trio-WES not only highlights potential novel pathogenic genes linked to NDDs but also expands the range of reference genes for more accurate variant interpretation [58–61]. Revisiting exome data with a focus on de novo variants is crucial for discovering novel candidate genes in patients who remain undiagnosed after initial analysis, thereby improving targeted genetic counseling and patient care. Additionally, determining the parental origin of de novo variants is sometimes imperative, especially for imprinted genes like those involved in Angelman syndrome (OMIM#105830). Angelman syndrome, caused by loss-of-function variants in the UBE3A gene located at the 15q11q13 locus, leads to severe developmental delays, limited speech, gait ataxia, distinctive facial features, microcephaly, and seizures [62]. Diagnosis adheres to established consensus criteria and includes mechanisms such as large maternal deletions, paternal uniparental disomy, imprinting defects, and UBE3A variants. For instance, in case 3068, a de novo pathogenic variant in the imprinted gene UBE3A was suspected to originate from the maternal chromosome, pending further verification to support an Angelman syndrome diagnosis. Similarly, in case 1748, two pathogenic variants in the DUOX2 gene associated with hypothyroidism were identified, with one variant inherited from the mother and the other de novo and presumed to be on the paternal chromosome. When de novo variants are detected, it is important to consider the possibility of parental reproductive mosaicism or somatic hypochimerism [63, 64]. For example, in case Z0001, a de novo variant was found in siblings, where the brother exhibited moderate IDD, DLD, slurred speech, myelin deficiency, and lower limb muscle weakness, while his sister showed similar symptoms but less severe symptoms due to early intervention. Given these findings, prenatal diagnosis may be required if the parents have another child.

This study has some limitations, including its focus on Chinese patients, which may not be globally representative. The complexity of data analysis and interpretation indicates that further optimization is needed. Future research should aim to expand the sample size, incorporating patients from diverse regions and ethnic backgrounds. Additionally, conducting functional studies on the identified variants will be crucial for accurately assessing their pathogenicity. Future efforts will also include integrating multi-omics approaches to achieve a more comprehensive genetic characterization of NDDs.

In conclusion, we conducted a retrospective analysis of WES data from 128 individuals diagnosed with NDDs. Our findings underscore the prevalence of variants in genes involved in transcription and transcriptional regulation among these cases, underscoring the utility of WES in identifying pathogenic variants. These efforts are instrumental in expanding our understanding of the pathogenic gene spectrum and deepening our insights into the genotype-phenotype associations as we continue to advance in this area of study.
