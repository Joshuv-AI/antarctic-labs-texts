Flap vs. Flapless Implant Placement in Immediate Functional Loading: A Comparative Analysis
Authors: Shailesh Jain, Sahba Hassan, Anu Sharma, Dinesh Kumar, Prashanti Wahengbam, Surabhi Duggal, Keshav Goyal, Shabnam Chaudhary
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:The aim of the current study was to evaluate crestal bone loss by utilizing flapless and flap implant placement procedures.Methods:For this investigation, a total of 60 patients who met the inclusion criteria were chosen. Following selection, patients were split evenly into two study groups: flap surgery and flapless surgery for implant placement. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for statistical analysis. The crestal bone loss was taken at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months.Result:The mean age of all study participants, who were males, was 42.53 ± 4.25 years. Statistically significant P values (P = 0.03) were attained on comparing the groups on the mesial side, while on the distal side, the results were not significant. The flapless method resulted in less bone loss.Conclusion:Compared to flap surgery, flapless implant implantation showed decreased crestal bone loss.

INTRODUCTION

Osseointegration, which is predicated on the interface growing between an implant and the bone, is necessary for effective implant implantation. In dental implantation, osseointegration is usually accomplished by a flap-based technique that requires suturing after the dental implants are positioned and reflects a soft tissue flap.[1] The primary issue is the reduction in supra-periosteal vascular supply, which results in the loss of alveolar crestal bone. In addition, a patient may experience pain in addition to discomfort during surgery, as well as postoperative bleeding or loss of blood.[23]

When it comes to the maintenance of the alveolar crestal bone’s height and the osseointegration process, the flapless technique has proven to be superior to the flap technique when it comes to implant placement.[4] The extended treatment period—typically lasting 6–14 months—and the ensuing discomfort to patients are the disadvantages of the delayed loading regimen.[5] As a result, several researchers are now focusing on the possibility that osseointegration might also be achieved with immediate loading. Crestal bone loss and implant survival rates differ significantly between immediate functionally loaded and nonfunctionally loaded implants, according to certain research.[6]

Within 48 hours of implant placement, an implant can be loaded with a provisional restoration (immediate loading).[7] There is a notable variation in implant survival rates and crestal bone loss between immediate functionally loaded and nonfunctionally loaded implants, according to certain studies. There is insufficient evidence about research conducted on humans. The purpose of this research is to assess and contrast variations in crestal bone height surrounding implants placed during flapless and flap procedures.

METHODOLOGY

This study is a prospective, one-center, cross-sectional investigation carried out by a single operator. We acquired informed consent from each individual. Sixty patients who desired to replace lost teeth were enrolled for flap and flapless implant insertion procedures in the posterior mandibular area. The subjects were split into two groups: group I comprised patients who underwent flapless surgery (n = 30), and group II comprised 30 subjects who underwent flap surgery (n = 30). The alveolar crest’s radiological and clinical examination served as a guide for implant placement. The following conditions had to be met for a subject to be considered for inclusion: the subject must be older than 18 years, there must be enough bone in the area near the crest, the area must also not have any undercuts that measured more than 15° on a radiograph, and there must be keratinized soft tissue that can be measured with William’s periodontal probe to be at least 5 mm. Individual risk factors and comorbidities of any kind were assessed, and research participation was excluded. In both groups, cases with type II bone type in the mandible were selected.

Preoperative evaluation

Complete patient histories and vital signs were determined prior to implant placement. Comprehensive oral prophylaxis was done. Prior to implant placement, data regarding risk factors that impact implant success were assessed. These included an older age group, smoking, periodontal disease, cause of tooth loss, edentulous duration, implants, and bone condition. Local risk factors included diabetes and cardiac problems.

Surgical procedure

Implant insertion using flap surgery

At the chosen edentulous site for implant insertion, 2% lignocaine and adrenalin were injected at a 1:100,000 ratio to provide the local anesthetic. To reflect a full-thickness flap and expose the site, a crestal surgical incision was made to put the implant using traditional flap surgery.

To indicate the location of the implant, a surgical stent was positioned on the alveolar crest. After that, the implant location was designated to make bleeding points and carry out the osteotomy’s first stage. Drill diameter was increased after using a pilot drill. After that, a last drill was utilized to the predetermined depth to produce an osteotomy site with precise measurements for every patient. These osteotomy sites received the placement of the chosen implants. After that, healing abutments were placed onto the implants to seal the opening site and allow them to heal faster. After that, the flap was closed tightly with non-resorbable 3-0 silk sutures after extensive irrigation. Following surgery, a week postoperative course of antibiotics and analgesics was administered to every patient in Group II.

Insertion of implants through flapless surgery

Group I patients followed a preoperative program that was comparable to that of group II patients. There was no flap elevation in patients in group I. The reference template served as a crucial manual for the flapless procedure and was utilized during implant site preparation. To use the flapless approach, a punch of tissue was taken out of the planned implant placement site, and a final osteotomy was then created by consecutive drilling. A soft tissue punch was used in each patient to indicate the site of the implant insertion after a surgical stent was implanted into their mouth cavity. After the surgical stent was removed, the soft tissue overlaying the implant site was removed using a soft tissue punch of the same size. The size of the drills was then increased after the usage of a pilot drill. The last drilling to the necessary depth to form an osteotomy site came next. These osteotomy holes were subsequently filled with carefully chosen implants.

Implant and prosthesis placement

In the end, all implants (in both groups) were placed just below the level of the alveolar crestal bone. Following implant placement using one of these methods, the implants were stored for osseointegration in accordance with early loading standards, which called for two months (for the mandible).

Observation

After the implants were placed, each patient was brought back for a radiological examination to assess any changes in the height of the crestal bone surrounding the implant at 3 months.

Statistical analysis

The Social Package for Statistical System (SPSS) version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to statistically evaluate the obtained results. A P value was deemed significant if it was less than 0.05. The difference between the baseline, 3 months, and 6 months in both groups was ascertained using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

RESULTS

The mean age of all study participants, who were males, was 42.53 ± 4.25 years. There were no statistically significant variations between the two groups’ mean ages (P = 0.387).

Out of the patients that were included in the study, four had to be removed from the study because of radiograph distortions, and six had to be pulled because they were unable to attend the follow-up appointments. As a result, implant placement was performed on 24 patients after flap surgery and on 26 patients without flap surgery [Figure 1]. Conversely, losses on the distal portions of both groups were noted to be 0.4 mm and 0.3 mm, respectively [Table 2]. DISCUSSION

Soft tissue flap reflection is a step in the “two-staged” flap-based clinical process that is typically followed while inserting dental implants. Over time, this surgical approach has shown great results. The primary disadvantage of implant insertion using flap surgery is the significant loss of alveolar crestal bone.[2]

A multitude of factors could be influencing alveolar crestal bone, which varies greatly from person to person. These variables could include loading considerations in addition to implant design, surgical technique, and prosthesis design.[8]

A gingival flap is not raised using the sophisticated technique known as “flapless implant surgery” to insert an implant. As the crestal bone area experiences the most stress when surrounding an implant, it is thought to be a key predictor of the health of the implant. Elevation of the mucoperiosteal flap has been observed to cause bone resorption because it reduces blood flow to the periosteum.[9] The literature does not, however, provide enough data to compare the crestal bone heights of the two surgical approaches. As the periosteum is removed, the crestal bone has a noticeable reduction in blood flow, which exacerbates bone loss and is observed in flap procedures.[10]

Gupta et al.[11] also found a higher decrease in crestal bone height in implants inserted when the mucoperiosteal flap was elevated, which is consistent with the results of our investigation. Either a small portion of soft tissue that may be needed to prepare an osteotomy site is punched out to reach the alveolar bone using the flapless surgical approach, or the osteotomy process is carried out directly through the soft tissue that is on top of it.[12] According to certain suggestions, limiting the inter-proximal loss of the crestal bone can be achieved using surgical methods that do not involve creating a mucoperiosteal flap.[13]

In the current study, flapless and typical flap surgery were used to put implants one at a time, followed by an early loading phase.[14] In implant therapy, early loading has been widely utilized, primarily in mandibles with good bone quality. The functional loading of implants after a healing period ranging from 6 weeks to 2 months is known as early implant loading.[15]

While there are certain benefits to traditional flap surgery, there are also some disadvantages. These include the surgeon’s inability to see important anatomical structures and landmarks, as well as an increased risk of implant placement errors related to depth or angulation.[16]

Large numbers of trials with identical functional loading procedures can be employed, on average, to compare flapless implant placement with traditional surgical intervention for an appropriate assessment of the numerous merits associated with the flapless technique.[17] According to the results of the current study, placing implants using the flapless technique and an early functional loading may be a better option than placing implants using the traditional flap technique and an early implant loading.

CONCLUSION

The results of this investigation showed a decrease in crestal bone height in both methods, which may have been caused by early loading. On the contrary, a statistically significant difference was found between the flapless approach and conservative flap surgery in terms of the amount of crestal bone height reduction.
