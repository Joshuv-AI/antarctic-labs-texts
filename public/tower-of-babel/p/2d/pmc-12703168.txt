Isolated oral CD30+/CD4+ CAR+ T cell lymphoma in long-term remission after radiotherapy
Authors: Evandro Bezerra, Ludmila Lupu, Marcos de Lima, Lena Pfaller, Alison Neal, Nelly R. Hajizadeh, Qiuhong Zhao, Martin Beibel, Catherine Chung, Myriam Lemmens, Austin Sim, Mevion Oertli, Timothy Voorhees, Rebecca Bohnert, Nathan Denlinger, Yoann Gilbart, Lara Bontadelli, Laura Monovich, Jennifer Willert, Daniel Stover, Jingbo Gao, Li Li, Stefan Reinker, Ulrike Naumann, Hans D. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)+ CD30+/CD4+ T cell lymphoma (TCL) manifested as a single oral ulceration 22 months after treatment with tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), an anti-CD19 CAR T cell-based therapy. The TCL showed lentiviral integration in ANKHD1-EIF4EBP3, loss of function of TET2, and NTRK1 copy number gain, suggesting that genetic alterations unrelated to insertional mutagenesis contributed to lymphomagenesis. The patient remains in remission 2 years after radiotherapy. This is the only CAR+TCL case reported at the Ohio State University James Comprehensive Cancer Center out of 288 patients treated with CAR-T cells.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape of hematologic malignancies and is currently being investigated in autoimmune diseases.1 Since 2017, six CAR-T products have been approved by health authorities, including the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency.2

Although CAR-T cell-based therapy has a well-described safety profile, concerns have been raised about the risk of development of secondary malignancies of T cell origin due to lentiviral-driven insertional oncogenesis. Lentiviral vectors are reportedly safe viruses as they tend to integrate preferentially into intergenic regions and introns.3 However, in rare cases, insertion into a cancer-associated gene might occur, as reported by Fraietta et al.4 and Shah et al.5 Interestingly, in both instances, clones carrying transgene integration in the cancer-associated gene became dominant but regressed spontaneously.4,5

Here, we describe and investigate a case of an early-stage CD30+/CD4+ CAR+ T cell monoclonal lymphoproliferation after tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), an anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy. Besides the expression of the CAR construct in T cell lymphoma (TCL), this case is notable for the isolated mucosal disease, lymphomatoid papulosis phenotype, and the reassuring long-term remission after radiotherapy.

Results

Patient details and clinical findings

A 71-year-old-male with a history of metastatic prostate cancer and non-muscle invasive bladder cancer both in remission, treated (before TCL diagnosis) with radiotherapy and hormone therapy and with transurethral removal of bladder tumor, respectively, received tisa-cel for relapsed/refractory CD10+ t(14;18)-rearranged diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), which was refractory to R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) and R-GDP (rituximab, gemcitabine, dexamethasone, and cisplatin) chemotherapy. Tisa-cel resulted in an ongoing complete remission of the DLBCL to date after over 4 years of follow-up and was only complicated by expected persistent B cell aplasia but no cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity, hematotoxicity, or recurrent/major infections. Twenty-two months after tisa-cel, the patient developed a painful ulceration (1.5 cm in length) on the hard palate (Figure 1A). A biopsy of the rim of the lesion showed a dense submucosal infiltrate of large lymphoma cells expressing CD4, CD5, CD30 (∼80%), and Ki67 (>95%) (Figure 1B). Lymphoma cells were CD3+, granzyme B+, negative for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and Epstein-Barr virus. Based on the pathology findings and clinical presentation, the patient was diagnosed with CD30+/CD4+ T cell lymphoproliferative disorder, consistent with TCL.6 Given the localized disease (stage IAE), the patient was treated with radiotherapy to a total dose of 2,400 cGy in 12 fractions,7 which resulted in ongoing complete remission of TCL after over 2 years of follow-up (Figure 1A).Figure 1T cell lymphoma clinical manifestation and histology(A) T cell lymphoma (TCL) on the hard palate before and after radiotherapy. Molecular findings

To better understand the pathophysiology of TCL, a molecular investigation was conducted on five samples collected at different time points: apheresis material used for CAR-T cell manufacturing (T-1); CAR-T cell final product (T0); peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from blood withdrawn 10 months after CAR-T cell infusion (T1); palate ulceration biopsy collected 22 months after CAR-T cell infusion (T2); and PBMCs isolated from blood collected 10 months after TCL resolution or approximately 32 months after CAR-T cell infusion (T3) (Figure 2A). PB collected at the same time point as the TCL biopsy (T2) was not available for investigation. T cell receptor β (TCRβ) analysis revealed varying levels of polyclonality in all analyzed samples, except in the TCL biopsy, where a dominant TCRβ rearrangement (V10-3, J2-3) was identified (Figure 2B). Tisa-cel is manufactured from autologous T cells transduced with a lentiviral construct (muCTL019) expressing a CAR against CD19. The quantification of muCAR19 transgene in the samples was performed using droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR). The TCL biopsy presented a vector copy number (VCN) of 0.64 viral copies/cell (Figure 2C). With estimated infiltration of T cells in tumor biopsy at ∼70%, the corrected VCN was ∼1 viral copy/cell, prompting lentiviral integration site analysis (LISA) by standard shearing extension primer tag selection/ligation-mediated PCR (S-EPTS/LM-PCR) to determine the exact integration sites (ISs) of muCTL019. In the final CAR-T product, LISA identified 16,285 ISs, indicating a highly polyclonal CAR-T population (Figure 2D). In the TCL biopsy, one dominant IS (>99.9% of the reads) was found in the first intron of the ANKHD1 gene, as part of the ANKHD1-EIF4EBP3 gene readthrough (location chr5:140430906). This IS was not detected in any other sample. ANKHD1, encoding a scaffold protein involved in cell survival, was observed to be upregulated in leukemias.8 Nevertheless, there is no evidence demonstrating a role for the ANKHD1 gene in lymphomagenesis and ANKHD1-EIF4EBP3 is not classified as a cancer-associated gene according to the Catalog of Somatic Mutation In Cancer (COSMIC).9 Therefore, it is unlikely that the integration of the CAR transgene into the ANKHD-EIF4EBP3 gene has played a major role in T cell malignant transformation.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of CAR+TCL lymphomagenesis, we performed genomic profiling via targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS). In the TCL biopsy, loss of one copy of the TET methylcytosine dioxygenase 2 gene (TET2) was found (Figure 2G). The other TET2 copy presented the missense mutation p.H1380Y (c.4138C>T, NM_001127208.3) at a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.39 (Figure 2E). TET2 p.H1380Y is a variant of unknown significance (VUS) but possibly pathogenic, as predicted in silico to cause the loss of zinc ion coordination in the catalytic domain. Using targeted error-corrected duplex sequencing (duplex-seq), which has a limit of detection of ∼0.008%,10 the TET2 p.H1380Y was also found in apheresis and final product samples at a frequency of 0.00078 and 0.00057, respectively (Figure 2F). TET2 p.H1380Y in one allele combined with the loss of the second allele might have resulted in TET2 functional knockout. TET2 is a known tumor suppressor gene, involved in DNA demethylation and cytokine expression in T cells.11 Furthermore, in the TCL biopsy, copy numbers of 3 and 4 were found for ETV1 (ETS variant transcription factor 1) and NTRK1 (neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor type 1) (Figure 2G), respectively. NTRK1 and ETV1 are involved in cell differentiation and proliferation.11,12 Mutations in NTRK1 and ETV1, including amplifications and translocations, have been found in many tumors.9,12,13 According to the COSMIC classification, both NTRK1 and ETV1 are categorized as tier 1, indicating that there is documented evidence that their mutations can promote oncogenic transformation.9 NTRK1 amplifications are reported as oncogenic driver mutations (NTRK1 | OncoKB, COSMIC).9,14 Gain-of-function fusions and amplifications of ETV1 have also been classified as oncogenic (ETV1 | OncoKB, COSMIC).9,14 TET2, NTRK1, and ETV1 copy numbers in the TCL biopsy were confirmed by ddPCR (see materials and methods). The genomic profile also revealed mutations in other genes: DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha gene (DNMT3A) p.D529_S535dup (c.1585_1605dup, NM_022552.5) was detected in all samples except in the final product (T0); SET domain containing 2, histone lysine methyltransferase gene (SETD2) (c.-97_-89del, NM_014159.7), and platelet-derived growth factor receptor A (PDGFRA) p.Ser876Arg (c.2628T>G, NM_006206.6:c.2628T>G) were found only in the TCL biopsy (T2) (Figure 2E).

Collectively, the data suggest the following timeline: a clonal population with the possibly pathogenic TET2 p.H1380Y was present at a low frequency (0.00078) in the apheresis material. During manufacturing, the CAR transgene integrated into the first intron of the ANKHD1 gene in this population, which did not gain a proliferative advantage as indicated by its still-lower frequency (0.00057). This subpopulation subsequently lost one copy of TET2 and gained two copies of NTRK1, becoming dominant in the palate lesion, where various factors may have contributed to its homing and proliferation (e.g., inflammation). Available data are not sufficient to establish precise timing or cause for the TET2 copy loss nor for the NTRK1 copy gain. The contribution of ETV1 is particularly ambiguous as it was already detected in apheresis material, and its role in T-lymphomagenesis is unclear. The plausible cause of lymphomagenesis in this case is a likely hypofunctional TET2, in combination with copy gain in the NTRK1 gene, spontaneously acquired after previous therapies or other unknown factors. The suspected pathogenic mutations in TET2 may be linked to clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential mutations, the rate of which increases with age15 or previous exposure to chemotherapy.16 TET2 inactivation alone could grant a proliferative advantage to the cells without inducing their malignant transformation. Such a case was reported for a chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patient treated with CD19-directed CAR-T therapy. The patient carried a germline mutation in TET2, while the other TET2 allele was disrupted by CAR transgene integration.4 The resulting CAR+ TET2 knockout clonal population proliferated and became dominant, but it regressed after CLL remission. However, in another patient treated with CD19-directed CAR-T therapy (tisa-cel), in which the biallelic TET2 mutations were not caused by CAR transgene integration, the clonal population evolved in a TCL, likely due to the presence of a co-existing mutation in DNMT3A.17 In our case, the mutation detected in DNMT3A is a VUS and the mutation in another methyltransferase (SETD2) is inferred to be benign based on to the high allele frequency in the population (accession ID: gnomAD v.4.1.0: 3-47164012-CGCGGCGGCG-C). However, besides the TET2 mutations, the tumor clone presented, with respect to the apheresis material, a copy number gain in the oncogene NTRK1. Of interest, to our knowledge, co-mutations in NTRK1 and TET2 have been documented in cBioportal in only one instance of peripheral TCL not otherwise specified. This patient exhibited a truncating mutation in TET2 and an amplification in NTRK1 (cBioPortal: P-0051735).

Discussion

Most of the reported CAR+TCLs have a resemblance beyond the molecular features. Two cases of TCL with primary cutaneous involvement,18 two cases with primary gastrointestinal involvement,19,20 and one case with mucocutaneous involvement21 suggest homing of CAR-T cells to areas of antigen presentation and chronic inflammation as being a feature of CAR+TCL. Interestingly, a similar trafficking mechanism of CAR-T cells to the gastrointestinal tract might occur in immune effector cell-associated enterocolitis.22

The incidence of CAR+TCL is estimated to be rare.2 In a retrospective analysis of 3,038 patients aged 30 years or younger, receiving CAR-T treatment for B cell malignancies, no CAR+TCLs were reported.23 In two similar studies of 724 adult and 783 adult and pediatric patients, a single CAR−TCL each was reported.24,25 At Ohio State University James Comprehensive Cancer Center (OSUCCC), from all CAR-T infusions for BCL (n = 288), the median follow-up among living patients was 3.0 years, and the cumulative incidence of CAR+ T cell malignancies at 2 years was 0.41% (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.05%–2.14%). From tisa-cel-infused patients (n = 119), the median follow-up of 3.4 years; with one single case of CAR+TCL, the cumulative incidence at 2 years was 0.92% (95% CI 0.08%–4.47%).

In summary, we presented a case of localized CAR+TCL in a patient treated with tisa-cel. Our data suggest that CAR integration played a negligible role in lymphomagenesis, whereas predisposing mutations may have driven the disease pathogenesis, as observed in TCLs of non-CAR-T cell-treated patients. Furthermore, the favorable outcome of the tisa-cel treatment, as observed in this patient, combined with the rarity of CAR+TCL, suggests a positive benefit-risk ratio of CAR-T therapy for treating patients with otherwise incurable B cell malignancies.

Materials and methods

Pathologic evaluation and immunostaining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections using monoclonal antibodies against various markers, including CD3, CD4, CD5, CD8, CD10, CD20, CD30, CD56, ALK, BCL2, BXL6, EMA, granzyme B, Ki67, MYC, PAX5, and TdT, as well as in situ hybridization (ISH) for Epstein-Barr Virus Early RNA. Staining was done on automated Leica Bond, Ventana Ultra, or Agilent Dako autostainers with peroxidase/diaminobenzidine detection at the OSU Wexner Medical Center clinical laboratory. A hematopathologist (D.J.) reviewed the sections, manually estimated marker positivity, and classified them according to the World Health Organization fifth edition schema.

TCL reporting

The TCL was diagnosed based on the clinical presentation, medical history, and pathology assessment of the TCL biopsy, performed at the OSUCCC. The pathology assessment included standard histology, IHC, and ISH staining, which were evaluated by a hematopathologist.

The treating physician reported the TCL case to Novartis Pharma AG via the US pharmacovigilance system. Upon notification, in accordance with internal procedures, the reporting physician was offered additional assays for causality assessment between CAR-T product and TCL. After the patient provided consent for evaluations in accordance with standard protocols approved by the Ohio State University Institutional Review Board, samples from PB and tumor biopsy were used for VCN measurements via ddPCR (Novartis), the result of which triggered LISA (ProtaGene), genome profile (Eurofins Genomics), error-corrected duplex-seq (Novartis), CNV by ddPCR (Novartis), and TCRβ repertoire analysis (iRepertoire). Reagents and methods used for these analyses are available to the scientific community as described in the respective paragraphs.

VCN

A TaqMan-based assay for muCTL019 (CAR lentiviral vector) was used to quantify viral copies via ddPCR in the CAR-T final product (T0), blood samples after CAR-T infusion (T1), TCL biopsy (T2), and blood sample after TCL radiotherapy (T3). Human p21 was used for diploid genome quantification. Custom-made TaqMan assays were ordered from Thermo Fisher. ddPCR was performed as previously described with 10 ng template DNA/replicate.17

CNV by ddPCR

Hydrolysis-based assays were used to quantify the copy number of TET2 (Bio-Rad, ID dHsaCP1000515), NTRK1 (Bio-Rad, ID dHsaCNS466622935), and ETV1 (Bio-Rad, ID dHsaCNS864159275) in the TCL sample.

Diploid genomes were measured using a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention RPP30 assay26: RPP30-probe: 5′-TTC TGA CCT GAA GGC TCT GCG CG-3′HEX; RPP30-forward: 5′-AGA TTT GGA CCT GCG AGC G-3′; RPP30-reverse: 5′-GAG CGG CTG TCT CCA CAA GT-3′.

ddPCR was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, a single ddPCR reaction contained 10 μL 2× ddPCR SuperMix for Probes (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 1863024), 1 ng template DNA, and two assays (one for the target gene, one for RPP30). The ddPCR reactions were pipetted, in duplicate, into a ddPCR 96-well plate (Bio-Rad), which was loaded in the QX200 Automated Droplet Generator (AutoDG, Bio-Rad). Standard PCR amplification was performed in a thermocycler (BioRad, My Cycler). Droplet positivity and counting were measured with a QX200 droplet reader (Bio-Rad). The average copy number per cell was calculated by dividing the target gene copies/μL by half of the RPP30 gene copies/μL. A factor of 2 is incorporated into the formula to account for the presence of two copies of RPP30 in each cell.

In the TCL biopsy, copy numbers of 1.3, 2.7, and 3.4 were measured, respectively, for TET2, ETV1, and NTRK1. As technical controls, copy numbers for TET2, ETV1, and NTRK1 were measured in a stable cell line (MCF10A) that carries two copies of TET2 and ETV1 and three copies of NTRK1.

CAR+TCL cumulative incidence at OSUCCC

The time to TCL was calculated from the date of CAR-T cell infusion to the date of TCL diagnosis or to the date of death or last follow-up for those without TCL. The cumulative incidence was estimated, treating death without TCL as a competing risk and censoring those alive without TCL at the last contact. STATA 18 (StataCorp) was used for this analysis.

LISA

S-EPTS/LM PCR followed by deep sequencing was used for lentiviral vector IS analysis. This technology has a lower limit of quantification corresponding to a clonal frequency of 0.05%.

The experiment was performed by ProtaGene GmbH with the following samples: CAR-T final product (T0), blood samples after CAR-T infusion (T1), TCL biopsy (T2), and blood sample after palate lesion therapy (T3). Briefly, 2,400 ng genomic DNA (gDNA) isolated from T0, T1, and T3 and 300 ng gDNA isolated from TCL biopsy (T2) were mechanically sheared to a length of about 500 bp using a Covaris M220 instrument. The sheared DNA, split into three replicates, was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Long terminal repeat-specific biotinylated primers were used for primer extension. The extension products were purified as described above, followed by magnetic capture of the biotinylated DNA and two washing steps.

The captured DNA was ligated to linker cassettes with molecular barcodes. The ligation product was amplified in a first exponential PCR using biotinylated vector- and linker cassette-specific primers. Biotinylated PCR products were magnetically captured and washed. Half of this eluate served as a template for amplification in a second exponential PCR step, allowing deep sequencing by MiSeq technology (Illumina) after purification. Preparation for deep sequencing was previously described.27,28 DNA double barcoding was applied to allow parallel sequencing of multiple samples in a single sequencing run while minimizing sample cross-contamination. ISs were identified using the GENE-IS tool suite. Biologically relevant clusters of integration, termed common ISs (CISs), were also identified. CISs are statistically unlikely to occur by chance and may either represent a hotspot for viral integration or suggest a selective advantage associated with clonal outgrowth. An arbitrary distance of 50 kb was used to consider two ISs connected in the same cluster.

TCRβ sequencing

TCRβ repertoire was performed using arm-PCR technology with Illumina Dual Index labeling by iRepertoire. gDNA from the five samples (T-1, T0, T1, T2, and T3) were amplified using a commercially available nested, multiplex primer mix covering the TCRβ chain (diHTBIvj, iRepertoire). Samples T-1 and T0 were concentrated by SPRIselect bead selection (Beckman Coulter). The theoretical nanogram input for sample T-1 was 637 ng and for sample T0, it was 714 ng. The actual nanogram input for sample T1 was 33 ng, for sample T2 31 ng, and for sample T3 868 ng. The listed nanogram inputs were added to each PCR1 reaction, and VDJ rearrangements were amplified using Promega GoTaq. The PCR1 products were selected and unused primer removed by SPRIselect bead selection (Beckman Coulter), followed by a second round of amplification performed with a pair of primers that are specific for communal sites engineered onto the 5′ end of the J- and V-primers used during PCR1. The final constructed library is ∼500 bp and includes Illumina sequencing adapters and a 6-nt internal barcode associated with the J-gene primer. All amplified libraries were multiplexed and pooled for sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 1000 platform using a 600-cycle kit and sequenced as 300 paired-end reads. The output of the immune receptor sequence covers within the first framework region through the beginning of the constant region, which includes CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3.

Genomic profiling and in silico prediction of single-nucleotide variant impact

Genomic profiling was conducted at Eurofins Genomics Europe Sequencing GmbH from gDNA of T-1, T0, T1, T2, and T3 samples, by using Eurofins INVIEW Oncoprofiling (728 genes, 81 microRNAs, 281 extra-exonic regions).

The impact of single-nucleotide variants was evaluated using the variable ensemble predictor (VEP Ensembl), with a combination of in silico predictors and the variant allelic frequencies across different databanks. The analysis was further supported by assessing quantitative trait loci associations with the variant (Open Targets). Additionally, where applicable, an examination of the effect of the variant on amino acid substitution and its implications in the protein structural context was performed.

Error-corrected duplex-seq

Duplex-seq was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions for the TwinStrand Biosciences AML-29 kit, covering 29 genes, including TET2. DNA input amounts and sequencing depth were calculated to achieve sufficient duplex depth for TET2 mutation detection in patient samples. In brief, 1,000 ng input DNA were used for time points T-1 and T0. We used 1,000 ng input DNA for negative control samples and 500 ng input DNA for technical control samples. DNA was enzymatically fragmented to ∼300 bp fragment length and proceeded to library preparation. After end repair and A tailing, duplex-seq unique molecular identifier adapters were ligated, damaged DNA removed through library conditioning, and the libraries amplified using sample specific indexing primers. Libraries underwent two rounds of hybrid capture using the AML-29 biotinylated 120-mer probes and final amplification with 6 PCR cycles. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 Illumina instrument with 151 bp paired end reads, with a minimum of 900 million reads per sample. Sequencing data were analyzed using the TwinStrand AML-29 app on DNA Nexus aligning to hs38DH for error correction through duplex consensus calling.
