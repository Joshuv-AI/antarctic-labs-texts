Diagnostic value of circulating tumor cells in gastric cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kang Yang, Peiwen Ouyang, Ke Gao, Zhaohui Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn order to find new biomarkers related to gastric cancer diagnosis, in recent years, some researchers have confirmed that circulating tumor cells (CTCs) can be used in the diagnosis of gastric cancer, but the results are inconsistent among studies. In this study, we used meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the diagnostic value of CTCs for gastric cancer.MethodsA comprehensive search was carried out using the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Ovid MEDLINE, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, Weipu Chinese database, Chinese Biomedical Literature (CBM) database, and Duxiu database. The aim of this extensive search was to find published studies that investigated the role of CTCs in the diagnosis of gastric cancer, especially in patients with both gastric and non-gastric cancer. The search spanned from the inception of each database to January 30, 2025. Subsequently, eligible studies underwent a stringent screening process. They were evaluated according to a set of pre-determined literature inclusion and exclusion criteria. The quality of the included literature was appraised using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) scale, a tool developed to assess the quality of studies in the area of cancer research. Relevant data were then extracted, and a meta-analysis of the included studies was conducted using MetaDiSc 1.4 and Stata 16.0 software.ResultsA total of 18 papers were included in this study, encompassing 1,399 gastric cancer patients and 750 non-gastric cancer controls. Meta-analysis indicated that the heterogeneity of the included papers was due to a non-threshold effect. Meta-regression analysis revealed that the identification of the test method might be the main source of heterogeneity. The random-effects model analysis showed that the combined sensitivity of the cell-based CTC test for diagnosing gastric cancer was 0.78 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.76–0.80], the combined specificity was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.94), the combined positive likelihood ratio (PLR) was 9.13 (95% CI: 6.36–13.12), the combined negative likelihood ratio (NLR) was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.18–0.33), and the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was 43.83 (95% CI: 27.12–70.84). The area under the summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curve (AUC) was 0.9274, and the Q* value was 0.8621. Regarding sensitivity analyses, the results of this study were relatively stable, and the diagnostic test Deeks funnel plot demonstrated that there was no publication bias in this study.ConclusionsCTCs have relatively high sensitivity, specificity and accuracy in the diagnosis of gastric cancer patients, which has certain value for the clinical diagnosis of gastric cancer.

Introduction

Currently, gastric cancer, one of the common malignant tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, ranks fourth in incidence and third in mortality among malignant tumors worldwide (1,2). In China, it is a high-incidence area, with its incidence ranking second and mortality ranking third among malignant tumors, posing a serious threat to human life and health (3,4). Most gastric cancer patients are not effectively diagnosed until the middle or late stages, missing the optimal treatment time and resulting in a poor prognosis (5). The 5-year survival rate of early-stage gastric cancer patients can reach over 90%, while that of advanced-stage patients is only 25% to 30% (6,7). Early-stage gastric cancer has non-obvious clinical symptoms, and diagnostic techniques mainly rely on endoscopy, pathology, imaging, and blood tumor markers. However, these methods all have certain limitations. Blood tumor markers have poor specificity and sensitivity; imaging has lag-time and radiation risks; and pathology is invasive and specimens are not easily obtained, making it difficult to dynamically assess the disease condition (8-10).

Ashworth first put forward the concept of peripheral circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in 1869. In recent years, CTCs have gradually demonstrated their value in the clinical diagnosis of various cancers and have become a potential new type of tumor diagnostic marker (11-13). CTCs are popular among patients and clinicians due to their non-invasive, reproducible, and simple characteristics. They are of positive significance for the early diagnosis of cancers, disease condition monitoring, and prognosis assessment. Although the diagnostic value of CTCs for gastric cancer has been reported in the literature, most studies have small sample sizes. Moreover, detection methods, tumor-staging thresholds, and other factors vary, leading to significant differences in study results, which are thus unconvincing (14-16).

To comprehensively evaluate the value of CTCs in the diagnosis of gastric cancer, this study conducted a comprehensive search of relevant domestic and foreign literature. We screened the literature strictly according to inclusion and exclusion criteria and used Meta-analysis for quantitative and comprehensive analyses. The aim is to comprehensively and objectively explore the overall diagnostic value of CTCs for gastric cancer, providing a theoretical basis for more clinical application studies. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA-DTA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-565/rc).

Methods

Registration and protocol

This study plan was registered on the INPLASY platform on 6 February 2025 (registration number: INPLASY202520034).

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Ovid MEDLINE, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, Weipu Chinese database, Chinese Biomedical Literature (CBM) database, and Duxiu database was carried out in both English and Chinese. Published studies regarding the diagnosis of CTCs in gastric cancer patients and non-gastric cancer controls were collected. The search spanned from the inception of each database to January 30, 2025. The literature search strategy was based on a combination of subject terms and free words. The English search terms included: “neoplastic cells, circulating”, “CTCs”, “circulating tumor cells”, “stomach neoplasms”, “stomach cancer”, “sensitivity and specificity”, “predictive”, etc. In Chinese, the search terms were: “循环肿瘤细胞”, “CTCs”, “胃肿瘤”, “胃癌”, “敏感性和特异性”, “诊断”. Relevant research literature was also retrieved through other methods.

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (I) the study was a diagnostic test of CTCs for gastric cancer diagnosis; (II) gastric cancer patients in the study had to be pathologically evaluated; (III) in the study population, the test group consisted of gastric cancer patients, and the control group was a non-gastric cancer group (including patients with non-gastric benign diseases and/or healthy individuals); and (IV) the study was publicly published and the full-text was accessible.

Exclusion criteria: (I) conference reports, reviews, commentaries, meta-analyses; (II) studies that were animal experiments; (III) studies from which valid outcome data could not be obtained; and (IV) studies repeatedly published by the same authors.

Literature screening and data extraction

EndNote X9 software was utilized to remove duplicates. Two evaluators independently screened the literature in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initially, they read the titles and abstracts to conduct an initial screening of the literature and exclude those not relevant to the diagnosis of gastric cancer by CTCs. After downloading the full-text, the literature was carefully read again to exclude those that did not match the study content. When the two evaluators had different opinions, they discussed to resolve the differences. If disagreement persisted, a third evaluator could make an adjudication to determine the final set of included literature. Excel was used to record and extract information. The extracted information mainly included: (I) basic information of the included studies, such as the study title, first author, publication year, and language; (II) basic patient information, including the CTCs isolation and enrichment method, CTCs identification and detection method, the number of cases in the gastric cancer group, the composition and number of cases in the control group, the diagnostic criteria for gastric cancer patients, and diagnostic thresholds; (III) the type of study design; and (IV) end-point indicators, including true-positive rate (TPR; sensitivity), false-positive rate (FPR; misdiagnosis rate), false-negative rate (FNR; missed-diagnosis rate), and true-negative rate (TNR; specificity).

Quality assessment

In this study, the quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool (17). This tool assesses applicability and risk of bias through aspects such as case selection, gold standard, trials to be evaluated, case flow, and progression. Seven items (four related to bias and three to applicability) from the included literature were evaluated. The risk of bias was categorized as ‘low’, ‘high’, or ‘uncertain’ based on whether the answers to the inclusion questions were ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘uncertain’, respectively. For applicability, only a comprehensive evaluation was carried out, with answers of ‘low risk’, ‘high risk’, or ‘uncertain’. It should be emphasized that ‘uncertainty’ was chosen in the evaluation only when there was incomplete information in the literature. In this study, two evaluators independently appraised the quality of the included literature. In case of any disagreement, it was resolved through mutual discussion or with the adjudication of a third party.

Statistical analysis

In this study, RevMan 5.3 software was utilized to comprehensively assess the quality of the included literature. Meta-analysis was conducted using MetaDiSc 1.4 and Stata 16.0 software.

First, to determine the presence of heterogeneity among the studies, the Cochran Q-test was employed. If P<0.05, it indicates the existence of heterogeneity. Additionally, I2 was used to quantitatively evaluate the degree of heterogeneity. I2 values range from 0 to 100%, with larger I² values signifying more substantial heterogeneity. If there is no heterogeneity among the studies, the fixed-effects model is used to combine the effect sizes. However, if the heterogeneity test shows that P<0.05 and/or I2>50%, it suggests significant heterogeneity among the studies, and a random-effects model should be used to combine the effect sizes (18). Threshold-effect heterogeneity is one of the main sources of heterogeneity in diagnostic studies. By plotting the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) effect size of the summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) scatter plot and calculating the Spearman correlation coefficients, we can determine whether there is a threshold effect. If there is no threshold effect, meta-regression is carried out to preliminarily explore the source of non-threshold-effect heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis is also performed simultaneously. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by removing the included studies one by one and then evaluating the impact of individual studies on the overall results. Deeks funnel plots were used to analyze the publication bias of the included studies, in order to assess the true reliability of the original studies.

Heterogeneity test

Threshold-effect heterogeneity test

The data were imported into MetaDiSc software for analysis. The Spearman correlation coefficient between the logarithm of sensitivity and the logarithm of (1 − specificity) was 0.146 (P=0.56>0.05), which was not statistically significant. This implies that there was no threshold-effect heterogeneity in this study. Simultaneously, a symmetric sROC curve was plotted, and it did not exhibit a ‘shoulder-arm shape’. This further indicated that there was no threshold-effect heterogeneity in this study (Figure 4).

Scatter plot of sROC analysis. AUC, area under the sROC curve; SE, standard error; sROC, summary receiver operating characteristic.

Non-threshold effect heterogeneity

The Cochran Q-test for the DOR resulted in Cochran Q=31.61, with (P<0.05) (Figure 5), indicating that there was heterogeneity due to non-threshold effect in this study. Moreover, the Cochran Q values for sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and DOR in the current study were 143.61, 44.93, 28.31, 169.35, and 31.61, respectively, all with (P<0.05). The corresponding (I2) values were 88.2%, 62.2%, 40%, 90%, and 46.2%, respectively. Therefore, the random-effects model was employed for the combination of these five effect sizes.

DOR forest plot. CI, confidence interval; DOR, diagnostic odds ratio.

Meta-regression analysis

The sources of heterogeneity resulting from the non-threshold effect in this study were thought to potentially be: (I) variations in the composition of control groups; (II) differences in the methods of CTCs isolation and enrichment; and (III) disparities in the methods of CTCs identification and detection. Meta-regression analysis was employed to preliminarily explore the source of heterogeneity. When (P<0.05), it can be deduced that a particular factor may be the source of heterogeneity. Here, different CTCs identification and detection methods (P=0.04) were found to potentially be the source of inter-study heterogeneity (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

The control group was divided into two groups: one consisting solely of healthy individuals; the other of non-gastric cancer patients, including those with benign gastric diseases, or a combination of healthy individuals and those with benign gastric diseases. There are two main methods for the isolation and enrichment of CTCs (33). One is based on morphology, including density-gradient centrifugation and membrane-filtration separation. The other is based on immunological isolation. The methods for the identification and detection of CTCs were categorized into nucleic-acid assays, with CTCs identified by nucleic-acid assays, RNA multiplex in-situ hybridization, and immunofluorescence in-situ hybridization. The three subgroup analyses presented in Table 3 were based on different CTCs isolation and enrichment methods (immunological and morphological isolation methods), different CTCs identification methods (nucleic-acid detection and cell counting), and the composition of the control group (healthy population and patients with benign gastric disease or a combination of healthy and those with benign gastric disease). Notably, the results indicated that the immunological separation method had better diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.92) compared to the morphological separation method (AUC: 0.91). There was inter-study heterogeneity within the morphological separation method group, which might be attributed to the inconsistent results caused by the varying morphology and size of CTCs cells. In contrast, the results of the immunological separation method were more stable. There was no heterogeneity within the subgroup analysis cell-counting method group, while there was heterogeneity in the nucleic-acid assay method. This could be related to the fact that fewer studies were included in the nucleic-acid assay method. Combining these results with those of the meta-regression analyses, it was suggested that the heterogeneity might be due to between-study heterogeneity, or that the heterogeneity within the nucleic-acid assay method group contributed to the overall study heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses of the control-group composition showed that using the healthy population as the control group was more accurate (AUC: 0.96) than using the non-healthy population (AUC: 0.91). The healthy-population group had no heterogeneity and was more stable. The non-healthy population group had obvious heterogeneity, which might be related to the status, disease type, and progression of the selected patients with benign gastric diseases. The analysis demonstrated that the CTCs test was more effective in healthy controls than in patients with benign gastric diseases, including those with gastric cancer, suggesting a higher FPR or FNR for CTCs in the diagnosis of benign gastric diseases.

Evaluation indicators for diagnostic tests

Due to the heterogeneity resulting from non-threshold effects among the included studies, a random-effects model was employed for the combined statistical analysis. The results indicated that the combined sensitivity of CTC in the diagnosis of gastric cancer was 0.78 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.76–0.80], the combined specificity was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.94), the combined PLR was 9.13 (95% CI: 6.36–13.12), the combined NLR was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.18–0.33), the DOR was 43.83 (95% CI: 27.12–70.84), and the total area under the sROC curve (AUC) was 0.9274 with a Q* value of 0.8621 (Figure 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted on the data of this study using STATA 16.0. The results were as follows: as can be clearly seen from Figure 6, there were four original studies, namely studies 3, 8, 12, and 18, that showed strong sensitivity. This was mainly because the sensitivity of these four studies was lower compared to that of all the combined studies. In particular, study 18 was the most prominent case. The reason for this, upon analysis, was related to the fact that the gastric cancer patients included in this study were human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative patients. This might be associated with the low risk of recurrence and metastasis in HER2-negative patients, resulting in a low CTC detection rate. The other original studies did not significantly affect the sensitivity of the calculated results. Overall, the results of this study are relatively stable.

Discussion

Gastric cancer, a common malignant tumor, is often detected at an advanced stage because of its lack of obvious early-stage clinical symptoms, which is one of the main reasons for its poor prognosis (37). Comparatively, the prognosis of early-stage gastric cancer patients after surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy, and other interventions is better than that of mid- and late-stage patients (38). Thus, early diagnosis of gastric cancer is of particular importance. Currently, the commonly used screening and diagnostic methods for digestive tract tumors in clinical practice include blood tumor markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9). Their diagnostic sensitivity and specificity are relatively low. These tumor markers are also clinically applied to the early screening of gastric cancer. However, since they can also increase in benign gastric diseases and can also rise in other malignant tumors, especially digestive tract tumors (39-41), a relatively high rate of missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis occurs. Imaging diagnosis lags behind, and pathological examination has low compliance, with specimens being difficult to obtain. Hence, traditional diagnostic methods have limitations. CTCs are cancer cells that circulate in the bloodstream, shed from the primary tumor or metastatic sites (either spontaneously or due to diagnostic or surgical procedures) (42). CTCs have the potential to initiate metastasis and are closely associated with cancer metastasis and disease progression, not only in advanced-stage tumor patients but also in those with early-stage tumors (43,44). Literature reports indicate that CTCs are detected positively in the peripheral blood of tumor patients before the formation of certain metastatic foci detectable by imaging (45,46). The discovery and application of CTCs provide a new clinical basis for the early diagnosis of gastric cancer. Nevertheless, the current methods for CTCs separation, enrichment, and identification are not standardized, and the diagnostic efficacy of CTCs in gastric cancer remains unclear.

To systematically and comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic value of CTCs for gastric cancer, this study was screened. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 18 papers, involving a total of 1,399 gastric cancer patients and 750 non-gastric cancer controls, were finally included. The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool. It was found that the risk of bias in the literature was low, and the clinical applicability was high. However, the studies did not specify whether there was an appropriate time interval between the test to be evaluated and the gold standard. The heterogeneity test shows that there is no heterogeneity caused by the threshold effect in this study, indicating that although the cut-off values of CTC detection are different in the included studies, it will not lead to differences in the sensitivity and specificity of CTCs in the diagnosis of gastric cancer. This may be related to the relatively low overall expression level of CTCs in the research subjects. Further analysis revealed that the heterogeneity in this study was caused by a non-threshold effect. Meta-regression and subgroup analysis suggested that different CTCs identification and detection methods might be the main source of heterogeneity, and that heterogeneity also existed in different isolation and enrichment methods as well as in different compositions of control groups. It is hypothesized that CTCs, as an emerging tumor marker for gastric cancer diagnosis, are still in the immature stage in terms of detection methods. Various techniques for CTCs isolation, enrichment, identification, and detection in gastric cancer are still under research, leading to heterogeneity among different techniques. On the other hand, it may be related to the stage of gastric cancer and the greater variability of CTCs. Due to the limited number of included literature in this study, in-depth analysis of all factors and a specific type of CTCs was not possible. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the original study by Zhu et al. (36) was more sensitive to this study. Analyzing the reasons, it was found that the gastric cancer patients included in that original study were all HER2-negative patients. This may be related to the low risk of recurrence and metastasis in HER2-negative patients, resulting in a low CTC detection rate. The other original studies did not cause sensitivity in the arithmetic results. The Deeks publication bias test showed that there was no publication bias in the literature included in the current study, making the study more reliable. This study used a random-effects model for combined statistics. The results showed that the combined sensitivity of CTCs for the diagnosis of gastric cancer was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76–0.80), indicating moderate sensitivity. The combined specificity was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.94), suggesting that the misdiagnosis rate of CTCs for gastric cancer diagnosis was 0.07<0.15, indicating that CTCs are a highly specific diagnostic factor. The combined PLR was 9.13 (95% CI: 6.36–13.12), and the combined NLR was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.18–0.33), indicating that CTCs have high diagnostic efficacy. The DOR measures the discriminatory ability of diagnostic methods; the larger the value, the better the discriminatory ability. In this study, the combined DOR was 43.83, suggesting that CTCs have high diagnostic value for gastric cancer. The AUC of CTCs for diagnosing gastric cancer was 0.9274, and the Q* value was 0.8621, indicating that CTCs have high diagnostic value and high diagnostic accuracy for gastric cancer. This study has the following limitations: (I) the literature included in this study was based on single-center studies, with a limited amount of literature and a small sample size, which affects the statistical validity of the results. (II) Only two of the included literature were clearly prospective studies, and the rest were retrospective studies. (III) None of the included studies specified the specific blood collection time for CTC detection, and there was no specific staging in the description of the cases in the gastric cancer group. In the future, there is still a need to include a larger number of original, multi-center, prospective studies to explore the clinical value of CTCs detection in gastric cancer.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that CTCs could potentially be regarded as reliable and accurate biomarkers for gastric cancer, and they can serve as a suitable tool for gastric cancer screening. However, for future clinical applications, there is a need to gather and enrich Meta-analysis data from large-scale multicenter prospective studies.
