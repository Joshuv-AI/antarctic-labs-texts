ER-resident CCDC134 safeguards TLR4 maturation by maintaining gp96 stability
Authors: Yang Bai, Chao Zhang, Hao Liu, Fan Deng, Zeyu Wu, Wanyan Deng, Zengzhang Zheng, Rui Min, Shenglin Mei, He Kang, Huiqing Yu, Youdong Pan, Judy Lieberman, Jingxia Zhao, Xing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceToll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a key mammalian pattern recognition receptor, mediates proinflammatory signaling in response to pathogenic stimuli. Here, we identify CCDC134 as a critical regulator of TLR4 biogenesis. Unlike its secreted form, the ER-lumenal CCDC134 stabilizes the TLR4 chaperone glycoprotein 96 (gp96), ensuring proper TLR4 folding and maturation. Our findings reveal a mechanism governing TLR4 processing and highlight CCDC134 as a potential therapeutic target for TLR4-driven inflammatory diseases.

As sentinels of the host defense system, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) respond to invading pathogens and mediate host innate immunity. Toll-like receptors (TLRs), a type of PRR, are expressed on the cell or endosome membrane and topologically sense extracellular or internalized insults to activate NF-κB-, MAPK-, or IRF3-mediated innate immune signaling (1–3). TLR1/2/4/5/6 reside on the plasma membrane to detect bacterial cell wall components or flagellin, whereas TLR3/7/8/9 are located in endolysosomes, where they bind to self-aberrant or microbe-derived nucleic acids (3). Proper folding, trafficking, and subcellular distribution of TLRs are prerequisites for their immune-sensing function. As type I transmembrane proteins, TLRs are synthesized on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and translocated to their final destination via the secretory pathway, which is facilitated by specific chaperones. The ER-resident chaperone glycoprotein 96 (gp96) and protein associated with Toll-like receptor 4 (PRAT4A) contribute to the proper folding and maturation of most TLRs (4–8). The multispanning membrane protein UNC93B1 has been reported to be critical for the endolysosomal translocation of nucleic acid-sensing TLRs as well as TLR5 (9–13). However, the processing, maturation, and trafficking mechanisms of plasma membrane-bound TLRs remain largely unknown.

TLR4, the first TLR family member identified in mammals (14), is responsible for mediating host anti-infection inflammatory and interferon responses and is also involved in the pathogenesis of various inflammation-related diseases and cancers (15–17). It is initially synthesized on the ER, where it undergoes critical posttranslational modifications (PTMs), including N-linked glycosylation and the formation of intramolecular disulfide bonds. These PTMs are essential for proper folding of TLR4 before it binds with the secreted chaperone protein myeloid differentiation factor-2 (MD-2) to form a functional complex (18–21). This heterodimeric complex is subsequently transported to the Golgi apparatus via the intracellular vesicular trafficking system, where it undergoes further glycan processing en route before ultimately reaching its final destination on the plasma membrane (22, 23). Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying TLR4 maturation in the ER and transportation to the Golgi remain unknown.

The coiled-coil domain-containing (CCDC) protein family represents a large group of structurally conserved proteins involved in diverse biological processes, including male/female reproduction, the DNA damage response, cell proliferation, and cytoskeletal dynamics (24). While certain members have been functionally characterized, the functions of many CCDC proteins remain poorly understood, particularly their potential roles in innate immune regulation. CCDC134 plays different roles depending on its cellular location; it can inhibit ERK and JNK signaling, increase the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells in a secretory manner (25, 26), and mediate the acetyltransferase activity of the p300/CBP-associated factor (PCAF) complex in the nucleus (27).

In this study, we used a CRISPR/Cas9 screen to identify CCDC134 as an essential and key molecular chaperone required for TLR4 maturation in the ER. This function of CCDC134 relies on its localization in the ER lumen but is independent of its secretion into the extracellular space. Deficiency of CCDC134 leads to aberrant N-glycosylation of gp96, which induces gp96 degradation and abolishes the binding of TLR4 with other cofactors or chaperones, including MD-2 and PRAT4A, thereby restricting TLR4 within the ER for subsequent ER-associated degradation. TLR4-mediated signaling was blocked in CCDC134-deficient macrophages. In summary, our study indicates that CCDC134 is a critical factor in facilitating the glycosylation and maturation of TLR4.

Results

Genome-Wide CRISPR Screen Reveals That CCDC134 Is Critical for NLRP3-Mediated Pyroptosis.

Initially, we performed an unbiased genome-wide CRISPR screen to identify factors involved in NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis induced by LPS plus nigericin treatment to elucidate the mechanism of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Cas9-expressing immortalized bone marrow–derived macrophages (iBMDMs) infected with lentiviruses encoding genome-wide sgRNAs were primed with LPS, followed by treatment with nigericin to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent pyroptosis (Fig. 1A). Surviving cells were retreated twice with LPS plus nigericin to isolate cells resistant to NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis, and these cells were harvested for genome sequencing to identify enriched sgRNAs that edit the targeted genes required for NLRP3 inflammasome activation. NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis-resistant cells were highly enriched with sgRNAs targeting genes encoding TLR4 signaling components (Fig. 1 B and C), which was expected because TLR4 signaling-mediated priming is required for NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In addition, the abundance of sgRNAs targeting the gene encoding CCDC134, a member of the CCDC protein family, was also significantly increased in NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis-resistant cells. Because CCDC134 is exceptional in the list of enriched sgRNAs we focused on CCDC134 to determine its putative role in inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. To verify the contribution of CCDC134 to inflammatory immune responses, we generated Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. Two clones with the Ccdc134 sequence targeted and edited by distinct sgRNAs were produced. As expected, treatment of wild-type (WT) iBMDMs with LPS plus nigericin resulted in rapid pyroptosis, as indicated by the release of intracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), whereas CCDC134-deficient cells exhibited significant resistance to LPS plus nigericin-induced pyroptosis (Fig. 1D). Similarly, caspase-1 maturation and GSDMD cleavage, which were detected in LPS plus nigericin-treated WT iBMDMs, were not detected in CCDC134-deficient cells (Fig. 1D). To determine whether CCDC134 is also involved in inflammasome-independent RIPK1-directed caspase-8-mediated pyroptosis, WT, and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs were treated with LPS plus the TAK1 inhibitor 5z7, after which cell death and GSDMD cleavage were assessed. In response to LPS plus 5z7, WT iBMDMs underwent marked pyroptosis with evidence of GSDMD cleavage and activation, whereas iBMDMs with Ccdc134 knocked out remained alive and expressed inactive GSDMD (Fig. 1E). In contrast, WT and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs died similarly upon stimulation with the NLRC4 inflammasome agonist anthrax protective antigen plus lethal factor-flagellin fusion protein (PA + LFn-Fla) (Fig. 1F). This finding is consistent with the comparable level of active GSDMD detected in both cell lines in the presence of PA + LFn-Fla (Fig. 1F), suggesting that CCDC134 is not involved in NLRC4 inflammasome activation. In addition, the induction of NLRP3 expression by LPS-triggered priming was significantly inhibited in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs (Fig. 1D). Thus, we speculated that, like other top gene hits identified in our initial screen, CCDC134 is also a key component of TLR4 signaling for the priming process rather than being directly involved in inflammasome assembly and caspase activation.

CCDC134 Is Essential for TLR4 Signaling.

To test our hypothesis that CCDC134 is required for TLR4 signaling, we performed high-throughput RNA sequencing to analyze the gene expression profiles of WT and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs treated with or without the TLR4 agonist LPS. As expected, LPS treatment of WT iBMDMs triggered severe inflammatory responses by inducing marked expression of a panel of inflammatory genes, which was mediated by the transcription factors NF-κB and IRF3, which are activated in response to LPS (Fig. 2 A and B). The induction of these inflammatory genes was dampened in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs stimulated with LPS (Fig. 2 A–C), while the LPS-induced inhibition of downstream genes was also blocked in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs (Fig. 2C). Global transcriptome analysis suggested that CCDC134 is essential for LPS-triggered TLR4 signaling to initiate the inflammatory response. The changes in gene expression patterns observed via RNA sequencing were further confirmed by quantitative PCR. The mRNA levels of several representative proinflammatory, interferon, and interferon stimulated genes (Il1a, Il1b, Il6, Il12b, ccl5, and ifnb) were rapidly and consistently increased in WT iBMDMs treated with LPS for different durations (2, 4, and 6 h), whereas these genes were barely induced in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs upon LPS treatment (Fig. 2D), which is consistent with gene expression profiling results obtained via RNA sequencing. As expected, the secretion of IL-1β upon LPS treatment was also abolished in the absence of CCDC134 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Moreover, the loss of CCDC134 led to defects in host inflammatory immune responses in macrophages infected with the gram-negative bacteria Salmonella typhimurium (SL1344) or Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PAO1), which suggests the key role of CCDC134 in host defense against infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Notably, the inhibitory effect of CCDC134 deficiency on TLR4-mediated signaling was comparable to that of Tlr4 knockout (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). These data suggest that, similar to Tlr4 knockout cells, CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs are unresponsive to LPS challenge. Unlike CCDC134, CCDC107 had no effect on LPS-triggered TLR4-dependent proinflammatory gene induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). In accordance with the inflammatory gene expression profile, LPS-induced nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65, a marker of NF-κB activation downstream of TLR4, was blocked when CCDC134 was absent (Fig. 2 E and F). To determine whether CCDC134 is specifically involved in TLR4 signaling, we also stimulated WT and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs with the TLR1/2 agonist Pam3CSK4 and measured TLR2-dependent proinflammatory gene expression by qPCR. WT and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs with the same proinflammatory gene expression profile responded similarly to Pam3CSK4 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Collectively, these data suggest that CCDC134 is required for TLR4-mediated proinflammatory signaling in response to LPS.

The Function of CCDC134 Is Dependent on Its Localization in the ER Lumen.

To dissect how CCDC134 is involved in TLR4 signaling, we first examined the subcellular localization of CCDC134 by cell fractionation and fluorescence microscopy. When iBMDMs expressing C-terminal Flag-tagged full-length CCDC134 or truncated CCDC134 lacking the N-terminal signal peptide (Δ1-26) were separated into cytosolic and membrane fractions and subjected to immunoblot analysis, the membrane fraction contained exclusively full-length CCDC134, whereas the cytosolic fraction contained CCDC134 Δ1-26 (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, a lower band at less than 25 kDa was detected only in the cytosolic fraction from iBMDMs expressing either full-length or Δ1-26 CCDC134 (Fig. 3A). Mutation of the methionine at position 38 in CCDC134 resulted in the loss of this lower band, and the truncated CCDC134 lacking the N-terminal fragment (Δ1-37) presented the same molecular weight as this lower band (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), suggesting that the truncated CCDC134 is a shorter variant translated starting from the second methionine of CCDC134 (M38). Consistent with the results of the cell fractionation analysis, fluorescence microscopy revealed that full-length CCDC134 was localized in the ER but not in other organelles, including endosomes, mitochondria, lysosomes, or the Golgi (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), whereas truncated CCDC134 Δ1-26 was not localized in the ER and was distributed throughout the cytosol (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Localization of CCDC134 in the ER aligns with its possession of an ER retention signal (QSEL) at the C terminus (28). Reexpression of WT CCDC134, but not CCDC134 Δ1-26, in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs restored the expression or secretion of proinflammatory cytokines in response to LPS treatment, suggesting the importance of the ER localization of CCDC134 for its role in TLR4 signaling (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). In addition, CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs reconstituted with CCDC134 M38A rather than CCDC134 Δ1-26 M38A or CCDC134 Δ1-37 presented an equivalent inflammatory immune response to WT iBMDMs upon LPS treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E), suggesting that the truncated CCDC134 variant (aa 37–229) has no effect on TLR4 signaling.

To further determine the localization and membrane topology of CCDC134, we performed a protease protection assay according to previous reports (29, 30). The resulting EGFP-tagged fusion protein-expressing iBMDMs were treated with digitonin, which selectively permeabilizes the plasma membrane but not the intracellular membrane of other organelles, to allow the release of soluble contents from the cytosol to the extracellular space, followed by incubation with trypsin to digest the cytosolic domains of membrane proteins (Fig. 3D). As expected, the fluorescent signals of cytosolic EGFP and calnexin tagged with EGFP at its C-terminal cytosolic domain in iBMDMs were lost after trypsin digestion, whereas the EGFP signal in the ER lumen was unaffected before and after trypsin treatment (Fig. 3E), which is consistent with their subcellular topology. Like that of the EGFP signals in the ER lumen, the EGFP signals of both CCDC134 with an EGFP tag at either its C terminus or N terminus (after its signal peptide 1–26 aa) were maintained after trypsin digestion (Fig. 3E), indicating that CCDC134 is a protein localized to the ER lumen. To verify whether the localization of CCDC134 in the ER lumen is sufficient for its regulation of TLR4 signaling, we generated an ER-lumenal and a Golgi-lumenal CCDC134 fusion protein by replacing the N-terminal signal peptide (1–26 aa) of CCDC134 with the signal peptide of the ER-lumenal protein FKBP7 (FKBP7-SP-CCDC134 Δ1–26aa) or the Golgi-lumenal protein SDF4 (SDF4-SP-CCDC134 Δ1–26aa) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and found that FKBP7-SP-CCDC134 Δ1–26aa colocalized with the ER and that SDF4-SP-CCDC134 Δ1–26aa overlapped with both the ER and the Golgi (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). Reexpression of either fusion protein in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs restored the induction of proinflammatory cytokines in response to LPS (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), suggesting that the signal peptide 1–26 aa of CCDC134 is required for the ER distribution of CCDC134 but is not involved in TLR4 signaling. Moreover, we generated additional chimeric CCDC134 proteins in which the lumenal or cytosolic domain of the ER membrane protein SHISA5 or the Golgi membrane protein LMAN2 was replaced with CCDC134 Δ1 to 26 aa (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). CCDC134-SHISA5 and CCDC134-LMAN2 chimeras were found to be exclusively colocalized with the ER and Golgi, respectively (Fig. 3 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Interestingly, reconstitution of the fusion membrane proteins with lumenal CCDC134 Δ1–26 aa instead of cytosolic CCDC134 Δ1–26 aa in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs restored the sensitivity of the cells to LPS (Fig. 3I and SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). Taken together, these data suggest that the lumenal localization but not the ER distribution of CCDC134 is critical for mediating TLR4 signaling.

The Role of CCDC134 in TLR4 Signaling Is Independent of Its Secretion Into the Extracellular Space.

CCDC134 has been reported to be a secretory protein that functions as a cytokine (25, 26). Thus, we determined whether iBMDMs expressing Flag-tagged CCDC134 release CCDC134 into the culture medium by immunoblot analysis (Fig. 4A). Whereas signal peptide-deficient CCDC134 Δ1-26 was detected only in cells, full-length CCDC134 was detected in culture supernatants, which is similar to the classical secretory cytokine IL-12 p40 released through the canonical ER–Golgi vesicular trafficking pathway (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the release of CCDC134 was completely blocked when the cells were treated with the exosome inhibitor GW4869, whereas GW4869 had no effect on IL-12 p40 secretion (Fig. 4A). These data suggest that CCDC134 can be exported to the extracellular space as exosome cargo instead of by the conventional protein secretion pathway. To test whether the role of CCDC134 in TLR4 signaling depends on its release, we pretreated WT iBMDMs with GW4869 followed by LPS challenge and found that blockade of CCDC134 release barely affected TLR4-mediated induction of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4B). Consistently, pretreatment of CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs with CCDC134-containing cell culture supernatants from WT iBMDMs or iBMDMs overexpressing CCDC134 did not restore the expression of proinflammatory cytokines in response to LPS treatment (Fig. 4C), indicating that the release of extracellular CCDC134 does not regulate TLR4 signaling.

The signal peptide of CCDC134 was predicted to be cleaved after Gly22, according to the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/Q8C7V8/entry#ptm_processing). To verify this, we introduced a double mutation (G22V, A23V) into the potential signal peptide cleavage site of CCDC134 M38A (blocking alternative translation from methionine 38) and found that the mature form of CCDC134 was lost when G22 and A23 were mutated (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), suggesting that the double mutation (G22V, A23V) limits CCDC134 to the precursor form and inhibits the removal of its signal peptide. It is believed that removal of the signal peptide is critical for the maturation and release of a secretory protein (31, 32). Nevertheless, reconstitution of the CCDC134 mutants G22V and A23V in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs fully restored TLR4-mediated cytokine expression in response to LPS (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), indicating that removal of the signal peptide is not required for the function of CCDC134. To further verify the critical region of CCDC134 that mediates the function of TLR4, we generated CCDC134 truncations according to its secondary structure and reconstituted them into Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). We found that reconstitution with CCDC134 truncations lacking 134–175 aa and the coiled-coil motif partially or fully restored the response to LPS in Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs, respectively, suggesting that the C-terminal region of CCDC134 is not as important as the N-terminal region for TLR4 signaling (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Taken together, these data suggest that the role of CCDC134 in TLR4 signaling is independent of its release into the extracellular space.

CCDC134 Deficiency Inhibits TLR4 Signaling by Limiting TLR4 Maturation.

To further elucidate which TLR4 signaling node CCDC134 targets and regulates, we initially overexpressed key adaptor proteins of the TLR4 signaling pathway, including MyD88, TRAF6, TAK1 + TAB1 and IKKβ, in HEK293T cells to activate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB, as measured by a luciferase reporter assay. CCDC134 deficiency had no effect on MyD88-, TRAF6-, TAK1 + TAB1- or IKKβ-induced NF-κB activation (Fig. 5A), implying that CCDC134 may function upstream of MyD88. Similarly, the key events of the TLR4 signaling pathway downstream of MyD88, including the phosphorylation of IRAK4, TAK1, and IκBα; the degradation of IκBα; and the nuclear translocation of p65 in response to LPS treatment, were all blocked in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs (Fig. 5 B–E), and were restored by the reconstitution of full-length CCDC134 but not CCDC134 Δ1-26 in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs (Fig. 5 B–E). These data indicate that CCDC134 regulates the function of the TLR4 signalosome. To substantiate these findings, we assessed the maturation and plasma membrane localization of TLR4 via immunoprecipitation of TLR4 from iBMDMs with biotinylated surface proteins (Fig. 5 F and G), and flow cytometry analysis of cell surface TLR4 (Fig. 5H). TLR4 was biotinylated in WT but not Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs, and the loss of TLR4 biotinylation caused by CCDC134 deficiency was abrogated by reexpressing full-length CCDC134 but not CCDC134 Δ1-26 (Fig. 5G), suggesting that ER-resident CCDC134 is required for cell surface expression of TLR4. Consistently, cell surface staining with anti-TLR4 did not detect plasma membrane TLR4 in CCDC134-deficient cells and CCDC134-deficient cells reconstituted with CCDC134 Δ1-26, in contrast to WT and CCDC134-deficient cells reconstituted with CCDC134 (Fig. 5H). Taken together, these data suggest that CCDC134 deficiency blocks the membrane trafficking of TLR4. In addition, CCDC134 depletion partially decreased the surface level of TLR2, whereas knockout of Prat4a, a chaperone that mediates the membrane expression of TLRs (6, 7, 33), completely inhibited the membrane trafficking of TLR2 (Fig. 5I), indicating some selectivity of CCDC134 on TLR4 maturation.

CCDC134 Mediates TLR4 Maturation in the ER Through gp96.

To further explore the regulatory mechanism by which CCDC134 affects TLR4 maturation, we examined the N-glycosylation of TLR4, a posttranslational modification critical for TLR4 function (18–20), to track its intracellular trafficking and processing in WT and CCDC134-deficient cells. The complex N-glycosylation of TLR4 in the Golgi apparatus, which can be digested by peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F) but not endoglycosidase H (Endo H), was lost in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs, whereas the precursor N-glycosylation pattern of TLR4, which forms in the ER and is sensitive to both PNGase F and Endo H, still existed in Ccdc134-knockout iBMDMs compared with their WT counterparts (Fig. 6A). Reexpression of full-length CCDC134, but not CCDC134 Δ1-26, in CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs restored TLR4 complex N-glycosylation (Fig. 6A). Subcellular fractionation also confirmed that TLR4 with precursor N-glycosylation was distributed only in the ER but not in the Golgi fractions of both WT and CCDC134-deficient cells (Fig. 6 B and C). In contrast, TLR4 with complex N-glycosylation was exclusively present in the Golgi fraction of WT iBMDMs but was absent in the Golgi fraction of CCDC134-deficient cells (Fig. 6 B and C), indicating that TLR4 processing in the ER and TLR4 trafficking from the ER to the Golgi apparatus are abrogated when CCDC134 is depleted. To determine how CCDC134 regulates TLR4 maturation in the ER, we immunoprecipitated TLR4 and analyzed the immunoprecipitates by mass spectrometry (MS). The abundance of multiple TLR4 chaperones or cofactors, including Ly96 (MD2), CD14, HSP90B1 (gp96), CNPY3 (PRAT4A), and CNPY4 (PRAT4B), markedly decreased in the TLR4 signalosome in CCDC134-deficient cells (Fig. 6D), which was confirmed by coimmunoprecipitation in WT and CCDC134-deficient iBMDMs coexpressing TLR4 with gp96, MD2, and PRAT4A (Fig. 6E). Intriguingly, the band corresponding to Flag-tagged gp96 from Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs shifted in comparison with that in WT cells (Fig. 6E). Consistently, a subpopulation of endogenous gp96 with a relatively high molecular weight appeared, and the expression of endogenous gp96 in general was markedly decreased in CCDC134-deficient cells and was restored by reexpression of WT CCDC134 (Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Moreover, reexpression of CCDC134 truncations, except the truncation lacking the coiled-coil domain (CCDC134 Δ196-218), in CCDC134-deficient cells restored the migration pattern of gp96, suggesting that the C-terminal coiled-coil region of CCDC134 was not required for its effect on gp96 (Fig. 6F). Previous studies revealed that gp96 hyperglycosylation with N-linked glycans converts it into a less active form with a higher molecular weight under certain circumstances (34, 35). Accordingly, we performed glycoproteomic analysis of WT and Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs by MS and found that the hyperglycosylation sites of gp96 (Asn445, Asn481, and Asn502) were supplemented with glycans in Ccdc134 knockout but not WT iBMDMs (Fig. 6G). Mutation of these three residues [N445Q-N481Q-N502Q, referred to as gp96 N4-6Q (35)] also blocked the shift in the molecular weight of gp96 in CCDC134-deficient cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). gp96 was reported to be a critical chaperone for proper TLR folding (36). Indeed, treatment of cells with the ER-associated protein degradation (ERAD) pathway inhibitor NMS-873, which blocks the clearance of unfolded or misfolded ER proteins, led to the accumulation of TLR4 in Ccdc134 knockout, but not WT, iBMDMs (Fig. 6H). These data suggest that CCDC134 is required for the processing and maturation of TLR4 in the ER by protecting gp96 from hyperglycosylation and maintaining its stability.

The formation of intramolecular disulfide bonds in the TLR4 extracellular domain has been reported to be a prerequisite for TLR folding and maturation (19). Given that the binding of TLR4 with protein disulfide isomerases (P4HB, PDIA3, PDIA4, and PDIA6) was affected in CCDC134-deficient cells (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), we also tested whether CCDC134 regulates the formation of disulfide bonds. Discussion

Our study reveals that CCDC134 is an ER-resident chaperone that modulates TLR4 maturation through the precise regulation of gp96 glycosylation. By maintaining gp96 in a folding-competent state, CCDC134 prevents aberrant hyperglycosylation of gp96 and ensures proper TLR4 trafficking to the cell surface—a process indispensable for its function in the inflammatory immune response. Consistent with our findings, emerging evidence indicates that CCDC134 facilitates the maturation of diverse receptors, including surface TLRs, endosomal TLRs, and LRP6, through mediating the stability of gp96 (37–39). The ER-localized activity of CCDC134 contrasts with prior reports of its extracellular role in cancer or its nuclear function in DNA repair (25, 40, 41), as blockade of its secretion has no effect on TLR4 signaling. Notably, the absence of a direct CCDC134‒gp96 interaction in iBMDMs suggests a noncanonical chaperone mechanism in which CCDC134 indirectly modulates gp96 activity, potentially through spatial coordination with auxiliary ER factors. These findings redefine CCDC134 as a compartment-specific gatekeeper of TLR4 biogenesis, expanding its functional repertoire beyond its role in previously described pathways.

The TLR4 maturation cascade involves a hierarchy of chaperones with distinct mechanistic roles. However, gp96 directly facilitates TLR4-MD-2 assembly (4). PRAT4A plays a regulatory role in TLR4 maturation and influences both its glycosylation and subsequent membrane localization by forming a complex with gp96 (6, 7, 36). Although the regulatory effects of CCDC134 and PRAT4A on TLR4 signaling depend on gp96, there are some differences in the roles of CCDC134 and PRAT4A. PRAT4A broadly regulates the maturation of TLR1, TLR2, TLR6, and TLR9 (7). In contrast, CCDC134 deficiency only slightly impaired the membrane trafficking of TLR2 and barely affected Pam3CSK4-induced TLR1/2 signaling (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Notably, our study revealed that the absence of CCDC134 attenuated the interaction between TLR4 and PRAT4A in iBMDMs (Fig. 6E), which suggested that CCDC134 operates upstream of PRAT4A to regulate TLR4 maturation.

In addition to sensing bacterial LPS and initiating an acute immune response to combat gram-negative bacterial infection, TLR4 also recognizes environmental allergens or fungal effectors to initiate type 2 inflammation and promote the progression of allergic airway inflammation or infection, exhibiting remarkable ligand plasticity and complicated roles in host inflammatory responses (42–45), positioning CCDC134 as a promising therapeutic target. Our data revealed that CCDC134 depletion impairs antibacterial responses while preserving TLR1/2-dependent immunity, suggesting that increasing ER-localized CCDC134 activity could selectively improve TLR4 function in the context of infection. In addition, TLR4 drives the transcriptional upregulation of genes encoding the sensor components of various canonical inflammasomes, such as NLRP3 or AIM2, which also contribute to anti-infectious responses or noninfectious inflammatory diseases (46–48). Given the importance of TLR4 in inflammation, targeting and manipulating TLR4 signaling represents a promising strategy for treating related inflammatory diseases. The FDA-approved drug disulfiram inhibited TLR4 activation by covalently modifying MD-2 and markedly improved pathological changes in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease (PD) (49). Antagonists such as Rhodobacter sphaeroides LPS (Rs-LPS) attenuate allergic bronchoconstriction in a preclinical model (43). These studies highlight the potential of TLR4 as a therapeutic target. The development of ER-targeted modulators could exploit compartmentalized roles of CCDC134 to achieve some pathway specificity.

While previous studies reported CCDC134 as a secretory protein involved in cancer and DNA repair, our work redefines it as a noncanonical ER chaperone for anti-infection immunity. This paradigm shift highlights the context-dependent roles of quality control factors. Future studies should address unresolved questions, including how CCDC134 selectively regulates TLR4 but not TLR2 despite shared gp96 dependence, and whether human CCDC134 variants contribute to immune disorders. Examining these mechanisms in humanized models and identifying the broader client repertoire of CCDC134 will help translate these insights into precision therapies that both control inflammation and promote host immunity—a critical goal for treating complex immune diseases.

Materials and Methods

Plasmids and Reagents.

Plasmids and reagents including antibodies, chemicals, and commercial assays are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. Primers used for cloning are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.

Bacterial Strains and Infection.

All the bacterial strains used in this research are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. For infection, Salmonella typhimurium (SL1344) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PAO1) were grown at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth. For the cell-based infection assay, overnight cultures of bacteria were inoculated into fresh media at a ratio of 1:50 and grown for another 4 h (Salmonella typhimurium) or 1 h (Pseudomonas aeruginosa). The bacteria were subsequently collected and washed with PBS before being added to the cell culture for infection at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI).

Cell Lines, Culture, and Treatment.

All the cell lines used in this research are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. HEK293T cells, mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), and mouse iBMDM cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1× GlutaMAX, 100 U/mL penicillin G, 100 µg/mL streptomycin sulfate and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Insect Sf9 and Hi5 cells were grown in ESF 921 serum-free media (Expression Systems) at 27 °C with 110 rpm orbital rotation. The cells were verified to be free of Mycoplasma contamination. Transient transfection of HEK293T cells was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable transfection and stimulation were performed as described in SI Appendix, Supplemental Materials and Methods.

Genome-Wide CRISPR/Cas9 Screen.

A genome-wide screen using CRISPR/Cas9 was performed as described previously (50, 51). In brief, two pooled mouse CRISPR half-libraries (GeCKO v2, addgene #1000000053) were mixed equally and cotransfected with packaging plasmids (pMD2.G and psPAX2) into HEK293T cells to generate lentiviruses containing the sgRNA library. The viral titer was quantified using the QuickTiter™ Lentivirus Titer Kit (Cell Biolabs, VPK-107). Subsequently, Cas9-expressing iBMDMs were infected with lentiviruses at an MOI of 0.3, and puromycin was added to remove uninfected cells. The transduced cells were treated with 1 μg/mL LPS plus 20 μM nigericin for three rounds. The cells that survived and untreated transduced cells (as the control sample) were harvested for sgRNA sequencing and enrichment analysis.

Targeted Gene Knockout by CRISPR/Cas9.

The cells were infected with lentiviruses carrying Cas9- and sgRNA-expressing cassettes (derived from LentiCRISPR v2-puro or CROPseq-EFS-SpCas9-P2A-blast), and selected with puromycin or blasticidin. Infected cells were cultured as single and isolated cells. The candidate gene knockout cell clones were verified by genomic DNA sequencing and immunoblot analysis. The sgRNA sequences used for targeting the genes of interest are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.

Cell Death Assay.

Cell death was measured via a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay on a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader with CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

qRT–PCR.

RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent and subjected to reverse transcription using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT–PCR was performed with Power SYBRGreen PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI QuantStudio™ 7 Flex instrument. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.

Measurement of Cytokine Levels.

The concentration of IL-1β in the cell culture supernatants was measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reporter Assay.

The reporter plasmids (κB-luciferase and pTK-Renilla) were cotransfected into HEK293T cells and luciferase activity was analyzed with Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) using a Synergy Plate Reader (BioTek). Renilla luciferase expression was used to normalize the transfection efficiency.

Immunoprecipitation Assay and Immunoblot Analysis.

The immunoprecipitation and immunoblot assays were performed as described in SI Appendix, Supplemental Materials and Methods. In brief, the cell lysates were incubated with indicated antibodies and protein A–G agarose beads at 4 °C. The beads were then washed for three times and boiled in SDS-loading buffer to obtain immunoprecipitated samples. For immunoblot analysis, protein samples were separated via SDS–PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore). The immunoblots were probed with the indicated antibodies and visualized using a chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For nonreducing gel electrophoresis, the immunoprecipitated samples were extracted from the beads with NuPAGE™ LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated via electrophoresis with NuPAGE™ 3 to 8% Tris-acetate mini protein gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Deglycosylation Assay.

The cells were washed with PBS, lysed with glycoprotein denaturing buffer (0.5% SDS, 40 mM DTT) and homogenized by ultrasonication. The cell lysates were then treated with endoglycosidase H (Endo Hf) or glycopeptidase F (PNGase F) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs) before being subjected to immunoblotting.

Cell-Surface Biotinylation Assay.

The cell-surface protein biotinylation labeling was performed as described in SI Appendix, Supplemental Materials and Methods. In brief, cells were washed with PBS, incubated with EZ-Link Sulfo NHS-LC-biotin reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min on ice and washed with 100 mM glycine to stop the biotinylation reaction. Then the cells were lysed for an immunoprecipitation assay with the EZview Red anti-HA affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich) as described above. Immunoprecipitated samples were subjected to immunoblot analysis to detect biotinylated proteins with a mouse monoclonal anti-biotin antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch).

Flow Cytometry.

Single-cell suspensions of iBMDMs in flow cytometry buffer (PBS with 2% fetal bovine serum) were stained with LIVE/DEAD™ aqua fluorescent reactive dye (Invitrogen) and blocked with the TruStain FcX™ (anti-mouse CD16/32) antibody (BioLegend) for 10 min before being subjected to cell surface protein staining with PE TLR4/MD-2 complex monoclonal antibodies, PE anti-mouse CD284 (TLR4) antibodies (BioLegend), PE anti-mouse CD282 (TLR2) antibodies or PE rat-IgG2a κ Isotypes (BioLegend). The stained cells were washed with PBS 3 times and then analyzed on the FACSCelesta™ Cell Analyzer (BD) with FlowJo software.

Recombinant Protein Expression and Purification.

Recombinant PA and LFn-Fla were produced by a Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previously (51). In brief, pFastBacDual plasmids carrying indicated protein sequences were transduced into the Escherichia coli EbacY strain to generate recombinant bacmid DNA. The bacmids were then transfected into Sf9 insect cells by ExpiFectamine Sf Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to produce P0 recombinant baculovirus. The medium containing P0 viruses was collected and applied to infect Sf9 cells for an extra two rounds to obtain high-titer baculovirus stock (P3). Hi5 insect cells were subsequently infected with P3 viruses before being harvested for protein purification using Ni-NTA agarose (QIAGEN), followed by Superdex 200 (10/300) gel-filtration chromatography and mono-Q ion exchange. All the purified recombinant proteins in the storage buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT) were stored at −80 °C for long-term use.

Immunostaining.

The cells seeded on coverslips were fixed with acetone at 4 °C or with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and then blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h before being stained with the indicated primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. The samples were then incubated with fluorescent-dye-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) (Sigma-Aldrich). The slides were mounted with aqueous mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were captured using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope at 20× magnification with Olympus cellSens Dimension software. Confocal images were captured using the Olympus SpinSR10 Confocal System at 63× magnification with Olympus cellSens Dimension software.

Cytoplasmic and Nuclear Component Extraction.

The cells were washed and resuspended in precooled PBS before being harvested by centrifugation at 400×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were then resuspended in buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl) and placed on ice for 15 min. The suspension was mixed with NP-40 at a final concentration of 0.1% (v/v), vortexed for 10 to 30 s and centrifuged at 4,000×g rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic extract. The pellet was washed with buffer A, centrifuged at 8,000×g rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1%(v/v) TritonX-100) and homogenized by ultrasonication to obtain the nuclear extract.

Cytosolic and Membrane Fractionation.

Adherent cells cultured in plates were washed with precooled PBS and incubated with digitonin permeabilization buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 42 μg/ml digitonin) on an orbital shaker at 100 rpm on ice for 10 min. The supernatants were collected as the cytosolic fraction. The remaining cells were washed with precooled PBS, resuspended in buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl) and placed on ice for 30 min. The cell suspensions were passed through a 25-gauge needle 5 times with a syringe and centrifuged at 800×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected as the membrane fraction.

Protein Secretion Analysis.

iBMDMs expressing the indicated proteins were pretreated with the exosome inhibitor GW4869 (MedChemExpress, 10 μM) for 3 h. The cell culture medium was then changed to Opti-MEM™ reduced serum medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific 31985088) containing the indicated inhibitors at the same concentrations. Six hours later, the medium was collected and mixed thoroughly with an equal volume of methanol and a quarter volume of chloroform. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 13,500×g rpm for 20 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded and the remaining pellets were washed with methanol and boiled with 2× SDS-loading buffer for immunoblot analysis.

Subcellular Fractionation of the Golgi and ER Compartments.

The fractionation assays were performed using a Golgi isolation kit (Sigma-Aldrich) and an ER isolation kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions as described in SI Appendix, Supplemental Materials and Methods. In brief, to isolate the Golgi apparatus, the cells were washed with PBS, lysed in hypotonic buffer and centrifuged at 2,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were supplemented with sucrose to a final concentration of 1.25 M for ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 3 h at 4 °C with indicated gradients (from bottom to top: 0.5 ml of 1.84 M sucrose, 1.5 mL of supernatant sample, 1 ml of 1.1 M sucrose, and 1 mL of 0.25 M sucrose). Golgi compartments were withdrawn from the interphase with 1.1 M/0.25 M sucrose.

For ER isolation, the cells were washed with PBS, lysed in hypotonic buffer and centrifuged at 1,000×g for 10 min and 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The 8 mM CaCl2 solution was added in a dropwise manner to the supernatant with constant stirring until the total volume reached 8.5 times the initial volume. The mixture was further stirred for 15 min at 4 °C before being subjected to centrifugation at 8,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellets were collected as the ER fraction.

Protease Protection Assay.

To determine the membrane topological features of CCDC134, a protease protection assay was performed according to protocols described in previous publications (29, 30). In brief, iBMDMs expressing the indicated EGFP-tagged proteins and lumenal-EGFP (EGFP fused with a signal peptide of the mouse ER-lumen protein FKBP7 at the N terminus and an HDEL motif at the C terminus) seeded in a 12-well plate were washed with KHM buffer (110 mM KCl; 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4; 2 mM MgCl2) and permeabilized with 20 μM digitonin (dissolved in KHM buffer) for 30 s. Then, the cells were washed with KHM buffer and treated with 6 mM trypsin (Aladdin) (dissolved in KHM buffer) for 30 s and washed with KHM buffer again to stop the digestion of trypsin. Images of the cells were captured before and after each treatment.

MS Analysis.

For TLR4-interatomic analysis, WT and Ccdc134 knockout iBMDMs stably expressing C-terminal HA-tagged TLR4 were subjected to immunoprecipitation with an α-HA antibody as described above and the enriched proteins were purified via SDS–PAGE. The proteins were extracted from the gel and loaded into an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer equipped with a Vanquish Neo UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The extraction of gel samples and analysis of MS data were performed as described in SI Appendix, Supplemental Materials and Methods. For glycoproteome analysis, the indicated cells were washed with PBS and lysed on ice with lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100 supplemented with complete Protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)]. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 15,000×g rpm for 15 min and the supernatants were collected for trypsin digestion and LC–MS/MS analysis.

Statistics.

Student’s t test (two-tailed) was used for the statistical analysis of two independent treatments. P values < 0.05 were considered significant. For heatmap visualization, rowwise Z score normalization was applied to the abundance of the indicated proteins to standardize comparisons across samples. Heatmaps were generated with TBtools software, with color gradients representing z scores (blue: below mean; red: above mean). The software used is listed in SI Appendix, Table S4.
