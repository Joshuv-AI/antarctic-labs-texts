The energy crisis differentially impacted Swiss and German citizens’ energy literacy and efficiency preferences but not their support for climate policies
Authors: Nina L Frings, Jessica F Helm, Ulf J J Hahnel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe energy crisis reshaped energy politics, resulted in energy price surges, increased energy capacity, and large-scale energy-saving campaigns. While an energy demand reduction was observed in Europe, little is known about how the crisis impacted citizens’ climate-relevant judgment and decisions. Here we report a preregistered two-wave cross-national study (N = 1040) spanning the winter of 2022/2023 to investigate how support for renewable energy policies, energy literacy, and energy-efficient product choices developed as this crisis unfolded. We couple longitudinal experimental data with real-world energy price data. This natural experiment indicates that energy-efficiency preferences were impacted by the salience of the crisis and real-world price signals. Changes in energy literacy were contingent on the numerical abilities of participants, suggesting that the crisis increased knowledge gaps between different groups in society. Support for renewable energy policies, and prioritization of key policy attributes, remained stable in Germany and Switzerland despite shifts in public attention and uncertainty.Subject terms: Climate-change policy, Psychology and behaviour, Energy and behaviour, Human behaviour

Introduction

Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 entailed not only a humanitarian crisis but also fundamental negative consequences for the environment. Military actions caused deforestation and wildfires, threatened biodiversity, and stoked fears of a radiation leakage1. In addition, the invasion had profound indirect impacts on the environment and society by reshaping energy supply and politics2,3. The delivery shortages and subsequent bans of oil imports from Russia led to unprecedented energy price spikes and spiraling inflation rates, which exacerbated energy vulnerability and poverty, as well as mounting energy security concerns in Europe4–7.

Additionally, the energy crisis increased tensions in the “energy trilemma”, the trade-off between energy sustainability, affordability, and security5. At the onset of the crisis, dependence on Russian gas imports was especially salient and underscored the urgent need to transition to a more independent and resilient European energy system3,8. During the fall and winter of 2022, as energy prices continued to surge amid supply uncertainty and inflation, the call for energy independence was joined by growing negative public sentiment and concerns over social unrest resulting from high energy prices9,10. This, in turn, prompted national and joint European emergency measures to reduce the burden of increasing energy prices for households and industries. This encompassed the implementation of energy price caps for private households in countries such as Germany11, where household energy prices were among the highest across European Union (EU) countries12,13.

In response to the new geopolitical reality and the resulting need to increase energy independence and security, political action for strengthened renewable energy policies has gained momentum in Europe14. The long-term success of ambitious energy policies, however, hinges on the public’s continued support15,16. Cross-sectional studies conducted immediately after the Russian invasion indicate stable or even increased public support for renewable energy policies in mid-202217,18. Public support, however, is volatile and can change rapidly when other societal issues require attention19. The Russian invasion not only increased energy dependence and security concerns, but also amplified other existing societal and geopolitical issues, such as inflation20 and military tensions21, aspects which could further divert public attention from sustainable climate and energy policies. Moreover, European governments themselves may have signaled decreased prioritization of renewables with some governments, including Germany, lifting restrictions on coal-fired electricity generation and expanding fossil gas import infrastructure2,22–24. In light of the multidimensional impact of the energy crisis, longitudinal data on public policy support is needed to better understand how support for renewable energy policies developed across this critical period, including phases of high and low public attention and uncertainty.

Apart from overall public policy support, little is known about how priorities assigned to fundamental policy attributes change across time within society. Energy security and dependence concerns, for instance, were particularly salient in fall 2022 when uncertainty about stable energy supply was high. Such concerns could affect how the public weighs key policy attributes, such as the policy’s impact on energy independence25. Another important aspect is the timing of policies: energy and climate policies are urgently needed, thus their implementation should not be delayed26,27. Public support for policies, however, may vary depending on the current economic situation and media attention to the topic. Furthermore, because the benefits of renewable energy policies are typically expected in the future, the urgency is not always recognized by the public and these benefits can be discounted28. In the present research, we examine how citizens value fundamental attributes of renewable policies, such as their relevance to achieving energy independence and reducing CO2, their implementation time, and associated costs at two critical time points during the crisis.

In addition to policy efforts aiming to improve energy affordability and increasing renewable energy supply, European governments aimed to increase energy security and reduce energy demand. Data shows that the energy crisis indeed had direct impacts on energy demand in the EU29,30. Key drivers of demand reduction include responses to actual or anticipated energy price increases, voluntary energy savings in response to energy-saving campaigns, and efficiency improvements29. The EU’s commitment to energy efficiency, emphasized by the “energy efficiency first” principle31, aims to reduce dependence on Russian fossil fuels and to address important societal issues such as energy sustainability, security, and affordability5,31,32. This strategy received widespread public support in 2022, with 87% backing associated measures33.

The potential for energy efficiency, depends on the implemented policies, technological developments, as well as on consumer choices. One factor that acts as a barrier to a more rapid and widespread adoption of energy-efficient products is the absence of energy price signals34,35. Research analyzing retail data shows that consumers are sensitive to local energy prices when making purchase decisions36. This tendency may be intensified when there are drastic price changes37 accompanied by high public attention, such as during the energy crisis. In addition to price signals, affective and cognitive inter-individual differences, such as environmental concerns and energy literacy are associated with energy-efficient purchases32,38.

During the energy crisis, many governments launched informational campaigns to promote energy efficiency and to curb energy demand and poverty through improved energy literacy, which tends be low among the public39–41. From a crisis as opportunity perspective, the energy crisis could indeed have acted as a window of opportunity42,43 as higher energy literacy is associated with energy conservation behavior39,44 and energy-efficient appliance choices45,46. Improving energy literacy could therefore have a two-fold beneficial effect both on energy curtailment and energy efficiency product choices. Encouragingly, intervention studies have shown some success in improving energy literacy across levels of numerical abilities46. This is important, since individuals who are better at processing numerical information (i.e., who have higher levels of numeracy) are more likely to have high energy literacy47. In summary, while previous research underlined the importance of energy literacy for making energy-efficient choices, there is a lack of research examining how energy literacy can change overall and for specific population groups across critical societal events.

Taken together, there is convincing evidence that the energy crisis has had widespread impacts on the economic, social, and energy system. However, there remains a substantial gap in our understanding of how the energy crisis and related events impacted public judgments and decisions, pivotal for the success of climate change mitigation and the energy transition. Here, we aim to give a holistic overview of outcomes pertinent to the energy sector and climate change that are likely to have been affected by the energy crisis: energy judgments as a relevant predictor of several climate-relevant behavioral outcomes, individual consumer behavior (i.e., energy-efficient product choices), and citizen behavior (i.e., supporting renewable energy policies). We report a pre-registered two-wave study in Switzerland and Germany investigating changes in these outcomes across the 2022–2023 energy crisis winter. Using experimental designs within two study waves – one each in fall 2022 and spring 2023 – showed that public support for renewable energy policies as well as priority assigned to specific policy attributes remained stable during this time of economic downturn and uncertainty. We further analyzed how energy literacy and preferences for energy efficiency in product choices were subject to temporal changes. To better understand how temporal changes in product efficiency preferences were moderated by real-world energy price increases, we coupled experimental preference data with fine-grained municipal-level data on energy prices in Switzerland. While energy-efficient product choices declined overall, there was no reduction in efficiency preferences for participants exposed to large real-world energy price increases. Improvements in energy literacy were contingent on the numerical abilities of participants, indicating that only a subgroup benefited from wide-spread governmental energy-saving campaigns.

Results

Assessing determinants of judgment and decision-making across time

We conducted a representative survey experiment in Germany and Switzerland (N = 1040) across two time points (November/December 2022 and March/April 2023), which included the same main tasks: renewable energy policy support, energy literacy, and product choices, as well as an assessment of individual differences. To assess policy support and prioritization of policy attributes at both time points, participants performed a 16-trial binary choice task, deciding whether to accept or reject a series of renewable energy policy proposals. Each policy proposal differed in its implementation time, CO2 reduction potential, energy independence potential, and projected tax increases (see Methods for specific attribute levels). To assess energy literacy, participants estimated the energy savings in kilowatt-hours (kWh) associated with eight different low- and high-impact household actions, which were combined into an energy literacy score. Energy efficiency product choices were assessed with a choice task in which participants made seven trade-off choices each involving two products with varying energy-efficiency and purchase price characteristics. In Switzerland, we coupled this experimental data with objective data about Swiss energy price changes at the municipal level from 2022 and 2023 to assess the impact of real-world energy price changes on energy-efficient product choices (average increase = 27%48). The Swiss energy market is non-liberalized, meaning households must receive their electricity supply from the local energy provider and municipality-wide energy prices are fixed for each year, which allowed us to examine energy-efficient product choices pre/post energy price changes in 2022/2023.

Shifts in public attention toward the crisis and energy prices

Media and internet search data in Switzerland and Germany showed that both media, and public attention toward the crisis decreased from November/December 2022 (Wave 1) to March/April 2023 (Wave 2). Swiss media articles referring to the term “energy crisis” reduced by 71.6 % between our two assessment waves (See Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3 for full information). In addition, fear (t(1039) = 2.41, p = 0.016) and anger (t(1039) = 3.29, p = 0.001) about the energy crisis, interest in the crisis (t(1039) = 2.79, p = 0.005), as well as energy expense concerns (t(1039) = 2.21, p = 0.028) significantly reduced in our sample between both time points, illustrating that not only attention but also emotional responses to the crisis attenuated between assessment waves (See Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Fig. 4 for analyses).

While attention towards the energy crisis decreased between our two assessment waves, energy prices significantly increased in Switzerland and Germany48,49. We therefore observe two main temporal differences between our assessment waves; a) decreases in attention towards the energy crisis and b) increases in energy prices. This circumstance can lead to different policy responses during critical events like the energy crisis (i.e. price caps implemented in Germany and most EU countries, but not in Switzerland) as well as structural differences, such as Switzerland having a non-liberalized energy market whereas next-to-all EU countries have liberalized their energy markets50. The non-liberalized Swiss energy market allowed us to examine the effects of municipality level energy price changes on energy efficiency preferences.

Support of renewable energy policies across time

We tested how policy attributes impacted policy support and how both, the importance assigned to policy attributes and overall policy support changed across the energy crisis winter with decreasing salience of the crisis in the public. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, in both waves all policy attributes predicted renewable energy policy support (confirming preregistered Hypothesis 1), with tax increases having the strongest influence (OR = 0.07, 95% CI [0.06; 0.08], p < 0.001), followed by policy implementation time (OR = 0.29, 95% CI [0.26; 0.34], p < 0.001), CO2 reduction potential (OR = 1.46, 95% CI [1.36; 1.56], p < 0.001), and energy independence potential (OR = 1.34, 95% CI [1.25; 1.43], p < 0.001). Overall support for renewable energy policies was stable across both waves (OR = 0.97, 95% CI [0.92; 1.03], p = 0.387). Also, the importance participants assigned to specific policy attributes remained stable, including the importance assigned to the policies’ potential to increase energy independence (OR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.83; 1.06], p = 0.338); not confirming preregistered Hypothesis 1a). This shows that public support for renewable energy policies remained high, especially under moderate policy costs and rapid policy implementation time (Fig. 1b), even when the public paid less attention to the energy crisis (see Supplementary Note 2 for a detailed analysis of public attention).

In both waves, inter-individual differences in climate change concern were positively associated with support for policies (OR = 2.02, 95% CI [1.85;2.20], p < 0.001; confirming preregistered Hypothesis 1b) and higher individual weights assigned to the CO2 reduction potential of the policies (b = 0.11, 95% CI [0.09; 0.12], p < 0.001; confirming preregistered Hypothesis 1c). Accuracy of energy judgments across time

We then analyzed how participants’ judgments about energy-saving potential (i.e., their action energy literacy39) changed across the 2022/2023 winter, examining the extent to which higher energy prices and information campaigns during the energy crisis may have motivated participants’ to increase their action energy literacy. While considering action impact in their judgments (confirming Hypothesis 2; see Supplementary Note 4 for more information,) participants underestimated high impact actions and overestimated low impact actions in both waves (see Fig. 3a). On average, low impact actions were overestimated by 250% (95% CI [246%; 256%]) and 259% (95% CI [255%; 264%]) in Wave 1 and Wave 2, respectively. High impact actions were underestimated by 29% (95% CI [26%; 31%]) and 23% (95% CI [20%; 25%]) in Wave 1 and Wave 2, respectively. Accordingly, biased estimations were driven by the impact of the action (b = 0.78, 95% CI [0.74; 0.82], p < 0.001). There was no overall improvement in judgment accuracy between waves (t(979) = 0.64, 95% CI [−0.02; 0.05], p = 0.523; not confirming preregistered Hypothesis 3a2).

Additional exploratory analyses indicated that changes in literacy over time were subject to participants’ numeracy levels (see Fig. 3b; b = 0.05, 95% CI [0.00; 0.09], p = 0.030). As depicted in Fig. 3b, this pattern was significant in Switzerland (b = 0.09, 95% CI [0.03; 0.15], p = 0.004) where the government implemented large-scale energy-saving campaigns at the national level, but not in Germany (b = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.05; 0.07], p = 0.740), where campaigns were less orchestrated at the national level. Swiss data shows that the gap in energy literacy between participants with high and low numerical abilities increased in Wave 2 relative to Wave 1. In accordance, for individuals with low numeracy energy literacy significantly decreased (b = 0.04, SE = 0.02, p = 0.008), whereas individuals with high numeracy maintained constant levels of literacy (b = −0.05, SE = 0.03, p = 0.077). Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, these findings should, however, be interpreted with caution. Finally, frequency of use of household appliances was not associated with judgment error (b = 0.00, 95% CI [−0.03; 0.03], p = 0.873; not confirming preregistered Hypothesis 3a1). Confidence in estimates in contrast showed a u-shaped association with energy literacy (see Hypothesis 3b, b = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01; 0.03], p < 0.001), indicating that both, high and low confidence was associated with higher estimation errors. See Supplementary Tables 9–14 for complete statistics.

Energy-efficient product choices across time

Next, we tested the extent to which energy-efficient product choices were predicted by individual differences in energy literacy. Findings show that individuals with higher energy literacy were more likely to choose energy-efficient products across both waves (OR = 0.84, 95% CI [0.77; 0.92], p = <0.001). This effect was qualified by a significant interaction of energy literacy and the energy efficiency attributes of the products (OR = 1.41, 95% CI [1.28; 1.55], p < 0.001, confirming preregistered Hypothesis 4a). As depicted in Fig. 4a, individuals with higher levels of energy literacy (i.e., lower judgment error = −1 SD) strongly favored the more energy-efficient product particularly when the energy efficiency difference between the products was high (78%), but less when the products’ energy efficiency differed only moderately (42%). Conversely, participants with lower levels of energy literacy (i.e., higher judgment error = +1 SD) displayed a reduced responsiveness to high and low energy efficiency product differences when choosing products (72% and 50% respectively).

Finally, individuals with higher levels of climate change concern were more likely to choose the more energy-efficient product (OR = 1.51, 95% CI [1.35; 1.68], p < 0.001, confirming pre-registered Hypothesis 4). This positive relationship was more pronounced when the difference in energy efficiency between products was high (see Fig. 4b OR = 0.84, 95% CI [0.76; 0.92, p < 0.001).

Lastly, we tested whether preferences for energy efficiency changed across the 2022–2023 energy crisis winter as a function of salience of the crisis and increased energy prices. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, overall levels of energy-efficient choices show a statistically significant decline in both countries (OR = 0.79, 95% CI [0.73; 0.86], p < 0.001). We subsequently analyzed the Swiss sample to determine whether real-world price changes moderated temporal changes in energy-efficient product choices. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the decline in energy-efficient choices from Wave 1 to Wave 2 was lessened by the effect of real-world price increases (X2 (1, N = 409) = 4.938, p = 0.026; confirming preregistered Hypothesis 5). Specifically, individuals facing the highest energy price increases did not exhibit a decrease in energy-efficient choices (slope for +1 SD price difference: b = −0.18, SE = 0.10, p = 0.067). In contrast, there was a statistically significant decline in preferences for energy efficiency among those exposed to relatively modest energy price increases (slope for −1 SD price difference: b = −0.29, SE = 0.09, p = 0.002). The results remained stable when controlling for 2022 energy price levels (X2 (1, N = 409) = 8.053, p = 0.005).

Discussion

In the present research, we provide a comprehensive picture of the factors influencing energy-relevant judgment and decisions across the 2022/2023 energy crisis winter. We analyzed two assessment waves, capturing periods of high and low public attention towards the energy crisis, and increasing energy prices. We find that public support for renewable energy policies remained stable and that preferences for key policy attributes, such as energy independence, exerted consistent and significant influences on policy support across times with differing public attention. In contrast, preferences for energy-efficient products decreased in both countries from winter 2022 to spring 2023, corresponding with a decline in public attention to the energy crisis across this period (see Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4). By coupling experimental survey data with real-world energy price data in Switzerland we found that the reduction in efficiency preferences was only evident among individuals experiencing modest price increases. Those facing substantial energy price increases continued to exhibit high preferences for energy efficiency. Our analysis further revealed that action energy literacy, the ability to accurately judge the energy-saving potential of household actions, is an underlying driver of energy-efficient product choices. Between the two waves, gaps in energy literacy among individuals with low and high numerical abilities remained in Germany and even intensified in Switzerland. Altogether, these results illustrate how preferences and energy-efficient choices are based on an interplay between contextual conditions and inter-individual preferences and abilities.

In terms of policy support, we corroborate recent cross-sectional research on societal crises, showing that economic downturns do not necessarily equate with lowered climate change concern or diminished policy support17,18,51–53. While some of these studies observed an increase in policy support, we find that support for renewable policies remained stable, which may be due to methodological differences in assessing policy support. Most recent studies used cross-sectional designs and self-report questionnaires to measure policy support17,18, including retrospective questions about participants’ perceptions of how their climate policy support has changed in the past. To the best of our knowledge, the only comparable multi-wave longitudinal study in this context finds that support for increased governmental investments in renewable energy is stable across March 2022 to January 2023. We not only assessed policy support at two critical time points, but also used within-subject experimental paradigms to assess climate policy support as a function of key policy attributes.

Extending previous findings on the implementation timing of climate policies28, we find that the importance assigned to rapid policy action depended on individual levels of climate change concern, showing that individuals with high concern particularly supported policies that are implemented rapidly, whereas individuals with low concern were more likely to equally support rapid and delayed action. The latter tendency may be symbolic of a wait-and-see approach54,55, whereas those more concerned with climate change may have realized that rapid policy action is necessary and therefore support immediate policies. This support of rapid action aligns with robust evidence from the natural sciences that delayed climate action may have drastic consequences56. Climate disinformation strategies have shifted from denying the existence of climate change to employing so-called delay tactics, aiming to decelerate systematic mitigation actions57.

While policy support remained stable, we observed an overall decline in energy-efficient product choices in Germany and Switzerland, an effect that could be attributed to a higher salience of the energy crisis and energy efficiency measures in winter 2022 compared to spring 2023. This interpretation is supported by fundamental research in psychology, showing that human decision-making is strongly impacted by the availability and salience of decision-relevant information at the time of decision-making58,59. It is furthermore consistent with previous research indicating that the limited attention of consumers to energy efficiency stems in part from the lack of its relevance in everyday life, which likely changed at the beginning of the energy crisis60. Therefore, the energy efficiency attribute in our product choice task may have received more attention and weight in the decision process during times of high public attention to the crisis. This encompasses elevated energy expense concerns and overall interest in, and fear of the energy crisis in Wave 1 compared to the subsequent assessment Wave 2.

Although a pronounced reliance on salient information contradicts models of rational decision-making that assume equal consideration of all information61,62, other research has reframed this phenomenon as “rational inattentiveness”34,63. That is, not paying attention to less salient attributes such as energy costs or energy efficiency, can be considered rational, since this strategy streamlines the decision process when strong preferences for other attributes (i.e. high purchase prices) are present. For example, small differences in energy efficiency between products may be ignored when making decisions, since the cognitive effort required to accurately evaluate each product’s value based on all attributes is more costly than making a decision based on incomplete information63,64.

Our findings furthermore demonstrate that respondents also exhibited economic rationality by actively incorporating real-world energy price increases in their decisions. This suggests that energy-efficient choices are shaped by objectively measurable price signals as well as the salience of information available to the public. Both factors can be steered by policy-making, through targeted awareness campaigns about energy efficiency as well as through economic instruments. In January 2023, the German government introduced measures to cap energy price increases equally across societal groups, at a time point following our first assessment wave. While this measure reduced financial pressure on households across the socioeconomic spectrum, it simultaneously attenuated the impact of energy price signals on consumer purchase decisions, including those within the upper income range. More targeted, income-specific price caps would serve the dual purpose of sustaining price signals that encourage energy-efficient purchases while simultaneously protecting low-income households from high energy costs65,66. The benefit of sustaining price signals is also highlighted in recent analyses, arguing that ideally only the most vulnerable households should be targeted by relief measures as they are burdened most by rising energy prices67.

In both countries, our analysis of energy literacy revealed substantial knowledge gaps as the effectiveness of high impact actions was underestimated, while low impact actions were overestimated46,68. This pattern of misperception is suboptimal considering that individuals must prioritize which energy-saving actions they pursue in everyday life and are prone to choose actions with limited effectiveness. Our analysis showed no significant improvements in energy literacy across waves despite substantial governmental action in both countries. Explorative analyses even provide tentative evidence that gaps in energy literacy between certain population groups intensified. In the Swiss sample individuals with low numerical abilities ineffectively adjusted their estimates of the energy-saving potential of most actions in Wave 2 relative to Wave 1, despite the widespread visibility of the governmental campaign (56% of participants indicated having seen the Swiss energy saving campaign). One potential explanation for this finding is the varying capability of individuals to process numerical information: previous research has shown that individuals with lower levels of numerical abilities are more affected by the framing of numerical information than those with high numerical abilities. Therefore, presenting numerical information in formats that enable individuals with varying degrees of numerical abilities to make accurate inferences (e.g. correctly inferring the magnitude of energy saving potential relative to other actions), is of particular importance. This could include presenting the energy saving potential of each action relative to overall household energy consumption (rather than relative to different baseline behaviors), or presenting energy savings in absolute terms to better convey the overall impact of each action. These findings require further attention by future longitudinal research to examine the conditions under which governmental information campaigns can improve levels of energy literacy in different population groups.

Our study extends previous cross-sectional findings on the impact of societal crises on sustainable judgment and decision-making, but some limitations are worth noting. We acknowledge, that while two waves improve upon cross-sectional research, they remain insufficient for drawing causal conclusions. Future investigations should undertake further longitudinal research with multiple assessment waves. Nevertheless, our approach of investigating two waves across the energy crisis provides a valuable steppingstone, illustrating how public judgment and decision-making depends on contextual conditions. Moreover, we cannot exclude the fact that several external factors independent of the assessed control variables may have influenced our results, such as anxiety about the war, seasonal effects, inflation, or implemented policies. Aside from structural policies aiming to address storage and supply issues, the main European policy response were fiscal measures to reduce energy vulnerability, which may have differentially affected citizens in different countries and societal groups69,70. Therefore, even though we find most results are consistent across Germany and Switzerland, these findings may not generalize to other (European) countries, which differ more strongly in cultural, political, and economic aspects. Furthermore, while our analysis offered insights into the relevance of numeracy for energy literacy, it is important to note that knowledge is a necessary but imperfect precursor of behavior. Future research should couple experimental survey data with real-world retail data on actual energy efficient product purchases to increase our understanding of the complex interplay between individual differences in literacy and climate change concerns, preferences, and behavior across major societal events.

Methods

Inclusion & ethics statement

Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Committee at the University of the Faculty of Psychology of the University of Basel (number 015-22-1).

Participants

All participants provided their informed consent to provide their data. Data collection was conducted via the panel provider Bilendi We gathered representative samples in terms of gender, age, and income from the population of 18- to 80-year-olds in Germany and the German speaking part of Switzerland. Data for the first assessment wave was collected between 29 November 2022 and 15 December 2022. The second assessment wave with the same participants was conducted between 28 March 2023 and 12 April 2023. Both waves included the same main tasks for judgments, policy decisions, and product choices. A total of 2809 respondents passed the attention check and completed Wave 1. Those participants were then contacted to conduct Wave 2 (48% started the survey), which was completed by 1040 participants. This included 552 German participants (of which we excluded two non-binary individuals due to analyses being controlled for gender) and 448 Swiss participants (of which one individual was excluded due to indicating “prefer not to answer” in the gender category).

All deviations from the intended representative sample can be found in Supplementary Table 3. Overall, over and under sampling were low with a maximum deviation of 4.3% for age, 4.8% for gender, and 5.3% for income.

Assessment of demographic and psychological variables

After providing consent, participants answered demographic questions as well as an attention check. The demographic items included: age (assessed in segments for quota control: 18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and 60–80); gender (female, male, non-binary, prefer not to say); income (assessed in country-specific segments for quota control based on monthly household income; for Germany <1500, 1500–2499, 2500–4000, >4000; for Switzerland <3100, 3100–4299, 4300–5900, >5,900); and education (no formal education, obligatory school, middle school, high school, or higher). After completing the judgment and decision tasks, participants were asked to provide further information, including: climate change concern (4 items, e.g., “climate change has severe consequences for humans and nature”, on a scale from 1 – completely disagree to 6 – completely agree, taken from ref. 71); political orientation (1 = extremely left-wing, 10 = extremely right-wing); trust in government (3 items about trust in different political institutions (local and regional; the government; the parliament), on a scale from 1 – not at all trustworthy to 6 – completely trustworthy, taken from ref. 72); emotions towards renewable energy technologies and the energy crisis (“When I think about the current energy situation/ renewable energy technologies, I feel”: anger, fear, hope, and interest, on a scale of 1- completely disagree to 6 – completely agree, adapted from ref. 73,74); energy expense concerns (1 item: “How worried are you that energy may be too expensive for many people in the country”, on a scale of 1- not at all concerned to 6- very concerned, taken from ref. 75); financial impact (1 item, “How did the crisis affect your financial status”, on a scale from −3 very negative to +3 very positive, adapted from ref. 76; and numeracy (1 item, Berlin numeracy test77). Please refer to Supplementary Table 4 for descriptive statistics for all variables.

Experimental assessment of renewable energy policy support

For the renewable energy policy task, participants read an explanatory text introducing the task and informing them of the attributes of each policy (see Supplementary Information 1 for complete text). To proceed, participants had to correctly answer a comprehension question about when the benefits of the proposed measure would come into effect based on the implementation time attribute in the policy task. Participants conducted 16 trials in which they reported, in a binary response, whether they would be in favor of or against a given renewable energy policy package (“Would you be in favor of or against the introduction of the following policy package to increase renewable energy?”). We chose to use a binary response because it best reflects the nature of decision-making about public policies (especially in the Swiss direct democracy context), where policy measures can be accepted or rejected.

Each policy consisted of the four attributes CO2 reduction potential (30%, 15%), tax increases to cover policy costs (6%, 1%), implementation time (in seven years, in one year), and energy independence potential (20%, 10%). Based on a full factorial design this resulted in 16 combinations of attributes, which were presented in a random order to all participants. The order in which the attributes were presented (top to bottom) was also randomized. The values for the CO2 reduction potential attribute were based on scenarios calculating the relative emission reductions achieved by past measures and on future predicted emission reductions with existing measures or additional measures78, and also considered goals set by governments and environmental agencies79. The low value (1%) for the taxes was chosen as lower boundary considering only whole numbers, while the high value (6%) was based on similar percentages for health insurance80. Implementation times (1 vs. 7 years) were chosen because 1 year is the shortest realistic full-year timeframe and 7 years (plus time for the policy to take effect) would exceed the time window that has been defined as crucial for climate change mitigation81. We informed participants that the two time points refer to when actions are implemented and that it would take about 10 years before the effects of a policy, such as CO2 reduction, would be realized. The energy independence values were based on targets for substantially increasing the share of renewable energies and baseline energy independence in 2020, which was around 40% in Germany and 30% in Switzerland82.

Assessment of energy literacy/judgments

To assess participants’ energy literacy, they were asked to estimate six curtailment and two efficiency actions in terms of their energy consumption per year in kWh: showering (6 min instead of 10 min every day; reference values: water output of 10 l/minute, 38 °C water heated via gas); washing clothes (at 30 °C instead of 60 °C; reference values: 4 times per week for an average 8 kg washing machine); drying clothes (letting clothes air dry instead of using the dryer; reference values: 4 times per week, assuming a full load and an average 8 kg washing machine); hair drying (letting hair air dry instead of using a hairdryer every day; reference values: 10 min); heating (setting the thermostat to 20 °C instead of 22 °C; reference values: for 6 months (October until April), for a ~100 sqm apartment); dishwasher (on eco vs. automatic mode; reference values: 4 times per week, assuming a full load); Buying and using a fridge of efficiency class A vs. F (based on kWh usage per year); and Replacing 5 60 W lightbulbs with 5 LEDs (based on a usage of 4 hours/day for an entire year). The actual values for these actions were gathered from the literature (see Supplementary Table 20 for each assessed behavior). In line with prior research46,83, we facilitated participants’ decision-making by offering a kWh range within which they provided their estimates. This range included examples: on the lower end of the range, charging a smartphone every day for a year uses about 5 kWh and on the upper end, running an electric heater for 4 h every day for 6 months amounts to about 3,000 kWh. See Supplementary Information 3 for more information.

Assessment of energy-efficient product choices

For the product choice task, participants were presented with trade-off decisions featuring two washing machines (See Supplementary Information 4, based on adapted experimental paradigm from ref. 84). The washing machines had three different prices (699CHF/ 499€; 959CHF /759€; 1299CHF/ 999€) and three different energy efficiency values (46, 58, and 70 kWh). These were paired to keep the product choices realistic, such that the most energy-efficient product could be either expensive or intermediate (but not cheap) and the least energy-efficient product could be either cheap or intermediate (but not expensive). The trade-off choices were then combined so that there was no dominant obvious choice, i.e., no product could be both less expensive and more energy efficient. This resulted in a total of seven trade-off choices that were presented to participants in a randomized order in a binary forced choice. Additionally, for half of the participants, price information was listed before energy efficiency information, and for the other half of the participants the energy efficiency information was listed before the price. See Supplementary Table 19 for all trade-off choices.

To examine the impact of real-world energy price changes on participants energy efficiency choices in our natural experiment in Switzerland, we used publicly available electricity price data on the municipality level from the Swiss Federal Electricity Commission (ElCom)85. We computed the difference between the 2023 and 2022 electricity price in ct/kWh and matched this with the product choice data via the postal code of participants who had provided this information in the survey. See Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8 for a distribution of energy price differences in our sample.

Deviations from the preregistration

We preregistered only including participants who gave correct answers to three out of four data quality check questions, including one attention check and three comprehension checks (about the implementation time attribute in the policy task, about comparing energy behaviors, about what to estimate in the energy judgment task; for exact wording see Supplementary Information 1–3). However, applying this exclusion criterion would have only left 600 participants, leading to a sample reduction of 42%. To retain a sufficient power, we therefore decided to only exclude participants based on the attention check for the main analysis reported in the main manuscript (N = 1037 for policy analyses, N = 980 for energy literacy, and product choice analyses), and to report all analyses with the stricter exclusion criteria (N = 600) in the Supplementary Tables 5–18.

Furthermore, we excluded 56 individuals who gave the exact same estimate eight times in the judgment accuracy task because we attribute these responses to inattention and not deliberate estimates. We excluded these participants for the judgment analyses as well as for the product choice analyses since the latter include the energy literacy index. We report all analyses without this exclusion in the Supplementary Tables 9–18.

For the policy support analyses we used random slopes for the four attributes rather than random intercept for individuals as this improved model fit substantially, but barely had an impact on the size of estimates, the importance of different estimates, or the significance of estimates. See Supplementary Table 1 for a list of all hypotheses and deviations.

Statistical analyses

All analyses followed the preregistration except for the deviations described above (all models can be found in the Supplementary Tables 5–18). For policy support, we ran mixed effects logistic regressions with random slopes for the policy attributes, the probability of supporting a policy as binary dependent variable, policy attributes as predictors, and controlling for demographic variables. For Hypothesis 1a, to investigate differences in the effect of energy independence on policy support between assessment waves, we entered the interactions between policy attributes and assessment waves in the mixed effects logistic regression model. For Hypothesis 1b, to investigate the effect of climate change concern on policy support as a function of policy implementation time, we entered z-standardized climate change concern and the two-way interaction with assessment wave into the mixed effects regression model. For Hypothesis 1c we used the individual regression weights of the CO2 attribute (based on the 16 choices made) as dependent variables in linear regression, and climate change concern and demographic variables as predictors to test the impact of climate change concern on the weight participants assigned to the CO2 reduction potential of policies.

For the energy judgments assessing participants’ action energy literacy, we followed conventional procedures in the literature46,68, using log10 transformation of the estimates and actual values to improve distribution. Judgment error was computed by taking the absolute value of the log10 transformed estimate divided by the log10 transformed actual value. The energy literacy score is comprised of the mean value of these eight transformed judgment error values, such that higher values indicate higher error and conversely lower literacy (Cronbach’s α = 0.83 in Wave 1 and α = 0.82 Wave 2). We entered frequency of use of the household devices (Hypothesis 3a) and confidence (Hypothesis 3b) separately into linear regression models to predict energy literacy. We used a mixed effects regression model with participant id as random intercept, energy literacy (judgment error) as dependent variable, and assessment wave and the interaction with numeracy as predictors, controlling for demographic variables.

For the product choice task, we ran mixed effects logistic regressions using id as random intercept. Climate change concern and energy literacy were entered separately into the models and z-standardized to ensure interpretation of the interaction terms. For the effect of real-world energy price changes on choices, we added the z-standardized price difference, as well as the two- and three-way interactions with assessment wave, and energy-efficiency difference in the choice to the logistic regression model.
