Accelerated Yellowing of Paper after Deposition of Aqueous Cosolvent Solutions
Authors: Sajjad Karimnejad, Nicolae Tomozeiu, Anton A. Darhuber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Water-based inkjet inks typically contain 5–50 wt % of so-called cosolvents, which are nonvolatile, polar compounds such as glycerol or ethylene glycol oligomers (EGOs). We found that the yellowing of printing paper in sunlight is notably accelerated after the deposition of aqueous solutions of EGOs. Using a combination of accelerated aging and fluorescence spectroscopy, we identified the root cause to be the increased rate of UV-induced degradation of whitening agents in the presence of atmospheric oxygen and EGOs. Such fluorescent brighteners are standard additives in office paper.

Introduction

Printing paper is a multicomponent material with a complex hierarchical structure that undergoes irreversible chemical and mechanical changes when exposed to various ambient environments. − For instance, degradation is triggered by heat and high humidity levels. − Exposure to sunlight or ultraviolet (UV) radiation causes yellowing of paper. − Analogous effects occur in textiles based on cotton, wool, and silk fibers. ,−

Fluorescent brightening agents (FBAs) are commonly added to commercial printing paper in order to increase its visual appeal. − These compounds absorb light from the UV to the blue spectral range and re-emit light primarily in the violet and blue region. − In this fashion, the yellowish tint of cellulose is transformed into a bright white appearance. In textiles such as cotton, the presence of FBAs thereby reduces the UV transmission and improves the UV protection ability of the fabric. −

In paper manufacture, FBAs are added either to the pulp suspension used for paper making or on top of the already formed paper sheet, e.g., during a coating or sizing step. , In the first case, FBAs are distributed homogeneously across a paper sheet, whereas in the latter, they are present only near the surface. Wilhelm reported the UV- or heat-induced activity loss of FBAs as a potential mechanism for the apparent yellowing of paper. Daruwalla and Peter observed that UV-induced fluorescence degradation of cellulosic substrates treated with FBAs was correlated with a decrease of their mechanical strength. Suggested mechanisms causing loss of fluorescence in common stilbene-based FBAs include trans–cis isomerization, − dimerization, photoreductive cleavage, and an oxidative reaction involving the ethylene bond. Dyer et al. identified hydroxylation as the key initial mechanism of photodegradation of a stilbene-derived FBA.

Cosolvents such as glycerol , or ethylene glycol oligomers (EGOs) , are common additives in inkjet inks to prevent inkjet nozzle clogging and to reduce cockle and intercolor bleed. − In this paper, we investigate the accelerated yellowing of commercial printing papers after deposition of aqueous cosolvent solutions. While the effect of water on paper degradation has received much attention, the effect of cosolvents did not. Our study is motivated by the experiment shown in Figure a, which shows that deposition of tetra­(ethylene glycol) (TEG) can greatly increase the yellowing rate of paper upon daily exposure to sunlight for the period of one month. Figure b illustrates a control experiment, which proves that TEG deposition alone, i.e., without exposure to sunlight, does not cause any noticeable yellowing. Using fluorescence emission spectroscopy, we show that the observed paper yellowing is caused by UV-induced FBA degradation. Our results are important for the lightfastness and long-term color stability of inkjet-printed patterns deposited on printing paper.

Experimental Methods and Procedures

In this section, the setup and experimental procedures utilized for monitoring paper yellowing will be discussed. All experiments were conducted at room temperature (298 ± 3) K and an ambient relative humidity of (40 ± 10)%. Paper ‘A’ is used for all experiments, unless stated otherwise. The term ‘untreated’ refers to ‘as-is’ paper samples (never wet, out of the box) that have not been in contact with any liquid or cosolvent solutions and that have not undergone any treatments.

Sample Preparation

A volume of 32 μL of either pure water or aqueous cosolvent solutions is deposited homogeneously on square-shaped paper samples (dimensions 25 × 25 mm) using a digital syringe (Hamilton). In the case of pure water, the added liquid volume corresponds to a moisture content of MCwater = 0.63 kg/kg, which is approximately 20% higher than the fiber holding capacity of paper A. Subsequently, the paper is left to dry in the ambient air on a wire grid for at least 45 min.

In the case of a so-called rehydration treatment, − paper samples were sprayed with pure water twice by means of an atomizer (Timbertech Airbrush Kit ABPST06) after cosolvent deposition and drying. After completion of each rehydration step, the samples were allowed to dry for at least 40 min.

For one experiment, the sample is kept moist with water throughout UV exposure by adding 50 μL of pure water every 10 min.

Immersion and Washing Step

Some paper samples were submerged in either EG for approximately 17 h or in tetrahydrofuran (THF, Sigma-Aldrich product number 360589) for 4 h. Following these soaking periods, the samples were carefully transferred onto a wire grid and allowed to dry for 4 h.

A washing step consisted of submerging a sample in a bath of pure water for 1 h and then allowing it to dry for another hour.

FBA Deposition

Some Whatman filter paper samples were treated by deposition of 30 μL of an aqueous solution of Tinopal LPW with a concentration of c FBA = 3 wt %. In case of a subsequent cosolvent deposition, 25 μL of an aqueous TEG solution (c 0 = 60 wt %) is deposited on the paper sample, optionally followed by rehydration steps. Samples were allowed to dry for 3 h after the final step.

Accelerated Aging

Accelerated aging was performed in a UV-curing chamber [Fusionet, ELC-500, main spectral emission around λ = (370 ± 20) nm] with optical output of 30 mW/cm2. The samples were placed in the process chamber for 20–30 min at a time, after which a fluorescence spectrum was recorded. For each sample, these two steps were repeated several times to obtain the fluorescence spectra as a function of cumulative UV treatment time Δt UV. The sample temperature in the UV chamber reaches approximately 45 °C due to the heat load from the lamps.

Fluorescence Emission Spectrometry

Figure d illustrates the experimental setup for fluorescence spectrometry measurements. The paper samples are placed on a black anodized substrate 4 cm underneath a specular reflection probe equipped with a UV cutoff filter (cutoff wavelength 415 nm). A metal ring with an inner diameter larger than the illuminated area is placed on the paper to keep it as flat as possible. The paper is irradiated with a UV light-emitting diode (LED Engin, product number LZ4–04UV00, center wavelength of 365 nm, radiant flux of 3 W). The light collected by the reflection probe is analyzed using a spectrometer (Ocean Optics, model USB4000). For each reported emission spectrum, a mean of 10 consecutive readings is obtained. The fluorescence spectra in the region of 400–600 nm are reported. All experiments were done in a room without natural light, with room lights turned off and the setup covered with an intransparent box.

The reproducibility of the measurements is generally satisfactory. Possible limiting factors include noise due to counting statistics and the dark current of the spectrometer sensor, sample-to-sample heterogeneities, e.g., in paper thickness and FBA content, and a nonflat surface topography after liquid deposition and drying. A typical error bar is indicated in Figure c.

The visual appearances of samples that are artificially aged by 3 h of UV irradiation and samples that have been exposed to sunlight for 6 months are rather comparable. The same holds for their fluorescence emission spectra, as evident from a comparison of Figures g and S1 in the Supporting Information.

We were curious whether the decrease in fluorescence could be due to a partial removal of the FBAs from the surface and their displacement to the interior of a paper sheet. Although Tinopal LGW is water-soluble, Figure b shows that deposition of water does not change the fluorescence intensity. Similarly, immersion into EG, which is chemically very similar to TEG, in a bath for 17 h does not affect the UV degradation behavior, as shown in Figure b. Discussion

Root Causes of Yellowing

Figure b shows that the presence of UV light is a prerequisite for yellowing to occur. However, the presence of atmospheric oxygen is also required. To support this claim, an experiment is done where a sample of paper A is placed in a gastight container (polyethylene zip-lock bag) after TEG deposition. The bag allowed UV radiation around 365 nm to pass essentially unattenuated (transmission larger than 90%), but significantly limited the availability of oxygen. Over a period of five months exposure to sunlight, no signs of fluorescence degradation or yellowing were observed, suggesting that in the absence of a sufficient oxygen supply, yellowing does not occur, despite UV exposure. This highlights the crucial role of oxygen in the photochemical reactions that lead to yellowing. This is consistent with the results of Kramer et al., who showed that FBAs are degraded significantly faster if the partial pressure of oxygen is increased. Analogous effects are frequently observed with photobleaching of other fluorescent dyes. ,

Analogous conclusions have been drawn in studies of the degradation of polymers, paper, cellulose-based fabrics, and wool. Douminge et al. investigated the aging of high-density polyethylene exposed to 254 nm UV radiation. They concluded that the polymer degradation is controlled by oxygen diffusion and involves the formation and decomposition of hydroperoxides. According to Appleby, factors which appear to accelerate photochemical degradation of textiles are the availability of oxygen from the air, the presence of moisture, and light of short wavelength. Graham and Statham studied the effect of sunlight on wool treated with FBAs. They showed that appreciable photochemical oxidation of wool occurred during irradiation when FBAs were present. The presence of water was found to accelerate the rate of discoloration to a very great extent. According to Millington, the presence of oxygen and the presence of water are principal factors that influence color changes of wool exposed to UV radiation. Wet photoyellowing of wool always occurs far more rapidly than under dry conditions. Daruwalla and Peter studied the fluorescence degradation of FBAs on cellulosic substrates after UV exposure. They found that the extent of degradation depends on the amount and chemical composition of FBA as well as the spectral characteristics and duration of UV irradiation. They also found a correlation between the rate of FBA decomposition and the decrease in the mechanical strength of paper.

Impact of Cosolvents

The experimental data indicate that deposition of TEG, PEG6, and PEG12 and to a lesser extent also DEG and EG accelerates UV-induced yellowing of printing paper. The reason could be that cosolvents exhibit a low volatility and thus provide a liquid phase environment for the fluorescent molecules. Many fluorophores exhibit a stronger fluorescence when dissolved in a solvent compared to the dry state, which in turn may explain the faster degradation of the whitening agents. This is supported by a comparison of Figures b and d. In the former case, water was deposited on paper ‘A’ once, followed by drying and several UV-exposure steps of 20–30 min each, during which the paper was dry. In the latter case, water was deposited prior to each UV exposure step, and the sample was placed in the UV oven while still wet, which led to a noticeably stronger UV degradation. In this context, it seems peculiar that the presence of glycerol has no discernible effect, as shown in Figure . We will discuss possible reasons in the next section.

A comparison of Figures b and b with Figure c shows that EG and DEG apparently have a lower impact on the fluorescence degradation rate than TEG. The reason is linked to their lower molecular weight, which implies a higher vapor pressure and thus a higher volatility compared to TEG. We deposited 20 μL of EG and DEG onto paper substrates and measured the mass of EG and DEG as a function of time. After 50 min of UV exposure, nearly 90% of the EG and after 150 min 30% of the DEG had evaporated.

Oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, and nitroxides frequently act as quenchers of fluorescence. If a given solvent is very viscous, then diffusive transport of the quencher toward the fluorophores is slow and quenching is inhibited. Eftink and Ghiron studied material systems where the fluorescence is quenched by increasing the oxygen concentration, but where the quenching action of oxygen is strongly reduced if the solvent viscosity is increased by admixing glycerol. Eftink and Hagaman drew analogous conclusions for a different material system. In the material system studied by Bhavya et al., the effectiveness of the quencher increases in solvents of lower dielectric permittivity. Since glycerol has both a higher viscosity and a higher permittivity (see Table ), this could explain why the yellowing rate of TEG is substantially higher. In contrast, other authors found that the quenching efficiency increases with increasing ϵ r . , However, since the ratio of the cosolvent viscosities μglycerol/μTEG is about 20 but the ratio of the permittivities ϵglycerol/ϵTEG is about 2, the impact of μ effectively may outweigh that of ϵ r .

In Figure , we systematically varied the EGO molecular weight. The fluorescence degradation rate after deposition of TEG, PEG6, and PEG12 is rather similar. In this set of cosolvents, the viscosity increases by a factor of 2.5 and the dielectric permittivity decreases by a factor of approximately 2. While it is possible that these two effects cancel each other, we can exclude the possibility that the fluorescence degradation rate for EG oligomers sensitively depends on viscosity.

Glycerol contains 3 alcohol groups, whereas EGOs contain both alcohol and ether groups. It is conceivable that these differences in chemical composition rather than in specific physical properties are responsible for their different activities concerning FBA degradation.

Boundary Discoloration

Souguir et al. and Jeong et al. studied the impact of wet zones on degradation and aging on otherwise dry Whatman filter paper sheets. − They partially immersed vertical paper sheets in a layer of deionized water. After 2 h, a stable wet zone was established with a stationary wet–dry boundary line based on the equilibrium of evaporation and capillary wicking. After 16 h, brown lines had formed at the wet–dry boundary, which were accompanied by increased levels of formic and acetic acid as well as hydroperoxides and alkoxy- and hydroxyl radicals at the boundary.

We did not observe a noticeably stronger yellowing at the boundaries of the cosolvent solution wet zones in our experiments. We believe that this is due to two key differences. Souguir et al. ascribe the discoloration to the (evaporation-driven) migration and increasing concentration of active species present in the paper sheet to the wet–dry boundary. In our experiments, water evaporation was complete, typically 20 min after solution deposition. Moreover, the presence of cosolvents drastically increases the viscosity and thus proportionally reduces the migration rates. Thus, both the duration and magnitude of any flow toward the wet zone boundaries were much shorter in our experiments, which may explain the absence of a boundary effect.

Summary and Conclusion

In this article, the accelerated yellowing of commercial printing paper after deposition of aqueous solutions of glycerol and EGOs is investigated by means of artificial aging and fluorescence spectroscopy. These compounds are typical representatives of so-called cosolvents, which are common additives in water-based inks for inkjet printing. We found that the root cause of yellowing is the degradation of FBAs present in printing paper, which requires both exposure to UV light and atmospheric oxygen. It is well-known in the literature that the presence of moisture can significantly accelerate the rate of yellowing of cellulose. We believe that EGOs have an analogous effect that is assisted by their low volatility.

Glycerol does not accelerate yellowing as much as EGOs, which makes it the superior choice for inkjet printing in this respect. Possible reasons include the absence of ether groups that are present in EGOs and its significantly higher viscosity, which may suppress the transport of compounds, promoting fluorescence quenching. EGOs of low molecular weight, such as ethylene glycol, accelerate yellowing to a lesser extent. This is primarily linked to their higher vapor pressure and evaporation within the time scale of the artificial aging experiments.
