Standard complete blood count to predict long‐term outcomes in febrile infection–related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES): A multicenter study

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe investigated whether complete blood count (CBC) analyses during intensive care unit stay could predict 12‐month outcomes in patients with cryptogenic febrile infection–related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES), a subset of new‐onset refractory status epilepticus (NORSE).MethodsOutcomes at 12 months were classified as “unfavorable” (Glasgow Outcome Score [GOS] 1–3) or “favorable” (GOS 4–5). Demographic, clinical, and serial CBC data were collected across treatment phases: (1) no immunotherapy (before initiation or no treatment), (2) first‐line immunotherapy, and (3) second‐line immunotherapy. For each treatment phase, predictive models stratified outcomes based on CBC features using decision tree regression, with separate models for adults and children. Model performance was tested using a leave‐one‐patient‐out approach.ResultsWe studied 63 patients (34 adults, 29 children) from 12 centers. Unfavorable outcomes occurred in 18 adults and 12 children. Children were more likely to receive second‐line immunotherapy. We analyzed 1530 CBCs (adults: 997 CBCs, including 539 for unfavorable outcomes; children: 533 CBCs, including 415 for unfavorable outcomes). Subgroup analyses revealed differences in CBC levels according to the outcomes and the treatment received. Adults with unfavorable outcomes notably had higher neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratios (NLRs) and monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratios (MLRs), whereas children with unfavorable outcomes had higher red cell distribution width. NLRs and MLRs increased when CBCs were collected after the initiation of immunotherapy for both adults and children. The variables of interest differed in the different predictive models but always included the proportion of at least one subtype of leukocyte. Prediction accuracy with our models was higher in children (87% overall, with the best performance in no‐treatment and first‐line phases) than in adults (83% overall, with the best performance during/after the initiation of second‐line).SignificanceFindings suggest the potential for standard CBCs to serve as a rapid, accessible tool for early prognostication in cryptogenic FIRES, particularly in children.Keywords: complete blood count, FIRES, NORSE, outcomes

INTRODUCTION

Aberrant immune activation of the central nervous system represents a potential pathological mechanism underlying cryptogenic NORSE (new‐onset refractory status epilepticus) and its subset with a prodromal febrile illness known as FIRES (febrile infection–related epilepsy syndrome). 1 , 2 , 3 In addition to the elevated pro‐inflammatory markers in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 markers of active systemic inflammation have been described in individuals with cryptogenic NORSE (c‐NORSE). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 These observations suggest that systemic inflammation may also contribute to the pathology of c‐NORSE.

Studies profiling cytokines and chemokines in individuals with c‐NORSE, including FIRES, have provided insights into cerebral and systemic inflammation, as well as potential candidates for treatment to correct the innate immune dysregulation. 7 Of interest, serum cytokine profiles may be associated with short‐ and long‐term outcomes, 7 , 9 suggesting that the systemic inflammatory state may reflect the current clinical status and predict the long‐term outcomes of patients with c‐NORSE. Complete blood count (CBC), a widely available laboratory study of the hematopoietic system, provides leukocyte number and composition, as well as platelet counts and hemoglobin values. All of these can be influenced by active systemic inflammation and are therefore potential surrogates of the systemic inflammatory state.

We hypothesize that serial CBC values may reflect changes in the systemic inflammatory states and, therefore, may be associated with outcomes in c‐NORSE. Focusing on adults and children with cryptogenic FIRES defined as c‐NORSE with a preceding febrile illness, 10 we investigated the feasibility of constructing predictive models for outcomes using solely CBC components and considering the immunotherapies received.

METHODS

Study design, patient selection, and data extraction

This is a retrospective study comprising a multi‐center international cohort of 34 adults and 29 children (<18 years old) with cryptogenic FIRES. The local governing committee for human subject research for each participating center approved the study. We identified eligible patients for inclusion from the following sources: (1) NORSE multicenter study and NORSE/FIRES biorepository at Yale (IRB #1511016840 and #2000031611, for a total of 34 patients); (2) Pitié‐Salpêtière Hospital in Paris, France (AP‐HP, COLETTE, 13 patients); And (3) Texas Children's Hospital/Baylor College of Medicine (H‐31344, 16 patients). Each contributing investigator determined the diagnosis of cryptogenic FIRES following a negative diagnostic evaluation. All the cases enrolled in the NORSE studies have been reviewed by experts who confirmed the diagnosis. The study was designed and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 11

Basic demographic and clinical information were collected, including age at seizure onset, duration of status epilepticus (SE), length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and administration of immunomodulatory therapies. CBC values were included from SE onset to ICU discharge. We recorded the administration of first‐line and second‐line immune modulators. First‐line immune modulators included steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and plasma exchange (PLEX). Second‐line immune modulators included anakinra, tocilizumab, rituximab, intrathecal dexamethasone, and cyclophosphamide. Laboratory data related to potential infections were reviewed, and CBCs conducted on days with any positive cultures were excluded from the analysis.

Patient outcome was evaluated at 12 months after SE ended using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS), ranging from 1 (death) to 5 (good recovery). Scores from 1 to 3 were considered as unfavorable outcomes, and scores of 4 and 5 as favorable outcomes. Only patients with functional outcomes available at 12 months after SE ended were enrolled.

Development of the predictive models

This study aimed to construct predictive models for patient outcomes based on CBC findings. Given the known differences in the normal CBC values between adults and children, we developed separate models for each age group. Furthermore, to account for potential variations in CBC levels with immunotherapy, we developed models for each treatment phase: No treatment (either before first‐line, or at any time in patients who did not receive immunotherapy), First‐Line treatment (after initiation of first‐line immunotherapy and before the initiation of a second‐line therapy), and Second‐Line treatment (after the introduction of second‐line immunotherapy), for both adult and pediatric populations.

We employed balanced decision tree regression for outcome prediction. 12 This method serves as an interpretable clinical algorithm, using patient data to build a tree‐like structure based on binary decisions, analogous to a diagnostic flowchart. Balanced decision tree regression also compensates for the imbalanced distribution of favorable and unfavorable outcomes by weighting the less represented class more heavily during training. The resulting model automatically stratifies patients into subgroups with differing predicted outcomes (e.g., favorable or unfavorable outcomes based on GOS scores). In predictive modeling, an essential preliminary step is variable selection. 13 Removing noninformative and/or redundant variables typically enhances model performance and reduces computational burden. For variable selection, we evaluated multiple subsets of variables and retained those that produced the best model performance. Consequently, for each treatment phase and patient group (adults and children), we identified a distinct set of informative variables. These subsets were then used to train the corresponding models.

To evaluate the models' performance, we first employed a leave‐one‐patient‐out (LOPO) cross‐validation strategy. 14 , 15 In this method, all CBC data from a single patient are withheld while the model is trained on the CBC data from the remaining patients. The trained model is then tested on the data of the held‐out patient. This process is repeated iteratively for each patient in the dataset. This method was chosen due to the relatively limited size of our patient cohort. We next evaluated the model's performance by using data from 80% to 90% of the patients to train the model, and the remaining patients to test it. A cross‐validation procedure was used with 500 folds. For patients with multiple CBCs collected during the study period, a prognosis value was predicted for each CBC, and the average of these predictions was used to determine the outcome. We then compared the predicted outcome at each treatment period with the true functional outcome score at 12 months. Model performance was assessed using standard metrics, including balanced accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), F1‐score, precision, and recall. Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions but can be misleading when dealing with imbalanced data. Balanced accuracy adjusts for this by considering each class equally. AUC evaluates the model's ability to distinguish between classes, which is especially useful when adjusting decision thresholds. The F1‐score balances precision (the proportion of predicted positives that are correct) and recall (the proportion of actual positives that are correctly identified), making it crucial when both false positives and false negatives matter.

Statistical analyses

CBC findings were compared between patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes using a linear mixed‐effects model, with the fixed effect including the outcome status and the random effect including the repeated measures from the same patient. We conducted a generalized linear mixed‐effects model to assess the impact of immunotherapies on the CBC values. For this model, the immunotherapy period was used as a fixed effect, the patient as a random effect, and post hoc pairwise comparisons between periods were performed using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. The mixed‐effects models were chosen because they allow the inclusion of a random intercept for each patient, which accounts for the non‐independence of repeated CBC measurements within patients. This approach prevents inflated type I error rates that would otherwise arise from treating repeated samples as independent observations. In addition, it accommodates the unequal number of CBC measurements per patient, ensuring that individuals with more samples do not disproportionately influence the results.

RESULTS

Association of CBC features with outcome

A total of 1530 CBC results were collected from 63 patients, including 997 from adults (median 19 per patient, interquartile range [IQR] 9–49) and 533 from children (median 8 per patient, IQR 4–16). The first CBC was collected after a median delay of 2 days (IQR 0–10) in adults and 1 day (IQR 0.6–6) in children (p = 0.71).

In adults, linear mixed‐models analysis revealed a higher monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR) for patients with unfavorable outcomes (18 patients, 539 CBCs) compared to those with favorable outcomes (16 patients, 458 CBCs) (p = 0.028) (Table 2). Similarly, when considering the GOS score as a categorical variable ranging from 5 (least impairment) to 1 (most impairment), patients with better outcomes showed a lower MLR (p = 0.0082) and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (p = 0.032), but higher platelet counts (p = 0.040) and a higher percentage of eosinophils (p = 0.037).

In children, linear mixed‐model analyses showed significantly higher red cell distribution width (RDW) and mean platelet volume (MPV) in patients with unfavorable outcomes (12 patients, 415 CBCs) compared to those with favorable outcomes (17 patients, 118 CBCs), with no significant differences observed for the other variables (Table 2).

Impact of immunotherapies on CBC parameters

We next evaluated the impact of immunotherapies on CBC parameters at three periods, analyzing adults and children separately (Table S1). CBC data were available for 16 adults in the no‐treatment period (74 CBCs), 31 in the first‐line therapy (487 CBCs), and 16 in the second‐line therapy (436 CBCs) periods. The generalized linear mixed models revealed significant differences across the three groups for all CBC parameters, except for the percentage of monocytes and the white blood cell counts. Following the initiation of immunotherapy, there were increased neutrophil percentages, NLRs, MLRs, and RDW, along with decreased lymphocyte percentages, hemoglobin concentration, and hematocrit levels. Although there is a trend for a decrease in neutrophil percentages with the second‐line therapy (p = 0.061), they did not return to levels observed during the no‐treatment period. The percentages of eosinophils and basophils were higher for CBCs collected after the initiation of the second‐line therapy, compared to both the no‐treatment and first‐line groups.

CBC data were available for 21 children in the no‐treatment period (35 CBCs), 14 in the first‐line therapy (49 CBCs), and 22 in the second‐line therapy (449 CBCs) periods. The generalized linear mixed models revealed significant differences across the three groups for all parameters, except for the percentage of monocytes, white blood cell counts, and the platelet counts (Table S1). Following the initiation of either first‐ or second‐line immunotherapy, there were increased neutrophil percentages and decreased lymphocyte percentages (both outside the normal range), as well as higher NLR and MLR values, and lower hemoglobin and hematocrit levels (outside the normal range). Similar to in adults, the percentages of eosinophils were higher after the initiation of the second‐line therapy.

Identification of the most relevant variables for outcome prediction

Our analyses revealed significant differences in CBC results based on patients' outcomes, age group, and treatment phase. To optimize model performance, we chose to separate adults and children and develop a distinct model for each treatment phase. Only patients with complete CBC data were included in the analysis. Consequently, two children with missing MPV values were excluded from subsequent analysis.

In addition to the previously studied CBC features and commonly used ratios, such as NLR and MLR, we explored whether combining new features in the form of ratios could enhance model performance. Four such ratios emerged as relevant for outcome prediction: (1) the ratio of RDW to MCV, which was significant in both adults and children; and three significant ratios only in children: (2) the ratio of the lymphocyte percentage to RDW; (3) the ratio of MLR to MPV; and (4) the ratio of the monocyte to the neutrophil percentages. Figure S1 illustrates the separation between patients with unfavorable and favorable outcomes based on these ratios.

We then applied a feature selection approach to identify the most relevant features for each group of patients and each period of treatment, resulting in six distinct feature combinations. Figure 1 illustrates the selected variables for each model along with their relative importance. Based on these features, a decision tree was constructed for each model to stratify patients into subgroups with differing predicted outcomes. Model performance

In adults, the performance of the models was assessed in 16 patients within the first period (no treatment), 31 within the second period (first‐line therapy), and 16 within the third period (second‐line therapy), for a total of 63 periods. The models accurately predicted the outcome 83% of the time (52/63) when considering all periods together (Figure S2). In children, the performance of the models was assessed in 19 patients within the first period (no‐treatment), 14 within the second period (first‐line therapy), and 21 within the third period (second‐line therapy), for a total of 54 periods. The models accurately predicted the outcome 87% of the time (47/54) when considering all periods together (Figure S3). Detailed performance metrics for each treatment period in both adults and children are presented in Table 3. Overall, performance was slightly lower in adults compared to children, particularly during the first‐line treatment period.

DISCUSSION

This study represents a proof‐of‐principle that CBC components, which reflect the systemic inflammatory states, measured at different stages of immune therapy, may assist in predicting the outcome of patients with cryptogenic FIRES. Notably, CBC parameters differed significantly between patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes, with MLR and NLR associated with prognosis in adults, as well as RDW and MPV in children. Furthermore, we highlighted that immunotherapy impacted several CBC components, notably increasing NLR and MLR and decreasing lymphocyte percentages, with eosinophil percentages rising only during or after second‐line treatment. Although the key features varied across the six models, all included at least one leukocyte subtype percentage.

Systemic inflammation is well documented in NORSE, notably through the elevation of serum cytokine levels, which differ from those observed in refractory SE of known etiology and are linked to prognosis. 7 In particular, elevated serum CXCL8 (chemokine ligand 8), CCL2 (C‐C motif chemokine ligand 2), and MIP‐1α (macrophage inflammatory protein 1‐alpha) levels in patients with c‐NORSE suggest an important role for neutrophils and monocytes in c‐NORSE pathophysiology. 7 Although cytokine profiling holds promise for highlighting underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and guiding immunotherapy, 16 its clinical application remains limited by accessibility, cost, and delayed results. In contrast, the CBC is widely accessible, cost‐effective, and rapidly processed, and offers a practical surrogate for real‐time monitoring of systemic inflammation and therapeutic guidance.

Emerging evidence further indicates that immune dysregulation represents a major pathological mechanism underlying c‐NORSE and may account for some of their long‐term consequences. 7 , 8 , 16 , 17 , 18 In line with this, we found that overall leukocyte subtype composition differed between adults with favorable and unfavorable outcomes and may be predictive at different stages of the illness or therapies for both adults and children.

Unfavorable outcomes in adults were notably associated with higher NLRs and MLRs. A hyperinflammatory monocyte response to otherwise benign bacterial stimuli has been observed in a child with FIRES, resulting in an excessive neutrophil recruitment, which may explain the parallel prognostic value observed for both NLRs and MLRs. 8 The absence of significant differences between NLR and MLR levels between children with favorable or unfavorable outcomes may be due to the disproportionate number of CBCs collected for those with unfavorable outcomes (78%) or to the higher likelihood that these children received second‐line immunotherapy and had CBCs obtained after treatment initiation. Altogether, these changes point toward a deleterious overactivation of the innate immune system in c‐NORSE, including cryptogenic FIRES. 7 , 8 , 19 , 20

Beyond innate immunity, adaptive immune alterations may also contribute. Adults with unfavorable outcomes exhibited a trend for decreased lymphocyte percentages, suggesting impaired adaptive immunity and disrupted immune regulation. Lower lymphocyte percentages were especially noted when CBCs were collected after the initiation of immunotherapy, potentially reflecting both treatment‐induced immunomodulation and concurrent infections. Indeed, lymphopenia has been documented in the early phase of sepsis and has been linked to increased mortality risk. 21 Although we excluded days with positive cultures, we cannot rule out the impact of infections immediately preceding or following positive cultures on CBC components. Infections are frequent complications during SE, particularly in patients requiring mechanical ventilation or experiencing prolonged seizure duration, features often present in c‐NORSE. 22 , 23 This interpretation is further supported by similar hematological trends observed in other neurocritical care conditions, including subarachnoid hemorrhage, stroke, and traumatic brain injury, where elevated neutrophils and reduced lymphocytes correlate with poor functional outcomes and increased risk of progressive hemorrhagic brain injury. 24 , 25 , 26 Likewise, elevated neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte platelet ratios predicted mortality in a retrospective cohort study of 311 critically ill patients and in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) patients. 27 , 28 Consistent findings, such as increased platelet counts, leukocytosis, neutrophilia, and elevated NLR, have also been reported in patients with generalized tonic–clonic seizures compared to controls, with neutrophil and white blood cell counts distinguishing between seizures lasting less than 5 min and those lasting 5 min or longer, thus qualifying as SE. 29

It is surprisingly that eosinophils, a leukocyte subtype traditionally associated with allergic disease processes, were a key outcome feature for both children and adults. To our knowledge, eosinophils have not been discussed previously in the pathology of c‐NORSE, raising the possibility of an underrecognized immunological role. However, the association between elevated eosinophil levels and favorable outcomes may also reflect the effects of immunotherapy. Indeed, the eosinophil percentages increased significantly for patients who had received a second‐line immunotherapy, with 11 of the 17 patients showing eosinophil percentages outside the normal range having received at least one second‐line immunotherapy. Although our cohort size is too small to study the effect of a specific therapy, it is interesting to note that anakinra, used more frequently in children in our cohort, has been associated with an increase in absolute eosinophil counts, with eosinophilia reported in 9% of patients in clinical practice. 30 , 31 This may explain why the percentage of eosinophils carried greater prognostic weight in children in our model. 32

Children with unfavorable outcomes also exhibited elevated levels of RDW, a marker of red blood cell size variability, with a median value of 18.6%. These findings support prior reports highlighting the prognostic value of RDW for both short‐ and long‐term outcomes in critically ill patients. 33 , 34 , 35 In adults, no significant differences in RDW were observed between outcome groups; however, RDW values were relatively high in both favorable and unfavorable outcome groups compared to thresholds commonly reported in the literature for in‐hospital mortality prediction. 34 The underlying mechanisms leading to elevated RDW remain poorly understood. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including (1) a reflection of anemia, as observed in our patients 36 ; (2) systemic inflammation or sepsis, possibly explaining why patients with worse outcomes had lower ratios of lymphocyte percentage to RDW 21 , 34 ; and (3) oxidative stress. 34

A wide variety of predictive modeling approaches are available in data‐driven research, ranging from classical statistical methods such as logistic regression to more recent machine learning techniques including decision trees, support vector machines, ensemble methods (e.g., random forests, gradient boosting), and deep neural networks. The choice of model depends on multiple factors, including the size and nature of the dataset, the complexity of the underlying relationships, and the balance between predictive performance and interpretability. Although complex models may offer superior accuracy in some contexts, they often behave as “black boxes,” making it difficult to understand how predictions are generated. We have chosen decision trees as the predictive model for this study. A key advantage of this approach lies in its inherent transparency: the tree structure highlights the main predictive variables and the decision rules derived from the data. This offers a straightforward and interpretable framework that allows clinicians to understand how predictions are generated. Furthermore, the logic of the model closely mirrors clinical reasoning, in which outcomes are inferred through a sequence of simple, conditional steps. In addition to its interpretability, this structure allows for direct application in practice, as the decision rules can be followed manually using new patient data, making the model not only accurate, but also easily usable at the bedside.

Several considerations may limit the interpretation and generalizability of this study. We focused on patients with cryptogenic FIRES, and therefore, future studies are needed to evaluate the performance of these predictive models in patients with NORSE without preceding fever (non‐FIRES NORSE) or in those with an ultimately identified etiology (non‐cryptogenic NORSE). The dataset included a small number of adult and pediatric subjects, and there was no independent validation group, which may affect the validity of the models. Although our rigorous patient‐level leave‐one‐out cross‐validation strategy effectively prevents data leakage by removing all of a patient's CBCs from the training set, thereby mitigating risks associated with varying numbers of CBCs per patient in the test phase, we acknowledge that patients with more CBCs still contribute more heavily during training. However, the use of a balanced decision regression tree helps to partially compensate for this by robustly addressing the imbalanced distribution of outcomes. A further limitation of the LOPO method's construction is that it does not readily allow for the derivation of confidence intervals for the results. Moreover, the size of the cohort did not allow us to consider age as a continuous variable; therefore, teenagers and young adults have been classified into two different age groups, whereas we may expect no significant differences in CBC values between them. Due to the relatively small cohort size for this rare condition, we also categorized outcomes as either favorable or unfavorable, limiting the possibility of more nuanced outcome prediction. Similarly, the limited sample size within each treatment period precludes us from analyzing the individual impact of specific treatments or determining whether the CBC predictors reflect causal mechanisms or are simply surrogate markers of certain therapies that may be more effective. Each treatment period was analyzed independently; however, it is important to note that outcomes are influenced by the overall hospital course and all treatments received. A favorable outcome during a “no treatment” period does not imply that clinicians should withhold immunotherapy for that patient. Therefore, the decision trees should not be used as guidance on treatment, and treatment should be based on traditional clinical decision‐making. Although we excluded the days with positive cultures, we cannot rule out that the days immediately preceding or following may have been influenced by an ongoing or recently controlled infection. Given the heterogeneity of infection screening in real‐life ICU settings, where cultures are not performed daily and results are delayed, some infections may have gone undetected or persisted beyond the documented culture date. This limitation may have affected CBC values despite our exclusion criteria. Finally, the predictive value of the CBC may reflect factors beyond systemic inflammation, such as overall illness severity or individual treatment effects, and warrants further investigation. Additional studies may be beneficial to explore the impact of specific anti‐seizure medications, anesthetics, or immunotherapies, as well as the impact of the seizure type.

CONCLUSION

Our data demonstrate the potential value of CBC components in predicting FIRES long‐term outcomes, with stronger predictive performance observed in children. Although further studies are needed to fully elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, our findings highlight the potential of an accessible tool for early prognostication in cryptogenic FIRES.
