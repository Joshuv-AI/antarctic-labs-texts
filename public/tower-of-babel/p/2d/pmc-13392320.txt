Suspected ruptured medulla oblongata abscess treated conservatively with a favorable outcome: A rare case report from Vietnam
Authors: Ha Thi Duong, Loi Thi Dinh, Khoi Hong Vo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brainstem abscess is a rare and serious condition that requires intensive medical treatment whenever a suggestive diagnosis is made. This report presents the conservative treatment of a middle-aged female patient with a suspected ruptured medulla oblongata abscess. The patient reported a 7-day history of headaches and right-sided weakness. Her neurological symptoms gradually worsened, resulting in right hemiplegia, urinary retention, and swallowing difficulties. Brain magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a severe abscess in the medulla oblongata extending to the upper cervical spinal cord. After 8 weeks of antibiotic and steroid treatment, the patient demonstrated considerable improvement, and follow-up contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging revealed a significant reduction in abscess size. Neurological function recovered nearly to normal, with slight right hemiplegia noted at the 6-month follow-up. These findings suggest that conservative treatment may be feasible in carefully selected patients with a suspected ruptured brainstem abscess and may result in a good outcome; however, treatment decisions should remain individualized.

Introduction

Brainstem abscess is a rare and life-threatening condition. 1 In developed countries, the incidence of cerebral abscess ranges from 0.3 to 1.3 cases per 100,000 people per year. In other regions, the incidence may be higher, particularly among those with risk factors for an immunocompromised state. 2 The clinical symptoms of brainstem abscesses are highly variable, depending on the location of the lesion and the stage of the disease. Clinical presentations may include headache, neck stiffness, hemiplegia, quadriplegia, and coma, with or without infection, and may worsen rapidly if the patient is not diagnosed and treated immediately.3,4 Treatment is based on clinical manifestations, disease stage, and the underlying cause of the condition. 5 To date, 15 cases of brainstem abscess successfully treated with conservative therapy have been reported in the literature.6–20 In addition, among previously published cases, only two described intraventricular rupture,13,20 and most lesions were located in the pons, whereas involvement of the medulla oblongata remains particularly uncommon. We present a case of a middle-aged female patient with a suspected ruptured medulla oblongata abscess who was treated conservatively with antibiotic therapy, highlighting the potential of conservative treatment.

Case presentation

A female patient in her late 40s reported a 7-day history of headache and right-sided weakness. The patient had no history of chronic diseases, allergies, or head trauma. She sometimes experienced chills but did not check her body temperature. Her neurological symptoms gradually worsened, resulting in right hemiplegia, urinary retention, and swallowing difficulties, requiring admission to the emergency department of a local hospital in Thanh Hoa in October 2021. A non-contrast brain computed tomography (CT) scan was performed and yielded normal results. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit at the Bach Mai National Institute of Neuroscience (formerly the Neurology Center), Bach Mai Hospital.

The patient was alert on admission, scoring 15 on the Glasgow Coma Scale. The patient was well oriented and had a severe headache, neck stiffness, right hemiplegia with strength graded as 3/5, a sensory disturbance with a borderline level at the first cervical (C1) segment, urinary catheterization, and slight swallowing difficulty. Other cranial nerves and brainstem reflexes were intact. Her vital signs at presentation were normal, except for a temperature of 37.8°C. Blood tests showed increased inflammatory markers. These tests revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 13.1 × 109 /L (normal reference range: 4.0–10.0 × 109 /L), neutrophils of 90.5% (normal reference range: 50%–75%), and a C-reactive protein level of 34 mg/L (normal reference range: 0–10 mg/L). The patient was screened for diabetes; the results showed a fasting glucose level of 14 mmol/L and a hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) level of 10.8%. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis on the first day revealed 08 cells, no erythrocytes, and a total protein level of 1.08 g/L. CSF microorganism tests were all negative. Thereafter, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy (4 g/day ceftriaxone), steroids, and insulin were administered.

After 12 h of hospitalization, the patient suddenly became completely paralyzed on the right side of her body and developed new weakness on the left side, with muscle strength graded as 3/5. The patient experienced worsening head and neck pain and slight shortness of breath; however, she was still able to eat orally. A contrast-enhanced brain and cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan was performed immediately, revealing a ring-enhancing lesion with diffusion restriction and surrounding edema in the medulla oblongata extending to the C1–C3 segments of the cervical spinal cord, consistent with an abscess (Figure 1). The MRI also showed possible accumulation of pus in the ventricles. A lumbar puncture was repeated on day 2 of admission; the CSF showed white pus discharge and slightly increased pressure. The CSF results were 11,190 cells, with 90% neutrophils and 10% lymphocytes, a total protein level of 5.14 g/L, and a CSF-to-serum glucose ratio of 0.90. Microbiological tests were all negative. We consulted with neurosurgeons and spine surgeons and agreed on a conservative treatment strategy. We attempted to identify a primary source of infection through ear, nose, and throat endoscopy; dental examination; echocardiography; and lung and abdomen CT scans. However, none of these investigations identified a primary infection. The patient was immediately given aggressive antibiotic therapy consisting of meropenem (6 g/day) for 7 weeks, vancomycin (3 g/day) for 5 weeks, levofloxacin (1 g/day) for 2 weeks, steroids (dexamethasone initiated at 10 mg intravenously, followed by 4 mg every 6 h for 3 days), and insulin for blood glucose control. During hospitalization, the patient developed pneumonia caused by multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii and urinary candidiasis sensitive to fluconazole. She was subsequently treated with colistin for 9 days and fluconazole for 14 days.

After 8 weeks of intensive treatment, the patient was discharged from our hospital and transferred to a local rehabilitation center with right hemiplegia (grade 3/5), slight left arm weakness (grade 4/5), and complete recovery of the left leg. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient showed remarkable recovery, with normal strength on the left side (5/5) and significant improvement on the right side of the body (4/5). A follow-up brain MRI showed a scar in the medulla oblongata extending to the upper cervical spinal cord (Figure 2).

Before treatment was initiated, the patient's caregiver provided written informed consent for treatment. Our institution does not require ethical approval for individual case reports or case series. The case report was approved by the Medical Review Board of the Bach Mai National Institute of Neuroscience (formerly the Neurology Center), Bach Mai Hospital, Hanoi, Vietnam, on 20 February 2023. We extensively discussed the case with the patient and her caregiver, who provided verbal consent for publication of the data and accompanying images. All patient details have been deidentified. This case report conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 21

Discussion

Our report presented a case with rapid neurological deterioration due to the space-occupying effect of a suspected ruptured brainstem abscess. We applied aggressive antibiotic therapy early, resulting in dramatic improvement in the patient's clinical presentation and imaging findings. Medulla oblongata abscess is a rare and dangerous condition; its anatomical location makes drainage operations particularly challenging. Therefore, intensive medical treatment should be considered whenever a suggestive diagnosis is made. This is the best option to reduce mortality and limit neurological complications and sequelae. 1

Rupture of a brain abscess into the ventricular or subarachnoid space is usually associated with a high risk of ventriculitis, widespread inflammation, and poor outcomes. 22 In previously reported cases, such rupture often led to rapid neurological deterioration or required urgent surgery. 22 In contrast, our patient did not develop progressive ventriculitis or obstructive hydrocephalus, although the CSF findings suggested rupture. Several factors may have contributed to the favorable outcome, including early antibiotic treatment, stable hemodynamic status, and close neurological monitoring. These case-specific factors should be considered when interpreting the success of conservative management.

Conservative treatment for brainstem abscesses has been reported in 15 previous studies.6–20 Seven cases did not identify the underlying microorganism, and these cases had favorable outcomes similar to that of our patient.6–10,13,20 However, these cases represent limited experiences and may be subject to selection bias. Selected patients under close neurological and radiological monitoring, particularly those without severe mass effect, may be suitable candidates for conservative treatment. Therefore, the good outcome observed in this case should not be generalized to all patients with suspected ruptured medulla oblongata abscesses.

The microbiological diagnosis of brainstem infections remains challenging. Negative cerebrospinal fluid and blood cultures are not uncommon, particularly when broad-spectrum antibiotics are administered prior to sampling, as this can significantly reduce culture yield.23,24 In the present case, the negative findings are most likely explained by prior antibiotic exposure and the inability to obtain direct abscess material due to anatomical constraints. In addition, the infection may have involved fastidious or anaerobic organisms that are difficult to culture using routine methods. Although the CSF-to-serum glucose ratio appeared relatively preserved despite the markedly inflammatory CSF profile, this atypical finding was interpreted within the broader clinical, radiological, and treatment-response context. Furthermore, CSF may not accurately reflect the microbiological environment within a localized abscess cavity.

Current recommendations support prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, typically for 6–8 weeks or longer when microbiological confirmation is unavailable. 25 If drainage intervention can be performed successfully or CSF culture detects microorganisms, the antibiotic regimen should be adjusted according to the antibiogram, if necessary.5,25 Additionally, the role of corticosteroids in brain abscess remains controversial, and routine use is generally not recommended. However, short-term therapy may be considered in cases with severe edema or life-threatening mass effect, especially in the brainstem. 25 In our patient, corticosteroids were administered due to rapid neurological deterioration and concern for brainstem compression, with close monitoring.

After treatment, patients are required to undergo clinical and radiological follow-up. In our case, the source of infection was not identified, and the CSF analysis was negative for microorganisms; therefore, treatment with high-dose meropenem, vancomycin, and levofloxacin was considered reasonable. Follow-up MRI showed that the abscess cavity had resolved but left residual scar tissue in the medulla oblongata. This finding suggests permanent tissue damage rather than complete structural recovery. Therefore, clinical improvement does not necessarily indicate full neurological recovery, and long-term follow-up is required.

Surgical intervention remains an important treatment option in patients with progressive deterioration, significant mass effect, obstructive hydrocephalus, or failure of medical therapy. Management decisions should therefore be individualized according to clinical evolution and radiological findings.

The strength of our case report was the presentation of a rare case of suspected ruptured medulla oblongata abscess, with comprehensive clinical, imaging, and laboratory findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our case report describes a single case and therefore cannot be generalized. In addition, some investigations, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) screening, were not performed because the patient did not meet the criteria for testing according to Vietnam's current clinical guidelines.

In conclusion, we have highlighted that conservative antibiotic treatment may lead to a favorable outcome in carefully selected cases, especially those with unidentified causes. Initial management with broad-spectrum antibiotics may be considered before attempting invasive procedures when anatomical constraints make drainage difficult. However, treatment strategies should be individualized, and surgical therapies should not be delayed when clinically indicated.

Summary

Conservative treatment may be considered in selected patients with a brainstem abscess under close neurological and radiological monitoring. Broad-spectrum antibiotics with adequate central nervous system penetration are essential, and treatment duration should be guided by clinical and radiological responses.
