Microbial volatile compounds enhance drought resilience and productivity in CAM and C3 crops
Authors: Z. F. Nieves‐López, I. U. Merino‐Espinoza, F. Ureta‐Quiroz, S. Gudiño‐Vallejo, M. Heil, L. P. Partida‐Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Plants interact with their associated microbiota through diverse chemical cues, including microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs) that act as airborne signals that modulate plant physiology and stress resilience. This is particularly relevant under drought, a major constraint on global plant productivity. Although short‐term physiological responses to microbial volatiles have been widely documented, their longer‐term effects on functional plant traits and performance remain poorly understood, especially in species with contrasting photosynthetic strategies and water‐use characteristics.We investigated the effects of selected mVOCs—camphene (CAMP), ethyl isovalerate (EV), their combination (EV + CAMP), and nonanal—in Agave tequilana and Solanum lycopersicum cv. Micro‐Tom (tomato), representing CAM and C3 species, respectively. Agave and tomato seedlings were exposed once to volatiles and then grown for eight and four and a half months, respectively, under defined water‐limiting regimes. In Agave, we evaluated four groups of traits: leaf and biomass, roots, pigments and sugars and proline; whereas in tomato, we assessed growth and yield.EV followed by CAMP enhanced A. tequilana performance under moderate and severe water limitation, promoting leaf and root development, increased relative water content, as well as higher carbohydrates and chlorophyll levels. In tomato, volatile exposure increased fruit yield from 0.10 to 2.65‐fold under both well‐watered and moderate drought, with EV exerting the strongest effect under moderate drought. Nonanal yielded net negative effects on agaves, while the mix EV + CAMP showed positive effects on tomato, but poor results on A. tequilana.Our results demonstrate that EV and CAMP, alone or in combination, act as biologically active airborne signals that modulate plant physiological plasticity under water limitation. Remarkably, a single volatile exposure produced lasting effects on vegetative and reproductive development, highlighting their potential as sustainable tools to enhance drought resilience and crop productivity.

INTRODUCTION

Plants experience recurrent fluctuations in water availability, and drought represents one of the most pervasive selective pressures shaping plant physiology, growth and life‐history strategies (Farooq et al. 2012; Jägermeyr et al. 2021; Estrada et al. 2022). To cope with water limitation, plants deploy a wide array of morphological and physiological responses, including adjustments in root architecture, water‐use efficiency, osmotic regulation and photosynthetic metabolism (Pepe et al. 2022; Qiao et al. 2024; Rahmani et al. 2026).

These responses are particularly pronounced in species with contrasting photosynthetic strategies, such as Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM) plants, which exhibit specialized temporal separation of carbon fixation (Heyduk 2022). In contrast, C3 plants, which rely on continuous daytime CO2 uptake, are generally more sensitive to water deficit (Haghpanah et al. 2024).

Beyond intrinsic physiological traits, plants do not respond to drought in isolation but rather as part of complex biotic assemblages. Increasing evidence indicates that plant‐associated microorganisms play a critical role in modulating plant responses to abiotic stress, contributing to growth promotion, nutrient acquisition, and stress tolerance (Al‐Turki et al. 2023; Narayanasamy et al. 2023; Harkhani & Sharma 2024; Kumar & Sindhu 2024; Chattaraj et al. 2025). Within this context, the concept of the plant holobiont emphasizes that plant performance emerges from the integrated functioning of the host and its associated microbiota, mediated through multiple chemical and physical interactions (Partida‐Martínez & Heil 2011; Vandenkoornhuyse et al. 2015; Hassani et al. 2018).

Among the diverse mechanisms underlying plant–microbe communication, microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs) have gained attention as signals capable of diffusing and acting over distance (Kanchiswamy et al. 2015; Schulz‐Bohm et al. 2017). These compounds, produced by a wide range of bacteria and fungi, can influence plant growth, development, and defence by affecting processes such as root system architecture, photosynthesis, and hormonal signalling (Tyagi et al. 2018; Fincheira & Quiroz 2018; Garbeva & Weisskopf 2020; Poveda 2021).

The growth‐promoting activity of microbial volatiles has been primarily documented under well irrigated conditions and applying a prolonged or continuous exposure of the full bouquet of volatiles produced by specific bacterial or fungal strains in different C3 and C4 plant species such as Arabidopsis thaliana, tomato, tobacco, beans, sorghum and rice (Xie et al. 2009; Piechulla et al. 2017; Lopes et al. 2024; Tahir et al. 2017; Elizarraraz‐Martínez et al. 2024; Adame‐Garnica et al. 2026; Almeida et al. 2023; Dutta et al. 2025). Typically, seedlings are exposed to mVOCs for several days to weeks using experimental systems such as divided Petri dishes or sealed culture vessels, and short‐term responses, including changes in plant biomass, vegetative traits and gene expression, are evaluated either during or immediately after exposure (Gutiérrez‐Luna et al. 2010; Poveda 2021; Tan et al. 2026). Consequently, it remains unclear whether a transient, early exposure to microbial volatiles can induce persistent physiological and developmental changes that influence plant performance under prolonged water limitation.

CAM plants provide a particularly relevant yet under‐explored system to investigate volatile‐mediated stress modulation. Characterized by nocturnal CO2 uptake and high water‐use efficiency, CAM species are well adapted to arid and semi‐arid environments (North & Nobel 1998; Linton & Nobel 2001; Heyduk 2022). Nevertheless, even drought‐adapted plants experience significant constraints on growth and productivity under water‐limited conditions (Campos et al. 2014; Puente‐Garza et al. 2017; Jimenez‐Torres et al. 2021). In this context, the potential contribution of microbial‐derived volatile signals to plant stress resilience has received little attention.

In this study, we examined the effects of camphene (CAMP) and ethyl isovalerate (EV), employed alone and in combination, on plant performance under contrasting water availability regimes. These two bioactive microbial volatiles are emitted by Beauveria pseudobassiana and Bacillus spp., respectively, core members of the Agave and cacti microbiome (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019, 2021). CAMP (10 μm) increased primary and lateral root length in Arabidopsis and the length of the main root in A. tequilana and A. salmiana (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2021), while EV (50 μm) induced a higher number and density of lateral roots in Arabidopsis and a 37% increase in the relative growth rate in A. salmiana (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019). Using Agave tequilana as a representative CAM species and Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) as a C3 crop plant, we evaluated whether a single, early exposure to the bioactive doses of these volatiles is sufficient to induce lasting effects on vegetative growth, root development, physiological traits, and reproductive output under well‐watered, light, moderate and severe drought conditions. In addition, we included nonanal, a biologically active herbivore‐induced volatile organic compound (HI‐VOC) previously associated with the induction of systemic resistance and enhanced plant performance, as a comparative volatile treatment (Yi et al. 2009; Elizarraraz‐Martínez et al. 2024). We hypothesized that a single and early exposure to microbial volatiles could induce long‐term effects on modulating plant physiology under water‐limiting conditions, promoting traits associated with drought tolerance and potentially improving yield (Ryu et al. 2003; Sharifi & Ryu 2018; Garbeva & Weisskopf 2020). By integrating physiological, developmental, and productivity‐related traits, this work supports the notion that mVOCs derived from the microbiota associated with desert plants help improve plant performance under drought not only in CAM plants but also in C3 crops such as tomato.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

Micropropagated plants of Agave tequilana F.A.C. Weber (agave) and seeds of Solanum lycopersicum L. var. Micro‐Tom (tomato) were used as representatives of CAM and C3 photosynthetic strategies, respectively. A set of 960 micropropagated plants of A. tequilana were produced from seeds collected at Cinvestav Irapuato (20.7196141, −101.3304995), micropropagated via indirect organogenesis (Ramírez‐Malagón et al. 2008) and grown in vitro at 25 °C ± 3 °C under an 8 h/16 h dark/light photoperiod for 2 months. Agaves were grown in a soil mixture of red volcanic rock (tezontle) and oak leaf soil (1:1) as before (Chávez‐González et al. 2024), and acclimatized for 3 weeks in a growth chamber under the same photoperiod and at 28 °C ± 3 °C. Afterwards, agaves were transferred to a greenhouse under natural light, with a 9 h/15 h dark/light photoperiod, a temperature of 32 °C ± 5 °C, and a relative humidity of 17% ± 8%. After 1 week in the greenhouse, agaves were exposed to volatiles and maintained under the same conditions for the duration of the experiment.

For tomato, Micro‐Tom seeds (Tomato Growers, SKU: 6536‐BULK1) were sown in germination trays containing a peat‐based soil mixture supplemented with perlite and vermiculite (3:1:1) as reported (Patruno et al. 2006; Pirinç & Akalp 2024). Seeds were germinated in darkness at 30 °C, and seedlings were transferred to a growth chamber after cotyledon emergence. After 15 days, seedlings were transplanted into 1 L pots and maintained under the same greenhouse photoperiod as agave plants, at 25 °C ± 3 °C.

Exposure of plants to microbial volatiles

Camphene (CAMP; CAS No. 79‐92‐5), ethyl isovalerate (EV; CAS No. 108‐64‐5), and nonanal (CAS No. 124‐19‐6) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Volatile doses were selected based on previous studies reporting biologically active concentrations that promote plant growth and elicit physiological responses in different plant systems (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019, 2021; Elizarraraz‐Martínez et al. 2024).

Volatile treatments were applied once at an early stage of development. For agaves (age ca. 3 months, size 9.8 × 0.6 cm leaf and root length, respectively), 50 ml of volatile solution were applied per plant by spraying. CAMP was applied at 10 μM (ethanol‐diluted) and EV at 50 μM (water‐diluted), either individually or in combination (one volatile applied after the other). Nonanal was applied separately as a lanolin‐based paste (8 mg g−1 nonanal in lanolin) at the centre of each plant tray for 48 h. Mock‐treated plants received tap water only. Each volatile treatment was applied to 160 plants arranged in 40 plants per tray (n = 40 replicates).

For tomato (15‐day‐old seedlings), CAMP was applied at 10 μM and EV at 25 μM, either individually or in combination. The lower EV concentration (25 μM) was chosen because the higher dose used in agaves (50 μM) induced noticeable stress symptoms in tomato seedlings during the preliminary tests (data not shown). Volatile solutions were prepared in an aqueous buffer containing 0.5% (v/v) Tween 20 to ensure uniform coverage. Mock plants were treated with the same solution but without volatiles. The volume of solution applied per plant was estimated based on canopy coverage of 15‐day‐old seedlings. Following application, tomato plants were temporarily enclosed with cellophane for 6–8 h to allow volatile exposure. Each volatile treatment was applied to 40 plants divided into 20 plants per drought level (n = 20).

The methodological differences in volatile application between agave and tomato were associated with differences in plant morphology and age. Tomato seedlings exhibited limited aerial biomass and a dispersed architecture, whereas agave plants displayed a compact rosette structure. Accordingly, tomato seedlings were temporarily maintained in a closed space after application, and Tween 20 was included to improve volatile dispersion during spraying, whereas agave plants allowed uniform application without surfactant. A schematic overview of the experimental design is provided in Fig. 1.

Drought treatments and experimental design

After volatile treatment, different levels of water limitation were imposed on plants by maintaining fixed relative soil moisture (RSM) values throughout the experiments using a soil moisture monitor (MeteoMex Terra) (Winkler 2021). Well‐watered plants (WW) were maintained at 70% relative soil moisture (RSM), whereas light (LD), moderate (MD), and severe drought (SD) treatments corresponded to 50%, 30%, and 20% RSM, respectively. For Agave tequilana, plants were subjected to WW, LD, MD, and SD conditions for 8 months. This duration reflects the typical period of low or negligible precipitation in agave‐growing regions (Coleman‐Derr et al. 2016). Based on the results on agaves, tomato plants were exposed to WW and MD conditions for four and a half months, allowing plants to complete the vegetative cycle and reach the productive stage (Fig. 1). Each treatment—volatile and drought level—included 40 individual plants for agaves and 20 plants for tomato.

Assessment of vegetative and root traits

Phenotypic traits of agaves were assessed every 2 months over the 8‐month trial by randomly sampling six plants per volatile and drought treatment. Leaf height, leaf length and leaf area were measured from scale‐calibrated digital images using Fiji software (Schindelin et al. 2012). Fresh and dry biomass were determined using analytical balances (OHAUS PA214 and HR‐60, ±0.01 g precision). Root system architecture was evaluated after gently washing roots free of substrate. Root images were captured with a flatbed scanner and analysed using RhizoVision Explorer (version 2.0.3; (Seethepalli et al. 2021)) to extract total root length, average diameter and branching patterns. Semi‐automatic root tracing parameters were set according to the software guidelines to ensure consistency across treatments.

In tomato, vegetative traits including leaf and branch number and plant height were recorded weekly during the first 2 months following volatile exposure. At the end of the experiment, four plants per volatile and drought treatment were harvested. Shoots and roots were separated and dried at 37 °C for 3 days prior to dry biomass determination.

Physiological traits

Physiological traits were assessed only in agaves. Relative water content (RWC) was estimated from leaf disks harvested at midday following the Weatherley method (Weatherley 1950) with adaptations from Begum et al. (2022). Measurements were performed on fully expanded leaves at comparable developmental stages across treatments.

Biochemical analysis

Biochemical markers were quantified in A. tequilana leaves and stem tissue. These included photosynthetic pigments, free proline and soluble and insoluble sugars, measured using a Multiskan GO microplate reader (Thermo Scientific) following established protocols. Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents (pigments traits) were measured spectrophotometrically after extraction from 10‐mm diameter freeze‐dried leaf disks in 80% acetone, following Wellburn (1994) and Duarte‐Aké et al. (2016), with far‐red wavelength corrections (710–750 nm) as described by Porra et al. (1989).

Free proline was quantified in 0.5 g of fully expanded A. tequilana leaves per treatment using the rapid determination method of Bates et al. (1973). Carbohydrates were quantified from 10 mg of dry‐ground leaf and stem (“piña”) tissue using colorimetric assays adapted from Doran & de Souza (2021). These metabolites were selected due to their well‐established roles in osmotic regulation, carbon storage and stress protection, particularly in plants exposed to prolonged water limitation (Cushman & Borland 2002; Chaves et al. 2009).

Fruit yield assessment in tomato

Reproductive performance in tomato was assessed based on fruit yield under well‐watered (WW) and moderate drought (MD) conditions. Ripe fruits were harvested weekly from individual plants, considering only fruits exhibiting a uniform and intense red coloration as an indicator of full ripeness. Fruit yield was determined as the total number of fruits produced per plant across the harvest period, using all plants included in each volatile treatment and water regime. Fruit fresh weight was estimated from a representative sub‐sample of 50 ripe fruits per volatile treatment and water regime, randomly selected from the total pool of harvested fruits and weighed individually.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Core Team 2026). For experiments with agaves, data were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis, which is an appropriate alternative to ANOVA since small sample sizes (n = 3 to 6 per treatment and sampling time) do not allow for a reliable assessment of normality. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant variation (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons between each treatment and the Mock control were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (Mann–Whitney U‐test). P‐values were not corrected for multiple comparisons due to the low statistical power caused by reduced sample sizes. This makes the usual FDR (False Discovery Rate) corrections too strict and risks masking biologically relevant differences. For the same reason—reduced power with n ≈ 3–6—relaxed significance thresholds of P < 0.10 and P < 0.15 were deliberately included alongside the conventional P < 0.05, as these represent trends that may reflect genuine biological effects insufficiently supported by the sample size rather than true absence of biologically relevant patterns (Hammer et al. 2025). Tomato data did not meet normality assumptions; therefore, non‐parametric Wilcoxon tests were applied. Comparisons were defined a priori between mock‐treated plants and each volatile treatment. P‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction. In these analyses, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Early exposure to EV or CAMP improves Agave tequilana performance under drought, while the combined EV + CAMP and nonanal treatments reduce plant fitness

Every 2 months, we analysed the effects of early volatile exposure in micropropagated plants of Agave tequilana subjected to four defined and contrasting drought levels for a total of 8 months (Fig. 1). Twenty‐two response variables grouped in leaf and biomass (5), roots (7), pigments (6) and sugars and proline (4) were evaluated at each drought level, volatile treatment and time (Fig. S1). In Fig. 2, we summarise the effects observed at the end of our 8‐month experiment, which mimics the length of the dry season that agaves normally experience in their natural habitats. Detailed data supporting this heatmap has been included in Fig. S2. In Fig. 3, we present photos of the agaves that were sampled and measured at this last time point. These figures show that plants exposed to EV or CAMP tended to display improved physiological and biochemical performance compared with mock‐treated plants, particularly under moderate (MD) and severe drought (SD) (Figs 2 and 3). EV elicited the most positive response, enhancing some shoot‐ and root‐related traits, water status and pigment content. Also, all plants treated with EV survived and looked more vigorous across drought treatments (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2c). CAMP, on the other hand, induced moderate but consistent improvements. In contrast, the combined EV + CAMP and nonanal treatments significantly reduced plant fitness, especially by limiting root development across all or most drought treatments, which negatively impacted shoot development, biomass, sugars accumulation and proline levels (Figs 2 and 3).

Plant numbers per treatment ranged from three to six due to mortality caused by drought and/or plant vulnerability (Fig. S2c).

Under well‐watered (WW) conditions, EV‐treated plants exhibited a significantly higher relative water content (RWC) and increased chlorophyll B levels compared with mock‐treated plants (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). In addition, insoluble carbohydrate content was significantly higher in plants exposed to CAMP, EV or the EV + CAMP combination relative to the control (P < 0.05), indicating that microbial volatiles can modulate carbon allocation even in the absence of water limitation.

When plants were subjected to light drought (LD), CAMP‐treated plants tended to increase RWC, leaf area and length, but reduce sugars and proline (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). EV treatment, on the contrary, resulted in a significant increase in free proline content relative to mock‐treated plants (P < 0.05), which may be associated with an osmotic adjustment under mild water deficit. Moreover, these EV‐treated plants tended to have more chlorophylls and carotenoids, as well as greater leaf area (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2).

Under MD conditions, EV‐treated plants exhibited a significant increase in root width (P < 0.05) compared with mock plants, that suggests a robust modification of root system architecture under intermediate water limitation. Additionally, EV exposure tended to increase leaf area and length, RWC, dry biomass, the number of roots, root system depth and diameter, as well as carotenoids, but reduce soluble carbohydrates (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). CAMP promoted higher leaf area and length, RWC, root system depth and soluble carbohydrates, but lower chlorophyll ratio, insoluble and total carbohydrates, and proline. However, these responses did not reach statistical significance at the P < 0.05 threshold (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2).

Under SD, EV‐treated plants tended to increase root width and maintain higher chlorophyll content, chlorophyll‐to‐carotenoid ratios and carbohydrate levels, which may indicate enhanced physiological adjustment. In the case of CAMP, a tendency to increase insoluble and total carbohydrates but reduce proline was observed. However, none of these traits differed significantly from mock‐treated plants at P < 0.05 (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2).

Together, these results support that early exposure to microbial volatiles, especially EV followed by CAMP, mitigates drought‐associated physiological and phenotypic damage in Agave tequilana by sustaining root and shoot traits linked to water status and carbon allocation, including statistically significant improvements in key parameters under specific drought regimes. However, the poor performance observed in Agave plants treated with EV + CAMP and nonanal makes us hypothesize that the applied doses were not appropriate (applicable to both), or that the combination of volatiles acts in different and/or competing pathways that cannot achieve synergy (EV + CAMP), or that host‐factors vary and responses are not conserved across plants (nonanal).

Long‐term effects of early volatile exposure on tomato vegetative traits

The promising results from EV and CAMP on agaves prompted us to investigate if these volatiles could also have positive effects on tomato plants under moderate drought. Thus, we assessed changes on morphology and yield of a single and early exposure of tomato plants to CAMP, EV and their combination under WW and MD (Figs 4 and 5). Leaf and branch numbers were monitored up to the third‐ and sixth‐weeks post‐exposure, respectively. Under WW, mock and EV‐treated plants produced significantly more leaves than those exposed to CAMP or the EV + CAMP mixture. However, under MD, EV‐treated plants outperformed all other treatments (Fig. 4a). Branch counts were lower in CAMP and EV + CAMP treatments under WW, whereas under MD, EV‐ and EV + CAMP‐treated plants showed a significant increase compared with the mock (Fig. 4b). These results may reflect that the effects of volatiles on vegetative traits depend on both the compound and the water regime.

In addition, we measured the root and shoot biomass at the end of the experiment. Root dry weight did not differ among treatments under WW; however, under MD, CAMP‐ and EV + CAMP‐treated plants had significantly higher root biomass than the mock (Fig. 4c). Shoot biomass increased in EV‐treated plants under WW, whereas no differences were observed under MD (Fig. 4d). Plant height increased in CAMP and EV + CAMP treatments under WW, but remained unaffected under MD (Fig. 4e). Representative images illustrating overall plant architecture under WW and MD are shown in Fig. 4f.

Volatiles increase tomato fruit yield, especially under drought

To assess the long‐term effects of early volatile exposure on tomato productivity, we monitored the number of flowers as well as the number and weight of harvested fruits from week 14–18 post‐exposure under WW and MD (Fig. S3). The number of flowers per plant and the weight of fruits did not differ significantly between treatments under either water regime (Fig. 3c and Fig. S3a). However, the number of total harvested fruits was lower under MD than under WW, as expected (Fig. 5a). Volatile‐treated plants produced more fruits than mock plants in both water regimes, with the largest increases observed under MD (Fig. 5a). Under WW, EV, the mixture and CAMP increased fruit yield by 27%, 18% and 9.9%, respectively (mock = 212 fruits). Under MD, EV‐treated plants produced 165% more fruits than mock, while CAMP and the mixture increased yield by 95% and 78%, respectively (mock = 60 fruits). This remarkable increase in total yield was associated with a higher productivity per plant under MD (Fig. S3b). Our findings demonstrate that a single early exposure to EV, CAMP or their combination during the seedling stage promotes fruit production and ripening, even under drought.

DISCUSSION

Early exposure to a single dose of EV or CAMP induces long‐term drought resilience in a CAM species

The growth‐promoting effects of mVOCs have been documented across diverse plant species (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019, 2021; Poveda 2021; Almeida et al. 2023; Srikamwang et al. 2023; Lopes et al. 2024). However, most studies have focused on short‐term physiological responses and/or model plants under controlled and well‐irrigated conditions (Fincheira & Quiroz 2018; Tyagi et al. 2018; Poveda 2021). As a result, it remains unclear whether volatile‐induced effects persist over time and remain relevant under long‐term and ecologically realistic drought scenarios. Drought remains the most pervasive abiotic stress affecting terrestrial ecosystems, with increasing frequency and intensity driven by climate change and the global expansion of drylands, severely constraining plant fitness and productivity (Nobel Park 1994; Farooq et al. 2012; Huang et al. 2016; Fahad et al. 2017). Even species adapted to arid environments, such as CAM plants, may approach their tolerance limits under prolonged or recurrent water limitation (Linton & Nobel 2001; Borland et al. 2014; Heyduk 2022). In this context, agaves—long‐lived CAM crops of major economic, ecological and cultural importance—represent a relevant but under‐explored system to evaluate whether desert‐derived microbial volatile cues can induce durable drought resilience beyond immediate stress responses (Nobel Park 1994; Jimenez‐Torres et al. 2021). Here, we show that a single and early exposure to EV and CAMP tended to improve drought‐associated traits in A. tequilana, as depicted in Figs 2 and 3. Despite the fact that we used 40 Agave plants per treatment, our sampling strategy of using six random plants every 2 months together with plant mortality significantly reduced the number of individual plants that we had at the end of the experiment. This fact limited the robustness of our statistical analyses, but the trends observed along time were consistent (Figs S1 and S2), as well as the phenotypes (Fig. 3). EV‐ followed by CAMP‐treated plants showed trends towards enhanced leaf traits, plant water status, and root system architecture, especially under moderate drought (MD) (Figs 2 and 3), a condition that closely resembles the native climatic regimes of A. tequilana cultivation and distribution areas (Maples‐Vermeersch 2002; Ruiz Corral et al. 2002). The concurrent tendencies towards increases in leaf area and length, RWC, and root dimensions suggest a combination of traits that plausibly optimizes water acquisition and retention, thereby potentially supporting plant function under drought (North & Nobel 1998; Linton & Nobel 2001; Farooq et al. 2012; Riaz et al. 2016; Qiao et al. 2024).

In EV‐treated plants, RWC increased significantly only under WW (P < 0.05). Under drought conditions, EV‐ and CAMP‐treated plants showed trends towards increased RWC in specific regimens (in MD: EV and CAMP; in LD: CAMP). Elevated RWC may indicate improved water retention capacity (González & González‐Vilar 2003) and enhanced ability to maintain cellular turgor and physiological function under water limitation (Sapes & Sala 2021).

In parallel, chlorophyll content and carotenoids tended to be enhanced in EV‐treated plants across water regimes (Fig. 2). These patterns suggest that EV may enhance the maintenance of the photosynthetic machinery, potentially by mitigating pigment degradation and sustaining photochemical capacity. In this context, preservation of chlorophyll pools is consistent with improved stress tolerance and has been associated with sustained photosynthetic efficiency under drought (Campos et al. 2014; Riaz et al. 2016; Simkin et al. 2022).

Additionally, EV and CAMP modulated carbohydrate‐related traits (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). These findings suggest that EV and CAMP may further reinforce carbon allocation strategies potentially supporting osmotic protection and mitigating stress. In agaves, non‐soluble sugars such as fructans are predominantly stored in the stem (Pérez‐López et al. 2021). Therefore, the modulation of carbohydrate pools by EV and CAMP aligns with mechanisms known to maintain turgor, protect cellular structures, and sustain metabolic function under water limitation (Valluru & van den Ende 2008; Singh et al. 2020). Notably, EV also significantly increased proline levels under LD (P < 0.05), further supporting its role in osmotic adjustment and stress resilience.

Unexpectedly, the EV + CAMP and nonanal treatments caused negative effects on A. tequilana, with plants exhibiting the poorest physiological performance even under WW. These treatments had positive effects on Agave marmorata and beans, respectively (Elizarraraz‐Martínez et al. 2024; Lovaco‐Flores 2025). Thus, the most plausible explanation for the inhibition of root development and its negative effects on global plant fitness are that the doses used were higher than those required to promote growth (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019, 2021), potentially triggering toxicity or metabolic trade‐offs that interfered with stress‐response pathways rather than enhancing them (Jin et al. 2021). These observations underscore the need to perform preliminary tests with each volatile using a range of bioactive concentrations in the desired plant hosts to determine the optimal host‐volatile dose.

Taken together, these findings align with previous reports suggesting that microbial volatiles enhance drought tolerance by reinforcing coordinated leaf, root, water status and metabolic traits, including increased synthesis of photosynthetic pigments (Poveda 2021; Yasmin et al. 2021; Lopes et al. 2024). The pronounced responses under moderate drought reflect the water regimes typically experienced by A. tequilana in its native range. Such findings indicate that early volatile exposure can induce long‐term improvements in drought‐associated traits, likely by modulating pre‐existing adaptive physiological processes.

Microbial volatiles enhance tomato fruit yield even under drought

The promising results achieved by EV and CAMP on agaves made us wonder if a C3 plant such as tomato would also favourably respond to them under moderate drought.

Previous studies using tomato and microbial volatiles showed positive effects on vegetative traits such as biomass, plant height and lateral root development (Lee et al. 2016; Asha et al. 2021), but no study before analysed the effect of specific mVOCs on fruit yield under prolonged drought.

Our study shows that a single and early exposure to EV, CAMP and EV + CAMP increases fruit yield, especially under moderate drought (Fig. 5 and Fig. S3b). These increases in fruit production occurred without reductions in fruit weight (Fig. S3c), indicating that increased productivity did not come at the expense of fruit quality. Fruit production, as expected, was strongly affected by drought, with reductions of 72%, 50%, 41% and 58% in Mock, CAMP, EV, and EV + CAMP plants, respectively. The observed effects in drought‐stressed plants are consistent with previously reported negative impacts of water stress on plant growth, physiology, and productivity (Meel & Saharan 2024). Although drought reduced fruit harvest, plants exposed to microbial volatiles showed smaller reductions compared to Mock plants. Exposure to EV mitigated these reductions most effectively, highlighting the potential of microbial volatiles to buffer drought‐induced losses in crop productivity.

These findings are truly remarkable and promising, but the underlying mechanisms remain unresolved. In particular, it is not possible to determine whether the observed long‐term effects on fruit yield arise from a priming mechanism or from sustained physiological changes initiated by the early exposure. Further studies will be required to disentangle these possibilities and identify the molecular mechanisms underpinning these long‐term responses.

Shared long‐term effects of microbial volatiles across CAM and C3 plants

Despite their contrasting photosynthetic pathways and water‐use strategies, Agave tequilana (CAM) and tomato (C3) exhibited convergent long‐term improvements in drought‐associated performance following a single early exposure to microbial volatiles. In both species, volatile treatments enhanced root‐related traits that are potentially linked to improved water and nutrient acquisition under moderate drought.

In tomato, CAMP significantly increased root dry weight per plant under MD, whereas in agave, both CAMP and EV promoted deeper and wider root systems (Figs 4c and 2). This convergence across distinct plant functional types suggests that microbial volatiles can reinforce drought resilience by mitigating the negative effects of water limitation on root systems, thereby supporting whole‐plant performance and, as shown in tomato, increased fruit yield (North & Nobel 1998; Comas et al. 2013).

Mechanistically, these shared responses point to functional adjustments in root system architecture that facilitate access to deeper soil water pools, a trait strongly associated with the maintenance of productivity under water‐limited conditions (Comas et al. 2013; Meel & Saharan 2024). Consistent with previous reports (Camarena‐Pozos et al. 2019, 2021), the root architectural modifications induced by EV and CAMP likely enhance water and nutrient uptake, processes that are typically constrained under drought stress (Meel & Saharan 2024). Unfortunately, we did not generate molecular data in this study to address the mechanisms of volatile action in Agave and tomato. However, our results suggest the involvement of drought‐associated signalling pathways in mediating the observed responses to volatile exposure. Previous studies have shown that microbial volatiles can activate stress‐responsive signalling networks associated with drought tolerance. For instance, the bacterial volatile 2R,3R‐butanediol produced by Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 enhanced drought resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana through SA‐dependent mechanisms associated with reduced water loss and ROS production (Cho et al. 2008). Similarly, exposure to bacterial volatiles increased root growth in drought‐stressed Zea mays plants (Lopes et al. 2024). Reduced water loss and improved root development were observed and quantified for agaves and tomato in this study as described above. These responses might be associated with interconnected stress‐signalling pathways. Abscisic acid (ABA) is a central regulator of plant drought responses, including stomatal regulation, water conservation, and root architectural remodelling (Cutler et al. 2010). ABA, SA, and ROS signalling pathways are tightly interconnected during drought responses, with ROS acting as key mediators of stomatal regulation and stress acclimation (Bandurska & Stroi ski 2005; Miura & Tada 2014).

CONCLUSIONS

This work supports the view that microbial volatiles act as early chemical cues capable of shaping long‐term plant responses to water limitation. By extending volatile‐mediated signalling to long‐term drought responses in contrasting plant functional types (CAM and C3), this study highlights the relevance of microbial volatiles for plant adaptation in water‐limited environments and their broad application potential. The responses observed may be associated with enhanced nutrient acquisition and improvements in root system architecture promoted by volatile exposure. In line with the effects detected on tomato productivity, future studies should assess the applicability of this approach in C4 crops such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum. Research is also needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying microbial volatile perception and signalling in plants. In addition, it remains necessary to determine whether these responses involve priming or sustained physiological changes under both optimal growth conditions and drought stress.

The application of EV and CAMP, alone or in combination, is a cost‐effective, safe and scalable strategy. Both compounds are readily commercially available and used as commodities in the food and perfume industries. They act at low doses and, as shown here, a single exposure in young plants can elicit sustained positive effects on crop productivity and performance, even under drought. Currently, different formulations and application protocols are being tested for crop production in arid regions.
