Construction and characterization of recombinant duck enteritis virus expressing duck hepatitis A virus 3 immunogenic genes
Authors: Wenfeng Jia, Anping Wang, Zhi Wu, Lei Shi, Jun Xie, Qingkang Zhou, Yuting Cheng, Xinnuo Lei, Li Liu, Long Tian, Shanyuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck enteritis virus (DEV) is considered an ideal vector for waterfowl vaccine development due to its favorable safety profile and multiple genomic sites that accommodate foreign gene insertion. Duck hepatitis A virus (DHAV) causes acute hepatitis, neurological symptoms, and high mortality in young ducklings, and the predominant circulating serotype has shifted from type 1 to type 3, underscoring the urgent need for improved vaccines. In this study, we employed a CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing platform combined with dual single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) and a homologous directed repair (HDR) strategy to construct two recombinant DEV strains (rDEV-DHAV) expressing the immunogenic VP0 or VP1 proteins of DHAV-3. The recombinants were purified by plaque selection and validated using PCR, Western blotting, indirect immunofluorescence, and animal experiments. Both recombinant viruses replicated efficiently in chicken embryo fibroblasts and exhibited growth kinetics comparable to the parental DEV vaccine strain. The inserted VP0 and VP1 genes remained genetically stable over at least 15 serial passages. Immunization trials in ducklings demonstrated that both recombinants elicited strong humoral responses against DEV and DHAV-3. Safety evaluation showed that neither recombinant virus induced clinical signs, pathological lesions, or abnormal viral shedding, and both displayed safety profiles equivalent to the parental vaccine strain. Overall, the two rDEV-DHAV strains generated in this study are genetically stable, safe, and exhibit good immunogenicity. The HDR-CRISPR/Cas9 strategy employing dual sgRNAs provides an efficient approach for the rapid construction of multivalent DEV vector vaccines, highlighting its substantial potential in poultry vaccine development.Keywords: Duck enteritis virus, Duck hepatitis A virus type 3, CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, Vector vaccine, VP0 or VP1 gene

Introduction

Duck plague (DP), caused by duck enteritis virus (DEV), and duck viral hepatitis (DVH), primarily induced by duck hepatitis A virus (DHAV), are two major infectious diseases responsible for substantial economic losses in the global waterfowl industry. DEV infection can affect ducks of all ages, leading to severe hemorrhagic lesions, vascular damage, and necrosis of parenchymal organs, often accompanied by high mortality (Dhama et al., 2017). In contrast, DHAV predominantly affects ducklings younger than four weeks of age and is characterized by peracute onset and mortality rates that may exceed 95% (Niu, et al., 2019). Among the three recognized DHAV serotypes (DHAV-1, DHAV-2, and DHAV-3), DHAV-1 and DHAV-3 are the most prevalent type detected across Asia, Europe, and the Americas (Wen, et al., 2014; Yang, et al., 2023). Notably, the limited cross-protection between different types, particularly the insufficient efficacy of DHAV-1 vaccines against DHAV-3 infection, coupled with the increasing dominance of DHAV-3 strains in recent years, poses ongoing challenges to disease control programs (Kim, et al., 2024; Zhao, et al., 2024). Therefore, the development of a new vaccine capable of simultaneously protecting against DVE and DHAV-3 is of great importance for enhancing biosecurity in duck production.

Duck hepatitis A virus belongs to the genus Avihepatovirus within the family Picornaviridae. Its genome consists of a linear, single-stranded positive-sense RNA containing a large open reading frame that encodes a polyprotein, flanked by untranslated regions (UTRs) (Wen, et al., 2018). This polyprotein is first cleaved by viral proteases into three precursor proteins (P1, P2, and P3), which undergo further proteolytic processing to yield the final functional protein products: the P1 region generates three structural proteins(VP0, VP3, and VP1), the P2 region yields five non-structural proteins(2A1, 2A2, 2A3, 2B, and 2C), while the P3 region yields four non-structural proteins: 3A, 3B, 3C, and 3D. The major antigenic epitopes of DHAV are primarily located within the three structural proteins VP0, VP3, and VP1, which determine the antigenicity and pathogenicity of the virus, making them key targets for developing novel DHAV vaccines (Niu, et al., 2020; Truong and Cheng, 2022; Wen, et al., 2023).

In veterinary vaccinology, recombinant viral vectors have emerged as an efficient and safe strategy for delivering protective antigens, particularly for multivalent vaccine development. Among the various viral vectors, herpesviruses occupy a prominent position (Jia, et al., 2022; Li, et al., 2024). As an alpha-herpesvirinae, DEV possesses several characteristics that make it an attractive vaccine vector: a large genome containing multiple nonessential regions suitable for foreign gene insertion, rapid replication capable of inducing early humoral and cellular immune responses, relative insensitivity to maternal antibodies, long-lasting immunity due to persistent infection, and a restricted natural host range limited to Anatidae, ensuring high safety when used as a live vaccine. DEV-vectored vaccines have been explored for delivering protective antigens of a wide range of avian pathogens, including avian influenza virus (Liu, et al., 2011), Newcastle disease virus (Ding, et al., 2019), infectious bronchitis virus(Li, et al., 2016), duck tembusu virus (Chen, et al., 2014), DHAV (Yang, et al., 2021), goose astrovirus (Chen, et al., 2025), Pasteurella multocida (Apinda, et al., 2022), and evenChlamydia psittaci (Liu, et al., 2024).

Traditionally, the construction of herpesvirus vectors has primarily relied on homologous recombination or bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) technology (Sun, et al., 2017; Wang, et al., 2018). These methods typically involve lengthy operational cycles, limited efficiency, and high technical barriers, particularly for herpesviruses with large genomes (Liu, et al., 2019). The advent of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) genome editing has greatly enhanced both the efficiency and precision of recombinant herpesvirus generation. Previous studies have demonstrated that the CRISPR/Cas9 system can successfully integrate exogenous genes into the herpesvirus genome, enabling the development of safe and effective recombinant vaccine candidates (Chang, et al., 2019; Luo, et al., 2022). In our previous work, we systematically optimized the key experimental parameters of a homology-directed repair (HDR)-based CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system for the construction of recombinant DEV, thereby markedly enhancing the efficiency of recombinant DEV generation. Using this optimized strategy, the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) gene was precisely inserted into the intergenic region between the UL27 and UL26 genes, resulting in a recombinant DEV (rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26). Cells infected with this recombinant virus exhibited clear and stable green fluorescence, and the inserted genetic trait remained stable during serial passaging. This provides a solid technological foundation and vector platform for the rapid and efficient development of DEV-based viral vector vaccine candidates.

In this study, we employed an HDR-CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing strategy to generate two recombinant DEV carrying the VP0 or VP1 gene of DHAV-3. The foreign genes were precisely inserted into the intergenic region between the UL27 and UL26 genes of the DEV vaccine strain. The biological properties, safety, and antibody-inducing capacity of the resulting recombinant DEVs were subsequently systematically evaluated.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus, and antibodies

Chicken embryo fibroblast (CEF) cells were prepared from 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs. CEF cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibico) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Forma, Marietta, OH, USA). The DEV vaccine strain (NCBI accession number EU082088.2) is maintained in our laboratory. The recombinant virus rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, used as the intermediate vector in this study, was previously constructed and is also preserved in our laboratory. The DHAV-3 JSYC strain were isolated and are maintained in our laboratory. Rabbit polyclonal antibodies specific for DHAV VP1, VP0, and DEV gC proteins were generated and likewise stored in our laboratory.

Construction of gRNA-Cas9 plasmids and donor plasmids

Guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) were designed using the CRISPR design tool (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/crispor.py). The synthesized oligonucleotides were cloned into the pX459-V2 plasmid (Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA) at the Bbs I restriction site to construct the gRNA-Cas9 expression plasmids, named px459-gRNA-5′EGFP and px459-gRNA-3′EGFP. Construction and purification of recombinant DEV-DHAV

CEF cells were co-transfected with the two gRNA-Cas9 plasmids and the corresponding donor plasmid (pDonor-VP0 or pDonor-VP1) using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (Mirus, Madison, WI, USA). Six hours post-transfection, the cells were infected with the intermediate vector, rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.2. The cells were incubated for an additional 48 h until a cytopathic effect (CPE) was evident. The resulting cell supernatant and cell lysates were collected, and crude virus stocks were prepared by three cycles of freezing and thawing. Recombinant viruses were subsequently isolated through successive rounds of plaque purification combined with PCR screening for confirmed foreign gene integration. The final purified recombinant viruses were designated rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1.

Purity analysis of recombinant DEV-DHAV

Recombinant viruses rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 were inoculated into CEF cells. When approximately 80% CPE was observed, DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). PCR amplification was performed using primers TUL27/26-F/R, EGFP-F/R, VP0-F/R, and VP1-F/R to verify the presence of the VP0 and VP1 genes and the purity of the recombinant virus. Primer sequences are shown in Table 3. The PCR products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Western blotting assay

CEF cells were individually infected with the DEV vaccine strain, rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, rDEV-DHAV-VP0, or rDEV-DHAV-VP1 at an MOI of 0.01. At 48 h post-infection (hpi), cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer (Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Solarbio Biotech, Beijing, China). Protein samples were mixed with protein loading buffer (Yeasen Biotech) and denatured by boiling at 100°C for 10 min. The proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Solarbio Biotech). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk powder (Solarbio Biotech) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-DHAV VP0 polyclonal antibody (1:500), rabbit anti-DHAV VP1 polyclonal antibody (1:500), rabbit anti-DEV glycoprotein C (gC) polyclonal antibody (1:500), and a rabbit anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (Yeasen Biotech, 1:5000) as an internal loading control. After washing three times with TBST (5 min each), the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Yeasen Biotech, 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Indirect immunofluorescence (IFA) assay

CEF cells were cultured on coverslips and infected with the DEV vaccine Strain, rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1 at an MOI of 0.01. After 48 hpi, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-DHAV VP0 and VP1 specific primary antibodies diluted 1:200. After washing, the cells were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Solarbio Biotech) at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at 37°C. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Solarbio Biotech) at room temperature for 5 min. Protein expression was visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Growth curve analysis

The replication capacity of the recombinant viruses was evaluated by a multi-step growth curve. CEF cells were infected with DEV Vaccine Strain, rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, rDEV-DHAV-VP0, and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 at an MOI of 0.01. Cell lysates and supernatants were collected at 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 144 hpi. Viral titers were determined as 50% tissue culture infectious doses (TCID50) using the Reed–Muench method, and growth curves were generated to compare the replication kinetics of the recombinant viruses with those of the parental strain.

Genetic stability analysis

The genetic stability of rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 was assessed by serial passaging in CEF cells. Each recombinant virus was serially passaged 15 times. At every 5th passage (P5, P10, and P15), viral DNA was extracted. The presence and integrity of the inserted foreign genes (VP0, VP1) were verified by PCR using primers TUL27/26-F/R. The expression of the foreign proteins was also confirmed by IFA.

Antibody induction capability assessment

Three-day-old ducklings confirmed negative for both DEV and DHAV viruses and their corresponding antibodies (n = 5 per group) were used for the immunization trial. Each group was intramuscularly inoculated in the leg with 10⁵ TCID50/0.2 mL of either recombinant virus (rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1) or the parental DEV vaccine strain. A control group was inoculated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Blood samples were collected weekly for 4 weeks to monitor serological responses. Serum samples were analyzed for antibody titers against DEV and DHAV using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Indirect ELISA. The indirect ELISA was performed to determine total antibody titers against DEV and DHAV. Purified His-tagged recombinant proteins (gC, VP1, or VP0) were diluted to a final concentration of 1 µg/mL in 50 mM carbonate buffer (pH 9.6). 100 µL per well was used to coat flat-bottom 96-well microplates by incubating overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), each well was filled with 100 µL of PBS containing 5% skim milk (blocking buffer) and blocked at 37°C for 2 h. The plates were washed again with PBST three times, and 100 µL of serum samples diluted in blocking buffer were added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following another wash cycle, 100 µL of HRP-conjugated goat anti-duck IgG antibody (KPL, USA) diluted 1:1000 was added and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Color development was performed by adding 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate for 15 min in the dark. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of 2 M H₂SO₄ stop solution. The absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

Safety assessment

To evaluate the safety profile, three-day-old ducklings verified as negative for both DEV and DHAV viruses and antibodies (n = 8 per group) were intramuscularly inoculated with a 106 TCID50/0.2 mL dose of each recombinant virus. A negative control group received PBS. All ducklings were monitored daily for 14 days for any adverse clinical signs, including depression, reduced feed intake, ruffled feathers, swelling of the head and neck, and diarrhea. Five days post-post-inoculation, three ducks from each group were humanely euthanized and subjected to necropsy. Gross pathological lesions were recorded, and tissue samples (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and intestines) were collected for subsequent histopathological examination. After the observation period, the same treatment was applied to the ducks, and pathological changes in their tissues and organs were observed.

Assessment of virus shedding. Virus shedding was monitored by collecting cloacal swabs from five ducks per group on days 3, 5, and 7 post-inoculation. Each swab was placed in 1 mL of PBS containing penicillin G (10,000 IU/mL) and streptomycin (10,000 µg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were used for DNA extraction with the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, China). Viral shedding was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using primers UL31-F (5′-TGGCTCATGTTTGG CTTCTAC-3′) and UL31-R (5′-ACGCTGTCCACGTCAGTTTG-3′). The qPCR reaction volume was 20 μL, comprising 10 μL 2 × SYBR Green PCR master mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), 0.4 μM forward and reverse primers each, 2 μL DNA template, with the remaining volume adjusted with ddH₂O. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and a final melting curve analysis (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 60 s, and 95°C for 15 s).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

Construction and characterization of the recombinant DEV

A transfection-then-infection strategy was used to generate recombinant DEV strains (Fig. 1). CEF cells were co-transfected with the gRNA–Cas9 plasmids and the corresponding donor plasmids, followed by infection with rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26. After 48 h, the F₀ generation recombinant viruses were harvested. Upon infection of CEF cells with F0 rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1, both non-fluorescent plaques and fluorescent plaques exhibiting a grape-like clustered morphology were observed (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the F₀ generation recombinant viruses consisted of a mixture of rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 and rDEV-DHAV. In contrast, plaques formed by the DEV vaccine strain showed no fluorescence. After six consecutive rounds of plaque purification and PCR screening, two genetically pure recombinant viruses were successfully obtained and designated as rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1.

PCR identification was performed using the primers listed in Table 3 to verify the genomic structures of the DEV vaccine strain, rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, rDEV-DHAV-VP0, and rDEV-DHAV-VP1, and the locations of the primers are illustrated in Fig. 1. Amplification with primers TUL27/26-F/R yielded products of approximately 2400 bp for rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, rDEV-DHAV-VP0, and rDEV-DHAV-VP1, whereas the DEV vaccine strain produced a shorter fragment of approximately 670 bp (Fig. 2E). Using EGFP-F/R primers, the specific EGFP fragment was detected only in rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 (Fig. 2D). Similarly, amplification with VP0-F/R primers produced a specific VP0 band exclusively in rDEV-DHAV-VP0 (Fig. 2B), while amplification with VP1-F/R primers yielded a VP1-specific band only in rDEV-DHAV-VP1 (Fig. 2C).

Identification of exogenous gene expression

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was first performed to assess the cellular expression of the inserted DHAV immunogenic genes. Strong green fluorescence signals were observed in CEF cells infected with rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 when probed with VP0- and VP1-specific antibodies, respectively, whereas no specific fluorescence was detected in cells infected with the DEV vaccine strain (Fig. 3A).

Cells infected with rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1 were stained with VP0- or VP1-specific antibodies and FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies; nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Specific fluorescence signals were observed only in cells infected with the corresponding recombinant viruses. (B) Western blot (WB) analysis was performed to further verify the expression of VP0 and VP1 proteins in recombinant virus-infected CEF cells. Immunoblotting with DHAV VP0-, DHAV VP1-, EGFP-, and DEV gC-specific antibodies demonstrated the selective expression of the corresponding heterologous proteins. β-Actin was used as a loading control.

Western blot analysis further confirmed protein expression (Fig. 3B). When probed with a rabbit polyclonal antibody against DEV gC, a specific band of approximately 48 kDa corresponding to the native gC protein was detected in cells infected with the DEV vaccine strain, rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26, rDEV-DHAV-VP0, and rDEV-DHAV-VP1, while no signal was observed in uninfected CEFs. Using a VP1-specific antibody, a distinct band of approximately 27 kDa was detected exclusively in rDEV-DHAV-VP1-infected cells, consistent with the predicted molecular weight of VP1. Similarly, a VP0-specific band of approximately 28 kDa was detected only in rDEV-DHAV-VP0-infected cells. In addition, an approximately 27 kDa EGFP band was observed only in rDEV-EGFP-infected cells when probed with an anti-EGFP antibody. No VP1, VP0, or EGFP signals were detected in cells infected with the DEV vaccine strain or in uninfected controls. Comparable β-actin levels were observed across all samples, confirming equal protein loading.

Replication kinetics of the recombinant DEV

To determine whether insertion of the DHAV immunogenic genes affected viral replication, the growth kinetics of rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 were compared with those of the DEV vaccine strain and rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 in CEF cells. All viruses exhibited similar multistep growth curves (Fig. 4A). Viral titers increased progressively following infection and reached peak levels at 96–120 h post-infection. Notably, no significant differences in viral titers were observed between the recombinant viruses and the parental DEV strain at any time point (P > 0.05).

Genetic stability of the recombinant DEV

The genetic stability of rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 was evaluated over 15 serial passages in CEF cells. PCR amplification using primers TUL27/26-F/R confirmed the persistent presence of the inserted genes at passages 5, 10, and 15, with no detectable deletions or size variations (Fig. 4B). Consistent with PCR results, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that VP0 and VP1 proteins maintained stable expression throughout serial passage (Fig. 4C).

Antibody responses induced by the recombinant viruses

To further evaluate the humoral immune responses induced by the recombinant viruses, serum samples were collected weekly from immunized ducklings and analyzed by ELISA using DEV gC, DHAV VP0, or DHAV VP1 proteins as coating antigens. When DEV gC protein was used, both rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 induced detectable DEV-specific antibodies as early as 1 week post-immunization, with antibody levels increasing progressively and reaching peak values at 4 weeks post-immunization (OD values up to approximately 0.9). Throughout the observation period, antibody levels in the recombinant virus–immunized groups were comparable to those induced by the DEV vaccine strain, whereas no antibody responses were detected in the PBS control group (Fig. 5B).

ELISA using DHAV VP1 protein as the coating antigen demonstrated that ducklings immunized with rDEV-DHAV-VP1 developed VP1-specific antibody responses. Antibody levels in this group were significantly higher than those in the DEV vaccine strain and PBS control groups at all time points examined (P<0.05), whereas no VP1-specific antibodies were detected in the DEV vaccine strain or PBS control groups (Fig. 5C).

Similarly, using DHAV VP0 protein as the coating antigen, a clear VP0-specific antibody response was detected in the rDEV-DHAV-VP0–immunized group. Antibody levels increased steadily from week 1 to week 4 post-immunization, reaching OD values of approximately 1.0 (Fig. 5D). Throughout the experimental period, VP0-specific antibody levels in the rDEV-DHAV-VP0 group were significantly higher than those in the DEV vaccine strain group (P<0.05), while no VP0-specific antibodies were detected in the DEV vaccine strain or PBS control groups.

Safety evaluation of the recombinant viruses

To evaluate the safety of the recombinant viruses, ducklings were monitored daily for clinical signs following inoculation. During the 14-day observation period, no ducklings in the recombinant virus-immunized groups exhibited depression, reduced feed intake, diarrhea, neurological signs, or mortality. The general activity levels of ducklings inoculated with rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1 were comparable to those observed in the DEV vaccine strain group, indicating that insertion of the VP0 or VP1 gene did not increase viral pathogenicity.

Viral shedding was assessed by quantitative PCR using UL31-specific primers on cloacal swabs collected at 3, 5, and 7 days post-inoculation. No viral DNA was detected in any of the swab samples from recombinant virus–immunized ducklings, with all Cq values exceeding 35 (data not shown), indicating the absence of detectable viral shedding during the monitoring period.

Discussion

Duck enteritis virus (DEV) is widely regarded as an attractive viral vector for waterfowl vaccine development due to its large genomic capacity, high genetic stability, relatively limited host range, and favorable safety profile. However, compared to other more extensively studied herpesviruses, DEV was discovered relatively late, and its characteristics remain incompletely elucidated (Castro, et al., 2021; Deng, et al., 2023). At present, certified cell lines suitable for large-scale production and purification of DEV are still lacking. Nevertheless, DEV-based vectors continue to exhibit substantial developmental value and broad application potential in vaccine research and production.

In this study, a dual single-guide RNA (sgRNA) mediated HDR-CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing strategy was employed, using rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 as an intermediate backbone, to generate two recombinant DEV strains expressing immunogenic genes of DHAV-3. The HDR-based repair strategy ensured precise integration of the foreign genes into the DEV genome, which is a critical requirement for the construction of reliable vaccine candidates (Jayavaradhan, et al., 2019). The inclusion of the EGFP reporter gene as a selection marker allowed rapid and convenient discrimination between recombinant rDEV-DHAV and the parental rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 virus. However, it should be noted that Cas9-induced double-strand breaks can also be repaired through non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which may generate non-fluorescent rDEV-EGFPUL27/UL26 mutants lacking foreign gene insertion. To exclude this possibility and ensure accurate selection of authentic recombinant viruses, each non-fluorescent plaque was subjected to a dual-track screening strategy: one portion was used for further plaque purification, while the other was used for total DNA extraction followed by PCR verification. Only plaques that were confirmed to be PCR-positive were advanced to subsequent purification rounds. Using this strategy, two recombinant viruses, rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1, were successfully obtained within approximately one month. These results demonstrate the high efficiency and precision of the HDR-CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing platform for the rapid construction of recombinant DEV-based vaccine candidates.

A critical consideration for viral vector–based vaccines is whether insertion of foreign genes allows stable antigen expression without compromising viral replication or genetic stability (Romanutti, et al., 2020). These properties are influenced by multiple factors, including the intrinsic characteristics of the inserted gene, the genomic insertion site, and the promoter driving transgene expression (Wang, et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that various regions of the DEV genome can accommodate foreign gene insertion, including US10, US2, UL41, UL44, and intergenic regions such as UL55/LORF11, US7/US8, UL26/UL27, SORF3/US2, and UL44/44.5 (Jia, et al., 2025). Among these, the US7/US8 and UL26/UL27 loci have been most frequently utilized. Notably, one study reported that insertion of duck hepatitis A virus serotype 3 (DHAV-3) P1 and 3C genes into the UL26/UL27 site (rDEV-UL26/27-P13C) conferred more effective protection against both DEV and DHAV-3 infection compared to insertion at the US7/US8 site (rDEV-US7/8-P13C) (Yang, et al., 2021). In this study, two recombinant DEV strains were constructed by inserting foreign genes into the UL27/UL26 intergenic region. Both recombinant viruses exhibited high genetic stability during serial passaging and retained in vitro replication kinetics comparable to the DEV vaccine strain. Furthermore, heterologous DHAV-3 VP0 and VP1 genes were successfully expressed in infected cells, corroborating that the UL27/UL26 site supports stable foreign gene insertion and expression with minimal impact on viral replication in vitro.

Notably, the expression levels of VP0 and VP1 were relatively modest, particularly when compared with the major DEV structural protein gC. This observation suggests that foreign expression efficiency may be limited by promoter strength and/or intrinsic sequence features of the inserted genes. In this study, the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter was employed to drive DHAV-3 VP0 and VP1 expression. Although previous studies have demonstrated that the CMV promoter retains functional activity in avian cell line, including chicken embryo fibroblast (CEF) and duck embryo fibroblast (DEF), its transcriptional efficiency may be suboptimal compared to native avian promoters or optimized synthetic promoters, such as the elongation factor-1 alpha (EF1α) promoter or the Pec promoter (a hybrid of the CMV enhancer and chicken β-actin promoter) (Li, et al., 2016; Sun, et al., 2017). Additionally, intrinsic sequence characteristics of the inserted genes can also affect expression efficiency. Although full-length immunogenic genes are commonly used in recombinant vaccine development, previous studies have demonstrated that strategies such as codon optimization, rational truncation of non-essential regions, or incorporation of regulatory elements that enhance transcription and translation can substantially improve antigen expression and immunogenicity (Chen, et al., 2019; Zai, et al., 2022). Therefore, optimization of promoters, insertion sites, and foreign gene sequences represents a promising avenue for enhancing the efficacy of DEV-based multivalent vaccine candidates.

Safety and efficacy are the primary criteria for evaluating vaccine candidates. In the present study, ducklings inoculated with rDEV-DHAV-VP0 or rDEV-DHAV-VP1 remained clinically normal throughout the observation period, with no evidence of abnormal virus shedding or apparent gross or histopathological lesions. Viral DNA was undetectable in cloacal swabs, and the histological architecture of major organs remained intact, further indicating that insertion of the VP0 or VP1 gene did not enhance DEV virulence. These findings demonstrate that the recombinant viruses maintained an in vivo safety profile comparable to that of the parental DEV vaccine strain.

Previous studies have shown that DEV-based vectors expressing DHAV immunogenic genes can confer effective immune protection (Niu, et al., 2020; Zou, et al., 2016). Consistent with these reports, both recombinant viruses in the present study preserved the intrinsic immunogenicity of the DEV vector and elicited DEV-specific antibody responses comparable to those induced by the parental vaccine strain, indicating that foreign gene insertion did not compromise vector-mediated immunity. Importantly, ELISA results further demonstrated that rDEV-DHAV-VP0 and rDEV-DHAV-VP1 were capable of inducing DHAV-3–specific humoral immune responses. Nevertheless, the protective efficacy of these vaccine candidates should be more definitively validated through challenge experiments, which will constitute a major focus of our future investigations.

Conclusion

In this study, two CRISPR/Cas9-engineered recombinant duck enteritis virus strains expressing the DHAV-3 immunogenic proteins VP0 or VP1 were successfully generated and characterized. Both recombinant viruses exhibited replication kinetics comparable to the parental DEV vaccine strain, maintained genetic stability during serial passage, induced detectable humoral immune responses against both DEV and DHAV-3, and demonstrated excellent safety profiles in ducklings. These findings demonstrate the successful construction of genetically stable, safe, and immunogenic recombinant DEV vectors, highlighting the practical value of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated DEV genome engineering as an effective platform for developing multivalent vaccines for waterfowl.
