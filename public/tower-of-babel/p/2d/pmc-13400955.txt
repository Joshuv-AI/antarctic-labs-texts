Resident based disparities in HIV testing among the general population in Ethiopia: Evidence from the Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey
Authors: Samrawit Birhanu Alemu, Mekdes Tamiru Yizengaw, Aynalem Belay, Henok Tsegaw Bahiru, Melaku Birhanu Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDespite substantial progress in the fight against HIV/AIDS, the epidemic continues to pose a major global public health challenge, with persistent residential disparities. However, the contributing factor for urban-rural disparities in Ethiopia is limited. Therefore, this study aims to identify inequalities in HIV testing across residences and identify contributing factors in Ethiopia.Study designA cross-sectional study design was used to analyse secondary data.MethodsWe used Individual Recode (IR) dataset, which includes women of reproductive age, and the Male Recode (MR) dataset, which includes men aged 15–59 years, from the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS). Descriptive statistics summarised participants’ socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. A multivariate decomposition analysis was employed to assess residential disparities in HIV testing.ResultOverall, 44.9% of participants reported having been tested for HIV. Uptake was substantially higher among urban residents, with 69.3% reporting HIV testing compared with 38.3% of rural residents. The urban-rural gap was explained by both differences in participant characteristics and differences in the effects of those characteristics. Overall, 23.5% of the disparity was attributable to compositional factors, while 76.5% was attributable to coefficient effects. Being married (−12.7%), secondary (12.2%), and higher education (17.3%), and metropolises (3.7%) were the major compositional factors.ConclusionThere was a substantial disparity in HIV testing between urban and rural residents in Ethiopia. The residential difference in HIV testing was largely driven by differences in characteristics such as marital status, education level, and metropolises, which influenced HIV testing. Policy makers should strengthen interventions aiming to increase HIV testing, particularly in rural areas, with a focus on enhancing education and engagement with health services.Keywords: HIV testing, Inequality, Decomposition analysis, Ethiopia

Introduction

Despite the significant progress that has been achieved in these efforts to fight against HIV/AIDS. The HIV/AIDS epidemic remains a major global public health problem [1]. Globally, in 2024, around 40 million people were estimated to be living with HIV, with 1.3 million new infections [2]. From this, 25 million were from Africa, and approximately 20 million of these are from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [2].

HIV testing uptake serves as the foundation for the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) strategic framework in the fight against HIV [3,4]. In SSA, where the prevalence is disproportionately high, HIV testing is crucial to fight against AIDS, as it is the portal to HIV prevention and care [5].

In Ethiopia, in 2024, an estimated 601,039 people were living with HIV, of which 64% are females and 11% are children and adolescents below the age of 20 years [6]. Despite the burden of HIV/AIDS in Ethiopia, the coverage of HIV testing within 12 months was low at 20% for women, ranging from 36% in urban areas and 15% rural. Similarly, HIV testing was 19% for men, ranging from 33% to 15% in urban and rural areas, respectively [7].

Examining healthcare coverage and gaining a better understanding of socioeconomic inequality in HIV testing are important [8]. Navigating healthcare coverage progress over time allows us to understand the added value or the persistent gaps due to the absence or presence of interventions [9,10]. Socio-demographic characteristics as age, education status, marital status, and wealth, influence the provision of HIV testing and the differences observed in urban and rural [[11], [12], [13]].

The recent strategic plan includes a goal to achieve at least 95% people knowing their HIV status by 2025 through population campaigns, such as school-based campaigns, peer service providers, community outreach, and health facility HIV testing [11]. In the past years, HIV testing has shown increments in response to the UNAIDS 90-90-90 and 95-95-95 targets by 2020 and 2030, respectively [3,4].

Understanding the inequality in HIV testing will, therefore, facilitate the implementation of the strategic plan [11]. However, the contributing factor for urban-rural disparities in Ethiopia is unknown. Therefore, this study aims to identify inequalities in HIV testing across residences and identify contributing factors in Ethiopia. This study will provide evidence for policymakers to apply targeted interventions and ensure equitable access to HIV testing in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional design based on nationally representative secondary data.

Study setting and data source

This study was conducted in Ethiopia, a low-income country located in the Horn of Africa. In this study, a secondary data analysis was conducted using EDHS 2016 data, which was designed to provide estimates of indicators for the country, for urban and rural residents separately, and for the nine regions and the two-city administrative. Although the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) is more recent, it does not contain the HIV testing information necessary to assess HIV testing. Therefore, the 2016 EDHS was selected as the most appropriate data source for this study. The EDHS dataset is publicly available at https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm.

Sampling procedure

The EDHS employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design. In the first stage, 645 enumeration areas (202 urban and 443 rural) were selected from the 2007 Population and Housing Census sampling frame. In the second stage, 28 households were systematically selected from each enumeration area.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using standardised and pretested DHS questionnaires administered by trained interviewers. Information on HIV testing and other study variables was obtained from eligible respondents during the survey period.

Study population and sample size

This study used Individual Recode (IR) data from the EDHS, comprising 15,683 women of reproductive age, and Male Recode (MR) data, comprising 12,688 men aged 15-59 years. Overall, a total sample of 28,371 individuals was included in the analysis. The weighted and unweighted sample sizes were both 28,371 (Fig. 1).

Sample selection flowchart.

Measurements of variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was HIV testing. It was coded as “yes” for those who had ever tested for HIV before the survey and “no” for those who had not [14].

Independent variables

Residence was categorised as urban or rural. Regions were recoded into three groups: “central” (Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, SNNP), “peripheral” (Afar, Gambela, Harari, Somalia), and “metropolises” (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa). Occupation was coded as “yes” for working participants and “no” for non-working participants. Education was classified as no education, primary, secondary, and higher. Wealth status was categorised into five groups: poorest, poor, middle, rich, and richest. Marital status was recoded as never married, currently married, or formerly married (divorced, separated, or widowed). Media exposure included access to TV, radio, or newspapers. Internet use was coded as yes for users and no for non-users.

Data analysis

The data was analysed using Stata 17. To restore the representativeness of the survey, the data were stratified and weighted using sampling weights and the primary sampling unit. Descriptive statistics were done to describe the socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of participants. The outcome variable and independent variables had no missing values. To assess residential disparities in HIV testing uptake, a logistic regression-based multivariate decomposition analysis for a binary outcome was conducted using the mvdcmp command in Stata [15]. This approach decomposed the observed disparity into differences attributable to population characteristics (endowments) and differences in the effects of these characteristics (coefficients) between urban and rural participants. The endowment effect represents the portion of the observed disparity attributable to differences in the distribution of characteristics between rural and urban, whereas the coefficient component reflects differences in the influence of these characteristics on the HIV testing [15,16]. Finally, the results were presented using tables and graphs.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 28,371 weighted respondents were included, of whom 78.9% were from rural areas. Females accounted for 55.3% of participants. Nearly 40.0% had no education, while 7% attained higher education. Approximately 22.4% of respondents belonged to the richest wealth group, and 17.0% to the poorest. The majority were married (62.4%) and Orthodox Christians (44.0%). Most respondents (91.7%) had no internet access, and 46.8% had no media exposure (Table 1).

HIV testing

The overall uptake of HIV testing was 44.9%. Among urban residents, 69.3% reported having been tested for HIV. Conversely, the proportion who had never been tested was higher in rural areas (61.7%) than in urban areas (30.7%) (see Fig. 2).

HIV testing uptake by place of residence in Ethiopia.

Among the total respondents, Females had a higher HIV testing uptake (43.3%) than males (45.3%). Uptake increased with education, ranging from 36.2% among those with no education to 80.4% among those with higher education. Wealthier groups also showed greater uptake, with 56.4% in the richest quintile compared to 31.1% in the poorest. Married individuals (49.9%) tested more than unmarried participants (36.4%). Similarly, participants with media exposure (55.2%) and internet access (70.3%) had higher uptake than their counterparts (Table 2).

For the endowments (E) effect, Education was the largest contributor to the explained component, with higher education accounting for 17.3% and secondary education for 12.2% of the observed disparity. Marital status also contributed substantially, with married individuals accounting for −12.7% of the explained difference. Other notable contributors included age 25-35 years (2.2%), internet use (2.2%), religion, and residence in metropolitan regions (3.7%). Conversely, primary education (−3.5%) and media exposure (−2.5%) reduced the observed gap.

Regarding the unexplained component, differences in the effects of occupation (15.4%), age 25-35 years (12.4%), Muslim religion (11.9%), and marital status (−10.1%) were major contributors to the rural-urban disparity in HIV testing. The coefficient effect of being in the richest wealth quintile (−15.4%) and media exposure (−14.4%) also significantly contributed to the disparity (Table 3).

Discussion

This study identified differences in HIV testing between urban and rural residents in Ethiopia and identified factors contributed for the observed differences. Only 38.3% of rural residents had been tested for HIV, compared with 69.3% of urban residents. This difference may indicate unequal access to HIV testing services, despite Ethiopia's national efforts to expand HIV testing coverage and improve case finding [17,18]. Evidence from Africa indicates that the place of residence accounted for substantial variation in HIV testing utilisation among both men and women [19]. Multiple sub-Saharan African nations show similar patterns of HIV testing differences between urban and rural areas. These disparities may reflect the structural, socioeconomic, and cultural barriers that rural communities face in accessing HIV testing services [20,21].

The Multivariate decomposition result showed that 23.5% of the difference was attributable to compositional elements, while 76.5% was attributable to how these characteristics affected the groups. This finding suggests that the observed disparity is driven not only by differences in socioeconomic and demographic composition but also by how these characteristics translate into utilisation of HIV testing services. Therefore, reducing rural-urban inequalities may require interventions that go beyond demographic and socioeconomic disadvantage by addressing potential behavioural barriers among rural populations, including low perceived risk, stigma, fear of disclosure, limited trust in services, and social norms surrounding HIV testing [22,23]. Furthermore, HIV testing service preference variation among rural and urban residents could contribute to the low uptake rate in rural areas [24].

Educational attainment emerged as the most important compositional factor, with higher education contributing 17.3% and secondary education contributing 12.2% to the observed urban-rural gap. This finding suggests that the higher concentration of educated individuals in urban areas contributes substantially to the greater uptake of HIV testing services. A possible explanation is that individuals with higher levels of education may have better HIV-related knowledge, greater awareness of the benefits of testing, and increased exposure to health information, which could contribute to higher HIV testing uptake [[25], [26], [27]].

Marital status made a negative contribution to the explained component of the rural-urban disparity in HIV testing uptake. Specifically, differences in the distribution of married individuals reduced the observed disparity by 12.7%. This suggests that if urban and rural populations had a similar marital status distribution, the rural-urban gap in HIV testing would have been larger. This might be because married individuals have more frequent contact with healthcare services through antenatal care, PMTCT, and family planning programs, which may increase opportunities for HIV testing [28,29].

The analysis identified residence in metropolitan areas as an important contributor to 3.7% of the observed inequality in HIV testing across residence. A possible reason is that residents of metropolitan areas may have better healthcare infrastructure, greater availability of HIV testing services, and increased exposure to health information and awareness campaigns [30]. Furthermore, the coverage and quality of HIV testing and counselling service was found to be lower in rural and remote regions [31].

Strength and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of large, nationally representative data, which enhances the generalisability of the findings. In addition, decomposition analysis provided insights into the factors contributing to socioeconomic inequalities in HIV testing uptake. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. HIV testing uptake was self-reported and may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Furthermore, the analysis relied on 2016 DHS data, which may not fully reflect current patterns of HIV testing uptake. Due to the availability of data, we have only included reproductive aged women.

Conclusion

There was a substantial disparity in HIV testing between urban and rural residents. The residential difference in HIV testing was largely driven by differences in characteristics such as marital status, education level, and region, which influenced testing. Policy makers should strengthen interventions aiming to increase HIV testing, particularly in rural areas, with a focus on enhancing education, access to information, and engagement with health services.

Ethics statement

The Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data are publicly available upon request from the DHS Program website. Access to the maternal record dataset was granted after submitting a request outlining the study objectives. As this study is based on secondary data analysis, patients and the public were not directly involved in the study design, conduct, or reporting; however, the findings are expected to benefit the public.
