Prevalence of eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments in the Debre Markos referral hospital
Authors: Daniel Wudie, Helen Nigussie, Tegenu Gelana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Eye diseases are conditions that affect any part of the eye and the structures immediately around the eyes. The overall objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments among patients who were admitted to Debre Markos Referral Hospital. Retrospective data were collected from 2019 to 2023. A total of 61,946 patients were examined at the referral hospital for Eye diseases and Eye conditions. Age, sex and visual acuity were considered from the case record files. Data analysis was performed via SPSS version 20 software. Cataract was the most common eye disease observed followed by presbyopia and glaucoma in the study area. Moreover, monocular blindness, visual impairment and eye binocular blindness were observed within three years in the study. Compared with female patients, more than half of the patients were male. In general, eye disease records were highly prevalent at the productive age level, which is between 15 and 64 years in both sexes. This may affect the national economy by minimizing productivity and increasing medical costs. Therefore, sustainable solutions, such as the creation of awareness in prevention mechanisms, should be developed.

Introduction

Eye diseases are conditions that affect any part of the eye and include conditions that affect the structures immediately around the eyes. This condition could develop quickly, develop more slowly, or last a long time. The eyeball itself is where most eye diseases occur, but it is not the only place where the disease occurs; it also affects the eye muscles, eye sockets, eyelids, or the skin and muscles immediately around the eyes1. Eye problems are known to have a negative impact on quality of life. The occurrence of eye diseases is influenced by an individual’s socioeconomic status, geographic location, and accessibility to facilities2.

According to a report from WHO3 globally, at least 2.2 billion people experience near or distance vision impairment3. Among these 1 billion people, the main conditions causing distance vision impairment or blindness are cataracts (94 million), refractive error (88.4 million), age-related macular degeneration (8 million), glaucoma (7.7 million), and diabetic retinopathy (3.9 million)4. The main condition causing near vision impairment is presbyopia (826 million)5.

In terms of regional differences, the prevalence of distance vision impairment in low- and middle-income regions is estimated to be 4 times greater than that in high-income regions4. With respect to near vision, rates of unaddressed near vision impairment are estimated to be greater than 80% in western, eastern and central sub-Saharan Africa, whereas comparative rates in high-income regions of North America, Australasia, western Europe, and the Asia–Pacific region are reported to be lower than 10%5. Population growth and aging are expected to increase the risk that more people acquire vision impairment.

The leading causes of vision impairment and blindness worldwide are refractive errors, cataracts, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration3.There is substantial variation in the causes of vision impairment between and within countries according to the availability of eye care services, their affordability, and the education of the population. For example, the proportion of vision impairment attributable to un-operated cataracts is greater in low- and middle-income countries. In high-income countries, diseases such as glaucoma and age-related macular degeneration are more common. Among children, congenital cataracts are a leading cause of vision impairment in low-income countries, whereas in middle-income countries, they are more likely to cause retinopathy of prematurity. Uncorrected refractive error remains a leading cause of vision impairment in all countries among children and adults3.

Refractive error is the primary cause of subnormal and poor vision in children between the ages of 6 and 15. Therefore, the prevalence of blindness and visual impairment is strongly related to population age. Over 15 million people living in Ethiopia struggle with eye health issues, including blindness and visual impairment, which are serious public health issues6.

According to the review of Sume and Seyoum6 the overall prevalence of refractive errors among school students was 7.36%. The prevalence rates of myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism were 5.10%, 0.95%, and 0.01%, respectively6. As stated in their report, the highest prevalence of refractive errors was reported in the Amhara Region (9.18%), followed by the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples regions (6.78%), whereas the lowest prevalence of refractive errors was reported in Addis Ababa (3.93%)6.

The prevalence of visual impairment was reported in studies conducted in Saudi Arabia 13.9%7 South Korea 4.1%8 India 8.4%9 South Sudan 11.8%10 South Africa 4.9%11, Debre Berhan 16.8%12 and Gondar, Ethiopia 15.3%13.

In Ethiopia, many studies were conducted previously on visual impairment, however, most of them focused on childhood visual impairment14,15.The studies also included participants aged a cut of point at 18 years and above. In a study conducted in Debre Markos, Ethiopia, the magnitude of VI was 36.52%16 at St Paul’s Millennium Hospital Medical College, Ethiopia, low Vision and Blindness were found to be 10.3% and 7.3%, respectively17 and a community-based study using worst eye seeing visual acuity in Debre Berhan town, Ethiopia among aged 18 and above the prevalence of visual impairment was found to be 16.8%12.

To date, no studies have been conducted on the prevalence of eye diseases and visual impairments at the community level in the study area of Debre Markos referral hospital in the east Gojjam Zone of the Amhara region, Ethiopia. Thus, the aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments in Debre Markos referral hospital, East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Region, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

This research was conducted at Debre Markos referral hospital, which is located in Debre Markos Town. Debre Markos Urban Administration Woreda is one of the 21 Woredas in East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Region of Ethiopia located in East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Region. The relative location of the woreda is 290 km southwest of Addis Ababa and 256 km northeast of Bahir Dar, which is the capital city of the Amhara Regional State.

The boundary demarcation of the study area is East Gozamin Woreda, North Snan Woreda, Southeast Gozamin Woreda and West Gozamin Woreda to Northwest Gozamin (Fig. 1). The administrative center of this woreda is Debre Markos, which is located at 10° 17′ 55" N latitude to 10° 22′ 15" N latitude and 37° 41′ 15" longitude to 37° 46′ 15" E longitude.Fig. 1Map of Ethiopia showing Amhara region Debre Markos districts (adopted from GIS. Research design

An institution-based retrospective study was conducted on eye patients visiting the ophthalmology ward of Debre Markos referral hospital from 2019–2023.

Study population and sampling procedure

The total population of the study included all patients registered at the ophthalmology ward at Debre Markos referral hospital during the last five consecutive years. For the study of eye diseases and refractive errors secondary data of five years (2019–2023) were used. However, for the study of visual impairment and blindness the three years (2021–2023) data were used.

Inclusion criteria

The study included all those who went to Debre Markos Referral Hospital, issued a card and underwent eye treatment, and the full data were recorded.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded all those who went to Debre Markos Referral Hospital, issued a card and underwent eye treatment, and the full data were not recorded.

Study variables

The dependent variables were eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairment, and the independent variables are age and sex.

Ethical consideration

To carry out this study, a supported letter from the Department of Zoological Science, Addis Ababa University was submitted to the Debre Markos Referral Hospital Administrative Body, and written ethical approval letter was obtained from the Debre Markos Referral Hospital Administrative Office (Ref number 609/22.217/16). In addition, the local administrative bodies were informed regarding the objective and purpose of the study to encourage them to provide relevant data for this study. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of the country. Since there is no experimental test and involvement of vulnerable group for this study (retrospective data), there is no need of experimental protocols approval by institutional review board. However, written informed consent with the reference number 609/22.217/16 was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) to access their data from Debre Markos Referral Hospital.

Data collection techniques

A format was prepared on a computer spreadsheet (Excel) to collect the secondary data from logbooks of Debre Markos Referral Hospital with the support of health professionals of the study area. Individual level data on Eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments and available demographic data (age, sex) was registered on the computer spreadsheet. All records of Eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments patients who visited the Debre Markos Referral Hospital during the time frame considered and treated as Eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments patients were including in the study.

Data quality assurance and data analysis

Each data point was collected and confirmed with the consent and verification of the reality of the data by the Debre Markose referral hospital ophthalmology ward. Moreover, the collected data were compared and confirmed with the annual reports of the ophthalmology ward. Descriptive data analysis was performed via SPSS version 20.

Presbyopia

A total of 12,747 presbyopia patients were recorded during the period of 2019–2023. The majorities (58.98%) were females, and the rest (41.02%) were males. The prevalence of presbyopia disease was high (58.04%) in the 36–64 years age group, followed by > 65 age group (41.96%), compared with the other age groups. However, no cases of presbyopia disease were reported in those under the age of 35 year (Table 7).

Myopia

A total of 2359 myopia patients were recorded from 2019 to 2023. The majority of the patients (59.2%) were male, and the remaining 40.8% were female. A high prevalence of myopia (43.9% and 37.7%) occurs within the age range of 15–64 years. However, no myopia patients were reported within the age range of < 4 years (Table 7).

Astigmatism

A total of 1392 astigmatism patients were registered during the period of 2019–2023. The majority of the patients (61%) were male, and the remaining 38.5% were female. A high prevalence of (44%) astigmatism was observed in the 30–64 years age group, followed by the 15–29 years age group (34.9%), compared with the other age groups. However, no astigmatism patients were reported within the age range of < 4 years in either sex (Table 7).

Discussion

A total of 61,946 eye records were reviewed. Among these, more than half of the eye patients (57.8%) were males, and 42.2% were females. Eye disease is more prevalent in the active age groups of 15–29 years and 30–64 years and older than in the other age groups. Cataracts (26.4%), followed by presbyopia (20.63%), glaucoma (13.9%) and conjunctivitis (13.6%), were the leading causes of eye disease and refractive error in the study area. Globally, the prevalence of refractive error was 12%, and visual impairment due to refractive error was 2.1%. In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of refractive error and visual impairment due to refractive error was 12.6% and 3.4%, respectively18.

A cataract is a cloudy area in the lens of your eye (the clear part of the eye that helps to focus light). Cataracts are very common as individuals’ age increased, which is also confirmed by the current study, where cataracts are more prevalent in the age group > 65 years and between 30 and 64 years . The prevalence of cataracts in the study area was within the range reported in other parts of the country, ranging from 20.1% to 29% in the central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia19; in the Arba Minch district, southern Ethiopia20; and in the Waghimra Zone, Amhara, Northeast Ethiopia. However, it is higher than that reported by Tefera and Adamu21, who reported a prevalence of cataracts of 2.4% in the Abeshge and Kebena districts, South of Addis Ababa; 11.4% in Merhabete22 and 7.8% in South Ari District, southern Ethiopia23, but lower than the report of Wale et al.24 who reported 57% of cataracts in northwest Ethiopia. Moreover, Abebe et al.16, reported that among the study participants, the leading clinical diagnosis was glaucoma 72 (23.1%) followed by cataract 65 (20.8%) and allergic conjunctivitis 60 (19.2%).

Presbyopia is the gradual loss of one’s ability to focus on nearby objects. Presbyopia usually becomes noticeable in the early to middle 40s and continues to worsen until approximately age 6525. This is also confirmed by the current results, where presbyopia was more prevalent in the age group between 36 and 64 years (58.04%), followed by the age group > 65 (41.96%). The prevalence rate reported in the present study was lower than that reported by Yalew et al.26, who reported a 63.62% prevalence of presbyopia in Debre Tabor Town, and27, who reported 68.7% prevalence in Gonder City, Northwest Ethiopia. However, this percentage was higher than that reported by Munaw et al.28, who reported 51% presbyopia in Hawassa city in southern Ethiopia.

Glaucomas are a group of eye conditions that damage the optic nerve. The optic nerve sends visual information from your eye to your brain and is vital for good vision. Damage to the optic nerve is often related to high pressure in the eye. However, glaucoma can occur even with normal eye pressure. Glaucoma can occur at any age but is more common in older adults. It is one of the leading causes of blindness for people over the age of 6029. Glaucoma is recognized as the third leading cause of eye disease in Debre Markos referral hospitals and is the second leading cause of blindness worldwide30, and some developed countries, such as Japan and Germany, have reported glaucoma as the second leading cause of blindness31. However, it is the fifth leading cause of blindness in Ethiopia32. The prevalence of glaucoma reported in the present study (13.87%) was lower than that reported by Wubet and Assefa33, who reported a 26% glaucoma incidence in Northwest Ethiopia, but higher than the prevalence rates reported at the global level (2.4%) and 4% at the continental level34.

Conjunctivitis, also known as pink eye, is inflammation of the outermost layer of the white part of the eye and the inner surface of the eyelid. This makes the eye appear pink or reddish. Pain, burning, scratching, or itchiness may occur. The affected eye may have increased tears or be “stuck shut” in the morning. Swelling of the white part of the eye may also occur. Itching is more common in cases due to allergies. Conjunctivitis can affect one or both eyes35. The prevalence of conjunctivitis reported in the present study (13.87%) was lower than that reported by Mokbul et al.36, who reported a 33% Conjunctivitis prevalence in Bangladesh.

However, the very low prevalence rates of keratoconjunctivitis sicca (0.7%), infectious keratitis (0.8%) and porcine ulcers (1.68%) reported in the study area are in line with the reports of Cao et al.37 and38 who reported a low prevalence of corneal diseases and infectious keratitis eye disease in China. However, the abovementioned disease is more common in young patients, i.e., those between 5 and 14 years of age.

The prevalence rates of blindness and visual impairment among eye patients who attended Debre Markos referral hospitals were 86% and 14%, respectively. A high prevalence of monocular blindness (75%) was reported among those aged 30–64 years, followed by those aged 15–29 years (21%) and those aged > 65 years (11%). However, no visual impairment or blindness was reported in individuals under the age of one in either sex. This result is higher than those of institutional-based studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (17.6%) (Cherinet, 2018), Ghana (31.9%)39, South Africa (28.0%)40, Debre Berhan town, Ethiopia (16.8%)12, the Gurage Zone, Ethiopia (20%)41, Ghana (22.7%)42, and Yugan County, China (19.2%)43, Arba Minch Zuria District in Southern Ethiopia (36.95%)44. According to the meta-analysis report of Atlaw et al.18 the first pooled prevalence of visual impairment due to refractive error in Ethiopia. The pooled prevalence of visual impairment due to refractive error was found to be 6% in Ethiopia, and myopia was identified as the commonest cause of visual impairment due to refractive error.

As reported by Getachew et al.44, the prevalence of visual impairment among adults aged 40 and above was found to be 36.95%. Among the overall prevalence of visually impaired participants, 62.40% and 37.60% had monocular and bilateral visual impairment, respectively. Besides Abebe et al.16, reported that the most prevalent eye diseases in people with visual impairment were cataract 48 (42.1%) and glaucoma 42 (36.8%). Myopia was the most common refractive error in southernmost province in China45. The prevalence of myopia, high myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism in Singapore adults aged over 40 years was 38.9%; 8.4%; 31.5% and 58.8% respectively46.

This difference might be due to variation in the study setting, sampling size, and eye care service-seeking behaviors of the participants. In addition, the availability and accessibility of eye care services could also contribute to these differences. The prevalence estimates of eye disease and visual impairment are affected by race and ethnicity, age, income, and education level47. The same authors indicated that environmental and nutritional factors may have an impact on group differences in prevalence. Variations in case definitions may be the cause of the observed variations. In regard to eye disorders, this could be based on the patient’s self-reported history, the results of a clinical examination, or imaging (such as optical coherence tomography, film fundus photography, or digital fundus photography). The capacity to estimate prevalence, detect temporal trends, and identify cohort effects may be further complicated by variations in the diagnostic techniques used to classify conditions (phenotypes)48.

Generally, the high prevalence of eye disease, cataracts, presbyopia, glaucoma and conjunctivitis in the study area was related to the age and sex of the patients. Presbyopia is more prevalent in females aged 30–64 years. However, the other three diseases, cataract, glaucoma and conjunctivitis, are more prevalent in males than in females at an advanced age. On the other hand, for Keratoconjunctivitis sicca, eye disease was more prevalent in young and active working-age individuals, which might have affected the productivity of the population in the study area. Hence, immediate intervention in awareness creation and early treatment are needed to avoid preventable eye disease and blindness.

Conclusion

The current findings showed that cataract was the most common eye disease followed by presbyopia and glaucoma respectively. Furthermore, 75% monocular blindness, 14% visual impairment and 11% eye binocular blindness were observed within the three years. In the case of astigmatism, high prevalence was observed in the productive age group, males were more strongly affected than females. On the other hand, in the cases of corneal scars/opacities and corneal ulcers, the number of patients increased with increasing age. The current findings indicated that high prevalence of eye disease in children was due to conjunctivitis, but in adults and elders, it was due to cataracts, presbyopia, and glaucoma. The prevalence of visual impairment could be greatly reduced by providing health education and awareness of eye care in primary health care centers and through regular visits to ophthalmologists and optometrists.

Strengths and limitations

Showing the prevalence of eye diseases, refractive errors and visual impairments at Debre Markos Referral Hospital East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia is the strength of the current study whereas, focusing only on the secondary data is the limitations of this. However, the current findings will help as base line information for further investigation in the study areas or beyond.
