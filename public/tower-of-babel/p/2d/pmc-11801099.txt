Impact of Number and Placement of High-dose Vertices on Equivalent Uniform Dose and Peak-to-valley Ratio for Lattice Radiotherapy
Authors: A. T. Bhagyalakshmi, Velayudham Ramasubramanian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aims:This study evaluated the influence of high dose (HD) vertex numbers and its placement on equivalent uniform dose (EUD) and peak-to-valley dose ratio (PVDR) in lattice radiotherapy (LRT).Settings and Design:One hundred and eighty-eight RapidArc (RA) plans were created for a cohort of 15 patients.Materials and Methods:RA plans were created with zero to eight HD vertices to analyze their relationship with EUD. Eight lattices were systematically and optimally placed (by avoiding proximity to organs at risks [OARs]) to study the impact of vertex placement. Variations in PVDR were assessed using PVDR1 (mean dose to HD vertices by the difference of mean doses to planning target volume [PTV] and HD vertices) and PVDR2 (D10/D90 of PTV in composite plans) across 38 RA plans with HD vertex doses of 9 Gy, 12 Gy, 15 Gy, and 18 Gy. PVDR3 (product of PVDR1 and PVDR2) was evaluated for its variation with peak dose.Statistical Analysis Used:Hypothesis testing between vertex placements was performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test.Results:EUD values ranged from 32.88 Gy to 40.63 Gy. In addition, statistical analysis revealed significant associations (P = 0.0074) between the placement patterns of HD vertices, both in systematic and optimized arrangements. The PVDR and D10/D90 product values were 1.6, 1.8, 2.1, and 2.3 for peak doses of 9 Gy, 12 Gy, 15 Gy, and 18 Gy, respectively.Conclusions:The addition of one HD vertex increased EUD, emphasizing the impact of individual vertex increments on outcomes. Systematic and optimized vertex placements enhance EUD, with optimized placement yielding better doses to PTV and OARs. PVDR3 offers superior dose reporting for LRT compared to PVDR1 and PVDR2.

INTRODUCTION

Tolerance doses in normal tissues limit radiotherapy doses, hindering effective tumor control, especially in inoperable bulky tumors near critical organs.[1] Such tumors often contain radioresistant hypoxic cells, necessitating advanced techniques such as grid or lattice radiotherapy (LRT).[23] LRT uses volumetric modulated arc therapy to create high-dose (HD) vertices within a bulk tumor, forming a lattice structure.[45] HD vertices of 1–2 cm diameter deliver a HD, whereas surrounding tissues receive conventional doses, minimizing toxicity.[67891011] Preliminary results suggest that this spatially fractionated approach may enhance immune response and tumor cell death.[12131415161718] To assess plans with such heterogeneity, it is necessary to evaluate them by determining the radiobiological measure of the equivalent uniform dose (EUD).[19202122] Our study examines how EUD and peak-to-valley dose ratio (PVDR) vary with vertex number in LRT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study included 188 plans for a cohort of 15 patients (5 bladder cases, 5 rectum cases, and 5 cervix cases) who underwent radiotherapy using a 6MV beam in a Varian Clinac iX linear accelerator. To assess the dosimetric considerations in the context of lattice therapy for cancer patients with extensive disease, computed tomography (CT) slices of 15 patients with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm were acquired using a 16-slice Discovery IQ3 ring positron emission tomography CT machine manufactured by Wipro GE was utilized. Four patients, including two with bladder cancer and two with rectal cancer, were treated using LRT-three with radical intent and one rectal cancer case with palliative intent. Due to variations in the number, placement pattern, and volume of vertices among these cases, the resulting treatment plans were not suitable for inclusion in the study. However, since the maximum number of vertices used was eight, this was set as the upper limit for the study. An additional 11 patients with extensive disease were selected for the purpose of study, with contours delineated and treatment plans generated accordingly. In this dosimetric study, the primary aim was to analyze the impact of the number and placement pattern of HD vertices within lattice on EUD and a preliminary investigation on the behavior of PVDR with change in the number of vertices in LRT for bladder cancers with extensive disease. The planning target volume (PTV) in this study varied from 1450.83 cubic centimeters (cc) to 1649.9 cc. The prescribed conventional radiation dose for the PTV was 50 Gray (Gy) delivered in 25 fractions. Contouring of the HD vertices, entire PTV, bowel, left and right femur head, rectum, etc., was performed using the SomaVision contouring station version 16.1. Eight small spherical vertices with an average diameter of 1.3 cm were contoured by optimizing its position within the gross tumor volume (GTV) of each patient. The combined volume of these vertices within the patients ranged from 13 cc to 20 cc. These small HD regions within the GTV were prescribed an additional simultaneously integrated HD of 27 Gy delivered in three fractions, alongside the conventional fractionated dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions for the entire PTV. Over the course of the following 22 fractions, a conventional fractionation approach of 2 Gy per fraction was used without HD areas. Equivalent uniform dose

The radiobiological measures such as biological effective dose (BED), tumor control probability (TCP), and EUD can be used to assess dose distributions. BED for lattice therapy is similar to conventional BED because HD targets only a small part of the tumor and involves fewer fractions, whereas TCP is influenced by the dose per fraction and the number of fractions. In this study, EUD was preferred because it simplifies the analysis of dose heterogeneity and varying fractionation schedules. Since doses differ between the first three fractions and the remaining 22 fractions, EUD condenses this complexity into a single, representative dose, making it easier to analyze while accurately reflecting the biological impact of the treatment. The radiobiological quantity EUD derived from the linear quadratic model was calculated for multiple treatment plans by employing different HD vertex numbers and different placement patterns (systematic and optimized) at the same HD vertex dose of 27 Gy in 3 fractions, whereas the rest of PTV was prescribed to a dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions (base plan).[23] The Niemierko equation was used to transform the raw dose volume histogram (DVH) data from the treatment planning system into a biologically equivalent physical EQD2. The DVH was then transformed into the DVH of the entire volume of the organ receiving the EUD.[4242526]

A series of RapidArc (RA)-based vertex plans were developed for all patients using a consistent approach. The first plan involves placing a single spherical HD vertex at the central plane of the target volume. The average diameter of HD vertices in the patient cohort was approximately 1.5 cm. In the subsequent plans, progressive addition of vertices was implemented, with one HD vertex added per plan. These vertices were positioned within the coplanar slices at regular intervals, with an average separation of 1.3 cm yielding a center-to-center distance of 2.6 cm. In the slice where the previous set of contours stopped in both the superior and inferior directions, an additional HD vertex was added consecutively in the subsequent treatment plans. On slices parallel to these two contours, two more HD vertices were positioned, maintaining an average separation of 1.5 cm between the contours in coplanar slices. This resulted in the creation of two more plans with increased HD vertices, and each vertex was placed at the specified average separation of 1.5 cm, allowing for a systematic and consistent spatial distribution of vertices throughout the target volume. In total, a set of eight treatment plans was systematically generated by incrementally increasing the number of HD vertices from one to eight. It was ensured that both the diameter and center-to-center spacing remained consistent for a given patient. In each of these plans, the HD vertices were optimized with a dose of 21 Gy delivered in three fractions, whereas the remaining 6 Gy contribution in three fractions was obtained as part of the base plan with a dose fractionation of 50 Gy in 25 fractions contributing a dose of 27 Gy in 3 fractions to HD vertices. To analyze the variation of EUD with placement pattern of vertices, a treatment plan created by placing eight HD vertices in optimized location within the treatment volume and the forementioned plan with eight HD vertices systematically placed were used. By optimized locations, it was meant that HD vertices are not placed in the vicinity of OARs or in slices that are closely overlapping with OARs. Optimized vertices were arranged in varying diameters and varying center-to-center separation avoiding the slices overlapping with the critical organs. However, a key consideration was to maintain the overall volume of HD vertices arranged in optimized pattern to be equivalent to the volume of HD vertices arranged systematically with eight vertices, within the treatment volume for each patient. Figure 3b demonstrates the optimized placement of eight vertices. The vertex plans were individually summed up with the base plan to create separate composite plans.

The relationship between the EUD and the number of vertices can be analyzed by examining the EUD values obtained from composite plans that consist of a base plan combined with individual vertex plans ranging from one to eight, including the base plan with a zero vertex. To analyze the effect of the placement pattern on EUD change, plans created by systematic and optimized placement of eight vertices within the treatment volume were used. The approaches of adding different numbers of vertices and assessing placement patterns were examined for variances using Levene’s test. Python code employed the Levene function from the “scipy.stats” module to conduct this test, evaluating the comparability of variances between datasets. Subsequently, the datasets were tested for statistical significance using the Student’s t-test.

Peak to valley dose ratio

PVDR analysis was conducted, utilizing 38 plans with peak doses set at 9 Gy, 12 Gy, 15 Gy, and 18 Gy to HD vertices for five bladder cancer patients. These peak doses were selected based on literature data and were also aligned with our institution’s clinical practice.[3] The base plan with a prescription dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions was common for all the peak doses. Vertex plans, numbered from one to eight, were placed systematically, and the plan with eight vertices positioned at optimized locations used in EUD evaluation was also used for the PVDR analysis. In this part of the study, HD dose vertex contours were optimized for just a single fraction rather than for three fractions adopted in EUD analysis. Composite plans were created by integrating the base plans with their corresponding vertex plans. Unlike the most common PVDRs used in the conventional grid therapy technique, here the PVDR was determined in three methods.

PVDR1 was defined as the ratio of the mean dose to HD vertices to the mean dose of a structure created by subtracting PTV from HD vertices utilizing its definition in grid therapy[28] PVDR2 was calculated as the ratio of D10 and D90 for all the vertex-based composite plans. Here, D10 represents the dose received by 10% of the PTV, whereas D90 signifies the dose received by 90% of the PTV and was obtained from DVH for PTV in composite plans[2331] PVDR3 was introduced by us to improve the clarity of the parameter and is calculated as the product of the above two PVDRs.PVDR3=PVDR1×PVDR2 Equation 4

RESULTS

Equivalent uniform dose

A total of 10 RA plans were examined in a single patient, consisting of nine plans with a systematic increase in the number of vertices from zero to eight and one plan with vertices at optimized location for each patient. For the plan with a prescription dose of 50 Gy delivered in 25 fractions without HD vertices, the average EUD calculated is 36.12 Gy (zero vertices). Sequential increases in EUD were observed as the number of vertices systematically increased from one to eight and the average increase in values was as follows: 36.33 Gy, 36.64 Gy, 36.77 Gy, 36.85 Gy, 37.11 Gy, 37.45 Gy, 37.67 Gy, and 37.91 Gy. Specifically, when eight vertices were systematically placed within the PTV, average increase in EUD was 37.91 Gy. In contrast, a higher average EUD of 38.41 Gy was obtained when eight vertices were placed in optimized locations and sizes within the tumor volume. Peak-to -valley dose ratio

It was observed that for systematic placement of vertices, PVDR1 values decreased with vertex number and varied from 1.42–1.30, 1.59–1.41, 1.75–1.51, and 1.91–1.58 for peak doses 27 Gy, 36 Gy, 45 Gy, and 54 Gy, respectively, whereas PVDR2 increased with vertex number and varied from 1.09–1.22, 1.12–1.32, 1.14–1.41, and 1.15–1.50, respectively, for forementioned doses. However, the product of PVDR1 and PVDR2 denoted as PVDR3 was nearly a constant with vertex addition beyond two vertices with average values of 1.60, 1.87, 2.14, and 2.42, respectively, for peak doses 9 Gy, 12 Gy, 15 Gy, and 18 Gy, respectively. The increase of PVDR3 with peak dose was linear with a slope equal to 0.03 and a Y-intercept equal to 0.78. Figure 5 shows the variation of all the three PVDRs with respect to HD vertex number. The values of PVDRs for eight optimized vertex plans matched those of eight vertices placed in a systematic pattern.

DISCUSSION

To achieve better tumor control through grid therapy, it is recommended to pair it with existing external beam therapy using three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, intensity-modulated radiotherapy, or volumetric modulated arc therapy. Evidence from both clinical outcomes and theoretical models indicates that SFRT can amplify treatment efficacy. Thus, LRT helps in this progress by allowing the incorporation of HD vertices in reduced fractions while concurrently utilizing conventional fractionation.[18] A comprehensive analysis was conducted on a total of 188 treatment plans with the objective of investigating EUD and PVDR measures in lattice therapy for patients with diseases characterized by large volumes. The novel approaches in the study will help in shedding light on the effects of various aspects including the techniques employed in the creation of HD vertex-based plans, the effect of HD vertex number, and HD vertex placement patterns on EUD and PVDR in bulky tumors.

Equivalent uniform dose

By systematically increasing the number of vertices within the PTV, we observed a corresponding increase in the EUD, indicating a potential enhancement in treatment effectiveness. There are only limited studies on lattice therapy, and one related study by Natasha et al. explored a somewhat similar concept. Natasha et al. found that as the aperture of the grid (which is the 2D equivalent of vertices) increased, EUD decreased. In contrast, our study observed that as the number of vertices increased, EUD also increased.[30] The initial plan without HD vertices exhibited a EUD of 36.12 Gy, representing the baseline value against which subsequent plans were compared. As the number of HD vertices was systematically increased from one to eight, a gradual and consistent rise in EUD was observed. This trend suggests that the presence of additional HD vertices contributes to heterogeneous dose distribution within the target volume, resulting in an elevated EUD. It is evident that as the number of vertices increases EUD also increases, this increase becomes statistically significant when just one HD vertices are contoured within the GTV. In addition, the rate of increase in EUD suffered a decline when more than two HD vertices were placed in coplanar slices and a noticeable increase in EUD was observed when HD vertices were placed in non-coplanar slices suggesting that the spreading of HD vertices within the three-dimensional volume as in grid therapy will help in achieving better therapeutic results. Our study reveals that implementing lattice therapy improves the EUD without compromising the dose of OARs. Optimized placement of vertices leads to even better outcomes in terms of both OAR and PTV doses. Thus, the technique can be rationally adopted in routine clinics by respecting the OAR doses as per RTOG constraints.[32]

When examining the proportion of the HD vertices that receive an HD compared to the entire PTV prescribed with a standard 2 Gy dose per fraction, it becomes evident that any underestimation of tumor control using the LQ model will be minor. This is because HDs are administered to only about 1% of the target volume, while the remaining portion of the PTV receives 2 Gy. For the variations in dose, percentage volumes receiving such HDs, and number of fractions with HD, the appropriateness of the choice of EUD equation for dose calculation is a topic for further research in LRT.[3133]

On investigating the effect of placement pattern of HD vertices, both the strategies – systematic placement of vertices and optimized placement of HD vertices – showed an enhancement in EUD compared to the baseline plan, whereas the plan with optimally spaced HD vertices exhibited a higher EUD of 38.41 Gy, suggesting that the spatial arrangement of HD vertices plays a role in optimizing the dose distribution, and an optimized placement can offer certain advantages over a systematic arrangement. When placing the HD vertices systematically, it happens that vertices of equal dimensions need to be placed in the vicinity of the OARs, which can potentially increase the doses delivered to these critical structures. The constraint of placing HD vertices in predetermined positions for systematic placement can limit the flexibility of avoiding OARs or optimizing the dose distribution around them. Consequently, the systematic placement of HD vertices may inadvertently lead to higher doses being delivered to OARs, potentially increasing the risk of radiation-induced toxicity and compromising treatment outcomes. In contrast, the optimized placement of HD vertices offers more freedom and flexibility in arranging them within the GTV and ensuring its placement away from the respective OARs. This liberty allows for a more individualized and patient-specific approach to HD vertex placement, considering the unique anatomical considerations and dose constraints in each case. Optimizing vertex placement patterns is crucial, and Zhang et al. have developed a novel lattice treatment planning method that optimizes vertex placement patterns and other plan variables, resulting in improved target PVDR and OAR sparing.[34] Our study results indicate that both the placement pattern of HD vertices is statistically significant and hence placement of vertices in optimized locations can be adopted in clinics as it offers clinical and dosimetric advantages over systematic placement.

Peak-to-valley dose ratio

PVDR is another important dosimetric parameter in spatially fractionated radiotherapy. Diverse definitions of PVDRs are found in the literature,[35] so it is crucial to select an appropriate definition for accurate use in LRT. We, thus, evaluated the PVDRs using 38 RA plans, focusing on the increase in the number of HD vertices across different peak doses. In grid therapy, while using the traditional concept of PVDR for PVDR1, we chose to evaluate the ratio of the mean dose to HD vertices versus the mean dose to the remainder of the PTV, rather than focusing on the dose at specific points. Notably, as the number of HD vertices increased, this quantity decreased, even at a constant peak dose. PVDR2, defined as the ratio of the dose received by 10% of the entire PTV to the dose received by 90% of the entire PTV (including HD vertices), also showed a decrease as the number of HD vertices rose for a constant peak dose. Interestingly, when the HD vertex numbers increased beyond 2, the value derived from multiplying PVDR1 and PVDR2, which is defined as PVDR3, remained constant. PVDR3, introduced by us, on the other hand, solely depends on the peak dose and makes PVDR3 an ideal reporting metric for LRT. Adopting PVDR3 for reporting will standardize the dose reporting criteria across any number of HD vertices, as long as they have consistent peak doses. A meticulous examination of this metric clearly highlights the dose distribution’s heterogeneity due to HD vertex introduction and is also independent of the placement pattern of HD vertices. When we plotted the change of PVDR3 against peak doses, the result was a linear relationship with a slope of 0.03 and a Y-intercept of 0.78 is shown in Figure 6. This Y-intercept represents the quantification of dose heterogeneity in treatment when no HD vertices are included. One of the limitations of this study is that the accuracy of delivery for the treatment plans was not verified on the treatment unit. Ensuring accurate delivery involves a detailed analysis of various parameters, which is beyond the scope of this work. In future research, we will focus on comprehensive quality assurance methods to evaluate and ensure the accuracy of treatment plans developed using lattice therapy.

HDs of radiation have been shown to contribute to the generation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which play a crucial role in the immune response against cancer. They are key effectors of the adaptive immune system and are capable of recognizing and eliminating cancer cells. When delivered at HDs, radiation therapy can induce various immunogenic changes within the tumor microenvironment.[36373839] Thus, lattice therapy may overcome the associated radioresistance of hypoxic bulk tumor volumes and activate the intercellular signaling pathways that arise as a bystander effect by specifically targeting hypoxic areas with an intense hypofractionated radiation dose.[40] At present, there is no conclusive evidence proving that LRT is superior for specific diseases, and this remains a subject of ongoing debate.[41]

CONCLUSIONS

EUD measures revealed that increasing the number of HD vertices within the PTV systematically resulted in a corresponding increase in the EUD and also the placement of vertices should be spread out within the bulk volume rather than restricting to coplanar slices, suggesting an enhancement in treatment effectiveness. The study also highlighted that any number of HD vertices above zero was statistically significant and could be employed in clinics by carefully considering the OAR doses. Furthermore, optimized placement of HD vertices showed a higher EUD compared to the systematic placement, indicating that spatial arrangement plays a role in optimizing the dose distribution. The quantity PVDR3 did not change with vertex number beyond two for a constant peak dose and has the potential to be considered as a dose reporting metric for LRT plans. Further research and long-term follow-up are necessary to confirm and expand these findings, facilitating the translation of lattice therapy into a common clinical practice. This research will contribute to advancing our knowledge in this field and potentially improve the efficacy and outcomes of LRT in patients.
