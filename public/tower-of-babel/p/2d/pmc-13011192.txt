Biocatalytic potential of Ensifer adhaerens S-5 D-Carbamoylase: Insights from in silico and in vitro analyses
Authors: Marina Paronyan, Haykanush Koloyan, Hovsep Aganyants, Sona Avetisyan, Marina Melkumyan, Tigran Soghomonyan, Lev Khoyetsyan, Artur Hambardzumyan, Vehary Sakanyan, Anichka Hovsepyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The need for optically pure amino acids for producing food products and pharmaceuticals increases every year. These amino acids can be obtained through enantioselective ring opening of 5-membered hydantoins using an enzyme known as hydantoinase 1, followed by decarbamoylation of the precursors using carbamoylase. The first mention of bacterial D-carbamoylase (EC 3.5.1.77) from Agrobacterium was noted in the synthesis of D-amino acids in 1979 2. The process in which hydantoinase and carbamoylase convert chemically synthesized precursors into optically pure amino acids is known as the “hydantoinase process“”. Both enzymes are D-stereospecific: D-hydantoinase (EC 3.5.2.2) exclusively catalyzes the stereospecific cleavage of 5-monosubstituted hydantoins yielding N-carbamoyl-D-amino acids, while D-carbamoylase converts N-carbamoyl-D-amino acids into corresponding D-amino acids by removing the carbamoyl group 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8.

The “hydantoinase process” is a technique for the development of D-amino acids that offers several advantages. Firstly, it is not harmful to human health. Secondly, it is environmentally friendly, sustainable, and reusable. For these reasons, the process is of particular interest.

In addition to its primary function of opening the ring of 5-monosubstituted hydantoins, D-hydantoinase has the ability to catalyze the same reaction for dihydropyrimidines. Therefore, a physiological role for this enzyme in microorganisms as a pyrimidine scavenger has been proposed 9. However, the physiological role of carbamoylase remains unclear, although it has been found in many microorganisms, including Arthrobacter sp. 5, Pseudomonas sp. 10, Comamonas sp. 11, Blastobacter sp. 12, Agrobacterium sp. 13, and Agrobacterium tumefaciens AM10D 8.

It should be noted that the coexistence of both enzymes (D-hydantoinase and D-carbamoylase) in microorganisms is quite rare. Only a small number of co-presence cases have been described 2, 5, 6. However, in a separate study, the authors presented new organisms capable of converting 5-monosubstituted hydantoins to the corresponding D-amino acids possibly via D-carbamoylase pathway: Brevibacillus, Empedobacter, Microbacterium, and Staphylococcus 14.

A number of studies have been documented that pertain to the cloning and characterization of D-carbamoylases, which are encoded by genes from a variety of microorganisms. These studies have also focused on the expression of these enzymes in the E. coli system 5, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19. However, over the past few decades, some studies have reported that it is often challenging to achieve overexpression of soluble D-carbamoylase in heterologous bacterial expression systems, especially in E. coli 20, 21, 22. This phenomenon is attributed to the process of protein aggregation. Protein aggregation occurs independently of the type of bacteria 23, 24. In the absence of secretion of the overexpressed protein into the medium, an accumulation of protein occurs, resulting in the formation of inclusion bodies (IBs) 25. A portion of the recombinant proteins that form IBs persist within the insoluble fraction. Consequently, these proteins are excluded from the protein purification process, which exclusively encompasses soluble forms.

Proteins can be extracted from IBs by separating them from cell debris. Several procedures have been proposed to address this issue. In the initial approach, the aggregated proteins undergo complete denaturation, solubilization, and refolding to restore the product to its native conformation and full functionality 26, 27. However, this is not always the case. This paper presents methodologies for extracting non-denatured proteins from IBs. These methodologies use mild detergents, such as N-lauroylsarcosine, as solubilizing agents. In such instances, the extracted proteins maintain their inherent biological functionality 28. The feasibility of this method is due to the presence of recombinant proteins in their native form within IBs.

Worall and Goss, along with Tokatlidis and their respective co-authors, were the first to document biologically active IBs 30, 31.

Carratalá et al. discussed the performance of N-lauroylsarcosine in the chromatographic purification of complex recombinant proteins. These proteins are produced in E. coli cell extracts and maintain biological activity. Additionally, no detergent remnants remain in the end product. The authors recommended using N-lauroylsarcosine as both a solubilizer of protein aggregates and a purifier of soluble proteins, suggesting its incorporation as a useful tool in purification steps applied to the soluble cell fraction 32.

Korean scientists described the construction of an artificial N-carbamoylase-D-hydantoinase fusion enzyme comprising N-carbamoylase from Agrobacterium radiobacter NRRL B11291 and D-hydantoinase from Bacillus thermocatenulatus GH2. The scientists also evaluated the enzyme's activity. Consequently, they engineered a bifunctional enzyme (CAB-HYD1) that exhibited superior efficiency compared to the individual enzymes 33.

Biochemical studies of known D-carbamoylases have demonstrated that enzymes from different species exhibit homodimeric, homotrimeric, or homotetrameric quaternary structures. The molecular masses of the monomers range from 32 to 40 kDa, while the native enzymes have molecular masses ranging from 67 to 150 kDa 34.

For the past two decades, research has focused on developing crystallographic structures of D-carbamoylases. The crystal structures of D-carbamoylases from Nitratireductor indicus, along with mutant variants 35, Agrobacterium radiobacter 36, and Agrobacterium sp. strain KNK712 37 have been determined and made accessible in databases.

Sophisticated in silico methodologies have made investigating catalytic indices of enzymes and designing their performance more attainable. In their study, Bellini et al. used a multifaceted approach that included structural analysis, molecular modeling, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics to study N-carbamoyl-D-amino acid amidohydrolase (D-NCAase) from the Bradyrhizobium japonicum strain. Their work suggests using D-NCAase from that strain for industrial production of D-amino acids, including D-p-hydroxyphenylglycine (D-HPG), an intermediate in beta-lactam antibiotic synthesis 38.

We recently conducted studies on the D-carbamoylase of Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A. These studies employed in vitro and in silico methods 39. While in vitro studies are an effective approach to biochemical research, in silico analyses are becoming increasingly important due to their ability to accelerate scientific discovery through simulations and predictive modeling.

This study aims to explain the substrate specificity of the recombinant D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 heterologously expressed in E. coli, using both in silico and in vitro approaches. We compared the substrate specificities of D-carbamoylases from E. adhaerens S-5 and Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A 39 to elucidate their potential applications.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms, vector

D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 was chosen for in silico studies (molecular modeling, molecular docking, molecular dynamics), cloning and expression. The gene encoding D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 (GenBank: AY787759.1) was chemically synthesized and cloned into the pET24a(+) expression vector, which was then transformed into E. coli BL21 Star cells, serving as the host strain for gene expression. The UniProt database was used to search D-carbamoylases as in our previous research 39.

Reagents and substrates

The D-carbamoylase gene was amplified by PCR using ROTI®PolTaqSMix, purchased from Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG (Karlsruhe, Germany). The amplified product was purified using the GeneJET PCR Purification Kit from Thermo Scientific™ (Waltham, MA, USA). pET24a(+) vector was double-digested with NdeI and XhoI restriction enzymes according to the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The purified PCR product was cloned according to Gibson assembly protocol by Gibson Assembly® Cloning Kit, which was received from New England Biolabs (Hitchin, UK). Plasmid DNA isolation and purification were carried out with GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania). A 1 kb DNA ladder from Solis BioDyne (Tartu, Estonia) was used for gel electrophoresis. Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was used for the induction process and N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt was used as a solubilizing agent and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein purification was performed using the Ni-NTA Protein Purification System (K95001) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Altrincham, UK). A pre-stained 10–245 kDa protein ladder for SDS-PAGE was provided by Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, ROTI®Mark TRICOLOR (Karlsruhe, Germany).

To measure the D-carbamoylase activity of E. adhaerens S-5, N-carbamoyl derivatives of several amino acids were used as substrates. N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, N-carbamoyl-L-tryptophan, and N-carbamoyl-L-leucine were received from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). N-carbamoyl derivatives of D-leucine, D-valine, D-phenylalanine, and D-alanine were synthesized at the Scientific Technological Centre of Organic and Pharmaceutical Chemistry (Yerevan, Armenia). The data from 1H and 13C NMR analyses were provided in the Supplementary Materials, Figs. S1–S4 39.

Molecular docking

One 3D conformer for each ligand was downloaded from the PubChem database as.sdf files. Gnina 1.0, a molecular docking software, was used in Colab Notebooks for molecular docking analysis 40. For the analysis, a blind, whole docking approach was applied to all enzyme-ligand complexes. To specify the binding site of the D-carbamoylase structure a ligand file (autobox_ligand) was provided, the minimum and maximum values for the x , y, and z coordinates of the ligand were used to generate respective rectangular prism. The explicit random seed was set to 0, while exhaustiveness to 8. The convolutional neural net (CNN) was disabled. The ligand structures were energy minimized with OpenBabel 3.1.1 software in Colab Notebooks 41 applying the MMFF94 force field and specifying the maximum number of steps to 1500. Discovery Studio 2021 was utilized to visualize and analyze enzyme-ligand interactions in 2D and 3D interpretations.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

In this study GROMACS 2023 software was used to perform all MD simulation experiments 42. To generate the topology files of all used ligands, the SwissParam online server was used 43. The dodecahedron-shaped water box was generated with a minimum distance of 10 Å from the enzyme-ligand complex applying the Tip3p water model to solvate the complexes. In a simulation cell periodic boundary conditions were implemented. Subsequently, Na+ and Cl- ions were introduced to neutralize the system. MD simulations were conducted to validate the quality of the generated model by evaluating the stability through 100 ps simulations maintaining constant temperature at 300 K (NVT-constant: Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature). This was followed by NPT (constant Number of particles, Pressure and Temperature) optimization conducted for 100 ps, too. Each MD run lasted 100 ns. The Charm27 force field parameters were applied for MD simulations. MD simulation’s trajectories were saved every 10 ps, resulting in 10,000 frames for each analysis. Free energy calculations using the last 10 ns of simulation trajectories were performed with the gmx MMPBSA tool, which is based on AMBER’s MMPBSA.py 44.

Cloning of D-carbamoylase gene

We amplified the gene coding for D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 strain by PCR using the forward primer D-carb_Ens_pET24a(+)_NdeI_F (5′-GGAGATATACATATGACCCGTCAGATGATCCTGG-3′) and the reverse primer D-carb_Ens_pET24a(+)_XhoI_6H_R (5′-GTGGTGGTGCTCGAGCAGTTCCGCAATCAGACCGTAG-3′).

The primers used for cloning the D-carbamoylase gene contained motifs from the gene and vector sequences, as well as sites for the NdeI and XhoI restriction enzymes.

The reverse primer was designed without the stop codon of the D-carbamoylase gene to promote the synthesis of the polyhistidine tag (His-tagged).

Regions of homology with the E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase gene are marked in both primers. The D-carbamoylase gene of E. adhaerens S-5 was amplified by PCR 45.

The purified PCR product was cloned into the expression vector pET24a(+) using the Gibson assembly method 46. Prior to assembly, the vector was double-digested with the restriction enzymes NdeI and XhoI. The resulting recombinant plasmid was designated pET24a(+)-EnsDcase. E. coli BL21 Star strain cells were transformed with the recombinant plasmid, and kanamycin-resistant colonies were later selected on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar medium in the presence of kanamycin (50 μg/mL).

The presence of the recombinant plasmid, pET24a(+)-EnsDcase, was confirmed by direct colony PCR analysis of the transformed colonies 47.

To overexpress of E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase, single recombinant E. coli BL21 Star/pET24a(+)-EnsDcase colonies were cultivated overnight at 37 °C in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin. The following day, protein expression was induced by adding 0.75 mM IPTG at 30 °C, after which the sample was incubated for 20 h. After the induction process was finished, the recombinant E. coli cells were harvested by centrifugation. The precipitated cells were dissolved in a 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). The next step was to disrupt the suspended cells using ultrasound on ice.

The crude extract was clarified by centrifugation. The resulting supernatant and pellet fractions were then analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Solubilization of inclusion bodies

For this purpose, we used a modified method described by Peternel et al. 48. The insoluble protein fraction IBs of the crude extract was separated. This was done by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 ℃. To carry out the dissolution process of the insoluble fraction, a 0.4% solution of N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt was prepared in a 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and 80 µL of this solution was added to each 1 mg of precipitate. The mixture was first incubated for 24 h at 20 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. Then, it was subjected to a centrifugation step for 15 min at 4400 × g. We analyzed the resulting supernatant, in which our target protein was dissolved, by SDS-PAGE.

Purification of recombinant D-carbamoylase

The process of purification was performed as described in our previous article 39.

SDS-PAGE analyses

SDS-PAGE was performed using a 12.5% polyacrylamide gel in the presence of 0.1% SDS at pH 8.9, following Bio-Rad's protocol. The electrophoresis buffer used in the electrode chambers was 0.1 M Tris–glycine at pH 8.3. Proteins were stained with a 0.1% solution of Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, according to Bio-Rad recommendations 49.

Gel-Filtration

Gel-filtration was performed using the FPLC NGC Quest 10 Plus system by Bio-Rad, equipped with a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg column. Three milliliters of crude enzyme preparation (obtained by centrifugation of the supernatant at 10,000 g and filtration through a 0.2 μm Millipore syringe filter) were applied to the column. Elution was carried out with 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0, at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. For subsequent analyses 4 mL fractions were collected.

D-carbamoylase activity Assay

D-carbamoylase activity was measured in a 100 mM phosphate buffer as described in our previous study 39, but incubating 15–120 min at 40 °C. The protein concentration was determined by the method of Groves and Davis 50. Enzyme activity was expressed in units (U), with 1 U defined as the conversion of 1 μmol of substrate per minute. The specific activity of the enzyme was expressed in U/mg protein.

Results and Discussion

Modeling of the D-carbamoylase

To find out more appropriate biocatalyst, compared to previously studied D-carbamoylase 39, we continued our research on the E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase. The comparison of the active sites of both enzymes will facilitate the understanding of their tendency in terms of substrate specificity. To give more insights on active site of E. adhaerens S-5.

D-carbamoylase, we generated, for the first time, a model of D-carbamoylase E. adhaerens S-5 monomer using ColabFold 51, 52, a slightly simplified and automated version of AlphaFold2, with default parameters and relaxing with Amber. Used ColabFold differs from the original implementation from Deepmind 53 by replacing the homology detection of AlphaFold2 with MMseqs2 (many-against-many sequence searching) 54.

The quality of the model was confirmed by predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT), where values lower than 50 are considered unstructured. For our model the overall values were higher than 50. Most parts had values over 90 and close to 100, indicating the high quality of the model.

Molecular docking

In continuation, the modeled enzyme was applied to molecular docking analysis to detect the binding pocket of the enzyme, to find the right orientation of ligands in it and to compare interacting amino acids with that of the previously studied enzyme. To predict and select the binding cavity from a variety of possible options, Discovery Studio 2021′s “From Receptor Cavities” tool was utilized. The analysis of molecular docking results has shown that the model of E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase has affinity towards all five carbamoyl-D-amino acids tested, with binding energies of N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan (−7.02 kcal/mol),

N-carbamoyl-D-leucine (−5.43 kcal/mol), N-carbamoyl-D-valine (−5.24 kcal/mol), N-carbamoyl-D-phenylalanine (−5.20 kcal/mol) and N-carbamoyl-D-alanine (−4.75 kcal/mol). Our previous results showed that the Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A D-carbamoylase model had affinity towards the above-mentioned molecules with the highest affinity to N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan (−5.1 kcal/mol) 39, as in the current study. Even, Arg176, Asn173 and other amino acids are presented in binding cavities of both enzymes when they interact with the N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, we would like to notice that in the current study, for instance, Arg176 forms a hydrogen bond with carboxyl group rather than carbamoyl group like in the Pseudomonas enzyme. These observations indicate that while the cavities are similar, the ligands are positioned differently, likely due to differences in the environment or structure around the active site. The results show that the D-carbamoylase can be studied further, as a biocatalyst of these molecules.

Our further work was pursued on N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, due to its highest affinity and possibility to parallelly use the D-carbamoylase along with previously designed D-hydantoinase 55 in a one biocatalytic process.

One of the most essential interactions for this biocatalytic process is the interaction between the enzyme and the ligand’s carbamoyl group. Based on the molecular docking analysis, it is proposed that in the hydrogen interaction between the NH group of the N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan and the carbonyl oxygen of Val205, the ligand play a role of donor, while an acceptor is the enzyme’s carbonyl oxygen. In Asp209, upon interaction with the N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, the side-chain carboxyl group (–COO−) forms a hydrogen bond with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan’s NH group, where the carboxyl group is an acceptor and NH group of the N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan is the donor. Even though earlier structural investigations suggested that the catalytic Cys172 is situated right next to the carbamoyl carbonyl during its nucleophilic attack 34, the molecular docking study illustrates a pre-reactive enzyme–substrate complex. In this arrangement, the residues Asp209, Thr212, Asn202, Val205, etc. are located in close proximity to the carbamoyl group and form stabilizing hydrogen-bond interactions (except for Asn202) with the carbonyl moiety. These interactions are therefore interpreted as contributing to substrate binding and pre-organization of the active site, facilitating subsequent nucleophilic attack by the conserved catalytic Cys172. This may allow the carbonyl group to approach the active site, which undergoes further reorganization.

The data available suggest a stable and specific orientation of the ligand in the binding pocket, which is a crucial point in the biocatalytic process.

Comparison of the amino acid residues of Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A 39 and E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylases interacting with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan showed that in both cases ligands’ carbamoyl groups form hydrogen bonds with enzymes’ amino acids. Although, we noticed that the interacting amino acids differ between the two compared enzymes, they share similar properties including polarity (Asp, Thr of E. adhaerens S-5) and (Asn, Arg of Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A), enabling them to establish crucial hydrogen bonds for catalysis.

Comparison of the active sites of the two enzymes has indicated that the group of amino acids, i.e., Thr212, Asn173, Cys172, His144, Pro199, Thr198 and Arg 176 are common for both enzymes, implying analogous active sites, however with the different orientation of the ligand, may be due to van der Waals contacts or electrostatic interactions with nearby residues. Such data reveal how the ligand fits and interacts within the binding pocket, representing a milestone towards engineering the enzyme with improved properties and efficiency.

Molecular dynamics

To confirm the molecular docking results and further monitor the involvement of the enzyme’s residues in substrate binding, we ran a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation for E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase model alone and in complex with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan. To evaluate the stability of the enzyme and enzyme-ligand complex, Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) was monitored, as shown in Fig. 2a. Both the enzyme and complex are stable throughout the simulation, with the slightly higher range for the last one, which is typical, especially when forming hydrogen bonds. The calculated average RMSD for enzyme alone and for complex was 0.25 ± 0.02 nm and 0.34 ± 0.04 nm, respectively. Comparable findings were observed for the D-carbamoylase of Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A, indicating slightly a higher RMSD for complex 39. Root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) analysis was performed, to evaluate the fluctuations of every amino acid in the enzyme’s structure. The analysis performed showed that the fluctuations of amino acids for both models are in the 0.039–0.64 nm range during the 100 ns simulation, which is considered acceptable. The average RMSF for the enzyme was 0.121 ± 0.02 nm and for complex 0.125 ± 0.03 nm, indicating no major changes in fluctuations. If we compare the enzyme alone and enzyme-ligand complex, the RMSF values of the amino acids closely interacting with ligand (Arg176, Pro199, Val205, Asp209, Thr212 and His215) have close values with slight deviation, indicating that ligand binding does not significantly alter the local flexibility of these residues, which may suggest stable and consistent interactions. Thus, RMSF of D-carbamoylase revealed approximately the same fluctuation when bound to N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, on average (Fig. 2b), which supports the structural integrity of the complex. The RMSF analysis of binding site residues of Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A D-carbamoylase showed stabilization of the enzyme when ligand is bound to it 39.

To evaluate the behavior of the enzyme compactness in response to the presence of the ligand, we monitored the radius of gyration (Rg) in enzyme and enzyme-ligand complex during 100 ns simulation (Fig. S1a). The deviation in compactness can be seen during the first 10 ns, where the complex seems to be more dispersed compared to the enzyme. Structures of both, the enzyme and complex, become almost convergent throughout the rest of the simulation and can be considered as stabilized. The average Rg value for the enzyme was estimated to be 1.88 ± 0.05 nm, and for complex 1.87 ± 0.05 nm. Compared to Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A enzyme, where the process of adaptation continues till 35–40 ns 39, here the process of convergence is shorter, indicating faster equilibration.

To define the extent of the atoms on the enzyme’s surface to interact with the solvent, SASA was calculated during the 100 ns simulation for both complex and enzyme alone (Fig. S1b). The average SASA value for the enzyme was 145.4 ± 1.72 nm2 and for the complex 143.4 ± 1.68 nm2. As the values are relatively the same for both models, it can be concluded that ligand has negligible influence on enzyme-solvent interactions.

In further confirmation of the molecular docking outcomes, we monitored the establishment of hydrogen bonds between the enzyme and the ligand as a significant determinant for ligand stability within the binding pocket. The simulation showed that the enzyme forms up to 6 hydrogen bonds with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan per nanosecond, satisfying the distance and angle criteria (angle – 30°, cutoff radius – 0.35 nm) (Fig. 3). MM-PBSA calculation

To calculate the average binding free energy for the last 10 ns of MD trajectory gmx_mmpbsa tool was used 44. In the formation of enzyme-ligand complex, Van der Waals interactions (ΔVDWAALS = − 24.42 ± 1.98 kcal/mol), electrostatic interactions (ΔEEL = − 16.65 ± 1.45 kcal/mol) and nonpolar contribution of repulsive solute–solvent interactions to the solvation energy (ΔENPOLAR = − 3.17 ± 0.41 kcal/mol) were playing the principal role. Meanwhile, nonpolar interactions in a solvated system (ΔEPB = 28.93 ± 3.23 kcal/mol) had a negative influence on enzyme-ligand formation. Similar data are shown for Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A D-carbamoylase, where the principal role in enzyme-ligand formation is played by Van der Waals and electrostatic interactions 39, indicating flexible nature of the enzyme-ligand complex. The total binding energy of the enzyme-ligand complex, in the current study, was − 15.3 ± 1.71 kcal/mol, indicating an energetically beneficial, relatively favorable and stable interaction, where the contributions of ΔGGAS and ΔGSOLV were also accounted for.

Residue-level decomposition analysis identified key contributors to substrate stabilization and catalysis. It was demonstrated that Asp209 exhibited the most significant favorable binding free energy, dominated by electrostatic and polar interactions, consistent with its stabilizing role. Lys127 and Asn173 also contributed substantially, stabilizing the carbamoyl group while orienting the substrate. Asn202 and His208 provided additional stabilization, thereby supporting substrate positioning. The catalytic nucleophile Cys172 exhibited a positive total binding energy, indicative of its chemical role in covalent catalysis rather than maximizing non-covalent interactions. This finding aligns with its function in the preparatory phase of covalent catalysis, as opposed to the formation of stable complexes. The Val205 and Thr212 residues contributed to this process through hydrophobic and polar contacts, thereby forming a substrate-binding pocket. Conversely, the absence of a substantial contribution from Glu47 is anticipated, as its primary function pertains to general acid–base catalysis and transition-state stabilization through proton transfer. These processes are not encompassed by classical MM-PBSA. These results provide energetic evidence linking residue-specific interactions to catalytic mechanisms. Therefore, the MM-PBSA result aligns with the established catalytic mechanism of D-carbamoylase 37 and should be interpreted as an indicator of the functional importance of the Cys-Glu-Lys triad.

Gene Cloning, D-carbamoylase expression and purification

We previously cloned the D-hydantoinase gene from the thermophilic bacterium Geobacillus stearothermophilus and investigated its properties as a potential enzyme for the initial step of the “hydantoinase process” 55, 56. The investigation of some characteristics of D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 as a valuable catalyst for the synthesis of D-amino acids therefore appeared relevant and of interest, as this enzyme catalyzes the second step of the “hydantoinase process”. In subsequent in vitro studies, the results of in silico analyses were taken into account; in particular, N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, predicted to have the highest affinity for the enzyme, was used as the substrate for determining the activity of D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5.

At this stage of our study, we cloned the gene that codes for D-carbamoylase in E. adhaerens S-5 (see Fig. S3). We amplified the synthetic D-carbamoylase gene, which originated from the E. adhaerens S-5 strain, by PCR. Then, we cloned the purified PCR product into the pET24a(+) expression vector according to the protocol 46. We transformed E. coli BL21 Star cells with the resulting recombinant plasmid, pET24a(+)-EnsDcase, and demonstrated its presence in the colonies by direct colony PCR analysis 47.

To overexpress E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase, induction was performed with IPTG at 30 °C, followed by a 20-hour incubation period. The recombinant E. coli cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in a phosphate buffer. Then, the cells were disrupted by ultrasound while maintained on ice.

The crude extract (Fig. 5a, Lane 1) was clarified by centrifugation, and the resulting supernatant and pellet fractions were then analyzed by SDS-PAGE. In this study, however, we found that almost all of the overexpressed D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 accumulated in an aggregated form within the insoluble cellular fraction after IPTG induction. This aggregation complicated further purification and subsequently led to very low enzyme activity. One approach to addressing the issue of insoluble protein is to reduce the rate of recombinant protein synthesis by lowering the temperature during expression. This strategy aims to extend the protein folding time, thereby promoting proper folding and reducing IBs formation. The use of low temperatures to minimize IBs formation was first demonstrated by Schein and Noteborn 57. We performed the induction of E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase at 25 °C and lower temperatures; however, this approach did not yield a positive effect. Similar results have been reported by other authors 21, 58.

To address the issue, we used the N-lauroylsarcosine solubilization to convert the synthesized protein from its insoluble form into a soluble, functional state. Several procedures for protein extraction from IBs have been described, employing either denaturing or non-denaturing conditions. Non-denaturing methods allow for the direct recovery of biologically active proteins from IBs using mild solubilizing agents, such as N-lauroylsarcosine 28. The success of this approach is based on the large number of recombinant proteins in native or native-like conformation contained in IBs 29. N-lauroylsarcosine is one of the most commonly used agents for the extraction of non-denaturated proteins from IBs 32.

Measurement of carbamoylase activity revealed that, although the crude extract exhibited a specific activity of approximately 0.077 U/mg when using N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan – the substrate predicted by in silico studies to have the highest affinity – the supernatant of the crude extract was nearly entirely lacking enzymatic activity. This means that practically all of the synthesized enzyme is located in precipitated IBs. Using a modified method described by Peternell et al. 48, we were able to extract significant amounts of the target protein from IBs. The target protein that was solubilized using N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt was analyzed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 5a, Lane 2). The Ni-NTA purification system was used to purify the polyhistidine-tagged D-carbamoylase after solubilization with N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt (Fig. 5a, Lanes 3–5).

The enzyme’s physicochemical and catalytic properties, as well as its activity, were subsequently studied. The physicochemical properties were studied using SDS-PAGE and gel filtration techniques. The gene sequence and corresponding amino acid translation of the His-tagged D-carbamoylase from E. adhaerens S-5 are presented in Fig. S3. The calculated theoretical pI for this protein is 6.55, and the subunit molecular mass is 35.562 kDa. These values were obtained using the Compute pI/Mw tool available on ExPASy server 59.

The subunit molecular mass determination revealed 36.2 kDa for fraction B1 of Ni-NTA chromatography (Fig. 5a, Lane 3), and 39.0 kDa for fractions B2 and B3 (Fig. 5a, Lanes 4 and 5), calculated according to the formula presented in Fig. 5b. This difference may be caused by the presence of 250 mM imidazole in fraction B1, which was removed from fractions B2 and B3 by dialysis to combine them for future use as a purified enzyme. The measured molecular mass of 39.0 kDa is slightly higher than the calculated molecular mass of 35.562 kDa for the enzyme subunit. For comparison, Wu et al. reported a molecular mass of 38.0 kDa for the D-carbamoylase subunit from Sinorhizobium morelens S-5 (in present E. adhaerens S-5) by SDS-PAGE 19.

The molecular mass of the intact recombinant D-carbamoylase was determined by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 FPLC column (Fig. S4). The results indicated a molecular mass of 148.0 kDa for the His-tagged E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase, which is slightly higher than the calculated molecular mass of 142.2 kDa for the homotetramer. These findings are consistent with the data reported by Wu et al., who observed a molecular mass of 150.0 kDa for the quaternary structure of S. morelens S-5 (in present E. adhaerens S-5) D-carbamoylase by gel filtration, confirming its homotetrameric nature 19. For comparison, the D-carbamoylase from Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A was shown to be a homodimer 17.

In vitro analysis showed that the enzyme has activity towards aromatic and aliphatic N-carbamoyl-amino acids, which aligns well with the in silico data. Moreover, higher activity was detected when dealing with bulky substrates, such as N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan.

The comparison of results between Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A and E. adhaerens S-5 showed that both enzymes have strict D-stereospecificity. Overall, D-carbamoylase from Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A exhibits significantly higher specific activity compared to the enzyme from E. adhaerens S-5. The highest specific activity of the Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A enzyme was observed with N-carbamoyl-D-alanine, which, in contrast, showed the lowest detectable activity for E. adhaerens S-5. The same data (the lowest detectable enzyme activity with N-carbamoyl-D-alanine) for S. morelens S-5 (in present E. adhaerens S-5) was shown by Wu et al 19. For E. adhaerens S-5, the highest specific activity was recorded with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan, which ranked as the second highest for the Pseudomonas sp. strain KNK003A enzyme.

Conclusion

To provide more insights into the active site and understand substrate specificity, a high-quality model of E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase was predicted through the ColabFold platform.

Molecular docking of the generated model of E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase with five tested carbamoyl-D-amino acids indicated the highest interaction energy with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan (−7.02 kcal/mol), followed by N-carbamoyl-D-leucine, N-carbamoyl-D-valine, N-carbamoyl-D-phenylalanine and N-carbamoyl-D-alanine, showing binding energies between − 5.43 and − 4.75 kcal/mol.

The molecular docking results for N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan and enzyme interaction were further monitored by 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations for E. adhaerens S-5 D-carbamoylase model alone and in complex with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan. Both the enzyme and complex are stable throughout the simulation, with the slightly higher range for the last one, which is typical, especially when forming hydrogen bonds. The study of the compaction of the enzyme N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan complex, monitored by the radius of gyration during 100 ns simulations, revealed a slight decrease in Rg as a result of complexation. In the 100 ns simulation experiments, it was revealed that the solvent-interacting enzyme surface also slightly decreased after complexation with the substrate. The molecular docking results were further supported by MD simulations, which revealed the formation of up to 6 hydrogen bonds with N-carbamoyl-D-tryptophan per nanosecond, satisfying the distance and angle criteria.

The total binding energy calculated for the last 10 ns of the MD trajectory using the gmx_mmpbsa tool for the enzyme-ligand complex was − 15.3 ± 1.71 kcal/mol, indicating energetically favorable and stable interaction. The contributions of ΔVDWAALS, ΔENPOLAR, ΔEPB, ΔGGAS, and ΔGSOLV were included in the calculation. Furthermore, residue-level decomposition analysis delineated principal contributors in enzyme-ligand interactions thereby underscoring the functional importance of the Cys-Glu-Lys triad.

Free energy landscape calculation for the enzyme and enzyme-ligand complex backbone, using the two principal components as reaction coordinates, provides a comprehensive view of the conformational states of both systems during the 100 ns simulation. The results showed that, compared to the enzyme, the enzyme-ligand complex has a narrower low-energy basin, suggesting that ligand binding influences conformational rearrangements.

The synthetic D-carbamoylase gene from E. adhaerens S-5 was cloned into the pET24a(+) expression vector in a His-tagged variant and expressed in E. coli BL21 Star cells. It was observed that nearly all overexpressed D-carbamoylase accumulated in an aggregated form within the insoluble cellular fraction. When N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt was applied, the largest portion of the target protein was solubilized. Physicochemical analysis of the solubilized recombinant His-tagged D-carbamoylase indicated that the enzyme forms a homotetramer with a molecular mass of approximately 148.0 kDa as estimated by SEC chromatography (142.2 kDa based on amino acid sequence). The subunit molecular weight was estimated to be around 39.0 kDa by SDS-PAGE, closely matching the calculated molecular weight of 35.56 kDa. The study of D-carbamoylase substrate specificity indicated that the enzyme exhibits strict stereoselective activity against all studied aliphatic and aromatic amino acids.

The combined (in silico and in vitro) approaches revealed the precise mechanism of E. adhaerens S-5 recombinant D-carbamoylase action, its strict specificity for aromatic and aliphatic N-carbamoyl-D-amino acids, and the possibility of its production in E. coli cells for biotechnological applications.
