Structured illumination microscopy for high SNR 3D imaging from millimeter-thick tissues to cellular dynamics
Authors: Mengrui Wang, Manming Shu, Jiajing Yan, Chang Liu, Xiangda Fu, Jingxiang Zhang, Yuchen Lin, Hu Zhao, Yuwei Huang, Dingbang Ma, Yifan Ge, Huiwen Hao, Tianyu Zhao, Yansheng Liang, Shaowei Wang, Ming Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) optical imaging of intact tissues with high spatiotemporal resolution is a highly desirable goal, as it offers visualization of biological structures and processes in their physiological context. Optical sectioning structured illumination microscopy (OS-SIM) stands out among various 3D imaging techniques for its comprehensive superiority, as it offers high spatial resolution, rapid imaging speed, and low phototoxicity. However, under the challenging conditions of dominant background and increased fluorescence scattering in thick tissues, which leads to a decrease in modulation contrast, OS-SIM faces the problem of low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and limited imaging depth. We have developed a high SNR OS-SIM strategy for 3D imaging of biological samples ranging from millimeter-thick tissues to cellular dynamics. By enhancing the SNR by ∼10 dB with rapid processing, our method enables imaging of tissue-cleared sample with thickness >2 mm and low light dose for live-cell recording, while also doubling the imaging penetration depth. With our OS-SIM system, we achieve high-quality 3D images of various biological samples, including mouse brains, Drosophila clock neurons, organoids, and live cells. We anticipate that this approach will render OS-SIM a powerful technique for the research of cellular organelles or thick tissues in 3D morphology.Keywords: three-dimensional imaging, optical sectioning, structured illumination microscopy

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) fluorescence imaging has become an indispensable tool in biomedical research, offering the ability to visualize intact biological structures and processes in their physiologic context.1,2,3 The quest for high spatiotemporal resolution in observing intricate tissue structures and dynamics of cellular processes has led to the development of several advanced imaging techniques.1,4

Among the prevalent 3D fluorescence imaging techniques, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) is characterized by its ability to optically section specimens using a pinhole to eliminate out-of-focus light and thus obtain background-free images.5,6 However, its use is limited by photobleaching concerns and relatively slow imaging speeds, which are less than ideal for live-cell imaging. Spinning disk confocal microscopy enhances imaging speed by using multiple pinholes to scan the sample area simultaneously, but it results in out-of-focus light entering adjacent pinholes, which compromises axial resolution.7,8 Light sheet microscopy, however, involves illuminating the specimen with a thin sheet of light, allowing for rapid image acquisition with minimal photodamage, yet it has restrictions on the sample size and can suffer from uneven illumination due to scattering.9,10,11 Light field microscopy can image the entire volume simultaneously in a single camera frame, so it has extremely high 3D imaging speed, but its inherent trade-off between spatial and angular resolution leads to a sacrifice in spatial resolution.12,13,14

Taking advantage of the wide-field imaging approach, optical sectioning structured illumination microscopy (OS-SIM) demonstrates both high spatial resolution and fast imaging speed.15,16,17,18,19 OS-SIM uses sinusoidal fringe patterns projected through the objective onto the focal plane.20 Root mean square (RMS) or Hilbert transform (HT) decoding algorithms are used to extract the in-focus information while eliminating the background to obtain optical sections.21,22 Structured illumination can also be replaced with speckle patterns, as in HiLo microscopy, where high-frequency information from wide-field images and low-frequency information from structured illumination images are combined to obtain optical sections.23,24,25 The employment of the digital micromirror device (DMD) has led to a substantial enhancement in the imaging speed of OS-SIM, making OS-SIM applicable to a wide range of applications, including natural-color 3D imaging and morphologic measurement.26,27,28 However, most of the previous studies have been performed on reflective surfaces where signal and modulation contrast are high.

The application of OS-SIM to bioimaging is encumbered by significant challenges, including the presence of a weak fluorescence signal and substantial noise. For instance, OS-SIM faces the problem of low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for 3D fluorescence imaging of thick specimens and is considered suitable only for specimens thinner than 20 μm.7,29 Even in shallow regions, the dominant background could reduce the modulation contrast, resulting in significant noise contamination in the decoded optical sections.30 Dai et al. achieved a penetration depth of 350 μm using near-infrared II (NIR-II) SIM for tumor imaging, but this was mainly due to the effects of the NIR excitation light.31 In this work, we develop a high SNR OS-SIM strategy for 3D imaging of biological samples ranging from millimeter-thick tissues to cellular dynamics. The proposed OS-SIM strategy adopted a new OS recombination (HT-SHiLo) algorithm for noise suppression, which can improve the SNR of optical sectioning images by ∼10 dB and reduce the processing time to 5.4 ms per layer at 2,048 × 2,048 pixels. We applied the OS-SIM system to 3D fluorescence imaging of thick tissues including mouse brains, Drosophila clock neurons, and organoids. The results exhibit satisfactory noise suppression, and the imaging penetration depth of OS-SIM is also doubled, up to 2.4 mm for a tissue-cleared sample. We further demonstrate its application in live-cell imaging, achieving long-term 3D recording of mitochondrial and migrasome dynamics, thanks to the low phototoxicity and fast imaging speed of OS-SIM. This method holds promise for enhancing the application of OS-SIM in studies of thick biological tissues and live-cell dynamics.

Materials and methods

OS-SIM system

The OS-SIM system for 3D fluorescence imaging is illustrated in Figure S10A. The illumination light is emitted by light-emitting diodes (X-Cite Turbo, Excelitas) with switchable wavelengths of 385, 488, 561, and 635 nm, and passes through the excitation filter. Entering the total internal reflection (TIR) prism, the light is reflected onto the DMD (V7000, ViALUX GmbH) to be modulated into a binary grating. The modulated light field then passes through the achromatic tube lens (f = 300 mm) and the dichroic mirror and is projected onto the focal plane of the objective lens (4×/0.2 numerical aperture [NA], TL4X-SAP, Thorlabs; 10×/0.45 NA, S Fluor, Nikon; 20×/0.75 NA, Plan Fluor, Nikon; 40×/1.3 NA, Plan Fluor, Nikon; 60×/1.49 NA, Apo TIRF, Nikon; 100×/1.49 NA, Apo TIRF, Nikon). The illumination pattern on sample becomes sinusoidal distribution naturally due to the limited bandwidth of the objective lens. The sample is mounted on an XYZ motorized translation stage (M-112.1DG1, 25-mm travel range, Physik Instrumente GmbH) enabling volume data acquisition through axial movement of the stage. Meanwhile, moving in landscape orientation with image stitching applied enables the extension of the FOV. For fast imaging of dynamic process, we use the piezo stage (P-726 PIFOC, 100-μm travel range, Physik Instrumente GmbH) to axially scan the objective. A scientific (s)CMOS camera (ORCA-Flash4.0 V3, 100 fps at 2,048 × 2,048 pixels, 16 bits, Hamamatsu, Japan) is employed to capture the two-dimensional image with an emission filter blocking the excitation light. Custom software programmed in C++ was used to control DMD pattern generation, stage movement, and image recording. Through systematic optimization and compact optical design, we achieved ∼10× improvement in light throughput compared with previous systems,21 reaching a maximum of 20 mW non-coherent illumination power under a 20×/0.75-NA objective. This enhancement enables our system to meet the imaging requirements for the vast majority of biological specimens. A 100-nm fluorescent bead (505 nm excitation, 515 nm emission) is used to measure the point spread function (PSF) of the OS-SIM system (Figure S10C). OS-SIM removes the background in wide-field images, and the full width at half-maximum of the bead’s PSF is measured at 280 nm in x and 510 nm in z under a 60×/1.49-NA oil immersion objective.

PEGASOS tissue clearing method for preparing mouse brain samples

All samples were collected and processed using the PEGASOS tissue clearing kit (Leads Bio-Technology [http://www.leads-tech.com], catalog no. PSK100N). Mice were transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were then dissected and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. They were then processed following the kit protocol. Finally, samples were immersed in benzyl benzoate-polyethylene glycol medium (solution 7 in the kit) for at least 1 day for clearing. Samples were immersed in the clearing medium for imaging.

Drosophila brain preparation

The corresponding secondary antibodies were added and incubated overnight. Brains were mounted in KIT04 Mounting Medium Sunbloss (HUAXIA Imaging).

Cell culture and staining of cells

The Biologics Standards-Cercopithecus-1 (BSC-1) cell line was purchased from Pricella Life Technology. The COS-7 cell line was purchased from Applied Biological Materials. Both cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Invitrogen, catalog no. 11965-118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, catalog no. 16010-159). To prevent bacterial contamination, 100 μg/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, catalog no. 15140122) was added in the DMEM. Cells were grown under standard cell culture conditions (5% CO2, humidified atmosphere at 37°C).

For the immunofluorescence staining of BSC-1 cells, cells were seeded in a 35-mm cell dish with a no. 1.5 glass coverslip (SunBloss, catalog no. STGBD-035-1). To increase cell adhesion, we pre-treated the glass-bottom dishes with fibronectin (SunBloss, catalog no. HXAR-01) for 1 h at 37°C. When the cells grew to a confluence of around 60%, they were fixed with 37°C pre-warmed fixation buffer for 10 min containing 4% PFA and 0.1% glutaraldehyde (SunBloss, catalog no. HXKx01) in PBS. Then, the cells were washed three times with PBS. To quench the background signals, we incubated the cells with 2 mL 0.1% NaBH4 solution (SunBloss, catalog no. HXIK023) in PBS for 7 min and optionally shook the dish on a shaker (<1 Hz). The cells were washed 3 times with 2 mL PBS and then incubated for 30 min in PBS containing 5% BSA and 0.5% Triton X-100 (SunBloss, catalog no. HXKx02) at 37°C. All antibodies were diluted in the 5% BSA + 0.5% Triton X-100 solution described above. Next, we incubated the cells for 40 min with the appropriate dilution of primary antibody: Tom20 (abclonal, catalog no. A19403) at 25°C. After primary antibody incubation, the cells were washed 3 times for 5 min with 2 mL PBS. Secondary antibody and dye were incubated for 60 min with the appropriate dilutions of the following: 1:100 goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (heavy and light chain) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher, catalog no. A-21428), and 50 μmol/mL Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher, catalog no. 62249) at 25°C. After being washed 3 times with PBS, the cells were fixed with post-fixation buffer (SunBloss, catalog no. HXIE-2) for 10 min.

For the mitochondria labeling of the COS-7 cell line, cells were seeded in a 35-mm cell dish with a glass bottom and incubated overnight. When the cells grew to a confluence of around 60%, cells were washed with PBS three times, and fresh culture medium containing PK Mito Orange (Nanjing Genvivo Biotech) at a concentration of around 200 nM was added into the cell dish. After additional incubation for 20 min, the COS-7 cells were washed with PBS three times and fresh culture medium was added. The PK Mito Orange-stained cells were placed on the microscope stage for imaging.

Sample preparation for migrasome observation

The imaging chamber was coated with fibronectin at a concentration of 10 μg/mL, and the coating process took place for a minimum of 30 min at 37°C but did not exceed 2 h. Following the coating, Tspan4-GFP stable overexpressed normal rat kidney (NRK) cells were seeded onto the pre-coated glass-bottom imaging chamber. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin solution. The culture was maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 for ∼15 h. Subsequently, the sample was ready for further imaging analysis.

Results

HT-SHiLo algorithm for high SNR optical sectioning

Strict optical sectioning through OS-SIM requires three phase-shift raw images for RMS operation. To achieve higher imaging efficiency, we adopted HT decoding, which only requires two phase-shift images, I1 and I2, at the cost of certain high-frequency loss (Note S1.1). However, we utilize only its low-frequency component, while the high-frequency part is taken from the wide-field image WF. They are then combined in proportion according to a scaling factor to obtain the final optical sectioning image (Figure 1A; Note S1.2. This process is performed entirely in the spatial domain; therefore, we call it the HT-SHiLo algorithm. Simulation results show that the HT-SHiLo algorithm improves the PSNR by about 14 dB compared with HT decoding and processes 7.3 times faster than the traditional HiLo algorithm at 2,048 × 2,048 pixels on a graphics processing unit (Note S1.3). Based on the physical principles of light scattering in biological tissues, we demonstrate that HT-SHiLo achieves enhanced imaging depth compared with direct decoded optical sectioning (Note S1.4).

The major issue with traditional RMS or HT decoding is the weak modulation-induced low SNR encountered in imaging thick tissue. To test the HT-SHiLo algorithm for thick tissues imaging, we first used OS-SIM to image the dense nerves and neurons in the mouse brain. A 2-mm-thick coronal block was sectioned from an adult Thy1-EGFP-M mouse brain, processed following the PEGASOS tissue clearing method,2,32 and excited at 488 nm. With an objective lens of 10×/0.45 NA, we obtained an imaging volume of 942 × 942 × 572 μm3. As imaging depth increased, the noises in the HT-decoded 3D image became stronger, and the structures of nerves became gradually unclear and invisible from a depth of about 300 μm (Figure 1B; Video S1). In contrast, the 3D image processed by the HT-SHiLo algorithm maintained high SNR and clear structure up to a depth of 572 μm. We made a detailed comparison between the WF images and the optical sections processed by HT, HiLo, and HT-SHiLo algorithms, respectively, under different imaging depths (Figure 1D). HT decoding could achieve optical sections only in shallow regions, which were otherwise submerged in noise when imaging depth exceeded 180 μm. The HiLo algorithm improved the SNR and recovered the nerve structure to a certain extent, but it introduced increasing artifacts at deeper positions. In contrast, HT-SHiLo-processed images exhibited high contrast and SNR at all depths. Through quantitative analysis of the SNR of optical sections (Figure 1C), we found that the SNR of HT-decoded images decreased exponentially with imaging depth, reducing to 1/e of the peak value at a depth of about 200 μm. The HT-SHiLo algorithm highly enhanced the SNR by an average of 7.09 dB, while the SNR of HiLo-processed images fell between the two. Detailed quantitative analysis further verifies that HT-SHiLo achieves optimal performance across various imaging depths (Note S2).

Video S1. HT-SHiLo algorithm enhances the SNR and imaging depth of OS-SIM

In the field of live cell imaging, temporal resolution is critical. It is important to note that very short exposure times can result in a reduction of the SNR. Alternatively, increasing excitation power can lead to photobleaching and potential cellular photo damage. To evaluate the performance of the HT-SHiLo algorithm in noise suppression with moderated excitation power, we applied OS-SIM to imaging of the cell nucleus and mitochondria in BSC-1 cells. Cell nuclei stained with Hoechst 33342 and mitochondria stained with Alexa Fluor 555 were excited at 385 and 561 nm, respectively. The excitation power density used was lower than 1 W/cm2 at the focal plane and the same in subsequent live-cell experiments. An oil immersion objective lens of 100×/1.49 NA was employed with a field of view (FOV) size of 94 × 94 μm2, and a scan range of 5 μm along the z axis was adopted. The maximum intensity projection (MIP) image of the BSC-1 cell revealed distinct contrast and background subtraction, with the outer membrane of the mitochondria clearly visible (Figure S1A; Video S1). Direct application of background removal algorithms, such as the Dark algorithm,33 to WF images yields poor axial PSF (Figure S1B). While RMS or HT decoding provides optical sectioning capability, the results are severely contaminated by noise. The HT-SHiLo algorithm maintains the same optical sectioning performance as HT decoding while effectively eliminating noise, thereby clearly delineating the 3D morphology of mitochondria. The stacked distribution of thick mitochondria demonstrates the 3D optical sectioning capability of HT-SHiLo (Figure S1C), while the details of thin mitochondria further showcase its superior spatial resolution (Figure S1D). Among all algorithms compared, HT-SHiLo demonstrates the best SNR and SBR, achieving an ∼11-dB improvement in SNR and 8 dB enhancement in SBR over RMS and HT decoding methods (Figures S1E and S1F).

To validate the imaging fidelity of our HT-SHiLo-based OS-SIM system, we conducted comparative experiments with CLSM (Leica SP8 DIVE). Standard bovine pulmonary artery endothelial (BPAE) cell samples (FluoCells, catalog no. F36924) were imaged, with mitochondria, F-actin, and nuclei labeled by MitoTracker Red CMXRos (561 nm excitation), Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (488 nm excitation), and DAPI (385 nm excitation), respectively. The confocal system employed a 63×/1.4-NA oil immersion objective, while OS-SIM used a 60×/1.49-NA oil immersion objective. Comparative results demonstrate that OS-SIM achieves imaging quality comparable to that of confocal microscopy, without computationally introduced biases or artifacts (Figures S2A and S2B). The matching intensity profiles of OS-SIM to confocal microscopy confirm its resolution parity and imaging fidelity (Figure S2C). However, the confocal microscopy required significantly longer imaging time than OS-SIM—∼55-fold greater—demonstrating the superior imaging efficiency of OS-SIM (Figure S2D). The preservation of intensity linearity further validates the fidelity of HT-SHiLo (Figure S7; Note S3).

Deep insight into neurons and vascular endothelial cells in mouse brain

The neurons in an animal’s brain dictate a myriad of physiological processes and behaviors. Understanding how these neurons interact with one another and form a complex network in the intact brain is a fundamental question in neuroscience. To further expand the application of OS-SIM in neuroscience research, we acquired a large block of mouse brain from Thy1-EGFP-M sample at the hippocampus region, representing the largest coronal dimension of the mouse brain. The sample was cleared using the PEGASOS tissue clearing method. We first scanned at 5 × 3 FOVs with a 4×/0.2-NA objective lens to acquire 3D images of the whole-brain slice, which is 9.4 × 6.2 × 1.3 mm3 (Figure 2; Video S2). The Thy1-EGFP-M mouse model specifically labels excitatory neurons, including both their somas and long axonal projections. Typically, neuron somas are around 10 μm, while axons measure ∼0.5 μm. In the low-magnification image, all neuron somas are clearly visible. A total of 14,698 neurons were detected (Figure 2B), with the peak spatial distribution density reaching ∼5,500/mm3 (Figure 2C). To capture more detailed features, a 20×/0.75-NA objective lens was used to obtain higher-resolution images (Figures 2D–2F and S8). All somas and axons in the imaging blocks were clearly identified with comparable resolution, and depth-coded colors help distinguish the height distribution of each axon. Dendritic spines are small protrusions on the dendrites of neurons that play a crucial role in synaptic plasticity and the formation of neural networks. Notably, dendritic spines of micrometer size can be observed densely distributed throughout the space, confirming the high resolution of our system. Detailed images demonstrate that HT-SHiLo achieves higher SNR compared with HT, along with clearer visualization of deeper neural structures (Figures 2G–2I).

We further imaged the vasculature within a mouse brain. We acquired a large block of mouse brain from a Tie2-Cre;Ai14;H2B-GFPflox mouse. In this model, all vasculature nuclei were specifically labeled with GFP fluorescence, while their cytoplasm was labeled with tdTomato fluorescence, respectively excited at 488 and 561 nm. The sample was cleared with the PEGASOS method. The whole-brain slice was scanned at 5 × 3 FOVs with a 4×/0.2-NA objective lens, resulting in a 3D image size of 8.6 × 5.6 × 2.4 mm3 (Figure 3A; Video S3). The low-magnification image revealed the overall organization of brain vasculature throughout the entire coronal section (Figure 3B). Based on nuclei labeling, the cell density map was quantified, with the maximum density reaching 2 × 105 cells/mm3 (Figure 3C). Detailed images were captured using an objective lens of 10×/0.45 NA (Figures 3D–3F). The achievable imaging depth is shallower in regions with higher cell density (Figure 3D) and deeper in areas with lower cell density (Figures 3E and 3F). The imaging depth of HT is consistently lower than that of HT-SHiLo. In all indicated regions, nuclei with a dimension of ∼5 μm and cytoplasm with a dimension of ∼10 μm were both clearly visualized. The detailed 3D structure of the vasculature could be detected (Figure 3G). Achieving an imaging depth over 600 μm (10×/0.45 NA objective lens) in such a densely packed sample demonstrates the deep 3D imaging capability of our system.

3D imaging of the clock neurons in intact Drosophila brains

Drosophila are invaluable for studying the underpinnings of behavior and neurological disorders due to their genetic simplicity and conservation of key neural processes with humans. We applied OS-SIM to the study of Drosophila clock neurons, a relatively small network regulating the locomotor activity and sleep of these animals. A pan-clock neuron driver, Clk21-Gal4, was crossed with UAS-EGFP to label the clock neurons with excitation at 488 nm. The brains were dissected and fixed before undergoing the standard immunostaining. NC82 staining was used to label synaptic neuropil regions in the brain, with excitation at 635 nm. With a 40×/1.3-NA oil immersion objective lens, the brain was scanned at 3 × 2 FOVs, resulting in an FOV size of 584 × 317 μm2 and a depth of 123 μm. OS-SIM offered a WF view of the clock network in the intact brain and, at the same time, its descent resolution revealed the precise projection patterns of clock neurons (Figure 4A; Video S4). More important, there was much less signal attenuation between the lateral and dorsal clock neurons. The height map enabled a better understanding of the connections within the clock neuron network, as well as its interactions with other neuron circuits (Figures 4B–4D). Combined with MultiColor FlpOut and circuit trancing techniques such as trans-Tango, we envision OS-SIM becoming a powerful toolkit to study the neuronal network in fly brains.

Video S4. 3D visualization of clock neurons and synaptic neuropils in a Drosophila brain

To evaluate the performance and improvements of HT-SHiLo-based OS-SIM against established benchmarks, we conducted further comparisons using Drosophila brain samples with confocal microscopy. The complete 3D image of clock neurons in the Drosophila brain was acquired using HT-SHiLo-based OS-SIM (Figure S9A). The lateral neural network region was selected for comparison with confocal microscopy, using the same 63×/1.4 NA oil immersion objective. MIP and cross-sectional views demonstrated that HT-SHiLo and confocal microscopy achieved comparable lateral and axial resolution (Figure S9B). Comparative imaging across different depth ranges revealed strong background and noise in HT-decoded images, with imaging depth limited to within 30 μm (Figure S9C). In contrast, HT-SHiLo achieved background suppression equivalent to confocal microscopy, maintaining clarity up to depths of 60–75 μm. This demonstrates that HT-SHiLo-based OS-SIM enables thick tissue 3D imaging with high resolution, contrast, and fidelity comparable to that of confocal microscopy. Notably, HT-SHiLo-based OS-SIM achieved ∼10-fold faster imaging speed, requiring only 21 min 30 s for complete Drosophila brain imaging, whereas confocal microscopy would require an estimated 3 h 24 min (Figure S9D).

Tomographic imaging of human colonic organoids

Organoids are tissue analogs with a certain spatial structure formed by 3D culture of tissue samples or pluripotent stem cells in vitro, which can simulate real organs in structures as well as functions. Colonic organoids can reproduce the 3D features of colon tissue, especially the specific crypt-villi structure of colon epitheliums. The PFA-fixed and stained human colonic organoid sample was bestowed by the Genome Institute, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The sample was stained with Ki67 to indicate cell growth and with E-cadherin to indicate cell adherens junction, respectively excited at 385 and 488 nm. The organoid was scanned at 3 × 3 FOVs with a 60×/1.49-NA oil immersion objective lens, which is 350 × 364 × 52.8 μm3. The adherens junction within cells is scanned clearly, which is expressed only on the periphery of colonic organoid (Figure 5; Video S5). The scanned result indicates the well-differentiated zones, where the colonic epithelium forms (Figure 5A, red arrows), and the less-differentiated zones (Figure 5A, green arrows), as the E-cadherin is one of the markers to reflect enterocytes. The bulging area of Figure 5B shows the growing villi (purple arrow) and crypt (cyan arrow). The colonic cavity can also be scanned with high spatial resolution—that is, the cavity morphology changes with different depths (Figure 5C, white arrows), indicating the variational structure within the organoid.

High SNR imaging of mitochondria in live cells

Mitochondrial dynamics, encompassing fusion, fission, and transportation, constitute essential regulatory processes for organelle quality control.34 The precise regulation of these dynamic processes is fundamental to cellular homeostasis and has profound implications for various physiologic and pathologic states.35,36 While mitochondrial dynamics inherently occur in 3D spaces, the rapid nature of fusion and fission events limits the use of 3D methods for studying the kinetics of mitochondrial dynamics in living cells. The superior spatiotemporal resolution and 3D imaging capabilities of OS-SIM provide an unprecedented opportunity to address the long-standing challenge of quantifying mitochondrial dynamics in three dimensions. We exploited this advanced imaging technique to capture and analyze comprehensive mitochondrial dynamics in living COS-7 cells. Stained with PK Mito Orange and excited at 561 nm, the mitochondria were imaged using a 100×/1.49-NA oil immersion objective lens and a z scan range of 3.2 μm to cover their stacked thickness (Figure 6A; Video S6). The dynamics of mitochondria were recorded for 5 min with 10-s intervals.

Taking advantage of the rapid 3D imaging capabilities of OS-SIM, we obtained unprecedented spatiotemporal resolution of mitochondrial dynamics across multiple Z planes. Our observations revealed detailed mitochondrial behaviors, including elongation and transport along microtubules, followed by subsequent network dissociation (Figure 6B). Notably, post-fission mitochondria exhibited increased structural flexibility and enhanced mobility within the cytoplasm. The superior z axis resolution enabled direct visualization of membrane tubule formation preceding mitochondrial fusion events, occurring both within the same focal plane and across different Z levels (Figures 6C and 6D). These observations provide direct evidence supporting previously proposed fusion mechanisms derived from structural biology studies.37

Long-term intravital imaging of migrasome dynamics

Migrasomes are cell organelles produced during cell migration. At the trailing edge of a migrating cell, retraction fibers form, and migrasomes develop at their cross-points or tips into vesicular structures measuring 0.5–3 μm in diameter.38,39 Migrasomes act as carriers for a diverse range of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, mediating lateral transfer of materials and information and establishing micro-environments that play crucial roles in embryonic development, angiogenesis, blood coagulation, and immune responses.40,41,42 Tetraspanin4, a four-transmembrane protein, is a recognized marker of migrasomes and plays a critical role in their formation.43

To further elucidate the mechanism and function of migrasomes, we employed the OS-SIM to visualize them in high-quality, 3D live-cell images, capturing detailed information. Using a 100×/1.49-NA oil immersion objective lens and excitation at 488 nm, we acquired images with a z scan range of 3.8 μm, recording the dynamic processes of migrating cells over 80 min with 1-min intervals (Figure 7A; Video S7). The time-lapse imaging showed that the cell left behind retraction fibers at its tail during migration (Figure 7B). The migrasome grew from 0.5 to 1.2 μm in x-y diameter and from 0.8 to 2 μm in height during 80 min, which also indicated that the migrasome was ellipsoidal, with an approximate aspect ratio of 1:1.6 (Figure 7C). We also captured the fragmentation of retraction fibers, which tended to break at the connection points with migrasomes (Figure 7D). However, migrasomes remained attached at the local site and retained their complete structure even after detaching from the retraction fibers. These observations suggest that migrasome membranes may seal before retraction fiber breakage, potentially endowing them with properties characteristic of extracellular vesicles at later stages.

Discussion

For 3D imaging of biological tissues and structures, OS-SIM offers high spatial and temporal resolution. However, the traditional RMS decoding method leads to two major challenges. First, three raw images are required for the reconstruction of one optical section, which not only implies a relatively low imaging efficiency but also brings more phototoxicity and photobleaching to the sample. Second, the SNR of the optical section relies on the modulation depth of fringe that faces severe reduction when imaging thick tissues. The HT decoding method reduces the required raw images to two, but the high-frequency part of the HT decoded optical section undergoes a deviation, and it faces the same problem of low SNR. Applying the HiLo processing can effectively improve the SNR of the optical section to the same level of WF image, but the traditional HiLo algorithm involves the time-consuming Fourier transform. Therefore, we proposed the HT-SHiLo algorithm, which performs the HiLo operation entirely in space domain and thus with a 7.3-fold increase in processing speed. Meanwhile, the inaccurate high-frequency part of the HT decoded optical section can be revised. In addition, compared with traditional HiLo, HT-SHiLo demonstrates superior SNR and more accurate low-frequency information representation. It should be noted that this improvement does not primarily rely on post-processing, which mainly assists in optimizing image contrast and visual perception. The choice of employing a simple low-pass filter for post-processing, rather than advanced methods such as Dark sectioning33 or Hessian denoising,44 was made to ensure faster processing speed. In general, the HT-SHiLo algorithm provides an elegant solution for fast and high SNR optical sectioning via SIM.

Using our OS-SIM system, we successfully acquired 3D images of mouse brains, Drosophila clock neurons, organoids, mitochondria, and migrasomes, verifying the broad biological sample compatibility of OS-SIM. The results demonstrate high SNR image production using the HT-SHiLo algorithm compared with direct HT decoding, with an improvement of ∼10 dB in SNR. Meanwhile, the imaging depth can be doubled thanks to the information fidelity of the HT-SHiLo algorithm under noise submergence, which is of significant importance for in vivo imaging such as brain imaging. This approach is also applicable to NIR excitation, which can further increase the penetration depth for in vivo imaging due to the low scattering characteristics of NIR light.31 Incorporating adaptive optics into SIM can also extend the imaging depth and correct aberrations,17 but it leads to sophisticated imaging systems and high cost. Notably, although the HT-SHiLo method enables OS-SIM to achieve deeper imaging depths, it is still limited to tissue-cleared thick tissues or the superficial layers (∼100 μm) of opaque tissues. For deep imaging within opaque tissues, multiphoton microscopy would perform better.

The temporal resolution of our system is primarily limited by the scanning speed of the z axis stage and the brightness of the fluorescent probes. With an exposure time of 10 ms and a stage translation time of 10 ms, the maximum volumetric imaging frame rate is ∼3.3 Hz when scanning 10 layers (for a cell sample with a thickness of 4 μm and a z step of 0.4 μm). This limitation can be addressed by replacing the translation stage with a faster focal plane changing solution, such as TAGLENS, which can achieve sub-millisecond zooming. The use of brighter fluorescent probes can also contribute to a reduction in exposure time. At the shortest exposure time and the fastest axial scanning speed using TAGLENS, the optical sectioning speed can reach nearly half of the camera frame rate (400 fps at 512 × 512 pixels). Consequently, the scanning of a cell sample could attain a volume frame rate of nearly 20 Hz, thereby enabling in vivo 3D imaging of rapid biological processes. Combined with the rapid processing capability of HT-SHiLo algorithm, real-time 3D reconstruction of dynamic processes becomes possible, which empowers biologists to conduct in situ observation of biological phenomena.

The spatial resolution of the current system has already reached the optical diffraction limit. Further improvement in resolution can be achieved by applying image reprocessing such as sparse deconvolution,45 multi-resolution analysis deconvolution,46 deblurring by pixel reassignment,47 zero-shot deconvolution networks,48 and so on. Although super-resolution SIM can physically provide higher resolution,49,50,51,52 it requires many more raw images for reconstruction of one layer, which is not suitable for fast dynamic 3D imaging and can record fewer time points compared with OS-SIM. Expansion microscopy achieves high-resolution imaging through physical expansion of specimens, particularly for brain tissue samples.53 Its integration with OS-SIM, which possesses rapid and deep imaging capabilities, will enable even more powerful applications. Therefore, OS-SIM is a quite promising technique for research of the evolution and functional processes of cellular organelles or tissues in 3D morphology.
