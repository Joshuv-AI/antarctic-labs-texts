Medication Instruction and Mirogabalin Treatment Satisfaction in Patients with Neuropathic Pain due to Orthopedic Diseases: Results of MiroSearch, a Japanese Exploratory Interventional Study
Authors: Hiroaki Nakashima, Kiyofumi Yamada, Yoshiaki Kato, Wataru Hashimoto, Taiki Toyama, Shiro Imagama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe postulateded that providing medication instruction to patients with various orthopedic diseases and scheduled to receive mirogabalin would improve their satisfaction with treatment. The MiroSearch study investigated the impact of medication instruction on treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin.MethodsThis was a multicenter, open-label, exploratory, interventional study, conducted in Japan. The study included outpatients with mild to more severe neuropathic pain (NeP) (numerical rating scale [NRS] score 0–10) due to various orthopedic diseases. Patients received medication instruction for mirogabalin from a physician or pharmacist at baseline and week 4. The primary endpoint was patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin.ResultsOf the 201 patients who provided informed consent, 194 were included, and 166 completed the study. Patient treatment satisfaction (at least satisfied) at week 8 was 52.8% (86/163) (95% confidence interval [CI] 44.8–60.6%). Patient understanding at week 8 was associated with patient treatment satisfaction at week 8 (Cochran-Armitage test, p = 0.021 for trend). In cases in which physicians were satisfied or more with treatment at week 8, agreement rate between patient and physician treatment satisfaction at week 8 was 49.1% (80/163), and the disagreement rate was 30.1% (49/163). Patient satisfaction with communication with the physician was associated with patient treatment satisfaction. The NRS score for pain decreased from baseline to week 8 (change from baseline, − 2.0 [95% CI − 2.4, − 1.5], p < 0.001). Treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 53.8% (105/195) of patients; most were mild or moderate in severity.ConclusionOver half of patients with NeP due to orthopedic diseases were satisfied with mirogabalin treatment. Medication instruction can improve patient understanding of their disease and medications and enhance their treatment satisfaction. Furthermore, communication between patients and physicians/pharmacists may contribute to improving patient treatment satisfaction.Trial Registration NumberJapan Registry of Clinical Trials identifier, jRCTs041230059.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03516-4.Keywords: α2δ ligand, Gabapentinoid, Medication instruction, Mirogabalin, Neuropathic pain, Treatment satisfaction, Treatment understanding

Introduction

Pain is mainly classified as nociceptive pain, neuropathic pain (NeP), or nociplastic pain [1, 2]. The International Association for the Study of Pain (ISAP) defines NeP as a pain that requires evidence of a demonstrable lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system [3]. In clinical practice, NeP is often difficult to control and requires prolonged treatment, and unless adequately treated, chronic pain, including NeP, can negatively impact patients’ activities of daily living (ADL) and quality of life (QoL) [4, 5]. The therapeutic goal for patients with pain is to improve ADL and QoL, thereby preventing disruptions in daily functioning [6], and this requires appropriate and continuous treatment.

Patients’ satisfaction and understanding regarding their treatment are important for continuation of appropriate treatment. Patient satisfaction is positively correlated with patients’ understanding of their pain medication, including its effectiveness and safety [7]. Furthermore, improving patient satisfaction with pain management requires educating them about the implications of pain treatment, and the types of available medications [8], through appropriate physician-patient communication.

Regarding NeP, treatment satisfaction was generally low, despite variations in types of disease, pain medications, and assessment scale for satisfaction. It was reported to be 29.4% in patients with chronic pain on NeP medications [9], 27.9% in those with diabetic peripheral NeP [10], and 29% in those with chronic musculoskeletal pain [11]. These findings highlight that many patients, especially those with NeP, are not satisfied with analgesic treatment [9]. Furthermore, there is currently a lack of reports on treatment satisfaction specifically in patients with NeP due to orthopedic disease in Japan.

Mirogabalin besylate (mirogabalin), a selective α2δ ligand (gabapentinoid), has been approved for the treatment of NeP based on the results of several phase 3 studies [12–15]. In randomized, controlled, clinical studies, mirogabalin improved NeP and QoL in patients with traumatic spinal cord injury, lumbar spinal stenosis, cervical spondylotic radiculopathy, and lumbar disc herniation [14, 16–18]. Given these reports, we hypothesized that mirogabalin may improve treatment satisfaction in patients with NeP due to orthopedic disease. However, no interventional clinical studies have directly examined patient treatment satisfaction and its relationship with the understanding of diseases and medications in patients with various orthopedic diseases who received mirogabalin. Furthermore, the effects of medication instruction on patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin and understanding of diseases and medication have not been well investigated.

The purpose of the present study was to investigate treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin and explore associated factors in patients with NeP due to orthopedic disease when their understanding of the disease and drug has been improved through medication instruction.

Methods

Study Design

The MiroSearch study was a multicenter, open-label, exploratory, interventional study conducted in Japan between July 2023 and August 2024 (Fig. S1). A complete list of participating investigators and institutions is shown in Table S1.

The study was approved by Nagoya University Clinical Research Review Board (CRB no. CRB4180004). It was conducted in accordance with the Clinical Trials Act in Japan, the Act on the Protection of Personal Information, and the ethical principles and relevant notifications stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The MiroSearch study was registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials under the identifier jRCTs041230059. All patients provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Patients

Full inclusion criteria were outpatients aged ≥ 18 years who had a diagnosis of NeP due to orthopedic disease; were to receive treatment with NeP medications for the first time; were scheduled to continue for ≥ 8 weeks; and were able to understand the study procedures, appropriately respond to questions in Japanese via electronic patient-reported outcomes with no assistance, and provide voluntary consent to participate through either electronic or written informed consent. NeP was diagnosed in accordance with the algorithm developed by the Neuropathic Pain Special Interest Group (NeuPSIG) of the IASP [19], based on either of the following criteria: (1) the presence of objective sensory abnormalities such as hypoesthesia, hyperesthesia, or allodynia; (2) the identification of a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory system that caused NeP, confirmed through diagnostic tests. For this study, patients were defined as having NeP if they met at least one of these criteria.

Full exclusion criteria were patients deemed to require hospitalization during the study period; scheduled to undergo surgery during the study period; with severe pain caused by other diseases than orthopedic disease; with creatinine clearance (CrCL) < 30 ml/min; with serious hepatic, renal, or cardiac disease; who were pregnant, might be pregnant, or breastfeeding at the time of informed consent; planning to become pregnant within 6 months of providing informed consent; scheduled to participate in another clinical study; or deemed inappropriate for study participation, as judged by physicians.

Intervention

Eligible patients received medication instruction (printed materials) from a physician or pharmacist in person before study drug administration (i.e., at baseline) and at week 4 of treatment. The physician or pharmacist provided the medication instruction according to the medication instruction manual, using the medication instruction form (Supplemental Methods), recorded the time needed for medication instruction, and made efforts to minimize variation in both contents and times of medication instruction across institutions.

In daily medical practice, mirogabalin was administered at dosages based on each patient’s baseline renal function, according to the Japanese package insert [15]: for patients with CrCL ≥ 60 ml/min, mirogabalin was administered at 5 mg twice daily (BID) for at least 1 week initially, followed by titration in 5-mg BID increments at intervals of at least 1 week, up to a maximum of 15 mg BID. The dose could be adjusted according to the patient’s age and symptoms, in the range of 10–15 mg BID. For patients with CrCL 30 to < 60 ml/min, the dose of mirogabalin was half that for those with CrCL ≥ 60 ml/min.

During study participation, concomitant use of gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine, opioids, probenecid, cimetidine, lorazepam, or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors was prohibited. Nerve blocks, intradiscal injection (condoliase), and any surgical analgesic treatments that might affect the efficacy assessment were also prohibited during study participation.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin at week 8. Secondary endpoints included factors associated with patient and physician treatment satisfaction at week 8; agreement regarding treatment satisfaction between patients and physicians at week 8; changes from baseline at week 8 in patient understanding of diseases and medications; relationships between patient treatment satisfaction and patient understanding at week 8. Furthermore, the efficacy of mirogabalin was evaluated based on change from baseline in numerical rating scale (NRS) score for pain [20] and QoL based on EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level (EQ-5D-5L) [21].

Safety endpoints included incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and treatment-emergent adverse drug reactions (TEADRs).

Scoring of Endpoints

Scoring for questions related to treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin was based on a 7-point scale: 1, extremely dissatisfied; 2, very dissatisfied; 3, dissatisfied; 4, somewhat satisfied; 5, satisfied; 6, very satisfied; 7, extremely satisfied. Satisfaction was then categorized as one of two levels: satisfied or more, scores 5–7; less than satisfied, scores 1–4.

Understanding level was scored on a 7-point scale from 0–6, based on the number of correct answers to a total of six questions about the patient's disease and the medication (mirogabalin). Understanding was then categorized as one of three levels: low, 0–2 points; moderate, 3–4 points; high, 5–6 points. Details are shown in the Supplemental Methods.

Communication level was scored on a 5-point scale: 1, “listened to me very well;” 2, “listened to me at least once;” 3, “neither;” 4, “didn’t listen to me very much;” 5, “hardly listened to me at all.”

NRS for pain was based on an 11-point scale from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst pain possible”). The NRS score was used because it is a comprehensive overall pain measure suitable for evaluating drug efficacy. The Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions, painDETECT, or Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs, which are indicators of neuropathic pain, were not employed because they are screening tools.

Statistical Analyses

This was an exploratory study; thus, the sample size was set at 200 patients, based on feasibility.

The primary efficacy analyses used data from the full analysis set (FAS) population, which was defined as all enrolled patients who received at least one dose of the study drug and for whom at least one efficacy datum was available. The same analyses were also carried out using data from the per-protocol set (PPS), which was defined as all patients in the FAS population who adhered to the study protocol and the instructions in the package insert of mirogabalin.

For the primary endpoint, the percentage of patients who were satisfied with mirogabalin treatment and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact method.

Multivariable analysis using a logistic regression model was used to examine factors associated with patient treatment satisfaction, and odds ratios, 95% CI, and p values were calculated by the Wald method. The following items were covariates in the multivariable analysis: sex (male or female), baseline NRS score (< or ≥ median value), disease duration (< or ≥ 12 months), and duration of NeP (< or ≥ 3 months). Similar analyses were performed to examine factors associated with patient understanding and physician treatment satisfaction. For this analysis, changes in patient understanding were categorized into “improved” and “non-improved.” Improved was defined as an increase of ≥ 1 point in the change from baseline at week 8, while non-improved was defined as any change other than improved. Furthermore, for analysis association between patient treatment satisfaction and changes in patient understanding, patients with high understanding levels (5–6 points) at baseline were excluded.

The Cochran-Armitage test was used to calculate p values for the trend between patient treatment satisfaction and patient understanding.

Regarding agreement rates between patients’ and physicians’ treatment satisfaction, kappa (k) coefficients and their 95% CIs were calculated as an index of the degree of agreement. A kappa score < 0.4 was classified as poor; 0.4 to < 0.6, moderate; 0.6 to < 0.8, good; and ≥ 0.8, excellent [22]. Agreement rate was defined for each endpoint as the number of pairs whose answers matched/the number of all analyzed pairs ×100.

The safety analysis set was defined as all patients enrolled in the study who received at least one dose of the study drug. TEAEs were coded using the Japanese Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, version 27.1. A TEADR was defined as a TEAE judged by the attending physician to have a causal relationship with the study drug.

The significance level for hypothesis testing was set at 5% (two-sided). Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

Among the 201 patients who provided informed consent, 194 were included in the FAS, of whom 166 (85.6%) completed the study (Fig. 1). The most common reasons for discontinuation were withdrawal of consent (n = 17) and TEAEs (n = 8).

Treatment Satisfaction

Patient treatment satisfaction at week 8, the primary endpoint, was 52.8% (95% CI, 44.8%, 60.6%) for satisfied or more (scores 5–7) (n = 163) (Fig. 2a). Physician treatment satisfaction at week 8 was 79.1% (95% CI not calculated) for satisfied or more (n = 163) (Fig. 2b).

The proportion of reporting being at least satisfied with mirogabalin treatment was numerically higher in patients with shorter duration of the primary disease than in those with longer duration (65.1% and 28.6%, respectively), and in those with shorter duration of NeP than in those with longer duration (65.6% and 37.0%, respectively) (Table S3). Among patients with NRS score 2 and 3, 63.6% (7/11) and 61.1% (11/18), respectively, reported being at least satisfied with mirogabalin treatment.

The results of multivariable analysis using a logistic regression model showed that patient satisfaction regarding efficacy, safety, and cost were associated with patient treatment satisfaction at week 8 (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). Change from baseline at week 8 in NRS score for pain, ≥30% or ≥50% responder rates in terms of NRS score for pain at week 8, change from baseline at week 8 in EQ-5D-5L, satisfaction with communication with attending physicians, and improved patient understanding were associated with patient treatment satisfaction at week 8 (all p < 0.05). Similar tendencies were observed for factors associated with physician treatment satisfaction at week 8 (Fig. 2d). Most (93.9%, 153/163) patients were satisfied (i.e., responded “listened to me very well” or “listened to me at least once”) with communication with their physicians/pharmacists at week 8 (data not shown).

In cases in which physicians were satisfied or more with treatment at week 8, the rate of agreement with patient treatment satisfaction at week 8 was 49.1% (80/163), and the disagreement rate was 30.1% (49/163), with a kappa coefficient of 0.303 (95% CI, 0.178, 0.428) (Fig. 2e). Similar results regarding treatment satisfaction, its associated factors, and agreement rates were observed in the PPS (Fig. S2).

The proportion of patients with high understanding increased from 32.0% at baseline to 65.6% at week 8 (Fig. 3b). Patient understanding at week 8 was associated with their treatment satisfaction at week 8 (Cochran-Armitage test, p = 0.021 for trend) (Fig. 3c). Patient treatment satisfaction was 58.9% (63/107), 45.2% (19/42), and 28.6% (4/14) among those with high, moderate, and low understanding, respectively.

Safety

TEAEs occurred in 53.8% (105/195) of patients; most of these were mild or moderate in severity (Table 2). Frequent TEAEs were somnolence (n = 69, 35.4%) and dizziness (n = 24, 12.3%). Serious TEAEs were dyspnea, clavicle fracture, and road traffic accident (each 1 patient), and all of these were judged as not related to the mirogabalin treatment. TEAEs leading to study discontinuation occurred in 5.6% (11/195) of patients.

Discussion

In the MiroSearch study, patients’ treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin was 52.8% for satisfied or more (scores 5–7 on a 7-point scale) at week 8, and their understanding level of their disease and mirogabalin was associated with patient treatment satisfaction. Furthermore, the improvement in understanding from baseline at week 8 was associated with increased patient treatment satisfaction. These findings indicate that an enhanced patient understanding through continuous medication instruction leads to greater patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin. While numerous studies have reported on the relationship between patient education/medication instruction and treatment satisfaction, studies investigating satisfaction and understanding of pain medications including mirogabalin have been limited to cross-sectional studies using questionnaires [7, 8]. Notably, the present study is the first longitudinal interventional study to clarify the relationship between treatment satisfaction and understanding of mirogabalin in patients with NeP due to orthopedic diseases. Furthermore, this study included patients who were first-time users of medications for the treatment of NeP and received medication instruction. These are novel and unique features of the MiroSearch study, although this was a single-arm study without a comparator group. The findings confirmed in this single-arm exploratory study should be further verified in future comparative studies.

In the present study, treatment satisfaction was assessed using a 7-point scale. The scale for treatment satisfaction used in this study differed from that used in the previous study [7], making it difficult to directly compare results for patient treatment satisfaction. In the MiroSearch study, patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin at week 8 was 52.8% for satisfied or more (scores 5–7 on the 7-point scale) and 85.9% for somewhat satisfied or more (scores 4–7); these results seem to be consistent with those of a previous questionnaire survey (76.1% for scores 4 and 5 on a 5-point scale) [7].

In clinical practice, appropriate dose titration of mirogabalin has been reported to improve treatment adherence [23]. Patients’ understanding of their diseases and pain medications has been reported to be an important factor in improving patient treatment satisfaction [7, 8]. Therefore, suboptimal treatment adherence, when observed, may reflect patients’ low treatment satisfaction with, or their lack of understanding of, this medication. Low treatment adherence may lead to reductions in the efficacy of mirogabalin, resulting in a negative cycle of further decreased patient satisfaction. In the present study, the multivariable analysis showed that an improvement in patient understanding was associated with increased patient treatment satisfaction. Given these considerations, an improvement in patient understanding through use of continuous medication instruction may be important to increase patient treatment satisfaction with mirogabalin as well as to improve adherence to mirogabalin treatment and thus the efficacy and safety of mirogabalin.

In the present study, patients’ understanding was evaluated based on their responses to six questions regarding their disease and medication. Rates of correct answers for questions regarding differences between types of pain (the difference between nociceptive pain and NeP, i.e., pain in response to noxious peripheral stimuli vs. pain arising from damage to the nerves themselves) were lower than those for responses to the other five questions. Relationships between patients’ treatment satisfaction and their understanding of specific questions were not investigated in the present study. However, to improve patients’ understanding of NeP, it may be necessary to carefully explain how this type of pain differs from nociceptive pain.

In the MiroSearch study, treatment satisfaction was lower for patients than for physicians (52.8% and 79.1%, respectively). Regarding agreement between patients and physicians, in cases in which physicians reported being at least satisfied with mirogabalin treatment, 49.1% of patients agreed with this assessment, and 30.1% were not satisfied. In addition to patient understanding of disease and pain medication, close communication between physicians and patients (e.g., via shared setting of treatment goals) and physician empathy are also important for patient satisfaction [7, 24–26]. In fact, in the present study, the satisfaction level with communication was found by multivariable analysis to have the third highest odds ratio for patient treatment satisfaction. On the other hand, this study shows that even when patient satisfaction with communication was high, there was a gap between patients and physicians in treatment satisfaction. This suggests that, while patients are satisfied with the physician listening to them to a certain extent, their expectations regarding the efficacy of mirogabalin treatment such as immediate pain relief are higher than those of physicians. In this study, physicians/pharmacists communicated with patients by explaining the efficacy and safety of treatments with the medication instruction form, and most patients (93.9%) were satisfied with communication provided by their physicians/pharmacists and responded “listened to me very well” and “listened to me at least once.” Therefore, setting common goals between patients and physicians to further improve the quality of communication may contribute to enhancing patient treatment satisfaction [24].

In the present study, patients who received mirogabalin were found to have reduced pain and improved QoL from baseline at weeks 4 and 8 of treatment, based on the results for NRS scores and EQ-5D-5L, respectively. The frequent types of primary disease were lumbar spinal stenosis (55.2%), cervical spondylosis (24.2%), and lumbar disc herniation (13.9%) in this study. The findings of the MiroSearch study regarding improvement of pain and QoL are generally consistent with those of the phase 3 clinical trial for spinal cord injury (changes from baseline to week 14: average daily pain score [least square mean ± standard error], − 1.23 ± 0.132; EQ-5D-5L, 0.0395 ± 0.13627) [14], MiroTAS study for lumbar spinal stenosis (changes from baseline to week 12 in the mirogabalin add-on to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs] treatment group: VAS score, − 24.1 mm [95% CI − 29.6, − 18.6]; EQ-5D-5L [mean ± SD], 0.0787 ± 0.1641) [16], Miro-Cens study for cervical spondylosis (changes from baseline to week 8 in the mirogabalin add-on to NSAIDs treatment group: NRS [estimated], − 2.24 [95% CI − 2.70, − 1.78]; EQ-5D-5L, 0.0467 [0.0240, 0.0694]) [17], and Miro-Hers study for lumbar disc herniation (changes from baseline to week 8 in the mirogabalin add-on to NSAIDs treatment group: NRS [estimated], − 3.8 [95% CI − 4.4, − 3.3]; EQ-5D-5L [estimated], 0.1816 [0.1524, 0.2109]) [18]. In contrast to the previous studies [14, 16–18], which excluded patients with mild pain (NRS score ≤ 3), 21.1% of patients in the present study had pain at this level of severity. These results suggest that mirogabalin could improve pain and QoL for patients with mild pain as well as for those with moderate or severe pain.

Regarding the safety of mirogabalin, TEAEs, most of which were mild or moderate in severity, were experienced by 53.8% of patients in the present study. The incidence of TEAEs was similar to that reported for previous studies of mirogabalin [12–14, 16–18] irrespective of the type of primary disease: 60.9% in the MiroTAS study for lumbar spinal stenosis [16], 48.6% in the Miro-Cens study for cervical spondylotic radiculopathy [17], and 54.4% in the Miro-Hers study for lumbar disc herniation [18]. Incidences of the most common TEAEs observed in this study, such as dizziness and somnolence, were also consistent with those reported previously [12–14, 16–18] and were not novel. Furthermore, no serious TEADRs were reported in this study. Although careful attention should be given to the possibility of patients developing TEAEs, the results of the present study suggest that mirogabalin is well tolerated, with no novel safety concerns.

In the present study, improvement in NRS score for pain and EQ-5D-5L were associated with treatment satisfaction. Among patients with NRS scores 2 and 3, about 60% reported being at least satisfied with mirogabalin treatment. Furthermore, the proportion of patients who were at least satisfied with treatment was higher in those with shorter duration of the primary disease and earlier onset (< 3 months) of NeP. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines chronic pain as pain that persists or recurs for > 3 months [3]. Patient treatment satisfaction in the present study were higher than that of a previous study on chronic pain (29.4%) [9]. Considering that the patients in this study were first-time users of pain medication, these results suggest that early pain assessment and pain treatment prior to the transition to chronic pain and continuous medication instruction from the start of treatment could improve treatment satisfaction for patients with pain regardless of its severity.

Limitations

First, the study design must be considered. This was an open-label, single-arm study, which limited interpretation of the effects of use of the medication instruction form on patient treatment satisfaction and understanding, and the efficacy and safety of mirogabalin. To more clearly isolate the effect of the intervention, a randomized controlled study comparing mirogabalin with medication instruction versus mirogabalin alone is required. Moreover, as this was an exploratory study, no adjustment for multiplicity to assess the efficacy of mirogabalin was done. Second, this study had a short intervention period of 8 weeks. However, this duration was considered sufficient to explore patients’ understanding and satisfaction with medication instructions, as well as efficacy and safety endpoints as preliminary trends. Third, in actual clinical practice, physicians and pharmacists adjust their explanations to patients according to their patients’ understanding level. In the present study, to standardize the quality and time required for medication instruction as much as possible, medication instruction was provided twice, using the same forms for all patients. However, customizing medication instruction according to patients’ understanding level may further improve patient treatment satisfaction. Finally, the questionnaire used in this study was developed originally for this study and has not been validated; however, it generally met the basic principles of item creation as described by Clark and Watson [27].

Conclusion

Treatment satisfaction among patients with NeP due to orthopedic diseases can be enhanced by providing medication instruction to improve their understanding of their disease and medications. Additionally, good communication between patients and their attending physician or pharmacist, in which they can share treatment goals and freely express their concerns and questions about treatment, can contribute to improvement in patient treatment satisfaction.
