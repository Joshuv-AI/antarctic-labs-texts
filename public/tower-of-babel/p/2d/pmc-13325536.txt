MRI Predictors of Outcome in Primary Central Nervous System Lymphoma Patients Before and After Autologous Stem Cell Transplant
Authors: Dewen Meng, Hamza Alshuft, Christopher P Fox, Stefanie Thust, Nicolas Martinez‐Calle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPrimary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) is treated with intensive chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplant (HDT‐ASCT), yet up to one‐third of patients relapse. Standard MRI response criteria may not reliably distinguish residual tumour from post‐treatment change; additional MRI sequences, such as apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), could assist in the determination of early relapses, although their prognostic value remains uncertain.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 63 PCNSL patients, assessed pre‐HDT‐ASCT IPCG response and ADC values, and examined associations with 2‐year relapse‐free survival.ResultsOnly pre‐HDT‐ASCT IPCG status, not the lesion ADC value, significantly predicted the 2‐year outcome (p = 0.005). An overall CR rate of 85% was observed at a median follow‐up of 24.7 months.Conclusions:Pre‐HDT‐ASCT IPCG status, not ADC values, predicts 2‐year IPCG status in PCNSL patients.Keywords: autologous stem cell transplant, diffusion weighted MRI, IPCG radiological consensus criteria, MRI, PCNSL, treatment response

Introduction

Primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) is an aggressive extranodal lymphoma confined to the central nervous system, accounting for 4% of intracranial neoplasms and 4%–6% of extranodal lymphomas [1]. For younger patients with good performance status, intensive therapy with MATRix (high‐dose methotrexate, cytarabine, thiotepa, rituximab) followed by high‐dose therapy and autologous stem cell transplantation (HDT‐ASCT) is now standard, with IELSG32 [2] and UK real‐world data reporting 7‐year survival approaching 70% in patients < 70 years [3].

Approximately 30%–40% of patients (18–60 years old) experience relapse after ASCT within the first 2 years [3], with very poor survival post‐relapse [4]. For these patients, early identification of residual disease or impending relapse is therefore critical for timely intervention.

Baseline evaluation and response criteria [5] mainly rely on anatomical MRI (contrast‐enhancing tumour volume on T1‐weighted images). Patients with limited (50%) and excellent (> 90%) enhancing tumour reduction are classed in the same ‘partial response’ (PR) category. Moreover, subtle post‐HDT‐ASCT enhancement, sometimes accompanied by intrinsic T1 shortening, may represent either gliosis or viable tumour. Within the International Primary Central Nervous System Lymphoma Collaborative Group (IPCG) criteria, such minimal abnormalities often fall into the unconfirmed CR category (CRu), reflecting the challenge of reliably distinguishing true complete response (CR) from post‐treatment effects using standard anatomical MRI alone. At present, there is no reliable method to predict whether patients with CRu will be reclassified to complete remission (as most often occurs), whether CRu status will persist (i.e., gliosis with tumoral remission), or whether findings represent residual disease that evolves to full‐blown relapse. Hence, therapeutic interventions based on these small residual MRI findings remain controversial and likely to have a high number needed to treat. Recent research has explored three‐dimensional MRI approaches, including volumetric enhancement and whole‐lesion analyses [6, 7], to improve response evaluation in PCNSL. As the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) is similarly derived from three‐dimensional diffusion imaging, examining its role fits within these emerging quantitative imaging strategies. PCNSL typically exhibits ADC values based on the principle that viable lymphoma deposits commonly show low diffusivity. Several studies demonstrate associations between lower ADC and adverse outcomes after methotrexate‐based regimens [8, 9, 10], suggesting that ADC values can help discern between residual gliosis and active lymphoma.

We report a single‐institution retrospective audit aimed at identifying candidate MRI imaging features that correlate with relapse after HDT‐ASCT. We have analysed standard‐of‐care MRI images from a homogeneously treated and imaged cohort of PCNSL patients who received HD‐MTX‐based regimens with an intensive approach and received HDT‐ASCT consolidation. We also explore whether ADC measurements could improve the interpretation of residual MRI abnormalities and provide additional prognostic information.

Materials and Methods

Consecutively diagnosed patients with biopsy‐proven PCNSL under treatment at Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust between June 2011 and April 2021 were included. Intensive approach was defined as HD‐MTX‐based induction therapy followed by HDT‐ASCT consolidation.

Patients received treatment with high‐dose methotrexate (HD‐MTX)‐based chemotherapy. Rituximab‐HD‐MTX‐cytarabine was used prior to 2016, after which the MATRix regimen was adopted. Exclusion criteria were secondary CNS lymphoma or non‐DLBCL histology. The predictive value of residual MRI findings was investigated in a 2‐fold approach (i) IPCG [5] radiological consensus criteria and (ii) additional MRI parameters, not currently captured by the IPCG standard.

Serial MRI follow‐up imaging followed the standard‐of‐care schedule for PCNSL patients in our institution, including multi‐sequence gadolinium‐enhanced brain MRI, performed at diagnosis, before the HDT‐ASCT, and every 2 months in the first 2 years after HDT‐ASCT. All MRI scans included ADC maps. Patients who relapsed or died during the follow‐up period were censored at the time of progression/death

Two independent reviewers (R1, 9 years’ neuroimaging research experience and R2, 4 years’ experience) who were blinded to clinical information assessed all MRIs. Based on IPCG criteria [5], the response was categorised as CR, requiring the complete disappearance of all enhancing abnormalities on contrast‐enhanced MRI. CRu included those patients who had a persistent minimal enhancing abnormality on MRI, discounting foci of T1 shortening/haemorrhage. PR required a more than 50% decrease in the contrast‐enhancing lesion seen on MRI as compared with baseline imaging. Progressive disease (PD) required a more than 25% increase in the contrast‐enhancing lesion, or the presence of a new lesion(s), compared with the baseline or best response MRI. Disagreement was resolved subsequently by consensus.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn on the scans with T1weighted‐visible residual lesions on the ADC maps constructed from three‐directional diffusion‐weighted images (DWI). For each lesion, three ROIs with approximately the same size (0.1 cm2) were manually drawn in the contrast‐enhancing lesion to record the mean ADC value of the lesion. Care was taken to exclude susceptibility effects/T1 shortening from the blood product, if present. To normalise values across patients and scans, three ROIs were drawn on the centrum semiovale and ADC values were recorded and averaged (Figure 1). ADC analyses were restricted to patients who demonstrated measurable residual contrast enhancement on the post‐ASCT MRI. Patients achieving complete radiological response (i.e., no residual enhancement) do not have a measurable region in which lesion‐specific ADC can be calculated. Including these cases would require imputation of hypothetical lesion ADC values, which are neither biologically meaningful nor statistically valid. Therefore, patients without measurable residual enhancement were excluded from the ADC–outcome analyses.

The primary endpoint was radiological response at the 2‐year landmark after HDT‐ASCT or the latest available MRI if follow‐up was shorter. Additional endpoints included correlation of ADC values in residual lesions and response rate at the 2‐year landmark timepoint.

The audit received institutional board approval as part of a local practice audit without the need for informed consent. All data were handled according to national confidentiality regulations and UK GDPR.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 24.0, Chicago, IL, USA). Cohen's kappa analysis was run to determine the interobserver agreementfor the treatment response. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to investigate whether the pre‐HDT‐ASCT IPCG status or ADC values predict the treatment response at the 2‐years follow‐up timepoint. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 63 PCNSL patients, 54 patients met the inclusion criteria for MRI analyses after exclusion of three lacking clinical endpoint data and six without post‐HDCT‐ASCT imaging (Figure 2).

The clinical characteristics of the 54 patients are summarised in Table 1. The median follow‐up period (from HDT‐ASCT to endpoint) was 743 days. A total of 350 MRI scans were reviewed. The reviewers disagreed on the CR versus CRu treatment response category in 25 (7%) MRI studies, most commonly regarding the presence of minimal residual enhancement. No disagreements occurred in any other radiological IPCG assessments. Interobserver agreement for IPCG response classification was good (к = 0.842, p < 0.001).

Pre‐HDT‐ASCT imaging demonstrated CR in 17 patients (32%), CRu in 17 patients (32%) and PR in 13 patients (24%; Table 1), while no progression events were observed. Seven patients had non‐evaluable response pre‐HDT‐ASCT (two lacked baseline MRI scans, three had no contrast‐enhanced MRI at pre‐HDT‐ASCT timepoint, and two patients had no contrast‐enhanced MRI sequence at baseline).

Regarding the conversion of response from pre‐ to post‐HDT‐ASCT, at the primary endpoint, 39 patients (72.2%) remained in CR/CRu, and 7 patients (12.9%) improved from PR to CR. Five patients (9.2%) progressed from PR to PD, and 2 patients (3.7%) progressed from CR to PD. One patient (1.8%) remained on PR.

Of the 54 patients with available serial imaging, 35 (65%) lacked residual enhancing lesions post‐HDT‐ASCT, whereas 19 (35%) had residual abnormalities (χ 2 = 1.7, p = 0.2). Focusing on those with measurable residual enhancement (n = 19), normalised ADC values were calculated for the pre‐ASCT scans and correlated with response at 2‐year landmark. Pre‐HDT‐ASCT IPCG treatment response (partial eta squared = 0.216, p = 0.005), not the pre‐HDT‐ASCT normalised lesion ADC value (partial eta squared = 0.004, p = 0.523), significantly predicted the treatment response status at 2‐year landmark. Relapse occurred in three patients without residual enhancement and four with residual enhancement post‐HDT‐ASCT. Among patients with residual lesions, the median number of post‐transplant scans was six. One of these four patients experienced resolution of residual enhancement at the second post‐transplant scan but relapsed on the third post‐transplant scan.

Discussion

In this single‐centre cohort of homogeneously treated patients with PCNSL, we observed that the post hoc centralised review of pre‐HDT‐ASCT treatment response by IPCG criteria was strongly correlated with the treatment response status at the 2‐year landmark. Overall, a low proportion of patients had documented residual MRI abnormalities after treatment, likely contributing to the observed lack of correlation between pre‐HDT‐ASCT normalised ADC value of residual lesions and 2‐year response status. To our knowledge, such an association with IPCG response in the real‐world setting has not been previously demonstrated, further supporting the validity of IPCG criteria in a non‐trial setting. Our study contrasts with a recent study in which IPCG categories failed to predict survival [11]. Differences in consolidation strategies, heterogeneous treatment regimens, and higher reviewer discrepancy in the latter study may justify the stronger correlation observed in our study.

CR was maintained in the majority of patients who achieved CR prior to HDT‐ASCT, with additional patients attaining CR after HDT‐ASCT. In our cohort, the CR rate at the primary endpoint was 81.5%, reflecting strong radiological remission following HDT‐ASCT. While not directly comparable with published survival outcomes, these findings reinforce the well‐established role of ASCT in achieving durable disease control in PCNSL [2, 12].

The low number of patients with residual MRI abnormalities limited the correlative analyses of the normalised ADC values with response. Several previous studies have indeed proposed ADC as a potential imaging biomarker to predict treatment response in PCNSL, despite some variations in treatment regimens [6, 7, 8, 10, 13]. Despite the non‐significant correlation, the numerically higher proportion of relapses in patients with residual MRI abnormalities in our cohort (4/19 cases as opposed to 3/35) suggests that these patients merit at least closer observation post‐treatment. The true risk of recurrence associated with these residual MRI findings and the normalised ADC values remain to be studied with an adequately powered cohort. One of the limitations of this study is the use of small, manually placed ROIs for ADC quantification. Although we aimed to standardise ROI size at approximately 0.1 cm2, minor variations were unavoidable, and even small deviations could introduce measurement error. Automated or volumetric approaches may reduce this variability in future studies.

Interobserver agreement for IPCG categorisation was good (к = 0.842), with disagreements confined to the distinction between CR and CRu. This level of concordance highlights the reliability and reproducibility of IPCG criteria despite their semi‐quantitative nature. Other studies have found larger rates of disagreement between reviewers. We attribute our results partly to the single‐centre nature of our cohort, in which all the patients were imaged following a similar MRI protocol, minimising the external factors that can influence image acquisition and quality. Our findings support the use of standardised radiological criteria in the real‐world setting and highlight the value of the imaging‐based endpoints used in this cohort.

Conclusion

In conclusion, among newly diagnosed PCNSL patients receiving HDT‐ASCT consolidation, pre‐transplant IPCG response provides a strong, reproducible predictor of 2‐year outcome, whereas normalised ADC offers no incremental prognostic value.
