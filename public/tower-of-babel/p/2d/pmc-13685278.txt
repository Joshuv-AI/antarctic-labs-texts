Infertility-related stress and attachment styles as predictors of fertility readiness in women undergoing infertility treatment
Authors: Rukiye Sulu Dursun, Mehtap Duran, Ebru Gozuyesil, Sefa Arlier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to identify the predictors of fertility readiness, specifically examining the roles of infertility-related stress and attachment styles in women undergoing infertility treatment.MethodsThis study used a descriptive and cross-sectional design. It was conducted with 316 women who sought treatment at the Assisted Reproductive Treatment Center at Adana City Hospital, located in Adana Province, Turkey, between April 2024 and September 2024. Data were collected through the “Personal Information Form,” the “Fertility Problem Stress Scale,” the “Relationship Scales Questionnaire,” and the “Fertility Preparedness Scale for Women Receiving Fertility Treatment”.ResultsThe participants’ Fertility Preparedness Scale total mean score was 89.88 ± 13.34. A negative, low-level, and significant relationship was found between fertility preparedness and personal stress domain score (p < 0.05). A negative and significant relationship was also found between the prepared body and brain and the social stress domain score (p < 0.05). Regression analysis, including all stress domain and attachment subscale scores, explained 4.6% of the variance in readiness for fertility (R²adjusted = 0.046, p < 0.001); within this model, increased personal stress and increased preoccupied attachment emerged as the only statistically significant individual predictors (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study suggested that women may feel more ready for fertility as their personal stress decreases and their preoccupied attachment levels decline.

Background

The World Health Organization, defines infertility as a condition related to the male or female reproductive system, characterized by the failure to achieve pregnancy after twelve months or more of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse [1]. Infertility-related stress has a multidimensional structure encompassing personal, marital, and social domains [2–5]. Studies show that infertility-related stress is felt most intensely in the personal stress domain, which is followed by the marital and social stress domains [2–4]. This stress can negatively affect readiness for fertility, quality of life, and psychological well-being.

Attachment styles in adulthood are investigated under four main types, including secure, dismissive, preoccupied, and fearful attachment [6]. Individuals with a secure attachment style develop positive attitudes towards themselves and others; they do not struggle to establish and maintain closeness in emotional relationships; and could seek support and offer support to others when needed. On the other hand, individuals with a preoccupied attachment style often consider themselves worthless, fear being abandoned by others, and may intensely seek approval and love [6, 7]. Individuals with a dismissive attachment style exhibit positive attitudes toward themselves but negative attitudes toward others; they tend to avoid close relationships and may insist on maintaining weak emotional bonds and preserving their independence. In the fearful attachment style, individuals develop negative attitudes towards both themselves and others; even if they desire to form relationships, they may avoid close relationships due to fear of rejection [7]. The literature indicates that while secure attachment facilitates coping with stress, insecure attachment styles (preoccupied, dismissive, fearful) increase stress levels [8, 9].

Readiness for fertility is a concept that reflects the extent to which an individual is physically and mentally ready for pregnancy. This concept, which is related to hope and awareness and a prepared body and brain, is an important indicator of an individual’s compliance with treatment, positive perception of childbirth, and psychological well-being [10, 11]. The literature indicates that increased levels of hope and awareness, support for mind-body harmony, and stress reduction have positive effects on readiness for fertility in infertile women [12, 13].

In this regard, identification of the effect of the stress level experienced by women in the process of infertility treatment and their attachment styles on their readiness for fertility is believed to provide important information in terms of better understanding women’s psychosocial needs and enabling them to manage this process more healthily. The literature contains various studies that examine infertility-related stress [2–4] and attachment styles [8, 9], and readiness for fertility, either separately or in the context of paired relationships [12, 13]. However, no studies were found to have evaluated these three variables in tandem. In this regard, the study sought answers to the following research questions:

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a descriptive and cross-sectional design. Participants were recruited from women who were diagnosed with primary infertility, defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 or more months of regular unprotected sexual intercourse [1], and who were receiving treatment at the Assisted Reproductive Treatment Center at Adana City Hospital, located in Adana Province, Turkey, between April 2024 and September 2024. The sample size was calculated at a 95% confidence level using a priori multiple regression analysis with the G*Power 3.1.9.2 program. The calculation was performed with an expected medium effect size (f² = 0.15), 0.95 power, 38 estimated parameters, and a 0.05 significance level. Accordingly, the minimum sample size was determined to be 287 [14–16]. The study was completed with 316 women, as planned with a 10% loss rate included. The study included women who were over 18 years of age, were married, had primary infertility, had experienced infertility problems and sought treatment in the center, understood and spoke Turkish, and were open to cooperation and communication.Women with visual, hearing, or any other disability, who were diagnosed with a psychiatric disease, and took any psychiatric medication were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected using the “Personal Information Form,” the “Fertility Problem Stress Scale,” the “Relationship Scales Questionnaire,” and the “Fertility Preparedness Scale for Women Receiving Fertility Treatment (FPSWRFT)”. Data were collected by the researcher by meeting the participants face-to-face after providing information about voluntary participation and confidentiality and obtaining their verbal and written consent. Data collection was completed in 10–15 min.

Personal information form

The Personal Information Form was prepared by the researchers using the related literature. The form consists of a total of 15 questions about the socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics of women and their spouses/partners [10–12].

Fertility problem stress scale

The Fertility Problem Stress Scale, to determine the stress experienced by infertile women, was validated and tested for reliability in Turkish by Yılmaz and Yeşiltepe Oskay [5]. The personal stress domain consists of six items (1, 2, 11, 12, 13, 14), with scores ranging from 0 to 20. Higher scores indicate increased stress in the personal domain. The marital stress domain consists of four items (3, 4, 5, 6). Scores to be obtained from the scale range between 0 and 14, with higher scores indicating increased stress in the marital domain. The social stress domain consists of four items (7, 8, 9, 10). The scores to be obtained from the scale range between 0 and 12, with higher scores indicating increased stress in the social domain. Cronbach’s alpha values for the three sub-scales of the scale were found to be 0.81, 0.73, and 0.79. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were determined to be 0.79, 0.73, and 0.88.

Relationship scales questionnaire

The Relationship Scales Questionnaire was developed by Griffin and Bartholomew (1994) and its Turkish validity and reliability were conducted by Sümer and Güngör [6]. The 17-item scale includes four sub-scales: secure(3, 7, 8, 9, 17), dismissive(2, 5, 12, 13, 16), preoccupied(5, 6, 11, 15), and fearful(1, 4, 9, 14) attachment styles. Cronbach’s alpha values range from 0.41 to 0.71. Higher scores on the scale indicate a distinct attachment style. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were determined to be 0.55, 0.53, 0.53, and 0.58.

Fertility preparedness scale for women receiving fertility treatment

The 23-item scale was developed by Fata and Aluş Tokat [17]. The scale consists of three sub-scales including “hope and awareness,” “prepared body and brain” and “positive feelings and thoughts.” Since the scale items consist only of positive statements, the scores are calculated normally. The median score on the scale is 56. A score of 56 or higher indicates that the woman feels more prepared for pregnancy, while a lower score indicates that she feels less prepared. Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient for the scale was determined to be 0.84 [17]. In this study, it was found to be 0.92.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed in the IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 program. Descriptive statistics included numbers, percentages, minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation values. The normality assumption of the data was tested by evaluating whether the skewness and kurtosis coefficients fell within the ± 2 range. Pearson correlation analysis and simple and multiple linear regression analyses were performed for relational analyses for data showing normality. Prior to the multiple linear regression analysis, model assumptions were systematically evaluated. Linearity and homoscedasticity were assessed through visual inspection of residual plots, revealing no substantial violations. Residual normality was confirmed using Q-Q plots. Influential observations were examined using Cook’s distance, and no observations exceeded conventional exclusion thresholds. Given the moderate intercorrelations among the personal, marital, and social stress domain scores, multicollinearity was assessed using VIF and tolerance values; all VIF values remained below 5 and tolerance values exceeded 0.20. These checks supported the appropriateness of the regression model for the present data [18]. In addition, Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated to determine the internal validity of the scales.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the predictors of fertility readiness and to examine the roles of infertility-related stress and attachment styles in women undergoing infertility treatment. The results showed that readiness for fertility scores were high, indicating that women felt ready for pregnancy; stress levels in the personal stress domain were higher than stress levels in the marital and social domains; the secure and dismissive attachment subscale scores were higher compared to the preoccupied and fearful attachment subscale scores.

The cut-off point for the Preparedness For Fertility Scale used in this study is 56. Accordingly, this study found that women had high readiness for fertility scores and felt ready for pregnancy. Several studies investigating readiness for fertility in women undergoing infertility treatment have reported similarly elevated readiness scores, which may suggest that women who actively seek fertility treatment tend to perceive themselves as more prepared for pregnancy. This pattern may reflect the motivational orientation of treatment-seeking individuals, who are likely to demonstrate greater psychological readiness compared with those who do not pursue medical intervention [2, 3, 11, 12]. Demirdağ Durgaç and Aluş Tokat [12] reported that women undergoing infertility treatment had high levels of fertility preparedness [12]. These studies in the literature and the present study included a sample group of women who sought infertility treatment at the hospital and had started treatment due to primary infertility. Therefore, their readiness for pregnancy is an expected finding.

This study found that women’s personal stress domain levels were higher than their marital stress and social stress domain levels. Erdemoğlu and Aksoy Derya [2], Topsakal [3], Fata and Tokat [11], and Demirdağ Durgaç and Aluş Tokat [12] reported that women undergoing infertility treatment experienced the highest levels of stress in the personal domain, followed by the marital domain, whereas social stress was the lowest. Similarly, Teklemicheal et al. [19] found that infertility-related psychological stress was predominantly experienced at the individual level, and Kocabaşa [20] reported that infertility primarily affected women’s personal emotional well-being. The findings of this study are in line with those in the literature, indicating that infertility causes stress at different levels in the personal, marital, and social domains.

In this study, the secure and dismissive attachment subscale scores were found to be higher than the preoccupied and fearful attachment subscale scores. Given that no validated cut-off points were used for categorical classification, these findings are evaluated as reflecting relative subscale scores rather than definitive attachment style classifications. Individuals with secure attachment can actively communicate and interact with others. They are also more likely to share their feelings and experience positive emotions with each other [9]. Ak [7] reported that secure attachment is associated with positive self-perception, healthier interpersonal relationships, and lower levels of anxiety, whereas insecure attachment styles are characterized by greater emotional vulnerability [7]. In contrast, Kocabaşa [20] found that women with unexplained infertility exhibited significantly higher levels of insecure attachment characteristics than women without infertility [20]. Since attachment styles are an innate mechanism and are shaped by adult experiences and life experiences, individual factors may contribute to variations in attachment styles. The discrepancy between our findings and those reported in the literature may therefore be explained by differences in study populations and individual psychosocial characteristics.

An analysis of the relationship between fertility awareness and fertility problem stress shows that women’s personal stress levels decrease as they feel more prepared for pregnancy; their hope and awareness and prepared body and brain increase. The regression analysis results support these findings. Increased personal stress in women has been identified as a predictive factor for readiness for fertility [16]. Increased stress and hopelessness are reported to be one of the primary reasons for discontinuing infertility treatment [21]. High readiness for fertility may contribute to individuals’ increased awareness of the treatment process and their ability to take action [12]. Psychological interventions aimed at reducing infertility-related stress, which is identified as a significant predictor of fertility readiness, may contribute to increased fertility readiness and more effective stress management in women undergoing infertility treatment. Women’s inner balance can be regulated as their hope and awareness increase and their bodies and minds relax In this regard, increasing hope and awareness along with mind-body preparedness could contribute to reducing personal stress.

This study found that women’s prepared body and brain increased as they felt more prepared for pregnancy, and their social stress levels decreased. Since infertility is an unpredictable and uncontrollable stressor, and most people do not know how to cope with this situation, appropriate social support systems could reduce the negativity or play a protective role caused by this stressor [10, 22]. Fata and Aluş Tokat [10] reported that hypnofertility-based interventions reduced stress levels in women receiving fertility support and contributed to their readiness for fertility by promoting mind-body harmony [10]. Sharing fertility-related treatment experiences with friends increases readiness for fertility [12]. The findings of this study are supported in the literature [10, 12], the more comfortable women feel in their bodies and minds, the more prepared they feel for pregnancy, and consequently, their social stress levels decrease.

Regression analysis results suggested that preoccupied attachment may also be a contributing factor associated with readiness for fertility in women. Attachment style is often related to coping style, stress levels, and anxiety [9]. The literature indicates that cognitive behavioral interventions reduce emotional distress [23], mindfulness-based practices reduce anxiety and depression [24]. Yi et al. [24] found that mindfulness therapy significantly reduced anxiety and depression in infertile female patients and contributed positively to their psychological well-being [24]. These findings suggest that lower levels of preoccupied attachment may be associated with higher fertility readiness scores; however, given the cross-sectional design and the limited explanatory power of the regression model, these associations have been interpreted as preliminary and exploratory.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted with women receiving treatment at a single center in Adana Province, Turkey, which limits the generalizability of the results to the broader population of women with infertility. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between infertility-related stress, attachment styles, and fertility preparedness. Third, data were collected through self-report measures, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Fourth, the study was limited to women with primary infertility; therefore, the findings may not be applicable to women with secondary infertility. Fifth, the relatively low Cronbach’s alpha values obtained for the Relationship Scales Questionnaire subscales in the present sample, despite these attachment subscales being used in the main analyses, should be considered as one of the limitations of this study. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse samples, as well as longitudinal designs, are recommended to overcome these limitations and to establish causal inferences.

Conclusion

This study found that readiness for fertility scores were high, indicating that women felt ready for pregnancy; stress levels in the personal stress domain were higher than stress levels in the marital and social domains; and the secure and dismissive attachment subscale scores were higher relative to the preoccupied and fearful attachment subscale scores. An analysis of the relationship between fertility awareness and infertility problem stress showed that women’s personal stress levels decreased as they felt more prepared for pregnancy and their hope and awareness increased, and their social domain stress decreased when their prepared body and mind increased. Besides, increased personal stress and preoccupied attachment were identified as predictive factors for readiness for fertility. It is important for midwives and nurses working in clinics to provide individualized care that is sensitive to women’s attachment styles and stress levels; to plan psychological support services in an integrated manner with infertility treatment; and to develop education programs aimed at increasing women’s awareness of the fertility preparation process.
