Evaluating the role of high-dose intravenous thiamine supplementation for lactate clearance in patients with cirrhosis and sepsis
Authors: Radiya Ali, Angel Yazdi, Keegan Collins, Danielle Guffey, Tianshi David Wu, Rebecca Kessinger, Ali Omranian, Meghna Vallabh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Thiamine is a vitamin that plays a role in lactate clearance. Patients with sepsis may present with elevated lactate, and also in cirrhosis, as lactate is hepatically metabolized. Prior studies showed mixed results regarding the role of thiamine for lactate clearance with limited inclusion of cirrhosis patients. The purpose of this study was to determine the association of high-dose intravenous thiamine on time to lactate clearance among patients with cirrhosis and sepsis.Methods:We performed a single-center retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients with cirrhosis with a diagnosis of sepsis with lactate >4 mmol/L between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Patients who received ≥400 mg were classified as receiving high-dose thiamine. Fine-Gray competing risks models adjusted for demographic, comorbidity, and illness severity variables estimated the association of receiving high-dose thiamine with time to lactate clearance, defined as a lactate <3 mmol/L. Additional secondary outcomes included intensive care unit and in-hospital mortality.Results:A total of 136 patients were identified and included in the analysis. Median age was 58.4, and 54% were male. Thirty-three patients received high-dose thiamine. Patients had similar baseline characteristics, including on indicators of illness severity, cirrhosis severity, and use of vasopressors. In both unadjusted and adjusted models, the receipt of high-dose thiamine was not associated with differences in time to lactate clearance or mortality.Conclusions:Thiamine use in patients with cirrhosis who experienced sepsis did not result in improved lactate clearance compared to those who did not receive high-dose thiamine. These data do not support empiric high-dose thiamine to aid lactate clearance in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a metabolically demanding and dysregulated response to pathogens, associated with a proinflammatory and pro-coagulant disease state.[12] The mortality associated with septic shock ranges from 42% to 53%;[3] therefore, early detection, identifying and eliminating the infectious source, and restoring hemodynamic stability have been the forefront of many research projects over the years.

Hyperlactatemia has been used as one of the markers for detection and early intervention in septic shock to assess tissue perfusion. Lactate accumulation during septic shock is due to increased production and reduced clearance. Elevation in lactate production reflects cellular dysfunction, such as insufficient oxygen delivery to tissues, impaired aerobic respiration, and acceleration of glycolysis,[24] a consequence of the hypoxic vasodilation seen in septic shock. Serum lactate levels >4 mmol/L in patients with septic shock are associated with a higher mortality rate;[56] therefore, efforts to improve tissue perfusion through fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support can improve patient outcomes.

In patients with liver injury, reduced lactate clearance can be seen as lactate is hepatically metabolized.[2] It is then logical to theorize that patients with poor liver function at baseline, such as patients with cirrhosis, will have worse outcomes with septic shock due to higher lactate levels and delayed lactate normalization.[7] Studies in fact show mortality among patients with cirrhosis admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with sepsis to range from 18% to 66%.[8]

In more recent years, the role of thiamine in aerobic metabolism and lactate clearance in patients with septic shock has been studied.[910] Thiamine is a water-soluble vitamin (B1), and its deficiency is associated with a reduction of pyruvate in the Krebs cycle and an increase in lactate production by altering oxidative metabolism.[1011] Although routine evaluation of thiamine levels is not practiced in critically ill patients, there are several studies that have looked at thiamine supplementation in patients with septic shock.[9121314] The literature that is available, however, differs in terms of study design and patient populations. In addition, the dosing strategies within the trials differ significantly. Some of the randomized controlled trials used doses of 200 mg intravenous (IV) for 3–7 days, while observational studies include a larger dose of up to 500 mg daily.[1] Furthermore, most studies exclude patients with liver cirrhosis from their study design mainly due to poor lactate clearance at baseline. Therefore, the role of thiamine supplementation in lactate clearance in septic shock, especially in patients with cirrhosis, remains controversial.

Thiamine is inexpensive and well-tolerated. Patients with liver dysfunction are at a greater risk of thiamine deficiency, and since there are limited data assessing the use of thiamine for lactate clearance in this patient population, we proposed a study to evaluate the use of IV thiamine on lactate clearance in patients with cirrhosis and sepsis compared to those without cirrhosis.

METHODS

Study design

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the electronic medical record of a quaternary care facility. Because this hospital is a high-volume liver transplant center, a significant proportion of patients in the medical ICUs (MICUs) have advanced cirrhosis, representing an ideal setting to conduct the analysis. The study was performed with Institutional Review Board approval.

Patient population

We identified all adult patients with cirrhosis who were admitted to any MICU from January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2024 with a diagnosis of sepsis and who had a blood lactate within the first 24 h of admission of >4 mmol/L, which we defined as elevated. Patients with lower lactate levels within the 24 h of MICU admission, thiamine supplementation prior to MICU admission, and those deceased within 24 h of MICU admission were excluded from the study.

Primary independent and dependent variables

The key independent variable was the total dose of thiamine received within the first 24 h of admission to the MICU, as obtained from the medication administration record. Based on prior literature, we defined patients who received high-dose thiamine as those who were administered at least 400 mg of thiamine.

The primary endpoint of the study was time to lactate clearance, defined as a lactate level <3 mmol/L. We additionally examined ICU and in-hospital mortality.

Additional data and confounders

We extracted additional information for descriptive purposes and confounder adjustment. We recorded age, sex, race, body mass index, and comorbid conditions (hypertension, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, end-stage renal disease, malignancy, heart failure, and etiology of underlying cirrhosis). We extracted severity scores for sepsis (the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score) and cirrhosis (the Model for End-stage Liver Disease [MELD] score). We extracted laboratory data such as leukocyte count, bicarbonate levels, and coagulopathy profile. Finally, we extracted information related to clinical interventions for sepsis, including fluid resuscitation, vasopressor use, and stress-dose steroid use.

Because the decision to utilize high-dose thiamine was thought to be influenced by patient severity, we selected SOFA score, MELD score, use of epinephrine (through stimulation of glycolysis), and age as the principal covariates for confounder adjustment.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics and outcomes are summarized using mean with standard deviation, median with 25th and 75th percentiles, and frequency with percentage. Characteristics and outcomes are stratified by thiamine dose in the first 24 h after qualifying lactate. Wilcoxon rank–sum test, Chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test are used to compare characteristics and outcomes by thiamine dose. Unadjusted and multivariable Fine-Gray competing risks regression models were used to assess the association between receipt of high-dose thiamine with time to lactate clearance, with death and hospital discharge modeled as competing risks. For ICU mortality, discharge from the ICU was considered a competing risk, and for hospital mortality, discharge from the hospital was considered a competing risk. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was used to ascertain statistical significance. All analyses were performed in Stata 18 (StataCorp; College Station, TX, USA).

Patients’ baseline characteristics were evaluated at the time of the qualifying lactate. The median age was 58.4 years (interquartile range 50.7–64.4), the mean SOFA score was 11.2, the median MELD score was 35, and 53.7% of the patients were male. Less patients in the high-dose thiamine group required mechanical ventilation compared to the no thiamine group (57.3% vs. 30.3%; P = 0.009), and a higher proportion of Hispanic patients were in the high-dose group. No other differences were seen regarding other clinical characteristics between the groups.

Primary outcome

Lactate clearance occurred in 87.9% of the high dose group versus 71.8% in the no thiamine [Figure 1]. In the unadjusted competing risks regression, high-dose thiamine was not associated with a higher hazard for lactate clearance (hazard ratio [HR] 1.38; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.93–2.04; P = 0.11) [Table 2]. When adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, MELD score, and epinephrine use, the results were not significantly different (adjusted HR 1.33; 95% CI, 0.86–2.06; P = 0.19).

Secondary outcomes

There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of secondary outcomes. There were no differences with hospital mortality (adjusted HR 0.75; 95% CI, 0.45–1.27; P = 0.29) or ICU mortality between the groups (adjusted HR 0.85; 95% CI, 0.47–1.53; P = 0.59).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with sepsis and elevated lactate, receipt of high-dose thiamine was not associated with differences in time to lactate clearance and ICU or in-hospital mortality. Overall, our data do not support the use of high-dose thiamine with the objective of hastening lactate clearance.

Thiamine is a water-soluble vitamin that has been increasingly utilized in patients with septic shock for possible benefit in lactate clearance. It is a coenzyme required for metabolism and can promote lactate clearance by facilitating aerobic metabolism. There are prior studies that evaluated the use of thiamine for lactate clearance with sepsis, which showed mixed results. In addition, the subset of patients included in those trials included a limited number of patients with cirrhosis. Thus, this study aimed to focus specifically on patients with cirrhosis, as lactate is hepatically metabolized, leading these patients to often have higher lactate levels at baseline.

A few studies have been conducted previously that assessed the use of thiamine for lactate clearance in patients with sepsis. Some retrospective cohort studies found lower mortality in patients receiving thiamine, and a study by Woolum et al. found improved lactate clearance.[115] Small randomized controlled studies were conducted that showed no differences in lactate clearance in most patients, except those who were thiamine-deficient. The studies vary in their design and doses of thiamine used.[1316] In addition, the number of patients who had cirrhosis was either unavailable or minimal.

There are several limitations to our study, including the retrospective nature and small sample size, which may diminish the ability to identify differences between the groups. We note that the upper bounds of the CI do not preclude the possibility of a clinically significant effect of thiamine. Larger studies are necessary to confirm the results from this study.

CONCLUSIONS

High-dose thiamine use in patients with cirrhosis who experienced sepsis did not result in improved lactate clearance compared to those who did not receive high-dose thiamine.
