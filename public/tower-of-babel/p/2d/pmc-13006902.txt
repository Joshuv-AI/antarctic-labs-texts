Integrative genomics reveal genetic links of chronic cough with risk factors and brain regional volumes
Authors: Zikai Lin, Junfeng Huang, Linzhi He, Shuojia Xie, Gongping Shu, Gengjia Chen, Weitong Lin, Xiaowen Zhong, Shiyue Li, Kian Fan Chung, Ruchong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesChronic cough is a common condition, particularly among women in their sixties, and is associated with a high disease burden and reduced quality of life. Limited understanding of its risk factors and genetic associations represents a barrier to its effective management.MethodsWe used genome-wide association study datasets for respiratory and other diseases, as well as behavioural phenotypes from the UK Biobank, FinnGen, Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative and Psychiatric Genomics Consortium. We identified pleiotropic genetic associations with chronic cough using linkage disequilibrium score regression, pleiotropy analysis and Bayesian colocalisation analysis. We conducted Mendelian randomisation analysis to determine the association between brain regional volumes and chronic cough risk.ResultsWe identified 19 significant genetic correlations between chronic cough and various risk factors and associations. Among 74 pleiotropic genomic loci identified, the 4p14 locus had the strongest effect on cough-associated asthma, with RFC1 linked to cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome. Gene enrichment analyses highlighted that the central nervous system and ion channel activity play roles in the pathogenesis of cough. A reduction in the volume of isthmus cingulate, thalamus proper and pallidum was associated with an increased risk of chronic cough. In ageing-associated cough, the direct effect of ageing on chronic cough showed an increased odds ratio of 1.044, while a reduction in left pallidum volume was associated with a decreased risk, with an odds ratio of 0.902.ConclusionsThis genetic basis of chronic cough risk and its associations highlights both structural and functional genetic overlap, confirming the impact of reductions in brain regional volumes on chronic cough as a result of ageing.

Introduction

Chronic cough is a long-lasting and burdensome condition that may persist for years, despite available medical interventions [1, 2]. It is one of the most common complaints in primary care, affecting up to 10% of adults, especially women aged 60 years and older. Many pulmonary and some extrapulmonary conditions can present with chronic cough, yet a significant proportion of cases have no identifiable cause. Our limited understanding of the mechanism underlying chronic cough is partly due to the heterogeneous nature of its causes, presentation and associations, together with the complex interaction between the peripheral and central nervous system involved in the genesis of cough with other organ systems, environmental factors, genetic susceptibility and the presence of other respiratory or extrapulmonary conditions.

Recent insights have led to the concept of “cough hypersensitivity syndrome” as a distinct condition [3, 4], characterised by a persistent cough that is often triggered by low-level thermal, mechanical or chemical exposure [5]. A cough that usually acts as a protective reflex can become a nuisance by its excessive presence.

Chronic cough is thought to involve peripheral sensitisation, a process in which airway inflammation is believed to increase the excitability of vagal sensory fibres and alters receptor and ion channel expression, leading to heightened cough reflex sensitivity [1, 6, 7]. It has also been proposed that central sensitisation, which may be driven by brainstem inflammation, can further amplify cough sensory inputs and impair cough suppression [4, 8, 9]. While this concept contributes to understanding the pathophysiology of chronic cough, the genetic mechanisms related to cough-associated diseases remain unclear.

Various factors, such as environmental and host-related influences, can sensitise and trigger cough [10–12]. Previous research has found associations between chronic cough and risk factors such as asthma, persistent smoking and low education/social status, while evidence remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding COPD and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) [13]. Research on the specific genetic risk factors for chronic cough has been relatively limited. However, previous work has implicated several genes involved in sensory pathways, including mutations in the ion channel genes TRPV1, TRPA1 and SCN9A, as well as in the TAC2R gene, in addition to an RFC1 repeat expansion associated with sensory neuropathy [1, 14–18]. Central sensitisation acts as a bridge between genetic predispositions and structural brain changes. This central component is increasingly supported by neuroimaging studies, which have revealed both functional and structural alterations in the brains of individuals with chronic cough. Functionally, increased activity in the midbrain cough network has been confirmed in patients with cough hypersensitivity [19, 20]. Structurally, a reduction in the volume of areas such as the anterior cingulate cortex and frontal lobes has been related to chronic cough [21, 22]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying specific genomic variations or loci that contribute to whole-genome genetic correlations and of further exploring the shared genetic mechanisms linking chronic cough and its associated brain regions.

In this study, we utilised large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to perform a pairwise trait pleiotropic analysis of chronic cough and its risk factors across genome-wide, single nucleotide variant (SNV), gene and biological pathway levels to elucidate potential shared genetic aetiologies. Additionally, the genetic associations between brain regions and chronic cough were analysed. Building on this, we highlighted the roles of ageing and specific brain regions in the underlying shared genetic aetiology of chronic cough.

Methods

GWAS datasets

GWAS datasets for respiratory system diseases, central and peripheral nervous–psychiatric disorders, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular diseases, and behavioural science were used, sourced from UK Biobank (UKB), FinnGen, the Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative and the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium. Chronic cough data was obtained from UKB, identifying subjects by positive responses to the query “Do you cough on most days for at least three months of the year?”. On average, these patients reported a chronic cough duration of 12 years. Summary statistics GWAS data for brain regional volumes and ageing phenotypes were also sourced from UKB. The 83 cortical and subcortical grey matter volumes were obtained by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. Further details on the MRI GWAS data in our analysis are available in the supplementary methods. Ageing was defined by six characteristics, including health span, parental lifespan, exceptional longevity, frailty index and self-rated health [23]. Detailed information on the included phenotypes can be found in supplementary table E1. The complete design of the study is shown in figure 1.

Genetic correlation analysis

We identified 41 chronic cough-related traits, listed in table 1, by systematically reviewing literature on risk factors and comorbidities published from 1 January 2010 to 1 January 2024, for which large-scale GWAS summary data were publicly available (see the supplementary methods for details). For each pair of chronic cough and risk factor, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) was performed using the R package ldscr (https://github.com/mglev1n/ldscr), with the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European-ancestry genotypes as a reference.

Pleiotropic analysis under composite null hypothesis (PLACO)

For the risk factors that were significant in LDSC, we used PLACO to scrutinise traits with substantial genetic correlation, investigate potential trait correlations and pinpoint pleiotropic single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) through the R package PLACO (https://github.com/RayDebashree/PLACO). SNPs with PLACO <5×10−08 were declared significant pleiotropic variants.

Identification of genomic risk loci

Based on PLACO, independent significant SNPs (p<5×10−08, r2<0.6) were initially identified. Genomic risk loci were defined as clusters of independent signals that are either physically proximate or overlapping within a single genomic region. Moreover, SNPs located within a 250 kb radius were consolidated into a singular genomic risk site. The identification and delineation of genomic risk loci were conducted using the Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genome-Wide Association Studies (FUMA) platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/) [24].

Bayesian colocalisation analysis

For the identified significant genomic risk loci, we performed genetic colocalisation analysis using the COLOC package. Essentially, the approximate Bayesian factor colocalisation analysis calculates the likelihood of these variants being shared by mapping each feature under a single causal variation hypothesis and subsequently integrating the two posterior distributions. If the posterior probability of H4 (PP.H4) exceeds 0.6, it is deemed to be a significant locus. Furthermore, the SNP boasting the highest PP.H4 in this locus is identified as a candidate causal variant.

Gene set-level analysis and tissue-specific gene expression analysis

We executed gene-level multiple marker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA) based on the PLACO output GWAS to spot potential pleiotropic genes and utilised a Bonferroni-corrected p-value threshold of less than 0.05 [25]. Additionally, we conducted a tissue-specific enrichment analysis using the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx v8), which contains gene expression data from 54 human tissues. The analysis tests whether the average gene expression level in a specific tissue, across all identified pleiotropic genes, is higher than that of genes not associated with the trait. A nominally significant threshold (p<0.05) was set for the parallel phenotype and tissue enrichment analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

For the genes identified by MAGMA, we carried out GSEA using gene sets sourced from the Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway databases [26] to identify potential biological pathways. The GSEA algorithm was utilised to discern if genes within a gene set were clustered at the top or bottom or were randomly distributed within a ranked gene list. The “clusterProfiler” package was used for this analysis.

Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis

We conducted MR analysis to explore the causal relationship between 83 brain regional volumes and chronic cough. Subsequently, we further performed a mediation analysis of the effect of ageing on chronic cough via brain regional volumes using a two-step MR analysis framework. Detailed methodology is provided in the supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

All results are reported as odd ratios, with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses and data visualisation were performed in R software 4.4.0 (www.r-project.org) and Python 3.6.0 (www.python.org).

Results

Genetic correlation analysis of chronic cough with cough risk factors

In our PubMed search, amongst 41 risk factors, we identified 19 with significant genetic correlations, as detailed in table 1. Specifically, five genetic correlations were found among 11 respiratory diseases, seven among 14 diseases of the peripheral and central nervous systems, three among eight gastrointestinal and cardiovascular diseases, and four significant genetic links with eight other risk factors (table 1). Notably, all diseases showing a significant correlation with cough had a genetic correlation coefficient greater than 0, indicating a positive genetic correlation with cough. In the field of social science, we found a negative genetic correlation between chronic cough and factors such as income and educational level.

Analysis of shared loci in chronic cough and its risk factors

PLACO was performed on the 19 identified chronic cough risk factors with significant genetic associations. We identified 125 genomic risk loci exhibiting pleiotropy, of which 74 are unique (figure 2 and supplementary table E2). Notably, certain loci manifested across a diverse array of phenotypes. Thus, locus 5q23.1 was prevalent in seven distinct phenotypes including COPD, GORD and depression; 22q12.2 was common in six phenotypes including income, inflammatory bowel disease and eating disorders; and 2q12.1 in five phenotypes including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, depression and GORD. Within the identified loci, a comprehensive analysis of the top 125 SNPs revealed that a substantial majority of 96 SNPs (76.8%) exhibited consistent associations with the risk factors linked to chronic cough (supplementary table E3).

Colocalisation analysis of shared genetic loci in chronic cough and its risk factors

In further colocalisation analysis of these shared loci, 14 loci were identified as significantly shared (table 2 and supplementary figure E1). Supplementary table E4 presents all the association loci. Among these, the 5q23.1 locus emerged as a significant genetic region for three distinct phenotypes, showing enriched associations in terms of COPD (PP.H4=0.814), income and GORD. These findings indicate a significant correlation among these traits that share a common causal variant.

Gene locus annotation in chronic cough and its risk factors

In the analysis of the 125 pleiotropic loci using MAGMA, we identified 291 potential pleiotropic genes, including 195 unique pleiotropic genes and 125 protein-coding genes (figure 2 and supplementary table E5). 64 of these genes were detected in two or more risk factors/associations. Notably, the most frequently enriched were CTD-2334D19.1 (long noncoding RNA) and RNU4-69P (small nuclear RNA), both appearing in seven phenotypes and enriched at the 5q23.1 locus. Furthermore, within the loci significant for colocalisation, 22q12.2 had the highest combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) score (CADD=21.1), suggesting its potential as an important pathogenic locus (supplementary table E6). Additionally, the locus at 4p14 had a CADD score of 18.81. Enriched within this locus was the RFC1 gene, now recognised as the basis for the CANVAS (cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome) that often presents early with chronic cough [27, 28]. MUC5AC and MUC2 in 11p15.5 were also enriched, which are associated with airway mucus secretion, and IL11-RA in 9p13.3 is related to airway inflammation [29, 30].

Tissue specificity and pathway enrichment analysis

An in-depth analysis of tissue specificity and gene expression datasets was conducted for these 125 protein-coding genes. A gene expression heatmap is provided in supplementary figure E2. In brain tissue, extensive enrichment was observed in 14 distinct brain regions (supplementary figure E3), including the hippocampus, frontal cortex and caudate basal ganglia. This suggests that a genetic predisposition to chronic cough may manifest through the dysfunction of central neural regulation, potentially reflecting mechanisms such as central sensitisation. Extensive enrichment was also observed in the oesophagus and lungs, aligning with the close association of chronic cough with gastroesophageal reflux and lower respiratory tract conditions. Detailed results of the tissue specificity analysis are available in supplementary table E7.

To elucidate the biological functions of these pleiotropic genes, we performed a pathway enrichment analysis. As detailed in supplementary figure E3C and supplementary table E8, these pleiotropic genes showed significant enrichment in ion channel activities, such as extracellular ligand-gated monoatomic ion channel activity, transmitter-gated channel activity and excitatory extracellular ligand-gated monoatomic ion channel activity. Additionally, they were enriched in neurotransmitter receptor and signalling activities, including post-synaptic neurotransmitter receptor activity, regulation of post-synaptic membrane potential and acetylcholine receptor activity. Significant enrichment was also found in immune response-related activities and DNA-interacting activities. Detailed results are shown in supplementary table E9.

Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis

We first conducted an MR analysis to explore the relationship between 83 brain regional volumes and chronic cough. We observed significant negative correlations between chronic cough and eight brain regions. An increase in bilateral amygdala volume was significantly associated with a reduced genetic susceptibility to chronic cough (left: OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98; right: OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.97), as well as the pallidum and the thalamus proper (figure 3a). Detailed analytic results can be found in supplementary table E10. Subsequently, we conducted a gender-stratified analysis, which revealed that the thalamus proper shares significant genetic influences in both male and female patients with chronic cough (figure 3b). In females, the volume of the right superior temporal region had a significant associated with chronic cough (figure 3c and supplementary table E11). Conversely, a bilateral reduction in the volume of the pallidum and amygdala volumes was significantly associated with chronic cough in males (figure 3d and supplementary table E12).

Because chronic cough usually presents in people in their sixties or later, we conducted a further MR analysis that explored the relationship between ageing and chronic cough. We demonstrated a significant positive correlation between ageing and chronic cough (OR 1.044, 95% CI 1.016–1.073; p=0.002). To further explore the potential mediating pathways, a two-step MR analysis was performed, which established a causal relationship between ageing and the volume of the left pallidum. The indirect effect of ageing on chronic cough-mediated through the reduction in left pallidum volume was significant (OR 1.004; p=0.047), such that an 8.4% of the increased risk of chronic cough due to ageing may be mediated by the decrease in left pallidum volume (figure 4 and supplementary table E13). In multivariable MR analysis adjusting for potential confounders, the association of ageing (OR 1.044; p<0.001) and left pallidum volume (OR 0.902; p=0.001) on chronic cough were significant.

Mediation analysis of effect of ageing on chronic cough via left pallidum volume under a two-step Mendelian randomisation analysis framework. “Direct effect” indicates the effect of ageing on chronic cough risk without adjusting for the mediator. “Indirect effect” indicates the effect of ageing on chronic cough risk through the left pallidum volume. Given that in the original dataset a higher value of ageing indicates youthfulness and a lower value indicates ageing, we took the negative of the beta coefficient (β) from the source data.

Discussion

We explored the genetic associations between chronic cough and various risk factors, identifying a broad genetic linkage with diverse respiratory, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular diseases or behavioural phenotypes. Notably, 19 of these phenotypes were associated with chronic cough, including well-recognised associations (such as asthma, GORD, COPD and heart failure) and the novel neurological disorder CANVAS [28]. Furthermore, we observed positive genetic correlations with conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and carpal tunnel syndrome. This may be linked through shared mechanisms such as peripheral and central sensitisation, and indeed, clinical observations have noted a comorbid trend between chronic cough and PTSD in certain populations [31–33]. The negative correlations of chronic cough with factors such as educational level and income imply potential socioeconomic influences on disease susceptibility, which also aligns with epidemiological reports [1, 10, 12, 13, 34]. Finally, we identified that atrophy in the isthmus cingulate, thalamus proper and pallidum was associated with increased chronic cough risk. Intriguingly, ageing-related increased risk (8.4%) of chronic cough was partially mediated by volume loss in the left pallidum. This observation underscores the significant role of central nervous system regulation based on genetic variations in the development of chronic cough, highlighting the intricate links between brain structure changes and respiratory health with ageing.

Our analysis has pinpointed multiple loci associated with chronic cough and its risk factors, as well as behavioural traits, such as 5q23.1 and 2q12.1, indicating that these regions may harbour key genes with significant impact on multiple conditions. That the locus 5q23.1 may represent a shared genetic background for COPD and asthma, conditions frequently presenting with chronic cough, is in alignment with the “Dutch hypothesis”, and is significantly enriched in lung tissue expression quantitative trait loci for COPD [35, 36], suggesting a broad genetic link between different respiratory diseases. Notably, the gene RFC1, significantly shared between asthma and chronic cough, has been identified as a causative factor of CANVAS and late-onset ataxia [37, 38]. Risk variants in MUC5AC are significantly associated with increased secretion of MUC5AC protein and with the frequency of mucus-secreting cells in chronic cough, with higher mucin levels observed in patients with cough-variant asthma [29, 39]. A recent study by Coley et al. [40] utilised a more specific “dry cough” phenotype and identified a distinct set of genes functionally pointing towards neurological pathways, such as KCNA10 (potassium channel), MAPKAP1 (mammalian target of rapamycin signalling pathway) and NTSR1 (neurotensin receptor). Their work also confirmed that genes previously linked to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor-induced cough, such as KCNIP4, are associated with chronic dry cough, further strengthening the evidence for a neurogenic basis [40].

The analysis of specific regions of the brain indicates that these genes are extensively enriched in 14 different regions, including the hippocampus, frontal cortex and caudate basal ganglia. Patients with chronic cough exhibit altered neural activities in cough suppression regions, such as the cingulate cortex and inferior frontal gyrus, coupled with diminished activation levels, indicative of neural dysfunction in cough hypersensitivity pathways [7, 20]. Functional brain imaging studies have shown widespread brain activity in response to inhalation-induced cough stimuli, such as capsaicin, encompassing primary and secondary sensory cortical areas, the cingulate gyrus, insula, and orbitofrontal cortex [1, 9, 21]. In pathway enrichment analysis, these pleiotropic genes also showed significant enrichment in ion channel activities, neurotransmitter receptors, and signal transduction, as well as immune response-related activities which have also been linked to chronic cough pathogenesis. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines, such as tumour necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6, which affect synaptic transmission and glial cell activation, may enhance cough sensitivity and induce changes in airway mucosal innervation [19, 41–43]. The key pathway results and detailed tissue-specific analysis elucidated in this study offer further insights into the potential biological mechanisms of chronic cough and its risk factors.

We found that a reduction in the volume of the left pallidum partially mediates the ageing-related increase in cough risk. Previous studies have identified the pallidum, cingulate and thalamus as part of the neural circuitry involved in voluntary cough suppression and response inhibition [44–46]. Within the basal ganglia–thalamocortical loop, cortical outputs from regions such as the inferior frontal gyrus and supplementary motor area are thought to engage the pallidum, which in turn inhibits the excitatory drive from the thalamus to motor cortical areas, thereby suppressing the cough motor output [44, 45, 47]. With ageing, the volume of the globus pallidus and its connections with the cerebral cortex decrease that can result in neurological dysfunction [48, 49], potentially leading to the cough hypersensitivity characteristic of chronic cough. Conversely, increased globus pallidus volume has been observed in adolescents and young adults with prolonged dextromethorphan use [50]. The use of codeine-containing cough syrups has been linked to increased sulcal depth, gyrification and cortical thickness in certain brain regions, as well as elevated degree centrality of the globus pallidus in whole-brain connectivity [51, 52]. Although our findings reveal a genetic association between reduced brain volume, particularly in the pallidum, and chronic cough, these results currently lack direct experimental or clinical validation. Further verification is needed in relevant animal models in the future. Our findings supplement these studies by highlighting the potential regulatory role of brain regions in this process and offer new insights into exploring the neurobiological mechanisms underlying chronic cough such as the importance of the pallidum.

In our gender-stratified analysis, we found that the superior temporal region was associated with chronic cough in females, while the pallidum and amygdala volumes were associated with chronic cough in males. In clinical practice, particularly in Western populations, the prevalence of chronic cough is generally higher in females [53, 54]. From a physiological perspective, females consistently exhibit a higher sensitivity of the cough reflex and a lower cough threshold to tussigenic agents compared to males [55]. These differences are thought to be influenced, at least in part, by oestrogen and progesterone [56]. Functional MRI has revealed differences in the central processing of cough stimuli between genders, with females showing greater brain activation in response to cough triggers [53]. Our findings provide new genetic evidence suggesting that different neural pathways may contribute to the sex differences observed in the pathophysiology of chronic cough, though this requires further investigation.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, our reliance on summary-level GWAS data precludes further analysis based on individual genetic profiles of chronic cough patients. This definition lacks specific data on cough severity, frequency or characteristics, such as productive cough or specific features of cough hypersensitivity syndrome, which limits a more granular analysis of cough subtypes. As a result, caution is advised when interpreting the role of specific genes within the chronic cough population. Secondly, while major confounders such as smoking were included as a trait in the genetic correlation analysis, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors at the summary-data level. Additionally, our study primarily focused on individuals of European ancestry, which limits the generalisability of our findings to other populations. Future research could address these issues by exploring individual-level genetic, longitudinal follow-up and experimental validation in other chronic cough populations.

We present the first comprehensive genetic analysis of chronic cough, revealing a complex genetic architecture and providing novel genetic support for the nervous system's role in its pathophysiology. Finally, the impact of ageing on chronic cough susceptibility through the loss of volume of the brain pallidum offers a specific insight into the central mechanisms of chronic cough. These findings support the concept of chronic cough as a disease rather than just a set of symptoms.
