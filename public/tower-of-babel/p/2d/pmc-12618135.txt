The Relationship Between Myocardial Fibrosis in Hypertensive Patients With Preserved Ejection Fraction and the Severity of Systemic Inflammatory Status Is Mediated by Epicardial Adipose Tissue: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Wanting Wang, Runze Zhu, Wenxian Wang, Yan Gao, Jie Liu, Liang Wu, Ximing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to investigate the impact of exacerbated systemic inflammatory status on the degree of myocardial fibrosis and strain impairment in hypertensive patients with preserved ejection fraction, as well as the role played by epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) in this process. A total of 236 hypertensive patients who underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) and blood routine examinations at two medical centers in China were included. Thirty healthy volunteers were included as the control group. Compared with the low systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) group, patients in the high SIRI group exhibited greater EAT volume, higher Native T1 value, and increased extracellular volume (ECV) (all p < 0.01). Additionally, significant differences were observed between the two groups in cardiac MRI parameters (all p < 0.001). Hypertensive patients had a significantly higher SIRI than healthy controls (p < 0.001). Binary logistic regression analysis indicated that SIRI and indexed EAT volume were independently associated with high ECV value (SIRI: p < 0.001; indexed EAT volume: p < 0.001), with results remaining stable after adjusting for confounding factors. Furthermore, mediation analysis showed that even after adjusting for confounding factors, EAT continued to play a role in SIRI‐mediated changes in ECV (indirect effect: 0.1773 [95% CI 0.0173–0.3147]). HTN may contribute to the increase in systemic inflammatory severity. The relationship between the degree of myocardial fibrosis and the severity of systemic inflammatory status in patients with early HTN is mediated by EAT. Early mitigation of systemic inflammatory status in patients with early‐stage HTN can reduce the adverse effects of EAT, thereby alleviating myocardial fibrosis and strain impairment.Keywords: epicardial adipose tissue, hypertension, myocardial fibrosis, systemic inflammatory response index

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is one of the most common chronic cardiovascular diseases worldwide. Poor long‐term blood pressure control can lead to HTN‐related myocardial lesions centered on myocardial structural remodeling and functional abnormalities, which serves as a crucial pathological basis for heart failure, arrhythmias, and cardiovascular mortality [1]. Systemic inflammatory status acts as a “key accelerator” of hypertensive myocardial lesions: the more severe the inflammation, the earlier the lesions initiate and the faster they progress. The pathological process evolves from early functional impairment to irreversible diffuse fibrosis and cardiomyocyte necrosis, accompanied by multitarget organ damage and poor prognosis [2]. In the early stage of HTN with preserved ejection fraction, systemic inflammatory status is one of the important mechanisms that promotes the occurrence and development of myocardial fibrosis [3]. In cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging, T1 mapping and extracellular volume (ECV) fraction are common non‐invasive indicators for quantifying myocardial fibrosis [4], and are particularly suitable for evaluating diffuse or early‐stage fibrosis. T1 mapping reflects the expansion of the extracellular space and collagen deposition by measuring the myocardial T1 relaxation time; ECV, calculated based on the distribution volume of contrast agents, primarily reflects the proportion of the extracellular matrix volume in myocardial tissue to the total myocardial volume. When myocardial fibrosis occurs, the extracellular matrix increases, leading to a significant elevation of ECV [5].

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) exerts dual effects on the body [6]. Under physiological conditions, it plays roles such as physical protection, metabolic regulation, and heat production at low body temperatures. However, under pathological conditions—including obesity, diabetes, and HTN—it can release a large amount of proinflammatory cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor and interleukins) through paracrine signaling, trigger inflammatory responses, and exert local regulation on the heart and vascular system, thereby causing severe damage to the body [7, 8]. The increase in EAT volume and its functional dysfunction are independent predictors of the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases and poor prognosis [9, 10].

Systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) is a combined biomarker index used in clinical practice to quantitatively assess the overall inflammatory status of the body, indirectly reflecting the severity of the inflammatory response. In patients with cardiovascular diseases such as HTN, coronary heart disease, and heart failure, long‐term elevation of SIRI may indicate a state of chronic low‐grade inflammation in the body [11]. This inflammatory state can accelerate vascular endothelial damage and myocardial fibroblast activation, thereby promoting the progression of myocardial fibrosis and atherosclerosis. In the early stage of hypertensive heart disease, left ventricular hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis may already occur. It is noteworthy that myocardial fibrosis may be reversible during this initial phase [12]. Therefore, alleviating the systemic inflammatory status as early as possible is expected to reduce the degree of myocardial fibrosis in patients with early‐stage HTN. However, the impact of increased systemic inflammatory severity on the degree of myocardial fibrosis and strain impairment in hypertensive patients remains unclear. This study aims to explore the relationship between SIRI, EAT, and myocardial lesions in patients with HTN with preserved ejection fraction.

Methods

Subjects

Two hundred and thirty‐six subjects with HTN were enrolled from two medical centers in China between June 2015 and July 2025. Our study included patients clinically diagnosed with HTN, with reference to the following criteria: systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 80 mmHg [13] measured at least twice, or were taking one or more medications for HTN. Eighty‐nine patients with suspected cardiac hypertrophy on previous echocardiography were used to further assess the extent of cardiac hypertrophy or to assess the response to medical therapy. Sixty‐five patients underwent a comprehensive cardiac function evaluation due to symptoms similar to heart failure. Suspected arrhythmia detected by electrocardiogram (ECG) was investigated for potential myocardial fibrosis or scarring in thirty‐three patients. In addition, forty‐nine patients underwent CMR examination to further detect potential myocardial abnormalities without obvious clinical symptoms. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: reduced systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] < 45% [14, 15]), coronary heart disease, significant valvular disease, renal impairment (glomerular filtration rate < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2), active tumor or paraneoplastic syndrome, acute infection, severe liver failure, known inflammatory/autoimmune diseases, active cerebrovascular disease, or use of steroids, antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs (Figure 1). Thirty healthy subjects, matched with the case group for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were included as the control group. They satisfied the following criteria: normal physical examination, optimal blood pressure levels (SBP < 130 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg), unremarkable ECG findings, no history of chest pain or dyspnea, no diabetes or hyperlipidemia, and normal results from two‐dimensional echocardiography and Doppler examinations. None of the participants were taking any medications. Any potential subjects displaying evidence of heart disease, HTN, or other systemic disorders were excluded from this study. This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. The requirement for informed patient consent was waived.

Clinical Data

The following clinical data were collected in this study, including history of smoking (defined as smoking one cigarette a day currently or in the past, with a duration of at least 6 months) and drinking (defined as consuming alcohol once every week for at least 6 months currently or in the past), history of diabetes, family history of HTN, and history of antihypertensive medication use. We collected the following fasting blood biochemical indicators of the patients: total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride (TG), absolute neutrophil count (NEUT#), absolute monocyte count (MONO#), and absolute lymphocyte count (LYMPH#). The SIRI index was calculated using the following formula: [NEUT ( × 10⁹/L) * MONO ( × 10⁹/L)]/ LYMPH ( × 10⁹/L) [16].

CMR Protocol

This study utilized a 3.0T (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Medical) magnetic resonance scanner to conduct CMR examinations according to the standard protocols [17], including pre‐ and post‐enhancement T1 mapping imaging, breath‐hold cine imaging, and steady‐state free precession (SSFP) sequences. The various parameters used in these sequences are detailed in the Supporting Information (Supporting Appendix text).

CMR Image Analysis

Two experienced CMR physicians, without access to the clinical information related to the images, independently performed contouring and assessment of all CMR images using the commercial post‐processing software CVI42 (version 5.12) (Figure 2A,B). They manually delineated the inner and outer membrane contours of the basal, middle, and apical segments of the left ventricle. According to the 17‐segment model of the American Heart Association (AHA), after excluding the apex area, they calculated the raw T1 values and the T1 values after injecting the contrast agent [18, 19]. Carefully removed the papillary muscles, moderator bands, and the epicardial boundaries on each slice [20]. Before calculating the ECV, they obtained the hematocrit level and manually delineated the blood pool area [21]. ECV was calculated using the following equation: Myocardial ECV = (1 − hematocrit) × [(1/Post contrast myocardial T1 − 1/Native myocardial T1)/(1/Post contrast blood T1 − 1/Native blood T1)] [22]. In addition, based on the SSFP cine sequence, the feature tracking technique was used to determine the global longitudinal strain (GLS), global circumferential strain (GCS), and global radial strain (GRS) of the left ventricle [23]. The specific process is as follows: On the cine images of the left ventricular short‐axis and long‐axis views, the endocardial and epicardial boundaries at end‐diastole and end‐systole are determined through automatic outlining combined with manual adjustment. Based on the outlined contours, the software will automatically track the movement of the endocardium and epicardium throughout the cardiac cycle, calculate the ventricular volume/time curve, and automatically compute the global and segmental strains, generating GLS, GCS, and GRS [24].

Epicardial Adipose Tissue

The tissue signal intensity module of commercial software (CVI42, Version 5.12) was used to measure EAT volume. EAT refers to the adipose tissue located between the outer wall of the myocardium and the visceral layer of the pericardium [25]. On end‐diastolic short‐axis slices, EAT contours were manually delineated, starting from the most basal slice around the atria and progressing sequentially toward the most apical slice around the ventricles [26]. To ensure the accuracy of EAT volume measurement, meticulous manual corrections were performed in the study. During EAT segmentation, non‐EAT regions such as pericardial fat and intrapericardial adipose tissue were excluded from the analysis; slice‐level validation was conducted to optimize EAT boundaries—manual adjustments were made to correct misclassified myocardial or vascular regions, and the brush tool was used to supplement incompletely segmented EAT areas. Prior to segmentation, image quality optimization (including adjustment of contrast and brightness) was performed to display EAT boundaries more clearly. Furthermore, the slice thickness of all datasets was strictly maintained consistent to reduce volumetric measurement bias in cross‐sectional analysis. These procedures adhered to standardized cardiac imaging protocols to ensure the reproducibility of quantitative EAT measurement results (Figure 2C,D).

Statistical Analysis

According to the median value of SIRI, patients with HTN were divided into two groups: the SIRI ≤ 1.17 group and the SIRI > 1.17 group. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software (Version 27.0). All tests were two‐tailed, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the distribution characteristics of variables: If variables conformed to a normal distribution, continuous variables were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD)”, and the Welch's t‐test was used for intergroup comparison; If variables did not conform to a normal distribution, continuous variables were expressed as “median (interquartile range) [median (IQR)]”, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparison. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed by chi‐square test for comparison purpose. Based on the distribution characteristics of variables, Spearman's correlation coefficient was employed to explore the associations among indexed EAT volume (EAT volume/BSA), CMR parameters, and SIRI in hypertensive patients. With ECV values (divided into high‐fibrosis and low‐fibrosis groups by the median) as the dependent variable, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the independent effects of SIRI and EAT volume on ECV values. In the univariate analysis, cardiac morphological and functional parameters, EAT volume, and SIRI were included as continuous variables; the adjustment variables included age, sex, BMI, SBP, DBP, history of smoking, history of drinking, history of diabetes, family history of HTN, lipid profiles, and history of taking antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering medications. Before conducting the multivariate regression analysis, collinearity diagnosis was performed on all variables included in the model. The results showed that there was no severe collinearity among these variables (all Variance Inflation Factors < 5). In the multivariate regression analysis, the covariates in the model 1 were selected based on p value and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) in a stepwise algorithm, with p<0.05 and the difference in AIC between two models >2 [27], representing significant and moderate difference, respectively. Through backward stepwise selection, GRS, index EAT volume, left ventricular end‐diastolic volume index (LVEDVi), and SIRI were retained. Four models were used to adjust for potential confounding factors in the multivariate models, as detailed below: model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, SBP, and DBP; model 3 was further adjusted for history of smoking, drinking, diabetes, and family history of HTN; model 4 was further adjusted for history of taking antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering drugs; model 5 was further adjusted for plasma TG, plasma TC, HDL, and LDL. In addition, the dependent variable was replaced with the native T1 value to further verify the experimental conclusions.

Pre‐specified subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential interaction effects and quantify heterogeneity across key demographic and clinical subgroups. In the subgroup analysis, subgroups were divided based on age (≤ 50 years vs. > 50 years), sex (male vs. female), obesity status (BMI < 27.5 vs. BMI > 27.5) [28], history of smoking, drinking, diabetes, family history of HTN (no vs. yes), and history of taking relevant antihypertensive drugs (no vs. yes). The likelihood ratio test was used to assess the interaction effects between subgroups. Furthermore, a mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether EAT volume plays a mediating role in the association between SIRI and various cardiac parameters. The purpose of conducting mediation analysis was to explore potential mechanistic pathways; it did not assume a chronological order nor aim to infer a causal relationship [29, 30].

Additionally, the SIRI > 1.17 group had a higher proportion of male patients, and the mean levels of SBP, plasma TG, and LDL were significantly increased (all p > 0.05). No significant differences were found in other lipid parameters, history of taking medications, or other variables.

Subgroup Analysis and Mediation Analysis for SIRI, Indexed EAT Volume, and ECV

Subgroup analysis demonstrated that demographic factors (age, sex, obesity) and clinical history did not significantly modify the associations among SIRI index, EAT volume, and ECV (all interaction p>0.05). However, there was a significant interaction between history of smoking (p = 0.036) and the use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEIs/ARBs) with the SIRI index (p = 0.043).

Before conducting the mediation analysis, we examined the relationships between SIRI and EAT (Figure 4A), SIRI and ECV (Figure 4B), and EAT and ECV, respectively, using the Spearman Correlation Coefficient method. All pairwise correlations were statistically significant (all p > 0.001). To investigate whether EAT might play a mediating role in the relationship between SIRI and ECV in hypertensive patients with preserved ejection fraction, we conducted a mediation analysis. In the analysis, we defined the SIRI index, EAT index, and ECV as the independent variable, mediating variable, and dependent variable, respectively. After adjusting for confounding factors, the results showed that EAT exerted a mediating effect on the relationship between the SIRI index and ECV (indirect effect = 0.1773, 95% CI [0.0173–0.3147]) (Figure 5A, Table S2). The same relationship occurred for SIRI and native T1 value (indirect effect = 1.9932, 95% CI [0.1560–3.7139]) (Figure 5B, Table S2).

Discussion

The primary findings of our research are as follows: (1) Compared to healthy controls, patients with HTN with preserved ejection fraction exhibited elevated native T1, ECV, indexed EAT volume, and SIRI, alongside reduced GRS, GCS, and GLS. Likewise, patients in the SIRI > 1.17 group had higher native T1 value, ECV, and indexed EAT volume than those in the SIRI ≤ 1.17 group, whereas their GRS, GCS, and GLS were lower than the latter. (2) Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that both SIRI and indexed EAT volume independently correlated with the high ECV value, and the results remained stable after adjusting for confounding factors. Consistent results were obtained when subgroup analyses were performed based on patients’ baseline characteristics and clinical features. (3) Furthermore, we found that after adjusting for confounding factors, EAT played a mediating role in the association between the SIRI index and ECV. This suggests that increased severity of systemic inflammation may exacerbate myocardial lesions by altering the function of EAT.

Systemic inflammatory status is one of the core mechanisms for the occurrence and development of cardiovascular diseases. As a novel composite inflammatory index, numerous studies have shown that individuals with an elevated SIRI have a significantly higher risk of adverse clinical events such as cardiovascular death [31, 32]. Systemic inflammatory status often coexists with HTN and is closely associated with myocardial lesions in hypertensive patients. Our research indicates that the inflammatory indicators of patients with long‐term HTN are higher than those of healthy individuals, and other studies have also confirmed this finding. Barsukov et al. used transthoracic echocardiography technology to assess the myocardial strain level through left ventricular end‐systolic volume (LVESV, mL), left ventricular end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV, mL), and LVEF, and used tissue Doppler echocardiography to evaluate left ventricular diastolic function. Their study found that the systemic blood inflammatory factors of hypertensive patients with severe myocardial lesions were significantly elevated [33]. Compared with transthoracic echocardiography, CMR has higher spatial and temporal resolution, and is non‐invasive with greater safety [34]. Our research is precisely based on CMR imaging, using native T1, ECV, GRS, GCS, and GLS to evaluate left ventricular diffuse fibrosis and myocardial strain [35]. Mitu et al. collected and compared indicators such as fibrinogen levels, quantitative C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels, and echocardiographic parameters between hypertensive patients and healthy individuals. The results showed that CRP and fibrinogen levels were significantly higher in hypertensive patients; even in the absence of obesity or diabetes, hypertensive patients still had an inflammatory syndrome [36]. In addition, numerous studies have confirmed that increased severity of inflammatory status can promote the progression of HTN‐related myocardial lesions. This study found that in the early stage of HTN with preserved ejection fraction, patients with higher SIRI levels had more significant myocardial fibrosis and worse myocardial strain indicators (suggesting more obvious impairment of myocardial function). This finding emphasizes the importance of early clinical intervention targeting the systemic inflammatory status. However, the specific mechanism by which the increased severity of systemic inflammation leads to myocardial fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction in hypertensive patients remains unclear at present.

Compared with other visceral fat, EAT has unique characteristics [37]. Under normal physiological circumstances, it protects the myocardium by secreting anti‐inflammatory factors such as adiponectin, thereby reducing the risk of adverse clinical events. However, under pathological conditions like obesity, diabetes, and HTN, the mRNA expression and protein levels of proinflammatory cytokines secreted by EAT have been proved to be upregulated, while the level of anti‐inflammatory adiponectin is downregulated. EAT adversely affects the myocardium through paracrine mechanisms that regulate ion currents and gap junctions and induce fibrotic remodeling [38]. Therefore, in patients with early‐stage HTN with preserved ejection fraction, alleviating the proinflammatory function of EAT may reduce the risk of adverse prognosis. A research by Ng et al. confirmed that the ECV and GLS parameters obtained through CMR quantitatively demonstrated a significant correlation between EAT volume and myocardial fibrosis burden as well as left ventricular systolic dysfunction [39]. Furthermore, a study by Ishikawa et al. further verified that EAT accumulation has a significant independent association with impaired left ventricular diastolic function [40]. Our study found that the high SIR group had larger EAT volume, higher native T1 and ECV value, and more severe strain impairment. After controlling for some confounding factors, these associations remained independent. Subgroup analysis revealed that when categorizing patients by history of smoking and the use of ACEI/ARB, there were differences in the association between SIRI and ECV. This suggests that smoking may weaken the positive correlation strength between SIRI and ECV, possibly due to the fact that smoking activates lung‐resident cells, recruits inflammatory cells into the lung tissue, and subsequently promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, oxygen free radicals, and proteases, ultimately altering the function of immune cells [41]. In addition, the differences caused by these drugs (ACEI/ARB) may be associated with their anti‐inflammatory effects and anti‐myocardial fibrosis properties [42]. Although, in theory, antihypertensive drugs may alleviate myocardial fibrosis, our cohort data show that patients receiving these medications have longer disease durations and more severe fibrosis, which may mask the detectable protective effect of the drugs. Further mediation analysis demonstrated that even after adjusting for confounding factors, EAT still plays a role in myocardial fibrosis and strain impairment mediated by the inflammatory state. Therefore, early alleviation of the systemic inflammatory status in patients with HTN with preserved ejection fraction can reduce the harmful effects of EAT, thereby mitigating the degree of myocardial fibrosis and strain impairment, and ultimately decreasing the risk of adverse clinical events.

EAT is a modifiable cardiovascular risk factor and a potential novel therapeutic target. Previous studies have shown that therapeutic agents such as pioglitazone (a glitazone), sitagliptin (a dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor), glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor (GLP1R) agonists, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have demonstrated potential in reducing EAT volume or thickness [43, 44, 45]. However, our research has found that an intensified inflammatory status in the body may, by altering the function of EAT, further aggravate the myocardial fibrosis and strain in patients with HTN. Therefore, early alleviation of the systemic inflammatory status is also of equal significance in reducing poor prognosis. Recent research findings have indicated that semaglutide not only has hypoglycemic effects but also possesses certain anti‐inflammatory properties [46]. At the molecular level, semaglutide can inhibit the activation of the NOD‐like receptor pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and reduce the production of interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) and interleukin‐18 (IL‐18) [47]; at the cellular level, it can reduce the expression of C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCL2), S100 calcium‐binding protein A8 (S100A8), and S100 calcium‐binding protein A9 (S100A9) in neutrophils [48], which may help alleviate cardiac inflammation and oxidative stress; at the tissue level, it can reduce vascular endothelial inflammation, as well as local inflammatory responses and fibrosis in the kidneys. Furthermore, studies have shown that a proinflammatory diet is a key factor in chronic low‐grade inflammation, while the management of beneficial dietary components (such as a high‐fiber diet) can reverse the inflammatory state to a certain extent [49]. A study by Luvián‐Morales et al. further found that foods like cranberries, whole‐grain products, and chia seeds have the effect of regulating inflammation [50]. Early intervention to improve the inflammatory state may alleviate the harmful effects of EAT, such as promoting inflammation and fibrosis, thereby reducing the incidence of adverse prognosis.

Study Limitation

The current research has several limitations. First, the limited sample size introduced instability in the coefficient estimates, despite adjustment for existing confounders. This limitation hindered our ability to test the sensitivity of key coefficients to the addition or deletion of variables. Second, the SIRI index may be influenced by sleep, dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and other lifestyle‐related variables. Although no significant differences in medication usage were detected between the high‐SIRI group and low‐SIRI group in our study cohort, residual confounding effects from these factors cannot be definitively excluded in observational analyses. Moreover, due to the limitations of the retrospective study design, we did not conduct detailed investigations into patients’ exercise patterns, nutritional patterns, socioeconomic status, family history of coronary artery disease, and other relevant information. Third, during the period we reviewed, the diagnostic threshold for HTN was revised from 140/90 mmHg (SBP/DBP) to 130/80 mmHg (SBP/DBP). Although we included patients with reference to the new criteria, it is more important that the inclusion was based on the patients' clinical diagnosis results. Indeed, some patients who were not clinically diagnosed with HTN according to the old criteria were excluded from this study; this has affected the accuracy of the study results to a certain extent. Fourth, after screening in accordance with the strict exclusion criteria mentioned earlier in the text, we finally obtained only 30 samples in the control group. The number of samples in the control group is not well matched with that in the case group, which will also affect the accuracy of the study results. Fifth, as this study is retrospective, the chronological order of the occurrence of inflammation, EAT volume changes, and myocardial fibrosis cannot be determined, which is also one of the limitations of this study. Lastly, the retrospective nature of this study may introduce bias into the research findings. Our study was mainly based on CMR data, which may have led to the omission of some mild cases that would typically only undergo cardiac ultrasound examinations. This, in turn, limits the generalizability of the study results. It is recommended that future research should conduct multicenter, large‐sample‐sized prospective studies and provide more detailed research data to ensure the accuracy of the study findings.

Conclusion

The present study revealed that compared with hypertensive patients with preserved ejection fraction with low SIRI and the controls, patients with an elevated SIRI exhibited significantly greater EAT volume, ECV, and native T1 value, as well as worse left ventricular multidirectional strain function. Moreover, we propose that EAT is involved in the process by which the SIRI affects ECV. Early mitigation of systemic inflammatory status is expected to alleviate the harmful effects of EAT, thereby reducing the incidence of poor prognosis.
