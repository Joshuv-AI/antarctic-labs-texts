Reply to: Effective buffers in intravenous solutions: reconciling the acid–base effects of organic anions
Authors: Raul Rigo-Bonnin, Arnau Ulsamer, John A. Kellum
Published: 2026

We thank Heldeweg et al. [1] for their interest in our review [2] and for their thoughtful contribution to the discussion on the acid–base effects of organic anions in intravenous (IV) solutions. We fully agree that acid–base physiology benefits from integrative frameworks rather than rigid adherence to a single descriptive language. However, we believe that several statements in their letter risk reintroducing the causal ambiguity our article aimed to clarify. Namely, attributing the alkalinizing effects of organic anion–containing solutions to metabolism-driven bicarbonate generation [3–5].

Organic anions, buffering, and terminology

The authors correctly note that monocarboxylate organic acids such as lactate, acetate, or gluconate have pKa values far from physiological pH and therefore exhibit negligible classical chemical buffering capacity in plasma [6]. This observation, however, does not contradict our interpretation. Our use of the term buffer was not intended in the narrow chemical sense of pKa-dependent proton binding, but rather in the broader physiological sense, as long adopted in acid–base analysis, in which pH stability arises from the redistribution of dependent variables under the constraints of electroneutrality and CO₂ handling [4, 5].

Within Stewart’s physicochemical framework, buffering is not restricted to weak acids but arises from changes in the strong ion difference (SID), total weak acids, and PCO₂, which act as independent variables determining plasma pH [7]. In this context, organic anions administered as sodium salts behave as strong ions, with their acid–base effects mediated by changes in SID, irrespective of their proton-binding capacity at physiological pH. Whether these solutes are labelled “buffers” or “strong ions” is therefore largely semantic and does not alter the underlying mechanism [3–5].

On the notion of obligatory initial acidification

Heldeweg et al. [1] further argue that sodium salts of organic anions, having a net SID close to zero, must initially reduce plasma SID and cause metabolic acidosis until the anion is removed from the extracellular space. While such an effect can indeed be derived in a closed, instantaneous, single-compartment model, its physiological relevance in vivo appears limited.

In living systems, infused fluids distribute across compartments, dilute weak acids, and interact dynamically with existing electrolyte gradients. Experimental and clinical studies consistently show that the net acid–base effect of crystalloids is quantitatively predicted by their effective SID and by weak-acid dilution, without evidence of a reproducible or clinically meaningful initial metabolic acidosis following the infusion of balanced solutions [4, 8]. A transient theoretical effect that is neither consistently observed nor clinically relevant cannot reasonably be invoked as a primary explanatory mechanism.

Removal and metabolism of organic anions: permissive, not causal

We agree that organic anions are removed from the extracellular space for cellular uptake and eventual metabolism, and that this removal may be coupled to proton transport in the case of monocarboxylates. However, citrate is not transported by proton-linked monocarboxylate transporters and yet produces a similar effect, suggesting that additional mechanisms must be involved. Nevertheless, we agree that proton-linked transport of monocarboxylates should be considered in a multicompartment model, which was not the aim of our article, as explained.

Importantly, as we aimed to emphasize in our article, the Krebs cycle metabolizes organic anions, producing carbon dioxide, which hydrates to carbonic acid, yielding bicarbonate and a proton. This process cannot generate a net base and therefore cannot explain alkalosis. Increases in bicarbonate concentration should be interpreted as dependent variables adjusting to electroneutrality and CO₂ handling, not as primary drivers of alkalemia [9].

Citrate: conditional effects rather than intrinsic acidification

The statement that citrate is “strongly acidifying unless metabolized” represents an oversimplification that conflates chemical form with systemic acid–base effect. Even careful examination of the study cited by the authors (Hamm and Simon, 1987) [10] does not clearly support this interpretation. At physiological pH, citrate is indeed predominantly trivalent [7] and its net effect on acid–base balance in vivo is entirely conditional on accompanying strong cation load, calcium binding, and extracorporeal handling.

Clinically, citrate is never administered as an isolated anion but as sodium citrate and/or calcium–citrate complexes. During regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA), a substantial fraction of citrate is removed in the effluent, while sodium and calcium may be retained, leading to a net increase in SID and a well-recognized risk of metabolic alkalosis, often attributed to citrate metabolization which is described as “citrate overload” [11, 12]. Conversely, when citrate clearance is impaired, citrate accumulation as an unbalanced strong anion is believed to contribute to metabolic derangements and hypocalcaemia, commonly referred to as “citrate accumulation” [12], yet the mechanisms underlaying such acidifying effect remain to be confirmed.

Neither scenario requires invoking citrate metabolism as a source of net alkalinity. Metabolism prevents citrate accumulation but does not generate base. The acid–base effects observed in citrate-based protocols are therefore more coherently explained by strong-ion balance than by bicarbonate production, fully consistent with Stewart’s framework and with clinical observations across a wide range of RCA settings [4, 7, 10, 11].

The acidifying effect of citrate anion is an issue that deserves separate consideration. Experimental evidence concerning whether non-metabolized organic anions display acid–base behavior comparable to chloride under physiological-like conditions is needed. We intend to address this question experimentally in the near future through easily reproducible experiments.

Conclusions

We welcome the authors’ call for integrative thinking in acid–base physiology. Our work was developed through collaboration across complementary areas of expertise, encompassing molecular biology, chemistry, and clinical medicine, reflecting the inherently multidisciplinary nature of this field. These complex questions benefit from careful, measured discussion and from the convergence of different perspectives.

Nevertheless, we maintain that the most predictive and mechanistically coherent explanation for the acid–base effects of organic anion salts in IV solutions and renal replacement therapy remains their impact on strong-ion balance, complemented, where appropriate, by weak-acid dilution and multicompartment redistribution.

Reframing organic anions as bicarbonate precursors may obscure, rather than clarify, the mechanisms governing acid–base balance in clinical practice.
