A novel transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation electrode configuration for treatment of heavy menstrual bleeding: an open-label trial
Authors: Christopher J Czura, Angela C Weyand, Maureen K Baldwin, Michael Recht, Caroline G Benner, Kimiko Harada, Brooke L Wade, Meghan Luellen, Michael J Darrow, Melanie A McWade, Alejandro Covalin, Navid Khodaparast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrical stimulation of the cervical vagus nerve reduces traumatic blood loss in models of soft tissue injury. This pathway, termed the neural tourniquet, can be accessed with a transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation (tAN) targeting vagus and trigeminal nerve branches on and around the ear. The cymba concha is the most targeted dermatome to activate the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN); however due to variable ear anatomy, participants can report electrode discomfort leading to reduced treatment compliance and device usability. This study examined a novel electrode configuration to improve hemostasis during menstruation and device usability.MethodsThis open-label pilot trial (NCT06814028) sought to determine whether the use of tAN reduced menstrual blood loss in women with heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB). The device included a novel electrode configuration through which the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN) was targeted posterior to the auricle, near the mastoid process. Participants with a history of heavy menstrual bleeding gave informed consent to participate in an IRB-approved, decentralized clinical trial. Participants were followed for three consecutive menstrual cycles, during which estimated daily blood loss using a validated pictorial blood loss assessment chart (PBAC) and menstrual symptoms were recorded at the end of each menstruation. During the first menstruation, baseline PBAC and menstrual symptom scores were measured without the use of tAN. During the second menstruation, participants self-administered two 1-h sessions of tAN daily throughout menstruation; during the third menstruation, participants self-administered one 2-h session of tAN daily throughout menstruation. The PBAC was also used to calculate the duration of each menstruation, defined by the use of menstrual products. Student’s paired T-test was used to compare mean outcome scores to baseline.ResultsUse of the novel electrode design to deliver tAN was associated with lower PBAC scores and reduced duration of menstruation as compared with baseline. Notably, device usability surveys of participants revealed high device satisfaction scores, including comfort of the novel electrode configuration.ConclusionsThese pilot results suggest that stimulating the ABVN posterior to the auricle is effective, safe and feasible for reducing menstrual blood loss in women with HMB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42234-026-00203-1.Keywords: Heavy menstrual bleeding, Dysmenorrhea, Transcutaneous, Neuromodulation, Medical devices, Wearable, Vagus nerve, Pictorial blood assessment chart, Menstrual symptoms, Clinical trial

Background

Over 10 million American adolescents and adults experience heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) (Division of Blood Disorders n.d.). Menstrual blood loss requiring changing pads/tampons every two hours or less, requiring use of more than one pad/tampon at a time, frequent passing of clots or flooding events, or periods lasting more than 7 days are leading indications of HMB (Sriprasert et al. 2017). HMB adversely affects social interactions, family life, school/work productivity, energy, mood, and sexual health (Henry and Filoche 2023; Schoep et al. 2019; Weyand et al. 2022; Oliveira., et al. 2024; Ponzo et al. 2022). Several different conditions can cause HMB, inlcuding underlying bleeding disorders such as von Willebrand disease, uterine conditions (e.g., submucous fibroid, endometrial polyp), and ovulatory dysfunction. Therapeutic strategies depend on the underlying cause, treatment preferences, and pregnancy intentions. Current treatments are frequently not tolerated or acceptable, creating an unmet medical need for non-invasive and non-pharmaceutical solutions (Le Guen et al. 2021).

Non-invasive neuromodulation strategies that engage vagal pathways have emerged as potential approaches for regulating hemostasis. Transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation (tAN) is delivered via a wearable device positioned on and around the ear and engages both the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN) and branches of the trigeminal nerve. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that electrical stimulation of the vagus nerve can reduce bleeding times and shed blood volume in models of soft tissue injury, including in the setting of hemophilia A (Czura et al. 2010; Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023), implicating an efferent vagal mechanism often referred to as the “neural tourniquet.”

Recent translational studies have shown that tAN can modulate platelet phenotype in healthy human subjects (NCT05977946) (Blanc et al. 2024; Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2024). In a separate open-label clinical study enrolling women with heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB; NCT06064851), use of tAN during menstruation was associated with significant reductions in menstrual blood loss and improvements in menstrual symptoms (Czura et al. 2025).

In prior clinical studies, tAN was delivered using an electrode positioned within the cymba concha to target the ABVN. While this configuration was generally effective, usability limitations were reported by some participants, including challenges with electrode placement in individuals with smaller or variable ear anatomy and difficulty integrating twice-daily stimulation sessions into daily routines (Czura et al. 2025). These observations highlighted the need to optimize both electrode placement and treatment implementation.

Anatomically, the ABVN innervates multiple auricular territories, including regions within and adjacent to the external auditory canal and areas posterior to the auricle (Watanabe et al. 2016; Silva and Dorsher 2014). Although the cymba concha has most commonly been targeted in auricular stimulation studies, converging evidence suggests that stimulation at the mastoid process provides an effective alternative site for engaging ABVN fibers(Verma et al. 2021; Thompson et al. 2021; Gerges et al. 2024; Percin et al. 2024). Electrical stimulation at the mastoid has also been shown to increase heart rate variability and reduce the frequency of episodic migraine and the symptoms of opioid withdrawal (Percin et al. 2024; Juan et al. 2017; Greenwald et al. 2025).

Given the neuroanatomy and the apparent functional equivalence of these two sites during ABVN stimulation, we assessed the therapeutic potential of tAN therapy when delivered to the mastoid process. Participants with HMB were recruited into a prospective, open-label, decentralized pilot to determine whether this new electrode configuration could effectively decrease menstrual blood loss and other menstrual symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as an open-label, single-arm, decentralized clinical trial. The protocol was approved by a central institutional review board (IRB; WCG, Princeton, NJ USA). The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06814028) and conducted between February and August 2025.

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether the use of transcutaneous auricular neurostimulation (tAN), when delivered at the anterior tragus (ATN) and mastoid process (ABVN), would result in reduced menstrual blood loss. Secondary objectives were to determine whether use of tAN is associated with improved menstrual symptoms, and to determine usability of the new electrode design. All participants were followed for three consecutive menstruations (Fig. 1). Baseline data were collected during the first menstruation (M1), during which tAN was not administered. In the second menstruation (M2), tAN was delivered for one hour twice per day during menstruation only, as described previously (Czura et al. 2025). During the third menstruation (M3), tAN was delivered for two hours once per day during menstruation only. This second stimulation paradigm was chosen as participants in the previous pilot reported the twice per day stimulation paradigm presented a burden on daily activities (Czura et al. 2025).

Clinical study design diagram. A total of 11 participants were recruited who provided data for analysis. Dots along timeline indicate collection of patient-reported outcomes: PBAC was recorded daily, while CMSS and RAND-36 were collected on the final day of each menstrual episode

Participants

Participants with heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) were recruited into this study from a pool of unscreened participants from a previous study (Czura et al. 2025). The menorrhagia screening tool (MST) was used during enrollment to assess a potential participant's history of heavy menstrual bleeding (https://www.cdc.gov/female-blood-disorders/media/pdfs/MenorrhagiaforTesting-508.pdf). Inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Appendix 1. Processes for obtaining informed consent, enrollment, and electronic data collection were conducted as described previously (Czura et al. 2025).

Outcome measures

Baseline data collected included self-reported medical histories and demographics; concomitant medication reviews were conducted monthly during the study. Pictorial Blood Loss Assessment Chart (PBAC) scores were collected daily during all menstruations, and menstrual symptoms and health-related quality of life were assessed on the final day of each menstruation using the Cox Menstrual Symptom Scale (CMSS) and the RAND-36, respectively (Czura et al. 2025). A device usability questionnaire was completed on the final day of the third study menstruation, as described previously (Czura et al. 2025). Usage logs were retrieved from returned devices and analyzed for compliance with the clinical trial protocol and for mean applied stimulation intensities.

Pictorial blood loss assessment chart

The primary outcome measure was the PBAC score, which is a validated, semi-quantitative tool to measure menstrual blood loss by comparing used menstrual products with graded illustrations of tampons and pads (Higham et al. 1990; Spence et al. 2021). Scores for graded menstrual products, flooding events and clots were summed for a total PBAC score per menstruation as described previously (Czura et al. 2025). A total PBAC score of > 100 correlates to 80 mL of blood over the duration of menstruation, which is the classical definition of heavy menstrual bleeding (Wyatt et al. 2001; Magnay et al. 2020). The number and size of clots were also recorded daily and included in the PBAC score calculation. Participants with a baseline PBAC > 150 were advanced to the treatment cycles. Duration of menstruation was derived from the daily PBAC scores as described previously (Czura et al. 2025). The type and brand of pads and tampons used by participants were not controlled in this study, but participants were asked to use the same product types during each of the three study menstruations.

Cox menstrual symptom scale and RAND-36

Menstrual symptoms were assessed on the final day of menstruation using the Cox Menstrual Symptom Scale (CMSS), which assessed frequency and severity of 18 symptoms associated with dysmenorrhea (Cox and Meyer 1978). The symptoms were organized into three domains, as described previously (Czura et al. 2025). In addition, a general health-related quality of life assessment was conducted using the RAND 36-Item Short Form Health Survey 1.0 (similar to Short Form 36 Health Survey; SF-36), as described previously (Czura et al. 2025; https://www.rand.org/health-care/surveys_tools/mos/36-item-short-form/scoring.html).

Device usability and compliance

At the end of the study, participants were asked to rate device satisfaction and usability. Satisfaction with the device was graded on a scale of 0–40, with 0–13 considered low satisfaction; 14–27 moderate satisfaction; and 28–40 high satisfaction. Usability was rated on a scale of 0–12 for use of the earpiece and ambulatory factors, as described in detail previously (Czura et al. 2025). Participants scored use of the Patient Controller on a scale of 0–16 (Czura et al. 2025). Usage logs were retrieved from devices returned after participants exited the study and analyzed for compliance with the clinical trial protocol and mean applied stimulation intensity. Compliance data were used to perform a responder analysis. For this analysis, PBAC responders were defined as achieving ≥ 20%, as determined previously (Lukes et al. 2010). The same 20% response definition was applied to the CMSS, which does not have a defined minimal clinically important difference. Compliance was defined as application of ≥ 80% of the anticipated stimulation, consistent with conventional pharmacology definitions (Dunbar-Jacob and Mortimer-Stephens 2001). The total number of PBAC or CMSS responders were calculated for both compliant and non-compliant menstruations.

Intervention

tAN was performed using a standardized protocol following international guidelines (Farmer et al. 2021). The Patient Controller used in this study was the Sparrow Link® (derived from the Sparrow Ascent) connected via a cable to an earpiece with integrated electrodes posterior to the tragus and at the mastoid process Fig. 2) (Tirado et al. 2022; Jenkins et al. 2021). Stimulation characteristics include an independent dual-frequency biphasic waveform with 30 Hz at the mastoid process (ABVN; Region 1) and 100 Hz at the temporal mandibular region (ATN; Region 2), pulse width of 250 μs, and a duty cycle of 5 min ON followed by 10 s OFF, as described previously (Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023; Czura et al. 2025; Tirado et al. 2022).The stimulation intensities (milliamps; mA) were set to 0 mA for both regions. Participants were instructed to increase the intensity in each region until the stimulation was perceivable but not painful by activating the appropriate (up or down) button.

The Region 1 electrode is positioned in the cymba concha to stimulate the ABVN at 30 Hz, while the Region 2 electrode is placed posterior to the tragus to stimulate the ATN at 100 Hz. The return electrode is positioned anterior to the auricle, and a lead connects the electrode to the external pulse generator (adapted from Czura et al. (Czura et al. 2025)). Experimental procedures

The first use of menstrual products was defined the first day of menstruation, and the last day of menstruation was defined as the last day those products were used. After baseline menstruation, participants were shipped a Sparrow Link Patient Controller, Cable, and Mastoid Earpieces. Participants were trained via video conference on device use. To determine whether tAN was associated with reduced menstrual blood loss, participants were instructed to use two different tAN dosing paradigms across two treatment menstruations. During the second menstruation (M2), participants applied an electrode to the left ear and self-administered two one-hour sessions per day; the first stimulation session was delivered in the morning and the second in the evening. After each one-hour session, stimulation concluded, and the Patient Controller was turned off and disconnected. The earpiece was removed and discarded. During the third menstruation (M3), participants applied an electrode to the left ear and self-administered one two-hour session of tAN at a convenient time of day as determined by the individual participant. After each two-hour session, stimulation concluded, and the Patient Controller was turned off and disconnected. The earpiece was removed and discarded.

Statistical analysis

A previous trial demonstrated that tAN, using a different electrode configuration, reduced PBAC scores by 54% in participants with HMB of unknonwn cause (Czura et al. 2025). For a statistical significance level of p < 0.01, 9 participants provided 90% power for detecting a 40% reduction in menstrual blood loss with 20% variability. All participants who received devices and returned data were included in the analysis. Baseline menstruation scores were compared to treatment menstruation scores with the paired Student’s T test.

Most participants were in their 30 s, were Black or of mixed race, had a body mass index of 30, and reported a typical duration of menstruation as 7 days. Changes in menstruation

The mean baseline PBAC score was 513 ± 94 (n = 11). The mean PBAC score during M2 was 366 ± 37, 29% lower than baseline (p = 0.06; Fig. 4). The mean PBAC score during M3 was 268 ± 51, 48% lower than baseline (p = 0.03); the M3 PBAC scores were also significantly lower than M2 PBAC scores (p = 0.02; Fig. 4). During M2, duration of menstruation was reduced by 10% as compared with baseline (p = 0.08; Fig. 4). During M3, duration of menstruation was reduced by 21% as compared with baseline (p = 0.003); there was no significant difference in duration of menstruation in M3 vs. M2 (p = 0.07; Fig. 4). Notably, three participants reported PBAC scores < 100 in M3, and therefore did not experience HMB while using tAN during this menstruation. Changes in menstrual symptoms

To determine whether the use of tAN could alleviate menstrual symptoms, participants completed the CMSS at the end of each menstruation. In M2, participants experienced a reduction in overall CMSS scores of 31% as compared with M1 (baseline = 59 ± 8 vs. M2 = 41 ± 6; p = 0.032), a 34% reduction in symptom severity score (baseline = 29 ± 4 vs. M2 = 19 ± 3; p = 0.025), and a 28% reduction in symptom frequency score (baseline = 30 ± 4 vs. M2 = 22 ± 3; p = 0.042). In M3, participants experienced an overall reduction of 68% in CMSS scores as compared with M1 (baseline = 59 ± 8 vs. M3 = 19 ± 4; p < 0.001), a 65% reduction in symptom severity score (baseline = 29 ± 4 vs. M3 = 10 ± 2; p < 0.001) and a 71% reduction in symptom frequency score (baseline = 30 ± 4 vs. M3 = 9 ± 2; p < 0.001). CMSS scores for overall symptoms (p = 0.02) and frequency of symptoms (p = 0.004) were also lower in M3 than in M2. CMSS domains assessing pain, gastrointestinal discomfort, and neurocognitive issues were also reduced in both M2 and M3 as compared with baseline (Table 2).

Changes in health-related quality of life

To determine whether the use of tAN could improve health-related quality of life, participants completed the RAND-36 at the end of each menstruation. In M2, only the RAND-36 scores for social functioning were significantly improved (baseline = 68 ± 4 vs. 84 ± 7; p = 0.02). In M3, RAND-36 scores for role limitations due to emotional problems, pain, and general health were significantly improved as compared with M1 (Table 2). Notably, participants reported spending 69% less extra time in bed during M3 (baseline = 9.4 ± 2.2 h vs. tAN = 2.6 + 1.5 h, p = 0.001; data not shown).

Device usability and compliance

Treatment adherence, accuracy of device use, and long-term engagement with therapy are critical determinants of treatment outcomes. Intuitive and ergonomically designed devices are particularly important for technologies designed for use at home (Czura et al. 2025; Center for Devices, Radiological Health Applying human factors usability engineering to medical devices. 2024). The 11 participants in this study reported mean (± SE) satisfaction scores of 37 ± 1 on a scale of 0–40, with all individual participants assigning a high satisfaction score for their experience using the tAN device. Usability of the device in terms of ambulatory factors (reduced blood loss, ability to wear the device while performing normal daily activities, and ability to integrate the device into daily routines) received a mean ± SE score of 11 ± 0.3 on a scale of 0–12, with all participants reporting a high satisfaction score. Usability of the Patient Controller received a mean ± SE score of 15 ± 0.5 on a scale of 0–16, with all participants reporting a high satisfaction score of 7. Usability of the earpiece received a mean ± SE score of 11 ± 0.4 on a scale of 0–12, with only one participant reporting a moderate satisfaction score of 8.

In conventional pharmacology, patients are considered adherent with use of a prescribed therapy if they have taken 80% of the medication (Dunbar-Jacob and Mortimer-Stephens 2001). Device use logs recorded the total number of minutes per day each participant applied tAN. Logs from 10 of the devices used in this study were available for analysis; the log from one device was accidentally deleted before it could be exported for analysis. Two participants failed to apply at least 80% of the anticipated stimulation in M2, and two different subjects missed this threshold in M3. One participant did not meet the 80% threshold in either menstruation, with the device log showing no use during M3. All participants, regardless of their compliance to protocol, are included in the intent to treat analysis. The low number of non-adherent participants in each treatment cycle precluded a dose–response analysis. A responder analysis revealed that, in 14/20 (70%) menstruations, participants were ≥ 80% compliant with the stimulation protocol. Of those 14, n = 10 (71%) experienced a reduction of PBAC scores ≥ 20%. Of the six that were not compliant with the stimulation protocol, two (33%) reported a reduction in PBAC scores ≥ 20%. Compliant participants experienced ≥ 20% reduction in CMSS scores in 11/14 (79%) menstruations, while non-compliant participants experienced ≥ 20% reduction in CMSS scores in 4/6 (66%) menstruations. Participants applied a mean of 1.5 ± 0.5 mA on the mastoid electrode, and 1.6 ± 0.6 mA on the outer electrode during M2. During M3, participants applied a mean of 1.9 ± 0.2 mA on the mastoid electrode, and 2.0 ± 0.2 mA on the outer electrode. The differences between stimulation intensities used in M2 and M3 are statistically significant (Table 3), but these differences do not correlate with changes in PBAC or CMSS scores (data not shown).

Adverse events

One (1) device-related adverse event (AE) was recorded in this study in one participant who reported mild skin irritation and sensitivity due to the adhesiveness of the electrodes; the symptoms resolved without medical attention and thus was considered Grade 1 (mild) symptoms according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (Basch et al. 2014). No severe AEs (SAEs) were reported during the study period.

Discussion

This open-label, decentralized pilot study demonstrated the feasibility and promising therapeutic efficacy of using tAN with a novel electrode configuration targeting the ABVN at the mastoid process rather than at the cymba concha. The primary endpoint in the study is the mean reduction in PBAC scores during tAN treatment as compared with baseline. The PBAC is a validated semi-quantitative assessment of menstrual blood loss. A prior study with nearly 300 women concluded that a 22% reduction in perceived menstrual blood loss was considered clinically meaningful. (Lukes et al. 2010). In this study, tAN was associated with clinically meaningful reduction in both treatment months: 29% reduction during M2, and 48% reduction in M3. The CMSS revealed that participants experienced significant improvements in the frequency and severity of menstrual symptoms, including pain, as well as spending nearly 70% less extra time in bed due to menstrual symptoms. These promising findings support further development of tAN targeting the ABVN at the mastoid process for improving menstrual health.

In a prior open-label study, some participants reported the cymba concha electrode presented some difficulty or discomfort in use, and that the twice-daily stimulation sessions were not easily integrated into daily routines (Czura et al. 2025). This study protocol was developed to improve both of these use factors. To avoid creating multiple confounding factors by changing both stimulation location and stimulation paradigm at once, we asked participants to apply tAN twice per day in the first therapy menstruation (M2) as per the previous study (Czura et al. 2025). In M3, we sought to demonstrate improved usability by changing the stimulation paradigm to once per day for 2 h. In this study, as well as the prior study, the reduction in PBAC scores exceeded 22%, the magnitude of reduction previously defined as clinically meaningful (Czura et al. 2025; Lukes et al. 2010). These results suggest that stimulating the ABVN at the mastoid process can impart effects similar to cymba concha stimulation. These observations support either single 2 h therapy sessions per day or one-hour sessions twice per day, improving usability through flexibility of daily use. Effects on duration of menstruation, menstrual symptoms and health-related quality of life scores are also similar between the mastoid and cymba concha electrodes (Czura et al. 2025). Nonetheless, this study identified some differences between PBAC and CMSS scores in the two menstruations during which tAN was applied: notably, PBAC scores were reduced by 29% in M2 (p = 0.06) and by 48% in M3 (p = 0.03); and overall CMSS scores were reduced by 31% in M2 (p = 0.03) and 68% in M3 (p < 0.001). There are several potential explanations for the differences in PBAC and CMSS scores observed between the two menstruations in this trial, including differences in stimulation paradigms, an accumulation of effect over multiple menstrual cycles, and influences of circadian rythym.

Reduction of menstrual bleeding is hypothesized to be due to activation of the neural tourniquet, an efferent function of the vagus nerve that modifies platelet phenotype (creating primed platelets) and improves hemostasis (Czura et al. 2010, 2025; Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023, 2024; Blanc et al. 2024). Preclinical descriptions of the neural tourniquet describe VNS paradigms that include 30 s bursts of 5 Hz stimulation every 5 min for 30 min (Czura et al. 2010) or constant voltage at 30 Hz applied for 5 min (Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023). VNS paradigms have been shown to prime platelets by increasing intracellular calcium levels, which renders the platelets more responsive to pro-coagulant triggers (Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023). These paradigms were applied in the context of soft tissue injury (partial ear or tail resections) and reduced bleeding times and shed blood volumes by 40–75% (Czura et al. 2010; Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2023). We noted that these hemorrhage models created bleeding events that reached hemostasis within minutes, whereas in HMB, blood loss continues over several days. We therefore hypothesized that, in HMB, platelets primed in a single tAN session would be rapidly integrated into clots forming at areas of uterine bleeding, and thus additional stimulation sessions would be required to maintain adequate numbers of primed platelets to maintain hemostasis. Interestingly, the results presented here suggest that a single, two-hour session of tAN per day has more profound effects on PBAC scores than does tAN when delivered in one-hour stimulations twice per day. Although tAN (30 Hz ABVN and 100 Hz ATN; both 250μs pulse width) has recently been shown to similarly prime platelets in a healthy human volunteer study, only 30 min of stimulation was delivered without further parametic testing (Blanc et al. 2024; Bravo-Iñiguez et al. 2024). It remains possible that longer stimulation durations create more primed platelets or further enhance the procoagulant activity of platelets. Longer continuous stimulation sessions may also alter autonomic regulation of unterine contractility, enhance centrally mediated antinociception, and/or influence brain neuroplasticity, leading to the reduced CMSS scores in M3 reported here (Sato et al. 1996; Bonaz 2024; Schambra and Hays 2025). Previous studies have examined whether the duration of transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) can affect outcomes. These studies have typically focused on short (< 60 s to 5 min) durations of stimulation, or long treatment cycles with multiple treatment sessions per week(s), with ambiguous results (Thompson et al. 2021; Bömmer et al. 2024; Schmidt et al. 2025; Giraudier et al. 2024). Whether different frequencies or pulse widths differentially affect platelet or bleeding phenotype also remain unknown.

The differences in effect between M2 and M3 may be the result of the order in which the different stimulation paradigms were applied. It may also be ther result of an accumulation of effect over time, resulting in increased efficacy over the two therapy menstruations. Wang et al. performed a study of the effects of taVNS on menstrual cramp pain as measured by the CMSS (Wang et al. 2023). Participants received 30 min/day of taVNS at 1 Hz over 10 consecutive days between menstruations. The investigators reported ~ 50% reduction in CMSS scores in two menstruations following the use of taVNS, suggesting a persistence of effect on menstrual symptoms over time. Additional rounds of dosing may further improve menstrual symptoms, as has been shown with VNS in, for example, drug-resistant epilepsy (Kostov et al. 2022). Moving forward, delivering tAN for consecutive cycles, during the luteal and menstrual phases, may demonstrate sustained or gradual improvements in menstrual symptoms. Thus, future treatment dosing studies are warranted.

Other reasons that may explain the observed differences include circadian rhythm or light/dark cycles. Vagus nerve activity changes throughout the day, and the effects of VNS on heart rate variability differ when stimulation is delivered at different times of day (Smets et al. 2022; Kentish et al. 2019; Geng et al. 2022). In M2, participants were directed to stimulate in the morning and evening, but in M3 were given freedom to choose time of stimulation. Device logs revealed the majority of participants applied stimulation during the afternoon or evening, with only one participant choosing the morning to stimulate (data not shown). Since many reproductive hormones have endogenous circadian rhythms, the timing of tAN stimulation may have a variable impact on gonadotropin stimulation or receptor modulation (Rahman et al. 2019). While the vagus nerve carries afferent and efferent signals between the brain stem and the ovaries and uterus, it is not known whether neuromodulation strategies targeting the vagus nerve influence hormone levels in circulation or in the uterus (Collins et al. 1999; Vieyra-Valdez et al. 2020). The intensity of stimulation applied also differed between M2 and M3 in this study. We found no correlation between intensity and change in either PBAC or CMSS scores.

The previous trial of tAN in HMB used an earpiece targeting the ABVN in the cymba concha. While participants generally reported high usability scores for that earpiece, it was noted that 25% of the participants reported only moderate satisfaction scores, and one participant withdrew from the study due to dissatisfaction with the cymba concha electrode (Czura et al. 2025). Those observations led to the redesigned earpiece tested here. Usability of the tAN device was rated favorably overall among participants in this study, who also reported a preference for once per day stimulation. Usability was assessed only at study exit, and not after M2, so whether these results reflect the change in electrode position, frequency of daily stimulation, or both, remains unknown. In contrast to the previous trial, here only one participant (< 10% of the study population) reported a moderate satisfaction score for the earpiece. The use of tAN during menstruation was safe over the duration of this study, with adverse events limited to minor skin irritation resulting from electrode placement in four of the participants. These observations are consistent with previous findings (Czura et al. 2025; Tirado et al. 2022; Jenkins et al. 2021). This study, like the previous tAN HMB study, was conducted as a decentralized trial, and participants did not visit with a healthcare provider for study-related activities (Czura et al. 2025). Nonetheless, participants easily learned to use the device and study assessments remotely, and reported satisfaction with the portability of the device and flexibility in therapy session duration. No adverse events related to thrombosis have been reported in studies of auricular neurostimulation, including implanted VNS devices (Kim et al. 2022). Indeed, an implanted VNS system was recently approved by the FDA to reduce upper extremity motor deficits and improve motor function during rehabilitation therapy in chronic ischemic stroke patients, a population that may be at increased risk of thrombotic events (Dawson et al. 2021). The non-invasive nature of tAN overcomes many of the shortcomings of traditional VNS systems, including the expense and risks associated with surgical implantation (Kalagara et al. 2025).

The current study is not without limitations that should be addressed in future studies. A sample size of 11 is generally regarded as relatively small compared with pharmaceutical studies. Participants were followed for only one baseline menstrual episode, compared with 2–3 episodes in most trials (Bryant-Smith et al. 2018), and use of concomitant therapies was heterogenous. We did not rule out underlying pathology that might have contributed to HMB, which suggests that tAN may be effective for multiple etiologies. While the use of the PBAC is generally recognized as having high utility in clinical trials, it has been validated for treatment effect in only one population with HMB (Larsen et al. 2013). The PBAC is known to underestimate blood loss for highly saturated menstrual products, yet has a good correlation with percent change in reported blood loss (Mackenzie et al. 2024). Secondary outcomes included menstrual symptoms and health-related quality of life, assessed by the CMSS and RAND-36, respectively. While these instruments have been validated, minimal clinically important differences have not been reported for the HMB population (Cox and Meyer 1978; https://www.rand.org/health-care/surveys_tools/mos/36-item-short-form/scoring.html). This was an open-label study with baseline measurements taken during one menstruation before using the device and then in two subsequent menstruations with the use of tAN. As a pilot investigation, the design allowed for preliminary evaluation of both treatment effect and safety profile of a novel therapy, but the absence of a proper control group limits the ability to distinguish true therapeutic benefit from placebo effect or natural variability. The short duration of the study was an additional limitation, as was lack of bloodwork to directly assess the effects of tAN on laboratory hemostasis in these populations. Future studies should include larger sample sizes, blinded and sham-controlled arms, treatment during multiple consecutive menstruations, stratification by bleeding etiology, and incorporation of laboratory-based assessments of coagulation and platelet function.

Conclusion

This is the first study of a transcutaneous auricular neurostimulator targeting the ABVN at the mastoid process using PBAC scores as a primary outcome. Across all study participants, tAN was safe, well-tolerated, and delivered clinically meaningful reductions in menstrual blood loss, menstrual symptoms, and quality of life. Importantly, this electrode configuration was well tolerated and found to be highly usable in this participant population. tAN activates vagal and trigeminal networks that modulate platelet phenotype and lead to improved hemostasis. This treatment modality offers a potential alternative to medications or surgery for women with HMB. A future FDA pivotal trial will be required to demonstrate safety and efficacy of tAN to treat menorrhagia. Additional studies of tAN as a non-pharmaceutical, non-invasive approach to improve hemostasis in other bleeding disorders, as well as in surgery and trauma, are ongoing.
