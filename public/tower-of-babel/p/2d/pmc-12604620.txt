Sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy with cardiomyopathy presenting as advanced heart failure despite autologous stem cell transplantation: a case report
Authors: Chen Guo, Jia-Hao Dou, Shan Zhao, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNemaline myopathy is a rare neuromuscular disorder characterized by severe muscle weakness and dyskinesia, predominantly affecting skeletal muscles. Notably, significant cardiac involvement, especially in the sporadic late-onset subtype, is exceptionally uncommon.Case SummaryA 31-year-old female patient presented to our hospital with a 6-month history of chest tightness and progressive peripheral oedema for 10 days, superimposed on a 2-year course of proximal muscle weakness. Four months prior, she received a diagnosis of sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy complicated by monoclonal gammopathy of uncertain significance, followed by autologous stem cell transplantation 2 months ago. The patient presented with advanced heart failure, characterized by dilated cardiac chambers and impaired ventricular systolic function, as evidenced by an echocardiographically derived left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25%, an elevated pro-brain natriuretic peptide of 4673 pg/ml, and polyserosal effusions. Extensive late gadolinium enhancement in the ventricular walls on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed the suspected diagnosis of heart failure secondary to sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy-related cardiomyopathy. Following active treatment with guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, the patient was discharged in an improved clinical condition, with her LVEF increasing to 57% at the 6-month follow-up.DiscussionThis case report highlights that sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy may progress to severe cardiomyopathy and even heart failure, notwithstanding autologous stem cell support. Current evidence supports the application of standard heart failure medications as a rational therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Nemaline myopathies constitute a group of rare neuromuscular disorders with variable onset, clinically characterized by progressive muscle weakness and the presence of nemaline rods within muscle fibres.1 Most cases of nemaline myopathy manifest during infancy or childhood, but a few cases present as an adult-onset variant, termed sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy (SLONM), which is an acquired myopathy with a partial autoimmune basis.2 Cardiac involvement is not infrequent in patients with nemaline myopathy, manifested by a variety of cardiac abnormalities (including cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and heart failure).3 In contrast, SLONM rarely involves the myocardium, and most cases of cardiac involvement are mild. We herein report a case that broadens the spectrum of myocardial involvement in SLONM and provides evidence for a viable therapeutic strategy.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old female patient was admitted to our hospital in May 2024 with proximal muscle weakness lasting for more than 2 years, progressive chest tightness and dyspnoea over the past 6 months, and lower extremity oedema for the last 10 days. Four months prior, she had been diagnosed with SLONM and monoclonal gammopathy of uncertain significance (MGUS). Two months ago, she underwent autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) following high-dose melphalan therapy, with subsequent improvement in muscle weakness. Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no family history of neuromuscular diseases.

On physical examination, the patient exhibited restricted activity, facial oedema, occasional moist rales, and diminished breath sounds (predominantly right-sided) in the lungs. Abdominal distension with positive shifting dullness and severe lower limb oedema were noted. Cardiac examination revealed an enlarged cardiac border and tachycardia (105 beats per minute), without abnormal heart sounds or pathological murmurs. Neurological assessment demonstrated muscle weakness, with Medical Research Council grades: shoulders (III), elbows (III), wrists (V), fingers (V), hips (II), knees (III), ankles (V), and toes (V).

Initial laboratory findings from 4 months prior revealed monoclonal gammopathy, with serum IgG at 17.3 g/L, κ-free light chain at 1540 mg/dL, λ-free light chain at 605 mg/dL, and IgG-κ restriction identified on serum immunofixation electrophoresis. Cardiac markers were slightly elevated: creatine kinase was 81 U/L, troponin T was 0.054 ng/ml, and proBNP was 629.4 pg/ml. Normal results were noted for myositis antibodies, thyroid function, tumour markers, human immunodeficiency virus, cytokines, rheumatoid factors, antinuclear antibodies, serum amyloid protein, urinary light chain, and urinary immunofixation electrophoresis. Current blood tests revealed elevated troponin T (91.10 pg/ml), proBNP (4673 pg/ml), and D-dimer (2070 ng/ml), alongside decreased albumin (32.5 g/L) and potassium (2.9 mmol/L). Serum immunoglobulin analysis showed a κ-light chain level of 3.77 g/L; serum protein electrophoresis demonstrated α1 globulin at 5.2% and γ globulin at 22.3%, with negative serum immunofixation electrophoresis.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the lower extremities showed bilateral muscle atrophy, with distinct muscle interstices evident on fat-suppressed sequences and heterogeneous increased signal intensity (Figure 1A). Functional ultrasound identified diffuse lesions in the rectus femoris, and electromyography further confirmed myogenic damage. Pulmonary function tests revealed moderate restrictive ventilatory dysfunction. Muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis exhibited pathological changes consistent with SLONM, characterized by the presence of sand-like nemaline rods within muscle fibres (Figure 1B). Bone marrow examination showed abnormal plasma cell proliferation (0.5% in smears; 0.06% by flow cytometry).

(A) Lower extremity magnetic resonance images showing muscle atrophy with diffuse hyperintensities (arrows); (B) electron micrographs of the vastus lateralis revealing multifocal collagen fibres disarray with sarcoplasmic nemaline rods (arrows).

Her electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm, with QS pattern in leads V1–V4 and low voltage in limb leads (Figure 2A). Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed biatrial enlargement (left atrium, 44.5 × 62.3 mm; right atrium, 41 × 61 mm) and left ventricular dilation (46.8 × 74.2 mm) using M-mode method, reduced left ventricular systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25% by Simpson’s rule and a global longitudinal strain of −6.6% on speckle-tracking analysis, and mild-to-moderate pericardial effusion (Figure 2B). Ultrasound and computed tomography identified pleural and peritoneal effusion. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed enlarged chambers (left atrium, 46 mm × 77 mm; right atrium, 49 mm × 64 mm; left ventricular end-diastolic transverse diameter, 63 mm) and impaired ventricular systolic/diastolic function (LVEF, 22%; end-diastolic volume, 169 ml; end-systolic volume, 132 ml; stroke volume, 37 ml; cardiac output, 3.1 L/min; left ventricular mass index, 98 g/m2). Late gadolinium enhancement (gadobutrol 0.1 mmol/kg) demonstrated diffuse linear and patchy hyperenhancement in the left ventricle intramural region, with striated enhancement in the right ventricular wall (Figure 2C). Endomyocardial biopsy was deferred due to severe cardiac dysfunction and patient refusal.

(A) Electrocardiogram showing low voltage in limb leads and QS pattern in leads V1–V4; (B) echocardiogram revealing enlarged cardiac chambers and reduced ventricular systolic function; (C) cardiac magnetic resonance showing extensive late gadolinium enhancement in the ventricular wall (arrows).

Based on clinical features and findings, other aetiologies (mitochondriopathy, dermatomyositis, myotonic dystrophy, Fabry disease, cardiac amyloidosis, autoimmune myocarditis)4 were excluded, and heart failure secondary to SLONM was suspected. The patient was treated with heart failure medications, including sacubitril/valsartan (24/26 mg twice daily), metoprolol (23.75 mg once daily), empagliflozin (10 mg once daily), spironolactone (20 mg once daily), ivabradine (2.5 mg twice daily), tolvaptan (7.5 mg once daily), and intravenous loop diuretics, recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide, and levosimendan. Diaphragmatic stimulation was employed to manage respiratory muscle dysfunction and aid sputum expulsion. Ten days after initiation of treatment, she was discharged with improved chest tightness and dyspnoea, and significant resolution of oedema. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained free of overt heart failure symptoms, and LVEF had improved to 57%.

Discussion

This case exemplifies a unique instance distinct from previous reports, documenting a rare occurrence of advanced heart failure secondary to SLONM-related cardiomyopathy. SLONM is an acquired myopathy partially associated with MGUS or human immunodeficiency virus infection; the underlying mechanisms may involve autoimmune dysregulation and the toxic effects of monoclonal proteins.5 Myocardial infiltration in SLONM is less common, with only a small minority of patients presenting with severe cardiomyopathy. Due to the lack of cardiac magnetic resonance or endomyocardial biopsy, most cases cannot be conclusively diagnosed as SLONM-induced cardiomyopathy. Previous relevant cases are generally mild in severity and rarely require infrequent specialized treatment.3,4 Notable differences from previous reports in this case include the development of severe heart failure alongside muscle involvement; furthermore, despite improved muscle weakness post-ASCT, the manifestations of heart failure continued to progress.

Little is known regarding the medical management of SLONM-related heart failure, and the treatment of advanced heart failure remains challenging due to reduced responsiveness to pharmacological and device-based therapies.6 The treatment regimens for SLONM primarily include glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, chemotherapy, intravenous immunoglobulin, and ASCT. Among these, ASCT is frequently utilized for SLONM complicated by monoclonal proteinemia, with an efficacy rate of 70%–90%.5 Previous cases have suggested that combining high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell support7 or using chemotherapy alone for MGUS8 may potentially reverse heart failure in SLONM with MGUS. In this case, comprehensive analysis of melphalan-induced cardiotoxicity9 and the pathogenesis of SLONM indicates cardiac involvement is more likely attributable to SLONM itself rather than melphalan toxicity. This further demonstrates that chemotherapy combined with ACST may not halt the progression of SLONM-related cardiac damage, suggesting that the mechanisms of myocardial involvement in SLONM with MGUS differ from skeletal muscle involvement. It’s worth noting that early initiation of the optimal pharmacological therapy for heart failure may represent a more sensible approach.

Our case highlights that SLONM patients remain at risk of severe cardiomyopathy and heart failure even post-ASCT, warranting vigilance. Additionally, guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure is currently regarded as a rational therapeutic strategy.
