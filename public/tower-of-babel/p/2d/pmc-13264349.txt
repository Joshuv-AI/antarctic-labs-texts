Ensemble learning uncovers novel metabolomic biomarkers for early osteoporosis prediction in Tibetan plateau populations
Authors: Jiawei Yang, Tao Zhou, Haichen Zhang, Qiong Zhang, Lening Chen, Qianqian Xiao, Shusheng Luo, Labasangzhu, Dunyou, Danbalangjie, Weidong Hao, Xuetao Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis represents a prevalent metabolic bone disorder among middle-aged and elderly populations, with its prevention and early detection holding important implications for clinical practice and public health. While extensive research has characterized osteoporosis in low-altitude populations, plateau regions present unique challenges.MethodsThis study enrolled 177 adult residents from Lhasa, Tibet. Data collection included demographic characteristics, health profiles, and female reproductive parameters. Osteoporosis is defined by T-score ≤ −2.5 (for males >50 years and postmenopausal women) or Z-score ≤ −2 (for males ≤50 years and premenopausal women). Serum metabolomic profiling identified 3381 metabolites via HPLC-MS/MS. Predictive models were constructed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) and Random Forest (RF) ensemble learning algorithm, with pathway enrichment analysis performed in MetaboAnalyst (P < 0.05).ResultsOur cross-sectional analysis of 177 participants revealed 41 osteoporosis cases (23.2%), predominantly postmenopausal women (36/41). Baseline characteristics showed significant differences in age, gender, difficulty initiating sleep, and menopausal status across groups (all P < 0.05). The 9 metabolites screened by LASSO classification demonstrated exceptional predictive capacity for osteoporosis (AUC: 0.995), while 10 metabolites based on RF better predicted osteopenia progression (R2: 0.848). Z-score-based models outperformed T-score subgroups. Menopausal status and difficulty initiating sleep were identified as high-risk factors, and three metabolites—Dihydro-3-hydroxy-4,4-dimethyl-2(3H)-furanone, gamma-Glutamyltryptophan, and di-homo-gamma-linolenate—emerged as potential early indicators of bone loss. Pathway enrichment highlighted pyrimidine metabolism and related diseases as a potential therapeutic target for bone loss.ConclusionsThis research proposes a metabolomics-driven model for osteoporosis prediction, establishing an innovative biomarker framework for early clinical intervention in plateau areas.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a disease characterized by reduced bone mass and disruption of normal skeletal micro-architecture, representing one of the most prevalent metabolic bone disorders [1]. It impairs bone strength, heightening susceptibility to fractures, particularly in the spine and hip. Fracture risk escalates with age and is increasingly common among older adults. In the United States, over 2 million osteoporosis-related fractures occur annually, including more than 0.7 million clinical vertebral fractures and 0.3 million hip fractures, contributing to at least 0.5 million hospitalizations. These fractures incur prolonged disability, excess mortality, and substantial socioeconomic costs [2]. In China, osteoporosis affects 10.9 million males and 49.3 million females [3]. A cross-sectional study indicated that the incidence of osteopenia and osteoporosis in postmenopausal Tibetan women is 54.6% and 9.6%, respectively [4]. Notably, osteoporosis remains asymptomatic until a fracture occurs, so that only a small proportion of patients can be evaluated and diagnosed promptly.

Current clinical screening relies on bone mineral density (BMD) measurement via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [5], which detects osteopenia after significant microstructural damage. DXA estimates “areal” BMD using X-ray technology, expressed as standard deviations (SD) from the mean of young healthy adults (T-score) or age-matched populations (Z-score). Besides, many risk assessment tools for osteoporosis have been applied, including SCORE (Simple Calculated Osteoporosis Risk Estimation) [6], ORAI (Osteoporosis Risk Assessment Instrument) [7], OSIRIS (Osteoporosis Index of Risk) [8], and OST (Osteoporosis Self-Assessment Tool) [9]. There are also emerging approaches that integrate artificial intelligence (e.g., artificial neural networks, ANN) to enhance early prediction in fragility fracture patients [10]. However, about half of patients with osteopenia or even a normal BMD suffer fragility fractures [11]. There are still few biomarkers and models for predicting early osteoporosis based on individual metabolic dimensions.

Metabolomics is a field that focuses on comprehensive metabolite analysis in biological systems. It employs chromatography-mass spectrometry platforms to identify metabolic pathway end-products reflecting organismal metabolic states and disease-associated abnormalities [12]. Serum profiles have been applied to predict metabolic, vascular, respiratory, and oncologic diseases. A notable example was a deep residual multitask neural network developed for blood metabolome analysis using UK Biobank data, which successfully discerned metabolomic profiles across multiple diseases [13]. This demonstrated the power of advanced computational models to extract clinically relevant patterns from complex metabolomic data, a strategy aligned with our objective to identify specific metabolic signatures for osteoporosis in high-altitude populations.

Bone homeostasis is closely linked to systemic metabolic regulation. Amino acids, peptides, and related compounds serve as critical modulators of osteoblastic activation and skeletal development [14]. Mechanistic studies reveal that sulfur-containing amino acid (SAA) metabolism may contribute to endogenous acid load, potentially impairing osteoblast function and stimulating osteoclast activity, thereby accelerating bone resorption and mass reduction [15]. Metabolomic profiling further identified the “phenylalanine-tyrosine-tryptophan biosynthesis” axis as a novel pathway dysregulated in age-related osteoporosis [16], suggesting changes of essential biomolecules may influence skeletal health. These findings underscore the imperative to identify metabolomic signatures capable of predicting osteoporosis progression risks, a need that remains particularly acute and unaddressed for populations residing in high-altitude regions.

Current predictive frameworks mainly focus on the changes in bone mass of populations in low-altitude areas. A representative study included a random forest model based on the serum profiles of the Shanghai population [17], integrating 9 metabolites for osteopenia prediction (AUC: 0.801). However, there is a lack of applicable models for high-altitude populations. The unique environmental stressors at high altitudes—such as reduced oxygen levels, lower temperatures and increased ultraviolet radiation—may exacerbate bone loss, increasing the risk of osteoporosis [18]. To address this gap, this study leveraged a cross-sectional survey in Xizang, integrating BMD and serum metabolomics to identify specific metabolic signatures predictive of early osteopenia. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) and Random Forest (RF) will be employed to identify early-specific metabolic markers of osteoporosis. Pathway enrichment analyses will elucidate potential mechanistic links between metabolites and osteopenia. The outcomes aim to establish a risk prediction model for early osteoporosis in high-altitude populations, providing a scientific basis for the proposal of relevant intervention measures.

Methods

Study participants and basic information survey

This cross-sectional study, conducted in Lhasa from July to August 2023, initially enrolled 202 adult residents who provided written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they had a history of or current pathological fractures, major metabolic diseases known to affect bone metabolism (including diabetes mellitus, thyroid or parathyroid disorders, and chronic kidney disease), or were on medications influencing bone turnover (such as glucocorticoids, bisphosphonates, hormone replacement therapy, or anticonvulsants). After applying these criteria, 14 participants with current/past fractures and 11 cases with incomplete baseline data were excluded, resulting in 177 eligible subjects were retained for final analysis. Standardized protocols captured three domains: 1) Demographic characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, educational attainment, type of work, marital status, and per capital monthly household income); 2) Health profiles encompassing body mass index (BMI), BMD, difficulty initiating sleep (DIS), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and hyperuricemia; and 3) Female-specific reproductive parameters including menarche age, menopausal status, and menopause age (where applicable). The inclusion of middle-aged and older individuals (≥45 years) is clinically relevant as this is the period when bone loss accelerates, especially in women post-menopause, making early intervention most critical. This investigation was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Xizang University (2023SQ001).

Physical examinations and diagnostic criteria

Anthropometric parameters (height, weight) and blood pressure were measured using calibrated instruments. Venipuncture was performed after an 8 h fasting period to collect serum samples, which were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min within 1 h post-collection. BMD assessment at the distal left radius employed the Sunlight Miniomni ultrasound bone densitometer (BeamMed Ltd.), represented by T- and Z-score. Z-score evaluated BMD in males aged ≤50 years and premenopausal females, a score of ≤ −2 indicating osteoporosis. T-score was used for males >50 years and postmenopausal females, with a definition of osteoporosis ≤ −2.5 [19]. Hypertension diagnosis required sustained systolic/diastolic pressures ≥140/90 mmHg across triplicate measurements [20]. DIS was operationalized as latency-to-sleep onset >30 min [21].

Metabolomic profiling and analysis

Serum samples were processed using organic reagent precipitation and analyzed via high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS). Briefly, serum proteins were precipitated by mixing 50 μL of sample with 200 μL of pre-chilled methanol/acetonitrile solution (1:1, v/v) containing internal standards. The mixture was vortexed, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, and then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for analysis.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on a ACQUITY UHPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. A gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min as follows: 0-1 min, 2% B; 1-9 min, 2%-98% B; 9-12 min, 98% B; 12-12.1 min, 98%-2% B; 12.1-15 min, 2% B for re-equilibration. The eluent was introduced into a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. Source parameters were set as: spray voltage, ±3.8 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; sheath gas flow, 40 arb; aux gas flow, 10 arb. Full MS scans (m/z 70-1,050) were acquired at a resolution of 120,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS scans (dd-MS2) of the top 10 most intense ions at a resolution of 30,000. To ensure data quality and reproducibility, a pooled quality control (QC) sample, generated by mixing equal aliquots of all serum samples, was injected at the beginning of the run and after every 10 experimental samples throughout the analytical sequence. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of peak areas for metabolites in the QC samples was calculated, and only metabolites with an RSD <30% were retained for subsequent statistical analysis, ensuring analytical precision.

Raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak picking, alignment, and integration. Metabolites were putatively annotated by matching their accurate mass (mass tolerance <5 ppm) and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and mzCloud libraries. The confidence level for these annotations corresponds to Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds). A total of 3,381 metabolites passed QC and were used for downstream analysis (Table S1). For missing values present in some samples, we fill them with the minimum value (i.e., the minimum positive value) detected by the corresponding metabolite in all samples. Metabolites identified as significant predictors by the subsequent machine learning models were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using the Pathway Analysis module on the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). The Homo sapiens (human) pathway library from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was referenced, and metabolic pathways with a Holm-Bonferroni corrected P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

LASSO regression

To address collinearity among variables, a LASSO regression model was constructed to identify serum metabolic biomarkers for early osteoporosis. Linear regression analyses were performed to assess the relationship between independent variables and the dependent variable (T- and Z-score) using the R “tidyverse” package. Independent variables that exhibited significant linear changes (P < 0.05) were retained to construct the hierarchical and continuous LASSO models. In the hierarchical model, the normal group was set to 0 and the osteoporosis group was set to 1. The larger the coefficient (β), the stronger the relationship between this independent variable and osteoporosis. Conversely, the coefficient was positively correlated with the Z-/T-score in the continuous model used to predict osteopenia. The “glmnet” and “caret” packages were used to calculate the regularization parameter (λ) and mean square error (MSE) for the LASSO model and perform cross-validation. The “pROC” and “ggplot2” packages were used for image display in R v4.4.

The equation for LASSO model represented the linear predictor and was expressed as follows:Y=∑j=1nβjXj+Interceptwhere Y is the calculated value based on Z- or T-score, βj is the parameter without units, Xj is the corresponding independent variable, n is the number of independent variables.

Random forest regression

Random Forest regression was utilized to investigate the association between serum metabolomic profiles and bone mineral density (Z- and T-score) measurements. The analysis was conducted using R v 4.4 with the random forest package for model implementation, alongside dplyr for data manipulation and ggplot2 for visualization. The random forest was configured with 500 trees, and variable importance was assessed through percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE). Model performance was evaluated using mean squared error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2). All analyses employed a fixed random seed (123) to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data were double-entered and validated using EpiData 3.1, followed by statistical analysis in SPSS v27.0. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent Student's t-test; otherwise, they are reported as median (interquartile range) and analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Given the exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature of this initial analysis aimed at feature pre-screening for subsequent regularized machine learning algorithms (LASSO/RF), which inherently penalize complexity and reduce overfitting, multiple testing correction was not applied at this stage to avoid over-filtering potential signals model entry.

Screening of metabolic biomarkers

We analyzed the associations between 3381 metabolites and Z-/T-score using univariate linear regression. This methodological approach was selected to initially identify individual metabolites exhibiting significant relationships with the outcomes, providing a foundation for further multivariate or pathway-based investigations. Specifically, in the Z-score subset, which included 79 participants, 105 metabolites demonstrated significant associations (Table S2). Similarly, for the T-score subset comprising 123 individuals, 132 metabolites were identified as significant (Table S3). These metabolites contribute to a more targeted and efficient analytical pipeline.

Filter of markers for osteoporosis using hierarchical LASSO regression

In this study, hierarchical LASSO regression was employed to rigorously assess the associations among the 105 metabolites retained from prior analysis, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, and bone mineral density Z-scores. This approach effectively handled multicollinearity and identified the most pertinent variables for osteoporosis prediction. The optimal λ was determined to be 0.02857168, resulting in a parsimonious model incorporating 9 key variables. Remarkably, this model demonstrated exceptional discriminative ability, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.995 (Fig. 1A, Table 2). The strongest predictors were gamma-Glutamyltryptophan (β = −3.07 × 10−3), followed by 4-Epi-4-F3t-Neurop Dpa6 (β = 1.09 × 10−3) and Orotidylic acid (β = 5.70 × 10−4) (Table 2). In the T-score dataset, LASSO regression with an optimal λ = 0.05975626 identified 18 predictors, yielding an AUC of 0.944 for osteoporosis prediction (Fig. 1C–Table 2). As expected, the top-weighted variables were gender (β = 1.08), followed by DIS (β = 7.89 × 10−2), and 5-hexyl-2-huranhexanoic acid (β = 1.61 × 10−3) (Table 2). The model demonstrated an accuracy of 0.867 (95% CI: 0.784 - 0.927), detection rate of 0.612, and kappa statistic of 0.698, confirming strong predictive performance (Fig. 1D).

Identification of early markers of osteopenia based on LASSO regression

In pursuit of identifying early metabolic alterations linked to osteopenia for effective osteoporosis prevention, this investigation utilized Z- and T-scores as continuous variables in a LASSO linear regression framework applied to metabolite data. Cross-validation on the Z-score dataset was performed to optimize λ and MSE values (Fig. 2A). The optimal model, with λ = 0.04188124, retained 28 variables and achieved a high coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.818, indicating strong predictive performance for bone density variations (Table S4). The strongest predictors were carglumic acid (β = −2.964 × 10−3), gamma-glutamyltryptophan (β = 9.332 × 10−4), and octyl 4-methoxycinnamic acid (β = 7.797 × 10−4). Markers of osteoporosis predicted by random forest regression

To address the challenges of excessive factors and model complexity inherent in LASSO regression, we employed random forests as an alternative machine learning approach to reanalyze the dataset. It achieved a superior performance with an R2 value of 0.848 and MSE of 0.442 (Fig. 3A), outperforming the previous LASSO model (R2 = 0.818) that retained 28 variables. Enrichment analysis of metabolic biomarkers

Due to the limited overlap of biomarkers between the LASSO and random forest methods, which insufficiently captured the underlying disturbed metabolic pathways, we proceeded with a focused analysis using the LASSO model to identify conserved metabolites for further investigation. In the Z-score dataset, four overlapping metabolites were consistently identified: gamma-Glutamyltryptophan, 4-Epi-4-F3t-Neurop Dpa6, Orotidylic acid, and Dihydro-3-hydroxy-4,4-dimethyl- 2(3H)-furanone (Fig. 4A, Table S6). These metabolites were established as key biomarkers for early osteoporosis prediction, specifically in males aged ≤50 years and premenopausal females, highlighting their potential clinical relevance for at-risk populations. Discussion

This cross-sectional study analyzed serum metabolomic profiles and BMD data from 177 residents of Lhasa, intending to identify early diagnostic biomarkers for osteoporosis. By integrating metabolomics with ensemble learning algorithms, we elucidated metabolic heterogeneity during osteoporosis progression and assessed its predictive value. In the Z-score subgroup, both stratified LASSO regression and random forest models demonstrated outstanding predictive performance (AUC = 0.995, R2 = 0.848). Compared to the T-score subgroup, this accuracy may be due to the more stable endocrine and metabolic homeostasis of the population in this subgroup (comprising males under 50 years and premenopausal females). Furthermore, we identified three key metabolites that show strong potential as biomarkers for predicting the transition from early bone loss to clinical osteoporosis, providing valuable insights for early screening and intervention strategies.

Orotidylic acid emerged as a critical biomarker implicating pyrimidine metabolism dysregulation in early bone synthesis impairment. As the terminal intermediate in uridine monophosphate biosynthesis, this nucleotide plays essential roles in osteoblast proliferation via DNA/RNA synthesis [22,23]. Deficient pyrimidine metabolism may constrain osteoblast clonal expansion, thereby accelerating osteopenia. It was worth noting that among the 11 key metabolic markers in the T-score subgroup, benzoquinone acetic acid (BQA) was positively correlated with osteoporosis. BQA is an oxidized form of homogentisic acid. Elevated BQA levels exert osteotoxic effects through collagen-bound polymer deposition, potentially mediating cartilage damage or osteoporosis [24]. Furthermore, 5-Hydroxydec-7-enoylcarnitine (a medium-chain acylcarnitine) was also significantly associated with osteoporosis in the older subgroup. Many medium-chain acylcarnitines have been used as useful markers for metabolic disorders [25], suggesting that 5-hydroxydecyl-7-enocarnitine may emerge as a novel predictor for early osteoporosis.

The cross-subgroup enrichment of pyrimidine metabolism pathways underscores its central role in bone homeostasis. Supporting our findings, WGCNA analyses have identified five pyrimidine-related genes as diagnostic biomarkers for osteoporosis [26]. Animal evidence further validated that selective clearance of senescent bone cells via INK-ATTAC caspase-8 activation improves bone remodeling by reducing resorption and enhancing formation in the bone microenvironment [27]. Additionally, the pyrimidine derivative Pym-18a demonstrates therapeutic potential by mitigating glucocorticoid-induced osteoblast apoptosis [28]. These convergent findings position pyrimidine metabolism modulation as a promising avenue for osteoporosis therapies.

Gender differences have attracted attention in the occurrence of osteoporosis. Similar to many previous studies [29,30], our research findings further confirmed that females (particularly postmenopausal individuals) constituted a high-risk population for osteoporosis. The progression of the disease is mainly linked to decreased estrogen levels [31]. It is estimated that 1 in 2 postmenopausal women will experience osteoporotic fractures in their lifetime [32], underscoring the imperative for early diagnosis and prevention of osteoporosis, especially in postmenopausal women. The abrupt estrogen declines during menopausal transition drive accelerated bone resorption coupled with impaired bone formation, constituting a primary pathophysiological mechanism for rapid osteopenia [33]. In contrast, the gradual age-related androgen reduction in males exerts comparatively modest skeletal effects.

Additionally, difficulty initiating sleep might be prioritized as an osteoporosis warning sign. While our study primarily focused on metabolomic biomarkers, the potential link between sleep disturbances and bone health is a valuable clinical consideration. The association between sleep duration, quality, and osteoporosis risk remains an area of active investigation, with current literature presenting inconsistent findings [34]. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the causal relationships and underlying mechanisms linking specific sleep disturbances to bone loss progression.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, as an exploratory investigation, the sample size was relatively modest, particularly for male participants. Second, the cross-sectional design cannot infer the causal relationship between metabolites and bone density. Third, methodological constraints exist: the assessment of physical activity via questionnaire may not fully capture the influence of this important confounder, and technical aspects of metabolomic profiling, including sensitivity for low-abundance metabolites and potential variability from unaccounted circadian rhythms, warrant consideration. Fourth, and most critically, the predictive models were validated only internally and lack an independent external validation cohort. Their generalizability and potential for clinical translation must be confirmed in future studies involving other high-altitude populations or longitudinal cohorts. Subsequent research should employ larger, multicenter longitudinal designs, incorporate objective measures of physical activity, and leverage emerging technologies such as single-cell metabolomics to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified 15 key serum metabolites for evaluating early osteoporosis in Xizang population using ensemble learning. These key predictive metabolites, such as gamma-Glutamyltryptophan and di-homo-gamma-linolenate, unveil novel molecular insights into early osteoporosis or osteopenia. The identified biomarker enrichment pathways, such as pyrimidine metabolism and related diseases, may illuminate novel therapeutic targets for osteoporosis. The proposed biomarkers and pathways need to be externally validated using independent cohorts in future research. In addition, the results suggested that postmenopausal females and people with difficulty initiating sleep may be high-risk groups for osteoporosis. The metabolic prediction framework proposed in this study establishes a novel reference for early prevention and management of high-altitude osteoporosis.
