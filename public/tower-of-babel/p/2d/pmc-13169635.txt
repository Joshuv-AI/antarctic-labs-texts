Whole transcriptome sequencing of different hair types in inner Mongolian cashmere goats
Authors: Gao Gong, Wenxin Zheng, Wenze Li, Zhiying Wang, Qi Lv, Rui Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistinct coat morphological differences are widely observed in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, and individuals can be classified into different hair types based on coarse-hair length. These different hair types directly affect cashmere fiber length, quality, and production efficiency. To elucidate the molecular basis underlying hair type differentiation, we performed whole-transcriptome sequencing of skin tissues from Long hair type cashmere goat (LHG) and Short hair type cashmere goat (SHG) at three key stages of the secondary hair follicle cycle (September, December, and March). High-quality sequencing data were obtained from both strand-specific and small RNA libraries (Q30 > 91.8%). Differential expression analysis identified 299 mRNAs, 497 lncRNAs, 217 circRNAs, and 29 microRNAs between LHG and SHG. Multi-layer enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG, and GSEA) consistently converged on functions related to keratin filaments, intermediate filaments, and cytoskeletal organization, suggesting that hair type differentiation is closely associated with hair shaft structural assembly and follicle structural remodeling, and differentially expressed genes were also enriched in canonical follicle-related pathways, including PI3K-Akt, Wnt, and TGF-β. PPI analysis further revealed two major interaction modules dominated by ribosomal proteins and KRT/KRTAP proteins, respectively. Moreover, ncRNA–mRNA network analysis combined with qRT-PCR validation of KRT79, LOC106503217, circ_0284, and chi-miR-221-5p indicated potential regulatory relationships between keratin genes and ncRNAs across different hair types. Collectively, this study provides a systematic resource for understanding different hair types in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats and offers candidate targets for molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12814-8.Keywords: Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, Different hair types, Skin hair follicles, Whole transcriptome sequencing

Introduction

Cashmere goats are mainly used for cashmere production. Inner Mongolian cashmere goats are one of the important cashmere goat breeds in China. They are local dual-purpose breeds for meat and wool production and can produce high-quality cashmere. Secondary hair follicles are responsible for cashmere production, showing an obvious annual cycle, which can be divided into the anagen phase (April-November), the catagen phase (December-January), and the telogen phase (January-February). During the production and breeding process of Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, significant morphological differences in the hair coat structure were observed, and it was found that hair length could be used as a reference for the indirect selection of other economic traits. By selecting for hair length, the quality of cashmere can be improved. According to the statistical analysis of the measurement of the coarse hair length of Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, they are divided into three hair types: Long hair type cashmere goat (LHG, hair length > 22 cm), Short hair type cashmere goat (SHG, hair length ≤ 13 cm), and Intermediate type cashmere goat (13 cm < hair length ≤ 22 cm).

Previous studies by Gong et al. [1, 2] used Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) to conduct in-depth analysis of the skin transcriptome data of cashmere goats with different hair types (LHG vs. SHG). They found that a gene module called the “Magenta module” was highly correlated with hair type, and the genes in this module were mainly enriched in functions related to intermediate filaments, cytoskeletal proteins, and keratin fibers. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis further indicated that these genes were significantly involved in the MAPK signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and arginine- proline metabolism. The expression of key genes such as FGF21, ASL, KRT39, and KRT74 was significantly higher in LHG than in SHG, and their expression levels were extremely significantly positively correlated with hair length; immunohistochemistry (IHC) detection also confirmed that the FGF21 protein was more strongly expressed in the primary hair follicles of LHG. These findings provide important clues for understanding the molecular basis of hair type differences.

However, the above-mentioned studies, as well as most traditional transcriptome analyses, mainly focus on the messenger RNA (mRNA) level. During RNA sample preparation, mRNAs with Poly(A) tails are typically enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, which inevitably leads to the loss of substantial information on non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). Although ncRNAs—including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs)—do not encode proteins, they play crucial roles in gene expression regulation and are widely involved in important biological processes such as development, differentiation, apoptosis, and the hair follicle cycle. Increasing evidence indicates that lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs can form precise regulatory networks through complex interactions (e.g., ceRNA networks) and jointly influence trait formation and variation. In goats, ncRNAs have been demonstrated to participate in regulating key economic traits, including skeletal muscle development [3], subcutaneous fat deposition, melanin deposition, reproductive performance [4], and cashmere growth [5, 6]. Moreover, circRNAs have been reported to play crucial roles in secondary hair follicle development [7–9], follicle development [10, 11], and skeletal muscle growth [12, 13], while recent studies on goat miRNAs have increasingly focused on secondary hair follicle cycling [14, 15], muscle development [16], disease infection [17], and fat metabolism [18].

Given the central roles of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) in hair follicle biology and trait regulation, and the current paucity of ncRNA-level investigations into the mechanisms underlying divergence between LHG and SHG cashmere goats, an mRNA-centered approach alone is insufficient to capture the full regulatory landscape of hair type differentiation. Accordingly, we applied whole-transcriptome sequencing to characterize goats with different hair types. Skin tissues from LHG and SHG individuals were collected at key stages of the secondary hair follicle cycle (anagen, catagen, and telogen) and profiled to simultaneously quantify mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs. Integrating differential expression analyses with functional enrichment (GO and KEGG), gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction, we aimed to systematically identify RNA molecules associated with hair type differences, elucidate coordinated molecular mechanisms contributing to the formation of different hair types, and provide more comprehensive insights into the genetic basis of this economically important trait. This study is expected to close the knowledge gap in ncRNA-mediated regulation of hair type divergence and to provide theoretical support and candidate targets for molecular breeding in cashmere goats.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample collection

Based on hair length measurements recorded in 2020 and 2021, five 2-year-old adult female Inner Mongolian cashmere goats were selected for each of the LHG and SHG. To minimize potential confounding effects, all selected animals were maintained under the same management conditions, and individuals with comparable body size and similar coat characteristics were chosen. Considering the cyclic activity of secondary hair follicles, we collected skin samples at three key stages of the follicle cycle to represent the major biological transitions of cashmere growth: September 2020 (anagen), December 2020 (catagen), and March 2021 (telogen). Skin tissue biopsies (2 cm²) were obtained from the lateral scapular region, and sampling was performed from the same anatomical site for each individual at each time point. The collected tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until total RNA extraction. Sample IDs and grouping information are provided in Table 1.

Total RNA extraction and quality control

Total RNA from skin samples was extracted using the TRIzol method, following the manufacturer’s instructions for TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). The extracted RNA was dissolved in 32 µL of buffer. Three methods were used for RNA quality assessment:

NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo) for preliminary quantification of RNA.

Library construction, quality control and sequencing

The whole transcriptome sequencing was performed in two libraries: one for ribosomal RNA depletion and one for small RNA. The ribosomal RNA depletion method was used to construct strand-specific libraries, which included all RNAs except ribosomal RNA and small fragments, such as lncRNA, mRNA, and circRNA. Small RNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext®Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina kit.

After library construction, the libraries were quality-checked. Initial quantification was done using the Qubit 2.0 (Thermo), followed by Agilent 2100 for insert size detection. Quantification was confirmed using qRT-PCR to ensure library quality.

After library quality verification, sequencing was carried out on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). The ribosomal RNA-depleted libraries were sequenced using Illumina PE150 sequencing, while small RNA libraries were sequenced using Illumina SE50 sequencing.

Sequencing data quality control and evaluation

Quality control of raw sequencing data in FASTQ format was performed using fastQC (v0.11.9), and metrics such as Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated for clean reads.

For ribosomal RNA-depleted libraries, the following quality control criteria were applied: (1) Removal of reads with adapter sequences. (2) Removal of reads with more than 0.002 N’s. (3) Removal of paired reads where more than 50% of bases in a single read were of low quality.

For small RNA libraries, the quality control criteria were as follows: (1) Removal of low-quality reads. (2) Removal of reads with > 10% N’s. (3) Removal of reads with 5’ adapter contamination. (4) Removal of reads without 3’ adapter sequences or insertion fragments. (5) Removal of polyA/T/G/C containing reads.

Reference sequence alignment, transcript assembly, and identification

The reference genome and gene annotation files were downloaded from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/), using the goat reference genome (version: GCF_001704415.1_ARS1, website: https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/001/704/415/GCF_001704415.1_ARS1/). The sequencing data was analyzed on a high-performance computing node server (ThinkSystem SR850P, Lenovo).

mRNA analysis

mRNA and lncRNA were aligned to the reference genome using hisat2 (v2.2.0) [19]. Indexing of the reference genome was done using hisat2-build with parameters (-p 20 --ss --exon). Clean data from each sample was aligned using hisat2 with parameters (-p 260 --dta --phred33 --no-unal -t --rna-strandness RF --un-conc-gz --summary-file -x -1 -2 -S). Files were converted using samtools (v1.13) [20], and aligned files were processed with StringTie (v2.1.7) [21] for transcript assembly.

Known mRNA transcripts were identified using gene annotation information from the gtf file, and transcript quantification was performed using StringTie with the following parameters: (stringtie -e -B --rf -p 50 -G -o -A). Sample count values and FPKM values were calculated using a Python script.

lncRNA analysis

lncRNAs were selected and identified from the transcript GTF files merged by StringTie. The identification process for lncRNAs was as follows: (1) Transcript Sequence Retrieval: The lncRNA transcript sequences were obtained using gffread from Cufflinks (v2.2.1) [22]. (2) Coding Potential Prediction: The coding potential of the transcript sequences was predicted using four software tools: CNCI [23], CPC2 [24], PLEK(v1.2) [25], and pfam_scan(v1.6) [26]. The parameters for each tool were set as follows: For CNCI, the parameters were: (python CNCI.py -f -o -p 150 -m ve). For CPC2, the parameters were: (python CPC2.py -i -o). For PLEK, the parameters were: (python PLEK.py -fasta -out -thread 20 -minlength 200). For pfam_scan, the parameters were: (pfam_scan.pl -fasta -dir -outfile -cpu 300 -as). The intersection of non-coding transcripts identified by these tools was used as the candidate lncRNA dataset for this analysis. The newly identified lncRNAs were quantified using StringTie, and sample count values and FPKM values were calculated using a Python script.

Target Gene Prediction for lncRNAs. The prediction of target genes for lncRNAs was performed using two approaches: cis-regulation and trans-regulation [27, 28]. For cis-regulation, potential target mRNAs within a 100 kb range of the lncRNA were selected based on the theory of cis-regulatory elements. bedtools (v2.30.0) was used to predict cis-regulatory target genes, with the following parameters: (bedtools intersect -a -b -wo and bedtools window -a -b -l 100000 -r 100000). For trans-regulation, target genes were predicted by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between the expression of mRNA and lncRNAs. The expression data from both mRNA and lncRNA were used to predict trans-regulated target genes for lncRNAs using a Python script.

circRNA analysis

The identification and analysis of circRNAs were mainly performed using CIRI_Full (v2.0) [29] and find_circ (v1.2) [30].

CIRI_Full software requires bwa (v0.7.17) [31] for reference genome alignment analysis. The reference genome index was built using bwa index with the following parameter: (-a bwtsw). Clean data from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using bwa mem with the parameters: (-t 200 -T 19). The resulting SAM files were then analyzed with CIRI_Full, using the following parameters: (perl CIRI2.pl -I -O -F -A -T 4).

find_circ software requires bowtie2 (v2.2.6) [32] for reference genome alignment analysis. The reference genome index was built using bowtie2-build, with the following parameter: (--threads 10). Clean data from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using bowtie2, with the following parameters: (-p 50 --end-to-end --very-sensitive --phred33 --no-mixed -X 600 --score-min = C,-15,0 --mm -x -q -1 -2 -S). The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM files using samtools, and unmapped reads were extracted with the following parameters: (samtools view -hf 4 | samtools view -Sb). The unmapped2anchors.py Python script from find_circ was used to extract anchors. These anchors were then aligned again using bowtie2, and find_circ.py from find_circ was used to identify circRNAs, with the following parameters: (-G -p prefix -n -s).

The intersection of circRNA results from CIRI_Full and find_circ was used to identify the final set of circRNAs. The expression levels of circRNAs were quantified using count values and TPM values [33]. The target genes of circRNAs were predicted based on their corresponding host genes.

microRNA analysis

MicroRNA analysis was performed using bowtie (v1.1.2) [34] for reference genome alignment. The reference genome index was built using bowtie-build, with the following parameter: (--threads 260). Clean data from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using bowtie, with the following parameters: (-p 260 -x -v 0 -m 1 --best --strata -f –un).

Subsequent analysis was performed using miRDeep2 (v2.0.1.3) [35]. The mapper.pl Python script was used to map reads back to the reference genome, with the following parameters: (-c -j -l 18 -m -p -s -t -v). The quantifier.pl Python script was then used for quick quantification, with the following parameters: (-p chi_hairpin.fa -m chi_mature.fa -r -y -g 0 -T 10). The expression levels of microRNAs were estimated using count values and TPM values.

Target genes for known microRNAs were predicted using three software tools: miRanda (v3.3a) [36], RNAhybrid (v2.1.2) [37], and qtar. The intersection of results from these tools was used for final target gene prediction. The parameters for each tool were as follows: For miRanda: (-sc 140 -en -10 -scale 4 -strict -out). For RNAhybrid: (-g jpg -b 1 -e -20 -p 0.05 -f 8,12 -d 1.9,0.28 -u 1 -v 1 -t -q). For qtar: (-s -t -o -m a).

Differential analysis of RNAs

After obtaining the expression matrices for mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and microRNA, differential analysis was conducted. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 (v1.32.0) [38, 39] R package, based on a negative binomial distribution. The parameters were set with |log2(fold change)| > 1 and (P-adj) < 0.05. The volcano plot of differentially expressed genes was generated using the ggplot2 (v3.3.6) R package.

Differential gene clustering heatmaps were generated based on the FPKM/TPM data of differentially expressed genes, using the pheatmap (v1.0.12) R package. Hierarchical clustering of gene expression (FPKM) was performed, and genes with similar expression patterns were grouped together.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

The accuracy of the transcriptome data was validated using quantitative real-time PCR. cDNA containing mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (RR047A, TAKARA). MicroRNA cDNA was synthesized using a microRNA reverse transcription kit (638315, TAKARA) by tailing the microRNAs. Specific primers for differentially expressed mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and microRNA were designed. The primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) [40] and Primer Premier (v5.0) software. Detailed primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA were analyzed using a fluorescent quantitative PCR kit (RR820A, TAKARA). GAPDH and β-actin were used as house-keeping genes for gene expression normalization. MicroRNA expression was analyzed using a fluorescent quantitative PCR kit (639676, TAKARA), with U6 as an internal control.

Relative RNA expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method [41]. Data were organized using Microsoft Office (v2019) Excel and analyzed by ANOVA using SAS (v9.2). P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference, and P < 0.01 indicates a highly significant difference. Expression trends of gene transcriptome data and qRT-PCR were compared and visualized using GraphPad Prism (v8.3.0).

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

GO functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the GOseq (v3.16) [42]. A corrected P-value < 0.05 was considered as the threshold for functional enrichment. Differentially expressed mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and microRNA target genes were selected for GO enrichment analysis, followed by the generation of GO bar plots and Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAG).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS (v3.0) [43] software. Pathways with a corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered enriched. Differential mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and microRNA target genes were selected for KEGG pathway analysis, and KEGG bubble plots were generated for enriched pathways.

GSEA analysis was performed using GSEA (v4.1.0) software [44]. Differential mRNAs were selected for PPI analysis, with the species set to goat (Capra hircus). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed and visualized [45].

Results and analysis

Detection of total RNA in skin samples

After total RNA extraction, the concentration of RNA was measured using a NanoDrop 2000, with RNA concentrations ranging from 278.2 to 2060.0 ng/µL, and the OD260/280 ratio ranged from 1.85 to 1.99. Three main bands of total RNA were observed, clear and intact without any tailing or degradation, and there were no DNA bands present. A few samples showed minor contamination with impurities. RNA integrity was further evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, with some results shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ranged from 7.2 to 9.9. These results indicate that the extracted total RNA samples meet the quality requirements for library preparation and sequencing, making them suitable for sequencing.

Sequencing data quality control results

The strand-specific library sequencing generated a total of 2,718,266,504 raw reads, yielding 407.72 GB of data. After quality control, 2,678,758,726 clean reads were obtained, with 401.85 GB of data. The total raw bases generated by all samples ranged from 12.04 to 16.18 GB, and the clean bases for all samples were above 11.93 GB. The overall sequencing error rate for all data was below 0.03%, with the Q20 index above 97% and the Q30 index above 91.80%. The GC content was stable at approximately 49.70%. The sequencing quality of the strand-specific library was generally high, as detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

The small RNA library sequencing generated 392,542,392 raw reads, yielding 19.62 GB of data. The data size for all samples ranged from 0.51 to 0.76 GB. After quality control, 383,495,280 clean reads were obtained. The overall sequencing error rate for the data was below 0.01%, with the Q20 index exceeding 98.93% and the Q30 index exceeding 96.37%. The GC content was stable at around 48.70%. The sequencing quality of the small RNA library was generally high, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Reference genome alignment analysis results

The clean data of the strand-specific library were aligned to the goat reference genome sequence (ARS1), and the detailed alignment results are shown in Supplementary Table 4. The results indicate that the total mapped reads for all samples ranged from 89.35% to 97.45%, multiple mapped reads ranged from 2.83% to 9.52%, and uniquely mapped reads ranged from 82.35% to 93.49%. The ratio of alignment to the positive and negative strands was balanced.

The clean data of the small RNA library were aligned to the goat reference genome sequence (ARS1), with the detailed alignment results shown in Supplementary Table 5. The results show that the total mapped reads for all samples ranged from 90.65% to 98.00%, with small RNA mapped to the positive strand ranging from 42.83% to 58.83%, and mapped to the negative strand ranging from 36.55% to 54.88%. The alignment ratio between the positive and negative strands was unbalanced.

Transcript assembly and identification

Transcript assembly from the sequencing data and alignment to the reference genome identified a total of 29,208 known genes. Additional transcripts were identified and screened based on lncRNA identification criteria. CNCI, CPC2, PLEK, and pfam_scan software were used to predict the coding potential of the transcripts, and those predicted as non-coding by all four tools were selected. The intersection of these predictions led to the identification of 14,848 new lncRNAs.

New circRNAs were identified by using CIRI_Full and find_circ software. The intersection of the results from both software identified 6,849 new circRNAs. By aligning to the goat small RNA database, a total of 436 known microRNAs were identified.

Gene expression level analysis

In this study, the expression levels of mRNA and lncRNA in all samples were analyzed using FPKM values, while the expression levels of circRNA and microRNA were analyzed using TPM values. Expression distribution boxplots of FPKM/TPM values for all samples of mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and microRNA were plotted, as shown in Fig. 1. It can be observed that the distribution patterns of log10(FPKM + 1) and log10(TPM + 1) values for each group of samples were relatively consistent. GO analysis results

For the 497 differentially expressed lncRNAs in cashmere goats with different coat types, cis- and trans-acting target genes were predicted. A total of 2,019 cis-acting and 3,764 trans-acting target genes were identified. For the 218 differentially expressed circRNAs, the host genes were analyzed, resulting in 202 host genes. For the 29 differentially expressed microRNAs, target genes were predicted, resulting in 6,837 target genes.

Differential mRNAs were significantly enriched in 48 GO terms (Corrected P value < 0.05), including 19 biological processes, 21 cellular components, and 8 molecular functions (Fig. 5A-D). These mRNAs played key roles in biological processes (BP) such as protein import into the nucleus, nuclear transport, nucleocytoplasmic transport, arginine metabolic processes, and glutamine family amino acid metabolic processes. The cellular components (CC) enriched were mainly intermediate filaments, intermediate filament cytoskeletons, keratin filaments, cytoskeletal parts, non-membrane-bounded organelles, and organelles. Molecular functions (MF) were enriched in fewer terms, primarily including structural molecule activity, dynein binding, protein transporter activity, and protein binding, bridging. The directed acyclic graph (DAG) revealed the relationships between the GO terms, with several biological processes being significantly enriched and several leading to protein import into the nucleus. In cellular components, multiple terms ultimately pointed to intermediate filaments and keratin filaments.

For cis-acting target genes of differentially expressed lncRNAs, the KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment in ribosome, p53 signaling pathway, spliceosome, RNA transport, VEGF signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway. For trans-acting target genes of differentially expressed lncRNAs, enrichment was observed in the spliceosome, protein export, RNA degradation, Notch signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, cell cycle, and MAPK signaling pathway. For the host genes of differentially expressed circRNAs, the main enriched pathways included MAPK signaling pathway, oxytocin signaling pathway, inositol phosphate metabolism, Jak-STAT signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway. For microRNA target genes, the KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, axon guidance, pathways in cancer, MAPK signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and fatty acid metabolism.

The overall KEGG pathway enrichment showed that these differentially expressed RNAs and their target genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, ribosome, and Wnt signaling pathway, all of which are closely associated with hair follicle growth and development.

GSEA analysis results

GSEA was conducted based on the ranked gene list derived from the differential expression analysis between different hair types at each stage. The results are summarized in Table 3; Fig. 7. Gene sets with |NES| > 1, NOM p-val < 0.05, and FDR q-val < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched. In total, 15 gene sets were significantly enriched, including 1 BP term (protein localization), 3 CC terms (keratin filament, intermediate filament, and intermediate filament cytoskeleton), 1 MF term (structural molecule activity), and 10 KEGG pathways (cell cycle, Hippo signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, Hedgehog signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, Jak–STAT signaling pathway, Notch signaling pathway, AMPK signaling pathway, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway). These enriched terms and pathways suggest their potential regulatory roles across different hair types. Notably, keratin filament (NES = 2.71) and intermediate filament (NES = 2.12) exhibited the strongest enrichment signals, further indicating coordinated regulation of hair shaft structural assembly and cytoskeletal organization between different hair types.

Discussion

Hair type differences in cashmere goats affect cashmere fiber length, fineness, and textile processing performance. Because hair follicle development and hair shaft formation are dynamic processes regulated at multiple molecular layers, interpretations based solely on transcriptomic changes at the mRNA level are often insufficient to fully elucidate the molecular basis underlying different hair types. Therefore, in this study, we focused on Inner Mongolian cashmere goats with different hair types and performed whole-transcriptome sequencing and integrative analyses at three key stages of the secondary hair follicle cycle, aiming to jointly dissect the potential mechanisms of hair type differentiation from both structural gene programs and ncRNA-mediated regulatory layers.

Hair follicles are mini-organs responsible for hair growth and are influenced by multiple intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including genetics, nutrition, seasonality, age, shearing, and the environment. In cashmere goats, hair follicle growth, differentiation, and cycling are orchestrated by several conserved signaling pathways, such as Wnt, TGF-β, MAPK, Notch, and PI3K-Akt [46, 47], together with structural and regulatory molecules including fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), keratins (KRTs), and keratin-associated proteins (KRTAPs) [48, 49]. In the present work, we conducted whole-transcriptome sequencing of skin tissues from Inner Mongolian cashmere goats with different hair types across three key stages of the cashmere growth cycle.

Across multiple analytical layers, functional enrichment analyses (GO and GSEA) consistently converged on biological processes related to keratin filaments, intermediate filament organization, and cytoskeletal architecture, and numerous differentially expressed KRTs/KRTAPs were identified between the LHG and SHG. Given that the structural framework of wool and cashmere fibers is largely determined by KRTs and KRTAPs [50–52], and that keratin composition is closely associated with fiber quality traits [53], these findings suggest that divergence among different hair types is primarily linked to coordinated regulation of hair shaft structural assembly and follicle structural remodeling rather than broad metabolic reprogramming. Consistent with this view, keratin family members have been repeatedly implicated in fiber traits across species. For example, Kang et al. reported that KRT25, KRT5, KRT71, and KRT14 may be associated with hair curliness in Tan sheep [54], and Zhang et al. suggested that the ratios of KRT85, KRTAP15-1, and KRTAP3-1 could contribute to fiber diameter differences and may serve as potential molecular markers for distinguishing fiber types [55]. Our results are also consistent with our previous WGCNA-based studies: Gong et al. [1, 2] identified a Magenta module strongly associated with hair type, with genes enriched in keratin/intermediate filament organization and cytoskeletal processes. Collectively, these lines of evidence support the robustness of structural gene programs as one of the core molecular foundations underlying different hair types.

A key advantage of the whole-transcriptome strategy is that it enables exploration of additional regulatory layers beyond mRNA co-expression patterns. Our integrative analyses suggest that lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs may converge on keratin and keratin-associated structural programs, thereby providing transcriptional and post-transcriptional fine-tuning during follicle differentiation and hair shaft formation. Cis- and trans-regulatory analyses indicated that some candidate lncRNAs may regulate keratin-associated genes; notably, the cis-targets of XLOC_04726 included KRT79, suggesting that lncRNAs may contribute to the construction of hair fiber structural components through local transcriptional regulation. In addition, multiple circRNAs and lncRNAs were predicted to interact with miRNAs and/or target keratin or keratin-associated protein genes, implying that ncRNAs may participate in hair follicle differentiation by modulating the expression of structural genes.

Moreover, qRT-PCR validation further supported the expression patterns of key candidates identified by sequencing. Across stages, KRT79 and LOC106503217 (KRTAP4-9) showed higher expression in the LHG than in the SHG, whereas chi-miR-221-5p displayed higher expression in the SHG (with significant differences at specific stages); circ_0284 was also higher in the LHG at key stages. Combined with predicted target relationships, these expression trends are compatible with a regulatory framework in which circRNAs may act as miRNA sponges and miRNAs may modulate the expression of structural genes involved in keratinization and hair shaft assembly. KRT79 is preferentially expressed in specific hair follicle compartments and has been implicated in hair development, regeneration, and epithelial cell migration [56–58]; Xu et al. also suggested a positive association between KRT79 and cashmere fineness based on multi-omics evidence [59]. In our dataset, KRT79 consistently showed higher expression in the LHG across the three stages, and qRT-PCR results were concordant with sequencing trends, supporting its candidacy as a marker gene associated with different hair types.

Overall, by integrating network analyses with expression validation, this study links ncRNA candidates to structural gene programs and extends previous mRNA-centered findings, providing a more comprehensive regulatory framework for understanding hair fiber formation. Pathway analyses further showed that differentially expressed RNAs were enriched in canonical hair follicle–related pathways, including PI3K-Akt, Wnt, TGF-β, and the cell cycle. The PI3K-Akt pathway is widely recognized as an important regulator of follicle cycling and stem/progenitor cell proliferation, and is closely related to anagen maintenance and follicle regeneration [60–64]. Together with previous reports that modulation of PI3K-Akt-related genes can affect cashmere yield [65–70], enrichment of this pathway in our study suggests that PI3K-Akt-associated transcriptional programs may contribute to hair type specific growth dynamics in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats.

In summary, we explored the mechanisms underlying different hair types from three complementary layers—differential genes, ncRNA regulation, and canonical follicle signaling pathways. On the one hand, stable enrichment of KRT/KRTAP-related cytoskeletal and structural processes indicates that hair shaft structural assembly and follicle structural remodeling represent an important basis for hair type differentiation. On the other hand, predicted interactions among lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs, together with qRT-PCR validation, provide supportive evidence that ncRNAs may participate in regulating structural gene expression. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the genetic basis of different hair types in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, but also provide a foundation and direction for future functional validation and the identification of molecular breeding targets.

Conclusion

We performed whole-transcriptome sequencing of skin tissues from Inner Mongolian cashmere goats with different hair types at the anagen, catagen, and telogen stages, and identified 299 differentially expressed mRNAs, 497 lncRNAs, 217 circRNAs, and 29 microRNAs between the LHG and SHG. Multi-layer enrichment analyses consistently converged on structural processes related to keratin filaments, intermediate filaments, and cytoskeletal organization, indicating that differentiation among different hair types is primarily associated with hair shaft structural assembly and follicle structural remodeling, accompanied by enrichment of canonical follicle-related pathways such as PI3K-Akt, Wnt, and TGF-β. Integrated network analysis further linked candidate ncRNAs with structural genes, and qRT-PCR validation supported the expression patterns of key candidates (KRT79, LOC106503217, circ_0284, chi-miR-221-5p, and XLOC_047269), suggesting that an ncRNA-mediated regulatory layer may contribute to hair type differentiation. Collectively, this study provides a more comprehensive reference for the molecular regulation underlying hair type divergence in cashmere goats and offers candidate targets and theoretical support for molecular breeding.
