Prevalence of zero-dose vaccination and its associated factors in urban areas of Somali region, Ethiopia: a mixed-methods approach
Authors: Abdifatah Elmi, Mahamed Ibrahim, Abdi Farah, Ahmed Tahir, Abdisalan Muktar, Abdirazak Mohamoud Abdilahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite global progress in immunisation, vaccine coverage remains uneven in many low- and middle-income countries. Urbanisation has widened disparities, particularly among children in informal settlements. Ethiopia has a high zero-dose burden, with the Somali region identified as a hotspot. However, urban areas in this region remain understudied. This study assesses the prevalence of zero-dose children and associated factors in urban areas of the Somali region to inform targeted interventions.MethodsUsing a mixed-methods approach, data were collected from 842 households in three urban towns through community surveys and six key informant interviews. The primary outcome was the proportion of zero-dose children, defined as those who had not received the first dose of the DTP-containing vaccine (Penta-1) during their first year of life, based on vaccination card records or caregiver recall. This included children who had never received any vaccine or had received some vaccines but not Penta-1. Quantitative data were analysed using logistic regression, while qualitative findings were examined thematically using the GAVI IRMMA framework. Strict quality control measures ensured the reliability of results.ResultsOf 842 targeted households, 827 participated (98.2% response). Findings reveal that 30% of children surveyed were identified as zero-dose for vaccination. Children were significantly more likely to be zero-dose if their mothers had no antenatal care (adjusted OR, AOR ~5.0), required permission for vaccination (AOR ~3.4), delivered at home (AOR ~3.1), or did not receive the second dose of tetanus toxoid (AOR ~2.7). In contrast, higher perceived household wealth and at least one postnatal care visit reduced the likelihood of zero-dose by about 45% and 55%, respectively. Zero-dose children were concentrated in urban peripheries and informal settlements with limited healthcare access, poor identification strategies and reliance on facility-based vaccination services.ConclusionsThe study highlights a higher prevalence of zero-dose children in urban areas, underscoring the urgent need for improved identification and mapping in underserved communities. Strengthening advocacy, diversifying immunisation approaches and addressing systemic barriers are essential to improving vaccine access and reducing the zero-dose burden.Keywords: Vaccination, Community Health, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Immunisation is one of the most impactful public health measures for preventing infectious diseases, particularly in children.1 Vaccination has significantly improved global health outcomes by reducing illness and death from preventable diseases. Despite these successes, ensuring high immunisation coverage remains a challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),2 where access is hindered by poverty, geographic barriers, inadequate healthcare infrastructure and limited resources.3

Although vaccine initiatives have curbed the spread of many diseases, immunisation rates have stagnated in the past decade, with further setbacks during the COVID-19 pandemic.4 The Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) seeks to strengthen immunisation systems, ensuring equitable vaccine access, with a special focus on ‘zero-dose’ children—those who have never received a single vaccine. These children are disproportionately found in remote rural areas, urban slums and conflict-affected regions, where systemic social, political and economic barriers prevent vaccination.4 They also suffer from broader deprivations, including lack of healthcare, poor nutrition and inadequate sanitation, contributing to high child mortality rates.5

Globally, the rapid urbanisation observed in recent decades has compounded disparities in vaccine coverage.6 Over one billion people now reside in informal urban settlements, where inadequate infrastructure and social exclusion create significant barriers to immunisation.7 While children in urban areas generally have better access to vaccines than those in rural regions, those living in poor urban communities continue to face challenges such as limited healthcare facilities, financial constraints and social discrimination.8 Data from 2022 estimates reveals that in LMICs, 28% of zero-dose children live in urban areas, 40% in periurban settings and 11% in remote rural locations.9

Ethiopia has one of the highest numbers of zero-dose children globally, with approximately 3.9 million unvaccinated children.10 Factors contributing to this issue include poor economic conditions, inadequate maternal healthcare utilisation and lack of women empowerment and demand-side barriers.11 12 Studies show that zero-dose children are concentrated in underserved communities, where geographic isolation and sociocultural norms play a major role in vaccine hesitancy.11 In Ethiopia, urban areas, particularly informal settlements, face unique barriers such as overcrowding, unreliable healthcare services, long wait times and vaccine stockouts—further decreasing immunisation rates.13

Despite ongoing global efforts to expand vaccination programmes, millions of children remain unvaccinated. Ethiopia ranks among the top 10 countries with the highest number of zero-dose children, with substantial regional disparities.14 The Somali region, in particular, has been identified as a hotspot for zero-dose children, with clusters in western, eastern and northern parts of the region.15 16 The high rate of zero-dose children in the Somali region is linked to healthcare institutions that primarily serve settled communities, making it difficult for nomadic groups to get care due to cultural and financial obstacles. Additionally, pastoralist groups’ seasonal mobility restricts their access to healthcare, underutilisation of health information particularly vaccinations .17 Low utilisation of health information in these parts of Ethiopia might also contribute to the high burden of zero-dose children.18 However, urban areas within the Somali region have not been specifically studied in depth.

This study aims to estimate the prevalence of zero-dose children and examine the factors associated with zero-dose vaccinations in urban areas of the Somali region by being the first to concentrate on urban zero-dose in a pastoralist context using GAVI IRMMA framework and mixed-methods approach. By identifying key challenges, this research seeks to provide actionable insights for targeted interventions to improve vaccine coverage and bridge immunisation gaps in vulnerable urban populations.

Materials and methods

Setting and study population

The Somali region of Ethiopia is characterised by diverse population groups, including predominant nomadic pastoralists, agro-pastoralists, riverine and sedentary farmers, the urban population, all of whom experience varying degrees of vulnerability. Vaccination coverage in the region remains inadequate due to the population’s mobility—particularly cross-border nomads—as well as low healthcare service availability and poor road infrastructure. Additionally, recurrent humanitarian crises such as droughts, measles outbreaks, and sporadic conflicts further strain healthcare services. The region also has internally displaced persons (IDPs) and hosts refugees from Somalia, adding to the complexity of healthcare delivery.

This study specifically focuses on urban areas and was conducted in three geographically distinct towns: Jigjiga, Gode, and Tog-Wajale. Each town has its own administrative structure, and collectively, the three towns consist of 42 clusters/kebeles.

The specific study population consisted of children between 12 and 35 months of age in the three selected towns. Mothers/caregivers of children within this age range were included as study participants.

Study design

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods approach consisting of two complementary components conducted concurrently

Survey of parents and caregivers: The first component was a community-based quantitative survey administered to parents and caregivers of children eligible for routine immunisation. The survey collected structured information on immunisation status, knowledge and perceptions of vaccines, access to immunisation services and barriers or facilitators influencing vaccination uptake.

The survey collected structured information on immunisation status, knowledge and perceptions of vaccines, access to immunisation services and barriers or facilitators influencing vaccination uptake.

Key informant interviews with local public health stakeholders: The second component consisted of qualitative key informant interviews with local public health officials, and health facility staff involved in immunisation service delivery.

In this design, data collection and analysis occur simultaneously, with both datasets being analysed separately before their integration. This approach ensures that independent datasets complement and validate one another, offering a comprehensive perspective. The primary objective of this design is to obtain a holistic understanding of immunisation challenges, corroborate findings across different methodologies and examine multiple systemic levels.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s Single Population Proportion Formula, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and a 47.3% prevalence of zero-dose children in the Somali region.16 A design effect (DEFF) of 2% and 10% compensation for potential non-responses was incorporated, resulting in a final sample size of 842.

For the qualitative component, key informants involved in vaccination service planning and delivery were purposively selected and approached for interviews. Six key informants were available and participated representing local health offices and healthcare facilities from three town administrations.

Sampling procedure

A two-stage cluster sampling method was used to select study participants:

Selection of clusters: 21 kebeles were randomly chosen from the total 42 kebeles across the three urban towns.

Selection of clusters: 21 kebeles were randomly chosen from the total 42 kebeles across the three urban towns.

Selection of households: A total of 842 households with children aged 12–35 months were proportionally selected across the towns based on population size (542 households in Jigjiga, 198 in Gode and 101 in Tog-Wajale).

Selection of households: A total of 842 households with children aged 12–35 months were proportionally selected across the towns based on population size (542 households in Jigjiga, 198 in Gode and 101 in Tog-Wajale).

Key informants from town health offices and healthcare facilities were approached for interviews in person at locations convenient for participants. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min and followed a semistructured guide designed to capture participants’ perspectives. The guide included open-ended questions and probing techniques to facilitate in-depth discussions.

The primary outcome of the study was the proportion of zero-dose children—defined as children who have not received the first dose of DTP or Penta-1 vaccine during their first year of life, as determined by either vaccination card records or caregiver recall. In line with WHO recommendation, children with missing information were considered as not vaccinated.19

Zero-dose children were identified through three pathways: children who had never received any vaccination; children who had received some vaccinations but not the first dose of pentavalent vaccine (Penta-1), as verified by their vaccination card; and children who had received some vaccinations but not Penta-1, based on caregiver recall or history (figure 1).

Data collection techniques and quality assurance

For the quantitative component, a structured questionnaire adapted from various sources was used.9 11 13 The survey was administered by trained enumerators with prior experience in immunisation programmes, using Kobo Tool for data collection. Four enumerators and supervisors underwent training on survey procedures. Supervisors and the principal investigator closely monitored data collection teams, with daily reviews of collected data to ensure completeness and accuracy. Feedback was provided regularly during data collection period which ran from 22 June to 20 July 2024.

For the qualitative component, a semistructured interview guide was developed based on previous study.13 Three trained personnel conducted face-to-face interviews in locations suitable for participants. The interviews followed an open-ended format with probes to encourage detailed responses.

To ensure data reliability, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by research team members. Interviewers reviewed transcripts for accuracy, and feedback was incorporated to enhance credibility. The study established dependability by employing a rigorous mixed-methods design and integrating results through triangulation in the discussion phase for a well-rounded interpretation.

Data analysis

Quantitative data entry and cleaning was done in KoboTool and Microsoft Excel sheet respectively. The data were analysed in SPSS V.25. Descriptive statistics and background characteristics of study participants were summarised by using frequencies and percentages.

A bivariate analysis was conducted to explore associations between zero-dose status and potential factors. All relevant variables in the literature were considered into multivariate analysis regardless of their p value in bivariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was used to control for confounding factors, and final independent factors for zero-dose status were reported using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) at a 95% CI with a significance level of p<0.05.

For the qualitative data, field notes were taken, and all interviews were audio-recorded in the Somali language. Transcriptions were completed by two researchers and cross-checked by a third team member before being translated into English. Keywords were identified, manually coded and analysed thematically using the GAVI IRMMA framework which comprises five steps: Identify, Reach, Monitor, Measure and Advocate.20 The IRMMA framework was developed to help countries adopt a structured approach to reach zero-dose children and missed communities as a pathway to equitable primary healthcare. The framework can be used to initiate multistakeholder discussions on determining appropriate interventions for GAVI investments.

Sociodemographic characteristic

This study involved 827 participating households out of the targeted 842, achieving a response rate of 98.2%. The majority of surveyed children (62.8%, n=519) were between 12 and 24 months old, with a nearly equal sex ratio. A significant proportion of respondents (74.6%, n=617) had no formal education (table 1). Most decisions for vaccination were made by the mother (43.2%), followed by the father (33.6%), while 23.2% of households reported joint decision-making. A large majority of respondents (76.7%) reported having permission to take the child for vaccination or healthcare.

Healthcare accessibility and utilisation

In this study, the majority of respondent mothers (71.2%, n=589) attended at least one antenatal care (ANC) visit during their last pregnancy, and a considerable proportion (69.3%, n=573) delivered in a hospital. However, slightly more than half (53.1%, n=439) did not attend any postnatal care (PNC) visits. Additionally, most of the surveyed children (83.3%, n=513) received vaccines from public hospitals. On vaccination cards availability, about two in every three children (65%) surveyed had no vaccination card (table 2).

Prevalence of zero-dose vaccination

The algorithm below (figure 1) summarises vaccination status and identifies children classified as ‘zero-dose’ (those who have not received Penta1). Among the 827 children included in the analysis, 246 (30%; 95% CI 26.7% to 32.9%) of children were classified as zero-dose, while 581 (70%) had received at least one vaccine. Most zero-dose children 211 (86%) had never received any vaccination, with a smaller proportion missing only Penta1 despite contact with services. Among those ever vaccinated (616), a considerable number lacked vaccination cards (399), indicating reliance on caregiver recall.

Geographically, the highest proportion of zero-dose children was observed in Gode (40%), followed by Jigjiga (28%) and Tog-wajale (17%). Overall, while a majority of children had some vaccination history, substantial gaps remain in both initial access and continuity of immunisation services.

Reasons for non-vaccination

The main cause of non-vaccination in this study was awareness about the necessity of vaccination as well as when and where to get vaccinated again. Barriers like vaccination stock outs, caregivers’ busy schedules and the distance to medical facilities come next (online supplemental table 1).

Factors associated with zero-dose prevalence in urban areas

All relevant variables with complete data (N=827) were included in the multivariate analysis, irrespective of their P-value in the univariate analysis. The results indicated that children were significantly more likely to be zero-dose if their mothers did not attend ANC during pregnancy (5 times more likely), had to seek permission for child vaccination (3.4 times), delivered at home (3.1 times more likely) or did not receive TT dose 2 vaccination (2.7 times more likely) compared with their respective counterparts. On the other hand, children from households with good perceived wealth and those whose mothers attended at least one PNC visit were approximately 45% and 55% less likely to be zero-dose compared with their counterparts (table 3).

Findings from the qualitative part

The qualitative part draws on six interviews from key informants selected from health offices and health facilities in the study towns. The key themes emerged were matched to the components of the vaccine Alliance IRMMA framework for zero-dose and missed communities. For this article, the first, second and fourth themes were prioritised for the discussion section.

Theme 1: identification of zero-dose children and missed communities

Understanding the identity, location and number of zero-dose communities and children as well as the reasons behind missing them is essential and is the first step in reaching zero-dose children. One of the key informants stated:

Children with zero doses or no vaccinations are primarily found in the outskirts of each town where access to services is limited. (A key informant from, Jigjiga town).

The process of identification for zero-dose children entails examining subnational data on vaccination, and other maternal and child health services and other preventive health services; as well as mapping the concentration of zero-dose children to comprehend the factors that contribute to under vaccination. Although gaps exist in meticulously and consistently undertaking this exercise in the study area, yet the key informants were cognizant of the need to further streamline:

The performance monitoring team evaluates and monitors data reports from various sources monthly, analyzing them to identify low performance causes and prioritize appropriate actions. (A key informant from Gode town).

Theme 2: reaching zero-dose children and missed communities

The key informants were aware of the various strategies for reaching zero-dose and missed children including fixed, outreach, mobile and catch-up strategies to augment the performance of the immunisation programme. However, they emphasised that urban areas heavily rely on fixed strategy (facility-based vaccination) with limited outreaches.

Strategies used to increase immunization coverage include conducting outreach sessions, static sessions, awareness-raising, enhancing data quality, receiving supervision feedback, and better delivery of vaccine supplies. Therefore, it is suggested to increase the number of outreach and fixed sites and to enhance partner support and Periodic Intensified Routine Immunization strategy. (a key informant from Tog-wajale town).

Multiple supply and demand side barriers were mentioned to exist limiting the reach of zero-dose and missed children. Reflecting on this, key informants remarked:

There are Limited outreach sites, transportation gaps and poor awareness on EPI service. (A key informant from Tog-wajale tow).

Using Vaccine Request Forms, health centers and district offices request vaccines from Pharmaceutical and supply service agency, but sometimes face delays. Non-functional refrigerators and delays in maintenance are also common. (A key informant from Jigjiga town).

The social behavior changes strategies used during service delivery include addressing and communicating five key messages to caregivers about routine immunization, while during vaccination campaigns, prominent individuals like women’s representatives, community leaders, and religious leaders are used for mass community awareness. (A key informant from Gode town).

Theme 3: monitoring and measuring the identification of zero-dose children and the progress of immunisation interventions

To make sure zero-dose communities are identified, reached and vaccinated, progress is evaluated using a variety of metrics and data sources. One of the key informants said:

Performance review team evaluates the EPI performance, the team discusses and improves the identified gaps additionally, the Reginal Health Bureau (RHB) comes for monitoring and supports though integrated supervision and provides corrective feedbacks. (A key informant from Gode town).

The key informants further indicated limitations related to population or denominator inaccuracies for planning which leads to overestimation of coverages thereby leading to missing of children despite influxes of people into towns:

The RED (Reach Every District) micro plan and annual plan are developed annually; however, the denominator is based on a predetermined population that affects the coverage and necessity of supplies. (A key informant from Tog-wajale town).

Theme 4: engagement and advocacy approach for reaching zero-dose children

Strategic advocacy can reinforce political commitment to prioritise zero-dose children and underserved communities. Engagement of political leadership and advocacy efforts—which involve interaction with the government, civic society, religious organisations, professional associations and particular contexts—are essential for mobilising. Key informants from the study areas reported several forms of engagement they carry out, though the extent of their involvement varies across contexts.

Stronger social mobilization and demand generation, support supervision, and the involvement and support of stakeholders such as Women affairs bureau, Education bureau, Islamic affairs council, kebele leaders, and community leaders are the suggested points. (A key informant from Tog-wajale town).

Discussion

This study aimed at estimating the prevalence of zero-dose vaccination and its associated factors in urban areas of the Somali region, Ethiopia. It found that 30% (95% CI 26.7% to 32.9%) of children aged 12–35 months had zero-dose vaccination status. Maternal characteristics were the main factors associated with zero-dose status. Children whose mothers did not attend ANC, delivered at home, did not receive the second dose of tetanus toxoid vaccination or lacked permission to take the child for vaccination were more likely to be zero-dose. In contrast, children from households with better perceived wealth status and those whose mothers attended PNC visits were less likely to be zero-dose.

The substantial zero-dose observed in this study, with a prevalence of 30%, aligns with findings from other Ethiopian studies, which report rates as high as 33.7%,11 46.5%21 in underserved settings, including pastoralist communities, urban areas and conflict zones.11 16 21 Similarly, studies in Somalia highlight a high zero-dose prevalence, with 60.2% of children in remote rural areas lacking vaccination.22 Furthermore, the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey found that 44% of children aged 12–23 months remained unvaccinated,23 underscoring persistent gaps in immunisation coverage.

While these findings highlight regions with high zero-dose prevalence, other studies report comparatively lower rates. For example, a secondary analysis of the 2019 Ethiopian Mini-Demographic and Health Survey found a zero-dose prevalence of 16.3% among children aged 12–35 months.12 While a study in Togo identified a prevalence of 26.9% among children aged 12–23 months.24 Additionally, an analysis spanning 99 LMICs revealed that zero-dose children are distributed across various settings, with 28% residing in urban areas, 40% in periurban regions and 11% in remote rural locations.9 These variations suggest that contextual factors including geographic location and study methodologies significantly influence reported prevalence rates.

Ethiopia, as Africa’s second-largest contributor to zero-dose children,25 exemplifies the widespread challenge of vaccine inequity. Among its regions, the Somali region is disproportionately affected, ranking among the top two in the country for zero-dose prevalence.16 A recent spatial analysis further identified this region as a hotspot, with concentrated clusters of zero-dose children.15 Compounding this issue, multiple measles outbreaks have occurred in the study area, with 1435 reported cases between 2019 and 2023, emphasising the urgent need for targeted immunisation strategies to reduce vaccine gaps and prevent further outbreaks.

On the other hand, the qualitative findings indicated that zero-dose children are mainly concentrated in urban peripheries and urban outskirts with gaps in its proper identification/mapping. While urban children generally have better vaccination rates compared with their rural counterparts, disparities persist within urban settings8 particularly informal settlements, which tend to have a high concentration of zero-dose children.26 Rapid urbanisation exerts pressure on infrastructure, leading to informal settlements with unequal service access.6 These communities often experience multiple deprivations, necessitating context-specific and tailored immunisation strategies that require political commitment and targeted investments.4 5 27

This study highlights the strong link between zero-dose and limited maternal healthcare access. Mothers who did not attend ANC, receive tetanus diphtheria (Td) vaccination or have home deliveries were more likely to have zero-dose children, while those who attended at least one PNC visit had lower prevalence. Supporting research confirms similar trends, associating childhood immunisation gaps with inadequate maternal care, lack of skilled birth assistance and geographic barriers.11 21 22 Maternal health services, particularly ANC and PNC visits, play a vital role in educating mothers about vaccinations and fostering engagement with healthcare providers, who serve as trusted sources of immunisation information.28 The broader health system offers multiple opportunities for immunisation, including ANC, PNC, growth monitoring and illness treatment.29 This reinforces the importance of an integrated primary healthcare (PHC) approach, where combining maternal, child and nutritional services enhances overall health outcomes.20

The findings further revealed gaps in strategies to reach zero-dose and missed communities as the most common strategy used in urban areas is mainly facility-based immunisation strategy. This is consistent with a qualitative study conducted in three major urban areas in Ethiopia which identified over-reliance on facility-based immunisation service delivery models, often hindered by inaccessibility, poor tracking systems and supply shortages.30 A number of supply and demand barriers are underpinning the zero-dose burden in most informal settlements or urban poor including invisibility in official surveys and census data used for planning, exclusion from services, information gaps about essential services,31 on top of limited healthcare availability, long wait times, transportation barriers and vaccine stockouts which further hinder immunisation uptake.6 8 Other studies also reaffirmed gaps in healthcare, high staff turnover, lack of awareness and limited media exposure and gender norms contributing to high zero-dose prevalence.12 14

Household perceived wealth status was also identified to significantly affect the zero-dose vaccination, with the poorer households experiencing the highest zero-dose prevalence. This is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia and Somalia.11 23 32 Despite overall progress in national immunisation rates, socioeconomic disparities persist, disproportionately affecting lower wealth quintiles.33 In Ethiopia, vaccination coverage follows a pro-rich pattern where children from wealthier households are less likely to be zero-dose compared with children from low-income or poorer households.34 Similarly, in the Somali region, children from lower-income households and those living in female-headed households are less likely to complete the full course of vaccinations,34 increasing their risk of vaccine-preventable diseases.35 Financial constraints often limit caregivers’ ability to afford indirect vaccination costs, with women frequently facing restricted access to family funds. Additionally, traditional gender roles reduce fathers’ involvement in childcare, including vaccination decisions, further affecting immunisation rates.20

Additionally, the study underscores the role of caregiver permission in vaccination decisions, revealing that children were more likely to remain zero-dose when caregivers lacked the autonomy to consent. This aligns with existing research showing a strong link between maternal autonomy and child immunisation rates, where greater household decision-making power for women is associated with improved child health outcomes.36 37

The study’s strength lies in its mixed-methods approach, combining community surveys with adequate sample size and interviews of mid-level policymakers involved in immunisation planning and delivery across three urban study sites. However, the study has limitations including reliance on maternal recall for vaccination status which may introduce recall bias and lead to inaccurate reporting. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between sociodemographic factors and immunisation status, and the qualitative component included fewer participants than anticipated. Moreover, incorporating focus group discussions with urban and periurban communities could have provided deeper insights into their perspectives on access to immunisation and other primary healthcare services, further enriching the findings.

Conclusions

The high zero-dose prevalence in urban areas is associated with multiple service delivery and demand-side barriers. Effective identification and mapping of zero-dose children in underserved urban communities, alongside the flexible and diversified outreach strategies beyond fixed service provision, are essential. Strengthening engagement, advocacy and targeted interventions to address both supply and demand barriers will be key in reducing the zero-dose burden and improving immunisation coverage.
