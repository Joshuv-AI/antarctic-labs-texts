Is Type 2 Diabetes a Modifiable Risk Factor for the Evolution and Progression of Heart Failure With a Preserved Ejection Fraction?
Authors: Milton Packer, Carolyn SP Lam, Javed Butler, Faiez Zannad, Muthiah Vaduganathan, Barry A Borlaug
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDType 2 diabetes is associated with an increased risk of heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), but it is not clear whether this metabolic disorder is causal or represents a modifiable risk factor. Mechanisms by which diabetes may be associated with HFpEF can be grouped into the following: 1) those related to hyperglycemia and amenable to antihyperglycemic drugs; and 2) those related to the association of type 2 diabetes with obesity and visceral adiposity, and thus, treatable with interventions that reduce adipose tissue mass or improve adipocyte biology.EVIDENCE AGAINST A ROLE FOR HYPERGLYCEMIAExperimentally, acute and chronic hyperglycemia caused by islet cell destruction can lead to cardiac dysfunction, but these models resemble type 1 (not type 2) diabetes. Heightened levels of environmental glucose can cause enzymatic or nonenzymatic modification of proteins and signaling through the polyol pathway, but interference with these mechanisms has not produce clinical benefits in patients with heart disease and type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, lowering of blood glucose in type 2 diabetes with insulin, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors and thiazolidinediones has not reduced the risk of heart failure.EVIDENCE FOR A MEDIATING ROLE FOR ADIPOSITYIn marked contrast, experimental models that link type 2 diabetes to HFpEF are typically accompanied by excess adiposity. Epidemiological studies demonstrate that the association between type 2 diabetes and HFpEF is mediated primarily through a common link with central obesity and an expanded visceral fat mass. Changes in the biology of adipocytes as a result of visceral adiposity are sufficient to cause systemic insulin resistance and diabetes. Interestingly, the primary metabolic defect in the diabetic heart is lipid overload, not an impairment in glucose uptake or insulin resistance. Adiposity can promote HFpEF through the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines that lead to sodium retention and cardiac steatosis and fibrosis. Additionally, excess adiposity can drive the production of and enhance cardiac sensitivity to advanced glycation end products. Glucagon-like peptide receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter reduce the risk or progression of HFpEF, but this benefit is not related by the presence of diabetes or to the glucose-lowering effects of these drugs. Instead, their favorable cardiac effects may be mediated by their action to induce or mimic a state of caloric deprivation, thus restoring adipokine balance and alleviating the state of cardiac steatosis. Similarly, bariatric surgery alleviates both visceral adiposity and type 2 diabetes and reduces the risk of HFpEF.CONCLUSIONSTaken together, these findings suggest that diabetes-associated HFpEF is mediated primarily through its association with excess adiposity. Diabetes is a modifiable risk factor if treatment is directed toward adiposity rather than hyperglycemia.Keywords: diabetes, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, visceral adiposity

Heart failure is the most important cardiovascular complication of diabetes.1 Among patients with type 2 diabetes, the incidence of heart failure is greater than the incidence of myocardial infarction or stroke.2 Diabetes doubles the risk of developing heart failure in the general population,3 and it triples the risk of developing heart failure in people with known coronary artery disease.4

Progressive increases in hemoglobin A1c are accompanied by stepwise increases in the risk of heart failure.4,5 Patients with type 2 diabetes are at particularly increased risk of heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). 6 In patients without coronary artery disease or a history of myocardial infarction, heart failure in patients with diabetes is largely manifest as HFpEF, rather than heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).6

Because of these strong epidemiological associations, it has been proposed that type 2 diabetes represent a causal pathway in the evolution and progression of HFpEF.7,8 Numerous molecular, cellular, and pathophysiological mechanisms have been postulated that might mediate a link between diabetes and HFpEF. These mechanisms can be broadly grouped into 2 domains: 1) those related to hyperglycemia and amenable to antihyperglycemic drugs or to agents that interrupt downstream effectors; and 2) those related to associated obesity and visceral adiposity and treatable with interventions that reduce adipose tissue mass or improve adipocyte biology (Figure 1).

Potential Pathways By Which Hyperglycemia or Visceral Adiposity Might Promote the Development and Progression of Heart Failure With a Preserved Ejection Fraction

Heightened environmental glucose may stimulate flux through specific pathways: 1) the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway leading to protein modification by O-GlcNAcylation; 2) the polyol pathway leading to depletion of antioxidants and osmotic cellular stress; 3) the pentose phosphate pathway, leading to activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase, yielding reactive oxygen species; and 4) advanced glycation end products, which can cause crosslinks or can interact with the receptor for advanced glycation end products, leading to adipokine imbalance, cardiac steatosis, and fibrosis. Visceral adiposity can also promote the synthesis of advanced glycation end products and can augment O-GlcNAcylation, but more importantly, leads to an imbalance in the secretion of protective and deleterious adipokines that have been demonstrated to cause ventricular hypertrophy and fibrosis as well as cardiac lipotoxicity, leading to heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). There is no defined role for insulin resistance in the development of HFpEF. GLUT4 = glucose transporter 4.

In this paper, we examine the evidence for and against hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and visceral adiposity as the potential mediators of HFpEF in patients with type 2 diabetes.

RELATIVE IMPORTANCE OF HYPERGLYCEMIA, VISCERAL ADIPOSITY, AND INSULIN RESISTANCE IN THE DEVELOPMENT OF HFpEF

Coronary microvascular dysfunction is a seminal feature of HFpEF,8 and microvascular complications of diabetes have been attributed to uncontrolled hyperglycemia.9 Therefore, it would seem reasonable to consider the hypothesis that hyperglycemia in patients with type 2 diabetes drives the development of HFpEF.

DOES HYPERGLYCEMIA IN TYPE 2 DIABETES CAUSE HFpEF?

Exceptional degrees of short-term hyperglycemia can cause oxidative stress, mitochondrial abnormalities, cellular dysfunction, and death in isolated cardiomyocytes and in coronary arterial endothelial cells.10–15 In animal models where streptozotocin has been used to induce the destruction of pancreatic islet cells, the resulting chronic hyperglycemia can be accompanied by cardiac hypertrophy and contractile dysfunction,16,17 Heightened levels of environmental glucose can cause enzymatic or nonenzymatic modification of proteins. The consequences may include protein O-GlcNAcylation (through the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway) or the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), respectively (Figure 1).

Mice with a genetic defect that results in sustained protein O-GlcNAcylation develop an HFpEF phenotype,18,19 but excessive O-GlcNAcylation eventually leads to a progressive dilated cardiomyopathy.20,21 Streptozotocin-induced diabetes is accompanied by enhanced O-GlcNAcylation, leading to cardiac abnormalities and the development of HFpEF.21,22 Additionally, hyperglycemia related to streptozotocin-induced diabetes can promote redox stress (by activating the polyol pathway), and it can also activate nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (through the pentose phosphate pathway), both potentially leading to HFpEF.23,24 In other models of islet cell destruction, chronic hyperglycemia is also accompanied by increased formation of AGEs, whose crosslinking has been hypothesized to lead to enhanced cardiac stiffness and HFpEF.25,26

However, the clinical relevance of experimental observations concerning a potential role for non-glycolytic glucose disposal pathways is unclear, since streptozotocin-induced diabetes mimics type 1—but not type 2—diabetes. Thus, efforts to translate findings in type 1 diabetes to patients with type 2 diabetes have been disappointing. Specifically, inhibition of aldose reductase (the rate-limiting enzyme for the polyol pathway) does not exert beneficial effects in patients with type 2 diabetes-associated cardiomyopathy.27 Additionally, pharmacological interference with AGE crosslinks does not yield favorable results in HFrEF, even in patients with diabetes.28

Therefore, although hyperglycemia can induce maladaptive pathways in experimental models of type 1 diabetes, there is little evidence that these mechanisms play a central role in the development of HFpEF in patients with type 2 diabetes.

EMERGENCE OF VISCERAL ADIPOSITY AS THE DRIVER OF DIABETES AND DIABETES-ASSOCIATED HFpEF.

The principal feature that distinguishes type 1 from type 2 diabetes is that type 2 diabetes is characterized by obesity and visceral adiposity, leading to insulin resistance. More than 80% of patients with type 2 diabetes are overweight or have obesity.29 Excess visceral adiposity precedes and predicts the development of type 2 diabetes and is present in >90% of patients.30–34 Most importantly, in Mendelian randomization analyses, visceral adiposity has emerged as the most important root cause of type 2 diabetes.35

Central adiposity and visceral fat mass also precede and predict the development of HFpEF36,37 (but not HFrEF38), and >80% to 90% of patients with HFpEF have central obesity with a waist-to-height ratio ≥0.5 (Table 1).39–42 Similarly, animal models that have relied on type 2 diabetes (not type 1 diabetes) as a mechanism for inducing experimental HFpEF are characterized by a high-fat diet and obesity.43,44

Bariatric surgery offers a direct test of the relative importance of excess adiposity, because its primary action is to create a state of caloric deprivation. Gastric bypass surgery acts to alleviate both visceral adiposity and type 2 diabetes, with the remission of diabetes being more common in those with greater reduction in visceral fat mass.45–47 Importantly, surgically-mediated weight loss is also associated with a markedly reduced risk of heart failure, particularly HFpEF,48 with decreases in left ventricular mass being more strongly related to changes in visceral adipose tissue than to changes in weight.49

In 2 large National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute–sponsored cohorts, the risk of heart failure was increased in patients with type 2 diabetes if they had a body mass index ≥30 kg/m2 or a waist circumference ≥100 cm (indicative of central obesity). Yet, in these cohorts, diabetes was not associated with HFpEF in the absence of adiposity.50 In accordance with these observations, the influence of diabetes and glycemic control on the risk of heart failure was markedly attenuated if central adiposity was included in the predictive model.51

Taken collectively, these observations support the premise that diabetes is associated with the development of HFpEF through its relationship with central obesity and visceral adiposity.

ROLE OF VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IN THE PATHOGENESIS OF DIABETES-ASSOCIATED HFpEF.

An increase in visceral fat mass in patients with diabetes can directly influence the heart because visceral adiposity is accompanied by increase in the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines (eg, leptin and others) and suppression of anti-inflammatory adipokines (eg, adiponectin and others).52,53 These derangements are particularly notable in diabetic patients with the metabolic syndrome, presumably because they have a greater degree of central obesity and visceral adiposity.54–56

This adipokine imbalance seen in states of adiposity can lead to cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, and thus to HFpEF.52,53 Furthermore, in predictive models, the inclusion of circulating levels of adipokines accounts for the association of visceral adiposity and type 2 diabetes.57 The actions of adipokines may be particularly pronounced and biologically relevant when they are secreted in close proximity to the heart, eg, from an expanded epicardial adipose tissue mass or from the drainage of pericardial fat into the right atrium.58 Diabetes potentiates the effect of obesity to cause expansion of epicardial adipose tissue.59 An expansion of epicardial and pericardial fat mass may also invade the pericardial space, leading to pericardial constraint and worsening HFpEF.60 Furthermore, obesity can promote O-GlcNAcylation independently of diabetes.61

VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IS THE PRINCIPAL CAUSE OF INSULIN RESISTANCE.

A central feature of type 2 diabetes is insulin resistance, a physiological event that—both experimentally and clinically—is causally related to a down-regulation of the expression of insulin-sensitive glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) in adipose tissue.62–64 Experimental down-regulation of GLUT4—specifically and selectively in adipocytes—is sufficient to produce systemic insulin resistance. Adipocyte-induced insulin resistance produces abnormalities of glucose uptake in both skeletal muscle and the liver, even though GLUT4 is not altered in these tissues.65 Conversely, adipose-specific overexpression of GLUT4 can reverse skeletal muscle insulin resistance and reverse diabetes.66

How does adipocyte down-regulation of GLUT4 cause insulin resistance in the liver and skeletal muscle? Adiposity-mediated down-regulation of GLUT4 in adipose tissue promotes lipolysis.67,68 The resulting increase in circulating free fatty acids promotes the hepatic overproduction of glucose, but impairs peripheral glucose utilization68 —the key features that lead to insulin resistance and hyperglycemia in people with type 2 diabetes. Experimentally, the release of fatty acids from adipose tissue as a result of lipolysis suppresses glucose uptake and induces insulin resistance in the heart.69

In addition, the deranged adipokine imbalance in states of visceral adiposity plays a seminal role in promoting insulin resistance.52,70 Adiponectin (a cardioprotective adipokine) enhances insulin sensitivity by promoting GLUT4 translocation to the cell surface,71 but the secretion of adiponectin is suppressed in states of excess adiposity.52 In return, GLUT4 down-regulation enhances the actions of retinol binding protein 4, a proinflammatory adipokine that is upregulated by visceral adiposity.52,72 The selective upregulated expression of retinol binding protein 4 in hypertrophied adipocytes promotes further insulin resistance by augmenting hepatic gluconeogenesis, impairing skeletal muscle glucose uptake and promoting lipolysis.72,73 The suppression of adipocyte GLUT4 also increases hepatic lipid production and peripheral lipid utilization.74

Taken together, these observations demonstrate how an expansion of visceral adipocyte mass and the resulting alteration in adipocyte biology can lead to the development of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

ROLE OF CARDIAC STEATOSIS IN THE PATHOGENESIS OF DIABETES-ASSOCIATED HFpEF.

Although GLUT4 can be down-regulated in the diabetic heart,75 such a cardiac isoform shift is not specific to diabetes,76 and nevertheless, the heart appears to retain its sensitivity to insulin in type 2 diabetes.77,78 The reported decrease in cardiac glucose uptake in diabetes is not related to changes in GLUT4 expression,79 and it does not impair energy production. Importantly, experimental restoration of glucose utilization does not improve cardiac function, but instead, it causes myocardial abnormalities.80

The primary metabolic abnormality in heart in type 2 diabetes is not mediated by changes in glucose uptake, but instead, it is characterized by a markedly augmented uptake of fatty acids.81,82 The down-regulation of adipocyte GLUT4 causes an increase in circulating free fatty acids (related to adipose tissue lipolysis) along with increased peripheral lipid uptake.68,69,72–74 At the same time, cell-surface and intracellular transporters of fatty acids are upregulated in the diabetic heart.83–86 Even when mitochondrial function is preserved, the oversupply of fatty acids in cardiomyocytes leads to the accumulation of deleterious intracellular lipid byproducts (eg, ceramide and diacylglycerol).86–89 Furthermore, enhancement of fatty acid uptake increases intramyocardial content of lipids (typically triacylglycerides), which present themselves as a surfeit of cytosolic lipid droplets.88–90

Cardioprotective adipokines (ie, adiponectin and its paralogs) would typically act to prevent the emergence of cardiac steatosis,91,92 because they suppress fatty acid uptake and promote fatty acid metabolic disposal. However, these signaling molecules are deficient in states of visceral adiposity.52–56

As a result, the heart in type 2 diabetes is saturated with fat, and cardiac steatosis precedes the development of cardiomyopathy in patients with diabetes.89,90,93 The resulting cardiac lipotoxicity impairs glucose oxidation, promotes oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, and induces structural changes in the heart.87–89,94 The magnitude of adverse remodeling in diabetes is related to the degree of myocardial lipid accumulation.95–99 The development of cardiac lipid overload is linked to an expansion of total body adipocyte mass100 but it is not linked to the level of glycemic control.101,102 Cardiac steatosis is strongly associated with obesity, diabetes, myocardial fibrosis, and diastolic filling abnormalities.103,104

CRITICAL REEVALUATION OF THE ROLE OF INSULIN RESISTANCE IN DIABETES-ASSOCIATED HFpEF.

In the clinical setting, when visceral adiposity induces insulin resistance,67–69,105 the hyperinsulinemia that is seen in states of obesity106,107 can further promote adipogenesis, including worsening of myocardial lipid accumulation.101,106–108 Theoretically, hyperinsulinemia can exert direct deleterious effects on the myocardium109 and may also exert antidiuretic actions and promote sodium retention.110 Despite these mechanistic possibilities, therapeutic augmentation of circulating insulin in a randomized controlled trial has not resulted in an increased risk of heart failure,111 thus raising questions about the pathophysiological importance of hyperinsulinemia.

Furthermore, although both insulin resistance and central adiposity are predictive of HFpEF,51 it is the excess adiposity—not the insulin resistance or hyperinsulinemia—that is specifically linked to HFpEF.112–114 Insulin resistance has not been consistently predictive of HFpEF,40,113,114 and the presence of insulin resistance does not identify patients with worse HFpEF.112

Moreover, thiazolidinediones improve insulin resistance and mitigate hyperinsulinemia.115 However, they promote left ventricular hypertrophy,116–118 and they do not have favorable effects on cardiac steatosis98 or on experimental high-fat diet-induced HFpEF119 —potentially because they promote adipogenesis and can exacerbate central adiposity.120,121 The central importance of adipocytes in mediating the action of thiazolidinediones on the heart is highlighted by experimental studies demonstrating that silencing of the target of thiazolidinediones specifically in adipose tissue (but not in cardiomyocytes) prevents the effect of these drugs on cardiac hypertrophy (Table 1, Figure 1).118,122

Therefore, current evidence suggests that insulin resistance—as a biological phenomenon—is not the primary driver of HFpEF in type 2 diabetes. Instead, both insulin resistance and HFpEF are associated with each other because they both arise from a shared upstream mediator—visceral adiposity. Visceral adiposity (leading to a deleterious adipokine imbalance)—working in concert with the paracrine consequences of myocardial lipid overload—emerges as the principal mechanism that precedes and underlies the structural and functional derangements of the heart in patients with type 2 diabetes (Figure 1). Once cardiac steatosis is established and heart failure ensues, patients with HFpEF and diabetes have more advanced structural heart disease, greater exercise intolerance, more heart failure hospitalizations, and a worse prognosis.123–127

INTERPLAY OF ADIPOSITY AND ADVANCED GLYCATION END PRODUCTS

Despite the importance of adiposity and lipotoxicity in mediating the effects of diabetes on HFpEF, is it possible that the downstream effects of hyperglycemia might still be playing a role? It has been proposed that the adverse effect of hyperglycemia on the heart may be mediated by the synthesis of advanced glycation end products (AGEs). AGEs are formed nonenzymatically by the interaction of carbonyl groups of sugar with free amine groups of circulating proteins or lipids. AGEs exert deleterious effects on the cardiovascular system (in large part) by interacting with a specific membrane-bound receptor, known as receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE). The AGE-RAGE axis can induce proinflammatory and profibrotic reactions in the heart.128,129

ADIPOSITY AS THE DRIVER OF ADVANCED GLYCATION END PRODUCTS SIGNALING.

Adipose tissue represents an important site of AGE production in patients with type 2 diabetes. The secretion of AGEs by adipocytes represents a mechanism by which adiposity can exert adverse effects on the cardiovascular system,130 particularly since hypertrophied adipocytes in visceral white adipose tissue upregulate their expression of RAGE. Activation of the AGE-RAGE axis leads to further adipose tissue expansion and inflammation,131–134 whereas RAGE antagonism alleviates diet-induced obesity.135 Additionally, augmentation of the AGE-RAGE interaction in adipocytes can promote further secretion of proinflammatory adipokines, thereby aggravating both insulin resistance and cardiac inflammation.52,136–138

Obesity not only increases the production of AGEs, but it also causes up-regulation of membrane-bound RAGE in the myocardium.139 This up-regulation would presumably act to facilitate the ability of circulating AGEs to exert effects on the heart. The resulting augmentation of RAGE signaling can promote derangements in calcium handling, cellular stress, and mitochondrial abnormalities. RAGE signaling can lead to cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, myocardial lipid accumulation, and cardiomyopathy.140–145 Suppression of RAGE ameliorates adverse ventricular remodeling in experimental HFpEF.146

Interestingly, a soluble form of receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE) acts as a decoy molecule for circulating RAGE agonists.145 Circulating levels of sRAGE are increased (as a compensatory response) in patients with heart failure, particularly those with diastolic filling abnormalities.147–149 However, the presence of obesity attenuates the formation of sRAGE,150 reducing this adaptive buffering mechanism. This action provides yet an additional mechanism by which adiposity might potentiate the ability of AGEs to exert deleterious effects.151

These observations suggest that, if hyperglycemia-related enhanced AGE signaling were relevant to the pathogenesis of HFpEF, the adverse effects of AGE-RAGE signaling are likely to be directly regulated by the degree of excess adiposity, and therefore, they might be ameliorated only if obesity were simultaneously managed.

DOES TREATMENT OF HYPERGLYCEMIA OR ADIPOSITY REDUCE THE EVOLUTION OR PROGRESSION OF HFpEF IN PATIENTS WITH TYPE DIABETES?

A potential role for hyperglycemia-related AGE signaling would be supported if there were evidence that therapeutic modulation of hyperglycemia influenced the development of heart failure (and HFpEF) in patients with type 2 diabetes. If such a mechanism were important, then the lowering of blood glucose with the use of antihyperglycemic drugs would be expected to reduce the risk of heart failure, including HFpEF.

However, large-scale randomized controlled trials have persuasively demonstrated the lack of any consistent relationship between glycemic control and a reduced risk of heart failure hospitalizations (Table 2) in patients with type 2 diabetes.111,152–156 In these trials, the use of basal insulin, sulfonylureas, and dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 inhibitors in diabetic patients has not been accompanied by a reduced risk of heart failure, even though each of these classes of drugs produces meaningful declines of glycated hemoglobin.152,153,155 Furthermore, the use of thiazolidinediones has led to a significant increase in the risk of heart failure, potentially related to the sodium retention that accompanies the use of these drugs.154 However, it should be emphasized that thiazolidinediones also have a direct effect to exacerbate obesity-related cardiac hypertrophy through an adipocyte-mediated mechanism.118,122

GLUCAGON-LIKE PEPTIDE 1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS AND SODIUM-GLUCOSE COTRANSPORTER 2 INHIBITORS DO NOT EXERT FAVORABLE EFFECTS IN PREVENTING OR TREATING HFpEF BY LOWERING BLOOD GLUCOSE.

In contrast with other antihyperglycemic drugs, the use of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors has led to a decrease in the risk of heart failure, including HFpEF.

In randomized controlled trials, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists has been accompanied by a small overall reduction in the relative risk of heart failure hospitalizations in patients with type 2 diabetes (HR: 0.89 [95% CI: 0.78–0.94]).155 This modest benefit is related to a meaningful reduction in the risk of HFpEF, which appears to be counterbalanced by a potential increase in the risk of HFrEF (Table 2).157 Specifically, in patients with type 2 diabetes in the EXSCEL (Exenatide Study of Cardiovascular Event Lowering) trial, GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced the risk of all-cause mortality and heart failure hospitalizations in patients with HFpEF (HR: 0.73 [95% CI: 0.60–0.89]).158 Additionally, GLP-1 receptor signaling has produced symptomatic benefits and a reduction in worsening heart failure events in patients with HFpEF with obesity.159,160 Yet, the magnitude of the improvement is not enhanced in patients who also have diabetes.161,162 Baseline levels of hemoglobin A1c or changes in hemoglobin A1c during treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists do not identify patients with HFpEF who experience a greater benefit from these drugs.162

Similarly, among patients with type 2 diabetes but without heart failure, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors has been accompanied by a striking decrease in the risk of heart failure hospitalizations (HR: 0.69 [95% CI: 0.62–0.76]).156 This benefit is apparent, even though these drugs exert only a modest glucose-lowering effect, as compared with other antihyperglycemic agents (Table 2). Furthermore, among patients with HFpEF, SGLT2 inhibitors reduce the risk of cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalization. Yet, the magnitude of this effect is not influenced by the presence of diabetes,163,164 even though diabetic patients experience an antihyperglycemic response, which is not seen in nondiabetic patients. Baseline levels of or changes in hemoglobin A1c during treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors do not identify patients with diabetes or with HFpEF who experience a greater benefit.164,165

Based on the totality of evidence, it is apparent that there is no relationship between the effect of a drug to lower blood glucose in patients with type 2 diabetes and its effect to prevent the development of HFpEF or improve the clinical course of patients with clinically identified HFpEF, if they also have diabetes. These findings are not consistent with the hypothesis that hyperglycemia and the downstream effectors of glucotoxicity (demonstrated experimentally) play a role in the development or progression of HFpEF.

EFFECT OF GLP-1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS AND SGLT2 INHIBITORS ON ADIPOSITY.

What mechanisms—other than glucose lowering—might underlie the effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors to prevent and treat HFpEF?

The clinical benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with HFpEF and obesity are related to the magnitude of weight loss.166,167 Similarly, SGLT2 inhibitors may be particularly effective in patients with HFpEF and obesity,168–170 with the magnitude of weight loss being related to the presence of obesity before treatment and the improvement in exercise tolerance during treatment.168,170 Although the weight loss produced by SGLT2 inhibitors is modest, these drugs have been shown to induce a state of global starvation mimicry, as evidenced by their effects to enhance intracellular nutrient deprivation signaling and autophagic flux across multiple organs, including the heart,94,171,172 thereby mimicking the antiadipogenic actions of incretin-based drugs.

Because both GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors promote nutrient deprivation reprogramming, both classes of drugs inhibitors act directly on adipocytes to improve mitochondrial health and fat utilization while reducing cellular hypertrophy, stress, and inflammation.173–182 Accordingly, both GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors produce meaningful decreases in visceral fat mass,182–193 with the reduction in visceral adiposity being disproportionately larger than the decrease in body weight. Importantly, the magnitude of the effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors on visceral adiposity is substantially greater than with other antihyperglycemic agents.190,194

The transformation of visceral adipose tissue during treatment with incretin-based drugs and SGLT2 inhibitors may lead to the suppression of the adipose secretion of proinflammatory adipokines from an expanded fat mass as well as enhanced adipose secretion of cardioprotective adipokines.52,195 Both GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors alleviate experimental diabetes-associated cardiac adiposity, lipotoxicity, and HFpEF.196–201 a benefit not possessed by other antihyperglycemic agents.101,102 Decreases in visceral fat in patients with HFpEF following SGLT2 inhibition are correlated with decreases in cardiac filling pressures.202

In contrast with the benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors on both visceral adiposity and HFpEF, treatment with insulin, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and thiazolidinediones does not exert favorable effects on (and may often exacerbate) visceral or myocardial adiposity11,101,102,152–154,203–208 or on the clinical evolution of heart failure (specifically HFpEF) (Table 2).101,102

Therefore, the benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors on the evolution and progression of HFpEF appear to be related—not to their glucose-lowering effects—but to their favorable effects on adipose tissue mass and biology. This hypothesis is strongly support by the findings that the shrinkage of visceral fat depots by bariatric surgery restores adipokine balance and reduces the risk of HFpEF.49–53,58,209

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Based on extensive experimental and clinical evidence, the association of type 2 diabetes and HFpEF appears to be primarily related to the effect of visceral adiposity to drive the development of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and HFpEF, independently of hyperglycemia. When visceral adiposity is accounted for, there is little evidence of an independent relationship among diabetes, hyperglycemia, or insulin resistance and the development of HFpEF. Furthermore, there is little evidence that drug-induced lowering of blood glucose or enhancement of insulin sensitivity has favorable effects on the evolution of HFpEF. Some antihyperglycemic drugs may exert deleterious effects on the heart through their actions on adipose tissue.

In contrast, bariatric surgery alleviates both diabetes and HFpEF, and the benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors on HFpEF may be mediated by their actions to reduce adipocyte mass, minimize fat deposition around the heart, and alleviate the deranged biology of adipocytes in states of excess adiposity. Obesity and visceral adiposity not only predispose to both diabetes and HFpEF, but it may also sensitize the heart to the adverse effects of diabetes- or adiposity-associated AGEs.

The term “diabetic cardiomyopathy” reflects a 50-year autopsy finding,210 and neither experimental nor clinical evidence supports the hypothesis that diabetes (hyperglycemia) has direct effects on the myocardium or leads to heart failure.211,212 A recent consensus statement has reinforced the conclusion that diabetes (acting by itself) is rarely responsible for myocardial dysfunction, but instead, the association of diabetes with HFpEF is mediated by its relationship adiposity.212

Taken together, multiple convergent lines of evidence support a conceptual framework in which diabetes-associated HFpEF may be best explained by a shared upstream driver for both diabetes and HFpEF—excess visceral adiposity. Accordingly, diabetes would represent a modifiable risk factor for HFpEF if treatment were directed toward excess adiposity rather than hyperglycemia.
