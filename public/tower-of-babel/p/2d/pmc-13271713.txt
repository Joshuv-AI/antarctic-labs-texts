Commentary on “A Clinical Approach to Pediatric Functional Urinary Disorders in Settings without Urodynamic Studies” – Role of uroflowmetry
Authors: Ramesh Babu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Viswanathan et al.[1] regarding the management of pediatric functional urinary disorders in resource-limited settings. While the authors’ effort to provide a structured clinical approach is noted, several conceptual and methodological inconsistencies warrant clarification to prevent the misguidance of trainees and clinicians.

The authors use “lower urinary tract symptoms” primarily in the context of toddlers, but their categorization of children under 4 years as having “functional voiding disorders” directly contradicts International Children’s Continence Society guidelines.[2] Bladder control typically occurs by age 5 and branding children who have not yet achieved toilet training as having “dysfunction” is misleading.

The study[1] repeatedly uses the term “clinical urodynamics,” which is misleading and unconventional. Urodynamics, by definition, refers to the study of the pressure-flow relationship during the filling and emptying phases. A more accurate description of the authors’ methodology would be “comprehensive clinical evaluation” incorporating history, physical examination, and voiding diaries.

The methodology used to reach specific urodynamic diagnoses without objective testing is highly illogical. The authors diagnosed detrusor overactivity (DO) based on ultrasound findings of bladder wall thickening. However, DO is strictly a urodynamic finding. Bladder wall thickening is nonspecific and can result from lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO) or detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD). Patients were diagnosed with DSD based on symptoms such as “poor urinary stream” or “hesitancy.” A poor stream can easily be attributed to LUTO or underutilization; only uroflowmetry demonstrating a staccato pattern can definitively depict DSD. Furthermore, the first-line treatments for DSD, biofeedback and urotherapy were not adequately emphasized or bypassed in favor of clean intermittent catheterization (CIC). The diagnosis of hypoactive bladder was reached clinically when a large postvoid residual (PVR) was noted. However, a significant PVR can stem from LUTO or DSD. Objective demonstration of poor detrusor pressure during the voiding phase is required for this diagnosis.

Claims that urodynamic study (UDS) findings are “limited” or unreliable in younger children are not substantiated by the data presented and are contradicted by the wide variability in UDS patterns observed in this very cohort.[1] The authors suggest that because invasive UDSs are unavailable, a purely clinical approach is the alternative. However, they admit to not performing uroflowmetry, which is a non-invasive, cost-effective tool that, when combined with EMG, provides superior information. The conclusion that management based on clinical assessment alone is “effective” is overstated, given the absence of a comparator group, or standardized scores like dysfunctional voiding symptom score. By blending over active bladder (OAB) with urodynamic diagnoses (DO/DSD) without the necessary objective data, this paper risks confusing readers and promoting a culture of “diagnostic guessing.”

Uroflowmetry has been applied as a very cost-effective noninvasive tool [Figure 1] in categorizing LUTD in children.[3] Typically, children with DSD have staccato uroflow pattern (trace does not reach baseline); EMG may show increased external sphincter contraction during voiding. They benefit from urotherapy and biofeedback after controlling constipation. Children with OAB often have tower shaped uroflow with large Qmax and they benefit from anticholinergics/beta agonists. Children with under active bladder have interrupted uroflow due to Valsalva voiding (trace reaches baseline) with large post void residuals. They benefit from bladder training (timed voids), cholinergics and lastly CIC. Addition of uroflow along with electromyogram has been proven to be more effective in characterisation of external sphincter activity during voiding.[4] We urge a move toward standardized nomenclature and the use of non-invasive tools like uroflowmetry, rather than declaring urodynamics[5] “unnecessary” based on a study that did not utilize basic flow assessments.

Uroflowmetry aids in segregation of children with LUTD into subgroups and select the appropriate treatment. Invasive urodynamic assessment is usually not performed for childhood voiding dysfunction. (In those with obstructive uropathy, post pull-through, spinal dysraphism etc. full urodynamic study is preferred)
