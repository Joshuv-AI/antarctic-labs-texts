The Triglyceride Glucose-a Body Shape Index: A Novel Composite Index for Prediction of Periodontitis Risk
Authors: Yuchen Cui, Wei Guo, Xiaoli Gao, Hao Zhang, Hongjia Yang, Zuomin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and AimInsulin resistance and obesity are significant metabolic risk factors for periodontitis. This study aimed to systematically investigate the association between the novel metabolic obesity composite index, triglyceride glucose-a body shape index (TyG-ABSI), and the risk of periodontitis, further evaluating its clinical predictive performance.MethodsData from 4545 participants were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2009 to 2014. To investigate the relationship between TyG-ABSI and the risk of periodontitis, weighted multivariable logistic regression models and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used. Exploratory mediation analysis was conducted to assess the statistical contributions of potential related factors to the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis. Predictive models were constructed using various machine learning (ML) algorithms. Model performance was evaluated and compared using the receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration curve, decision curve analysis, net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement.ResultsA significant positive association was observed between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis risk (OR = 1.193, 95% CI: 1.089-1.308), with the highest tertile conferring a 2.25-fold elevated risk relative to the lowest tertile (OR = 2.252, 95% CI: 1.428-3.550). Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a linear dose-response relationship. Subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction with age, with the highest risk observed in individuals aged 45 to 59 years (OR = 1.320, 95% CI: 1.110-1.560). Exploratory mediation analysis showed that HbA1c and WBC accounted for 38.8% and 12.2% of the total association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis, respectively. Machine learning models further suggested that the TyG-ABSI index has potential value in periodontitis risk prediction.ConclusionThe novel composite index TyG-ABSI exhibits a significant positive association with periodontitis risk. It can serve as a reference indicator for periodontitis risk prediction.Clinical RelevanceTyG-ABSI integrates information related to insulin resistance and abdominal obesity. The present findings suggest that individuals with this metabolic–body shape abnormality phenotype should pay greater attention to their periodontal health status.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a common chronic inflammatory condition marked by progressive destruction of the supporting tissues of the teeth, making it a primary cause of tooth loss in adults.1 The disease is primarily initiated by bacteria within subgingival plaque biofilms, with clinical manifestations including pathological changes such as gingival bleeding, periodontal pocket formation and alveolar bone resorption.2 According to the Global Burden of Disease Study data, there were 1.1 billion cases of severe periodontitis worldwide in 2019, with a prevalence rate of 13.1%.3 Periodontitis is not confined to the oral cavity but also impacts systemic health. Extensive research has confirmed that periodontitis can act as a source of systemic inflammation, exacerbating the overall metabolic burden through mechanisms such as bacteraemia, release of inflammatory factors and immune dysregulation.4, 5, 6 It is closely associated with conditions such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes.7, 8, 9, 10 Therefore, achieving early diagnosis and effective intervention is of significant importance for improving public health and reducing the disease burden.

Metabolic dysregulation is recognised as an important risk factor for periodontitis.11 Insulin resistance (IR), as a core pathological component of metabolic syndrome, is closely associated with periodontitis.12,13 Evidence indicates that IR intensifies local inflammation in periodontal tissues, accelerates alveolar bone loss and diminishes tissue healing ability.14 The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, computed from fasting triglyceride and glucose values, functions as a dependable proxy indicator of IR. Due to its operational simplicity and the fact that it does not require insulin measurement, it has been widely adopted. In recent years, this index and its derivative indices have demonstrated good application value in predicting the risk of periodontitis.15,16

Furthermore, obesity, particularly central obesity, is also a significant risk factor for periodontitis.17,18 It primarily contributes by inducing low-grade systemic inflammation through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (eg, TNF-α, IL-6) from adipose tissue, thereby disrupting periodontal tissue homeostasis.19,20 A body shape index (ABSI), which integrates waist circumference, weight and height, can effectively quantify the degree of central obesity. Research has established significant associations between this index and multiple adverse health outcomes.21, 22, 23

Compared with a single TyG index or a single anthropometric indicator, the advantage of the triglyceride glucose-a body shape index (TyG-ABSI) lies in its simultaneous integration of insulin resistance-related glucose-lipid metabolic abnormalities and abnormal abdominal fat distribution. These 2 pathological states are not independent; rather, central obesity and insulin resistance may mutually reinforce each other. On the one hand, central obesity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, can lead to adipocyte hypertrophy, local hypoxia and macrophage infiltration, accompanied by increased release of free fatty acids, adipokine imbalance and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby affecting insulin signalling pathways and aggravating insulin resistance.24,25 On the other hand, insulin resistance may promote lipolysis, lipid metabolic disorders and ectopic lipid deposition, further exacerbating adipose tissue dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation.26 These mutually reinforcing pathological processes may amplify metabolic abnormalities and inflammatory activation and constitute an important background for metabolic syndrome and related chronic inflammatory diseases.27 This composite metabolic-inflammatory state has certain biological relevance to periodontitis. The resulting increased chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress burden may affect host immune-inflammatory responses and be associated with periodontal tissue destruction processes, including osteoclast activation, alveolar bone resorption and periodontal attachment loss, thereby potentially influencing the occurrence and progression of periodontitis.28,29

Therefore, compared with a single indicator, TyG-ABSI may more comprehensively reflect a composite risk state characterised by the coexistence of metabolic dysregulation and central obesity, providing a simple and accessible integrated metabolic–anthropometric indicator for evaluating the relationship between metabolic health and periodontal health. However, the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis remains unclear. Based on this, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between the novel TyG-ABSI index and the risk of periodontitis, as well as its potential value, by combining traditional regression analyses with machine learning models.

Materials and methods

Study cohort and data source

This study utilised data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, which employs a stratified multistage probability sampling design that is nationally representative. Data were extracted from 3 consecutive NHANES cycles (2009-2014). The following exclusion criteria were rigorously applied: (1) age <30 years; (2) missing periodontal data; (3) incomplete TyG index; (4) missing body shape-related data; (5) missing white blood cell count (WBC) or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) data; (6) pregnancy; (7) a fasting subsample weight (WTSAF2YR) of zero. Assessment of periodontitis

The diagnosis and severity grading of periodontitis were conducted in accordance with the standards jointly established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the American Academy of Periodontology (CDC/AAP) (Supplementary Text 1).30 Based on these criteria, periodontitis was categorised into 4 levels of severity: none, mild, moderate and severe. For this study, participants were classified into 2 groups: a none/mild periodontitis group (encompassing none and mild cases) and a moderate/severe periodontitis group (encompassing moderate and severe cases).

TyG-ABSI and other TyG-related obesity indices

Based on data from laboratory assays and physical examinations, we calculated the TyG and other TyG-related obesity indices. The calculation formulas are as follows:(1)TyG=Ln[fastingtriglyceride(mg/dL)×fastingglucose(mg/dL)2](2)TyG−BMI=TyG×BMI(kg/m2)(3)TyG−WC=TyG×WC(cm)(4)TyG−WHtR=TyG×[WC(cm)/height(cm)](5)ABSI=WC(m)/[BMI23(kg/m2)×height12(m)](6)TyG−ABSI=TyG×ABSI

Definition of covariates

Based on previous studies, we selected and adjusted for the following covariates as potential confounders: demographic factors, including age, sex, race, education level, marital status and family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR); lifestyle and health behaviours, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, sleep disturbance and dental floss use frequency; comorbidities including hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD); laboratory parameters, including serum albumin (Alb), haemoglobin (Hb), serum creatinine (SCr), vitamin D (Vit D), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Smoking status was dichotomised into nonsmoker (<100 cigarettes in lifetime) and smoker (≥100 cigarettes). Alcohol consumption was dichotomised into nondrinker (<12 drinks in the past year) and drinker (≥12 drinks). Physical activity level was dichotomised into inactive (no reported moderate/vigorous work activity) or active. Sleep disturbance was self-reported and categorised as normal or disturbed. Dental floss use frequency was categorised as never (0 d/wk), sometimes (1-3 d/wk), or regularly (4-7 d/wk). Hypertension and CVD (including congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina and stroke) were defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis.

Among the above covariates, demographic factors were adjusted as classical confounders.31, 32, 33, 34 Lifestyle and behavioural factors were associated with both metabolic status and periodontitis, and their adjustment aimed to reduce residual confounding.31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Comorbidities and laboratory parameters were included to account for the burden of metabolic comorbidities and to provide a conservative estimate of the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis. In comparable studies, the adjusted variables included hypertension,31, 32, 33, 34,36 CVD,31,37 LDL-C and HDL-C,33,36,38 Alb37,38 and SCr.37,38 Given that some variables may have multiple roles (eg, potential confounders, downstream correlates, or collider variables), we further assessed the robustness of the association by performing sensitivity analyses after removing these variables.

Statistical analysis

Analyses accounted for NHANES complex sampling design, incorporating fasting subsample weight (WTSAF2YR), cluster variable (SDMVPSU) and stratification variable (SDMVSTRA). Baseline characteristics were described by periodontitis status: continuous variables as weighted mean ± standard deviation, between-group comparisons by design-based linear regression (F-test); categorical variables as unweighted count (weighted percentage), between-group comparisons by design-adjusted Rao-Scott chi-square test. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0), and statistical significance was set at P < .05.

The association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis was analysed using weighted logistic regression and compared with TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR. All indices were standardised to Z‑scores and categorised into tertiles. A stepwise adjustment strategy was applied: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sex, age, race, marital status, education level and PIR; Model 3 further adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, sleep disturbance, dental floss use frequency, hypertension, CVD and laboratory measures including Alb, Hb, SCr, Vit D, HDL‑C and LDL-C. Tests for linear trend across tertiles were performed. To explore the dose-response relationship, we fitted restricted cubic splines (RCS) to clarify the complex and potential associations between TyG-ABSI, other TyG-related obesity indices and periodontitis risk.

To examine potential heterogeneity in the association across different populations, subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, gender, alcohol consumption, hypertension and history of CVD. Effect modification was assessed by including relevant interaction terms in the regression models.

Additionally, given the limitations of cross-sectional data, this study employed an exploratory mediation analysis. Based on existing literature, we propose that the statistical association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis may be accompanied by 2 observable patterns of association: (1) a glucose metabolism-related process, in which TyG-ABSI is associated with dysglycaemia (marked by elevated HbA1c) and HbA1c levels have a known association with glycation-related damage in periodontal tissues;39, 40, 41, 42 and (2) an inflammation-related process, in which abdominal obesity is associated with adipokine imbalance and systemic chronic low-grade inflammation (marked by elevated white blood cell count), and systemic inflammation levels have a known association with periodontal tissue destruction.43, 44, 45 Based on this framework, we used nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations to assess whether WBC and HbA1c accounted for part of the statistical association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis.

In sensitivity analysis, to assess the influence of disease status and behavioural factors on the primary association, we employed sequential exclusion and model adjustment approaches. First, we performed exclusion analyses: (1) excluding individuals with diabetes to attenuate the direct effect of disease status on insulin resistance indicators; (2) excluding individuals with hyperlipidaemia to reduce interference from abnormal lipid metabolism on TyG-related components; (3) excluding individuals with depression (assessed using PHQ-9) to control for its concomitant metabolic and behavioural confounding effects; and (4) excluding current smokers to control for residual confounding from this strong risk factor for periodontitis. Subsequently, we further adjusted for diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and depression as covariates separately in the fully adjusted models to evaluate their potential confounding effects. To assess the potential influence of overadjustment or collider bias resulting from simultaneous adjustment for multiple variables in Model 3, we conducted sensitivity analyses using different covariate exclusion strategies. Specifically, we separately excluded: (1) hypertension and cardiovascular disease; (2) LDL-C and HDL-C; and (3) comorbidities (hypertension, cardiovascular disease) and laboratory parameters (HDL-C, LDL-C, Alb, Hb, SCr, Vit D). The robustness of the main findings was assessed by comparing the changes in the effect estimates of TyG-ABSI across different adjustment strategies.

To systematically evaluate the predictive performance of TyG-ABSI, we developed ML models. Based on clinical relevance, 24 potential predictor variables were initially selected. Feature selection was then conducted using the Boruta algorithm combined with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Variables selected by both methods were included in model development. Six ML algorithms were employed, including Decision Tree (DT), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) and Support Vector Machine (SVM). We used 50 repeated random data splits, with the dataset divided into training and testing sets at an 8:2 ratio in each iteration. For each split, 5-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set for model training and hyperparameter tuning, and model performance was evaluated in the corresponding independent testing set. The mean values of each metric across the repeated analyses were used as the overall performance evaluation of the model. The evaluation metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, recall, precision, specificity and F1 score. After identifying the optimal model, its calibration and clinical utility were further assessed through calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA), respectively. Variable importance was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values.

To quantify the independent contribution of TyG-ABSI, an incremental value analysis was performed. A control model excluding TyG-ABSI was compared with the full model containing TyG-ABSI on the test set, primarily based on AUC. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were computed to assess the added predictive value of TyG-ABSI.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

This study included 4545 participants, with a mean age of 50.67 ± 13.43 years; 2257 (49.51%) were male. According to periodontitis severity, participants were categorised into a no/mild group (n = 2478) and a moderate/severe group (n = 2067). Table 1 shows that participants with moderate to severe periodontitis tended to be older, male and smokers, while being less likely to use dental floss regularly. This group also had lower education and PIR levels, along with a significantly higher prevalence of comorbidities. Further analysis of metabolic and laboratory parameters revealed that the moderate/severe group exhibited a more pronounced trend of IR (reflected in various TyG-related obesity indices) and a more adverse laboratory profile, including higher SCr levels and lower HDL-C (all P < .05 for comparisons). However, there were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in alcohol consumption habits, physical activity levels, prevalence of sleep disturbance, LDL-C, hyperlipidaemia, or Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of the primary association, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 2). In analyses related to diabetes, after excluding diabetic patients, TyG-ABSI remained significantly and positively associated with periodontitis risk in the fully adjusted Model 3 (OR = 1.124, 95% CI: 1.005-1.258, P = .041), with a significant dose-response relationship observed (P for trend = .015). After further adjusting for diabetes, this association remained significant (OR = 1.156, 95% CI: 1.052-1.271, P = .005; P for trend = .009). In analyses related to depression, after excluding patients with depression, TyG-ABSI was significantly associated with periodontitis risk (OR = 1.173, 95% CI: 1.062-1.296, P = .003; P for trend = .047). This association remained robust after adjusting for depression (OR = 1.196, 95% CI: 1.090-1.313, P < .001; P for trend = .003). However, after excluding patients with hyperlipidaemia, the association was no longer significant in Model 3 (OR = 1.170, 95% CI: 0.936-1.464, P = .159; P for trend = .325). This finding may suggest a stronger effect within the hyperlipidaemia population, but reduced statistical power due to the smaller sample size cannot be ruled out. The association regained significance after adjusting for hyperlipidaemia status (OR = 1.204, 95% CI: 1.093-1.325, P < .001; P for trend = .002). Analyses excluding smokers also supported the robustness of the association (OR = 1.224, 95% CI: 1.078-1.389, P = .003; P for trend = .012). These findings suggest that the relationship between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis was generally robust across various sensitivity analyses.

To assess the potential influence of overadjustment or collider bias on the results, we conducted sensitivity analyses by refitting the models after separately excluding different groups of covariates (Supplementary Table 2). The results showed that after excluding hypertension and cardiovascular disease, TyG-ABSI remained significantly associated with periodontitis risk (OR = 1.206, 95% CI: 1.103-1.319, P < .001; P for trend = .001). After excluding LDL-C and HDL-C, the association remained significant (OR = 1.141, 95% CI: 1.065-1.222, P < .001; P for trend = .004). After further excluding comorbidities and laboratory parameters, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, HDL-C, LDL-C, Alb, Hb, SCr and Vit D, the results remained consistent (OR = 1.162, 95% CI: 1.091-1.238, P < .001; P for trend < .001). These findings suggest that the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis remained stable across different covariate exclusion strategies.

To assess their predictive value for periodontitis, this study compared the discriminative ability of various TyG-related obesity indices. Discussion

This research systematically evaluated the association between a novel composite index, TyG-ABSI and periodontitis, along with its potential utility. TyG-ABSI was positively associated with the risk of periodontitis, with a significant dose-response relationship between them. RCS analysis further indicated that this association overall followed an approximately linear trend. Exploratory mediation analysis showed that HbA1c and WBC statistically accounted for 38.8% and 12.2% of the total association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis, respectively, suggesting that both may have certain statistical contributions to this association. Sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of this association. Furthermore, ML model analysis demonstrated that TyG-ABSI has certain predictive value for periodontitis.

The results of this study revealed a significant association between TyG-ABSI and the risk of periodontitis. In the fully adjusted multivariable logistic model, each standard deviation increase in TyG-ABSI was associated with an approximately 19.3% higher risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.193, 95% CI: 1.089-1.308). Participants in the highest tertile of TyG-ABSI exhibited a 2.252-fold increased risk compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR = 2.252, 95% CI: 1.428-3.550). Compared with the standalone TyG index or its combinations with traditional obesity metrics, TyG-ABSI showed a stronger association with periodontitis in the present study. This association may be related to its ability to capture 2 key pathophysiological dimensions simultaneously. First, TyG, as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR), is associated with systemic metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory responses.33,46,47 Second, ABSI, as a body shape index adjusted for both height and weight, may provide a more accurate assessment of abdominal obesity and visceral fat distribution than conventional indices such as BMI or WC.48, 49, 50 TyG-ABSI integrates 2 major pathological dimensions, metabolic dysregulation and abnormal body fat distribution, both of which may simultaneously be correlated with chronic low-grade inflammation and be associated with periodontal tissue damage. Previous studies have shown that TyG-ABSI exhibits better performance in disease risk discrimination than traditional indicators.48,49 These findings suggest that simultaneously assessing insulin sensitivity and visceral fat distribution characteristics in clinical practice may be useful to more accurately identify individuals at high risk for periodontitis. However, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, the temporal sequence or causal relationship between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis cannot be established, and the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded. Periodontitis, as a chronic inflammatory disease, may induce systemic low-grade inflammation through the entry of periodontal pathogens and their virulence factors into the circulation, thereby affecting insulin sensitivity, glycaemic status, and lipid metabolism, and potentially influencing TyG-ABSI levels and its components.51,52 Therefore, the observed association in this study should be interpreted as an exploratory statistical association rather than a unidirectional causal relationship. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to further clarify the temporal sequence and potential bidirectional relationship between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis.

This study was based on cross-sectional data and used exploratory mediation analysis to quantitatively decompose the statistical association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis. Long-term glycaemic status, represented by HbA1c, accounted for approximately 38.8% of the total association. Previous studies have shown that hyperglycaemia promotes the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in periodontal tissues, thereby activating the AGE-RAGE signalling axis, inducing local oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, impairing immune cell function and ultimately disrupting periodontal tissue homeostasis.53 Clinical and epidemiological evidence has shown that HbA1c levels are positively correlated with periodontal inflammatory markers, while improved glycaemic control is associated with better periodontal conditions.39, 40, 41, 42 On the other hand, systemic inflammation, represented by WBC, statistically accounted for approximately 12.2% of the total association. Previous studies have shown that metabolic dysregulation drives the release of cytokines from adipose tissue, systemically activating white blood cells. These activated white blood cells migrate to periodontal tissues, where they release proteases, reactive oxygen species and additional inflammatory mediators, directly damaging tissues and amplifying local inflammatory responses.43, 44, 45 The above biological explanations may provide theoretical support for the associations observed in this study. However, due to the limitations of the cross-sectional design, causal inferences need to be further validated through longitudinal studies.

Subgroup analyses after Bonferroni correction showed a significant interaction of age in the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis. The association was most pronounced in the 45 to 59 age group, attenuated in the ≥60 age group and not statistically significant in the 30 to 44 age group. This pattern suggests possible age-related heterogeneity in the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis, with the strongest association observed among participants aged 45 to 59 years, rather than a consistent strengthening across all age groups younger than 60 years. This age-related pattern may be related to the gradual worsening of metabolic dysregulation during middle age. During this period, increased visceral fat accumulation and elevated insulin resistance may lead to greater release of adipose tissue-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, which may promote local periodontal inflammatory responses through activation of the NF-κB signalling pathway. Meanwhile, aging-related mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species accumulation may impair the reparative capacity of periodontal ligament fibroblasts, thereby providing a potential biological explanation for the link between metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory response and periodontal tissue damage.54,55 A previous large-scale study in a Chinese population also found that the association between similar metabolic-body fat composite indicators and periodontitis was stronger among individuals younger than 60 years than among those aged 60 years or older.56 The present study further suggests that this association may be mainly concentrated among individuals aged 45 to 59 years. Therefore, from the perspective of clinical assessment, concurrent attention to insulin resistance, central obesity and inflammatory status in middle-aged individuals may help provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between metabolic status and periodontal health.

In the sensitivity analyses, multiple analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. These analyses generally confirmed a positive association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis risk. However, after excluding patients with hyperlipidaemia, the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis lost statistical significance. This result may suggest effect modification by hyperlipidaemia. Alternatively, it may reflect reduced statistical power due to the decreased sample size in this subgroup. Our study could not fully distinguish between these 2 possibilities. Future large-scale studies in nonhyperlipidaemic populations are needed to clarify the true effect size in this subgroup.

This study combined ML methods with traditional regression analysis to enhance the credibility of the findings. Compared with traditional regression analysis, ML methods offer greater flexibility in handling multidimensional variable information and may help explore potential complex interactions between TyG-ABSI and other covariates.57 In addition, SHAP analysis quantified the relative contributions of risk factors across different dimensions to periodontitis risk prediction, providing a reference for prioritising attention to risk factors in clinical practice. SHAP summary plot visually displayed the direction and magnitude of the effects of different risk factors, helping to understand the association pattern between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis risk. Incremental value analysis showed that TyG-ABSI may provide some supplementary information for risk prediction. Together, these analyses support the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis and provide a reference for exploratory research on periodontitis risk prediction. However, it should be noted that although the RF model performed relatively well among the evaluated models, its recall was lower than its specificity. This indicates that while the model performed well in correctly identifying nonperiodontitis participants, it had limitations in detecting true periodontitis cases and missed some cases. This limitation may be partly due to the limited oral health-related information available for modelling in the public database used in this study. In clinical practice, the model's predictions should be integrated with periodontal examinations, medical history and other relevant clinical information for comprehensive decision-making. Future studies should focus on optimising the classification threshold, incorporating larger sample sizes and including additional relevant predictors to improve recall while maintaining acceptable specificity.

This study systematically evaluates the association between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis and its predictive value, yielding results with good statistical power and clinical interpretability. However, there are some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality between TyG-ABSI and periodontitis, and can only reflect the pattern of association among variables. The mediation analysis should be understood as an exploratory statistical decomposition, and all associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Future longitudinal cohort studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and provide stronger evidence for causal inference. Second, although we adjusted for a wide range of covariates, unmeasured or inadequately measured factors (eg, genetic susceptibility, differences in daily lifestyle behaviours) may still exert potential effects, and residual bias cannot be completely ruled out. Third, this study lacked an independent external validation cohort. In future research, we will conduct external validation in multicentre periodontitis cohorts to further evaluate the generalisability of the model and the predictive value of TyG-ABSI.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the novel composite index TyG-ABSI is significantly positively associated with the risk of periodontitis and may serve as a potential indicator for periodontitis risk prediction. This indicator integrates 2 risk dimensions, insulin resistance and central obesity, providing new insights into the relationship between metabolic dysregulation and periodontal health. The application value of this indicator requires further validation in future longitudinal studies.
