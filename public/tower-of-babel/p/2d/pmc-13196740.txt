On-demand linkage cleavage in two-dimensional conjugated metal-organic frameworks for closed-loop recyclable electronics
Authors: Quanquan Guo, Shaohong Shi, Ningfeng You, Chenchen Wang, Leilei Zheng, Wei Wang, Changjiang Yi, Liping Shu, Hao Xu, Jianjun Zhang, Leif Riemenschneider, Shirong Huang, Mingchao Wang, Fangchao Cheng, Xiaodong Li, Gianaurelio Cuniberti, Yiqiang Wu, Claudia Felser, Minghao Yu, Xinliang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of modern electronics has intensified concerns about electronic waste management at the end of a product’s life. Integrating closed-loop recyclability, where electronic materials can be efficiently recovered, reprocessed, and reused in regenerated products, is essential for achieving sustainable development, minimizing environmental impact, and realizing long-term economic benefits. However, achieving closed-loop recycling remains particularly challenging for complex electronic materials. Here, we demonstrate the closed-loop recycling of emerging multifunctional two-dimensional conjugated metal-organic frameworks (2D c-MOFs) through a mechanochemistry-induced on-demand degradation strategy. Exemplified with 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydroxytriphenylene (HHTP)–based 2D c-MOFs, we show that ultrasonic cavitation facilitates selective cleavage of metal-ligand linkages in alkaline solutions enabling rapid material degradation (up to 92.4% within 30 min). The HHTP monomers are subsequently recovered with high purity and yield (96.3%), and reused to regenerate 2D c-MOFs, establishing a complete circular material life cycle. Our cradle-to-cradle life-cycle assessment reveals that, compared with direct synthesis, this closed-loop recycling approach substantially reduces both total energy consumption (52 versus 358 MJ kg−1) and greenhouse gas (CO2) emission (4.8 versus 27.4 kg CO2-equiv), thereby substantially lowering the overall environmental impact relative to conventional electronic materials. Moreover, we demonstrate the practical utility of these recyclable 2D c-MOFs in several applications, including hydrogen gas sensors, supercapacitor electrodes, and degradable printed electronic devices. These results highlight the potential of 2D c-MOFs to advance circular electronics, laying the groundwork for a sustainable transformation within the electronics industry.

INTRODUCTION

Electronics are now deeply embedded in modern life, offering immense convenience through their integration into everyday products, such as consumer electronics and household appliances, as well as advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence systems and semiconductor architecture (1–3). However, the widespread use of electronics has also led to a significant increase in electronic waste at the end of a product’s life, raising global concern. It is projected that the annual global output of electronic waste will reach 82 million tons by 2030, with an incredible annual growth rate of 33% compared to 2022 (4). Despite this alarming growth, only 17% of electronic waste is properly recycled, and the majority is landfilled or improperly treated. This mismanagement is largely due to the complexity of electronic material recycling, resulting in not only serious environmental and human health risks but also substantial economic losses.

Incorporating closed-loop recyclable materials, those that can be fully recovered, reprocessed, and reused in regenerated products without loss of performance, is critical for promoting sustainability and minimizing environmental impact (5, 6). However, most existing electronic materials fall short of achieving this, either by relying on complex, costly, and energy-intensive processes or by compromising material quality and performance. This difficulty arises from the complex and often competing requirements of such materials, which must simultaneously exhibit outstanding electronic properties, structural stability, controlled degradability, good processability, and ease of recycling. These limitations are evident in common electronic materials, such as metals, carbon-based materials, and conductive polymers, whose closed-loop recyclability is hindered by high cost, poor degradability, and limited recovery efficiency (7). Traditional recycling methods, including mechanical grinding, thermal decomposition (pyrolysis), and chemical dissolution in organic solvents (solvolysis), often result in substantial material damage, high energy consumption, and the use of nonrecyclable or hazardous solvents (8). To address these challenges, researchers have explored alternative strategies using water-soluble and self-healing materials for sustainable recycling (9–11). However, such materials commonly suffer from high moisture sensitivity, inadequate thermal stability, and difficulty in sustaining long-term performance. Therefore, achieving simple, economical, and highly efficient recycling of reliable electronic materials has become critically important to facilitate their conversion into high-value secondary raw materials for modern electronics.

Recently, two-dimensional conjugated metal-organic frameworks (2D c-MOFs), unique layer-stacked organic 2D crystals with in-plane extended π-conjugation and out-of-plane electronic couplings, display distinct characteristics of well-defined porosity, structural precision, accessible active sites, high stability, superior charge transport, and versatile stimulus-responsive activity (12–14). These attributes make 2D c-MOFs promising candidates for a wide range of electronic applications, including field-effect transistors, force and gas sensors, ion conductors, and energy storage device (15–17). The synthesis of 2D c-MOFs typically involves metal-coordination reactions between the metal nodes (such as Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn) and conjugated ligands containing symmetrical functional groups (such as ─OH, ─NH2, and ─SH), resulting in various linkages [such as metal-bis(dioxolene) (MO4), metal-bis(diimine) (MN4), and metal-bis(dithiolene) (MS4)]. These metal-coordination bonds exhibit both reversibility and robustness, conferring excellent stability under typical device-operating conditions while simultaneously permitting selective bond cleavage in response to specific stimulus (18–20). Therefore, it is necessary to protect from these environmental factors and avoid unwanted degradation happening to the framework during storage or operation. While the theoretical framework for on-demand degradation can be conceived, the experimental realization of efficient closed-loop recycling for 2D c-MOFs remains unrealized.

In this work, we present a facial mechanochemical linkage cleavage approach that enables efficient, on-demand degradation and closed-loop recycling of 2D c-MOFs (Fig. 1A). Our approach specifically targets 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydroxytriphenylene (HHTP)–based 2D c-MOFs coordinated with various metal centers (Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn), leveraging the inherent reversibility of their MO4 coordination bonds. We reveal that alkaline conditions greatly weaken the coordination bonds through hydroxylation of the metal center. When combined with ultrasonic cavitation, this effect promotes efficient and selective cleavage of metal-ligand linkages, leading to rapid material degradation into Na-HHTP and corresponding metal hydroxide. It is notable that the HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs demonstrate exceptional degradation kinetics. The resultant Zn-HHTP, Cu-HHTP, Co-HHTP, and Ni-HHTP achieve degradation rates of 92.3, 74.2, 63.5, and 57.6%, respectively, within 30 min. The subsequent ligand regeneration process yields high-purity HHTP monomers suitable for resynthesizing fresh 2D c-MOFs, completing a fully circular material life cycle. The recycling approach exhibits remarkable consistency across different metal systems, with ligand recovery yields of 96.3% for Zn-HHTP, 91.8% for Cu-HHTP, 88.6% for Co-HHTP, and 85.4% for Ni-HHTP. Our cradle-to-cradle life-cycle assessment (LCA) using industrial-scale models reveals that the regenerated HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs using this closed-loop recycling approach not only present much decreased total energy consumption (52 versus 358 MJ kg−1) and greenhouse gas (CO2) emission (4.8 versus 27.4 kg CO2-equiv) compared to direct synthesized ones, but also substantially reduce their overall environmental impact relative to conventional electronic materials, such as copper, gold, silver, carbon nanotube, graphene, polyaniline, and polypyrrole. The developed closed-loop recycling protocol presents a promising pathway toward realizing sustainable and recyclable electronic components. As proof of concept, we demonstrate recyclable and regenerative hydrogen gas sensors and supercapacitor electrodes based on Cu-HHTP, both of which retain their original performance after material regeneration. In addition, we validate the feasibility of using Zn-HHTP to formulate degradable printed electronic devices, highlighting the practical potential of HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs for advancing circular electronics.

RESULTS

Degradation feasibility evaluation

The degradability of 2D c-MOFs is primarily governed by the cleavage efficiency of their square-planar metal coordination linkages. It is generally observed that 2D c-MOFs exhibit chemical instability under alkaline conditions. To systematically evaluate their degradability in alkaline environments, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations on a series of widely studied benzene- and triphenylene-based ligands [1,2,3,4,5,6-hexahydroxybenzene (HHB), HHTP, 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexaaminotriphenylene (HATP), and 2,3,6,7,10,11-triphenylenehexathiol (HTTP)] coordinated with four common metal centers (Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn). The calculated linkage cleavage energies per structural unit are provided in table S1. All cleavage reactions induced by an alkaline agent (i.e., NaOH) exhibit positive reaction energy values (Fig. 1B), indicating that the linkage dissociation is thermodynamically nonspontaneous and endothermic, requiring external stimuli to proceed. For 2D c-MOFs with Zn centers, we observed a distinct hierarchy of cleavage energies as follows: Zn-HATP (6.37 eV) > Zn-HTTP (5.03 eV) > Zn-HHTP (3.14 eV). This result indicates that the MO4 coordination linkages in Zn-HHTP are more likely to break compared to MN4 in Zn-HATP and MS4 in Zn-HTTP under alkaline solutions. This computational prediction was experimentally validated by the contrasting stability of Cu-HATP (maintaining crystallinity) versus Cu-HHTP (gradual degradation) in an alkaline solution (fig. S1). Further analysis of HHTP-based systems showed the following cleavage energy trend: Ni-HHTP (8.15 eV) > Co-HHTP (7.37 eV) > Cu-HHTP (4.52 eV) > Zn-HHTP (3.14 eV). Comparative studies between HHB- and HHTP-based systems revealed that the smaller conjugated HHB ligand forms stronger interactions with metal ions, resulting in higher degradation energies. Besides, the practical recycling of HHB ligands proves challenging due to their low molecular weight and high solubility in common solvents (fig. S2). Based on this analysis, we focused our subsequent investigations on MO4-linked HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs, which demonstrate more favorable cleavage characteristics under targeted stimuli.

To evaluate the reversible thermodynamic dissociation of MO4 linkages present in HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs, we performed in situ temperature-dependent Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy on Zn-HHTP (fig. S3). In this scenario, temperature acts as a thermal stimulus to trigger the cleavage of the metal-coordination bonds. Our analysis focused on two key spectral regions: 3700 to 3100 and 1100 to 900 cm−1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of ─O─H and ─C─OH groups (21). Compared with HHTP ligand, Zn-HHTP exhibits a characteristic ─O─H peak at higher wave number in the FTIR spectrum at room temperature, indicating the presence of “bonded” ─O─H arising from proton adsorption on the MO4 linkage (fig. S4). As the temperature increased from 30° to 210°C, the FTIR peak of the bonded ─O─H groups at 3419 cm−1 progressively decreased in intensity and blue-shifted to 3432 cm−1. Meanwhile, the intensities of multiple FTIR peaks (around 3700 cm−1) belonging to “free” ─O─H gradually increase (fig. S5) (22, 23). This observation suggests the dissociation of coordination linkages in Zn-HHTP and the concomitant formation of free ─O─H groups upon heating. Concurrently, the ─C─OH peak at 991 cm−1 progressively increased in intensity and shifted to higher wave numbers, reflecting the generation of the ─C─OH group and further confirming cleavage of the ZnO4 linkages at elevated temperatures (fig. S6).

Generalized synchronous 2D FTIR correlation spectra were used to evaluate the correlation between changes in different FTIR peaks, with red and blue patterns representing positive and negative correlations, respectively (24). As shown by the blue-patterned region in fig. S7, the disappearance of bonded ─OH groups and the formation of ─C─OH groups present a negative correlation. Generalized asynchronous 2D correlation spectra were further used to determine the sequence of these changes (Fig. 1C). A red pattern indicates that the change in the y-axis peak precedes that in the x axis, whereas a blue pattern indicates the reverse. The spectra of Zn-HHTP confirm that the loss of bonded ─OH precedes the formation of ─C─OH group, indicating a sequential dissociation pathway during the thermal degradation process. We further conducted moving-window 2D FTIR analysis to precisely determine the temperature-dependent dissociation behavior (25). As revealed, the bonded ─OH signal reaches maximum intensity at ~40°C, marking the onset of metal-coordination bond dissociation (fig. S8). Meanwhile, the characteristic ─C─OH vibration of regenerated HHTP appears most strongly at ~145°C, indicating complete linkage dissociation (Fig. 1D). These results confirm that MO4 linkages can undergo reversible and dynamic cleavage and reformation under thermal stimulation, although complete dissociation requires a relatively high temperature (up to 145°C). These insights motivated us to explore additional stressors beyond thermal input to accelerate MO4 linkage cleavage.

On-demand linkage cleavage of 2D c-MOFs

To explore the on-demand degradation of 2D c-MOFs, we synthesized four representative HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs via a facile hydrothermal method (fig. S9), including Co-HHTP (fig. S10), Ni-HHTP (fig. S11), Cu-HHTP (fig. S12), and Zn-HHTP (fig. S13). All four materials exhibit similar nanorod/microrod morphologies and well-defined π-stacked layered structures with high crystallinity. We found that immersion in an alkaline NaOH solution effectively weakens the coordination linkages. Using Zn-HHTP as the example, we collected Zn K-edge x-ray absorption spectra of standard Zn(OH)2, pristine Zn-HHTP, and NaOH-treated Zn-HHTP to understand the coordination environment variation of Zn-HHTP during alkaline treatment (fig. S14). The x-ray absorption near-edge structure of NaOH-treated Zn-HHTP shows nearly identical absorption energy compared with Zn(OH)2 and Zn-HHTP, confirming the preservation of Zn valence states throughout the process.

Wavelet-transform analysis of the extended x-ray absorption fine structure spectra (EXAFS) in R and k spaces provides detailed insight into the local Zn coordination environment (26). For pristine Zn-HHTP (Fig. 2A), we observe an intensity maximum at k = 15.7 Å−1 and R = 1.6 Å, which corresponds to the first-shell Zn─O scattering. Following alkaline treatment (Fig. 2B), this peak shifts notably to R = 2.4 Å, reflecting substantial elongation of Zn─O bonds due to additional hydroxyl coordination to Zn. NaOH-treated Zn-HHTP presents two additional intensity maxima at k = 14.9 Å−1 (R = 1.1 Å) and k = 15.4 Å−1 (R = 1.7 Å), matching the EXAFS signature of standard Zn(OH)2 (Fig. 2C). Complementary FTIR and Raman analyses further corroborate these findings (fig. S15). The blue-shifted hydroxyl groups in FTIR and diminished conjugated system features in Raman spectra collectively demonstrate Zn─O4 linkage cleavage. These comprehensive results establish that OH− ions act as strong nucleophilic ligands that coordinate with metal centers in the alkaline environment, forming labile OH−-bonded intermediates (Fig. 2D). This intermediate state initiates gradual degradation of the 2D c-MOF structures even at room temperature, yielding metal hydroxides and Na-HHTP (fig. S16).

Although an alkaline solution can initiate the MO4 linkage cleavage, we observed that the degradation of 2D c-MOFs is prohibitively slow, typically requiring weeks for completion. To address this limitation, we introduced ultrasonic cavitation (1 kW, 25 KHz) to accelerate the process. Just 30 min of sonication converted the majority of solid Zn-HHTP into a yellow solution, yielding a yellow-color solution, indicative of Na-HHTP formation (Fig. 2E). Time-dependent x-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed progressive weakening of Zn-HHTP characteristic peaks, confirming crystalline structure collapse (Fig. 2F). The degradation rate showed a strong dependence on NaOH concentration (fig. S17). Optimal conditions were identified as 156.6 ml of 0.05 M NaOH per gram of 2D c-MOF, corresponding to a molar ratio of NaOH to Zn─O bonds of 1.2, combined with ultrasonic treatment (900 W, 25 kHz) for 60 min. These conditions represent a relatively mild yet highly efficient approach compared to previously reported alkaline- or catalyst-induced degradation methods (table S2).

As the NaOH concentration increased, the degradation rate of Zn-HHTP accelerated, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in mass retention. Quantitative kinetic studies demonstrated dramatic acceleration under sonication, with Zn-HHTP achieving complete degradation within 1 hour with ultrasonic assistance, whereas only 75.6% degradation was achieved in the absence of ultrasonic stimulation (Fig. 2G). These results underscore the critical role of sonication in promoting coordination bond cleavage and accelerating framework degradation. We further compared the degradation rate of different HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs using the same method. As shown in Fig. 2H, within 30 min, the degradation rate follows the order of Zn-HHTP (92.3%) > Cu-HHTP (74.2%) > Co-HHTP (63.5%) > Ni-HHTP (57.6%), aligning with the DFT-calculated cleavage energies for their corresponding coordination bonds. Moreover, Zn-HHTP, Cu-HHTP, Co-HHTP, and Ni-HHTP achieved complete degradation after ultrasonic treatment for 60, 120, 180, and 240 min, respectively (fig. S18), corresponding to the degradation rate of 6.4, 3.2, 2.1, and 1.6 g liter−1 hour−1. We also found that the degradation behavior of 2D c-MOFs is highly influenced by ultrasonic parameters, including power, frequency, and temperature, as detailed in fig. S19. Furthermore, the energy efficiency of the mechanochemical ultrasonic degradation process was evaluated using the electric energy per order (EEO). The calculated EEO value is 9184.7 kilowatt-hour (kWh) m−3 order−1, which is much lower than those reported for alternative degradation strategies, such as conventional ultrasonication, photocatalysis, electrocatalysis, and ozonation (table S3). This comparison demonstrates the decent energy efficiency of the used ultrasonic degradation method.

Closed-loop recycling of 2D c-MOFs

Besides degradation, the recovery and separation of corresponding ligand and metal species are also very important for the closed-loop recycling of 2D c-MOFs. In many cases, however, isolating building blocks from the degradation mixture with high purity and yield remains a substantial challenge, even when the degradation process itself is theoretically feasible. The successful demonstration of on-demand degradation motivated us to further explore the closed-loop recycling of 2D c-MOFs, which critically depends on the high-yield recovery of organic ligands for the subsequent regeneration of 2D c-MOFs. Using Zn-HHTP as a model system, we established a practical ligand-recovery protocol, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Specifically, the degradation products comprise Na-HHTP, a Zn(OH)2 precipitate, and excess NaOH. Solid Zn(OH)2 is first separated from the mixture via filtration. Subsequently, HCl solution is added to neutralize residual NaOH and protonate Na-HHTP to HHTP, yielding an aqueous solution of NaCl and HHTP. Last, a high-purity HHTP ligand is extracted using environmentally benign ethyl acetate and obtained via rotary evaporation. Critical to the process is maintaining a nitrogen atmosphere during treatment, which prevents oxidation of the HHTP ligand. Under ambient conditions, oxygen participates in the degradation reaction, leading to the formation of carboxylic acid–terminated HHTP or other byproducts (fig. S20). For the recovery of metal species, the precipitated M(OH)2 sediments can be readily converted into various metal salts through simple acid-base neutralization reactions, enabling their reintegration into the closed-loop recycling pathway of 2D c-MOFs.

The chemical purity of the HHTP ligands recycled from degraded Zn-HHTP was confirmed using mass spectrometry and 1H/13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. Mass spectral analysis showed an identical molar mass (324 g mol−1) to pristine HHTP, with no detectable impurities (Fig. 3B). Both 1H and 13C NMR spectra of recycled HHTP were indistinguishable from those of the pristine HHTP. In 1H NMR spectra, characteristic signals at 9.3 and 7.6 parts per million (ppm) were observed (Fig. 3C), corresponding to hydrogen in the hydroxyl group (Ha) and the benzene ring (Hb), respectively. Meanwhile, the 13C NMR spectra showed expected resonances at 145.7, 122.3, and 108.2 ppm, corresponding to distinct carbon environments in the HHTP framework (Fig. 3D). FTIR spectroscopy (fig. S21) provided additional confirmation, with identical vibrational signatures for recycled and pristine HHTP (─OH and C─O stretching vibrations at 3176 and 1136 cm−1). Of note, this recovery protocol demonstrated broad applicability across various 2D c-MOF materials, successfully yielding high-purity HHTP from Cu-HHTP (fig. S22), Co-HHTP (fig. S23), and Ni-HHTP (fig. S24). Among the four materials, Zn-HHTP exhibited the highest recycling efficiency, achieving a yield of 96.3% after fully degradation (Fig. 3E), while Cu-HHTP, Co-HHTP, and Ni-HHTP achieved yields of 91.8, 88.6, and 85.4%, respectively. Besides the recovery of HHTP ligands, the corresponding metal species can also be recycled. The recycled ratios of Zn, Cu, Co, and Ni can reach 93.5, 88.3, 84.2, and 82.9%, respectively (fig. S25).

To prove complete closed-loop recyclability, we resynthesized Zn-HHTP using the recycled HHTP ligands. Structural characterization by XRD confirmed that the regenerated material maintains both high crystallinity and phase purity identical to the original Zn-HHTP (Fig. 3F). Electrical conductivity measurements, performed using the van der Pauw method, revealed that the regenerated material exhibits temperature-dependent conductivity values (10−6 ~ 10−3 S cm−1) of the same order of magnitude as pristine Zn-HHTP (Fig. 3G). Besides, porosity analysis through nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms showed comparable textural properties between the recycled and original materials, with specific surface areas maintained at ~150 m2 g−1 and pore size distribution centered at 1.9 nm (fig. S26). Even after multiple resynthesis processes, the resynthesized Zn-HHTP can still maintain good crystallinity, identical functional groups, and electrical conductivity (fig. S27), suggesting that the cumulative degradation effects over multiple recycling cycles exert negligible impact on the physicochemical properties of Zn-HHTP. Besides, the HHTP ligand recycled from Zn-HHTP can also be used to resynthesize the other 2D c-MOFs with well-maintained crystallinities and morphologies (such as Co-HHTP, Ni-HHTP, and Cu-HHTP), indicating the high-quality and broader utility of the recovered HHTP ligand (fig. S28). Collectively, these results validate the feasibility of a complete closed-loop recycling process for 2D c-MOFs, enabling their reuse without compromising structural or functional performance.

Life-cycle assessment

Environmental impact analysis provides a crucial framework for evaluating the sustainability of advanced materials (27, 28). We performed a systematic LCA using the Simapro software to quantify the environmental benefits of our 2D c-MOF system. The system boundary adopted in our analysis follows a cradle-to-grave approach, encompassing the full life cycle from raw material extraction and material synthesis to device use and the emission of waste gases, liquids, and solids at end of life. In addition, a cutoff allocation method was used to handle recycling processes, which avoids complex allocation ambiguities by assigning all upstream environmental burdens (e.g., resource extraction and primary material production) to the original product system. Our initial focus was on comparing the total energy consumption (Fig. 4A) and greenhouse gas (CO2) emission (Fig. 4B) associated with Zn-HHTP production via closed-loop recycling versus direct synthesis. Quantification was performed using the Cumulative Energy Demand v1.11, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and Global Warming Potential analysis methods (IPCC GWP 100). It is notable that the closed-loop recycling approach substantially reduced the environmental impact (fig. S29), yielding markedly lower total energy consumption (52 versus 358 MJ kg−1) and CO2 emissions (4.8 versus 27.4 kg CO2-equiv) compared with direct synthesis.

Furthermore, our cradle-to-cradle analysis, based on industrial models, examines the complete life cycle from material production, transportation, to end-of-life management through either disposal or recycling, comparing the synthesis and closed-loop recycling processes of Zn-HHTP with those of conventional electronic materials. Comprehensive evaluation across 11 environmental impact categories by the ReCiPe Midpoint (H) analysis method (Fig. 4C and fig. S30) reveals the superior performance of Zn-HHTP relative to conventional materials, including copper, gold, silver, carbon nanotube, graphene, polyaniline, and polypyrrole. The analysis demonstrates significant reductions of Zn-HHTP across multiple metrics compared to the largest value of different materials, such as 34.8% reduction in global warming, 6.5% in ozone depletion, 30.5% in particulate matter, 24.6% in acidification, 4.1% in terrestrial ecotoxicity, 4.2% in freshwater ecotoxicity, 20.5% in human carcinogenic toxicity, 6.3% in human noncarcinogenic toxicity, 2% in mineral use, 10.1% in fossil use, and 26.1% in water consumption. In addition, Zn-HHTP achieves notably low lifetime values of 410 MJ kg−1 total energy consumption (Fig. 4D) and 32.1 kg CO2-equiv emissions (Fig. 4E), representing the most favorable metrics among all compared materials. Overall, the environmental benefits of 2D c-MOFs and their corresponding recycling methods stem from three key attributes: a dramatic reduction in end-of-life waste, complete disassembly into reusable constituents, and the capacity for manufacturing regenerated functional electronic components. These advantages position HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs as an environmentally sustainable alternative for next-generation electronics.

Closed-loop recyclability in the functional devices

The closed-loop recyclability of HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs can offer a transformative approach for the development of sustainable and recyclable electronic devices. To illustrate this potential, we fabricated chemiresistive gas sensors using Cu-HHTP as the active sensing material due to its high d-electron count and good affinity to different gases (Fig. 5A). The sensor chip was constructed by depositing Cu-HHTP onto a patterned interdigitated electrode with a channel width of 4 μm and a channel gap of 3 μm. The selection of Cu-HHTP was guided by its inherent porosity and redox-active character, which promotes efficient hydrogen adsorption and subsequent electron transfer upon exposure, enabling sensitive detection (29). Sensor performance was evaluated by monitoring real-time resistance changes under a 3-V bias in response to varying H2 concentrations (10 to 50 ppm) in air. The device exhibited rapid response kinetics, achieving full signal deflection within 50 s of exposure, followed by complete recovery to baseline within an equivalent timeframe, demonstrating excellent reversibility (Fig. 5B).

After testing, we detached Cu-HHTP from the interdigitated electrode using a simple ultrasonication method and collected the spent Cu-HHTP suspension. Afterward, we recycled the HHTP ligand and resynthesized Cu-HHTP via our developed closed-loop recycling method. The regenerated sensor maintained comparable performance metrics, including consistent response time (around 52 s) and intensity (around 2% at 10 ppm, 4% at 30 ppm, and 5% at 50 ppm), confirming the functional stability of the recycled material. Based on the concentration-dependent responses under optimal conditions, we calculated the limit of detection (LOD) using the root-mean-square deviation (RMS) method. The original and regenerated devices exhibited impressive and similar LODs of 0.12 and 0.04 ppm, respectively (Fig. 5C), surpassing many previously reported chemiresistive H2 sensors (30–33). In addition, the Cu-HHTP–based gas sensors can generate stable response and signal output for repeated H2 stimuli even for the recycled devices, indicating the desirable recovery efficiency of our proposed closed-loop recycling approach (fig. S31).

Another important application of 2D c-MOFs lies in their use as active electrode materials for supercapacitors, benefiting from their decent electrical conductivity and high density of accessible ion adsorption/storage sites (34). To demonstrate the recyclability of such electrodes, we used our developed mechanochemical degradation method to recover Cu-HHTP from the spent electrode (Fig. 5D). Specifically, we disassembled the supercapacitors in the glovebox, and the electrode material complex (active materials, conductive carbons, and binders) were detached from the Al current collector. After that, the waste electrode composites were immersed in the alkaline solution and proceeded with the mechanochemical degradation and ligand recycling process. The recovered HHTP ligand was then used to resynthesize Cu-HHTP and fabricate a regenerated Cu-HHTP electrode.

We assessed the electrochemical performance of both the original and regenerated Cu-HHTP electrode in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (EMI-BF4) ionic liquid electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves and galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves were conducted to evaluate their capacitive behavior. Both electrodes exhibited typical faradaic pseudocapacitance behavior with nearly identical pairs of redox peaks in their CV curves (Fig. 5E and fig. S32), indicating that the redox-active sites and ion storage mechanism were well preserved during the recycling process. The regenerated Cu-HHTP electrode delivered a high specific capacitance of 213.6 F g−1 at a current density of 0.5 A g−1, closely matching the original Cu-HHTP electrode (216.6 F g−1), as shown in Fig. 5F. Even when the current density was increased 10-fold to 5 A g−1, the regenerated Cu-HHTP electrode maintained a capacity retention of 68.7%, compared to 70.3% for the original electrode, highlighting comparable rate capabilities (fig. S33). Furthermore, the regenerated Cu-HHTP electrode demonstrated excellent cycling stability, retaining 93.3% of its original capacitance after 1000 cycles at 1 A g−1 (fig. S34). In comparison, the directly reused Cu-HHTP electrodes after detaching from original electrodes show obviously decayed electrochemical performance (fig. S35).

Last, we showcased the application of Zn-HHTP in fully printable and degradable electronic devices, leveraging the higher sustainability and relative abundance of Zn compared to other metal species. For this purpose, Zn-HHTP was directly synthesized on cellulose nanofibers (CNFs), resulting in a composite material that serves as an effective ink (Zn-HHTP@CNFs) for direct ink writing (DIW) printing (fig. S36). CNFs, with their negatively charged surface functional groups, were introduced as a green viscosity modifier to both stabilize the dispersion of Zn-HHTP nanorods and finely tune the rheological properties of the ink (fig. S37). A variety of complex structures were programmatically printed using the Zn-HHTP/CNF ink, including quick response (QR) codes, circuit patterns, interdigitated electrodes, and textual elements (Fig. 5G). All printed features exhibit high resolution and fidelity, with no observable deformation or overlapping, confirming the excellent printability and structural precision of the ink.

The Zn-HHTP/CNF ink is both eco-friendly and sustainable. CNFs are biodegradable natural polymers, and Zn-HHTP can be efficiently recovered through the previously described recycling protocol. As shown in Fig. 5H, we placed the printed Zn-HHTP/CNF–based interdigitated electrodes in the alkaline treatment solution. With increasing the ultrasonication time, the interdigitated electrode gradually disappeared and completely vanished after 20 min, indicating the excellent degradability of resultant Zn-HHTP/CNF inks. Furthermore, to assess the practical and real-world recyclability, we also tested the recycling ratios of metal ions and HHTP ligands after being applied in gas sensors, supercapacitor electrodes, and printable electronics, which also can reach high values (fig. S38). These findings underscore the potential of HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs in recyclable and degradable electronics, highlighting their promise for future circular-economy applications.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have demonstrated a closed-loop recycling strategy for 2D c-MOFs as recyclable electronic materials, encompassing mechanochemistry-induced on-demand degradation, building block recycling, and resynthesis of 2D c-MOFs. Leveraging the reversible metal-coordination linkage-formed skeleton structure, 2D c-MOFs could be quickly degraded into original building blocks by the mechanochemical linkage cleavage approach. Taking HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs as an example, ultrasonic cavitation facilitates cleavage of metal-ligand linkages in alkaline solutions and achieves a rapid material degradation of 92.4% within 30 min. The HHTP ligand, with a high yield (96.3%) and purity, was recycled from these degradation mixtures and reused for the regenerative synthesis of 2D c-MOFs, enabling a complete circular material life cycle. Our cradle-to-cradle LCA results suggest that the regenerated 2D c-MOFs using this closed-loop recycling approach deliver reduced total energy consumption and green-house gas emission compared to directly synthesized one, as well as decreased environmental impact metrics in comparison with conventional electronic materials, such as copper, gold, silver, carbon nanotube, graphene, polyaniline, and polypyrrole. Furthermore, we validated our recycling method through functional devices, including gas sensors, supercapacitor electrodes, and degradable printable circuits. The regenerated devices fabricated with recycled 2D c-MOFs maintained performance parity with their original counterparts, meeting all operational requirements for these applications. We believe that this work promotes the end-of-life environmental friendliness and circularity of recyclable 2D c-MOFs, paving the way to a sustainable and eco-friendly electronic industry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and chemicals

All chemicals for 2D c-MOF synthesis, including HHTP, HATP∙6HCl, tetrahydroxyquinone, sodium acetate (NaOAc), ammonium acetate (NH4OAc), zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO3)2·6H2O], copper(II) nitrate trihydrate [Cu(NO3)2·2.5H2O], copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate [Co(NO3)2·6H2O], and nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate [Ni(NO3)2·6H2O], were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and were used without further purification. Carbon black, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), ethyl acetate solvent, NaOH, HCl, and EMI-BF4 ionic liquid electrolyte were supplied by Alfa Aesar. The activated carbon (AC; YP-80F) was supplied by Kuraray Europe GmbH. Carboxylated CNFs (carboxyl content: 1.2 mmol g−1, length range: 1 to 3 μm, average diameter: ~7 nm) are provided by Guilin Qihong Tech. Co., Ltd., China.

Synthesis of HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs

In a typical synthesis, the fresh HHTP ligand (260 mg) was dispersed by ultrasound in 15 ml of deionized (DI) water, and the aqueous solution (10 ml) of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (330 mg) was added slowly. The mixture was stirred for 15 min under ambient atmosphere, and the blue solution was obtained. Subsequently, 10 ml of aqueous solution of sodium acetate (400 mg) was added, and the mixture was heated at 85°C for 2 hours to complete the synthesis reaction. The obtained product was filtered and washed with water, ethanol, and acetone, respectively. The obtained blue product was dried under vacuum prior to use. The synthesis of Cu-HHTP, Co-HHTP, and Ni-HHTP was similar to that of Zn-HHTP, except that the zinc acetate was replaced by Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, Co(NO3)2·6H2O, and Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, respectively (35).

Synthesis of Cu-HATP

Cu-HATP was synthesized by the reported method (36). CuSO4·5H2O (3.5 mg) was suspended in 1.5 ml of dimethylacetamide and sonicated for 10 min. Then, a solution of 5 mg of HATP∙6HCl in 1.5 ml of water was added and sonicated for 5 min. To this, 2 ml of NaOAc aqueous solution (2 mol liter−1) was added at room temperature, and this mixture was heated in a 20-ml open glass vial with stirring for 2 hours at 65°C. The resulting black powder was filtered and washed with large amounts of water and methanol, and dried under vacuum.

Synthesis of Cu-HHB

Cu-HHB was synthesized by the reported method (37). Cu(NO3)2·2.5H2O (93 mg) and NH4OAc (308 mg) were dissolved in 5 ml of H2O to form a light blue solution. A solution of 42 mg of tetrahydroxyquinone in 5 ml of H2O was then poured into the mixed solution. The reaction mixture was sealed after bubbling with N2 for 5 min. After the reaction sample was placed at room temperature for 20 hours without stirring, the dark blue precipitate was filtered and washed with water and acetone. The product was dried under vacuum desiccator at 80°C.

Preparation and regeneration of Cu-HHTP–based gas sensors

The prepared Cu-HHTP was dispersed in 2 ml of solvent consisting of 1 ml of DI water and 1 ml of ethanol for 2 mg each, respectively, and sonicated for 10 min to make it uniformly dispersed in solvent. Then, a certain amount of suspension was extracted and deposited between gold electrodes on a Si substrate. Last, the device was placed on a hot plate at 40°C for 3 hours to remove the solvent. The prepared sensor device was placed in a vacuum oven, activated at 100°C for 4 hours, cooled to room temperature, and stored in a drying cabinet for testing. For the regeneration process, the Cu-HHTP materials could be easily detached from the used gas sensor using ultrasonic treatment. After collecting all the Cu-HHTP materials, we used the same close-loop recycling method to regenerate Cu-HHTP and assemble the regenerated Cu-HHTP–based gas sensors.

Preparation and regeneration of Cu-HHTP–based working electrodes

To prepare Cu-HHTP–based electrode, active material (Cu-HHTP), binder (PVDF), and carbon black were first mixed with a weight ratio of 7:2:1 in NMP. The mixed slurry was coated on an aluminum current collector, followed by drying at 80°C for 12 hours. The mass loading of Cu-HHTP was measured to be about 5 ± 1 mg cm−2. The resultant electrodes were cut into many disks with a diameter of 8 mm and functioned as working electrodes. For the regeneration of working electrode, we first detached the Cu-HHTP from Al current collector in the glovebox. After that, we used mechanochemical ultrasonic cleavage method to degrade the Cu-HHTP and recycle HHTP ligand from the mixture using the same method. Then, we resynthesized the Cu-HHTP using the recycled HHTP and fabricated the regenerated Cu-HHTP–based working electrode.

Preparation of the AC counter electrodes

AC free-standing electrodes were prepared by mixing AC powders and PTFE in a weight ratio of 95:5 with the help of ethanol as the solvent. The mixture was pressed into a film and then dried at 80°C overnight in a vacuum oven. After drying, the film was cut into many disks with diameters of 8 mm.

Preparation of Zn-HHTP/CNF ink

CNF (0.88 g) was first dissolved in 50 ml of DI water at 85°C. Then, the Zn-HHTP/CNF ink was prepared using the same solvent synthesis for Zn-HHTP in the presence of CNF solution. In this way, the Zn-HHTP will in situ grow along the surface of CNF due to the strong interaction between them. By means of changing the mass of CNF, we could obtain Zn-HHTP/CNF ink with different mass ratio.

3D printing via DIW

The printable ZnHHTP-based inks were formulated with the assistance of CNF, and the 2D patterns were procedurally written based on the pre-established models via a three axis–driven printing platform (TS-200BN, Shenzhen Tensun Precision Equipment Co., Ltd., China) assisted with an air-powered fluid dispenser (AP-III). Moreover, the typical printing parameters including nozzle diameter (0.40 mm), step resolution (20 μm), extrusion pressure (15 psi), and printing speed (5 mm s−1) were also provided. The degradation of printed patterns was carried out using the abovementioned combined ultrasonic and alkaline treatment.

Procedures for closed-loop recycling of HHTP-based 2D c-MOFs

NaOH (88 mg) was dissolved in DI water to prepare 44 ml of alkaline treatment solution with a concentration of 0.05 M. Meanwhile, 281 mg of Zn-HHTP was added to the solution, followed by ultrasonication with a power of 900 W and a frequency of 25 kHz under the protection of N2 gas. After that, the degradation mixture consisting of Na-HHTP salt and Zn(OH)2 sediment was isolated through the centrifugal separation. For calculating the mass retention of Zn-HHTP, the pH of the degradation mixture was regulated to 3. After that, the remaining Zn-HHTP sediment was collected and weighted. The Zn(OH)2 sediment was collected for recycling metal ions, and light-yellow Na-HHTP solution was used for recycling expensive HHTP ligand. Furthermore, HCl acid was used to neutralize the solution and protonate the HHTP ligand. To obtain high-quality HHTP, ethyl acetate solvent was used to extract HHTP. After removing the ethyl acetate solvent by rotary evaporation, HHTP powder would be obtained for next-round resynthesis. The resynthesis procedures are the same as mentioned above. The recycling ratio of HHTP was calculated based on the mass ratio of recycled HHTP and the HHTP content in pristine Zn-HHTP. Moreover, we use the same procedures to recycle other HHTP-based 2D c-MOF. For the recyclable gas sensors and electrochemical electrodes, the functional 2D c-MOF materials can be easily detached from Si substrates or aluminum current collectors and subsequently processed through the same recycling pathway.

DFT calculation details

The first-principles calculations were carried out with the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4) (40, 41). The interaction between ions and valence electrons is described using projector augmented wave potentials, and the exchange-correlation between electrons is treated using the generalized gradient approximation in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof form (42). DFT-D3 method was used to calculate the van der Waals interaction (43). To achieve accurate simulations, the plane wave cutoff energy was 450 eV. For structure optimization, M-HHTP (M = Zn, Ni, Co, Cu) monolayer was constructed, and a vacuum space of 15 Å was inserted in the c direction to avoid interactions between periodic images. The recompiled c-axis fixed VASP was used to optimize the x, y lattice parameters and atomic positions. The Monkhorst-Pack k-point meshes of 1 × 1 × 1 were used. Ionic relaxations were carried out under the conventional energy (10−5 eV) and force (0.01 eV/Å) convergence criteria. The lattice parameters of the optimized Zn-HHTP are determined to a = 22.0328 Å, b = 21.9998 Å, and c = 15 Å with α = β = 90° and γ = 60° (for the parameter for other structures, see the supporting cifs). All calculations were conducted under spin polarization.

Life-cycle assessments

Our modeling system encompasses the entire life cycle of different electronic materials, from raw material extraction and manufacturing to end-of-life disposal. All other variables that determine the amount of Zn-HHTP material and energy needed for remanufacturing and recycling scenarios were collected in the laboratory testing environments to provide accurate information for the life-cycle inventory. All background unit process inventories were derived from the Ecoinvent database (3.8) and implemented in the LCA software Simapro for impact assessment of the environmental footprint initiative.
