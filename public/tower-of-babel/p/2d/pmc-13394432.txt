Gastric Mucosal Molecular Alterations and Multimodal Field‐Based Risk Models for Gastric Cancer Development
Authors: Genki Usui, Atsushi Kaneda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGastric cancer risk involves Helicobacter pylori infection, lifestyle and environmental exposures, host susceptibility, and precancerous mucosal changes, such as atrophy and intestinal metaplasia. Although clinical, endoscopic, and histological findings underpin current risk stratification, outcome heterogeneity persists among individuals with similar phenotypes. Here, we review evidence supporting the gastric “multimodal field” as an additional risk assessment layer, emphasizing inflammation‐ and exposure‐associated DNA methylation as a scalable biomarker of field cancerization. Prospective studies and deep profiling of high‐risk intestinal metaplasia support methylation‐based field cancerization. In a longitudinal health check‐up cohort, our work supports an integrated approach combining methylation markers with routinely available clinical variables. We discuss near‐term multimodal extensions that may improve interpretability and predictive precision and support more effective personalized surveillance strategies.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health challenge with marked geographic heterogeneity [1]. Clinically, early detection and treatment improve outcomes; however, primary prevention and efficient surveillance targeting high‐risk individuals are equally crucial [2]. The risk of GC is multifactorial. Helicobacter pylori ( H. pylori ) infection is central, supported by classic epidemiologic evidence [3, 4, 5], and is recognized as carcinogenic [6]. Lifestyle and environmental exposures, such as high salt intake, smoking, and alcohol use, and host‐related factors, including age, sex, family history, and genetic predisposition, contribute to GC risk [2]. Furthermore, the severity and extent of precancerous mucosal lesions, such as atrophy and intestinal metaplasia (IM), are well‐established risk factors [2, 5]. Collectively, GC emerges from multilayered information comprising infection, environment, host, and mucosal phenotype. A long‐standing conceptual framework that connects chronic gastritis to atrophy, IM, dysplasia, and cancer is the Correa cascade, underscoring the clinical relevance of background mucosal pathology as a visible consequence of long‐term exposures (Figure 1) [7, 8].

Risk evaluation traditionally relies on clinical, endoscopic, or histological features. However, even among individuals with similar atrophy or IM, the subsequent pace of progression and occurrence of neoplasia differ [9, 10], indicating that clinical findings alone often lead to residual uncertainty. Optimizing surveillance intensity and preventive interventions requires molecular information that complements clinical phenotyping.

The concept of field cancerization is highly relevant [11]. Field cancerization refers to the accumulation of molecular abnormalities in the non‐neoplastic background mucosa, including epigenomic alterations, genomic changes, and clonal expansions, which constitute a substrate determining future tumor risk [11, 12, 13, 14]. Deep profiling studies have revealed that even in IM, genomic and epigenomic abnormalities and clonal dynamics can be detected, thereby improving the molecular resolution of risk [12, 13]. Among such field components, aberrant DNA methylation is important because it can accumulate in response to inflammation and environmental exposures and be measured stably [15, 16]. DNA methylation may capture an integrated history of long‐term exposures and inflammatory microenvironments rather than merely indicating the presence or absence of a single genetic event, making it a suitable molecular readout to combine with clinical information, such as age, lifestyle factors, and background mucosal phenotypes [15, 16, 17].

Accordingly, the central question for precision risk stratification in GC is how inflammation‐ and exposure‐associated DNA methylation can be integrated with routinely available clinical variables to generate actionable, individual‐level risk tiers. Although methylation‐based field markers have been reported in post‐endoscopic resection settings, it remained unclear whether mucosal methylation adds incremental value for primary GC risk prediction and how it should be combined with heterogeneous real‐world clinical variables [18, 19]. In our longitudinally monitored health check‐up cohort, we addressed this gap by integrating gastric mucosal DNA methylation in promoter regions with environmental and lifestyle information, demonstrating that methylation‐based field strength provides complementary risk information beyond conventional clinical factors and supports implementable integrated risk modeling [20].

In this review, we define the gastric multimodal field as an operational framework for assessing field cancerization in non‐neoplastic gastric mucosa through the integration of clinical and exposure‐related factors, endoscopic and histopathologic background mucosal phenotypes, and molecular information. This concept does not represent a fixed variable set; rather, it currently centers on implementable core components, including clinical risk factors and H. pylori history, endoscopic and pathologic assessment of atrophy and IM, and mucosal DNA methylation as a core molecular field marker. We then review DNA methylation accumulation in the background mucosa, cohort evidence for methylation‐based risk stratification, strategies for model development and validation, and future multimodal extensions that may translate calibrated risk estimates into surveillance planning.

Clinical Risk Factors for GC Development

H. pylori infection is a cornerstone determinant of GC risk, supported by landmark prospective studies [3, 4, 5] and recognized as carcinogenic [2, 6]. However, infection status alone inadequately defines individual risk. Despite similar infection backgrounds, future cancer probability diverges because of heterogeneity in inflammation persistence, mucosal atrophy, IM distribution, lifestyle exposures, and host factors [2, 5, 9, 21, 22]. Therefore, infection is the starting point, but implementable stratification requires integration of mucosal phenotype and other determinants.

Lifestyle factors, such as smoking, alcohol use, high salt intake, and low intake of fruits and vegetables, contribute to GC risk and represent modifiable targets for prevention [2]. However, these variables are often self‐reported in real‐world datasets and prone to measurement error and missingness, limiting their standalone use for precision stratification. DNA methylation in background gastric mucosa partially captures cumulative exposure and inflammatory history [20, 23, 24], supporting more robust risk modeling.

Family history is a readily obtainable clinical variable that can be integrated into risk models. Choi et al. demonstrated the clinical relevance of combining family history and infection status in stratification and intervention strategies [21]. Furthermore, host genetics can modify infection‐associated risk. Notably, Usui Y et al. reported that pathogenic variants in homologous recombination genes interact with H. pylori infection in relation to GC risk, underscoring that “infection‐positive” is not a homogeneous risk category [22]. These findings motivate an integrated framework that moves beyond infection status alone to identify vulnerable individuals within similar infection backgrounds, aligning naturally with an integrated approach combining clinical, molecular, and host susceptibility data.

Current Risk Stratification for GC and Its Limits

Risk stratification predominantly relies on assessment of precancerous mucosal changes, particularly atrophy and IM. The Operative Link on Gastritis Assessment (OLGA), proposed as a histology‐based staging system, integrates severity and topography of atrophy into stages 0–IV [25]. Because histologic atrophy may suffer from reproducibility issues, the Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment (OLGIM) was proposed as an IM‐based alternative, leveraging the typically higher interobserver agreement for IM [26]. Standardized sampling and histologic staging linked to surveillance strategies have been demonstrated in programs, such as the Singapore Gastric Cancer Epidemiology Program (GCEP) [9]. Lee et al. showed that IM severity predicts subsequent risk of GC and early gastric neoplasia and supports targeted surveillance if sampling and reporting are standardized [9]. Guidelines formalize this linkage between risk conditions and surveillance design, including the AGA guidance and MAPS II update [27, 28].

Besides tissue‐based frameworks, blood‐based risk stratification using serum pepsinogen combined with anti‐ H. pylori antibody identifies higher‐risk groups for endoscopic surveillance [29]. Miki proposed the “ABC method” as an operational approach combining these serologic measures to enrich higher‐risk groups for endoscopy [29]. Endoscopic assessment of gastritis and atrophy extent provides practical risk information, reflecting field‐wide mucosal injury and guiding risk management [30, 31].

Although current approaches are useful for enriching high‐risk groups, they remain limited for precise prediction at the individual level. The ABC method shows a stepwise increase in GC risk, but GC still developed in 7 of 3324 individuals in group A, whereas 12 of 443 individuals in group D developed cancer during follow‐up [32]. Endoscopic and histologic staged assessment is also informative, but in the GCEP study, OLGIM stage II was already associated with increased risk (adjusted hazard ratio: 7.34) [9]. Therefore, conventional methods remain fundamental for risk assessment, but incorporating molecular information from the background mucosa may help refine individualized risk prediction.

Field Cancerization and DNA Methylation in Gastric Mucosa

GC risk at the individual level depends on the molecular substrate that accumulates in background mucosa before cancer arises [2, 12, 13]. This field cancerization comprises epigenomic changes, low‐frequency genetic alterations, and clonal biases, driven by infection, chronic inflammation, environmental exposures, and host responses [12, 13, 33]. The gastric mucosa is susceptible to such field formation due to H. pylori ‐associated chronic inflammation, and differences in background molecular states may underlie the clinical observation that individuals with similar endoscopic and histological phenotypes can experience divergent outcomes [5, 12, 13, 15].

Field cancerization in the gastric mucosa can be viewed across overlapping histologic, epigenomic, and genomic‐clonal dimensions [11, 33]. Histologic phenotypes such as atrophy and IM reflect one layer of this field, whereas molecular alterations may provide a deeper readout of the underlying risk substrate [12, 13, 17, 34]. Huang et al. performed genomic and epigenomic profiling of high‐risk IM and identified molecular determinants of progression to GC, establishing that clinically defined IM comprises molecularly heterogeneous states [12]. Using spatiotemporal profiling, Huang et al. further described clonal dynamics during progression, reinforcing that IM is an evolving precursor ecosystem [13]. These findings support the view that IM is a heterogeneous precursor state at the molecular level, with evidence for IM‐to‐cancer conversion accompanied by accelerated DNA methylation [12, 13, 34]. Moreover, evidence from multiple tissues indicates that genetic and epigenetic alterations in morphologically normal tissues can be linked to cancer risk in a context‐dependent manner, supporting the rationale for measuring background mucosa as a risk substrate [14, 35, 36, 37, 38].

Chronic inflammation can induce abnormal methylation that accumulates over time. Niwa et al. demonstrated that inflammatory processes triggered by H. pylori infection can induce abnormal DNA methylation in gastric epithelial cells, supporting inflammation‐mediated processes as an important driver of epigenomic change [15, 33]. Maekita et al. reported high levels of abnormal DNA methylation in H. pylori ‐infected gastric mucosa and suggested associations with GC risk [16]. Furthermore, epigenome‐wide profiling studies reported that normal gastric mucosa shows methylome changes linked to H. pylori infection and cancer‐related states, suggesting methylation captures field‐wide alterations beyond individual loci [12, 17]. Mechanistically, reduced ten‐eleven translocation enzyme and increased DNA methyltransferase activity may act together to promote abnormal DNA methylation, providing a plausible framework for methylation accumulation under chronic inflammatory conditions [33, 39].

Compared with RNA‐based readouts, DNA methylation is relatively stable [40]. Because methylation values can reflect the combined effects of age, infection, and lifestyle exposures, DNA methylation may complement clinical data that are noisy or incomplete [16, 17, 20, 23]. Moreover, a subset of methylation changes can persist in gastric mucosa even after upstream triggers are eliminated, including after H. pylori eradication, suggesting that methylation can serve as a longer‐term record of exposure and inflammation history [33, 41, 42].

In high‐risk IM studies, progression‐related genomic and epigenomic signals contribute to risk heterogeneity, indicating that clinically similar IM can be molecularly distinct [12, 13]. Integrating molecular layers with clinical phenotypes may separate similar groups into different risk strata [9, 43]. However, genomic readouts in non‐neoplastic mucosa can include low‐allele‐frequency alterations and spatially restricted clones [12, 13, 44], whose biological and predictive significance may be difficult to interpret without deep sampling and longitudinal validation. Although high‐resolution genomics can characterize such clonal architecture, its cost and analytical complexity limit routine use. By contrast, DNA methylation is more scalable and captures field strength as a continuous variable, supporting its use in integrated models [18, 19, 20, 43].

Other modalities are also essential for a comprehensive understanding of field risk. Microbiome profiling may reflect microbial ecology beyond H. pylori and inflammation‐associated states, but is affected by sampling procedures, medication exposure, diet, and geography [45, 46]. Proteomic and metabolomic analyses can capture functional cellular or systemic states, whereas pre‐analytical variation and lack of assay standardization remain important barriers [47, 48]. Artificial intelligence (AI)‐based endoscopic and histopathologic image analysis may quantify morphologic features and fit into existing clinical workflows, although external validation, inter‐institutional variation, and interpretability remain critical issues [49, 50, 51]. Spatial omics may help resolve regional heterogeneity within background mucosa and precancerous lesions, but it currently remains primarily a research‐stage approach because of cost, tissue‐processing requirements, and analytical complexity [52, 53]. These modalities should not be viewed as competing with DNA methylation, but as complementary candidates that may refine methylation‐based field strength and improve model interpretability and predictive accuracy.

Post‐endoscopic resection surveillance cohorts are enriched for events and provide a practical setting to test field biomarkers. Suzuki et al. reported that higher miR‐34b/c methylation in noncancerous gastric body mucosa was associated with increased metachronous GC risk after endoscopic resection [54]. Asada et al. conducted a multicenter prospective cohort study showing that methylation markers in non‐neoplastic background mucosa stratify the risk of metachronous GC after endoscopic resection [18]. Maeda et al. reported that methylation accumulation significantly affects metachronous risk, reinforcing that field methylation is prospectively linked to future neoplasia in a setting with clear surveillance implications [19]. These studies established the clinical validity of methylation‐based field assessment in secondary prevention. However, whether mucosal methylation provides incremental, clinically usable value for primary‐risk prediction in largely asymptomatic populations and how it should be interpreted alongside H. pylori exposure, lifestyle factors, and heterogeneous real‐world clinical variables remain unclear.

Therefore, our translational study using the longitudinally monitored health check‐up cohort [20] performed comprehensive DNA methylation analysis using selected tissues from the check‐up cohort, including baseline biopsies from participants who eventually developed gastric neoplasia and control mucosae with or without H. pylori history. Baseline biopsies from individuals who later developed gastric neoplasia showed substantially higher methylation than controls. DNA methylation tracked with atrophy and IM severity, but remained higher in future‐neoplasia cases than non‐cases even within comparable atrophy or IM strata. In multivariable models, DNA methylation remained an independent predictor of gastric neoplasia development.

DNA methylation levels were higher in patients with H. pylori infection, and smoking and alcohol consumption were associated with methylation accumulation primarily among H. pylori –infected individuals [20]. Despite adjusting clinical factors, heavy drinking and smoking remained associated with increased methylation, consistent with a model in which H. pylori ‐associated inflammation initiates methylation changes that are amplified by adverse lifestyle exposures. We examined methylation trajectories in relation to smoking cessation, showing attenuated methylation increase among those who quit smoking compared with continuous smokers.

Furthermore, we investigated whether adding methylation improves discrimination beyond routinely available clinical variables [20]. In multivariable logistic regression models, the area under the curve improved as DNA methylation was included as an additional explanatory variable. Moreover, methylation stratified risk within clinically high‐risk strata. These results positioned mucosal methylation as a scalable field‐strength metric that reflects etiologic exposures and delivers incremental predictive value for primary gastric neoplasia risk prediction.

Yamada et al. conducted a multicenter prospective study showing that a DNA methylation marker (RIMS1) can stratify the risk of primary GC [43]. Collectively, these complementary cohort designs, including post‐resection cohorts, deep phenotyping studies of high‐risk IM, and large‐scale health check‐up datasets, provide convergent support for DNA methylation as a scalable molecular readout for GC risk stratification. Lopes et al. reported in a Caucasian population that hypermethylation in gastric mucosa predicts metachronous gastric lesions, supporting the generalizability of methylation‐based field markers beyond Asian cohorts [55].

Combining Health Check‐Up Cohorts and Protocolized Research Cohorts to Strengthen Evidence for Integrated Risk Models

Methylation‐based field markers can support stratification; however, considering model building and operational deployment, relying on a single design or data source is insufficient. An integrated model emphasizing DNA methylation plus comprehensive clinical information should function in large, generalizable real‐world populations, with inevitable missingness and heterogeneity, and must be biologically and methodologically valid under standardized endoscopy, pathology and deep phenotyping [56, 57]. Health check‐up cohorts and protocolized research cohorts have different but complementary strengths (Table 2), and combining them is a rational strategy [9, 12, 20].

The major strength of health check‐up datasets is their population‐scale size with long‐term follow‐up, enabling sufficient event counts and risk estimation and calibration under real‐world variability [20]. Health check‐ups systematically collect lifestyle factors, medical history, family history, and basic laboratory data, precisely the comprehensive clinical information needed for integrated models. Repeated check‐ups, if available, enable longitudinal modeling and dynamic risk updates [20]. Limitations include insufficient standardization of pathology and systematic biopsies, incomplete deep phenotyping, and frequent missingness and site effects. Large‐scale methylation measurement for all participants may be difficult, requiring staged measurement strategies [20]. Thus, health check‐up datasets are ideal for building models that run in real‐world systems, whereas deep biological validation requires complementary cohorts.

Protocolized research cohorts can standardize endoscopy, pathology, and sampling protocols, enabling reproducible assessment of atrophy and IM and facilitating precise characterization of heterogeneity among clinically similar individuals [9, 12, 18, 19, 43]. These cohorts can incorporate repeated sampling, inflammation metrics, and integrated genomic and epigenomic analyses to test whether DNA methylation quantifies field strength and complements clinical phenotypes. However, such cohorts may have limited scale or representativeness, necessitating recalibration if transported to general populations [57, 58]. Therefore, research cohorts function naturally as depth engines to validate and refine integrated models developed in large‐scale real‐world data.

Overall, these cohort types are complementary. Strategic iteration between them can provide the most reliable path to develop, validate, and implement methylation‐centered integrated risk stratification.

DNA Methylation‐Centered Multimodal Field Models: Operational Framework and Clinical Implementation

A DNA methylation‐centered multimodal field model should be understood as an operational framework rather than a fixed variable set. In its minimum implementable form, the model integrates three core layers: (i) clinical and exposure‐related variables, including age, sex, H. pylori infection or eradication history, smoking, alcohol intake, dietary factors, family history, medications, comorbidities, and routine laboratory data when available [2, 20, 21, 22]; (ii) endoscopic and histopathologic background mucosal phenotypes [9, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30], including endoscopic atrophy and OLGA/OLGIM stage; and (iii) mucosal DNA methylation as a core molecular field marker measured from standardized background mucosal biopsies [18, 19, 20, 40, 43]. This minimum framework can be expanded by additional modalities, such as AI‐derived endoscopic or histopathologic features [49, 50, 51], spatial omics, microbiome [45, 46], proteomic/metabolomic readouts [47, 48], and genomic or clonal analyses [12, 13, 44], when they provide reproducible incremental value.

These core layers are clinically relevant but imperfect. Clinical risk factors, including infection, lifestyle exposures, age, and background mucosal phenotypes, contribute to GC risk; however, real‐world data inevitably include missingness and measurement error [56, 57, 58]. Lifestyle variables depend on self‐reports, and endoscopic and pathologic assessments can vary by institution and sampling. DNA methylation can reflect the long‐term cumulative influence of multiple factors and may absorb some imperfections in clinical inputs, serving as a core element that increases the robustness of integrated models [20]. Additionally, the degree and trajectory of methylation accumulation in the background mucosa might be related to epigenotype [59, 60] and molecular subtype [61] of the eventual tumor, suggesting the need for an integrated approach.

In clinical settings, this framework could be applied at the time of index endoscopy, surveillance endoscopy, or health check‐up‐based endoscopic assessment. Clinical and exposure‐related variables would first be collected using structured questionnaires, medical records, laboratory data, and H. pylori testing [2, 20]. Background mucosal status would then be assessed endoscopically and, when biopsies are available, histologically using standardized criteria such as OLGA/OLGIM [9, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Mucosal DNA methylation would be measured from prespecified non‐neoplastic biopsy sites under defined quality‐control criteria [18, 19, 40, 43]. These inputs would be integrated into a prediction model, preferably a time‐to‐event model, to estimate calibrated absolute risk of gastric neoplasia over a defined time horizon [56, 57, 58]. At present, universal risk thresholds for methylation‐centered multimodal models have not been established. Therefore, such models should be viewed as tools to complement, rather than replace, existing risk stratification frameworks based on H. pylori status, endoscopic atrophy, OLGA/OLGIM stage, and guideline‐based surveillance recommendations [9, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 31]. In future implementation, thresholds should be defined according to calibrated absolute risk over a prespecified time horizon and should be adapted to the target population, background GC incidence, eradication status, and available endoscopic resources [56, 57, 58]. Conceptually, low‐risk individuals may be candidates for less intensive follow‐up, intermediate‐risk individuals for guideline‐concordant surveillance with periodic reassessment, and high‐risk individuals for intensified endoscopic surveillance, careful inspection, standardized biopsy‐based reassessment, and targeted prevention, including eradication confirmation and lifestyle intervention. These strategies require prospective validation and clinical impact studies before being incorporated into routine surveillance guidelines.

To support such threshold‐based clinical use, the model should either be restricted to essential variables or be modular so that risk can be computed even when some non‐core variables are unavailable. Simultaneously, integrated models are more susceptible to overfitting, missingness, and site effects as inputs increase. Therefore, prespecified variable selection, transparent handling of missing data, internal validation, and external validation are required. Beyond discrimination, external validation should focus on calibration and transportability across multiple populations, because a clinically useful model must estimate absolute risk reliably enough to support surveillance decisions [57, 58].

Calibration, external validation, and clinical utility should be regarded as requirements for clinical translation rather than established milestones of current methylation‐centered models. Although existing cohort studies support the association between mucosal methylation and future gastric neoplasia, evidence remains limited regarding whether predicted risks are well validated across diverse populations, including different ethnic backgrounds, geographic regions, H. pylori eradication contexts, and endoscopic surveillance systems [20, 43]. Future studies should evaluate calibration in addition to discrimination, ideally using calibration plots together with appropriate summary measures such as calibration‐in‐the‐large or observed‐to‐expected ratios and calibration slope [56, 58]. External validation should assess model transportability and, when necessary, recalibration in independent cohorts [57, 58]. Finally, clinical utility should be assessed in future studies, potentially using predefined risk thresholds, decision‐curve analysis, net benefit, and prospective impact studies to determine whether model‐guided surveillance could improve detection efficiency while avoiding unnecessary endoscopic burden [56, 57, 62].

After the minimum framework has been validated, staged multimodal integration may be considered. Whole‐slide imaging for hematoxylin and eosin staining combined with artificial intelligence may quantify and standardize morphologic features and provide reproducible phenotype inputs [50, 51]. Additionally, spatial omics can visualize spatial heterogeneity in background mucosa and precancerous lesions [52, 53], strengthening biological interpretation of methylation‐based field metrics. Microbiome, proteomic/metabolomic, and genomic or clonal analyses may also be incorporated if they provide reproducible incremental value [12, 13, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. These modalities are not intended to replace methylation; rather, they can add new information layers to methylation‐centered models to improve robustness and interpretability.

Limitations and Implementation Challenges of DNA Methylation‐Based Field Assessment

Despite its promise, DNA methylation‐based field assessment has several important limitations. First, methylation values can be influenced by sampling bias and biopsy‐site dependence. The gastric mucosa is anatomically and biologically heterogeneous [63], and methylation levels may differ according to biopsy location, proximity to visible lesions, severity of inflammation, and the distribution of atrophy or IM [12, 20, 64]. Because IM and molecular field alterations can be spatially heterogeneous [13, 44, 64], a single biopsy may not fully represent the overall field strength of the stomach. Therefore, standardized biopsy locations, minimal sampling rules, and explicit reporting of sampling protocols are required for reproducible risk assessment.

Second, measured methylation levels may be affected by tissue composition. Background mucosal samples can contain variable proportions of gastric epithelial cells, metaplastic epithelium, inflammatory cells, and stromal components [63]. Such cellular heterogeneity may influence methylation signals [65] and complicate comparisons across individuals and institutions. Future studies should evaluate whether epithelial enrichment, computational adjustment, or spatially resolved approaches can improve the interpretability of methylation‐based field markers.

Third, reproducibility across assays and laboratories remains a key issue. Existing studies have used different methylation assays, including pyrosequencing, quantitative methylation‐specific polymerase chain reaction, and array‐based profiling. These platforms differ in genomic coverage, quantitative dynamic range, DNA input requirements, compatibility with formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue, and quality‐control procedures [40, 66]. Assay harmonization, prespecified marker panels, reference materials, inter‐laboratory comparisons, and clinically acceptable thresholds will be necessary before broad implementation.

Finally, feasibility in routine clinical practice remains unresolved. Methylation‐based risk assessment requires decisions regarding who should be tested, when biopsies should be obtained, how many samples are needed, whether testing can be integrated into routine endoscopy or health check‐up workflows, and whether the added cost and turnaround time are justified by improved surveillance efficiency. Thus, DNA methylation should currently be viewed as a promising but still implementation‐dependent field biomarker. Its clinical use will require standardized sampling and assay protocols, external validation across diverse populations, demonstration of calibration and clinical utility, and prospective studies showing that methylation‐guided surveillance improves outcomes or resource allocation.

Conclusion

Quantifying molecular alterations in the background gastric mucosa enables precision risk stratification. Because DNA methylation integrates cumulative inflammatory and exposure history and can be measured robustly, it is a practical core component of multimodal field models when combined with comprehensive clinical information. Implementation requires a minimal feature set, models robust to missing data, standardized sampling and quality control, calibrated external validation across populations, and prospective evaluation of clinical utility. By leveraging the complementary strengths of health check‐up cohorts and protocolized research cohorts, high‐quality evidence can be generated to support more accurate risk prediction and individualized surveillance and prevention strategies.
