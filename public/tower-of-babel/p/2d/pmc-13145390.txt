PEBP1 inhibition as a therapeutic target for neurological recovery in ischemic stroke
Authors: Yu-Qian Niu, Ze-Yu Cai, Hao-Yang Zhi, Yu-Chun Zhu, Xin-Yuan Xi, Zhen Yang, Dong-Fu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke poses a substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden due to limited therapeutic efficacy and poor neurological outcomes. To uncover novel gene targets for intervention, we conducted an integrative analysis combining single-cell RNA sequencing with Mendelian randomization using large-scale genomic datasets from the European Bioinformatics Institute (34,593 cases and 624,214 controls), with validation in an independent European Bioinformatics Institute dataset (86,668 cases and 1,503,898 controls) and the UK Biobank (26,052 cases and 487,214 controls). Colocalization analysis identified four core genes—PEBP1, BMP4, APOA1 and CD86—strongly associated with ischemic stroke risk, with a posterior probability of a shared causal variant greater than 0.8. Among them, PEBP1 was markedly upregulated post-ischemia, particularly in endothelial cells, as confirmed by quantitative PCR and immunofluorescence in a middle cerebral artery occlusion model. Both pharmacological inhibition of PEBP1 with FerroLOXIN-1 and AAV-BI30-mediated shRNA knockdown reduced cerebral infarct volume, enhanced neuronal survival, and improved neurological functional recovery. In vitro, FerroLOXIN-1 enhanced cell proliferation and viability under oxygen-glucose deprivation conditions, with potential off-target effects of the interventions validated. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated through activation of the Akt/p38 MAPK signaling cascade. These findings highlight PEBP1 as a central mediator of ischemia-induced neuronal injury and a potential therapeutic target. The convergence of transcriptomic, genetic and experimental validation supports the translational relevance of PEBP1 inhibition in post-stroke neuroregeneration.

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for 5.2% of all deaths [1], and is classified as either hemorrhagic or ischemic stroke (IS) based on its pathological features. IS constitutes 87 % of all strokes and is responsible for 50.3 % of stroke-related deaths. Because it frequently results in long-term disability and diminished quality of life, IS poses a major clinical challenge [2,3].

IS arises from thrombosis and insufficient blood supply, which result in cerebral tissue necrosis, structural damage, neuronal death and functional deficits. The primary pathogenic mechanism involves the formation and rupture of cerebrovascular atherosclerotic plaques that obstruct cerebral arteries [4]. The incidence of IS is high and continues to rise globally. Epidemiologic studies have identified several major risk factors, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, obesity, heart disease, and family history [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Furthermore, the relationship between sex and stroke risk is modulated by age [11]. Unfortunately, no ideal pharmacotherapy for IS currently exists; intravenous thrombolysis with tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA) remains the sole thrombolytic therapy approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of IS during the hyperacute phase [12,13]. However, tPA is constrained by a narrow therapeutic window and is associated with adverse effects such as hemorrhagic transformation and ischemia/reperfusion injury, both of which substantially compromise patient outcomes and limit its clinical utility [14,15]. Recent studies have shown that restoration of blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity following stroke can effectively reduce hemorrhagic transformation and even mortality [16,17]. Additionally, various molecules, including endothelial cell-derived R-spondin 3 (RSPO3) and hairy/enhancer-of-split related with YRPW motif protein 1 (HEY1), have demonstrated therapeutic potential in IS [18,19]. Nevertheless, the clinical implications of these findings remain uncertain and the efficacy and long-term safety of these drug targets in patient populations require further validation. Consequently, exploring a broader spectrum of mechanisms and therapeutic targets for the treatment of IS is imperative.

Traditional disease research is both time-consuming and costly. However, the integration of genomics and bioinformatics can facilitate the efficient identification of key genes and associated mechanisms relevant to disease therapy. In this study, we combined Mendelian randomization and single-cell data to identify key genes, PEBP1, BMP4, APOA1 and CD86, which are strongly correlated with IS in population samples. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed to elucidate the potential mechanisms of action of these genes. The expression of these genes was validated in a murine model of IS induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) in vivo. We subsequently demonstrated that PEBP1 is highly expressed in vascular endothelial cells (ECs) and that the inhibition of PEBP1 by FerroLOXIN-1 promotes cell proliferation via the Akt/p38 signaling pathway, thereby facilitating neuronal recovery after IS. This finding was further confirmed by AAV-BI30-mediated shRNA knockdown of PEBP1. Collectively, our findings identify novel therapeutic targets for IS.

Materials and methods

1. Data Sources

1. Data Sources

Data Sources

Plasma protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) summary statistics for 34,557 European-descent participants were obtained from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project [20,21]. Outcome genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for IS in European populations were retrieved from the European Bioinformatics Institute database for the training (GCST90018864: 34,593 cases, 624,214 controls) [22] and validation sets (GCST90104535: 237,242 cases, 20,030 controls) [23]. Single-cell RNA sequencing data of brain tissues from sham and MCAO mice (n = 3/group) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE174574) [23].2.Mendelian Randomization (MR) and Colocalization Analyses

2.Mendelian Randomization (MR) and Colocalization Analyses

Mendelian Randomization (MR) and Colocalization Analyses

Instrumental variables were selected based on locus-wide significance (P < 1∗10−5), linkage disequilibrium (R2< 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window) and an F-statistic >10. Causal estimates were evaluated using inverse variance-weighted, MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode and Wald ratio methods [24]. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to verify robustness. Colocalization of expression quantitative trait loci and IS-related GWAS loci was assessed using the coloc R package [25]. Posterior probabilities were calculated within a 100-kb region flanking the index single-nucleotide polymorphism, with shared causal variants defined as posterior probability H4 > 0.8.3.Single-Cell Data Processing

3.Single-Cell Data Processing

Single-Cell Data Processing

Data processing was performed using Seurat (v4.3.0) [26]. Cells were filtered utilizing the median absolute deviation method to exclude outliers based on unique molecular identifiers, expressed gene counts and mitochondrial/ribosomal gene ratios. Following log-normalization, cell cycle scoring and highly variable gene identification, data were scaled to regress out mitochondrial/ribosomal proportions and cell cycle effects. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using principal component analysis and Harmony-corrected Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Cell types were annotated using SingleR [27], referencing the CellMarker and PanglaoDB databases alongside established marker genes [28].4.Animals and Ischemic Stroke Model

4.Animals and Ischemic Stroke Model

Animals and Ischemic Stroke Model

Male C57BL/6J mice (23–25 g; Charles River, Beijing, China) were used under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Interdisciplinary Research Center on Biology and Chemistry (Protocol 202003080011). Focal cerebral ischemia was induced via a 60-min MCAO using a silicone-coated filament, followed by reperfusion [29]. Successful occlusion (>70% cerebral blood flow reduction) was confirmed via laser speckle contrast imaging (PeriCam PSI-ZR). Sham animals underwent identical procedures without filament insertion. Core temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C. At 30 min post-ischemia, mice received an intravenous tail vein injection of either dimethyl sulfoxide or FerroLOXIN-1 (25 mg/kg; MedChemExpress) [30].5.Cell Culture and Oxygen-Glucose Deprivation

9.Behavioral Assessments

Behavioral Assessments

Neurological function was evaluated at 7 and 14days post-MCAO by two blinded investigators: 1) Corner Turning Test: Sensorimotor asymmetry was assessed by recording the percentage of left turns over 10 trials in a 30° corner apparatus. 2) Rotarod Test: Motor coordination was evaluated via an accelerating rotarod (4–40 rpm over 5 min), recording the average latency to fall across three trials. 3) Foot Fault Test: Limb placement deficits were quantified as the percentage of contralateral forelimb missteps during a 1-min elevated grid walk.10.Statistical Analysis

10.Statistical Analysis

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism 8. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were conducted using the two-sided Student's t-test. 4.PEBP1 inhibition moderates post stroke infarct volume and neuronal damage in vivo

PEBP1 inhibition moderates post stroke infarct volume and neuronal damage in vivo

These in vivo validation results suggest that PEBP1 changes most significantly after ischemia and may influence IS through cell proliferation (Fig. 4 A). PEBP1 is positively correlated with many cancers [33,34] and neurodegenerative diseases [35]. Dysregulated PEBP1 interacts with several pathways, such as the MAPK, GRK2 and NF-кB pathways, that regulate cell proliferation and differentiation [36,37]. Notably, cell proliferation and differentiation play crucial roles in neuronal recovery after stroke. However, few studies have investigated whether PEBP1 influences IS treatment. Thus, we used male WT mice and the PEBP1-specific inhibitor FerroLOXIN-1 to study the effects of PEBP1 on stroke and to verify the reliability of the above analysis.

We chose 1d and 3d after MCAO as the time points for testing (Fig. 5 A). At 24h after MCAO, PEBP1 expression was significantly elevated in the ischemic core and penumbra (Fig. 5 B). Importantly, the increase in PEBP1 colocalized with CD31 was more pronounced and the increase was inhibited by FerroLOXIN-1 (Fig. 5C–D). Discussion

Investigations into IS have focused on several critical areas. First, inadequate perfusion is associated with neuronal damage and metabolic dysfunction. Research has demonstrated that the endothelial-specific overexpression of Ascl1 enhances the transdifferentiation of astrocytes into neural progenitor cells, thereby promoting neuronal recovery following IS [41]. Additionally, the therapeutic potential of the vascular system, as a component of the neurovascular unit, has garnered increased attention in recent years. It has been shown that long-term neurological recovery poststroke can be significantly improved by specifically inhibiting microRNA-15a/16-1, a negative regulator of angiogenesis [42]. Furthermore, targeted activation of the BBB-specific Wnt signaling pathway via genetic engineering has been found to substantially reduce the infarct volume in IS [43].

Among the four key genes associated with IS identified through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses of population samples, PEBP1 has emerged as a focal point of research interest in recent years. PEBP1, also referred to as Raf kinase inhibitory protein (RKIP1), forms a complex with 15LO and has been shown to catalyze the oxygenation of polyunsaturated phosphatidylethanolamine [44]. This process induces ferroptosis in airway epithelial cells from asthma patients, renal epithelial cells from individuals with renal failure, and cortical and hippocampal neurons from patients with brain injuries [44]. PEBP1 functions as a rheostat, balancing pro-survival autophagy and ferroptotic cell death in asthmatic epithelial cells [45]. Specific knockdown of PEBP1 has been shown to mitigate hepatic necrotic apoptosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in diabetic and obese murine models [46]. Additionally, FerroLOXIN-1 and FerroLOXIN-2 have been shown to rescue mice from total body irradiation-induced mortality by inhibiting ferroptosis and increasing survival [30]. PEBP1 has also been implicated in various diseases, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma and osteoarthritis [34,47]; however, its significance in the context of IS remains to be established.

IS disrupts cerebral iron metabolism: transferrin-bound iron enters cells via TFR1, increasing the level of intracellular iron [48,49]. Excess iron catalyzes Fenton reactions, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage DNA, proteins and membranes, ultimately triggering ferroptosis [50]. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) upregulation further increases TFR1 expression, exacerbating iron overload [51]. Under physiological conditions, PEBP1 is enriched in the hippocampal pyramidal and granule layers, where it regulates neural development and synaptic plasticity. To verify the function of PEBP1 in IS, we performed immunofluorescence staining and observed that PEBP1 was significantly elevated in both the ischemic core and penumbra and that it colocalized predominantly with endothelial cells. We subsequently found that FerroLOXIN-1 mediated PEBP1 inhibition reduced infarct volume and, after reperfusion, increased neuronal survival. In vitro experiments demonstrated that inhibition of PEBP1 activated the AKT/p38 signaling pathway and promoted neuronal proliferation following hypoxia, suggesting that inhibition of PEBP1 attenuates ischemia-induced brain damage by promoting cell proliferation. H&E staining corroborated these findings by revealing less cellular disorganization and atrophy. Although the role of PEBP1 was further validated by in vivo AAV-BI30-mediated shRNA knockdown, this approach achieved only transient gene silencing rather than permanent genetic ablation. Even so, our findings uncover a previously unappreciated role of PEBP1, provide experimental support for the bioinformatic predictions, and suggest that PEBP1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for ischemic stroke.

In this study, PEBP1 was predominantly upregulated in endothelial cells following IS, and treatment with its inhibitor significantly increased neuronal number during the recovery phase, suggesting that PEBP1 is broadly involved in the pathological process of ischemic brain injury. Further in vitro experiments demonstrated that direct administration of a PEBP1 inhibitor promoted the proliferation of hypoxic-injured neurons, indicating that PEBP1 suppresses neuronal proliferation by negatively regulating the Akt pathway, whereas the inhibitor relieves this suppression and activates pro-survival and pro-proliferation signaling. These findings suggest that PEBP1 represents a potential intervention target for post-ischemic neural repair. Our results imply that PEBP1 in endothelial cells and neurons may synergistically contribute to the pathophysiological regulation of IS. Recent studies have reported that elevated cerebrospinal fluid PEBP1 levels are associated with reduced synaptic density in Alzheimer's disease patients and increased serum PEBP1 in children with sepsis may also be involved in neuronal dysfunction, indicating that PEBP1 exerts neural regulatory roles in multiple neurological disorders. However, the mechanisms underlying its crosstalk among different cell types remain to be further elucidated.

In addition, our study revealed that three other key genes are also strongly associated with IS. BMP4 governs neural development, driving neuronal differentiation and growth [52]. BMP4 exerts dual roles in the inflammatory response: it promotes tumor progression by facilitating the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells [53]; conversely, it may attenuate neuroinflammation by modulating the immune response. Specific inhibition of BMP4 expression increases inflammation and the secretion of proinflammatory factors from endothelial cells and promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation [54,55]. BMP4 also promotes c-Src phosphorylation via the BMPRI-II complex, activates VEGFR2 and triggers angiogenesis [56]. Thus, BMP4 may reduce brain damage in IS by promoting processes such as nerve regeneration or angiogenesis.

APOA1, a major component of HDL, is involved in cholesterol transport and metabolism [57]. This protein is synthesized in the liver but can also enter the brain via the choroid plexus and may contribute to the maintenance of cerebral lipid homeostasis. In cardiovascular disease, APOA1 exerts a protective effect and reduces the cardiovascular disease risk [58]. It is widely recognized that APOA1 functions primarily in disorders of lipid metabolism. However, APOA1 also plays a critical role in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease. One study revealed that APOA1 inhibits microglial activation and the release of inflammatory factors [59] and promotes neuronal survival in AD [60]. Moreover, plasma ApoA-I levels are inversely correlated with the risk of PD and lower levels of ApoA1 tend to be associated with poorer motor function [61,62]. This evidence suggests that APOA1 may contribute to the pathophysiology of IS by attenuating the inflammatory response or promoting neuronal repair; however, the precise relationship between APOA1 and IS remains to be verified.

CD86 is expressed primarily by antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells and macrophages [63]. The enhanced interaction of CD86 with its ligand CD28 in IS may promote the release of inflammatory factors [64,65], thereby exacerbating neuronal damage and inflammatory responses in brain tissues. Consequently, inhibiting the expression of CD86 and its ligand following cerebral ischemia may be beneficial for neuronal repair. Our data indicate that BMP4, APOA1 and CD86 may also be strongly associated with IS, but their specific roles still require further investigation.

In conclusion, our findings collectively suggest that PEBP1 represents a promising therapeutic target for IS. However, given the complexity of the underlying molecular mechanisms and the limitations inherent to the present study, these observations necessitate independent validation in additional experimental models and clinical cohorts, as well as further in depth mechanistic investigations to fully elucidate the role of PEBP1 in IS pathogenesis and its potential translational value.
