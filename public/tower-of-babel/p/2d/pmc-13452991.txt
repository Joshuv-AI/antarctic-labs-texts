Identification of UL3.5 as a novel substrate of DPV US3 kinase whose phosphorylation at Ser64/Ser84 enhances protein stability and promotes viral release, and cell-to-cell spread
Authors: Zhen Xiao, Bin Tian, Huanhuan Cao, Ran Ran, Dongjie Cai, Mingshu Wang, Ying Wu, Qiao Yang, Shaqiu Zhang, Dekang Zhu, Mafeng Liu, Xinxin Zhao, Di Sun, Juan Huang, Xumin Ou, Zhen Wu, Yu He, Yuhong Pan, Renyong Jia, Shun Chen, Anchun Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck plague virus (DPV) is a major pathogen threatening waterfowl industry. As a conserved alphaherpesvirus kinase, DPV US3 participates in viral replication, while its functional substrates are largely uncharacterized. In this study, we verified that US3 directly binds and phosphorylates UL3.5 at Ser64 and Ser84. This post-translational modification inhibits protein degradation and prolongs the half-life of UL3.5. A phosphorylation-deficient recombinant virus was constructed, and phenotypic assays demonstrated that mutated virus had defective replication. Further tests indicated that UL3.5 phosphorylation mainly regulates viral genome replication, progeny release and cell-to-cell spread, rather than attachment and entry. Our findings expand the regulatory network of DPV US3 and uncover the biological function of UL3.5, laying a foundation for developing novel antiviral strategies against DPV.Keywords: Duck plague virus, UL3.5, US3 kinase, Phosphorylation

Introduction

Duck plague virus (DPV), also known as duck viral enteritis, belongs to the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae and is the causative agent of an acute, septicemic disease in ducks, geese, and other waterfowl(Kruasuwan et al., 2025). Since the first report of duck plague in the Netherlands in 1923, the disease has become widespread in many countries and regions worldwide, causing substantial economic losses to the waterfowl industry The DPV genome is a linear double-stranded DNA of approximately 160 kb, encoding numerous proteins involved in viral replication, host immune regulation, and pathogenesis(Kruasuwan et al., 2025) . Among these, UL3.5 is a DPV-specific tegument protein located in the unique long (UL) region of the viral genome. Compared with other alphaherpesviruses, UL3.5 exhibits low evolutionary conservation; however, its homologs in pseudorabies virus (PRV) and bovine herpesvirus type 1 (BoHV-1) have been shown to participate in virion maturation and egress(Cao et al., 2025). Studies have demonstrated that PRV UL3.5 interacts with UL48 to facilitate secondary envelopment of virions(Fuchs et al., 2007), while BoHV-1 UL3.5 is a structural protein of the virion, and its deletion or mutation significantly impairs viral replication (Barber et al., 2017; Lam and Letchworth, 2004). Nevertheless, the specific function and post-translational modification of DPV UL3.5 remain unclear.na

Protein phosphorylation, mediated by protein kinases, is one of the most common and important post-translational modifications, playing critical roles in cellular signal transduction, protein function regulation, and the viral life cycle(Li et al., 2025). US3 is a conserved serine/threonine protein kinase encoded by alphaherpesviruses and was first identified in herpes simplex virus (HSV) (Erazo and Kinchington, 2010; Hossain et al., 2025). The molecular mechanism of HSV-1 US3-mediated phosphorylation centers on a core logic of "activation–binding–phosphorylation–functional regulation": US3 first achieves kinase activation through autophosphorylation at Ser147, a process essential for its substrate phosphorylation function and viral pathogenicity in vivo (Hossain et al., 2025; Shio et al., 2025). subsequently, activated US3 specifically recognizes and binds to target substrates, transferring the phosphate group from ATP to the serine or threonine residues of substrates through its kinase domain(Bahnamiri and Roller, 2023). This phosphorylation regulates substrate function mainly by altering protein conformation, thereby affecting stability, subcellular localization, or protein–protein interactions(Kliche and Ivarsson, 2022). Functionally, US3 is involved in regulating virion budding, cytoskeletal rearrangement, apoptosis inhibition, and antagonism of interferon signaling(Hossain et al., 2025; Shio et al., 2025). In HSV-1, US3 phosphorylates UL31 and UL34 to promote nuclear egress of nucleocapsids(Bahnamiri and Roller, 2023; Hossain et al., 2025); it phosphorylates envelope glycoprotein B (gB) at Thr887 to regulate its cell surface expression and facilitate viral envelope assembly (Imai et al., 2011; Kato et al., 2009); it phosphorylates UL47 at Ser77 to modulate its subcellular localization and ensure capsid assembly integrity (Kato et al., 2011; Liao et al., 2020); it also phosphorylates host pro-apoptotic protein BAD to inhibit apoptosis and lamin A/C and emerin to disrupt the nuclear lamina, thereby optimizing the cellular microenvironment for viral replication (Hossain et al., 2025). In PRV, US3 kinase activity is essential for viral replication and pathogenicity (Chang et al., 2013)

HSV-1 US3 recognizes a specific substrate motif, RNX(S/T)YY, where N represents ≥2 arbitrary amino acids, X can be Arg, Ala, Val, Pro or Ser, Y represents any amino acid except acidic residues, and the core phosphorylation site is Ser or Thr. This motif is highly conserved with that of the PRV US3 homolog, and its substrate recognition specificity is similar to that of cellular kinases PKA and Akt(Kato et al., 2014a; Kato et al., 2014b). In recent years, our research group has identified that DPV US3 phosphorylates UL47, regulating its localization in infected cells, and also antagonizes the host innate immune response by phosphorylating IRF7 (Deng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022). However, whether DPV US3 regulates viral replication through phosphorylation of other substrate proteins remains largely unexplored. Given that UL3.5, as a DPV-specific tegument protein, may contain US3 recognition motifs, we hypothesize that UL3.5 could be a novel substrate of DPV US3, and its phosphorylation may play important roles in viral replication.

This study aims to identify UL3.5 as a novel substrate of DPV US3 and to investigate the phosphorylation mechanism, including determining whether US3 interacts with and phosphorylates UL3.5 and mapping the key phosphorylation sites, elucidating the effect of this phosphorylation on UL3.5 protein stability, evaluating its impact on viral replication through phenotypic characterization of a phosphorylation site mutant recombinant virus, and further dissecting which specific steps of the viral life cycle—attachment, entry, genome replication, progeny release, and cell-to-cell spread—are regulated by the US3-UL3.5 axis. Our findings reveal the critical role of this regulatory axis in DPV replication and provide new insights for the development of anti-DPV intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was carried out in compliance with the standard guidelines. The duck embryos utilized were sourced from farms with no history of Duck Plague Virus (DPV) vaccination. All experimental procedures adhered to the operational guidelines of Sichuan Agricultural University and received formal approval from the Animal Welfare Committee under permit number20250264

Fertilized duck embryos were incubated in a constant-temperature incubator at 37.5°C with 55–60% relative humidity and regular ventilation. After experiments, all used duck embryos and embryo-derived biological wastes were collected and subjected to high-pressure steam sterilization (121°C, 30 min) for harmless disposal in accordance with the biosafety management regulations of Sichuan Agricultural University. No experimental materials were randomly discarded.

Cell culture and transfection

Duck embryonic fibroblasts (DEFs) were prepared from 9-day-old duck embryos and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Yeasen) at 37°C in 5% CO₂. HEK293T cells were maintained in the same medium. For transfection, cells were transfected with the indicated constructs using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Transfection Reagent (Yeasen) according to the manufacturer's instructions.Duck embryonic fibroblasts (DEFs) used in all experiments were maintained within 2 passages to guarantee cell activity and experimental reproducibility.

Antibodies and reagents

Commercial primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: mouse anti-NanoLuc antibody (1:1000, Promega, Cat.# N7000, Madison, WI, USA); mouse anti-GFP antibody (1:1000, Beyotime, Cat.# AG281, Shanghai, China); mouse/rabbit anti-HA antibody (1:5000, Abmart, Cat.# M20003 / R1201, Shanghai, China); mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (1:5000, Abmart, Cat.# M20006, Shanghai, China); rabbit anti-Phospho-(Ser/Thr) PKA Substrate monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat.# 9624, Danvers, MA, USA). Secondary antibodies used in this study were as follows: HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, Cat.# SA00001-1, Wuhan, China) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, Cat.# SA00001-2, Wuhan, China); Alexa Fluor™ 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) fluorescent secondary antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat.# A11011, Waltham, MA, USA).

Reagents used in this study were as follows: Cycloheximide (CHX, protein synthesis inhibitor), MG132 (proteasome inhibitor), Chloroquine (CQ, autophagy-lysosome inhibitor) and Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, v-ATPase inhibitor, MedChemExpress, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Plasmids and bacterial strains

The plasmid vectors pCAGGS, pEGFP-N1 and pGEX-4T-1 were preserved in our laboratory.The prokaryotic expression plasmid pGEX-4T-1-UL3.5, eukaryotic expression plasmids including pEGFP-N1-UL3.5, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-64A, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-84A, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-6-8A, pCAGGS-US3-HA, pCAGGS-US3-K126A-HA and pCAGGS-US3-D211A-HA, as well as BAC infectious clone recombinant plasmids DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5 and DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A were constructed in this study.

Escherichia coli (E. coli) DH5α, BL21(DE3) competent cells, and GS1783 strain carrying the DPV-BAC infectious clone plasmid were preserved in our laboratory, and the strains used in this study were screened from the above preserved strains.

Viruses and virus titration

The parental virus used in this study was the DPV-BAC recombinant virus, which was constructed based on the virulent duck plague virus Chinese strain DEV CHv (GenBank: JQ647509.1) and contains the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) selective marker gene. The recombinant viruses DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5 and DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A were constructed and rescued in this study used the platform as previously report (Wu et al., 2016). For virus titration, DEFs were seeded in 96-well plates and infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of the virus. After 5-7 days, the viral titer was calculated as the 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID₅₀) according to the Reed-Muench method.The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was calculated based on the viral titer (TCID₅₀) and the number of adherent cells. The formula was defined as: MOI = Total viral particles / Number of host cells. Before viral infection, cell density was counted under a microscope to calculate the required volume of virus working solution for corresponding MOI treatments.

DNA constructs and cloning

The expression constructs for pGEX-4T-1-UL3.5, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-64A, pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-84A, and pEGFP-N1-UL3.5-6-8A were generated by amplifying the corresponding cDNA via PCR and cloning into the respective vectors. The BAC constructs DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5 and DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A were generated using a two-step Red-mediated recombination system in GS1783 E. coli containing the DPV CHv genome. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.

Immunoblotting

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with SDS lysis buffer or IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.6) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing five times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies included mouse anti-NanoLuc (1:1000, Promega), mouse anti-GFP (1:1000, Beyotime), mouse/rabbit anti-HA (1:5000, Abmart), mouse anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Abmart), rabbit anti-Phospho-(Ser/Thr) PKA Substrate (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were harvested and lysed with IP lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail for 30 min on ice. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatants were collected. A portion of the lysate was retained as the input control. The remaining lysate was incubated with Anti-GFP magnetic beads (Beyotime) or Anti-HA magnetic beads overnight at 4°C with rotation. The beads were washed three times with PBS containing 1% Triton X-100, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling with 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min. The samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

GST pull-down assay

The GST and GST-UL3.5 recombinant proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells and purified using glutathione agarose resin (GSH) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For the pull-down assay, purified GST or GST-UL3.5 proteins were incubated with glutathione agarose beads for 4 h at 4°C. After washing, the beads were incubated with cell lysates containing US3-HA overnight at 4°C. The beads were washed three times with PBS containing 1% Triton X-100, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling with 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer. The samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with anti-HA antibody.

Cycloheximide chase assay

To examine the effect of US3 on the stability of UL3.5 protein, DEFs were transfected with pEGFP-N1-UL3.5 or its mutant constructs together with pCAGGS-US3-HA or the empty vector. At 36 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 200 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX, MCE) for 0, 2, 4, and 8 h before harvesting. Cell lysates were analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies, and the protein half-life was determined by quantifying band intensities using ImageJ software.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

DEFs were seeded on coverslips in 12-well plates and transfected with the indicated constructs. At 36 h post-transfection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 45 min. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-HA, 1:500) for 1.5 h at 37°C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor™ 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher) for 45 min in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides and examined using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon H550L)(Khattar et al., 1995).

NanoLuc luciferase reporter assay

DEFs were seeded in white opaque 96-well plates and infected with DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5 or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A at an MOI of 0.1. At 24 h post-infection, the NanoLuc luciferase activity was measured using the Nano-Light Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Melunbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were lysed with the provided lysis buffer. The substrate was added, and luminescence was measured using a GloMax luminometer (Promega). Three independent replicates were performed for each sample.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Trizol reagent and Magen viral DNA extraction kit respectively. Qualified RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA. The TaqMan MGB probe-based qPCR system with a total reaction volume of 10 μL was used for absolute quantification of DPV genomes targeting the UL30 gene. The sequences of forward primer, reverse primer and TaqMan probe were TTTCCTCCTCCTCGCTGAGTG,GTGAGTCGTCATCTAGAACG and FAM-CCCTGGGTACAAGCG-MGB, respectively. The amplification protocol was initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 55°C for 30 s. Viral copy numbers were calculated according to the standard curve. All samples were analyzed with three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

Rescue and identification of BAC recombinant viruses

Positive BAC recombinant plasmids were extracted using the NucleoBond Xtra Midi Kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL) and transfected into DEFs using Hieff Trans™ Liposomal Transfection Reagent. After 6-8 h, the transfection medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were cultured until green fluorescent plaques appeared, and the supernatant was collected to infect fresh DEFs. This process was repeated for five passages to rescue the recombinant viruses. Viral DNA was extracted from the rescued viruses and identified by PCR amplification followed by DNA sequencing.

Effect of DPV UL3.5 phosphorylation on virus attachment

DEFs were seeded in 12-well cell culture plates and cultured at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 12–16 h. The plates were then pre-cooled at 4°C for 1 h. Cells were inoculated with 0.1 MOI of DPV-BAC or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. After incubation, cells were washed three times with pre-cooled sterile PBS, collected, and viral DNA was extracted for qPCR detection of viral genome copy number. Three biological replicates were performed for each group. The effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation on DPV attachment was analyzed.

Effect of DPV UL3.5 phosphorylation on virus entry

DEFs were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured as above. After pre-cooling at 4°C for 1 h, cells were inoculated with 0.1 MOI of DPV-BAC or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. Cells were then washed three times with ice-cold PBS, overlaid with maintenance medium containing 2% FBS, and shifted to 37°C for 3 h. After three additional washes with PBS, cells were collected. Viral DNA was extracted and quantified by qPCR to analyze the effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation on DPV entry.

Effect of DPV UL3.5 phosphorylation on viral genome replication

DEFs were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured as above. Cells were inoculated with 0.1 MOI of DPV-BAC or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, incubated at 37°C for 6 h, washed three times with PBS, and then maintained in medium with 2% FBS. Cells were collected at 7, 8, 9, and 10 h post-infection. Viral DNA was extracted and analyzed by qPCR to assess the effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation on DPV genome replication.

Effect of DPV UL3.5 phosphorylation on virus release

DEFs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured as above. Cells were inoculated with 1 MOI of DPV-BAC or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, incubated at 37°C for 18 h, washed three times with PBS, and then overlaid with maintenance medium containing 2% FBS. Culture supernatants were collected at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after medium replacement, and TCID₅₀ titers were determined to analyze the effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation on DPV virion release.

Effect of DPV UL3.5 phosphorylation on cell-to-cell spread

DEFs were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured as above. Cells were inoculated with 0.005 MOI of DPV-BAC or DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, incubated at 37°C for 2 h, washed three times with PBS, and overlaid with a mixture of 2 × DMEM containing 2% FBS and 2% agarose. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 h. Fluorescent plaques were observed and counted under a fluorescence microscope. Thirty plaques were randomly selected, and plaque areas were measured using ImageJ software. The mean plaque area of the DPV-BAC group was set as 100% for comparison with DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, and the effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation on cell-to-cell spread was analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Interaction between DPV UL3.5 and US3 protein kinase

To investigate the relationship between UL3.5 and US3, a GST pull-down assay was performed. The results showed that GST-UL3.5 specifically bound to US3-HA, whereas no binding was observed with the GST control (Fig. 1A). Indirect immunofluorescence assay revealed that UL3.5 and US3 co-localized in the cytoplasm of DEFs (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that DPV UL3.5 interacts with the US3 protein kinase.

US3 protein kinase directly phosphorylates UL3.5

Given the kinase activity of US3, we examined whether it could phosphorylate UL3.5. DEFs were co-transfected with pEGFP-N1-UL3.5 and pCAGGS-US3-HA, and UL3.5 was immunoprecipitated using anti-GFP antibody. The results showed that a phosphorylation signal for UL3.5 was detected by the p-PKA substrate antibody only in samples co-expressing US3 (Fig. 2A), indicating that US3 phosphorylates UL3.5.The use of the phospho-(Ser/Thr) PKA substrate antibody to detect US3-mediated phosphorylation is based on the well-established finding that the substrate specificity of the US3 kinase is highly similar to that of the cellular cAMP-dependent protein kinase PKA, with both recognizing an arginine-rich motif preceding the phosphorylation site. This approach has been previously employed to identify US3 substrates(Benetti and Roizman, 2004). In the present study, the specificity of the detected signal was further verified through site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted US3 phosphorylation sites in UL3.5 (see below). HSV-1 US3 has a specific amino acid sequence recognition preference for substrates. Its core recognition motif is highly conserved with that of the US3 homologue of Pseudorabies Virus (PRV), which is RNX(S/T)YY. The characteristics of each amino acid are as follows: N represents ≥2 arbitrary amino acids, X can be arginine (Arg), alanine (Ala), valine (Val), proline (Pro) or serine (Ser), Y represents any amino acid except acidic amino acids, and the core phosphorylation site is serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr). Therefore, we analyzed the amino acid sequence of UL3.5 and identified two typical motifs that may be recognized and phosphorylated by US3: 59-66aa(RRRRGSEP),81-86aa(RRRSGF).

To further identify the phosphorylation sites, we mutated the predicted phosphorylation sites Ser64 and Ser84 to alanine. The results showed that compared with wild-type, single mutations (S64A or S84A) partially reduced the phosphorylation level, while the double mutation (S64A/S84A, designated 6-8A) completely abolished the phosphorylation signal (Fig. 2B,C). Additionally, the kinase-dead mutant D211A failed to phosphorylate UL3.5, whereas the K126A mutant retained phosphorylation activity (Fig. 2D), and these mutations did not affect the interaction between US3 and UL3.5 (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate that US3 directly phosphorylates UL3.5 at Ser64 and Ser84 through its kinase activity.

US3 phosphorylation enhances UL3.5 stability

To investigate the effect of phosphorylation on UL3.5 protein expression, we overexpressed US3 in HEK293T and DEFs. US3 upregulated UL3.5 protein in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays demonstrated that co-expression of US3 significantly prolonged the half-life of UL3.5 (Fig. 3B, C). Western blot analysis showed that US3 significantly increased the expression level of wild-type UL3.5, but had no significant effect on the expression of the phosphorylation-deficient mutant UL3.5-6-8A (Fig. 3D, E). These results indicate that US3-mediated phosphorylation enhances the stability of UL3.5 and inhibits its degradation.

Construction of DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A phosphorylation site mutant recombinant virus and evaluation of its effect on viral replication

To validate the role of UL3.5 phosphorylation in viral replication, we constructed a recombinant virus with inactivated UL3.5 phosphorylation sites, designated DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, using the DPV-BAC system(Wu et al., 2016). In this virus, both Ser64 and Ser84 of UL3.5 were mutated to alanine (S64A/S84A), preventing phosphorylation by US3. Additionally, as no antibody specific to UL3.5 is currently available, we engineered the viral genome to express UL3.5 fused with the NanoLuc reporter. This fusion allows convenient and dynamic monitoring of UL3.5 expression levels (Fig. 4A). After successful rescue of the recombinant virus (Fig. 4B) , we evaluated its in vitro replication capacity by growth curve analysis. The results showed that compared with the control recombinant virus DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5, the titers of the DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A mutant virus were significantly reduced at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h post-infection (Fig. 4C, D). Western blot analysis showed that NanoLuc-UL3.5 protein expression levels were significantly lower in cells infected with the mutant virus compared with the control virus (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, NanoLuc luciferase activity was also significantly reduced in cells infected with the mutant virus (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that inactivation of UL3.5 phosphorylation sites leads to reduced protein expression and severely impairs viral replication, confirming the critical role of US3-mediated UL3.5 phosphorylation in efficient DPV replication.

Effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation sites on the viral life cycle

Previous experiments have demonstrated that US3 phosphorylates UL3.5 at Ser64 and Ser84 to enhance its protein stability. To identify the specific step(s) of the viral life cycle regulated by this phosphorylation, we systematically examined four key stages: attachment, entry, genome replication, and progeny virus release. The results showed that inactivation of the UL3.5 phosphorylation sites had no significant effect on virus attachment (Fig. 5A) or entry (Fig. 5B), indicating that the US3-UL3.5 phosphorylation axis is not involved in the viral entry process, which is consistent with the functional localization of UL3.5 as a tegument protein. However, the mutant virus exhibited a moderate reduction in genome replication efficiency (Fig. 5C) and a substantial decrease in progeny virus release (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that US3-mediated phosphorylation of UL3.5, by maintaining its protein stability, plays a safeguarding role in both genome replication and virus release. Once the phosphorylation signal is lost, accelerated degradation and functional insufficiency of UL3.5 directly leads to a defect in single-cycle virus replication, providing a mechanistic explanation for the marked reduction in the growth curve of the mutant virus.

Effect of UL3.5 phosphorylation sites on cell-to-cell spread

Cell-to-cell spread is a key strategy employed by alphaherpesviruses to evade humoral immunity and achieve rapid amplification. We used an agarose overlay plaque assay to further investigate whether the US3-UL3.5 phosphorylation axis regulates this process. Direct microscopic observation of viral fluorescent plaques revealed that the plaques formed by the phosphorylation site mutant virus were visibly smaller than those of the wild-type virus (Fig. 6A). We randomly selected 30 viral plaques produced by each virus and performed statistical analysis of plaque area. The results showed that the plaque area of the mutant virus was significantly reduced compared with that of the wild-type virus (Fig. 6B, C), and infection was largely confined to the initially infected cells, failing to spread efficiently to surrounding cells. This demonstrates that US3-mediated phosphorylation of UL3.5 not only supports replication and release within single cells but also maintains the ability of the virus to spread cell-to-cell. Together with the preceding results, we can outline a multi-level regulatory model of the US3-UL3.5 phosphorylation axis: US3 phosphorylates UL3.5 at Ser64/Ser84 to enhance its protein stability, which in turn ensures genome replication and progeny release at the single-cell level and sustains cell-to-cell spread at the multicellular level. When phosphorylation is abolished, both levels are impaired, and the superimposed dual defects ultimately lead to a comprehensive decline in the overall replication capacity of the mutant virus.

Discussion

This study elucidates UL3.5 as a novel phosphorylation substrate of US3, whereby the US3 protein kinase of Duck Plague Virus (DPV) enhances the stability of the viral tegument protein UL3.5 through phosphorylation, thereby facilitating viral replication. We have demonstrated that US3 interacts with and co-localizes with UL3.5 in host cells, and that US3 directly phosphorylates UL3.5 at serine residues 64 (Ser64) and 84 (Ser84). This phosphorylation event markedly augments the stability of the UL3.5 protein. More importantly, by constructing the phosphorylation site-mutated recombinant virus DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, we have directly established the critical role of UL3.5 phosphorylation in DPV replication. The inability of this mutant virus to be phosphorylated by US3 led to a significant reduction in UL3.5 protein expression levels and viral titers, corroborating that the US3-UL3.5 axis serves as a crucial regulatory node in the process of DPV replication.

The herpesvirus US3 kinase is a multifunctional regulator with a variety of viral and host substrates(Hossain et al., 2025; Mori, 2012). US3 kinase family members are highly conserved within the Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily and exhibit similar substrate recognition patterns (Kato and Kawaguchi, 2018; Wang et al., 2025). For example, HSV-1 US3 phosphorylates UL31 and UL34 to regulate nuclear egress, phosphorylates UL47 to modulate its subcellular localization, and phosphorylates glycoprotein B to regulate its surface expression (Liao et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2014); PRV US3 phosphorylates the Bad protein to inhibit apoptosis (Deruelle et al., 2007). In recent years, our research group has identified that DPV US3 phosphorylates UL47 and VP16(Deng et al., 2022). This study identifies UL3.5 as a novel substrate of DPV US3, expanding our understanding of the US3 regulatory network. Similar to HSV-1 and PRV, DPV US3 promotes viral replication by stabilizing its substrate proteins. Notably, homologs of UL3.5 in PRV and BoHV-1 are involved in virion release and maturation (Cui et al., 2024; Fuchs et al., 2007), suggesting that different herpesviruses may have evolved unique US3 substrates to adapt to their specific hosts and replication strategies.

Interestingly, DPV infection rapidly disseminates to multiple organs in the host, whereas HSV-1 infection tends to be restricted to specific sites such as the eye, oral mucosa, genital area, and brain(Liu et al., 2004) (Poccardi et al., 2019). Our study identifies UL3.5 as a novel direct substrate of DPV US3 for the first time, with phosphorylation mainly occurring at Ser64 and Ser84. The full mechanism by which the US3-UL3.5 axis drives viral systemic dissemination remains unclear, and the following discussion is based on available research evidence. US3 regulates the nuclear egress complex and nuclear lamina Bahnamiri and Roller (2021), so phosphorylated UL3.5 translocates to the nuclear envelope, stabilizes related structures and facilitates lamina disassembly to enhance nucleocapsid egress. UL3.5 is involved in viral secondary envelopment, progeny release and cell-to-cell spread, and its phosphorylated form in the cytoplasm acts as an adaptor to support long-distance intracellular transport. DPV US3 disrupts epithelial barriers(Deng et al., 2023), and the virus spreads throughout the body via blood and lymph circulation, indicating this axis enables cross-organ dissemination. Besides, US3 inhibits the RIG-I-STING-IRF3 pathway and UL3.5 suppresses cGAS activity. Their combined action weakens type I interferon responses(Tian et al., 2025), allowing DPV to evade systemic immunity and sustain viremia. Given the above findings, the absence of UL3.5 homolog in HSV-1 may account for its localized infection, which is merely a reasonable inference.Notably, our present work also reveals that UL3.5 phosphorylation modulates DPV genome replication, an unexpected function because UL3.5 is conventionally recognized as a tegument protein that mainly acts during the late stages of the viral life cycle. Two potential mechanisms are proposed to explain this phenomenon. On one hand, phosphorylated UL3.5 may directly interact with core components of the viral replication machinery to regulate genome replication. On the other hand, partial degradation of UL3.5 during infection may produce truncated fragments that interfere with normal viral genome replication. Further biochemical experiments will be conducted to verify these hypotheses in follow-up studies. In addition, it remains to be determined whether the phosphorylation sites of UL3.5 (Ser64 and Ser84) are conserved in other alphaherpesviruses such as BoHV-1 and PRV. Given that the UL3.5 homologs in these viruses also function in virion maturation, and that US3 kinases in PRV and BoHV-1 are known to phosphorylate multiple viral substrates, it is plausible that phosphorylation by their respective US3 kinases plays a similarly conserved role in regulating protein stability and viral replication. Comparative studies across these viruses would provide valuable insights into the evolution of US3 substrate specificity and its contribution to viral pathogenesis.

The DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A recombinant virus constructed in this study represents a valuable research tool. Bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) technology provides an efficient platform for herpesvirus reverse genetics(Tischer and Kaufer, 2012) . Using this technology, we successfully mutated both Ser64 and Ser84 of UL3.5 to alanine and fused the NanoLuc reporter gene, allowing real-time, sensitive monitoring of UL3.5 expression levels. Using this virus, we confirmed that loss of UL3.5 phosphorylation leads to reduced expression and severely impairs viral replication, providing an ideal model for further investigation of UL3.5 function and for screening antiviral drugs targeting the US3-UL3.5 interaction.

Cross-talk between phosphorylation and ubiquitination is common in protein regulation, and the "phosphorylation blocks ubiquitination" paradigm is frequently observed in virus-host interactions. HSV-1 US3 can alter the interaction of substrate proteins with E3 ubiquitin ligases through phosphorylation (van Gent et al., 2022). Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that phosphorylation of the ZIKV prM protein by the host PIM1 kinase blocks its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase AMFR, inhibiting ubiquitin-proteasome degradation (Ren et al., 2024). Although this study did not investigate the ubiquitination degradation mechanism of UL3.5 in depth, combined with the above reports, we speculate that US3-mediated phosphorylation may similarly inhibit UL3.5 degradation through a comparable mechanism, representing an important direction for future research.

Finally, the replication defect of the UL3.5 phosphorylation site mutant virus further confirms the US3-UL3.5 axis as a critical regulatory node in the DPV replication cycle. US3 kinase not only regulates viral replication in DPV infection but also antagonizes the host innate immune response by phosphorylating IRF7(Tian, et al, 2025) (Liu et al., 2022), indicating that US3 plays multiple roles in DPV pathogenesis. This suggests that US3 kinase or its interaction interface with UL3.5 could serve as potential targets for anti-DPV drug development. Future research will focus on screening specific US3 kinase inhibitors and evaluating their anti-DPV efficacy using tools such as the DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A virus.

In conclusion, this study clearly identifies UL3.5 as a novel phosphorylation substrate of Duck Plague Virus (DPV) US3 kinase, demonstrating that US3 interacts with and co-localizes with UL3.5 in host cells, directly phosphorylating it at Ser64 and Ser84 to enhance UL3.5 protein stability and thereby promote DPV replication. The constructed recombinant virus DPV-BAC-NanoLuc-UL3.5-6-8A, with mutated phosphorylation sites, further validates the critical role of the US3-UL3.5 axis in DPV replication, as its replication defect confirms the necessity of UL3.5 phosphorylation. This finding expands the regulatory network of DPV US3, consistent with the multifunctional role of US3 kinases in Alphaherpesvirinae while revealing species-specific differences, such as the absence of UL3.5 in HSV-1 possibly linking to distinct viral dissemination patterns. The recombinant virus also serves as a valuable tool for monitoring UL3.5 expression and screening antiviral drugs. Additionally, we speculate US3-mediated phosphorylation may inhibit UL3.5 ubiquitin-proteasome degradation, and US3 could be a potential anti-DPV drug target. Future research should clarify UL3.5-mediated DPV dissemination mechanisms, verify phosphorylation site conservation, explore phosphorylation-ubiquitination links, and develop US3 inhibitors.
