Gender inequalities of caregiver mental health and burden in the three years post COVID-19 (CUIDAR-SE Study)
Authors: María del Río-Lozano, Diana Juanita Mora, Gracia Maroto-Navarro, Ricardo Ocaña-Riola, María del Mar García-Calvente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has significantly impacted progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (ensuring healthy lives and well-being) and SDG 5 (achieving gender equality), especially for caregivers. Increased demand for informal caregiving has disproportionately impacted caregivers, worsening mental health challenges. Intense burden reinforces traditional gender norms and exacerbates health inequities, linking it closely to both SDGs. This study investigates the relationship between intense caregiving burden and mental health among informal caregivers in Spain, emphasizing gender differences and the mitigating role of perceived social support.MethodsThis three-time repeated-measures analysis, part of the larger longitudinal CUIDAR-SE study, was conducted in two regions of Spain, Granada (Andalusia) and Gipuzkoa (Basque Country). 261 caregivers (165 female and 96 male) were enrolled in 2021 and followed through 2022 and 2023. Mental health was assessed using the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12), with independent variables including age, sex, and caregiving factors (intense burden, social support). Descriptive statistics characterized the caregiving situation by sex, followed by logistic regression to explore associations between GHQ-12 scores and burden, adjusted for age and social support, stratified by sex.ResultsResults showed that male caregivers, typically older and caring for spouses, received less informal support and more external assistance than younger female caregivers, whose care was more varied. A positive association was found between caregiving burden and mental health issues: odds ratio (OR) 7.1 (95% CI: 3.7–14.3) in 2021, OR 6.2 (95% CI: 2.8–14.6) in 2022, and OR 4.6 (95% CI: 1.9–12.0) in 2023, adjusting for age and social support. This association was stronger among male caregivers, particularly in 2021. Social support showed a protective effect against mental health problems for men in 2022 and both men and women in 2023.ConclusionsThis study underscores the mental health impact of caregiving during the pandemic, revealing gender inequities in caregiving tasks, burden, and mental health distress. The findings emphasize the need for gender-sensitive policies, aligned with the SDGs, to support caregivers’ well-being. Strengthening formal care services and promoting shared caregiving responsibilities are essential to reducing caregiver burden and improving mental health outcomes.Keywords: Informal caregiving, Mental health, COVID-19 pandemic, Gender differences, Caregiver burden

Background

The 2019 coronavirus pandemic (COVID-19), caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has been the most significant public health crisis in a century [1], placing a critical strain on care systems, increasing reliance on informal caregivers [2–5]. Informal care refers to caregiving activities carried out by individuals within the immediate environment of the dependent person, such as family members, to meet the dependent person’s physical, mental, and emotional needs, typically without remuneration [6, 7]. In Spain, with nearly 14 million cases and 120,000 deaths [1], the collapse of long-term care services intensified caregiving responsibilities, exposing caregivers—often family members—to heightened mental and physical stress [8, 9]. High levels of caregiving burden (intense burden) has been strongly associated with psychological distress [10]. This has raised concern about the mental health impacts of intense caregiving burden, especially with the reported unmet mental health needs of adults during this health crisis [11].

Across Europe and Latin America, informal caregiving is a widespread phenomenon. In the EU, 12–18% of adults aged 18–75 provide care daily or weekly [4], with certain European countries having greater support by policies that acknowledge caregiver rights and long-term care integration [12]. Latin American countries, like Argentina, Colombia, Guatemala, and Uruguay report a broader caregiving prevalence (4–22%), with men contributing the least time [13]. In Spain, informal caregivers accounted for 12.4% of the working-age population aged 18–64 in 2018 [12], with the COVID-19 pandemic leading many individuals to transition into informal caregiving roles [14]. Within the European context, Spain occupies a unique position—following similar cultural aspects of Latin American familism caregiving—but being institutionally embedded within the EU [12, 15]. In addition, caregiving patterns are deeply gendered: women account for 76% of daily caregivers in Spain [4] and 61% across 25 OECD countries [4], often assuming roles shaped by entrenched norms around domestic work, emotional labour, and kinship obligations [16–18]. Gendered caregiving not only reinforces traditional gender norms but also leads to differential health outcomes, with women reporting higher levels of stress, depression, and burnout [19–25]. The pandemic added quarantine-related stressors—such as isolation, fear, and financial strain—that heightened psychological distress, especially among informal caregivers [26, 27].

The unequal distribution of caregiving responsibilities has direct implications for the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality) [28, 29]. For example, indicator 5.4 calls for recognising and valuing unpaid care work through public services, infrastructure, and social protection policies [30]. The intensification of caregiving roles during COVID-19 highlighted systemic gaps in health protection for caregivers and revealed how gender norms exacerbate health inequities. Addressing caregiver mental health and promoting equitable care arrangements are essential for advancing both health equity and gender justice under the global SDG agenda.

This study draws on the Stress Process Model to examine caregiver distress. This model delineates how stressors emerge from the caregiving context, how they affect outcomes, and how certain factors may buffer their impact [31]. Within this framework, intense caregiving burden is conceptualised as a subjective primary stressor, stemming from the caregiving context and influencing mental health outcomes [31]. Despite its relevance, the role of burden in explaining mental health distress among caregivers remains underexplored. Importantly, not all caregivers respond similarly to comparable stressors [31]. Social support, previously defined as “the social resources that persons perceive to be available or that are provided to them by non-professionals in the context of both formal support groups and informal helping relationships” [32], has been identified as a buffer to the negative impacts of caregiving [33]. However, the buffering effect of social support is inconsistent in current research, and could be due to its conceptual diversity and the varying instruments used to measure social support [34, 35].

This study addresses a critical gap by examining the intersection of caregiving burden and mental health, guided by SDG 3 and 5. While much existing research focuses on caregivers of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease, broader investigations are needed to include caregivers supporting individuals with diverse conditions. There is also limited understanding of gender-based differences in mental health distress among high-burden caregivers, as well as how perceived social support may influence these experiences differently for men and women. We hypothesise that informal caregivers experiencing intense burden during the COVID-19 pandemic are more likely to report poor mental health outcomes. This study aims to examine the impact of intense burden on the mental health of informal caregivers in Spain between 2021 and 2023, with a focus on gender differences and the moderating role of perceived social support. Specifically, the study will quantify the association between intense burden and mental health, compare mental health outcomes between men and women with intense burden, and evaluate whether perceived social support moderates these relationships differently by gender.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a repeated-measures analysis at three distinct time points—2021, 2022, and 2023—nested within the longitudinal CUIDAR-SE study [36]. This multicentric cohort study is designed to examine the effects of caregiving on health among informal caregivers in two geographically diverse regions of Spain: Granada, Andalusia, and Gipuzkoa, Basque Country. The CUIDAR-SE study has been ongoing since 2013 until 2024, with the data collection occurring every 12 months. Therefore, the design reflects repeated observations on the same individuals, rather than independent cross-sectional snapshots. This allows for assessment of caregivers’ experiences at three different points, including during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The data for this study were collected using a questionnaire adapted from the “Healthcare in the Home: Care and Caregivers in Andalusia” study conducted by García Calvente in 1999 [37]. This questionnaire has proven effective in identifying the health impacts of caregiving tasks. Additionally, this questionnaire includes the 12-item version of the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) to assess the mental health of caregivers. The GHQ-12 is a validated and reliable tool widely used to measure psychological well-being and mental health symptoms of caregivers [38].

Participants

The study population included all individuals aged 18 years and older either cohabitating or not in family households in the provinces of Granada and Gipuzkoa who provide unpaid care to a dependent person. These participants were identified through caregiver registries in the health districts of Granada or the provincial council of Gipuzkoa at the beginning of the study. In 2021, the study included 261 caregivers (165 women and 96 men), the majority of whom have been part of the study since 2013 and were followed through 2022 and 2023, with subsequent attrition (Table 1). Only those who participated in the study at two time points were included. Reasons for exits included death of either caregiver or care recipient, admission of the care recipient into a nursing home or hospital and/or discontinuation of caregiving for another reason.

Variables

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable was the mental health status of caregivers, as measured by the GHQ-12 [39]. Although not a diagnostic tool, the GHQ-12 assesses symptoms indicative of anxiety and depression, social dysfunction, and loss of confidence, considered more appropriate for measuring the mental health of informal caregivers than instruments designed to detect only depression [40]. For this study, the GHQ-12 symptom scale (range 0–12) was dichotomized as follows, following the GHQ-12 manual: respondents reporting more than six symptoms were classified as “with mental health problems”, while those with six or fewer symptoms were classified as “without mental health problems” [41].

Exposure variables

Exposure variables were categorised into four groups related to the study’s outcome measure and were used for descriptive, explanatory, and adjustment purposes: characteristics of the informal caregiver; which included sex, age, province of residence, place of residence, educational level, employment status, perceived health status, and relationship with the care recipient; characteristics of the care recipient, including sex, age, degree of dependency, and cognitive impairment, the caregiving situation; which was assessed based on the type of tasks provided, the intensity of care measured in daily hours, and the degree of burden experienced by the caregiver; and the caregiver’s perception of the support received (Table 2). The degree of burden is categorized into No Burden and Light Burden as Light Burden, and Intense Burden, where the final category is also denoted as burden.

Sex was classified as male or female, assuming a cisgender population. Throughout the manuscript, we use the terms “male” and “female” as well as “men” and “women” interchangeably. This reflects the fact that sex was the variable collected in the dataset, while also recognizing that caregiving is a socially constructed and gendered experience. The interchangeable use of these terms allows us to acknowledge both the biological classification used in data collection and the sociocultural context that shapes caregiving roles.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis comprised descriptive and associative analyses between dependent and independent variables using bivariate and multivariate logistic regression models. A gender perspective was also included in the analysis. All data analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.3.1 [44].

Missing data

Missing data were assessed for patterns and nature. The analysis revealed a random pattern of missing data, with most variables showing less than 1% absence, except for perceived degree of dependency (6%) and daily hours of care (18%). Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied to address the missing data, utilizing predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables. Sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, and education were included in the imputation process to ensure the retention of informative values.

Descriptive analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed of the characteristics of the caregiver, the care recipient, and the caregiving situation by sex for each year of the study (Tables 3 and 4). Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as relative frequencies.

Bivariate and multivariate regression analysis

A bivariate analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between the GHQ-12 mental health outcome and each exposure measure. Logistic regression models were then used to examine associations between GHQ-12 scores and burden, adjusting for age and social support, and stratified by sex for each study year. The strength of these associations were estimated using odds ratios (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). A combined model of both female and male caregivers was also constructed. In this context, statistical significance is not extensively discussed due to limitations in sample size. The focus remains on presenting and analysing the distribution of findings by gender.

Results

Descriptive analysis of caregiver and care recipient characteristics

Male caregivers in the study were generally older, with median ages ranging between 65 and 67 years across the three years, and most resided in semi-urban areas, held secondary-level education, and were not engaged in paid employment. Their caregiving responsibilities primarily focused on providing extensive care to their greatly dependent spouses. Similarly, female caregivers shared comparable residence, education level, and employment status characteristics. However, female caregivers were slightly younger, with median ages ranging between 58 and 59 years old across the three years, and their caregiving duties were more evenly distributed among children, parents, and spouses. Overall, the majority of both male and female caregivers cared for care recipients with great dependency and no cognitive deterioration (Table 3).

Assessments using the GHQ-12 consistently showed that women were more likely to experience mental health issues compared to men. Specifically, the prevalence of mental health issues was 50% among females versus 46% among males in 2021; 47% versus 37% in 2022; and 58% versus 43% in 2023 (Table 3). Although these trends indicate a consistent gender inequity with females being at a greater disadvantage, the differences did not reach statistical significance. However, the gender gap remained persistent and appeared to widen over the study period.

Descriptive analysis of the caregiving situation

Over the three years, female caregivers were more frequently engaged in solitary caregiving tasks, such as personal care (e.g., assistance with eating, dressing, and bathing), and household chores related to caregiving responsibilities (Table 4). Conversely, male caregivers were more inclined to handle tasks related to the physical mobility of the care recipient. Both sexes were similarly engaged in the care of illness and outdoor tasks. Despite no significant difference in the overall burden experienced between men and women, a greater proportion of women consistently reported higher levels of burden compared to men across all three years, with the most pronounced gender inequity occurring in 2021. Analysis of perceived social support stratified by sex showed no clear pattern by sex, though most caregivers reported having good social support (Table 4).

Multivariate analyses of caregiver burden and mental health risk

To better understand the odds of experiencing mental health issues based on caregiver burden, we performed multivariate analyses. These analyses included caregiver burden as the primary exposure and considered age and perceived social support, which were significant in previous bivariate analyses and relevant according to existing literature on caregiving.

In the combined model for the entire sample, intense caregiver burden significantly increased the odds of experiencing mental health issues in all three years, independent of age, social support perception, or sex (Table 5). Specifically, in 2021, the odds of mental health issues were seven times higher with intense burden compared to light burden (OR: 7.08, 95% CI: 3.71—14.3), six times higher in 2022 (OR: 6.20, 95% CI: 2.77—14.6), and five times higher in 2023, (OR: 4.57, 95% CI: 1.91—12.0). Social support was a significant protective factor against mental health problems in 2022 and 2023, while age and sex did not show significant associations with mental health issues. Nevertheless, examining separate models for men and women remains valuable.

Discussion

Gender differences in caregiving context

Caregiving responsibilities were unequally distributed between men and women even before the pandemic [45]. Our study highlighted distinct differences in the caregiving roles between men and women, particularly regarding the relationship with the care recipient and the nature of the caregiving tasks. Men predominantly provided care for their spouses, while women assumed caregiving duties for a wider range of family members, including children, parents, and spouses. This suggests that women are more likely to become the primary caregivers for multiple relatives, whereas men typically step into the caregiving role primarily when their spouse becomes dependent. Although most care recipients of both men and women caregivers presented high levels of dependency and relatively no cognitive deterioration, the broader distribution of care recipients among women may increase the emotional complexity and psychological demands of caregiving. This variation in caregiving profiles likely influences the burden of care and mental health outcomes, potentially contributing to the gender differences observed.

In terms of the nature of caregiving tasks, women consistently reported assuming responsibility for personal care and caregiving-related household tasks alone more frequently than men in each of the three years. This aligns with traditional gender roles and the common stereotyping of care, that associate women with domestic and nurturing roles [16–18]. In contrast, men were more likely to report performing physical mobility tasks alone, which may reflect gendered expectations or physical capacity. For other caregiving tasks, such as household supervision, illness care, outdoor tasks related to caregiving, and the total daily hours of care provided, gender differences were minimal. Overall, the identified gender differences were most pronounced in 2021, likely due to the increased strain caused by the pandemic-related reduction of health and social services, which placed greater strain on caregivers. However, the larger sample size in 2021 may also have contributed to stronger observable associations in that year. The same trends might also be present in 2022 and 2023, but the smaller sample sizes in those years may have limited statistical power to detect significant differences. Importantly, we acknowledge that additional factors not fully accounted for in our analysis—the specific health conditions and more detailed dependency profiles of care recipients—may also influence caregivers’ perceived burden and mental health outcomes. These unmeasured variables could further shape the gender differences observed and merit attention in future research.

COVID-19 impact on caregiving

Our study highlights the profound impact of the caregiving burden on mental health, particularly within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic across the years 2021, 2022, and 2023. Evidence from a recent systematic review demonstrates that this period saw significant disruptions to traditional support systems, compounded by substantial intensified caregiving demands [46].

The Stress Process Model provides a valuable conceptual framework to understand how caregiver burden acts as a primary stressor, leading to adverse mental health outcomes [31]. The model emphasises the importance of the caregiving context in shaping the level of burden, especially during the pandemic, where disruptions to support systems exacerbated caregiver stress.

Our findings demonstrate that intense caregiver burden substantially increases the odds of mental health issues among caregivers of both sexes, regardless of age and perceived social support. The lack of a significant effect of sex in this association suggests that when caregiving intensity is more evenly distributed between men and women, gender differences in mental health outcomes diminish. While women typically bear a greater caregiving burden, our study found that during the pandemic, when men assumed more intensive caregiving roles, the traditionally gendered impact of caregiving burden on mental health became less pronounced. In contrast, the European Institute for Gender Equality’s 2021 survey reported that despite the increased demands of informal caregiving during the pandemic, care responsibilities predominantly remained with women. This contrast highlights the complex interplay between caregiving intensity and gendered caregiving norms.

The association between intense caregiver burden and mental health issues was most pronounced in 2021, a period closely following the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic’s unique context, marked by severe lockdowns, restricted access to essential services, and a strained healthcare system, provided a crucial backdrop for understanding caregivers’ heightened impact during this time [47, 48].

Despite the initiation of a vaccination campaign in late 2020 [49], the early stages of the pandemic were characterized by confusion, fear, and uncertainty, which exacerbated the mental health challenges faced by caregivers [8, 9]. The restructuring of health and social care systems during the pandemic further strained caregivers, as they had to adapt to changes in accessing resources and support networks [50].

The greater mental health issues observed among caregivers in 2021 reflect the compounded effects of intense caregiving demands, a healthcare system in flux, reduced social support, and the ongoing psychological toll of a global health crisis [48, 51–53]. While the association between caregiving burden and mental health issues decreased in 2022 and 2023, it remained significant, indicating that even as caregivers adapted to the “new normal”, they continued to be vulnerable to mental health problems if the intense caregiving burden persisted. Our findings emphasise the need to consider caregiving intensity in conjunction with gendered caregiving experiences, especially during crises, as they can alter the association with mental health outcomes.

Gender inequities in caregiver burden and mental health impact

The sex-disaggregated analysis revealed that intense caregiver burden had a more pronounced impact on men’s mental health, despite the higher prevalence of burden experienced by female caregivers. However, it is important to note that the small sample size of male caregivers in our study has resulted in wide confidence intervals and reduced statistical power, leading to greater variability in the estimates. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Studies have reported that while both men and women experienced increased caregiving demands due to the pandemic, men exhibited greater physical and mental health impacts [54]. This suggests that men may be more vulnerable to the mental health effects of caregiving, possibly due to their unfamiliarity with certain caregiving tasks that intensified during the pandemic that do not align with traditional male gender roles [55].

The role identity psychological theory supports this explanation, as men may experience additional psychological strain when taking on caregiving roles that are not traditionally “naturalized” for them, leading to gender role conflict [56]. Previous research suggests that female caregivers, especially those with long-term caregiving experience and increased exposure to care demands and commitment, may develop greater resilience, allowing them to better cope with the stresses of caregiving, particularly during periods of extreme stress and uncertainty [54, 57].

The previous qualitative study of the research group contextualizes these findings within the structure of gender norms and stereotypes [58]. Women internalize the caregiver role, as a result may take on high-intensity care, may feel socially pressured to perform caregiving tasks perfectly, and avoid seeking social support, which could be perceived as a sign of weakness [58]. In contrast, men, who do not traditionally take on caregiving roles, may approach caregiving more flexibly, seeking support and delegating tasks to maintain their health and quality of life [58]. Our findings support these internalized gender norms as women assumed responsibilities deemed more demanding in terms of caregiving and household management, while men take on tasks that require physical strength. The pandemic circumstances may have disrupted this flexible approach, leading to increased mental health issues among men, as they were forced to take on more intensive caregiving responsibilities.

Temporal variations in social support

Social support has long been recognized as a buffer against the negative impacts of caregiver burden, although its effectiveness can vary depending on how it is conceptualized and measured [32–35]. Our results indicate that perceived social support was an important protective factor against mental health issues for both male and female caregivers in 2022 and 2023, however the findings for male caregivers being close to the null could be due to a smaller sample size of caregivers. Social support was not protective in 2021 which suggests that the availability and effectiveness of social support evolved during the pandemic, with networks still disrupted during 2021, despite advancements in vaccination.

A qualitative study speaks to caregivers being more cautious about engaging with their social networks due to their heightened responsibility to prevent contagion, leading to prolonged periods of isolation [59]. Caregivers also did not accept external assistance, even in later periods of the pandemic, despite their increased caregiving workload, due to fear of contagion [59]. This finding underscores the critical role of having and strengthening informal and formal support systems–social and healthcare–in caregiving, especially during crises. Having a positive perception of social support could be a key intervention to alleviate caregiver burden and minimise negative mental health.

Impact of age on mental health among male caregivers

A noteworthy finding of this study is the increased odds of mental health challenges among younger male caregivers. This may be due to limited experience in caregiving and associated responsibilities, as well as potential role conflict between caregiving duties and age- or gender-related life expectations [55]. However, as men age, the lifespan perspective theory incorporates a late-life role change, and it becomes more socially accepted for men to come into a caregiving role, especially for their spouses, and this role becomes associated with feelings of self-efficacy and mastery [55]. In addition, research suggests that older caregivers report higher levels of perceived social support, which has been associated with better mental health, lower stress levels, and enhanced coping abilities [60].

However, if this shift into a caregiving role is taken on at an earlier age, younger male caregivers may experience greater mental health challenges due to gender role conflict, as they navigate the tension between their caregiving responsibilities and societal expectations of masculinity [56]. Age was not a relevant factor in the association between caregiver burden and mental health issues for female caregivers, perhaps due to the socialization of women to accept a caregiving role at any age. This highlights the need for further research into the intersection of gender, age, and other identity factors, such as socioeconomic status and cultural background, to better understand the diverse experiences of caregivers and develop inclusive support strategies.

The need for integrated caregiver support

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, Spain launched the Recovery, Transformation, and Resilience Plan, prioritising mental health through the 2022–2025 Mental Health Strategy and its operational counterpart, the 2022–2024 Mental Health Action Plan [61–63]. Aligned with the 2030 Agenda SDGs [64], these initiatives aim to strengthen mental health services nationwide. In parallel, the State Care Strategy was developed through multi-stakeholder dialogue, including various ministries and citizen input, to address post-pandemic caregiving challenges [65]. It promotes a shift toward shared responsibility between families and public institutions, recognising and supporting informal care [65].

While the 2022–2024 Mental Health Action Plan and the State Care Strategy represent crucial steps toward improving mental health care and recognising the value of informal caregivers in Spain, these efforts also align with broader global commitments under the SDGs, in particular with SDG 3 that calls for the promotion of mental health and well-being for all, and SDG 5, which emphasizes the need to recognise and redistribute unpaid care work equally. Yet, to fully realise these goals, greater coordination is needed between mental health and caregiving strategies to address the gendered nature of caregiving and the resulting inequities in mental health outcomes in the post-COVID-19 context.

In addition, the findings from our study underscore the necessity of implementing tailored mental health support programs that address the distinct challenges faced by both male and female caregivers. For men, mental strain may stem from adapting to caregiving roles that conflict with traditional gender norms. They may find themselves experiencing more mental health distress when assuming responsibilities that are unfamiliar or perceived as inconsistent with their expectations. In contrast, women may experience mental health distress related to societal pressures to be the primary caregiver and to do so without seeking social support. Support programs should encourage women to delegate caregiving tasks and seek assistance without the stigma of failure or weakness.

To advance SDG 3 and 5, integrated policies should go beyond acknowledging caregivers’ mental health needs to actively dismantle gendered expectations by promoting flexible caregiving roles and incentivising men’s participation in caregiving through workplace policies [66, 67], and ensuring targeted mental health resources for both women and men based on their caregiving experiences.

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of these national-level plans and strategies, as they underpin societal and governmental efforts to support individuals whose caregiving roles are vital to the community. Gender is a significant social determinant of mental health, especially within the context of informal caregiving, where inequities in mental health outcomes are often pronounced. As Kirkbride et al., recommend, social justice should be at the core of all public mental health interventions, investing in comprehensive approaches that support physical, mental, and social well-being across communities [68].

The intersection of SDG 3 and SDG 5 provides a framework through which we can advocate for the well-being of caregivers, ensuring their essential contributions do not compromise their own mental health or quality of life. By fostering collaboration between the Mental Health Action Plan and the State Care Strategy, and by ensuring gender-sensitive support for both men and women, we can build a more resilient caregiving system that promotes the well-being of both care recipients and caregivers.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s repeated-measure design limits our ability to draw causal inferences as this design only provides a snapshot of the relationships between variables at a single point in time. As such, it cannot establish causality or capture changes over time. Nonetheless, the original CUIDAR-SE study is longitudinal, with most participants being followed since 2013. This reflects a substantial commitment to maintaining long-term follow-up and data collection efforts.

This study’s strength lies in its focus on gender-specific analyses, which are crucial in caregiving research. By disaggregating the data by sex, we uncovered significant differences in how caregiving impacts men and women. This nuanced understanding is essential for designing targeted and effective interventions. However, the limited sample size of male caregivers each year reduces the statistical power for gender-specific analyses, leading to wider confidence intervals and potentially less precise estimates. This limitation may account for the lack of statistical significance observed in the findings for men. It is important to note that the reduced sample size stems from the fact that participants were drawn from a longitudinal follow-up study, where the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 significantly reduced the cohort, primarily due to the death of care recipients. Explaining this context helps address potential concerns about why the sample size was not increased.

The CUIDAR-SE questionnaire allowed for the careful capturing of caregiver burden with the Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale [42], perceived social support with the DUKE-UNC Social Support Index [43], and mental health problems with the GHQ-12 tool [38]. The use of these validated tools provided a comprehensive and reliable assessment of the variables under study. Although the findings may not be fully generalizable to all of Spain, the inclusion of caregivers from two regions with varying socioeconomic position and access to social care enhances the study’s relevance across different contexts, although we did not analyse geographic differences in this paper. A notable limitation is that our cohort consisted only of registered caregivers, likely representing long-term caregivers providing high-intensity care. Thus, the findings may be most applicable to similar caregiver profiles, who should be prioritised for support interventions. Further, the absence of data from a crucial year of the pandemic (2020) is a limitation, as the full scope of the pandemic’s impact on caregivers could not be captured at this time. This gap was primarily due to strict public health measures and face-to-face interaction restrictions, which made in-person data collection unfeasible. Consequently, data collection was delayed until the subsequent year, and telephone interviews were adapted to a new format to ensure participant safety and compliance with pandemic-related guidelines.

Conclusion

This study highlights the profound impact of intense burden levels during the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of informal caregivers in Spain, revealing critical gender inequities and emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. The findings demonstrate that caregiver burden exacerbates mental health risks for both male and female caregivers, although potentially for different reasons, particularly during times of crises. Addressing these inequities require a gender-sensitive approach to caregiving and mental health policies, aligned with the SDGs 3 and 5. Existing mental health and caregiving strategies must work in tandem to effectively mitigate the mental health challenges exacerbated by gender inequalities in caregiving. The complex health inequities faced by caregivers cannot be addressed in isolation, but rather through policies that promote social co-responsibility in caregiving and strengthen formal support systems, even during times of crises. Considering this, the State Care Strategy’s ultimate goal of recognising and supporting the diversity of caregiving experiences while promoting public administration’s shared responsibility is crucial. Not only to repair the consequences caused by the pandemic but also to drive a meaningful shift toward a modernized caregiving model. This model should ensure that caregivers have the right to provide and receive care in dignified conditions, with adequate support from both societal structures and governmental policies.

This study calls for sustained efforts to strengthen caregiver support well beyond the immediate post-pandemic period. It is essential to implement interventions that address the unique challenges faced by caregivers across genders and contexts. These interventions should enhance both access to and the perception of social support while prioritising mental health well-being. By promoting a culture of shared caregiving responsibilities and addressing the specific needs of male and female caregivers, we can work towards a more equitable, resilient, and supportive caregiving system for all.
