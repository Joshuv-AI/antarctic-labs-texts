Which Technique is the Optimal Option for the Treatment of Displaced Far-Lateral (Cho Type IIC) Distal Clavicle Fractures? A Comparative Analysis of Coracoclavicular Fixation Versus Anatomical Locking Plate Fixation
Authors: Chul-Hyun Cho, Eun-Seok Son, Chang-Jin Yon, Du-Han Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs achieving stable fixation in unstable far-lateral distal clavicle fractures is challenging, the optimal surgical treatment remains controversial. The aim of this study was to compare the clinical and radiological results of coracoclavicular fixation (CCF) versus anatomical locking plate fixation (ALPF) in patients with Cho type IIC fractures.MethodsThirty-eight patients who underwent surgical treatment for Cho type IIC fracture were included. Open CCF using a suture button device was performed in 25 patients and ALPF was performed in 13 patients. The mean follow-up period was 35.7 months (range, 12–132 months). Radiographic results were evaluated using serial plain radiographs. Clinical results were evaluated using the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV).ResultsNo significant differences were observed between the CCF and ALPF groups in terms of UCLA score (32.0 vs. 32.1), ASES score (90.6 vs. 91.0), and SSV (90.6% vs. 89.6%). Although significant differences between the 2 groups were observed in the mean coracoclavicular (CC) distance of the affected side at the immediate postoperative period (5.2 mm vs. 9.0 mm) and final follow-up (5.7 mm vs. 9.1 mm), the CC distances between the immediate postoperative period and final follow-up in each group were maintained without significant loss of reduction. In the CCF group, complications occurred in 4 of 25 cases (16.0%) including skin irritation caused by a superior button or knot in 2 cases, nonunion in 1 case, and shoulder stiffness in 1 case. In the ALPF group, complications occurred in 2 of 13 cases (15.4%) including screw pull-out in 1 case and shoulder stiffness in 1 case.ConclusionsBoth CCF using a suture button device and ALPF without CC augmentation for type IIC fractures showed satisfactory clinical and radiological results. Both techniques can be recommended as the optimal option that can provide sufficient vertical stability for solid union.

Although the prevalence of distal clavicle fractures (DCFs) is lower than that of shaft fractures, which comprise 10%–30% of all clavicle fractures, the treatment of certain fracture types is challenging.1234) Since displaced Neer type II fractures are associated with a high nonunion rate after conservative treatment, they may be treated operatively.25) In 2018, Cho et al.6) proposed a new classification system based on the Neer classification. This classification system addresses far-lateral fractures (type IIC) that are unclassifiable within the modified Neer classification, defined by complete detachment of both the trapezoid and conoid ligaments from the proximal fragment. Achieving stable fixation for Cho type IIC fractures is particularly difficult due to the small distal fragment.26)

Current surgical options for unstable DCFs are coracoclavicular fixation (CCF), hook plate fixation, anatomical locking plate fixation (ALPF) with or without coracoclavicular (CC) augmentation, tension band fixation, and trans-acromial intramedullary fixation.2578) However, as there is no current gold standard option, fixation technique may be decided by the surgeon’s preference based on the configuration and size of the distal fragment.

As the CC ligament is an important structure for the stability and healing of the fracture, CCF using a suture button device, tape, or autograft has been recommended for type IIC fractures.791011) Several studies have reported good to excellent clinical and radiological outcomes after CCF using a suture button device for unstable DCFs.10121314) Precontoured ALPF may be the best option in cases with a large enough distal fragment for sufficient fixation, but it may be challenging in cases with a small distal fragment.213) Recent studies have supported ALPF as a useful surgical option for the treatment of type IIB or IIC DCFs because multi-planar locking screws provide stability for the small distal fragment.1315161718)

As it is challenging to attain stable fixation for type IIC fractures, the optimal surgical treatment remains controversial. Furthermore, there have been few studies comparing these different fixation techniques for unstable far-lateral DCFs. The aim of this study was to compare the clinical and radiological results of CCF versus ALPF for Cho type IIC DCFs. This study was conducted to verify the hypothesis that ALPF without CC augmentation would provide sufficient fracture stability for Cho type IIC fractures and have comparable results to those of CCF using a suture button device.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Hospital (IRB No. 2024-09-032). Written informed consent was obtained.

Between 2010 and 2022, 45 patients with Cho type IIC (far-lateral) DCFs were treated surgically. A far-lateral fracture pattern was defined as a displaced fracture lateral to the CC ligaments with complete detachment of both the conoid and trapezoid ligaments. Surgical treatment was undertaken by 2 shoulder surgeons (CHC and DHK) in a single institution. The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: (1) Cho type IIC fracture, (2) CCF using a suture button device or ALPF without CC augmentation, and (3) follow-up period of more than 12 months after surgery. Out of 45 cases, 3 cases were excluded due to insufficient follow-up period. A further 4 patients were excluded due to the use of other fixation techniques. The remaining 38 cases were included in this study. In 25 cases, open CCF using a suture button device was performed. ALPF without CC augmentation was performed in 13 cases. The fixation technique was selected based on the patient’s fracture pattern—including the size and number of fragments—as well as age, sex, and bone quality.

The mean age at the time of index surgery was 51.8 years (range, 14–83 years). There were 23 male and 15 female patients. The injury mechanisms at the time of the initial fracture were slip-down injuries (23 cases), motor vehicle accidents (9 cases), sports injuries (4 cases), a fall from height (1 case), and blunt trauma (1 case). The mean interval from trauma to operation was 5.5 days (range, 1–18 days). The mean follow-up period was 35.7 months (range, 12–132 months).

In the CCF group, 25 cases were treated with a suture button device (Fig. 1). In the beach-chair position, a 5-6 cm longitudinal bra strap incision was made over the fracture site to the coracoid process. The fracture was reduced using temporary Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation after creating clavicular and coracoid tunnels. A Tight-Rope (Arthrex) was passed through the tunnels and fixed securely. Additional S-pin fixation (6 cases) or K-wire tension band fixation (1 case) was used depending on the configuration of the fracture.

In the ALPF group, 13 cases were treated with a precontoured distal clavicle plate (Acumed) (Fig. 2). A 6–7 cm transverse incision was made over the fracture site. After fracture reduction, the plate was fixed with proper bending. As many 2.3-mm locking screws as possible for the distal fragment were inserted. Depending on the configuration of fracture, additional cerclage wiring fixation (3 cases), miniplate fixation (1 case), or K-wire tension band fixation (2 cases) were used. The postoperative rehabilitation protocol was similar in both groups, and passive range of motion exercises were initiated 1–2 weeks after surgery.

Outcome Assessment

All patients underwent preoperative plain radiographs and computed tomography (CT) to assess displacement and the number of fracture fragments. Radiological results were evaluated using serial plain radiographs to measure CC distances and confirm bone union. CC distances were measured bilaterally between the superior border of the coracoid and the undersurface of the conoid tubercle of the distal clavicle. All radiological parameters were measured twice, and the mean value was used for analysis. Bony union was defined as a completely bridging bone in both anterosuperior and oblique views of the clavicle. Clinical results were evaluated using the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, and the Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV) at final follow-up. Intraoperative and postoperative complications were also evaluated.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp.). The Mann-Whitney U-test, the chi-square test, and the linear-by-linear test were used to determine the differences between the 2 groups regarding variables. A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) based on effect size. Statistical significance was set at p-values < 0.05.

RESULTS

With the exception of the size of fracture fragment, no significant differences between the 2 groups were observed regarding baseline demographics (Table 1). On preoperative plain radiographs and CT, the mean size of the distal fragment was 18.5 mm in the CCF group and 23.8 mm in the ALPF group, respectively; the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.001). The mean number of distal fragments at the fracture site was 1.4 in the CCF group and 1.5 in the ALPF group, respectively; the difference was not significant (p > 0.05).

Complete bony union was achieved in 37 cases (97.4%), except for 1 case in the CCF group. Union rates were 96.0% (24 / 25) in the CCF group and 100% (13 / 13) in the ALPF group (p > 0.05). At the final follow-up, mean UCLA score, ASES score, and SSV were 32.1, 90.7, and 90.3%, respectively. No significant differences between CCF and ALPF groups were observed in terms of UCLA score (32.0 vs. 32.1), ASES score (90.6 vs. 91.0), or SSV (90.6% vs. 89.6%) (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

In the CCF group, the mean CC distances between the affected and unaffected side were 5.2 mm vs. 5.9 mm at the immediate postoperative period and 5.7 mm vs. 5.9 mm at the final follow-up. In the ALPF group, the mean CC distances between the affected and unaffected side were 9.0 mm vs. 7.0 mm at the immediate postoperative period and 9.1 mm vs. 7.0 mm at the final follow-up. Although significant differences between the CCF and ALPF groups were observed in the mean CC distance of the affected side at the immediate postoperative period (5.2 mm vs. 9.0 mm; p = 0.001) and final follow-up (5.7 mm vs. 9.1 mm; p = 0.005), the CC distance between the immediate postoperative period and final follow-up (CCF: 5.2 mm vs. 5.7 mm; ALPF: 9.0 mm vs. 9.1 mm) in each group was maintained without significant loss of reduction. The mean immediate postoperative CC distance ratios were 0.8 in the CCF group and 1.3 in the ALPF group, respectively (p < 0.001); the mean final CC distance ratios were 1.2 in the CCF group and 1.3 in the ALPF group, respectively (p = 0.039) (Table 3).

In the CCF group, complications occurred in 4 of 25 cases (16.0%) including skin irritation caused by a superior button and knot in 2 cases, nonunion in 1 case, and shoulder stiffness in 1 case. In the ALPF group, complications occurred in 2 of 13 cases (15.4%) including screw pull-out in 1 case and shoulder stiffness in 1 case; no significant difference between the 2 groups was observed regarding the complication rate (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

One case with nonunion after CCF did not need reoperation due to good clinical results. In the CCF group, 9 of 25 cases (36.0%) underwent removal of the clavicular button and suture knot due to skin irritation (2 cases) or patient’s request (7 cases). In the ALPF group, 11 of 13 patients (84.6%) underwent implant removal after bony union due to either skin irritation (1 case) or patient’s request (10 cases); the difference between groups was statistically significant (p = 0.004).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to compare outcomes and complications of CCF versus ALPF for the treatment of Cho type IIC DCFs. The important finding of this study is that CCF using a suture button device as well as ALPF without CC augmentation for type IIC fractures can provide fracture stability and yield satisfactory clinical and radiological results. Several surgical options have been proposed for the treatment of type IIC DCFs, including open or arthroscopy-assisted CCF, hook plate fixation, ALPF with or without CC augmentation, and trans-acromial intramedullary fixation.257891920) Because of the challenge of achieving stable fixation, the best surgical option for type IIC DCFs remains controversial. According to a review article by Kim et al.,2) CCF and trans-articular intramedullary fixation can be recommended for treatment of type IIC DCFs because of the small distal fragment.

Considering type IIC fractures involve the complete rupture of CC ligaments from the medial fragment as well as the small distal fragment, torn CC ligaments may be restored for stability in these fractures. Recently, several studies reported on open or arthroscopy-assisted CCF using a suture button device for unstable DCFs.1012131421) Panagopoulos et al.10) reported on 7 patients with type IIC fractures treated by CCF using a suture button device. They concluded that open CCF using a suture button device is a simple and reproducible technique that produces a complete union rate and satisfactory clinical results without major complications. Ray and Nair11) reported on an excellent outcome after performing CC ligament reconstruction using semitendinosus autograft in a case with type IIC DCF. Malik et al.22) reported on a systematic review of the outcomes and complications of arthroscopy-assisted CCF for unstable DCFs. They found that bony union rates were as low as 70% and overall complication rates were as high as 28.6%. Pieringer et al.21) also reported high complication rates such as symptomatic nonunion, refracture, or stiffness after arthroscopy-assisted CC ligament reconstruction alone. Our study revealed satisfactory clinical results with a high union rate (24 of 25 cases, 96%) after open CCF using a suture button device. The CC distance between the immediate postoperative period and final follow-up (5.2 mm and 5.7 mm) was maintained without significant loss of reduction. We believe that open techniques can achieve more accurate reduction of fracture fragments, leading to better outcomes and fewer complications than arthroscopic techniques.

ALPF may not be the ideal surgical option as type IIC fractures involve a small “far-lateral” fragment. Levy et al.7) described the far-lateral fracture pattern as unstable because of the unimpeded posterior and superior pull of the trapezius on the medial fragment by the complete rupture of CC ligaments. However, ALPF is often considered as a useful surgical option for unstable DCFs with satisfactory clinical outcomes and high union rates.1315161718) Han et al.23) recommended ALPF for several reasons. First, the small distal fragment can be stabilized by fixing multi-planar locking screws. Second, precontoured anatomical distal clavicle plate has a low profile that can minimize softtissue irritation. Third, ALPF can avoid violation of the acromioclavicular joint. Shin et al.18) reported that ALPF without additional CC augmentation yielded satisfactory clinical and radiological results even with a small lateral fragment. Recently, several studies reported on outcomes after ALPF with additional CC augmentation for unstable DCFs.162324) These studies emphasized that additional CC augmentation enhances bony union by adding vertical stability. Our study revealed satisfactory clinical results with 100% union rate after ALPF without CC augmentation. The CC distance between the immediate postoperative period and final follow-up (9.0 mm vs. 9.1 mm) was maintained without significant loss of reduction.

In 2022, Panagopoulos et al.8) reported on a systematic review of the optimal surgical treatment for Neer type IIB or Cho type IIC DCFs. While similar major complication rates among techniques were reported, hook plate fixation and trans-acromial intramedullary fixation had higher rates of minor complications. Furthermore, the hook plate fixation demonstrated inferior clinical outcomes when compared to other techniques. They concluded that open CCF and ALPF without CC augmentation seem to be the best treatment options. Levy et al.7) reported on outcomes following 2 techniques (ALPF and open CCF) in the treatment of type IIC DCFs. If patients had a lateral fragment large enough for screw purchase, ALPF was performed. If not, CCF was performed. They revealed satisfactory clinical outcomes after surgery in both groups, without significant difference. In our study, with the exception of 1 case in the CCF group, complete bony union was achieved in 37 cases (97.4%). At the final follow-up, mean UCLA score, ASES score, and SSV were 32.1, 90.7, and 90.3%, respectively. No significant differences between CCF and ALPF groups were observed in terms of UCLA score (32.0 vs. 32.1), ASES score (90.6 vs. 91.0) or SSV (90.6% vs. 89.6%). Our study demonstrates that both CCF and ALPF can provide satisfactory clinical and radiological results after surgical treatment for Cho type IIC DCFs. As both surgical techniques are good enough to achieve stability and great outcomes, surgeons would make surgical plans case by case, considering the size of the fragment of the DCFs as well as age, sex, and bone quality.25)

This study has several drawbacks. First, this is a retrospective study. Second, there was no randomization with such a small sample size. Third, surgical procedures were performed by 2 surgeons and the method of fixation was not randomized and selected by consideration of the patient’s fracture pattern, including fragment size, and surgeon’s preference. As 13 patients had a distal fragment relatively large enough for screw purchase, they were treated by ALPF. As 25 patients had a distal fragment too small for screw purchase, they were treated by CCF. This could have resulted in strong selection bias. Prospective randomized multicenter studies with a large cohort are needed to verify the best option for the treatment of type IIC fractures.

Both CCF using a suture button device and ALPF without CC augmentation for type IIC fractures showed satisfactory clinical and radiological results. Both techniques can be recommended as the optimal treatment that can provide sufficient vertical stability for solid union.
