Clinical application of rapid on-site evaluation of technology for the diagnosis of respiratory system tumors: a retrospective study
Authors: Lingling Hong, Xiaoqiong Yang, Yuqing Chen, Weiwei Shao, Weikai Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the clinical application of rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) technology in the diagnosis of respiratory system tumors.MethodsA total of 175 consecutive hospitalized patients with a suspected respiratory system tumors between 2019 and 2024 were enrolled (124 from 2019 to 2022, and 20 from 2022 to 2024). Here, 144 cases underwent conventional pathological examination with ROSE assistance, while 31 patients received examination without ROSE. The concordance rate between ROSE and pathological results was compared, along with the timeliness of reports from 2019 to 2022. Using pathological examination results as the "gold standard," the difference in diagnostic positive rates between the groups with and without ROSE was compared.ResultsThe concordance rate between ROSE and pathological diagnosis was 95.83%. The median time of DQ staining in 2019–2022 was 8.0 min, and the median time of pathological results was 5645.5 min, with statistical difference between the two groups (P < 0.05). The average time of pathological report in 2022–2024 was 1446.5 min, the longest time was 2562.0 min, and the shortest time was 1118.0 min. The concordance rate with pathological examination was 95.83% for the ROSE group and 83.87% for the non-ROSE group. The difference in positive rates between the ROSE group and the non-ROSE group was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Using pathological examination as the “gold standard," the concordance rate for dual-operator ROSE was 95.97%, and for single-operator ROSE was 95.00% (P = 0.845). The average blood loss of the searchable ROSE group (16 cases) and non-ROSE group (27 cases) in 2022–2024 was 4.87 mL, 2.37 mL, and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionsROSE technology demonstrated a higher positive rate, thereby improving the pathological diagnostic yield compared to conventional pathological examination alone. This enables clinicians to obtain preliminary diagnoses earlier, guides sampling site and quantity, possesses considerable clinical value, and warrants promotion.

Introduction

Lung cancer development is multifactorial; smoking and exposure to secondhand smoke or cooking fumes are among the most common risk factors in China [1]. With the rising global incidence and mortality of lung cancer [2, 3], early detection, accurate diagnosis, and timely treatment have become crucial for improving patient prognosis and quality of life. Low-dose CT is an effective screening tool and can detect mediastinal and abdominal issues, crucial for subsequent treatment and care [4]. Traditional lung cancer diagnosis often relies on histopathological examination, a process typically taking several days. This prolongs patient waiting times and increases the risk of disease progression. Furthermore, traditional diagnostic methods face challenges with lesions difficult to sample adequately or located in complex positions. Consequently, an immediate assessment method is needed for the diagnosis of the targeted lesion and the appropriate specimen triage for the patients with lung cancer.

Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) is an immediate assessment tool, demonstrating unique advantages in lung cancer diagnosis [5]. Performing ROSE immediately upon sample acquisition significantly shortens the interval from sampling to obtaining a preliminary diagnosis [6]. ROSE not only improves sample utilization, ensuring sufficient material for further testing, but also provides real-time guidance for clinicians, helping them adjust biopsy strategies to obtain better samples [7]. While most domestic and international studies indicate that ROSE increases diagnostic positive rates and reduces complication rates [8, 9], some research suggests ROSE does not improve diagnostic yield [10], leaving its clinical value debatable. Further, there are limited studies comparing the positive rate and concordance rate of ROSE-assisted pathology in the diagnosis of respiratory system tumors. This may hinder the development of standardized ROSE protocols for optimizing the diagnosis workflows.

In this study, we aim to investigate the role of ROSE technology in lung cancer diagnosis, with a focus on evaluating its efficacy in enhancing diagnostic efficiency, accuracy, and real-time capabilities. This study demonstrates that ROSE significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy and operational efficiency considering the reduction of repeat procedures thereby catalyzing its adoption in more medical institutions to ultimately improve survival outcomes for patients with lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

A total of 175 consecutive hospitalized patients with a suspected respiratory system tumors between 2019 and 2024 were judged eligible for the study. Among the cohort, 144 cases underwent conventional pathological examination with ROSE assistance, while 31 patients received examination without ROSE. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the local ethical committee (No. 2023023).The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective observational design of the study. All information was anonymized before accessing.

Specimen acquisition

Specimens were obtained via respiratory interventional procedures (bronchoscopy, thoracoscopy, pleural biopsy, percutaneous lung puncture). Bronchoscopy is used here as an example. Bronchoscopy enables access to 4th–5th order bronchi. Prior to the procedure, imaging information (CT, etc.) was reviewed to assess the lesion location, size, and surrounding conditions. Mucosal biopsy and/or transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) were performed under bronchoscopic guidance [11, 12]. After sampling, tissue was gently lifted from the biopsy forceps using a disposable 5 mL sterile syringe needle. With minimal tissue loss, the sample was evenly rolled outwards from the center onto the staining end (approx. 1/3) of a sterile cytology slide to create a circular imprint smear (approx. 1–2 cm diameter) for ROSE. The remaining biopsy tissue was placed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin for conventional pathological examination with H&E staining. The ROSE imprint smear did not compromise the integrity of the subsequently submitted pathological material.

ROSE procedure

The obtained specimens were imprinted on clear glass slides, brushed, and spray fixed. Subsequently, the prepared slides were air dried or blow dried, followed by Diff-Quik (DQ) rapid staining. ROSE Diff-Quik (DQ) rapid staining was performed according previous reported method [13]. In short, the slides were gently dipped in DQ Solution A for 5–10 s 2–3 times for even distribution. After being rinsed in normal saline for the removal of Solution A, the slides were gently dipped in DQ Solution B for 10–20 s 2–3 times. For the removal of Solution B, the slides were rinsed in water. The slides were immediately blotted with absorbent paper. The entire staining procedure was completed within 2 min. Stained slides were immediately examined under a dedicated cytology microscope for comprehensive analysis and rapid interpretation [14]. ROSE reports were issued by pulmonologists trained in cytopathology [15].

ROSE result interpretation

Here, ROSE facilitates preliminary diagnostic assessment to guide the direction of subsequent clinical management in reference to previous reports [16, 17]. Specimens are collected from suspicious anatomical sites and immediately processed for on-site microscopic evaluation. A specimen is classified as adequate if it contains cellular material not typical of normal lung tissue. A positive result is confirmed if abnormal cells are observed in no fewer than three slides during slide interpretation. Conversely, the absence of tumor cells was defined as a negative result. The decision to discontinue further sampling is made at the operator’s clinical discretion. All remaining specimens are forwarded for pathological analysis and diagnosis. In cytologic samples, cellular atypia and abnormal cells suggestive of malignancy were considered as non-diagnostic evidence for malignancy. Using pathological examination results as the gold standard. Tumor cell atypia was characterized by (1) abnormal cell size (enlargement or persistence of small primitive cells), (2) significant variation in tumor cell size and shape (pleomorphism, presence of tumor giant cells), (3) increased nuclear volume, higher nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio (e.g., normal epithelium ~ 1:4–1:6; malignant cells ~ 1:1), (4) variation in nuclear size, shape, and staining (nuclear pleomorphism), presence of giant nuclei, binucleation, multinucleation, or bizarre nuclei, and increased nuclear DNA content (hyperchromasia), coarse granular chromatin unevenly distributed, often clumped beneath the nuclear membrane, (5) prominent, large, and/or multiple nucleoli, and (6) increased mitotic activity and presence of abnormal mitotic figures (e.g., asymmetrical, multipolar).

Outcome measures

This study recorded the results of both examinations and the time required for report generation, compared the consistency rate between ROSE findings and pathological outcomes, evaluated the timeliness of reports from 2019 to 2022, and analyzed whether there were differences in diagnostic positivity rates between the two groups. The pathological concordance rate for 124 cases using dual-operator ROSE technology (2019–2022) was compared with that for 20 cases using single-operator ROSE technology (2023–2024) (single-operator: biopsy sampling and ROSE performed by the same person; dual-operator: one person performs biopsy sampling, another performs ROSE).

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 27.0. Categorical data were described using frequency and percentage (%) and compared using the χ2 test. Normally distributed continuous data were described as Mean ± SD and compared using the t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as Median and analyzed using the rank-sum test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two sided).

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the malignancy with the highest incidence and mortality rates in China [18]. Traditional interventional diagnostic methods often suffer from a lack of real-time feedback, leading to issues such as repeated procedures and unnecessarily extensive intervention. ROSE can provide immediate guidance, enable operators to precisely control the scope of intervention, and reduce the need for repeated operations [19]. Most importantly, ROSE shortens the diagnosis and treatment cycle, thereby alleviating the physical, mental, and economic burdens on patients [20]. However, there are limited clinical reports regarding the effectiveness of ROSE in diagnosing respiratory system tumors. Therefore, this retrospective study aims to investigate whether the incorporation of ROSE can enhance diagnostic sensitivity.

ROSE technology involves the immediate preparation and on-site cytological assessment of specimens obtained during fine-needle aspiration or biopsy procedures. It functions as an initial on-site screening tool to rapidly assess specimen adequacy and presumptive findings during biopsy procedure, while definitive lung cancer diagnosis requires permanent pathology, including immunohistochemical analysis. ROSE has a wide range of applications, including its integration with various endoscopic procedures such as bronchoscopy, thoracoscopy, and gastroscopy, as well as percutaneous lung biopsy for diagnostic interventions. Additionally, it plays a significant role in diagnosing thyroid and gastrointestinal diseases [21, 22]. The primary advantages of ROSE are its speed and real-time capability [23], which are especially evident in respiratory interventions such as bronchoscopy, thoracoscopy, pleural biopsy, and percutaneous lung puncture. Particularly in bronchoscopy, which involves sampling (biopsy, brushing, lavage) of both visually accessible and non-accessible lung lesions, immediate ROSE of the acquired tissue or cells rapidly assesses sample adequacy, guides the interventional procedure, and even provides a preliminary diagnosis [6].

In this study, the median time for ROSE reporting (2019–2022) was 8.0 min, drastically shorter than the 5645.5 min for pathology reporting, while maintaining high concordance. This can reduce hospital stays, unnecessary tests, patient financial burden, and conserve medical resources. Chen et al. conducted a meta-analysis [5] finding that ROSE combined with lung biopsy had a sensitivity of 91.8% and specificity of 94.9% for lung cancer diagnosis, effectively improving diagnostic accuracy for lung diseases, with ROSE showing high consistency with pathology. Our study found a concordance rate of 95.83%. With the advancement of technology, the time required to issue pathological reports has gradually decreased. During the 2023–2024 period, the average time for issuing rapid pathological reports was 1446.5 min, with the shortest time being just 1118 min. However, this is still not as prompt as the ROSE technology.

In this study, two common complications associated with bronchoscopic biopsy, bleeding and pneumothorax, were rarely observed. Generally, under standard clinical practice, complications arising from fiberoptic bronchoscopy and specimen sampling are infrequent when these procedures are performed by experienced respiratory physicians. From 2022 to 2024, among retrievable cases, the mean blood loss was 4.87 mL in the ROSE group (n = 16) and 2.37 mL in the non-ROSE group (n = 27), with no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups (P > 0.05). No cases of pneumothorax or infection-related complications were observed. Supporting literature corroborates that ROSE procedures do not increase the incidence of puncture-related complications, including intraoperative bleeding, pneumothorax, or postoperative hemoptysis [24, 25]. In conventional fiberoptic bronchoscopy, sampling sites are typically determined by interventional physicians based on their clinical expertise and visual identification of airway abnormalities within the field of view. This inherently subjective approach frequently yields suboptimal specimens. When final pathological findings prove unsatisfactory, patients may require a repeat procedure [26]. Collins et al. demonstrated that ROSE significantly improves specimen adequacy rates, reduces the need for repeat procedures, enables rapid diagnosis, and shortens examination and hospitalization times [27].

Current lung cancer treatment emphasizes precision medicine, integrating standardization and personalization. Treatment modalities include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. Treatment planning depends on pathological and genetic testing results; precise diagnosis is essential for precise treatment. Relevant studies indicate that incorporating ROSE effectively improves diagnostic accuracy, particularly for endoscopically non-visible malignancies during transbronchial biopsy, and shows high consistency with histology for diagnosing squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and small cell carcinoma [20]. For small early-stage lesions or lesions in distal bronchi not directly visible during bronchoscopy, immediate ROSE can improve biopsy specimen adequacy, providing satisfactory samples for subsequent pathological examination [28, 29].

In this study, the ROSE group demonstrated a high concordance rate of 95.83% with pathological findings. This result confirms that it is feasible to determine the histological subtypes of the most common lung tumors during ROSE procedures, leveraging the identifiable cytomorphological characteristics. Specifically, this applies to the three major histological types: lung adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and small cell carcinoma, providing a preliminary indication of whether a patient has lung cancer. However, atypical cytology necessitates pathology or even immunohistochemistry for precise classification. Therefore, ROSE cannot replace pathology or immunohistochemistry as the diagnostic gold standard. This explains why the ROSE positive rate (98.61%) was higher than the pathology positive rate (97.22%) in this study. ROSE typically reports positive results when dysplastic cells are observed on more than 3 slides, while pathology and immunohistochemistry apply more stringent criteria. Cases in which pathology reported only"suspicious malignant cells,""consider malignancy possible, resubmit if necessary,"or"moderate-severe dysplasia"without definitive diagnosis were not classified as positive in this study. Nevertheless, the real-time interpretive value of ROSE remains significant.

Importantly, ROSE enables real-time preliminary diagnosis in select cases. For biopsy-negative cases, ROSE directs targeted resampling to avoid non-diagnostic procedures; validated samples are optimally allocated for downstream molecular/genetic profiling [30]. Our results also indicate that ROSE significantly increased malignancy detection rates versus standalone pathology by optimizing intraprocedural sampling. For biopsy-positive cases, ROSE redirected sampling to high-yield areas, enhancing specimen quality [31, 32]. When ROSE identifies malignant cytology discordant with subsequent negative histopathology, immediate retesting is mandated. Thus, this integrated approach leverages the sensitivity of ROSE and diagnostic rigor of pathology method to maximize diagnostic accuracy and patient safety. It is worth noting that while ROSE enhances real-time specimen triage for treatment-naïve lung cancer, its utility diminishes in previous treated cases (post-targeted therapy, immunotherapy, chemotherapy) due to profound cytomorphologic atypia. The cytomorphology of re-sampled material is often highly atypical, making on-site ROSE interpretation challenging. Therefore, ROSE is more suitable for initial diagnostic specimens.

Further, both dual-operator and single-operator ROSE techniques showed high pathological concordance rates. Although the difference was not statistically significant, both methods hold practical clinical value. In understaffed settings, bronchoscopists may need to perform both roles. In this study, the bronchoscopist performed ROSE after obtaining several specimens, proceeding with further bronchoscopy as needed. The single-operator reduces human resource demands. Result interpretation relies on the operator's smear preparation quality and interpretive experience. Less typical microscopic appearances might be misinterpreted as non-neoplastic by some operators. Although the single-operator sample size was small and the group sizes unequal in this study, the positive rate remained high. An experienced operator performing both"sampling"and"ROSE"can optimally assess specimen quality and quantity, guiding subsequent sampling sites and predicting adequacy. However, the dual-operator is preferable when resources allow.

Furthermore, ROSE technology has potential for broader application in diagnosing respiratory infections and immune-mediated diseases [33, 34], warranting further exploration. With the advancement of AI technology, research combining AI with ROSE is underway [35]. An ROSE-AI system achieved accuracies of 92.97% and 90.26% in identifying images containing malignant lung cells on internal and external test datasets, respectively, performing comparably to experienced cytopathologists. AI can also mitigate ROSE's limitations related to operator experience and subjectivity, automatically identifying biopsy tissue nature, providing objective quantitative analysis of lesions via image processing, detecting subtle details easily missed by the human eye, improving clinicians'diagnostic rates, while reducing labor costs and increasing efficiency. ROSE-AI systems show considerable potential in the clinical bronchoscopic diagnosis and management of lung malignancies.

Limitations

This study acknowledges several limitations: a limited sample size that restricts the feasibility of subgroup analyses, a single-center design that undermines generalizability; inadequate statistical power in single-operator ROSE vs. non-ROSE cohorts; and incomplete variables that copromises multivariable modeling. Future multi-center studies, incorporating pre-specified protocols for data completeness and balanced operator-specific sampling strategies, are critical to enabling robust validation.

Conclusions

ROSE technology provides rapid and accurate advantages in the diagnosis and treatment of respiratory system tumors, demonstrating significant clinical value. The concordance rates for both single-operator and dual-operator ROSE techniques were high. However, the interpretation of ROSE results is related to individual experience and technical skill, necessitating further training and accumulated practice. Collectively, ROSE technology combined with bronchoscopy holds significant clinical value in diagnosing respiratory system tumors. It enables earlier preliminary diagnoses, guides clinical management, reduces unnecessary tests and treatments, and conserves medical resources.
