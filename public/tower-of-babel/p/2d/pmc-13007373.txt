Prompt injection in manuscripts: exploiting loopholes or crossing ethical lines?
Authors: Shuchen Tang, Zilong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of AI in academic publishing has raised significant ethical concerns, particularly regarding the practice of prompt injection, where hidden instructions are embedded in manuscripts to manipulate AI responses in the peer review process.MethodsThis study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining a comprehensive content analysis of academic integrity guidelines with a survey of 194 stakeholders, including authors, peer reviewers, and journal editors from various academic fields. The survey focused on their awareness of prompt injection, perceptions of its ethical implications, and views on AI transparency in peer review.ResultsThe findings reveal that a substantial proportion of participants (80%) support greater transparency in the use of AI in peer review. Many respondents reported frustrations with the inconsistencies and inefficacies of AI-generated feedback, prompting some to consider the use of prompt injection as a strategy to secure favorable review outcomes. Importantly, the analysis identified a significant gap in current definitions of research misconduct, which do not adequately address the ethical implications of AI interventions.ConclusionsThis study highlights the urgent need for revised ethical frameworks that incorporate AI-related issues in academic publishing, advocating for policies that promote transparency and uphold the integrity of the peer review process.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Prompt Injection, Peer Review, Research Misconduct, Academic Integrity

Introduction

In July 2025, an investigation by The Nikkei revealed that at least 17 computer science papers from 14 prestigious universities, including Waseda University, Columbia University, and Tongji University, contained hidden instructions detectable only by AI [1]. These "hidden instructions," or prompt injections, are covert directives embedded within academic manuscripts, designed to manipulate AI systems during the peer review process. Prompt injections exploit the ability of AI models to process information in ways that can be influenced by specific, hidden commands. This raises ethical concerns as such manipulations may bias peer review outcomes, potentially undermining academic integrity.

AI has become increasingly integrated into academic publishing, particularly in peer review, where it is used for tasks such as plagiarism detection and automated manuscript evaluations. These tools promise to streamline the peer review process and enhance objectivity [2]. However, this integration is not without risks. Unreported AI involvement in peer review can reduce transparency and accountability, with potentially serious consequences for scholarly integrity [3]. As a result, many journals have implemented policies requiring disclosure of AI involvement [4]. Nevertheless, hidden manipulations, such as prompt injections, continue to present a challenge. Whether inserted into figure metadata, Unicode characters, or even subtle persuasive prose, these hidden instructions can bias AI evaluations in favor of the author, compromising the fairness of the review process. Current editorial guidelines often overlook such manipulations, considering them technical issues rather than ethical violations.

To better understand what "Prompt Injection" is and how it can influence the peer review process, it’s important to explain it clearly. Prompt injection, different from accidental prompt inclusion, is when someone secretly adds hidden instructions into a manuscript (such as in the text, figures, or metadata) that can change how an AI reads or evaluates the paper. (See Table 1) These tactics take advantage of how AI works, guiding its decisions without human reviewers noticing. This makes it very hard for editors and researchers to spot such hidden instructions during the review process because they might not have the tools to detect these manipulations.

This study seeks to explore the ethical implications of prompt injection as a form of academic misconduct. We address two central questions: (1) Do existing national and international integrity codes adequately address prompt injection within current definitions of misconduct? (2) How do authors, reviewers, and editors perceive the ethical status of such manipulations when AI participation in peer review is either explicit or implicit? By combining qualitative policy analysis with a cross-disciplinary survey, we aim to clarify whether hidden prompt injections constitute research misconduct and provide recommendations for policies and safeguards that protect academic integrity while enabling the efficient use of AI in peer review.

Literature review

The integration of AI into scholarly publishing is rapidly transforming the landscape of academic peer review. Proponents argue that AI-powered systems can enhance efficiency, reduce reviewer burden, and accelerate the dissemination of scientific knowledge [5, 6]. However, this technological shift has introduced novel vectors for academic misconduct. A particularly concerning development is the practice of researchers embedding hidden messages, such as white-text prompts, within manuscripts to adversarially manipulate AI review systems into rendering positive evaluations [1, 7, 8]. This phenomenon has ignited a critical debate within the academic community. This review synthesizes the extant literature by examining the arguments contextualizing this behavior, the overwhelming ethical condemnations against it, and a critical gap in the current research: the profound lack of empirical investigation.

Rationale and motivations for deceiving AI review systems

While ethically indefensible, a comprehensive analysis requires understanding the complex motivations that drive such behavior. The literature suggests these actions are not merely isolated acts of dishonesty but are symptomatic of deeper systemic pressures and a growing mistrust in the evolving peer review ecosystem [9].

One primary rationale is a form of protest against the perceived inadequacies and opacity of modern peer review. In an environment strained by reviewer fatigue and burnout [10], there is a growing suspicion that overworked human reviewers may overly rely on AI-generated summaries. Researchers engaging in prompt injection sometimes frame their actions as a countermeasure against this perceived lack of diligence, rationalizing it as a diagnostic tool to expose the fallibility of a superficial, overly automated review process [11]. This reflects a broader anxiety about the erosion of deep intellectual engagement in scholarly evaluation and a misguided attempt to hold the "black box" of AI-assisted review accountable [12].

A second, more pervasive driver is the relentless "publish or perish" culture institutionalized within academia [13]. The intense pressure to secure publications for career advancement, tenure, and grant funding—often measured by crude bibliometrics—creates a high-stakes environment where ethical boundaries can become blurred [8, 14]. The perceived vulnerabilities of an AI system can thus be viewed as a rational, albeit unethical, response to a hyper-competitive system. For some, it represents a calculated shortcut to navigate the increasingly competitive landscape of academic publishing, where submission volumes far outstrip the review capacity of the community [15, 16].

Finally, a more nuanced motivation relates to documented biases inherent in AI models. AI tools trained on vast but historically skewed datasets may inadvertently perpetuate and amplify existing biases against non-native English speakers, interdisciplinary research, novel methodologies, or scholars from underrepresented institutions and geographical regions [17, 18]. Consequently, some researchers may view the direct manipulation of an AI reviewer as a misguided attempt to level the playing field, circumventing a system they believe is inherently biased against their work and thus unlikely to provide a fair assessment.

Ethical condemnation and systemic risks of AI manipulation

The predominant view within the scholarly community is one of unequivocal condemnation, grounded in the foundational ethical frameworks established by leading oversight bodies such as the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) and the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE). The manipulation of AI peer review is widely regarded as a severe breach of research ethics that poses a significant threat to the integrity of the scholarly record.

At its core, while COPE and ICMJE have not issued specific guidance on “prompt injection,” the practice is arguably a form of scientific fraud if its intent is to subvert the fundamental purpose of peer review: to provide an independent, critical, and unbiased assessment of research quality [19, 20]. By circumventing this essential quality control mechanism, authors risk propagating flawed, unsubstantiated, or even fabricated findings. This not only pollutes the scientific literature but also erodes public trust in science and can have tangible, harmful consequences, particularly in biomedical fields where subsequent research builds upon published work [21, 22].

Furthermore, this behavior aligns with, and critically facilitates, the industrial-scale fraud perpetrated by "paper mills" [23, 24]. These illicit organizations could readily weaponize adversarial techniques to automate the process of by passing initial AI-driven quality checks, allowing fraudulent manuscripts to inundate submission systems [25, 26]. This would place an even greater burden on human editors and reviewers, making the already challenging task of detecting sophisticated fraud nearly insurmountable and further degrading the value of the scholarly publication as a mark of validated knowledge.

The practice also violates the foundational principle of transparency, a cornerstone of the social contract of science [27]. In response to the growing role of AI, numerous publishers and ethics bodies, including Springer Nature, Sage Publishing, and JMIR Publications, have established clear policies requiring authors to disclose the use of AI in manuscript preparation and explicitly forbidding reviewers from using generative AI to maintain confidentiality (Emerald [28], JMIR [29], Sage [28, 30]. Embedding hidden instructions is a direct and clandestine contravention of these evolving ethical guidelines, representing a breach of trust with editors, reviewers, and the entire readership.

The critical research gap: an absence of empirical inquiry

Despite extensive media coverage and vigorous ethical debate, the current body of literature on this topic is characterized by a significant gap: a near-total absence of systematic empirical research. The existing discourse is largely composed of journalistic reports [1, 7],The [31], expert commentaries [8, 12], and publisher policy statements. While these contributions are vital for raising awareness and framing the ethical debate, they lack the empirical foundation necessary for a comprehensive understanding of the problem and the development of evidence-based solutions.

Research design & methodology

This study aims to define "Prompt Injection in Manuscripts" as the practice of embedding covert instructions within manuscripts to influence AI reviewers and elicit favorable feedback. To explore this emerging issue, the research utilizes both qualitative and quantitative methods to assess its ethical and procedural implications within academic publishing.

Qualitative component

The qualitative component of this study aims to examine the ethical and procedural implications of prompt injection, specifically within the context of peer review. To achieve this, a comprehensive content analysis was conducted on national and international academic integrity codes, institutional ethics guidelines, and policy documents from prominent research institutions and publishers. These documents were reviewed to assess whether current definitions of research misconduct explicitly address prompt injection or whether this practice remains an emerging ethical issue that falls outside traditional misconduct frameworks. The content analysis specifically focused on the extent to which academic guidelines define and address manipulation, misconduct, and ethical boundaries, particularly in relation to AI-assisted systems in academic publishing.

Given the novel nature of prompt injection, the analysis also explored how traditional ethical frameworks might need to adapt to account for new challenges posed by AI technologies. The documents were analyzed using a deductive coding approach, categorizing the text into themes such as misconduct definitions, AI integration in peer review, and transparency in review processes. This approach allowed for a nuanced understanding of how prompt injection might fit within existing misconduct categories, and the insights gathered from this analysis were synthesized to propose recommendations for updating academic misconduct definitions and policies to incorporate emerging issues like AI-driven manipulations.

Questionnaire component

The quantitative component of the study aimed to assess the ethical perceptions and awareness of prompt injection among key stakeholders in academic publishing, namely authors, peer reviewers, and journal editors. The primary objective was to gauge the level of awareness regarding prompt injection and understand how participants perceive its ethical status. A stratified sampling method was employed to ensure that participants from a range of academic disciplines, journal types, and levels of experience were included. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation in the survey.

The survey included a combination of Likert-scale questions and open-ended items to capture both quantitative and qualitative data. The survey assessed three main dimensions: participants’ awareness of prompt injection, their ethical judgment on whether prompt injection constitutes research misconduct, and the influence of AI involvement in the peer review process on their ethical judgments. To evaluate the impact of AI involvement, the survey used a randomized design, where participants were either informed that AI had been involved in the review process or were not given this information. This design allowed the study to compare ethical judgments across these two scenarios and to determine whether the explicit disclosure of AI involvement influenced participants' views on prompt injection. The Likert-scale responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics to identify trends in the data, while open-ended responses were analyzed thematically to explore the motivations and rationales behind participants’ ethical judgments.

The review was structured to assess whether the practice of prompt injection—a form of manipulation of AI-driven systems to illicitly influence peer review outcomes—is explicitly or implicitly addressed in the existing misconduct frameworks. The documents reviewed in this study were selected based on their relevance and influence in shaping global academic integrity practices. These included international treaties and agreements on research ethics, such as the UNESCO Recommendation on Science and Scientific Researchers and the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity, as well as national integrity frameworks such as the U.S. Public Health Service (PHS) Policy on Research Misconduct and the NSF and HHS definitions.

At the institutional level, guidelines from top academic institutions such as Harvard University, the University of Oxford, and the University of Tokyo were examined. These institutions' policies on academic misconduct, which cover areas like fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism, provide insight into the standards by which academic misconduct is identified and addressed. Additionally, publisher-specific codes of conduct—such as those from the Elsevier and Springer Nature—were reviewed for their stance on AI-related ethical practices in the peer review process, particularly the use of AI tools in manuscript evaluation and the potential for manipulation through prompt injection.

The systematic review of these documents aimed to answer several key questions: Does the current framework for academic misconduct explicitly mention or define the manipulation of AI-assisted systems in peer review? Is prompt injection treated as a form of ethical misconduct? Do existing policies consider AI-driven manipulations—such as embedding hidden prompts in manuscripts—as a breach of academic integrity?

Through this rigorous process, the study seeks to identify whether there is an ethical gap in current academic integrity frameworks, leaving room for manipulation through AI, and whether additional clarity or new policies are needed to address such emerging threats. If prompt injection is not adequately addressed, this could signal the need for a more refined, contemporary understanding of research integrity—one that takes into account the evolving role of AI in academic publishing. By examining these various documents, this study aims to provide a detailed comparison of how ethical guidelines across the world are addressing—or failing to address—AI-driven manipulations like prompt injection in academic publishing.

Findings from academic norms and ethical guidelines

Recent examinations of academic ethics documents and guidelines have revealed a significant gap in addressing AI-related misconduct, particularly prompt injection. While traditional forms of academic misconduct, such as fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism, are well-defined within most academic frameworks, the emerging concept of prompt injection—where AI systems are manipulated to generate biased or fabricated academic content—remains notably absent. Prompt injection refers to the practice of embedding subtle instructions or covert prompts into AI-generated content, thereby influencing the outcomes of academic outputs such as manuscript evaluations or research findings. These manipulations can distort the peer review process, leading to misleading or unethical results. Although many academic integrity frameworks do not explicitly mention prompt injection, the increasing use of AI tools in academic publishing highlights the potential for such practices to be considered under broader misconduct categories, including academic manipulation and human intervention [32, 33].

Several academic institutions have developed guidelines emphasizing transparency and efforts to prevent undue influence in the peer review process. These guidelines aim to safeguard the credibility and impartiality of academic publishing by discouraging practices that could introduce bias, such as conflicts of interest or attempts to improperly sway peer reviewers. While these provisions address traditional concerns related to human biases and external pressures, they do not explicitly address the role of AI tools or the specific risks posed by practices like prompt injection. Nevertheless, these policies still acknowledge the risks posed by external manipulations of academic work, which can be conceptually extended to cover AI-driven misconduct in the peer review process [34]. The absence of explicit references to AI-related misconduct in most ethical codes signals a significant gap in academic integrity policies, suggesting the need for updates that reflect the evolving landscape of academic publishing, where AI tools play an increasingly prominent role.

Regional differences in defining "Manipulating peer review"

Academic misconduct, particularly the manipulation of the peer review process, is not only defined by the specific actions involved but also by how these actions are understood, regulated, and addressed across different global regions. In regions such as the United States and the European Union, academic integrity frameworks are highly formalized and typically feature detailed provisions aimed at maintaining transparency and fairness in peer review processes. These frameworks recognize the growing role of AI tools in academic publishing, acknowledging their potential to either enhance or undermine the integrity of scholarly work. Despite this awareness, prompt injection—the practice of manipulating AI systems to influence the outcomes of peer reviews—has not yet been explicitly integrated into the majority of official academic misconduct frameworks. For example, bodies like the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF) and the European Commission regulate researcher behavior and ethical responsibilities but primarily focus on more traditional forms of misconduct, such as plagiarism and data falsification, without addressing the ethical concerns posed by the use of AI in academic peer review [35]. This gap in regulations reflects a broader challenge of adapting traditional academic integrity frameworks to the new realities introduced by AI technologies.

In contrast, regions with less developed governance around AI—especially in parts of Asia and Africa—tend to be more permissive of AI’s role in the peer review process. Ethical guidelines in these areas often lack clear regulations or explicit references to AI's involvement in research or manuscript evaluation. Consequently, these regions show a higher tolerance for potential AI-related abuses, including practices such as prompt injection, which may go unchecked due to the absence of AI-specific ethical standards. The lack of comprehensive AI governance in these regions further underscores the need for international collaboration to establish uniform academic standards that can effectively address the emerging ethical challenges posed by AI-driven manipulations in academic practices [36]. As AI tools become increasingly integrated into scholarly publishing, it is critical that global academic integrity policies evolve to account for new forms of misconduct, ensuring that all regions are equipped with the necessary guidelines to mitigate AI-related risks.

Implications for policy and academic integrity

The findings highlight the growing ethical dilemma surrounding the use of AI tools, such as ChatGPT, in peer review processes. While traditional academic misconduct focuses on tangible, human-driven actions, AI-driven manipulations like prompt injection are increasingly posing new challenges. As the role of AI in academic publishing and peer review continues to grow, there is an urgent need for updated, comprehensive policies that explicitly address the risks associated with AI manipulation, including prompt injection. These updated policies should not only expand the definition of misconduct to include AI-driven actions but also introduce clear regulatory frameworks for the ethical use of AI tools in research [32].

As AI systems evolve, their role in scholarly publishing becomes more significant. Researchers, journals, and academic institutions must adapt their ethics codes to encompass new types of misconduct that were previously unanticipated. The increasing use of AI tools in writing, editing, and reviewing papers means that the traditional regulatory approaches to research integrity may no longer be sufficient to safeguard against manipulations that could undermine the authenticity and credibility of research [33]. This need for updated regulation aligns with ongoing discussions in the academic community about the future of research ethics and the integration of AI technologies [35].

Schrödinger's prompt injection in manuscripts

A central theme in the survey was the influence of transparency on ethical judgments regarding prompt injection. The data demonstrated a significant difference in ethical perceptions based on whether AI involvement was disclosed. When AI was explicitly stated as part of the peer review process, 72% of respondents classified Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as a form of misconduct. Respondents emphasized that when AI's role is known, there is a heightened expectation for transparency and integrity. Manipulation through prompt injection in these contexts was regarded as a deliberate act to exploit the review system, which compromises its fairness and credibility.

“这其实并不难理解, 若刊物明确声明使用AI进行审稿, 那么当你投稿时就相当于接受了AI作为你的审稿人。在这种情况下, 利用隐藏指令来获取更优的评审意见, 显然是一种学术不端行为, 可以类比为操纵同行评议。”

"This is actually quite easy to understand. If a journal clearly states that it uses AI for peer review, then when you submit your work, you are essentially accepting AI as your reviewer. In this case, using Prompt Injection in Manuscripts to obtain better review feedback is evidently an act of academic misconduct, comparable to manipulating peer review."

In contrast, when AI involvement was not disclosed, only 9% of participants viewed Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as misconduct.

“即便我使用了隐藏指令, 但如果审稿人并未使用AI进行审稿, 这对他并不会产生任何影响。相反, 如果审稿人确实使用了AI审稿, 而我没有利用隐藏指令, 那么我的稿件可能就无法获得公正的评审。”

"Even if I use Prompt Injection in Manuscripts, it won't affect the reviewer if they are not using AI for the review. Conversely, if the reviewer is indeed using AI for the review, and I haven’t used Prompt Injection in Manuscripts, my manuscript might not receive a fair evaluation."

This discrepancy suggests that transparency plays a critical role in shaping ethical perceptions. In the absence of clear AI involvement, some respondents seemed to view prompt injection as a less severe issue or one that might be overlooked. This discrepancy creates a peculiar ethical ambiguity, a phenomenon we term 'Schrödinger's Prompt Injection': the act seems to exist in a conceptual superposition of being both harmless text (if unobserved by an AI) and clear misconduct (if processed by an AI). This duality underscores that the perceived wrongfulness of the act is contingent on the transparency of AI's involvement in the review process. In such cases, participants were less likely to view the hidden text as an intentional act of manipulation.

This finding aligns with the broader literature on ethical decision-making, where the disclosure of relevant information significantly influences judgments of misconduct. Participants in this study appeared to hold more stringent ethical standards when AI's role was transparent, aligning with the view that academic integrity is closely tied to the openness of the peer review process [4, 37].

Why prompt injection in manuscripts might be used

The survey also explored the potential motivations behind the use of prompt injection. Several participants suggested that Prompt Injection in Manuscripts could be employed to address perceived inefficiencies in AI-generated reviews.

“我认为使用指令注入主要是因为AI生成的审稿反馈常常缺乏有效性。曾经我收到的反馈完全不负责任且缺乏逻辑, 但我还是不得不根据那些评论进行修改。这种情况让我感到非常挫败。”

"I believe the use of Prompt Injection in Manuscripts is mainly due to the inefficacy of AI-generated review feedback. I once received feedback that was utterly irresponsible and illogical, yet I still had to make revisions based on those comments. This situation left me feeling very frustrated."

This sentiment reflects a common frustration among scholars with AI systems that may not align with their understanding or expectations, prompting them to manipulate the review outcomes to meet their objectives. Moreover, some participants acknowledged that Prompt Injection in Manuscripts could be strategically employed to influence the peer review process, particularly in competitive academic fields. Manipulating AI feedback to generate more favorable reviews could increase the likelihood of manuscript acceptance, especially in journals that rely heavily on AI-generated recommendations. This highlights the ethical dilemma faced by researchers who may feel pressured to compromise their integrity for professional gain.

The results suggest that AI’s perceived impartiality and objectivity make it a tempting target for manipulation. As AI systems are often viewed as neutral, any perceived flaws in their evaluations could lead authors to seek advantages through prompt injection. This underscores the need for robust ethical frameworks that address the potential for such manipulations, ensuring that AI-enhanced peer reviews do not become a vehicle for unethical practices [37, 38].

Ethical judgments and the role of AI transparency

The survey results clearly indicated the significant role that transparency plays in shaping ethical judgments. A substantial 80% of respondents expressed strong support for the clear declaration of AI involvement in the peer review process. This overwhelming consensus underscores the importance of transparency as a means to mitigate ethical risks and maintain trust in AI-assisted evaluations. Transparency was seen not only as a good practice but also as a moral imperative that enhances accountability and reduces the temptation to engage in unethical behavior, such as prompt injection.

The study further revealed a perceived gap in existing ethical guidelines, particularly in the context of AI-driven peer review. While participants agreed that prompt injection was unethical when AI involvement was explicitly disclosed, many noted that the lack of clear guidelines regarding undisclosed AI use created ambiguity. This gap suggests an urgent need for updated policies that explicitly address the ethical use of AI tools in academic publishing. Such policies should ensure that all stakeholders—authors, reviewers, and editors—understand the boundaries of acceptable AI use and are aware of the ethical implications of manipulation [2, 33].

Discussion

Restatement of research objectives and key findings

This study aimed to explore the ethical implications of Prompt Injection in Manuscripts in AI-assisted peer review, a novel manipulation technique wherein hidden prompts are embedded in manuscripts to influence AI-driven decisions. The key findings reveal significant ethical concerns regarding the use of AI in peer review processes, particularly when AI’s role is either disclosed or not disclosed. When AI use was explicitly stated, 72% of participants viewed Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as academic misconduct, whereas only 9% of respondents considered it unethical when AI involvement was not disclosed. The study also highlighted broad support for increased AI transparency in academic publishing, with 80% of participants endorsing clearer guidelines for AI use in peer review.

Interpretation and analysis of results

The findings of this study underline the importance of transparency in ethical decision-making regarding AI-assisted peer review. When AI involvement was explicitly declared, participants overwhelmingly considered Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as misconduct, suggesting that transparency creates a clear ethical boundary. This supports the notion that, in contexts where AI’s role is explicit, any manipulation of the system—whether through prompt injection or other tactics—is seen as an intentional ethical violation that undermines the integrity of peer review.

Conversely, when AI use was not disclosed, participants' ethical judgments became more nuanced. The 9% of participants who did not view Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as misconduct in the absence of transparency suggests that some researchers and reviewers might not see hidden AI manipulations as unethical, especially when they perceive such practices as harmless or as tools for self-benefit. This ambiguity in ethical judgment indicates a broader issue in academic integrity frameworks: existing guidelines do not sufficiently address AI-driven misconduct, and there is a lack of clear policy guidance on undisclosed AI involvement in peer review. These findings suggest that academic publishing is entering a new era where AI-related ethical issues must be addressed in a manner that maintains both the efficiency and the integrity of the review process.

Additionally, a portion of the respondents viewed Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as a technical issue rather than a form of academic misconduct. This perspective highlights an emerging challenge in AI governance: while AI technologies are integrated into academic publishing workflows, they remain vulnerable to manipulation. The perceived technical nature of Prompt Injection in Manuscripts suggests a gap between technology developers and ethical regulators, where the complexity of AI systems is not always matched by the clarity of misconduct definitions in research ethics codes.

Comparison with existing literature

The findings of this study resonate with current debates surrounding the ethics of AI in academic publishing. For instance, Doskaliuk et al. [2] and Wiwanitkit & Wiwanitkit [3] discuss the potential of AI to improve the peer review process, yet they also caution about the ethical risks that arise from its improper use. This study adds a novel dimension to the debate by highlighting Prompt Injection in Manuscripts as a specific ethical threat that needs further scrutiny.

Previous literature on prompt injection [37, 38] has primarily focused on its impact in fields like healthcare and language models, where it has been shown to manipulate AI decisions. The present study extends these concerns to academic publishing, demonstrating how such vulnerabilities can disrupt the integrity of peer review processes and undermine the trust in AI-assisted evaluations. This extension fills an important gap in the literature, illustrating how prompt injection could become a major ethical issue in scholarly communication.

However, while there is substantial literature on the technical vulnerabilities of AI [39], less attention has been paid to the ethical dimensions of these vulnerabilities within the academic publishing context. The study's findings align with the work of Flanagin et al. [4], who emphasize the need for greater transparency in AI use, but this study goes further by identifying prompt injection as a specific issue that requires tailored policies and ethical frameworks. Additionally, the study supports arguments made by Caron et al. [40] and Dal-Ré & Marušić [41], who have called for updated misconduct definitions to incorporate new forms of manipulation in the academic process, such as AI-driven manipulations.

Implications for policy and academic integrity

The study’s findings underscore the urgent need for updated ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks to address the increasing integration of AI in academic publishing. A critical implication is that current definitions of research misconduct—which traditionally focus on fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism—do not adequately address the emerging ethical threats posed by AI technologies like prompt injection. As AI continues to play an essential role in the peer review process, ethical standards must evolve to recognize AI-related misconduct and ensure that scholars, editors, and reviewers adhere to fair and transparent practices.

Further, the study suggests that transparency in AI use is not merely a matter of good practice but an ethical requirement. The majority of respondents supported clearer declarations of AI involvement, signaling the academic community's recognition of transparency as a key factor in maintaining integrity. This finding highlights the need for journals and publishers to adopt clear policies regarding AI use, establishing guidelines that prevent covert manipulation of the peer review process and safeguard against the unintended consequences of AI-assisted evaluations.

Limitations and future research

While the study provides significant insights into the ethical implications of AI in peer review, there are several limitations to consider. First, the self-selected sample may have introduced bias, as respondents who were more aware of or concerned about AI-related issues were more likely to participate. As such, the findings may not fully represent the broader academic community. Additionally, the study focused on text-based prompt injections within the context of peer review, leaving multimodal AI vulnerabilities—such as those in image or data-based submissions—untested. Future research should explore these multimodal risks to offer a more comprehensive understanding of AI’s vulnerabilities in academic publishing.

Finally, while this study emphasizes the need for clear ethical guidelines, further empirical research is required to develop and test enforceable safeguards that balance the efficiency gains offered by AI with the ethical requirements of academic integrity. In particular, experimental research on AI transparency and auditing mechanisms could help refine best practices for AI in peer review systems.

Conclusion and future research directions

The integration of AI technologies into academic publishing, particularly within the peer review process, offers substantial benefits in efficiency and rigor. However, it also introduces significant ethical challenges, with prompt injection emerging as a major concern. This technique manipulates AI systems by embedding covert instructions in manuscripts, which can distort the peer review process and undermine the credibility of academic publishing. Our findings highlight the lack of sufficient frameworks to address AI-driven manipulations like prompt injection, emphasizing the need for updated guidelines to prevent such unethical practices. A critical aspect of addressing AI-related misconduct is transparency. Our study found that when AI involvement in the review process is explicitly disclosed, prompt injection is widely viewed as unethical. However, the lack of transparency—when AI’s role is not clearly stated—creates ambiguity, making it harder to judge the ethical implications of AI-assisted peer review. This underscores the urgent need for clear, standardized policies that define AI-related misconduct and mandate transparency in AI use throughout the academic process.

Looking ahead, the academic community must evolve its ethical standards to address the complexities introduced by AI tools. As AI technologies continue to play an increasingly central role in academic publishing, accountability and clear ethical guidelines are essential for maintaining the integrity of the peer review process. By promoting transparency and setting rigorous ethical standards, we can safeguard the future of academic publishing, ensuring that AI enhances rather than undermines its credibility and trust.

As for future research directions, the qualitative findings from this study raise several important questions about how different ethical standards are applied to human vs. AI involvement in peer review. A particularly intriguing result is the ethical ambiguity surrounding disclosed vs. non-disclosed AI use. Respondents viewed prompt injection as unethical when AI involvement was disclosed, but only a small percentage considered it unethical when AI’s role was not disclosed. This raises an important issue: does the non-disclosure of AI use make prompt injection seem like a justified response or “revenge” for what is perceived as an unfair system? These findings suggest a potential “AI arms race” in research integrity, where authors might engage in prompt injection as a defense mechanism against what they view as an unfair, undisclosed AI review process. Further exploration is needed to understand how the lack of transparency in AI involvement in peer review impacts researchers’ ethical decisions. What justifications do they provide for using prompt injection in cases where AI is not disclosed? How do respondents perceive the ethical implications of knowing they may be reviewed by AI rather than a human, and how does this influence their willingness to manipulate the system?

Additionally, future research could further explore the ethical justifications behind the manipulation of AI systems in peer review, particularly in non-disclosed scenarios. Are authors more likely to engage in prompt injection when they feel the system lacks accountability or transparency? What role do cultural and disciplinary differences play in shaping these ethical judgments? Understanding these nuances could help shape more robust guidelines for the ethical use of AI in peer review and academic publishing. Given the complexity of these ethical challenges, future studies could build upon this research to delve deeper into the motivations and ethical frameworks that govern authors’ and reviewers’ decisions when interacting with AI in academic settings. Exploring these questions could contribute significantly to the development of comprehensive policies that address the growing influence of AI in the peer review process, ensuring that the academic publishing community remains transparent, accountable, and ethically sound.
