Forecasting regional disparities in cataract surgery demand and ophthalmologist supply in Japan: a prefecture-level forecasting study
Authors: Hiroshi Yamaguchi, Larbi Aluariachy, Retsu Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCataracts are the leading cause of poor vision in Japanese adults. As Japan’s population is rapidly ageing, the demand for cataract surgery will continue to increase. Despite this, quantitative research evaluating both supply and demand of cataract surgery is lacking. The objective of this study was to forecast future cataract surgery demand and the supply of ophthalmologists in Japan by region and evaluate the cataract surgery demand-supply balance considering regional differences in demographics and medical resources.Methods and analysisBased on the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s ‘NDB Open Data’, age-specific and sex-specific cataract surgery rates were calculated from the number of billed procedures. These rates were applied to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications’ ‘Population Estimates’ and National Institute of Population and Social Security Research’s ‘Future Population Projections’ to estimate demand for 2030, 2040 and 2050. Future supply of ophthalmologists was estimated using linear regression based on data from the ‘Survey of Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists’. The demand-supply ratio (demand divided by supply) was calculated for each prefecture. The demand-supply ratio multiplier for each year was calculated using 2022 as the base year.ResultsThe estimated number of cataract surgeries was 1 929 848 in 2030, 2 373 260 in 2040 and 2 863 733 in 2050, with a significant increase projected for individuals in their 70s and aged 80+. Although most prefectures were expected to see a gradual increase in ophthalmologist numbers, a decrease was projected for some regional areas. Consequently, the demand-supply ratio was projected to increase nationwide, with a larger increase in regional areas, suggesting a widening disparity between regional and metropolitan areas.ConclusionDemand for cataract surgery would continue to rise, potentially increasing demand-supply imbalances, particularly in regions with ageing populations. Re-examination of the regional allocation of medical resources is necessary to correct disparities in access to treatment. These projections are subject to limitations related to the use of publicly available data and assumptions based on past trends, which may not fully reflect regional practice variation or future changes in surgical capacity.

Introduction

Cataracts—a primary cause of visual impairment—is a disease in which the lens of the eye becomes cloudy owing to ageing, leading to decreased vision. According to Iwase et al, cataracts are the leading cause of poor vision in Japanese adults.1 In Japan, the population is rapidly ageing, with the number of people aged 70+ projected to continue increasing,2 resulting in an increased number of cataract surgery candidates.

As of 1 October 2023, adults aged ≥65 years accounted for 29.1% of Japan’s population,3 and this proportion is expected to continue rising.4 As the population ages, the number of individuals with cataracts—and the demand for cataract surgery—will also increase. International reviews have also reported age-related increases in cataract incidence.5 A nationwide analysis of publicly available insurance claims data from Japan showed an increasing trend in the number of ophthalmic surgeries performed between 2014 and 2020.6 Another analysis using statistics from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) indicated that the total number of ophthalmic surgeries in Japan increased by approximately 40% from 1996 to 2016, with cataract surgeries increasing approximately 1.7-fold.7 In contrast, the number of ophthalmologists grew by approximately 20% during the same period, falling below the overall growth rate of all physicians.7 These trends suggest that while demand for cataract surgery is increasing, the current rate of expansion of the healthcare delivery system may not be sufficient.

Regional disparities within Japan’s healthcare delivery system were highlighted in a study comparing the geographical distribution of ophthalmologists in Japan between 1996 and 2006.8 Another study analysing regional disparities in the number of physicians by specialty in Japan, using data from 1996 to 2014, showed significant gaps between urban and rural areas in fields such as surgery, dermatology, ophthalmology, obstetrics and gynaecology, with these disparities widening since 2008.9 International comparative statistics further underscore these challenges, highlighting distinct features of Japan’s healthcare resource indicators and raising concerns about meeting future healthcare demand.10

Cataract surgery improves quality of life by restoring vision11 and reducing the risk of falls.12 From an economic evaluation perspective, multiple cost-utility analysis studies in Japan have estimated the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of cataract surgery at approximately ¥120 000–160 000 per quality-adjusted life year, demonstrating its cost-effectiveness.13 14

While previous studies have reported increases in cataract-related surgeries owing to ageing populations and uneven distribution of medical resources, quantitative research that comprehensively evaluates both demand (future cataract surgery volume based on demographics and age) and supply (number of ophthalmologists per region) within the same framework remains limited. International comparisons indicate a significant association between cataract surgery rates and socio-economic levels,15 suggesting that regional economic conditions may influence surgical provision systems.

Therefore, we aimed to provide evidence of the optimal allocation of regional medical resources and the establishment of a sustainable ophthalmic healthcare delivery system by predicting future demand for cataract surgery and ophthalmologist supply in Japan, taking regional characteristics and differences in population dynamics into account.

Methods

To evaluate demand and supply for cataract surgery, this study calculated age-specific and sex-specific procedure rates from the number of cataract surgeries billed based on the MHLW’s ‘National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan (NDB)’ (hereafter referred to as NDB open data).16 These rates were then applied to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications Statistics Bureau’s ‘Population Estimates’17 and National Institute of Population and Social Security Research’s ‘Future Population Projections’.18 To calculate future supply, future values were estimated from ophthalmologist numbers in the MHLW’s biennial ‘Survey of Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists’.19 The demand-supply ratio was calculated for each prefecture by dividing demand by supply. Furthermore, to understand future changes, a demand-supply ratio multiplier was calculated using the 2022 demand-supply ratio as the base, and the demand-supply balance for 2030, 2040 and 2050 was evaluated.

Data source

The NDB open data published by the MHLW reflects Japan’s universal health insurance system covering the entire population. Since 2011, all claims data have been submitted electronically and stored in the NDB managed by the MHLW. The NDB contains information on Japanese medical claims, including diagnostic codes based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, drugs and procedures for both outpatients and inpatients. The MHLW creates basic statistical tables aggregating NDB data across various dimensions and releases them as NDB open data. The first NDB open data release occurred in October 2016, summarising claims data from fiscal year 2014. Releases up to the 10th edition (fiscal year 2023) are publicly available (as of 10 September 2025).

Therefore, we analysed NDB open data from the first to ninth editions, covering the period from fiscal year 2014 to fiscal year 2022. The data used consists of the number of claims for procedure code 150253010 ‘Lens Replacement Surgery (When Inserting an Intraocular Lens) (Other)’ under the classification code K282 ‘Lens Replacement Surgery’. This included the combined number of claims for inpatient and outpatient services. Data on the number of claims by age group and sex were also used. Classification code K282 represents is considered to capture the vast majority of cataract surgeries performed nationwide. However, as a characteristic of the NDB open data, aggregated procedure counts fewer than 10 are masked and displayed as ‘–’. Consequently, cataract surgery counts in younger age groups or other low-volume strata may be imprecise.

Population data used for demand calculation came from the Statistics Bureau, Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications’ ‘Population Estimates: Population as of October 1st of Each Year’. Specifically, Statistical Table No.1017 ‘Population by Prefecture, Age (5-year groups), Sex-Total Population, Japanese Population’ was referenced. Population data by prefecture, age group and sex were extracted and matched using NDB procedure counts. Population estimates were revised based on the reference national census, and the analysis period (FY2017–FY2022) included changes in the base year. Furthermore, as population estimates were published in 5-year age groups, this study reorganised the age categories prior to analysis, consolidating them into six groups: under 40, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s and 80+. For example, the 40s age group combined ‘40–44 years old’ and ‘45–49 years old’. Similarly, other age groups were consolidated by merging 5-year intervals to form 10-year age groups. Future population projections were derived from the ‘Regional Future Population Projections for Japan: 2023 Estimates’, published by the National Institute of Population and Social Security Research. Specifically, the ‘Future Population Projections by Prefecture/Municipality, Sex, and Age (5-Year) Group’ data for the years 2030, 2040 and 2050 were used.

First, to estimate the number of cataract surgeries performed per prefecture and age group, the cataract surgery rate was calculated for each age group and sex using national data. Specifically, the number of surgeries performed in each age group and each sex was divided by the population in the same group to obtain the cataract surgery rate for that group. The estimated number of procedures per prefecture, age group and sex was calculated by multiplying the cataract surgery rate by the age-group-specific and sex-specific population of each prefecture.

Next, we combined these estimates with population data to construct the final dataset for analysis. Specifically, we stratified the data by prefecture, fiscal year (FY2017–FY2022), age group and sex, and then matched the corresponding number of surgeries with the population for each stratum.

For supply calculations, we used data from the ‘Survey of Physicians, Dentists, and Pharmacists’ conducted by the MHLW. This survey was conducted biennially to clarify the sex, age, type of practice, place of employment and specialties of physicians, dentists and pharmacists. This study used data from Table 4119, ‘Number of Physicians Working in Medical Facilities, Hospitals-Clinics/Department Name (Primary)’. Table 4119 aggregates the number of physicians listing ophthalmology as their ‘primary specialty’ by prefecture, which was adopted as a supply indicator. The target period encompassed biennial data from 1996 to 2022.

Forecasting future demand (number of cataract surgeries)

The glm.nb function in statistical analysis software R (V.4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used to forecast future demand for 2030, 2040 and 2050. A negative binomial regression model was fitted with the observation year as the continuous variable, age group, sex and prefecture as explanatory variables, population as the offset term and estimated number of procedures as the response variable. Using the estimated parameters from the negative binomial regression model and the projected population by sex and age group for 2030, 2040 and 2050, we calculated the projected number of cataract surgeries in the corresponding future years.

Estimation of future supply (ophthalmologist numbers)

Based on time-series data for supply, separate linear regression models were constructed for each prefecture using SPSS (V.29.0.2.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). The dependent variable was the number of ophthalmologists, and the explanatory variable was the observation year. The model fit was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), F-value and p value. An R² value <0.30 was judged a poor fit. For prefectures meeting this criterion, the model was re-estimated by gradually changing the range of the explanatory variable (observation year), and the model with an R² value >0.30 was adopted. In addition, sensitivity analyses using a fixed-window model that included the full available observation period were conducted, and the results are reported in the online supplemental materials. Ultimately, the estimated ophthalmologist numbers per prefecture for 2030, 2040 and 2050 obtained using this method were used as the ‘supply’ indicators in this study.

Primary outcome definition

To evaluate the future balance between cataract surgery demand and ophthalmologist supply, we defined the primary outcome as the demand-supply ratio, where demand was defined as the projected number of cataract surgery procedures and supply was defined as the projected number of ophthalmologists. This ratio corresponds to the number of cataract surgeries per ophthalmologist and directly represents the annual surgical workload required per ophthalmologist. In this metric, higher values indicate that a larger volume of surgical demand must be managed by a limited number of ophthalmologists, reflecting a greater relative shortage of ophthalmologist supply.

Calculation of demand-supply ratio multipliers

The demand-supply ratio for each year was calculated as ‘Demand (Number of Cataract Surgeries) ÷ Supply (Number of Ophthalmologists)’. The demand-supply ratio multiplier was then calculated as the ratio of each year’s demand-supply ratio to the 2022 demand-supply ratio. For visualisation, the demand-supply ratio multiplier relative to the 2022 baseline was categorised into predefined threshold values (≥0.9, ≥1.0, ≥1.1, ≥1.2, ≥1.3, ≥1.4, ≥1.5, ≥1.6 and ≥1.7), with each category displayed using a sequential colour scale. The geographical distribution of the demand-supply ratio was mapped using the ggplot2 package in R, combining the sf, dplyr and ggplot2 functions. The demand-supply ratio multiples for 2030, 2040 and 2050 were mapped at prefectural level.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant laws and regulations, including the Personal Information Protection Act. All data used in this study are publicly available prefecture-level data from Japanese government agencies. Because no personal data were handled, ethical review by individual research institutions was deemed unnecessary.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Cataract surgery procedure counts were organised by prefecture, age group and sex using NDB open data for the fourth (FY2017) to the ninth (FY2022) surveys. NDB open data from the first (FY2014) to the third (FY2016) surveys lacked information on cataract surgery billing counts by age group and was therefore excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the analysis covered data from the fourth (FY2017) to the ninth (FY2022) surveys, totalling six surveys.

The number of surgeries nationwide increased from 1 448 725 cases in fiscal year 2017 to 1 691 874 cases in fiscal year 2022. By sex, the number of surgeries for women exceeded that for men in all fiscal years, with 948 345 cases for women and 743 529 cases for men in fiscal year 2022. By age group, individuals in their 70s had the highest number of surgeries, totalling 800 392 in fiscal year 2022, followed by those aged 80+ with 481 592 surgeries and those in their 60s with 287 337 surgeries. Combined, the older groups (70s and 80+) accounted for approximately 1.3 million surgeries, indicating that the majority of the demand was concentrated among older individuals.

By prefecture, Tokyo recorded the highest number, reaching 155 407 cases in fiscal year 2022. This was followed by Osaka Prefecture (115 002 cases), Kanagawa Prefecture (114 663 cases), Saitama Prefecture (95 454 cases) and Aichi Prefecture (91 802 cases), all of which showed increasing trends. Conversely, compared with metropolitan areas, relatively fewer procedures were performed in prefectures with smaller populations. In the fiscal year 2022, the number of procedures was 8085 in Tottori Prefecture, 10 227 in Shimane Prefecture, 10 792 in Fukui Prefecture, 10 936 in Kochi Prefecture and 11 103 in Tokushima Prefecture.

Overall, while the number of cataract surgeries nationwide showed an increasing trend, disparities in the distribution by sex, age group and region were confirmed.

Ophthalmologist numbers by prefecture for every other year from 1996 to 2022 showed that the total number of ophthalmologists nationwide increased from 10 982 in 1996 to 13 554 in 2022, representing an increase of 2572 (approximately 23%) over 26 years. Tokyo Prefecture had the highest number, increasing from 1742 in 1996 to 2029 in 2022, followed by Osaka Prefecture (995 in 1996 to 1156 in 2022), Kanagawa Prefecture (653 to 894), Aichi Prefecture (609 to 799) and Hyogo Prefecture (536 to 680), indicating a concentration of ophthalmologists in major metropolitan areas. In these regions, physician numbers had steadily increased in response to population growth and rising medical demand. Conversely, prefectures with smaller populations had fewer ophthalmologists than metropolitan areas. The number of ophthalmologists in Tottori Prefecture increased from 55 in 1996 to 62 in 2022, in Shimane Prefecture from 59 to 69, in Fukui Prefecture from 57 to 82 and in Saga Prefecture from 68 to 79. The number in Aomori Prefecture remained stable (from 82 to 83).

Nationwide, ophthalmologist numbers showed a gradual upward trend. However, regional disparities persisted. Although continuous growth was observed in major metropolitan areas, increases in prefectures with smaller populations remained limited, indicating ongoing regional disparities.

Future demand forecast (cataract surgery volume)

Future projections for cataract surgery in 2030, 2040 and 2050 are presented (online supplemental tables 1–3). Nationwide, the estimated number of surgeries was projected to be 1 929 848 in 2030, 2 373 260 in 2040 and 2 863 733 in 2050, indicating a sustained increase throughout the period. Patients in their 70s and those 80+ accounted for the majority of cases. The number of cases in their 70s was estimated to be 763 558 in 2030, 952 946 in 2040 and 1 253 484 in 2050, representing the largest increase. For those aged 80+, the number was estimated to be 682 531 in 2030, 832 276 in 2040 and 1 037 526 in 2050, confirming a sustained long-term increase. The number of cases calculated for women exceeded that of men in all age groups.

The number of cases was estimated to be significantly higher in major metropolitan areas. Tokyo had the highest projected demand nationwide, with 182 222 cases by 2030, 246 069 by 2040 and 320 645 by 2050. Continuous increases were also projected for Osaka Prefecture (128 913 in 2030 to 196 754 in 2050), Kanagawa Prefecture (134 979 to 220 829) and Aichi Prefecture (106 233 to 171 266).

Even in regions facing population decline, the calculations showed an upward trend. Akita Prefecture was estimated to have 17 407 cases by 2030, 18 852 by 2040 and 20 082 by 2050. The number of cases in the Aomori Prefecture was projected to increase to 21 238 by 2030, 24 043 by 2040 and 26 285 by 2050.

By 2030, the demand-supply ratio multiplier (demand divided by supply, indexed to 2022) was projected to range from approximately 1.0 to 1.3 in most prefectures, indicating an increase in the expected cataract surgery workload per ophthalmologist at the national level relative to 2022. Relatively lower ratio multipliers were projected for metropolitan areas, including Tokyo (1.10), Kanagawa (1.07), Osaka (1.08) and Aichi (1.06). Conversely, relatively higher ratio multipliers were projected for regional prefectures such as Iwate (1.31), Aomori (1.26) and Tokushima (1.19).

By 2040, the nationwide demand-supply ratio multiplier was predicted to increase further, with many prefectures exceeding 1.2. Among the major metropolitan areas, Tokyo (1.40), Kanagawa (1.29), Osaka (1.31) and Aichi (1.26) were projected to maintain relatively lower multipliers compared with other regions. Conversely, substantially higher multipliers were observed in Iwate (1.80), Aomori (1.63), Tokushima (1.45) and Oita (1.47).

By 2050, the demand-supply ratio multiplier was predicted to rise markedly nationwide, exceeding 1.5 in many prefectures. While major metropolitan areas such as Tokyo (1.73), Kanagawa (1.48), Osaka (1.54) and Aichi (1.48) continued to show relatively moderate increases, Iwate Prefecture was projected to have the highest multiplier at 2.53. Elevated multipliers were also projected for Aomori Prefecture (2.07), Tokushima Prefecture (1.78) and Oita Prefecture (1.89).

Based on these results, the demand-supply ratio multiplier was predicted to increase progressively nationwide. While relatively lower multipliers were observed in major metropolitan areas, higher multipliers were observed in some prefectures in the Tohoku, Shikoku and Kyushu regions.

Discussion

This study estimated future demand for cataract surgery by age and sex based on NDB open data and future population projections. The number of cataract surgeries nationwide increased from fiscal years 2017 to 2022, with particularly significant increases in the 70s and 80+age groups. However, the total number of ophthalmologists showed only moderate growth, with decreases projected in some prefectures. Consequently, the projected nationwide demand-supply ratio multiplier gradually declined, with larger declines observed in regional areas. Conversely, the projected rate of decline in regional areas remained relatively higher than that in major metropolitan areas. These findings support the study’s hypothesis that future demand-supply gap between urban and rural areas will emerge, leading to increasing medical resource shortages and an inability to meet surgical demand in rural areas.

Regarding demand, the number of surgeries nationwide was consistently projected to increase by 2030, 2040 and 2050, with the 70s and 80+age groups dominating. Cataracts are an age-related disease with prevalence increasing with age. The observed increase in demand among older individuals (70s–80s) in this study and the prediction of continued increases were consistent with prior reports.6 Furthermore, the finding that the number of surgeries in women exceeded that in men in every age group aligned with reports indicating a relatively larger older female population in Japan.7 Regionally, the number of surgeries was particularly high in major metropolitan areas such as Tokyo, Osaka, Kanagawa and Aichi, possibly owing to population size, well-developed surgical infrastructure and patient influx from surrounding prefectures. Conversely, even in prefectures experiencing population decline, such as Aomori and Akita, the future number of surgeries was projected to increase, suggesting that the absolute number of surgeries will increase owing to an increase in eligible patients from ageing populations, even as total populations decrease.7 20

While the national total was projected to increase from 13 554 in 2022 to 16 017 in 2050, a decrease in ophthalmologist numbers was predicted in prefectures such as Aomori, Iwate, Tokushima and Tottori. An increase was anticipated in major metropolitan areas (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Osaka, Aichi, Saitama and Chiba), suggesting the likely continuation of physician concentration in urban areas. Previous research reports that after the introduction of the initial clinical training system in 2004, the ‘greater increases in ophthalmologist supply in low-density regions’ trend persisted, resulting in a gradual narrowing of regional supply disparities between 1998 and 2012; however, for physicians in specialties other than ophthalmology, concentrations in high-density areas intensified after the introduction, widening disparities.21 The future projections in this study indicated that despite this past trend of narrowing disparities, a decrease in ophthalmologists is anticipated in some prefectures, whereas increases are likely to continue in metropolitan areas. Attention must be paid to the risk of disparities widening again, driven by factors such as the retirement of older physicians and a reduction in the number of ophthalmology facilities.

The demand-supply ratio increased in all prefectures, with larger increases observed in regional areas, whereas metropolitan areas maintained relatively lower values. The larger increase in regional areas was likely due to the relatively rapid progression of ageing, combined with stagnant or declining physician numbers. The relatively lower demand-supply ratios maintained in metropolitan areas could be attributed to a steady increase in supply, coupled with a greater number of surgical facilities.

The novelty of this study lies in its integration of demand and supply data at the same unit level (prefecture×age group×sex), presenting future projections for each prefecture using both demand-supply and demand-supply ratio multipliers. This contrasts with previous research that has primarily focused on describing past trends in surgical procedures or physician distribution. Particularly significant is its quantitative demonstration of the ‘demand-supply divergence’ where demand may increase even in shrinking prefectures while physician numbers decline in some areas. This provides objective numerical indicators crucial for prioritisation and resource allocation in future regional healthcare planning.22

In terms of policy implications, while the demand-supply ratio multiplier was projected to increase nationwide by 2050, the increase was markedly steeper in some regional prefectures. Without effective countermeasures, the gap between supply and demand is expected to further widen. Although we evaluated prefectures, this trend is likely to be more pronounced at the secondary medical care area level, such as in depopulated regions within prefectures. More effective responses to access delays, prolonged waiting times and widening regional disparities are required.

This study has some limitations. First, the evaluation relied on a demand-supply ratio multiplier based on a 2022 ratio calculated independently from demand (implementation rate derived from billing frequency and future population) and supply (future estimates of ophthalmologist numbers) using publicly available government data. Thus, these approximations might not fully capture actual surgical capacity or potential demand from patients not reflected in billing figures. Second, the unit of analysis was the prefecture level. Consequently, we could not evaluate disparities within smaller regions such as secondary medical care areas or municipalities or assess patient inflows and outflows across prefectural borders. Third, as demand was based on billed procedures, bilateral surgeries and reoperations on the same patient could not be identified; thus, the number of billed procedures might not correspond with patient numbers. In addition, demand estimation applied national age-specific and sex-specific surgery rates uniformly across prefectures, and regional variation in practice patterns could not be fully captured. Fourth, supply was represented by the number of physicians. Not all ophthalmologists perform cataract surgery, and the number of surgeries performed per physician varies according to age, employment status and subspecialty, leading to possible underestimation or overestimation of actual supply capacity. Although alternative proxy variables and scenario assumptions—such as the number of surgical facilities, historical cataract volume per ophthalmologist or an ‘active surgeon proportion’—were considered, such information could not be consistently obtained from publicly available datasets at the prefectural level in Japan. Finally, projections for 2030, 2040 and 2050 were based on the extrapolation of past trends. Uncertainty in long-term projections was evaluated using a bootstrap-based approach, which indicated that the forecasts were associated with a certain degree of uncertainty. Nevertheless, future policy changes and technological innovations may alter these forecasts.

In conclusion, we estimated future demand for cataract surgery and ophthalmologist supply by prefecture using public statistics, evaluating both through demand-supply ratio and demand-supply ratio multiplier (based on 2022 figures). Demand was projected to increase through 2030, 2040 and 2050, with the greatest concentration among individuals in their 70s and 80s. Although supply was expected to gradually increase nationwide, a decrease was anticipated in some regional prefectures. Consequently, the demand-supply ratio multiplier was expected to increase nationwide, with particularly pronounced increases in rural areas, suggesting a widening gap relative to urban areas. Measures to prevent delays in access and prolonged waiting times must be considered in prefectures with larger increases. Future research should focus on evaluating demand-supply balance in more finely delineated geographical areas and incorporate physician practice patterns and patient flow to better identify areas at highest risk of service shortages.
