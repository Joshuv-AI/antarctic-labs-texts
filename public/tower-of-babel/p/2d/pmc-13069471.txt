Effectiveness of a Parent–Child Interaction Therapy‐Based Trauma‐Focused Interaction Module for Trauma‐Exposed Children: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Fatmanur Çimen, İsmail Seçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite the high prevalence of posttraumatic symptoms in early childhood, there remain significant gaps in clearly defined or widely accepted treatments specifically adapted for young children. This shortcoming highlights the urgent need for developmentally sensitive, evidence‐based psychological interventions targeting children who have experienced trauma during early childhood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a Trauma‐Focused Interaction module integrated into Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (TFI‐PCIT) and to preliminarily evaluate its effectiveness in reducing trauma‐related symptoms and behavioural difficulties in traumatized children aged 2–8 years, as well as its impact on parental stress and burnout.MethodA fully integrated mixed‐methods randomized controlled design was employed. A total of 19 parent–child dyads were assigned through stratified block randomization to an intervention group (n = 9) or control group (n = 10). The intervention group received the TFI‐PCIT module, while the control group received no intervention during the study period. Between‐group differences were analysed using linear mixed models, and within‐group changes were examined with independent samples t‐tests. Postintervention qualitative interviews were conducted to deepen interpretation of outcomes.ResultsCompared to controls, children who received TFI‐PCIT demonstrated statistically significant reductions in trauma symptoms and behavioural problems. Parents in the intervention group showed significant decreases in parenting stress and burnout. Qualitative findings reinforced quantitative results, indicating improved emotional regulation and strengthened parent–child interactions.ConclusionThese findings provide preliminary evidence that TFI‐PCIT may be a promising, developmentally sensitive intervention for young traumatized children and their caregivers.

Introduction

Trauma refers to exposure to an extraordinary event that disrupts an individual's emotional, cognitive and behavioural integrity and threatens life or physical safety. One of its most common and potentially long‐lasting consequences is posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). According to the DSM‐5 (APA 2014; Garland 2018; Bryant 2019), PTSD is characterized by trauma exposure, intrusive re‐experiencing, persistent avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood and heightened arousal lasting longer than 1 month. PTSD is highly prevalent among young children. A meta‐analysis by Woolgar et al. (2022) reported a prevalence rate of 21.5% among trauma‐exposed preschoolers. Similarly, Çimen et al. (2025) found that 2 years after an earthquake, 26.0% of children and adolescents exhibited moderate and 43.8% exhibited high levels of PTSD symptoms.

Professional guidelines emphasize that trauma treatment in early childhood should integrate comorbid conditions, prioritize trauma‐focused psychotherapies and actively involve caregivers (Willheim and Schechter 2025). Parent involvement is particularly critical because trauma often disrupts the parent–child relationship and alters parenting behaviours. Caregivers may respond to trauma‐related behaviours with harsh discipline or reduced emotional support (Chaffin et al. 2004). However, strengthening the parent–child relationship can function as a protective buffer against trauma‐related difficulties (Chaffin et al. 2011; Cinamon et al. 2021). Effective parent‐inclusive interventions commonly include psychoeducation about trauma, strategies to enhance children's emotion regulation and safety, modelling of supportive parenting behaviours and promotion of secure attachment (De Young et al. 2011). These components are grounded in attachment theory and social learning principles, suggesting that changes in parental responsiveness and modelling lead to improvements in child behaviour and emotional functioning (Tavkar and Hansen 2011; De Young et al. 2011).

Several evidence‐based parent–child interventions have been developed for traumatized children. Trauma‐Focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (TF‐CBT) is widely recognized as a first‐line treatment and includes structured trauma processing components summarized under the PRACTICE model (Cohen and Gadassi 2018). Although TF‐CBT has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing PTSD symptoms and comorbid difficulties across childhood and adolescence (Canale et al. 2022; Gusler et al. 2023; Thielemann et al. 2022; Xian‐Yu et al. 2022), preschool children may lack the cognitive and verbal capacities required for some CBT components, limiting its developmental suitability (Gigengack et al. 2015; Morina et al. 2016). Child–Parent Psychotherapy (CPP), grounded in attachment theory, has strong empirical support for young children exposed to trauma (Lieberman et al. 2005; Lieberman et al. 2015). However, CPP typically extends over 10–12 months and lacks a highly structured, skill‐based protocol with objective mastery criteria (Reyes et al. 2017). Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch‐Up (ABC) has demonstrated strong evidence for infants up to 24 months, improving attachment security and emotion regulation (Dozier and Bernard 2017; CEBC 2021). Nevertheless, its application is limited to very young children. Child–Parent Relationship Therapy (CPRT) has shown benefits in enhancing parent empathy and reducing problem behaviours (Carnes‐Holt and Bratton 2014; CEBC 2021), yet it is not trauma‐specific and may be insufficient for addressing trauma‐related dysregulation (McLaughlin et al. 2012; Kerns et al. 2014). EMDR and play therapy are also widely used in practice; however, evidence for children under seven remains limited, and these approaches do not consistently integrate structured parent training components (Perilli et al. 2019; Cohen and Gadassi 2018). Collectively, although multiple interventions exist, there remains a need for a brief, structured, trauma‐informed, parent‐coached intervention specifically tailored to children aged 2–8.

Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) is a well‐established, parent‐coaching intervention developed for children aged 2–8 with internalizing and externalizing problems (Eyberg and Funderburk 2011). PCIT is grounded in attachment theory and social learning theory. Through Child‐Directed Interaction (CDI), it enhances parental warmth, sensitivity and secure attachment. Through Parent‐Directed Interaction (PDI), it applies principles of modelling, reinforcement and consistent discipline to reduce disruptive behaviour (Eyberg and Funderburk 2011; Batzer et al. 2018). Studies indicate that PCIT is effective for traumatized children in reducing behavioural difficulties (Eslinger et al. 2015; Allen et al. 2016; Hawk et al. 2020; Graziano et al. 2023). However, standard PCIT does not directly process trauma memories and lacks explicit trauma‐focused components, leading to criticism that it is not a distinct trauma treatment (Warner‐Metzger and Funderburk 2021). Existing trauma‐adapted PCIT applications are limited. Gurwitch and Warner‐Metzger (2022) introduced a Trauma‐Directed Interaction (TDI) module tested in a single‐case design, while Seçer et al. (2026) developed TND‐PCIT for children exposed specifically to natural disasters. These adaptations are either methodologically limited or context‐specific, leaving a gap for a developmentally sensitive, broadly applicable trauma‐focused module integrated within PCIT.

Addressing this gap is clinically and theoretically important. A structured Trauma‐Focused Interaction (TFI) module embedded within PCIT may combine the relational strengths of attachment‐based approaches with the behavioural skill acquisition principles of social learning theory, while explicitly targeting trauma symptoms. Such an approach may not only reduce children's trauma symptoms and behavioural problems but also decrease parental stress and burnout by enhancing parenting competence and strengthening parent–child interaction (Chaffin et al. 2011; Tavkar and Hansen 2011).

Accordingly, the present study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a newly developed TFI module integrated into PCIT for traumatized children aged 2–8. It is hypothesized that:Hypothesis 1 The PCIT‐TFI module will have a significant positive effect on children's trauma symptoms, behavioural problems, adjustment and emotion regulation. Hypothesis 2 The PCIT‐TFI module will significantly reduce parental stress and parental burnout.

Method

Study Design and Procedure

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Atatürk University (approval date/no: 2024, 04/03). In addition, formal permission was obtained to conduct screening procedures in preschool education institutions affiliated with the local education authorities and in Healthy Life Centers (HLCs) operating under the local health administration, from which the parent participants were recruited. These institutions were selected because they provide psychological counselling and support services to parents and families, ensuring access to individuals meeting the study criteria. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or primary caregivers, and verbal assent was obtained from children when developmentally appropriate. All data were anonymized and stored securely to ensure confidentiality.

This study was conducted with two groups: an experimental group and a control group, with traumatized children and their parents randomly assigned to each. While the TFI‐PCIT module was administered to the experimental group, no intervention was applied to the control group. Following the completion of the experimental process, the intervention was also offered to parents in the control group who were willing to receive it. The study employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design, ensuring high validity and reliability of the findings.

Prior to data collection, meetings were conducted with institutional administrators to explain the purpose and procedure of the study and to obtain approval for recruitment. Following institutional approval, informational meetings were held with parents who were referred by these institutions. During these meetings, participants were informed about the study, provided written consent and completed the assessment measures. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group. The experimental process of this study, which was designed in accordance with the RCT model, is visualized below (Figure 1).

Experimental procedure flow.

Participants in the experimental group who completed the pretest assessment began the intervention in the following week. After the completion of the intervention, the post‐test assessment was conducted. No intervention was applied to the control group; however, participants in this group took part in pre‐intervention and postintervention assessments at the same time points as families in the active treatment conditions, following the completion of their own pretest assessments. Four weeks (1 month) after the post‐test assessment, follow‐up measurements were collected from the parents. After the conclusion of the experimental procedure, participants in the control group were informed that they could receive the intervention if they wished to do so voluntarily.

Randomization

In assigning participants to groups, stratified randomization was first employed, followed by block randomization. Stratified randomization is used to ensure balance between groups in terms of key participant characteristics and to control for the effects of confounding variables. Due to the limited sample size, stratified randomization by child gender was not feasible.

Since this study was conducted with traumatized children, the level of trauma symptom scores was considered a potential confounding variable, and stratified randomization was used to control for its effect. Accordingly, measurement results from the CATS assessment were used as a reference in the randomization process (based on the predetermined cut‐off scores: for ages 3–6, 12–15 and 15+; for ages 7–17, 15–20 and 21+, forming two levels). Subsequently, block randomization was used to calculate all possible balanced combinations of participants within each group, and blocks were randomly selected to assign all participants to their respective groups. Block randomization is designed to randomly assign participants into groups of equal sample sizes, with the block size determined by the researcher. Until the intervention began, participants were not informed of their group assignment, thereby maintaining allocation concealment. Therapists were not blinded to group assignment.

Therapist Training

The intervention was delivered by two certified PCIT therapists (the first and second authors). Both authors initially completed a 10‐module online PCIT training programme to qualify as therapists. Following this, they participated in 40 h of theoretical and practical PCIT training and successfully completed two PCIT cases. The second author obtained the Certified PCIT Therapist credential from PCIT International on 31 March 2022, and the first author received the same certification on 14 September 2022. In addition, the second author holds the title of Certified PCIT Trainer from PCIT International and is actively involved in training PCIT therapists. To further enhance clinical expertise, the second author served as a visiting researcher at the University of Würzburg under the supervision of regional PCIT trainer Med. Dr. Wolfgang Briegel. The first author conducted and successfully completed their cases under the supervision of the second author. Throughout the intervention process, regular weekly supervision meetings were held under the supervision of the second author (Certified PCIT Trainer).

PCIT‐Based TFI‐PCIT Module

Material/Content

The PCIT intervention, implemented based on the standard PCIT protocol, also begins with the CDI phase. In this phase, parents who meet the specified competency criteria proceed either to the TFI or the PDI phase. Children with high levels of behavioural problems and low levels of compliance move on to the PDI phase, while children with fewer behavioural problems and higher compliance levels transition to the TFI phase. Therefore, the phase a child enters after CDI may vary depending on the level of compliance. In PCIT, the PDI phase, which includes effective discipline techniques, is applied to increase compliance in children with behavioural problems. For traumatized children who exhibit high levels of behavioural problems, transitioning to the TFI phase, which involves trauma‐focused content and requires the child to follow parental directives, may lead to noncompliance and lack of participation in activities due to their low level of compliance. This situation can negatively affect the implementation of activities in the TFI phase. Therefore, it becomes evident that in traumatized children with low compliance, behavioural problems should first be addressed to increase their compliance level.

The most important distinction of the TFI phase compared to other phases is its trauma‐focused nature. Parents who do not reach this level of proficiency in an activity cannot proceed to another activity and must repeat the relevant session. Additionally, unlike other phases, the TFI phase is not time‐limited; it is completed only when parents meet the established criteria, at which point they move on to the next phase. Implementation of the Module

The TFI‐PCIT intervention was implemented according to the standard PCIT protocol, with the TFI phase focusing on trauma‐specific parent–child interactions. The child's level of compliance was determined using the Eyberg Child Behaviour Inventory (ECBI) Intensity and Problem scales, parent reports and therapist evaluation. For example, children scoring above 114 on the ECBI Intensity scale were considered to exhibit significant problem behaviours and were directed to the PDI phase. Sessions were individualized based on child behavioural compliance and parent progress. Session frequency, duration and total intervention length for each case are detailed in Table A5. Therapist training, supervision and fidelity checks were maintained using DPICS‐4 (Dyadic Parent–Child Interaction Coding System—Fourth Edition), PDI Coding Sheet and TFI‐specific checklists to monitor adherence and competence.

Once parents are deemed suitable for participation in therapy, an instructional session is scheduled at an appropriate date. During this session, information is provided to the parent or parents about the phase they will attend with their child, and the skills expected to be practiced by the parent are explained and demonstrated through modelling. Following the initial instructional session, PCIT sessions begin with the CDI phase. Sessions continue until the parent reaches the competency criteria in the PRIDE (Praise, Reflection, Imitation, Description and Enthusiasm) skills. When the parent meets these criteria, both the child and the parent are assessed by the clinician to determine whether the therapy should proceed to the TFI or PDI phase. At this point, the child's behavioural problems serve as a key criterion. If the child completing the CDI phase is evaluated as having intense behavioural problems and low compliance, the PDI phase is initiated first, followed by the TFI phase. However, if the child is assessed as having low levels of behavioural problems and a high level of compliance, the TFI phase is applied directly after CDI. If necessary, the PDI phase may be implemented following the TFI phase. The child's level of compliance is determined by jointly considering measurement results obtained from the parent (ECBI Intensity and ECBI Problem scales), parent reports and the therapist's evaluation. Depending on the progress of the child and the parent, the TFI phase may last between four and seven sessions. The PDI phase also begins with a one‐session instructional meeting. Sessions continue until the parent learns effective discipline strategies and meets graduation criteria. To evaluate whether the parent has met the expertise criteria, session‐specific forms with differentiated content are used. These forms used across the CDI, PDI and TFI phases can be seen in Appendix (see Table A3).

The level of progress demonstrated by the parent throughout the sessions is monitored using the DPICS‐4 tool during each session. Additionally, in the PDI phase, the PDI Coding Form is used to assess parental competence, while in the TFI phase, specially developed checklists are employed to evaluate the parent's proficiency in implementing the activities. For a parent to be considered successful in the CDI phase, they must use at least 10 instances of the target skills—behaviour description, labelled praise and reflection—while using no more than 3 instances of the undesirable skills: questions, commands and critical talk. In the PDI phase, the commands given by the parent must possess the characteristics of effective commands, and the child must comply with at least 80% of these commands. In the TFI phase, to be deemed competent in the taught activities, the parent must not skip any required skill, must not receive a “not competent” label for any of the implemented skills and must receive a “competent” rating in at least 80% of the listed items. Coding was performed independently by two trained evaluators, and inter‐rater reliability for content coding was 85%. Graduated parents are periodically invited to follow‐up sessions for further assessment.

Data Collection Tools

DPICS‐4

It is the standard coding form used in every phase of the PCIT therapy process. The form consists of two sections that record both desired skills and undesired skills. It is applied during the first 5 min of each session to determine the parent's level of skill use and to assess whether therapeutic goals are being met.

Checklist Forms for the TFI Phase

These are coding forms developed by the researcher and used exclusively during the TFI phase of the PCIT therapy process. There are six different observation checklists used in this phase: ‘Colour Breathing Checklist, Emotion Support Session Checklist, Safety Perception Strengthening Session Checklists 1 and 2, Sands of Time Checklist and Let's Stay Inside Together Checklist’. These checklists contain items that indicate whether the parent successfully performed the required skills during the implementation of trauma‐specific activities in the TFI phase. The Sands of Time Checklist includes 16 items, the Let us Stay Inside Together Checklist contains 17 items and the Colour Breathing Checklist and Emotion Support Session Checklist each consists of 15 items. Lastly, both Safety Perception Strengthening Session Checklists 1 and 2 include 13 items. All forms within the TFI module share a common rating structure that assesses the parent's execution of skills across three categories: adequate, needs improvement and inadequate.

Data Collection Tools in Child Dimension

Eyberg Child Behaviour Inventory (ECBI)

The ECBI is a 36‐item parent report scale used to assess behavioural problems in children with behaviour‐related difficulties. It is applied throughout the entire treatment process and serves as a measure of the child's behavioural changes and thus the therapeutic progress achieved (Eyberg and Pincus 1999). The scale consists of two sections: Intensity and Problem. In the Intensity section, the severity of problem behaviours is rated on a scale from 1 to 7 (1: never, 7: always). The Problem section is a checklist where parents indicate whether each behaviour is a problem for them (yes/no). The adaptation study for Turkish culture was conducted with a sample of 812 children, and the scale was found to be a valid and reliable assessment tool (Seçer and Ulaş 2022).

Weekly Assessment of Child Behaviour‐Parent Report (WACB‐P)

The WACB‐P is a brief parent report measure developed to help monitor externalizing problems in children. The scale consists of nine items rated on a 7‐point Likert scale and is used by parents to report on their child's compliance behaviours. In this regard, it is utilized starting from the PDI phase—where discipline skills are taught—until the end of therapy (Timmer et al. 2021).

Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen—Caregiver Version (CATS‐C) (Ages 3–6 and 7–18)

The scale, developed by an international working group, has been made openly accessible for researchers around the world to use freely and without licence (Sachser et al. 2017). The Turkish adaptation for ages 3–6 was conducted by Akkuş et al. (2021), and the adaptation for ages 7–18 was conducted by Çimen et al. (2025). PTSD symptoms are measured with 20 items in a 4‐point Likert format. The reliability analysis (Cronbach's alpha) values for the four subdimensions of the scale were found to be α = 0.73, α = 0.62, α = 0.62 and α = 0.74, respectively. Finally, the overall reliability value of the scale was reported as α = 0.86 (Akkuş et al. 2021). For the version for ages 7–17, Cronbach's alpha reliability values for the subdimensions were found to range between 0.72 and 0.91, and it was reported to have good construct validity (Çimen et al. 2025).

Child Emotion Regulation Scale‐Parent Form

This is a parent report scale developed by Shields and Cicchetti (1997) and adapted into Turkish by Altan (2006). The scale consists of two subdimensions: lability/negativity and emotion regulation. It includes 24 items rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (1: rarely, 4: generally). Analyses revealed that the reliability coefficients were high for the lability/negativity (α = 0.96) and emotion regulation (α = 0.83) subdimensions, as well as for the overall scale (α = 0.84).

Data Collection Tools Used in the Parent Dimension

Parenting Stress Index

Originally developed by Abidin (1983) to assess stress within the parent–child system, the scale was adapted into Turkish by Mert et al. (2007). The scale consists of three subdimensions: parental distress, parent–child dysfunctional interaction and difficult child. It includes 36 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1: never, 5: always). Statistical analyses showed that the internal consistency reliability coefficients were sufficient for the subdimensions, parental distress (0.81), parent–child dysfunctional interaction (0.76) and difficult child (0.78), as well as for the overall scale (0.71). As a result, it has been reported that the scale is appropriate for use in identifying parental stress levels in Turkish culture.

Parental Burnout Scale

The scale used to assess the burnout levels of the parents participating in the study was developed by Roskam et al. (2018) and adapted into Turkish by Arikan et al. (2020). The scale consists of four dimensions and includes 23 items rated on a 7‐point Likert scale (0: never, 6: every day). The adaptation study reported that the model fit indices of the scale were adequate. In addition, reliability analyses showed that the internal consistency value for the overall scale was α = 0.90, and for the subdimensions, it ranged between 0.50 and 0.84. Therefore, the scale has been reported as a valid and reliable measurement tool in the Turkish cultural context (Arikan et al. 2020).

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM‐5 (PCL‐5)

The scale that assesses PTSD symptoms based on DSM‐5 was adapted into Turkish by Boysan et al. (2017). It consists of 20 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (0: not at all; 4: extremely) and includes 4 subdimensions. In the adaptation study, the reliability coefficients for all subdimensions were reported to be sufficient: re‐experiencing (0.79–0.92), avoidance (0.73–0.91), negative alterations in cognition and mood (0.85–0.90) and hyperarousal (0.81–0.88). The scale measures symptoms experienced in the past month and scores range from 0 to 80. The cut‐off score for a PTSD diagnosis was determined by the authors as 47 points (Boysan et al. 2017).

Data Analysis

During the experimental phase, data obtained from parent reports were analysed step by step. In this context, the first step was to examine whether the intervention and control groups differed in terms of initial demographic characteristics and initial dependent variable measurements using independent samples t‐tests (for continuous measures) and chi‐square tests (for categorical measures). The clinical significance of differences between phases was evaluated using Cohen's d. The following thresholds were used for interpreting effect size: “0.20 = small; 0.50 = medium; 0.80 = large” (Cohen 1988). This analysis aimed to assess whether randomization was successful between the intervention and control groups. Since there were no missing values in the dataset, missing data analysis was not conducted. Linear mixed‐effects models (LMM) were employed to examine changes over time (pretest, post‐test and follow‐up) and between groups (intervention vs. control) for all primary and secondary outcomes. Time was treated as a fixed effect, and participant‐level random intercepts were included to account for within‐subject correlations across repeated measurements (Shek and Ma 2011; Winter and Wieling 2016).

Following the completion of the experimental procedure, interviews conducted with parents were analysed using content analysis. The responses were examined in detail, coded and categorized by the first and second authors. Both coders were also the therapists who delivered the PCIT intervention. To minimize potential bias stemming from their involvement in the intervention, coding was conducted independently, and categories were developed inductively from the data rather than being derived from the study hypotheses or expected treatment outcomes. Inter‐rater agreement was calculated to enhance analytic rigour and was found to be 85%, indicating good agreement.

Results

Findings Obtained From the Experimental Procedure Phase

It is observed that there are no significant differences (p > 0.05) between participants in the intervention and control groups in terms of demographic variables as well as child and parent assessments. This finding indicates that the groups were well randomized at the outset and that potential confounding factors were minimized in evaluating the effectiveness of the intervention (see Table A4).

When examining the phases attended by participants who received the PCIT intervention, it is observed that they participated in CDI sessions ranging from five to nine sessions (X̄ = 7.2); those five cases attended PDI sessions ranging from four to five sessions (X̄ = 4.4), and finally, that they attended TFI sessions ranging from four to seven sessions (X̄ = 4.8). Five cases participated in all three phases—CDI, PDI and TFI—while four cases participated in only two phases—CDI and TFI (see Table A5).

Findings Related to Child Assessments

It is observed that there is a significant difference in the child assessments between the pretest and post‐test, as well as between the pretest and follow‐up measurements for the experimental group that received the PCIT intervention (p < 0.05). However, there is no significant difference between the post‐test and follow‐up measurements, indicating that the effect of the intervention was sustained (p > 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2).

Findings Obtained From Interviews Conducted After the Experimental Procedure

The qualitative analysis of parent interviews revealed several recurring themes reflecting perceived changes in both children and parents following the intervention. Themes were generated inductively through content analysis and represent patterns consistently identified across participants.

Child‐Related Themes

Parents' statements regarding changes in their children clustered under four main themes: emotion regulation, increased self‐confidence, self‐expression and reduced behavioural problems (see Table A6). Emotion regulation refers to improvements in children's ability to recognize, manage and respond to intense emotions—particularly anger, distress and frustration—in more adaptive ways. Parents frequently described reductions in self‐harming behaviours, emotional withdrawal and emotional dysregulation. For example, one parent stated:

When he got angry, he would scratch his face … Now, he no longer harms his face … When he gets angry, he practices breathing. (P7)

When he got angry, he would scratch his face … Now, he no longer harms his face … When he gets angry, he practices breathing. (P7)

Similarly, another parent described a noticeable shift from emotional numbness to affective responsiveness:

‘My daughter was very frozen before therapy … Even when she fell badly, she wouldn't cry’. (P5).

‘My daughter was very frozen before therapy … Even when she fell badly, she wouldn't cry’. (P5).

Increased self‐confidence reflects children's enhanced sense of competence, persistence and willingness to try tasks independently. Parents described observable increases in autonomy and resilience when facing challenges. One parent noted:

Previously, we would think ‘He can't do it,’ but now we see that he can, and we give him the opportunity to try. (P3)

Previously, we would think ‘He can't do it,’ but now we see that he can, and we give him the opportunity to try. (P3)

Another parent emphasized change in frustration tolerance:

In the first session, when the tower collapsed, he asked if I was going to cry. Now, even if it collapses, we just build it again. (P9)

In the first session, when the tower collapsed, he asked if I was going to cry. Now, even if it collapses, we just build it again. (P9)

Self‐expression (emotions and thoughts) refers to improvements in children's verbalization of internal experiences rather than expressing distress through aggression, withdrawal, or silence. Many parents reported that their children became more communicative and emotionally articulate. As one parent explained:

Instead of shouting or acting aggressively, he now says, ‘I'm sorry, forgive me.’ He expresses his feelings verbally. (P3)

Instead of shouting or acting aggressively, he now says, ‘I'm sorry, forgive me.’ He expresses his feelings verbally. (P3)

Another parent described increased emotional awareness:

She now recognizes anger, fear, and worry … When she's happy, she tells me. (P4)

She now recognizes anger, fear, and worry … When she's happy, she tells me. (P4)

Reduced behavioural problems capture decreases in oppositional behaviours, noncompliance, excessive crying and rule‐breaking. Parents frequently linked these improvements to clearer boundaries and more consistent parent–child interactions. For instance,

He argues less … He follows the rules and even warns others who don't. (P1)

He argues less … He follows the rules and even warns others who don't. (P1)

Another parent stated:

She doesn't cry to get what she wants anymore. (P6)

She doesn't cry to get what she wants anymore. (P6)

Parent‐Related Themes

Parents' reflections on their own experiences were grouped under three primary themes: positive mental health, increased self‐efficacy and increased communication and interaction (see Table A7). Positive mental health refers to reductions in parental guilt, stress, hopelessness and emotional burden, as well as increased relief and psychological well‐being. Several parents described emotional exhaustion prior to treatment and relief afterward. One parent expressed:

When I first came, I hated myself. I thought I was a bad mother … Now I see myself as a very good mother. (P4)

When I first came, I hated myself. I thought I was a bad mother … Now I see myself as a very good mother. (P4)

Another stated:

Even just talking every week was relieving for us. (P7)

Even just talking every week was relieving for us. (P7)

Increased self‐efficacy reflects parents' strengthened confidence in managing their child's behaviours and navigating challenging situations. Parents frequently described gaining new skills and feeling more competent. For example:

Now when something negative happens, I know I can manage it better. (P1)

Now when something negative happens, I know I can manage it better. (P1)

Another parent noted:

Before, I didn't know what to do … Now I know the right way. (P7)

Before, I didn't know what to do … Now I know the right way. (P7)

Increased communication and interaction refer to improvements in emotional sharing, mutual understanding and quality of parent–child interaction. Parents emphasized greater emotional closeness and dialogue. As one parent stated:

We increased our emotional sharing. We started talking about feelings like happiness and sadness. (P1)

We increased our emotional sharing. We started talking about feelings like happiness and sadness. (P1)

Another parent explained:

Now I can understand him better … I encourage him to speak more openly. (P2)

Now I can understand him better … I encourage him to speak more openly. (P2)

Overall, the qualitative findings suggest that the intervention was associated with perceived improvements in children's emotional and behavioural functioning as well as enhanced parental psychological well‐being and relational competence.

Conclusion and Discussion

This study represents the first attempt to implement PCIT with a trauma‐specific TFI module for young children exposed to diverse traumatic experiences such as loss, grief, divorce and domestic violence. The findings indicate that children who received TFI‐PCIT demonstrated significant reductions in PTSD symptoms, improvements in emotion regulation, reductions in behavioural problems and enhanced overall adjustment. In addition, parents reported decreased parenting stress and burnout. These results suggest that TFI‐PCIT is a promising intervention for children affected by these types of trauma. Consistent with prior research, the buffering effect of a strong parent–child relationship supports the reduction of trauma symptoms in children (Chaffin et al. 2011; Cinamon et al. 2021).

The findings of this study align with and extend prior research on trauma‐focused interventions for young children. Unlike Trauma‐Focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (TF‐CBT), which primarily emphasizes trauma narration and cognitive restructuring, TFI‐PCIT integrates trauma processing within a dyadic parent–child framework that emphasizes emotion regulation and secure attachment. Compared to CPP, which also targets relational trauma, TFI‐PCIT incorporates live coaching and structured behavioural components that may facilitate more rapid improvements in externalizing behaviours.

Equipping parents with the knowledge and skills to foster secure attachment and manage disruptive behaviours is crucial, as children with secure attachments and fewer behavioural difficulties demonstrate greater resilience following trauma (De Young et al. 2011; McLaughlin and Lambert 2017). In line with the literature, our findings suggest that increased positive parenting practices and decreased negative practices are associated with reduced trauma responses, particularly externalizing behaviours and difficulties in self‐regulation (Kim‐Spoon et al. 2012). In this study, the combined PCIT and TFI module significantly reduced behavioural problems and enhanced child adjustment. The reduction in PTSD symptoms may have occurred through improvements in emotional security, parental responsiveness and children's regulatory capacities.

Standard PCIT emphasizes positive parent–child interactions and includes the PDI phase, which teaches effective discipline skills, including rule‐setting and monitoring adherence (Eyberg and Funderburk 2011). These components contribute to improvements in behavioural regulation and adjustment (McNeil and Hembree‐Kigin 2010; Thomas et al. 2017; Williford et al. 2019; Fleming et al. 2021; Onovbiona et al. 2023). Such skills are especially important for traumatized children, as trauma often manifests behaviourally (e.g., aggression, impulsivity and distractibility) (Lubit et al. 2003; Menand and Cox 2022). In addition to reducing disruptive behaviours, the PDI phase may indirectly contribute to reductions in PTSD symptoms by increasing structure, predictability and consistency in the home environment. For trauma‐exposed children, a more predictable and controlled caregiving context may enhance perceived safety and reduce hypervigilance and emotional reactivity. Prior studies similarly report that PCIT effectively reduces disruptive behaviours and enhances parent–child relationships among trauma‐exposed children (Cohen et al. 2004; Chaffin et al. 2011; Thomas and Zimmer‐Gembeck 2012; Pearl et al. 2012; Allen et al. 2014; Mersky et al. 2015; Costello et al. 2016; Lieneman et al. 2019). However, because PTSD symptoms were not assessed after completion of the PDI module and prior to implementation of the TFI module, the unique contribution of the TFI component beyond standard PCIT cannot be definitively determined. This measurement limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The TFI module specifically targets emotion regulation, a critical area for traumatized children. Our findings demonstrate significant improvements in children's emotion regulation skills following the intervention, which may represent a key mechanism underlying reductions in PTSD symptoms. The literature corroborates these effects, noting that PCIT can enhance children's ability to identify, label and express emotions (Rothenberg et al. 2019; Quetsch et al. 2018; Gurwitch and Warner‐Metzger 2022; Warren et al. 2022). Parents are trained to validate children's emotions rather than correct them, further supporting emotional awareness and resilience (Lieberman et al. 2015; Cohen et al. 2017; Gurwitch 2018). Although the trauma‐specific content of the TFI module primarily consisted of psychoeducation, the coping‐oriented components (e.g., breathing exercises, emotion coaching and co‐regulation strategies) may reduce PTSD symptoms by decreasing physiological arousal and strengthening children's capacity to manage trauma‐related distress.

Another key outcome is the reduction in parental stress and burnout. Given the well‐established association between parenting factors and child outcomes (Overbeek et al. 2017), interventions targeting parents are critical. While standard PCIT is known to reduce parental stress (Warren et al. 2022; Allen et al. 2014), this study extends the evidence by showing that TFI‐PCIT also decreases parental burnout. Parent interviews indicated that improvements in children's trauma symptoms, behavioural problems, adjustment and emotion regulation, as well as increased parental self‐efficacy and therapist support, contributed to reductions in stress and burnout. Parents participating in behavioural parent training programmes learn to manage their children's behaviours by modifying their own verbal expressions and actions. A decrease in parenting stress is expected in parents who manage their children's behaviours more effectively, as a result of the reduction in children's behavioural problems, which also enhances parents' distress tolerance (Hajal and Paley 2020). Studies in the literature report that PCIT interventions, both standard and combined with trauma modules, significantly reduce parental stress (Warren et al. 2022; Warren et al. 2024; Tehrani et al. 2024; Allen et al. 2014; Cooley et al. 2014; Patel et al. 2022). Furthermore, in the context of trauma, parents may themselves have experienced trauma or be under stress due to their child's experiences. The TFI module provides parents with knowledge and skills to respond effectively to children's traumatic reactions and receive immediate feedback, thereby enhancing parental self‐efficacy (Gurwitch and Warner‐Metzger 2022; Vaydich and Cheung 2023). Minimizing and managing parental stress are critically important to reduce parental burnout and ensure family well‐being.

The present findings suggest that the reconstruction of perceived safety within the parent–child relationship may represent a central mechanism of change in TFI‐PCIT. The re‐establishment of perceived safety likely facilitates the development of emotion regulation capacities. When children experience their caregivers as predictable, attuned and protective, physiological and emotional arousal can be modulated more effectively. In this framework, improved emotion regulation functions as a downstream mechanism that contributes to reductions in trauma‐related symptoms and externalizing behaviours. As child dysregulation decreases, parental stress and burnout are subsequently reduced, creating a reciprocal cycle of relational stabilization (TFI‐PCIT enhances parental sensitivity → strengthened child perceived safety and improved emotion regulation capacity → reduced PTSD symptoms and behavioural problems → decreased parental stress and burnout).

These findings have several clinical implications. TFI‐PCIT appears particularly suitable for preschool‐aged children exposed to interpersonal or relational trauma. Mental health practitioners working in early childhood settings, family counselling centres and community mental health services may consider integrating TFI‐PCIT protocols when trauma symptoms co‐occur with behavioural dysregulation. Given the live coaching format, clinicians require formal PCIT training and additional trauma‐focused adaptation training to ensure fidelity. The structured, session‐based format may make TFI‐PCIT feasible for implementation within outpatient community settings.

Policymakers should consider supporting training programmes that equip clinicians with dyadic trauma‐focused competencies. Early identification and intervention through community‐based services, such as Healthy Life Centers and preschool institutions, may help prevent chronic psychopathology and reduce long‐term societal costs associated with untreated childhood trauma.

Limitations and Suggestions

The TFI‐PCIT module was implemented with nine trauma‐exposed cases (e.g., maltreatment and parental divorce) in the experimental group, all of whom could participate in face‐to‐face therapy. Due to the small sample size and focus on specific trauma types, not all forms of trauma (e.g., sexual abuse) were examined. The small sample size reduced statistical power and limited the ability to conduct module‐specific or mediation analyses to determine which intervention components were most strongly associated with changes in PTSD symptoms. In addition, the use of a no‐treatment control group controls for the passage of time but does not control for nonspecific treatment factors such as expectancy effects, placebo effects or the positive attention of caring adults. While the lack of therapist and assessor blinding is acknowledged as a limitation, the mixed‐methods design and rigorous experimental procedures strengthen the validity of the findings. An additional limitation is the exclusion of parents with clinically significant PTSD symptoms to ensure stable therapy participation. This resulted in a relatively selective sample, limiting generalizability to families where caregivers have higher trauma burdens. Finally, the limited number of certified PCIT therapists in Turkey at the time prevented blinding of therapists to treatment conditions and qualitative data analysis. Reflexive strategies were employed to mitigate bias, but potential influences cannot be entirely ruled out. Long‐term follow‐up (6–12 months) and hybrid or online delivery methods should be considered in future studies to assess the sustainability and scalability of TFI‐PCIT outcomes and to more clearly disentangle the specific mechanisms responsible for reductions in PTSD symptoms. Moreover, the findings suggest potential implications for mental health policy and service provision. Integrating trauma‐focused parent–child interventions like TFI‐PCIT into early childhood mental health services could reduce the long‐term impact of trauma and support both children and caregivers in high‐risk populations. Comparative studies with other trauma‐focused interventions and the inclusion of active control conditions are recommended to situate TFI‐PCIT within the broader evidence base and clarify its unique contribution relative to standard PCIT. Future clinical applications could include hybrid or online delivery formats to increase accessibility and scalability.
