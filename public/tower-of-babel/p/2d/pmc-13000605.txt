Chelating-Assisted Green Synthesis of TiO2 Nanoparticles from Mangosteen Pericarp for Defect Control and DSSC Performance
Authors: Nofrijon Sofyan, Muhammad, Fiona Angellinnov, Mouna M’rad, Aga Ridhova, Akhmad H. Yuwono, Donanta Dhaneswara, Brian Yuliarto, Veinardi Suendo, Lydia H. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work aims to examine the properties of titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) synthesized through a sustainable, chelating-assisted green process using mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana L.) pericarp extracts for use as a semiconductor layer in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs). X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization revealed that the TiO2 NPs have a tetragonal crystalline structure and are pure anatase. The optical characteristics examined using ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–Vis DRS) showed a reduced bandgap energy, from 3.22 to 3.20 eV, with the use of mangosteen pericarp extract. The same trend is observed with the use of chelating agent concentration, decreasing from 3.20 to 3.10 eV at 30%, then increasing slightly to 3.14 eV at 50%. The TiO2 NPs were further used as a semiconductor layer in a DSSC device sensitized with the commercial dye N719 and a natural dye derived from Malabar spinach (Basella rubra L.) fruit extract. The highest efficiency of 2.34% was achieved with a DSSC device fabricated from green-synthesized TiO2 NPs using mangosteen pericarp extracted with 50% acetylacetone (AcAc) and sensitized with N719, slightly lower than that of commercial TiO2 P25 (2.65%). The efficiencies of the same materials sensitized with natural dyes from Malabar spinach fruit extract are 0.69% and 1.01%, respectively. These results demonstrate the potential of using mangosteen pericarp waste and natural dyes for the sustainable, cost-effective fabrication of DSSCs.

Introduction

As fossil fuel reserves continue to decline, transitioning to renewable energy sources has become essential for ensuring long-term environmental sustainability. One way to achieve this goal is to switch to renewable energy and use abundant solar radiation with solar-harvesting devices. Among various solar energy technologies, dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) have emerged as a promising third-generation photovoltaic platform due to their low production cost, ease of fabrication, and eco-friendly materials. However, their relatively low power conversion efficiency remains a key limitation.

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is widely used as a photoanode material in DSSCs because of its excellent optoelectronic properties, chemical stability, and environmental safety. These properties, however, depend on the crystallographic phase, such as anatase, rutile, and brookite; which, in turn, rely on their synthesis routes. There are many routes available to synthesize TiO2 NPs. Top-down, conventional approaches in synthesizing TiO2 NPs include lithography, laser ablation, and mechanical techniques. Although these approaches are relatively simple, they consume substantial energy and entail high initial costs. In contrast, the bottom-up approach via growing and self-assembling atoms and molecules, such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD), hydrothermal, and the conventional sol–gel method, , results in well-defined shapes and sizes, a narrow nanoparticle size distribution, and cost-effectiveness. However, these techniques have some disadvantages, including complex chemistry and the generation of toxic chemical waste. Therefore, many researchers have sought ways to address these weaknesses, including environmentally friendly methods such as green synthesis.

Green synthesis is an environmentally friendly method for producing nanomaterials using natural resources, such as plant extracts. , This method has attracted the attention of many researchers due to its simple protocol, low cost, and environmental friendliness and sustainability. The main reason is that the process involves only a nontoxic solvent medium, a reducing agent, and precursors, in addition to its energy efficiency, product selectivity, and safety for human health and the environment. During synthesis, the plant extract serves not only as a reducing agent for the precursor but also as a capping agent to prevent nanoparticle agglomeration. Several plant extracts have been used for green-synthesizing metal oxide nanoparticles, such as mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana) pericarp, , Morus nigra leaves, Terminalia catappa fruits, , Carissa carandas, Bixa orellana seeds, Jasminum sambac, , Citrus limon, gambier leaves, oil palm leaves, Saccharum officinarrum, and Calotropis gigantea. Mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana) pericarp, a phytochemical-rich fruit waste, is particularly attractive due to its high content of xanthones, anthocyanins, and phenolic compounds. Although it has been used in the green synthesis of TiO2 for pollutant degradation, antioxidant, and as a natural sensitizer in DSSC, its potential for producing TiO2 nanoparticles, combined with chelating agents and tailored for DSSC applications, remains underexplored.

Chelating agents play a crucial role in controlling the hydrolysis and condensation of titanium precursors, particularly in sol–gel synthesis. They regulate the hydrolysis and condensation rates of titanium alkoxide precursors, thereby enabling better control over nanoparticle size, morphology, and phase composition. Siwińska-Stefańska et al. demonstrated that AcAc-modified sol–gel routes produce TiO2 powders with greater uniformity and improved phase control, particularly when used with catalysts such as ammonia. Orchard et al. showed that catechol binds to TiO2 via bidentate chelation at surface Ti sites, forming robust Ti–O–C linkages that withstand aqueous operation and light exposure. This geometry creates strong orbital overlap between the catechol π-system and TiO2 surface states, enabling rapid interfacial electron transfer and directional charge flowboth critical for photocatalysis and photoelectrochemistry. Bellè et al. also demonstrated that chelating agents enhance coating durability and photocatalytic performance by stabilizing the sol and promoting film formation. Recently, Lo Porto et al. showed that surface modification of TiO2 nanorods with organic shells, including chelating ligands, can shift the balance between dye adsorption and photodegradation, thereby enhancing adsorption and optimizing photodegradation.

In parallel, a variety of synthetic dyes are utilized as sensitizers in DSSC devices. These include metallic complexes, porphyrins, and organic molecules, which have improved conversion efficiencies. These synthetic dyes, however, have several weaknesses, including high production costs, potential metal scarcity, and significant environmental impact. On the other hand, natural sensitizers derived from dyes harvested from plant parts can be easily prepared and extracted at low production cost, and, most importantly, are nontoxic and environmentally friendly. Several natural dyes extracted from plants or insects have been used as sensitizers in DSSCs. Sofyan et al. used a natural dye extracted from mangosteen pericarp and Curcuma longa. Kocak and Yildiz used carminic acid extracted from the cochineal insect, Ferreira et al. used the extracts from the petals of Lantana repens and Solidago canadensis flowers, dos Santos et al. used various color petals of the Gerbera jamesonii flower dyes, da Cunha et al. used natural dyes from scarlet eggplant, pitomba, and black grapes, and Vitis labrusca extract. Malabar spinach (Basella rubra) fruits, rich in betalains, are a promising yet underutilized source of natural dyes. While previous studies have employed this extract in DSSCs, , its integration with green-synthesized TiO2 remains unexplored.

This study reports a green, chelation-assisted synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles from a mangosteen pericarp waste extract. The synthesized TiO2 NPs were then used as photoanodes in DSSCs sensitized with Malabar spinach fruit extract and compared with devices using the commercial dye N719. The novelty of this work lies in three specific aspects. First, a chelation-assisted synthesis is demonstrated in which naturally derived ligands regulate nucleation and growth, thereby enabling defect control at the TiO2 surface. Second, the defect states are explicitly correlated with charge-transfer resistance and electron lifetime, providing mechanistic insight into recombination dynamics. Third, clear structure–property–performance relationships are established by linking synthesis conditions to impedance parameters and photovoltaic outputs. This integrated approach moves beyond reporting a green route and offers a deeper understanding of how defect engineering and chelation chemistry translate into DSSC performance.

Results and Discussion

Infrared Characteristics

The identification of functional groups in a molecule can be achieved by measuring its infrared spectrum and using the resulting information to determine the presence of these groups. The infrared spectra of the mangosteen pericarp extract obtained with different solvents and chelating agent concentrations are shown in Figure (a). The natural dye from Malabar spinach fruit extract and 0.8 mM commercial dye N719 for comparison are given in Figure (b). The infrared spectra of TiO2 NPs obtained during synthesis, along with those of commercial P25 as a control, were also recorded to examine the effect of the green medium, and the spectra are presented in Figure (c). The infrared spectrum of mangosteen pericarp extract shows several absorption bands. As shown in the figure, all spectra exhibit the appearance of hydrogen-bonded OH stretching bands, ranging from 3600 to 3000 cm–1, and bands from around 2973 to 2878 cm–1, which represent the stretching of the C–H groups. The band at 1640 cm–1 indicates a CO stretching vibration; it is small, except for the natural dye extracted with ethanol containing AcAc. The negligible absorption of C–N bond is detected at 1450 cm–1, an aromatic compound is detected at 1423–1274 cm–1, the C–O bond is detected at 1045, stretching vibration of the C–O–C esters is detected at 1046 cm–1, and the presence of aldehydes is detected between 880 cm–1 and 800 cm–1. These functional groups, specifically in the form of carboxyl or hydroxyl groups, support the presence of active compounds such as xanthone and mangostin in the extracts, , and are expected to interact and bind onto the TiO2 surface and affect the growth and formation of TiO2 NPs during the synthesis.

The commercial dye N719 exhibits a broad signal at 3600–3000 cm–1, indicating the presence of the O–H group, a characteristic of phenol compounds, as reported in our previous work. The presence of aromatic compound ring with CC bond is detected at 1570 cm–1, typical of alkane compound with C–H stretching is detected at 1417 cm–1, typical of alcohol with weak intensity C–O stretching is detected at 1181–1069 cm–1, typical of alkene compound with CC bending is detected at 872–751 cm–1. Furthermore, a characteristic absorption of flavonoids is detected at 3366 cm–1, 1650 cm–1, and 679 cm–1. Similarly, an intense and broad signal absorption band at 3600–3000 cm–1 is also shown by Malabar fruit extract due to the presence of −OH, while C–H stretching, O–H stretching, and C–H bending vibrations are detected at 2930, 1615, and 1383 cm–1, respectively. Others have also detected these absorption bands. , The interaction between these functional groups and TiO2 NPs is expected to drive electron transfer from the dye molecules to the semiconductor’s conduction band.

As shown in Figure (c), TiO2 P25 reveals weak absorption by stretching vibrations of hydrogen-bonded water molecules and hydroxyl groups, bending vibrations of the O–H group, and the Ti–O–Ti bridging stretching mode at 3600–3000, 1630, and 650 cm–1, respectively. The detection of surface hydroxyl (−OH) groups in TiO2 nanoparticles (NPs) is anticipated, as these functionalities readily form through interactions with ambient moisture. , Such features are consistently observed in TiO2 NPs synthesized via conventional ethanol-based routes and via green synthesis at varying chelating-agent concentrations. Interestingly, samples M30 and M50 exhibit an additional absorption band at 2326 cm–1. Although this band could, in principle, be linked to O–C–O stretching vibrations arising from AcAc-derived residues, the likelihood of such organic remnants persisting is minimal, given that calcination at 450 °C typically decomposes most organic species. Instead, this spectral region is well-known to overlap with atmospheric CO2 vibrations, a common artifact in FTIR measurements when background subtraction or purging is incomplete. Since the spectra were acquired under ambient conditions, the observed band is more plausibly attributed to CO2 interference, in agreement with prior reports.

Beyond this artifact, it is important to note that residual carbon incorporation into TiO2 lattices or surfaceseven in trace amountscan significantly influence material properties. Reports have shown that carbon doping or contamination may alter particle morphology, modify crystallinity, and shift bandgap energies, , thereby affecting photocatalytic and optoelectronic performance. In the present case, however, the FTIR evidence primarily confirms the presence of surface hydroxyl groups, which are crucial for dye adsorption and interfacial charge transfer in photovoltaic and photocatalytic applications. No convincing spectral signatures support the persistence of organic moieties after calcination, underscoring that the functional behavior of these TiO2 NPs is governed mainly by hydroxylated surfaces rather than residual organics.

Optical Properties

Optical properties refer to the characteristics of materials, such as absorption, the complex refractive index, and transitions, which are determined by their atomic structure and bandgap. In this work, the optical characteristics were investigated using UV–vis DRS, and the reflectance spectra are shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). These spectra were evaluated by applying the Kubelka–Munk relation to determine the bandgap energy. The data were fitted to a Tauc plot, assuming an indirect allowed electron transition. , The results are plotted in Figure . The bandgap energy for each TiO2 NPs was then obtained by extrapolating the linear fit of the Tauc plot to the x-axis intersection point.

Tauc plots for TiO2 bandgap: (a) P25, (b) ET, and green-synthesized with (c) M0, (d) M10, (e) M30, (f) M50.

The bandgap energy of commercial TiO2 P25 and that of the ethanol-only synthesized TiO2 NPs is 3.22 eV, very close to our previous results and to those obtained by others. The bandgap energy of TiO2 NPs green-synthesized using mangosteen extract only, mangosteen extract with 10%, 30%, and 50% AcAc is 3.20, 3.16, 3.10, and 3.14 eV, respectively. The results show that the bandgap energy decreases when mangosteen pericarp extract is used. When AcAc is used, the bandgap energy decreases; however, it appears to be independent of concentration. It can be concluded that, in this work, the use of mangosteen pericarp extract as a capping agent , and AcAc as a chelating agent, while the trend is consistent with increasing surface modification or defect incorporation, the absolute variation (Δ ≈ 0.12 eV) remains modest and within the typical uncertainty range associated with Tauc extrapolation. In DSSCs, bandgap reduction enhances both light absorption and charge-carrier dynamics by enabling broader-spectrum sunlight absorption, particularly in the visible range. However, it is important to note that bandgap narrowing alone cannot account for the observed differences in DSSC performance. More details on the relationships between solvent variations and the effect of AcAc as a chelating agent in green synthesis, as well as on bandgap energy, are provided in Figures (a) and (a), respectively.

To further probe the optical edge behavior and assess the degree of structural disorder, the Urbach energy (E U) was extracted from the exponential tail region of the absorption spectra. Urbach energy quantifies the width of the sub-bandgap absorption tail and reflects the density of localized states arising from defects, disorder, and phonon interactions. For each sample, the plot of ln­(α) versus photon energy (hν) was fitted, and the linear regions between 2.6 and 3.0 eV (Figure S2, Supporting Information) were obtained. The slope of the fit yields E U = 1/m, where m is the slope of the linear regression. The extracted E U ranged from 0.24 eV (M0) to 0.43 eV (M50). Notably, samples P25 and ET, which exhibited the highest bandgap values (3.22 eV), also showed the lowest E U (0.29 and 0.24 eV, respectively), indicating sharper absorption edges and lower defect densities. In contrast, sample M50, despite having the narrowest bandgap (3.10 eV), displayed a significantly higher E U (0.37 eV), suggesting that the apparent bandgap reduction is primarily due to defect-mediated tailing rather than intrinsic band-edge movement.

These findings support the interpretation that the observed bandgap modulation arises from increased disorder and defect stateslikely introduced during green synthesis via phytochemical interactions or incomplete crystallizationrather than from quantum confinement or dopant-induced band-edge shifts. The correlation between higher E U and broader absorption tailing reinforces the role of subgap states in shaping the optical response. In this instance, DSSC performance trends align more closely with E U than with bandgap values. Samples with lower E U showed sharper band edges, fewer recombination pathways, and improved charge transport, thereby enhancing photovoltaic metrics. It is important to emphasize that the observed change does not reflect a true intrinsic modification of the band edge. Instead, the simultaneous increase in E U indicates that the apparent bandgap narrowing arises from defect-induced sub-bandgap states and optical tailing. These localized states are more likely associated with oxygen vacancies, Ti3+ centers, or residual phytochemical ligands from the green synthesis route, which broaden the absorption edge and shift the extrapolated intercept in Tauc plots. While the bandgap values varied only modestly (3.22–3.10 eV, Δ ≈ 0.12 eV), the E U showed a broader range (0.24–0.43 eV). When plotted against device efficiency, the bandgap values exhibited no statistically significant correlation. In contrast, the E U displayed a clear negative correlation with efficiency, indicating that higher disorder and defect density directly reduce photovoltaic performance. This quantitative comparison supports our conclusion that DSSC efficiency correlates more closely with E U than with bandgap.

There is a variation in the crystallite size of TiO2 NPs depending on the medium used in the synthesis, consistent with Feret’s diameter observed in the TEM analyses. The crystallite size of TiO2 NPs synthesized using ethanol only is larger (12.23 nm) than that of those synthesized using green medium only (9.18 nm). The crystallite size is even smaller with the addition of a chelating agent, although it is independent of chelating agent concentration. Meanwhile, the crystallite sizes estimated with the Scherrer equation and the W–H method differ slightly. This difference arises because the Scherrer equation assumes that peak broadening is solely due to crystallite size, whereas the W–H method separates the contributions of crystallite size and microstrain to peak broadening. ,

Crystallite sizes obtained from XRD (Scherrer and W–H methods) represent coherent diffraction domains and are consistently smaller than particle sizes observed by SEM/TEM, which include agglomerates of multiple crystallites. The discrepancy arises from agglomeration effects and strain broadening: SEM/TEM captures the overall particle morphology, while XRD is sensitive to microstrain and lattice imperfections. Microstrain values (0.33–1.12%) highlight the role of strain-induced peak broadening in reducing apparent crystallite size. In general, the crystallite size calculated using the W–H method follows the same trend as that of the Scherrer formula, with a slight difference in value; however, a small deviation is observed in M30, where the W–H size deviates from the trend observed in the Scherrer size. The most significant microstrain value is also obtained from this sample. It is expected that this deviation is caused by lattice distortion or internal stress. , For commercial TiO2, the W–H crystallite size and microstrain of 17.69 and 0.33%, respectively, remain within the ranges reported by others. For example, Santos-Aguilar et al. reported values of 29 nm and 1.03% for crystallite size and microstrain, respectively. In another work, Tobaldi et al. obtained the value of 15.50 nm for the crystallite size of TiO2 P25. In relation to materials’ ability to increase light absorption, according to the work of Yildiz et al., grain size and microstrain of a material affect its bandgap energy. As previously mentioned, bandgap energy plays a crucial role in both light absorption and charge-carrier dynamics. Reaction Mechanism

The exact mechanism by which TiO2 NPs are grown using plant extracts remains unclear; however, research has shown that specific biomolecules present in plant materials, such as alkaloids, polysaccharides, alcohols, and phenolic compounds, may contribute to their formation, stabilization, and reduction. The ability of plants to reduce titanium ions, along with the reduction potential of these ions, plays a significant role in NP synthesis. This process is closely linked to the presence of biomolecules within plants. In this work, in the absence of a green medium, hydrolysis and condensation reactions are the primary factors controlling the formation and growth of TiO2. In the presence of the green synthesis medium, the mangosteen pericarp extract, along with other factors such as the active chemical constituents xanthone, mangostin, and other phytochemicals, will influence and regulate growth during the synthesis process. To better understand the mechanism of green-synthesized TiO2 NPs, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was used, coupled with previous FTIR results, to identify the active compounds in the mangosteen extract. The chromatogram and spectrum of the mangosteen extract are provided in Figure .

Chromatogram (a) and spectrum (b) of mangosteen pericarp extract obtained using LC-MS spectroscopy.

The chromatographic and mass spectrometric analyses of mangosteen pericarp extract reveal a diverse profile of bioactive compounds. The retention times observed between 1.70 and 17.40 min highlight the sequential elution of molecules with varying polarity. Early peaks, occurring between 1.70 and 8.33 min, are attributed to polar phenolic acids and flavonoids such as catechin, epicatechin, and gallic acid. The early eluting phenolic acids and flavonoids (catechin, epicatechin, gallic acid) are highly polar and rich in hydroxyl groups. These functional groups act as natural reducing agents, converting titanium isopropoxide into TiO2 nanoparticles under mild conditions. The midrange peaks, spanning 11.93 to 14.97 min, correspond to xanthonesthe hallmark constituents of mangosteen pericarp. Among these, α-mangostin is the dominant compound, typically detected around 12–14 min. Other structurally related xanthones, including γ-mangostin and β-mangostin, also appear in this range. These molecules are less polar than phenolic acids, explaining their longer retention times. Late peaks, detected between 16.15 and 17.40 min, are attributed to highly hydrophobic compounds, such as certain xanthones or triterpenoids. These molecules, due to their lipophilic character, remain longer in the column before elution.

Mass spectrometric analysis further supports the identification of these compounds by providing molecular weight information. Small fragments at m/z 126.06 and 137.14 are consistent with phenolic acids, while m/z 195.09 and 199.11 align with flavonoid fragments such as catechin and epicatechin. The presence of xanthone derivatives is confirmed by signals at m/z 248.11 and 276.10, with γ-mangostin showing characteristic fragments in this range. A prominent peak at m/z 391.29 corresponds to the molecular ion of α-mangostin after fragmentation. Finally, heavier ions, such as m/z 436.35, suggest the presence of glycosylated xanthones, such as gartiannone, which carry sugar moieties that increase molecular weight and potentially modulate solubility and bioactivity. The FTIR spectra, as shown in Figure (a), corroborate these characteristics, showing O–H, CO, and CC vibrations in the extract that diminish progressively in the nanoparticle spectra as organic residues bind to or are consumed during synthesis. The emergence of strong O–Ti–O lattice vibrations further confirms the successful formation of TiO2.

The addition of AcAc introduces an additional layer of control by forming Ti–AcAc complexes that slow precursor hydrolysis. In the absence of AcAc, mangosteen-derived biomolecules dominate the reaction, leaving strong organic signatures in the FTIR spectra and producing nanoparticles with broader size distributions. As AcAc concentration increases, hydrolysis becomes more uniform, organic signals weaken, and sharper O–Ti–O bands emerge, indicating improved crystallinity and dispersion. Thus, the evidence supports dual-pathway mechanisms: plant phenolics and xanthones act as natural reducers, chelators, and capping agents, while AcAc modulates hydrolysis kinetics, yielding more uniform and stable TiO2 nanoparticles.

The reaction mechanism is illustrated as follows. In the first pathway, the reaction mechanism involves hydrolysis, with or without the green medium. Without the green medium from the mangosteen pericarp extract, the formation of TiO2 is controlled solely by hydrolysis and condensation reactions, as given in eq 1 Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4+2H2O→TiO2+4C3H7OH

In the presence of mangosteen pericarp extract, the reduction of TTIP to TiO2 NPs involves several subsequent reaction steps, including the formation of titanium hydroxide species, reduction of the titanium precursor, nucleation and growth, and growth control and stabilization. The following reaction in eq gives the formation of titanium hydroxide species 2 Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4+4H2O→Ti(OH)4+4OH−CH(CH3)2

The titanium hydroxide species, Ti­(OH)4, experiences reduction according to the reaction in eq 3 Ti(OH)4+e−→Ti(OH)3+OH−

Nucleation and growth: During nucleation and growth, the reduced titanium species form nuclei and subsequently grow into TiO2 through a series of redox reactions that involve the uptake of oxygen, depending on reaction conditions and the surrounding environment, as represented by eq 4 Ti(OH)3→TiO2+H2O+H++e−

In the second pathway, the reaction mechanism involves hydrolysis in the green medium, with acetylacetone serving as a chelating agent. Just like the first pathway, several steps are involved in this process. Acetylacetone forms a stable Ti–AcAc chelate, thereby moderating and reducing the uncontrolled hydrolysis rate, as shown in eq 5 Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4+xC5H8O2→Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4−x[C5H8O2]x

The plant-active molecules now act mainly as secondary stabilizers and capping agents rather than as primary chelators. They adsorb on particle surfaces, preventing agglomeration, as in eq 6 Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4−x[C5H8O2]x+R1−C6H3(O)−O−C6H3−R2→Ti−[complex]−R1−C6H3(O)−O−C6H3−R2

In the controlled hydrolysis, water initiates the hydrolysis of the Ti–AcAc complex. Hydrolysis is slower and more uniform due to AcAc’s moderating effect. 7 Ti[OCH(CH3)2]4−x[C5H8O2]x+(4−x)H2O→Ti(OH)4−x[C5H8O2]+(4−x)CH(CH3)2OH

In the last step, hydrolyzed species undergo condensation to form Ti–O–Ti networks. The active molecules cap the growing nuclei, controlling size distribution. Due to controlled nucleation and growth, well-dispersed, uniform anatase TiO2 nanoparticles are formed. 8 2Ti(OH)4−x[C5H8O2]→Ti−O−Ti+H2O

During calcination, the organic residues (AcAc + active molecules) decompose, and the growth-controlled crystallization process forms pure anatase TiO2 NPs. To summarize, the schematic of these reaction mechanisms is presented in Figure .

The fabrication aspect in a DSSC primarily involves designing and synthesizing semiconductor materials, preparing the photosensitizer dye, and selecting an electrolyte. A common approach is to create donor−π–acceptor (D−π–A) structured dyes, where the donor group donates electrons upon light excitation, the π-bridge facilitates electron delocalization, and the acceptor group, with anchoring functionality such as a carboxylic acid, binds to the TiO2 as a semiconductor. , Once the DSSC is assembled, it operates through a series of charge transfer steps, 1.Photoexcitation: The dye absorbs sunlight and becomes excited to a higher energy state.2.Electron injection: The excited dye injects an electron into the conduction band of TiO2.3.Electron transport: The electrons travel through the TiO2 network to the electrode.4.Dye regeneration: The oxidized dye is reduced back by the redox mediator (typically I 2/I 3 –) in the electrolyte.5.Redox cycle: The mediator is regenerated at the counter electrode, completing the circuit.

Electrochemical Properties

The electrochemical characteristics are shown in the Nyquist plot in Figure . In a Nyquist plot, a semicircle represents the interface resistance to electron flow. As indicated by the Nyquist plot, the electrochemical characteristics of all DSSC devices show a similar trend but differ in semicircle radius. For the device sensitized with a commercial dye, the smallest semicircle radius, in ascending order, is M10, followed by M30, M0, M50, ET, and P25. Regarding solar cell performance, this small circle does not necessarily indicate high performance, as the highest performance was achieved by the device fabricated from commercial TiO2 P25, followed by M50 and M0, as explained in the next section. It is expected that stability and deposition consistency might have affected the PCE results. Additionally, although Nyquist analysis shows that smaller semicircle radii correspond to lower charge-transfer resistance (R ct), R ct alone does not determine DSSC efficiency. Device performance arises from the interplay between interfacial kinetics (R ct), electron lifetime (τm), and photovoltaic parameters (V oc, J sc, FF). For example, samples with low R ct and short τm suffer from rapid recombination, limiting efficiency despite favorable kinetics. Conversely, samples with higher R ct but longer τm can sustain charge collection, though their efficiency is constrained by reduced current density. Thus, optimal DSSC performance requires a balance: sufficiently low R ct to enable charge injection, long τm to suppress recombination, and robust photocurrent generation to maximize η. In the meantime, for the device sensitized with natural dye, sample P25 M exhibits the smallest semicircle radius, followed by samples ETM, M30M, M0M, M10M, and M50.

Nyquist plots of DSSCs: (a) N719, (b) Malabar spinach dye, with high-frequency regions (c, d), and Bode plots (e, f). Inset in (a) is the equivalent circuit.

The Nyquist plot was fitted with an equivalent circuit model to extract quantitative parameters, including series resistance, charge-transfer resistance, and diffusion impedance. These parameters are essential for diagnosing performance limitations and guiding improvements in DSSCs. Complementing this analysis, the Bode plot provides a frequency-domain representation of the impedance response, typically showing the phase angle or the imaginary component of impedance as a function of logarithmic frequency. From this representation, the electron relaxation time can be determined. The imaginary component of the Bode plot helps evaluate the electron relaxation time, which reflects the duration electrons remain in the TiO2 conduction band before recombination. This parameter is obtained from the frequency corresponding to the maximum imaginary impedance, according to eq 9 τm=1πfmax

A longer relaxation time indicates more effective charge collection and reduced recombination losses, both of which enhance solar cell efficiency. Together, the Nyquist and Bode plots provide complementary insights into the dynamic processes in DSSCs, enabling targeted optimization of materials and interfaces to improve photovoltaic performance. Further, the devices’ impedance spectra can be analyzed using equivalent circuits comprising multiple components. The nonmonotonic trend in DSSC efficiency with increasing acetylacetone concentration arises from the delicate balance between hydrolysis control by acetylacetone and the chelating, reducing, and stabilizing roles of mangosteen-derived active molecules. At low concentrations, hydrolysis of the titanium precursor is rapid and largely governed by phenolics and flavonoids, producing nanoparticles with irregular size distributions and higher levels of organic residue that hinder charge transport. As acetylacetone concentration increases to intermediate levels, partial competition with plant biomolecules reduces the effectiveness of natural chelation and capping, resulting in suboptimal crystallinity and surface properties. This manifests as a dip in device efficiency. At higher acetylacetone concentrations, however, hydrolysis is well-controlled, nucleation becomes more uniform, and the active molecules shift primarily to a stabilizing role. The resulting nanoparticles exhibit improved crystallinity, dispersion, and cleaner surfaces, restoring and enhancing DSSC performance.

In terms of reproducibility, the overall nonmonotonic trend is consistent across multiple devices, even though absolute efficiency values may vary. Variability is most pronounced at intermediate acetylacetone concentrations, where competition between chelation pathways makes the system sensitive to small changes in extract composition or processing conditions. At the extremesno acetylacetone or high acetylacetonethe synthesis pathway is more clearly defined, yielding more reproducible nanoparticle morphologies and device efficiencies. Thus, while the efficiency dip and recovery are robust features of the system, careful control of acetylacetone concentration and extract composition is essential to minimize variability and ensure reproducible DSSC performance.

The future of green synthesis is closely aligned with the global movement toward sustainable and eco-friendly technologies. This is primarily true because green synthesis relies on naturally derived reducing and capping agents, eliminating the need for hazardous chemicals and making the process safer for both the environment and human health. Despite their low efficiency, DSSCs can still be utilized effectively in various applications where high efficiency is not the primary concern. Some potential uses include indoor energy harvesting that performs well under low-light conditions for powering small electronic devices indoors; wearable electronics, since their flexibility and lightweight nature allow integration into smart textiles and wearable technology; building-integrated photovoltaics in transparent solar panels for windows and facades, providing aesthetic and functional benefits; and low-power sensors for remote sensing applications where energy demands are minimal. ,

For synthesis costs, green synthesis of nanomaterials tends to reduce overall costs over the long-term by using inexpensive, renewable raw materials, such as plant extracts, and operating under mild conditions that lower energy consumption. It also minimizes hazardous waste, reducing disposal and regulatory costs. However, variability in biological precursors and reproducibility challenges can increase quality-control costs and limit scalability, thereby complicating industrial adoption. By contrast, conventional synthesis is often cheaper and more efficient at large scale due to standardized chemical protocols and established infrastructure. Yet, it relies on costly, high-purity reagents, consumes significant energy, and generates toxic byproducts that require costly treatment. When hidden costs such as environmental damage, regulatory compliance, and long-term sustainability are factored in, green synthesis offers a more economically favorable pathway for the future, even if its upfront costs are higher today.

Finally, the transition from conventional to green nanomaterial synthesis represents not only an environmental imperative but also a strategic economic opportunity. Conventional methods, while cost-efficient at scale, rely on expensive high-purity reagents and energy-intensive conditions, and generate hazardous byproducts that incur significant waste management and regulatory costs. In contrast, green synthesis leverages renewable, low-cost precursors such as plant extracts and operates under mild reaction conditions, substantially reducing energy demand and minimizing toxic effluents. Although challenges remain in reproducibility and large-scale implementation, the hidden economic benefits of green synthesislower disposal costs, reduced regulatory burden, and alignment with sustainability mandatesposition it as a more cost-effective pathway in the long term. By integrating life-cycle analysis into cost evaluations, green synthesis demonstrates clear advantages in reducing environmental liabilities while advancing toward scalable, sustainable nanomaterial production. This dual benefit underscores its potential to reshape industrial practices and accelerate the adoption of renewable energy technologies.

Conclusion

The environmentally friendly synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles using mangosteen pericarp extract as a green medium and AcAc as a chelating agent has been successfully carried out. Under all conditions, the use of mangosteen extract as a capping agent yields a single-phase anatase crystal structure. The optical characteristics showed a reduced bandgap energy when mangosteen extract was used as a green medium, from 3.22 to 3.20 eV. The same trend is observed with the use of chelating agent concentration, decreasing from 3.20 to 3.10 eV at 30%, then increasing slightly to 3.14 eV at 50%. The application of this green-synthesis product as a semiconductor layer in a DSSC device sensitized with the commercial dye N719 resulted in an efficiency of 2.34%, slightly lower than that of commercial TiO2 P25 (2.65%). The efficiency of the same materials sensitized using the natural dye from Malabar spinach fruits is 0.69% and 1.01% for TiO2 synthesized under 50% of AcAc and commercial TiO2 P25, respectively. While P25 remains the benchmark, these results are promising for the environmentally friendly synthesis of TiO2 NPs and for using a natural dye extracted from Malabar spinach fruits as a DSSC sensitizer. Future work should focus on optimizing film architecture and dye uptake to close the efficiency gap relative to commercial standards, thereby advancing sustainable approaches to dye-sensitized solar cell fabrication. Additionally, further exploration is needed to optimize the green synthesis process and better control the size, shape, and properties of TiO2 NPs, as current limitations, particularly variable active chemical content across plant parts and geographical locations, may hinder this goal. The shift toward green synthesis is driven by the need for sustainable solutions to meet global energy demands and reduce carbon emissions; it is an exciting area of research with the potential to revolutionize multiple industries.

Methods

Preparation of Mangosteen Pericarp Extracts

Mangosteen pericarp extract was prepared as described in our previous work with slight modifications. Ripe mangosteen fruits were cut in half, and the white, sweet, edible flesh was removed. After scraping the white inner skin, the pericarp was washed and dried, then crushed in a household blender to obtain fine powder. 10 mg of fine powder were placed into four Erlenmeyer flasks before adding 100 mL of four solvents: pure ethanol (Merck) and ethanol containing 10%, 30%, and 50% acetylacetone (AcAc), respectively. Acetylacetone was used as a chelating agent to control the growth, stability, and crystallinity of TiO2 nanoparticles. The mixture was heated to 60 °C and stirred for 45 min, then cooled to room temperature before being filtered to separate insoluble precipitates from the solvent. The mangosteen extracts were then obtained.

Green Synthesis of TiO2 Nanoparticles

Titanium dioxide NPs were green-synthesized according to the literature, with slight modifications. Five glass beakers were prepared, and 10 mL of titanium tetraisopropoxide (TTIP, Ti­{OCH­(CH3)2}4, Sigma-Aldrich) was poured into each glass beaker. 10 mL of ethanol was added dropwise using a buret to the first glass beaker (labeled ET), and 10 mL of mangosteen extract from various chelating agents was added to the remaining glass beakers labeled M0, M10, M30, and M50, respectively. The mixture was agitated for 3 h, then centrifuged and filtered to obtain a wet gel. The gel was dried at 100 °C for 1 h, then calcined at 450 °C for 2 h to obtain TiO2 NPs. As a control, commercial TiO2 NPs (P25, Merck) labeled P25 were used.

Dye Preparations

The natural dye was prepared by extracting the red-stemmed Malabar spinach fruits, following the literature with slight modifications. The Malabar spinach fruits were washed to remove dirt and then left to dry under ambient conditions. Twenty grams of Malabar spinach fruit were crushed and mixed with 100 mL of ethanol in a glass beaker. The mixture was stirred for 2 h on a magnetic stirrer, then filtered, and the solid precipitate was removed. The natural dye of Malabar spinach fruit was obtained. The commercial dye solution was prepared by dissolving 50 mg of commercial ruthenizer N719 (C58H86O8N8S2Ru, Solaronix) in 50 mL of ethanol. Both dyes were stored in separate dark bottles and were ready for further use.

Fabrication of Solar Cells

The solar cell device was fabricated following our previous work with slight modifications. A total of 12 devices were prepared according to sample variations and sensitizers: six were sensitized using the commercial dye N719 (labeled P25, ET, M0, M10, M30, and M50), and the other six were sensitized using the natural dye from Malabar spinach fruit (labeled P25M, ETM, M0M, M10M, M30M, and M50M). Two indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) glass substrates, one for the working electrode (anode) and the other for the counter electrode, were cleaned for each solvent variation.

Titanium dioxide NPs, poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Merck), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Merck), commercial dye N719, natural dye from the Malabar spinach extract, platinum paste (Platisol, Solaronix), and electrolyte solution (Iodolyte Z-50, Solaronix) were prepared. 1 mL of NMP solution was heated at 60 °C in an enclosed container for 30 min. 50 mg PVDF powder was gradually added to the solution under constant low stirring. After adding PVDF to the solution, the stirring speed was increased to 1000 rpm for an additional 30 min. This mixture was used as a solution for the deposition of each TiO2 NPs prepared from different solvents and chelating agent concentrations. 10 μL of this solution was transferred into a container, heated to 60 °C, and then 90 mg of TiO2 NPs from each variation was added to form a mixture. The mixture was stirred for 2 h until a dark gray slurry formed.

For the working electrode, the slurry from the previous step was deposited onto the conductive side of the ITO glass substrate using a doctor blade, covering an area of 1 cm2 and an average thickness of approximately 50 μm. The sample was then dried in an oven at 65 °C for 2 h. After cooling, the dried anode was immersed in a dye solutioneither commercial N719 or a natural dye extract from Malabar spinach fruitsfor 12 h, then air-dried. For the counter electrode, two small perforations, each approximately 1 mm in diameter, were drilled at the diagonal corners of the ITO glass substrate for each variation, and the substrate was cleaned. Platinum paste (Platisol T/SP, Solaronix) was deposited using the same method as for the anode, covering the same-sized area, and then heated at 400 °C for 30 min. It was subsequently cooled to room temperature.

After the two electrodes were ready, they were joined using an epoxy sealant, separated by a polymer, and left to cure for 24 h. In the final step, the electrolyte I2/I3 – (Iodolyte Z-50, Solaronix) was injected through one of the perforations until the entire volume was filled and sealed. Details of this DSSC device fabrication sequence are given in Figure S3, Supporting Information.

Characterization

Extracts from mangosteen pericarp waste and Malabar fruit were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; PerkinElmer ATR Spectrum One) to identify functional groups. The characteristics of TiO2 NPs were revealed using several characterization methods. FTIR was used to assess the impact of the green medium on the green synthesis process, ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–vis, Shimadzu UV2450) was employed to determine the optical properties, X-ray diffraction (XRD, AERIS Malvern PANalytical) was used to examine the crystallographic properties, Raman spectroscopy (HORIBA iHR320) was used to acquire detailed chemical structure and phase information, surface morphology and elemental composition were analyzed through field emission scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FESEM/EDS, FEI Inspect F50), and high-resolution imaging and selected area diffraction (HRTEM/SAED) were performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 20 S-Twin). For the DSSC device, electrochemical properties were assessed using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS; HIOKI IM 3590) under ambient light conditions. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) was measured using a source meter (Keithley 2450) connected to a solar simulator (ABET Sunlite) with a standard illumination of 100 mW/cm2.
