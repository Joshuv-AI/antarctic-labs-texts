Extracellular vesicles from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum inhibit the biofilm formation of Listeria monocytogenes and reduce bacterial contamination on romaine lettuce
Authors: Jihyun Park, Jihyeon Baek, Esther Choi, Dongho Kim, Aram Lee, Seok-Seong Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Biofilms formed by foodborne pathogens such as Listeria monocytogenes pose a serious risk to food safety. Probiotics and their derivatives have been widely explored as alternatives to conventional antimicrobial strategies for biofilm control. In this study, extracellular vesicles (EVs) were isolated from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (LpEVs) and characterized, and their antibiofilm activity against L. monocytogenes was investigated. Their inhibitory effects on L. monocytogenes biofilm were evaluated using romaine lettuce as a food model. Results showed that LpEV treatment significantly reduced biofilm formation without affecting bacterial survival. LpEVs suppressed the expression of luxS and the production of autoinducer-2, thereby interfering with quorum sensing (QS) in L. monocytogenes. LpEVs downregulated QS-related genes associated with motility, adhesion, and virulence. They also impaired not only bacterial swimming motility but also adhesion to and invasion of intestinal epithelial cells. The surface application of LpEVs on romaine lettuce leaves markedly reduced L. monocytogenes biofilm formation and bacterial contamination without compromising visual sensory quality. Therefore, LpEVs could be used as natural antimicrobial agents that interfere with QS-mediated biofilm formation by L. monocytogenes and could also be effectively applied to enhance the microbial safety of fresh produce.

Introduction

Recent trends in human lifestyles and eating habits have led to an increased demand for healthy convenience foods (Raffo and Paoletti, 2022). Among foods, fresh-cut or minimally processed vegetables and fruits have gained remarkable popularity. The global consumption of fresh-cut produce has dramatically increased in recent years because of their convenient and health aspects (Baselice et al., 2017; Remize and Garcia, 2024). However, fresh-cut produce is highly susceptible to contamination and degradation because of various processing steps, including cutting. Moreover, certain processing methods, such as disinfectant use, may cause potential health problems to consumers to prevent contamination and extend shelf life (Raffo and Paoletti, 2022).

Fresh-cut vegetables and fruits are more vulnerable to attachment and colonization by pathogenic bacteria than intact produce (Alegbeleye and Rhee, 2024). Human pathogenic bacteria, including Listeria monocytogenes, attach to vegetable surfaces, consequently forming biofilms that enhance their survival in harsh environments (Elbakush et al., 2024). These biofilms increase their resistance to antimicrobial treatments and facilitate their spread in food processing environments. L. monocytogenes naturally occurs in agricultural environments and poses a risk to fresh produce because of its widespread presence in soil, water, and decaying vegetation (Sauders et al., 2012).

L. monocytogenes has been isolated from various vegetables, such as cabbage, lettuce, carrots, and corns (Zhu et al., 2017). It causes listeriosis, which is a serious food-borne disease; for instance, its outbreak in Europe resulted in an approximately 30 % mortality rate (EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards, 2018). Listeriosis outbreaks associated with fresh produce have occurred in various regions worldwide. For example, in 2010, a listeriosis outbreak affecting chopped celery resulted in 10 deaths in Texas, United States (Gaul et al., 2013).

Although efforts have been devoted to eliminating L. monocytogenes through washing or disinfection, it can persist in food-related environments for months or even years primarily because of its ability to form biofilms (Ferreira et al., 2014; Panebianco et al., 2024). Various strategies and interventions, including chemical disinfectants, biofilm-disrupting agents, mechanical removal, heat treatment, and radiation, have been used to effectively eliminate L. monocytogenes biofilms (Fagerlund et al., 2020). However, alternative interventions have been explored to inhibit the presence of L. monocytogenes in foods because of limited treatment options. For instance, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and their derivatives with antimicrobial properties have been investigated as potential solutions (H. B. Lee et al., 2022; Moradi et al., 2019).

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from bacteria are membranous nanostructures with diameters ranging from 20 nm to 800 nm (Li et al., 2024). They transport various biological molecules, including cell wall components, nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and other metabolites (Jeong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Although various cellular components and derivatives of LAB have demonstrated antimicrobial activities against foodborne pathogens, the inhibitory effects of LAB-derived EVs and their underlying mechanisms on biofilm formation have not been extensively studied. No studies have explored the application of LAB–EVs to control bacterial contamination on fresh vegetables as alternatives to conventional chemical and physical disinfectants. In the present study, EVs were isolated from Lactoplantibacillus plantarum (LpEVs) and characterized. Their possibility to inhibit L. monocytogenes biofilm formation and its molecular mechanisms were examined. Furthermore, LpEVs were applied to romaine lettuce leaves to evaluate their potential as novel antimicrobial agents for fresh produce.

Materials and methods

Isolation and characterization of LpEVs

L. plantarum ATCC 14917 was purchased from the American Type Cell Culture (Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in a Man–Rogosa−Sharpe medium (Neogen, Lansing, MI, USA) at 37 °C. For EV isolation, L. plantarum ATCC 14917 was grown and harvested at 24 h. Culture supernatants were filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane filter. Crude EVs were isolated via ultracentrifugation at 160,000×g at 4 °C for 2 h by using a Type 45 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The EV-containing pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g at 4 °C for 1 h. The yield and size of the EVs isolated from L. plantarum ATCC 14917 were measured through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) by using a NanoSight LM10 system and the NTA 2.3 nanoparticle tracking and analysis software (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was applied to examine the morphological characteristics of LpEVs. Briefly, LpEVs were placed onto glow-discharged formvar/carbon-coated copper grids (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and negatively stained with 2 % uranyl acetate. The samples were then visualized using a TEM (JEM-2100PLUS, JEOL, Japan). The protein concentrations of LpEVs were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA).

Quantification of L. monocytogenes biofilm by using crystal violet

L. monocytogenes KCTC 3569 (serotype 1/2a, originally isolated from poultry) was obtained from the Korean Collection for Type Culture (Jeongup, Korea) and cultured in a brain heart infusion (BHI) medium (MB cell, Seoul, Korea) at 37 °C for 24 h to assess the inhibitory effect of LpEVs on L. monocytogenes biofilm formation. L. monocytogenes suspension at a concentration of 1 × 107 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL was inoculated into a 96-well culture plate with or without LpEVs (0, 0.01, or 0.1 μg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 24, 48, and 72 h. After incubation, culture supernatants were carefully discarded, and the plate was gently washed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. The remaining biofilm was stained with crystal violet (0.1 %) for 30 min and washed with PBS. The bound crystal violet was solubilized using ethanol (95 %) containing acetic acid (0.1 %). Absorbance at 595 nm was measured with a microtiter plate reader (AMR-100, Allsheng, Hangzhou, China) to quantify the biofilm mass. In a separate experiment, LpEVs (0, 0.01, or 0.1 μg/mL) were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 3 h before L. monocytogenes (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was added; then, it was further incubated for 24 h. Conversely, L. monocytogenes (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was pre-incubated at 37 °C for 3 h before LpEVs (0, 0.01, or 0.1 μg/mL) were added and then incubated for another 24 h. After incubation, biofilm was quantified as described above.

Measurement of L. monocytogenes biofilm via fluorescence microscopy analysis

L. monocytogenes (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was incubated with or without LpEVs (0, 0.01, or 0.1 μg/mL) on a cover glass bottom dish (SPL Life Science, Pocheon, Korea) at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, planktonic cells were carefully removed, and the cover glass bottom dish was gently washed with PBS. L. monocytogenes biofilms were stained using a LIVE/DEAD BacLight bacterial viability kit (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA) and visualized with a fluorescence microscope (Axio Imager. Z2, Carl Zeiss, Berlin, Germany).

Survival of planktonic L. monocytogenes in the presence of LpEVs

L. monocytogenes was incubated with LpEVs at concentrations of 0, 0.01, or 0.1 μg/mL for 24, 48, or 72 h. Afterward, the survival of planktonic L. monocytogenes was assessed by serially diluting the cultures and plating on the BHI agar. Survival was quantified by enumerating the CFU.

Assessment of L. monocytogenes swimming motility

The BHI medium with 0.3 % (w/v) agar was prepared in 6-well culture plates supplemented with LpEVs (0 or 0.1 μg/mL). After the culture plates were dried, 10 μL of L. monocytogenes suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was inoculated onto the surface of the BHI medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The diameters of the swimming zones were measured using the ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/imagej/).

Adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells by L. monocytogenes

A human intestinal epithelial cell line, Caco-2, was grown in complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Burlington, ON, Canada) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 12-well culture plate to allow fully confluent monolayers. For the adhesion assay, L. monocytogenes (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was inoculated to Caco-2 cells with LpEVs (0 or 0.1 μg/mL) and incubated at ambient temperature (approximately 25 °C) for 1 h without penicillin/streptomycin. After non-adherent L. monocytogenes was removed by gently washing with PBS, Caco-2 cells were lysed with 0.2 % Triton X-100. Adherent L. monocytogenes was enumerated by performing serial dilution and plating on the BHI agar. For invasion assay, Caco-2 cells were infected with L. monocytogenes (1 × 107 CFU/mL) with LpEVs (0 or 0.1 μg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were then washed with PBS and added with a fresh DMEM containing gentamycin (100 μg/mL) to kill extracellular L. monocytogenes. They were washed with PBS, lysed with 0.2 % Triton X-100, serially diluted, and plated on a BHI agar to enumerate the invading L. monocytogenes.

Application of LpEV as an antimicrobial agent in fresh food

Fresh romaine lettuce leaves were obtained from a local market (Goyang, Korea), stored at 4 °C, and used within 2 days. They were washed with tap water, cut into disc-shaped pieces (approximately 7 cm2), and sterilized first with 70 % ethanol and then with distilled water. They were exposed to UV light for 30 min (15 min of irradiation on each upper and lower side) to eliminate the remaining natural microflora. L. monocytogenes was inoculated on the surface of the leaves and incubated with or without LpEVs (0, 0.01, and 0.1 μg/mL) in a 6-well plate at 4 °C, 25 °C, or 37 °C for 24 or 48 h to examine the inhibitory effect of LpEVs on L. monocytogenes biofilm. Afterward, the leaves were gently washed with PBS and vortexed for 3 min to detach the L. monocytogenes-forming biofilm from the leaf surface. Each suspension was diluted 10-fold in PBS and plated in the LB agar for determination. In a separate experiment, romaine lettuce leaves were gently washed with tap water and cut approximately into equal-sized pieces to a weight of 0.5 g. Distilled water with or without LpEVs (1 μg/mL) was sprayed onto the surface of leaves and stored at 4 °C for 14 days. Changes in appearance, including the discoloration and browning of the cut edges, were observed on days 1, 7, and 14. The total count of aerobic mesophilic bacteria was enumerated on day 14 by plating on the LB agar at 37 °C for 24 h.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed by comparing with the respective control through one-way ANOVA in IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software (IBM, Aromnk, NY, USA). For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments, and results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of LpEVs

EVs were isolated from the cell-free supernatant of L. plantarum through ultracentrifugation, isolated, and characterized via NTA and TEM. NTA revealed that the average diameter of LpEVs was 230 ± 3.5 nm, with a concentration of 2.00 × 1012 particles/mL. Protein quantification via the BCA assay showed that LpEVs contained 444.1 μg/mL protein (Fig. 1A). Visualization by TEM demonstrated that LpEVs were nanosized and exhibited a spherical vesicular morphology with lipid bilayers (Fig. 1B). Inhibitory effect of LpEVs on L. monocytogenes biofilm formation

The pre-treatment with 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs significantly reduced the L. monocytogenes biofilm formation by 30 %, whereas 0.01 μg/mL LpEVs showed no noticeable inhibitory effect (Fig. 2A). When LpEVs were co-treated with L. monocytogenes, biofilm formation was significantly reduced by 33 % and 47 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. 2B). LpEVs were applied after L. monocytogenes biofilm formation to examine whether LpEVs could eradicate the pre-established L. monocytogenes biofilm. LpEVs dose-dependently reduced the formed L. monocytogenes biofilm by 27 % and 33 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. 2C). These findings indicated that LpEVs could interfere with biofilm formation, but LpEVs did not effectively prevent or eliminate L. monocytogenes biofilm. L. monocytogenes was incubated with LpEVs for 24, 48, and 72 h to further evaluate the temporal effects of LpEVs. The treatment with LpEVs (0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL) for 24 h significantly inhibited biofilm formation (Fig. 2D), and this observation was consistent with the results shown in Fig. 2B. However, the extended treatment for 48 and 72 h did not further enhance the inhibitory effect of LpEVs (Fig. 2E and F, respectively). These results suggested that a 24 h treatment was sufficient to elicit the maximal inhibitory effect on L. monocytogenes biofilm formation. L. monocytogenes was co-incubated with or without LpEVs to examine the structure of L. monocytogenes biofilm in the presence of LpEVs. A dense and thick biofilm was formed by L. monocytogenes in the absence of LpEVs, whereas the presence of LpEVs resulted in a significantly less dense and thinner biofilm. Effect of LpEVs on the survival of L. monocytogenes

We further examined whether the inhibitory effect of LpEVs on L. monocytogenes biofilm formation was associated with the reduced survival of planktonic L. monocytogenes. The survival of planktonic L. monocytogenes in the group treated with 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs increased compared with that in the untreated group (0 μg/mL) after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 3A). After 48 and 72 h incubation, the survival of planktonic L. monocytogenes in the presence of LpEVs did not significantly differ from that in the untreated group (Fig. 3B and C, respectively). QS disruption and related gene downregulation involved in L. monocytogenes biofilm development in the presence of LpEVs

The expression of luxS and the production of AI-2, a luxS-dependent signaling molecule, were measured to investigate the ability of LpEVs to inhibit L. monocytogenes biofilm formation via QS disruption. The LpEV treatment significantly decreased the luxS expression (Fig. 4A). The AI-2 production level was also significantly reduced following exposure to LpEVs (Fig. 4B). These results suggested that LpEVs interfered with L. monocytogenes QS, potentially leading to biofilm inhibition. Given that QS-regulated motility, virulence, and adhesion genes contribute to L. monocytogenes biofilm development, we assessed the expression levels of these genes following the LpEV treatment. The LpEV treatment significantly downregulated the expression of all tested genes compared with that in the control groups (Fig. 4C). Specifically, the mRNA expression levels of flagellar motility-related genes (flaA, fliG, flgE, motB, and dgeU) were reduced by more than 60 %. The virulence factor gene prfA and the upstream regulatory gene agrA were also significantly suppressed. Additionally, the expression levels of adhesion-related genes (fbpA, ctaP, actA, lapB, ami, and inlA) were significantly downregulated. Inhibitory effect of LpEVs on the motility, adhesion, and invasion of L. monocytogenes

LpEV treatment slightly reduced the swimming motility of L. monocytogenes although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.052; Fig. 5A). As swimming motility is associated with L. monocytogenes adhesion (Dong et al., 2022), LpEVs inhibited L. monocytogenes adhesion to Caco-2 cells (Fig. 5B). LpEV control of microbial contamination on fresh romaine lettuce

The antibiofilm potential of LpEVs on the surface of fresh romaine lettuce leaves was evaluated at different storage temperatures and times to simulate common storage conditions. After L. monocytogenes was inoculated and incubated with LpEVs at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL at 4 °C for 24 h, L. monocytogenes biofilm was significantly reduced by 45 % and 67 %, respectively, compared with those in the untreated romaine lettuce leaves. Similar inhibitory effects were observed after 48 h of storage; as a result, they were reduced by 42 % and 68 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs, respectively (Fig. 6A). When incubated at an ambient temperature (25 °C) for 24 h, LpEVs reduced L. monocytogenes biofilm formation by 17 % and 22 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs, respectively. After 48 h of storage at ambient temperature, L. monocytogenes biofilm formation was reduced by 14 % and 48 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs, respectively (Fig. 6B). The inhibitory effect of LpEVs on L. monocytogenes biofilm was further investigated at 37 °C on the surface of romaine lettuce leaves for 24 and 48 h. LpEVs at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL significantly reduced L. monocytogenes biofilm formation by 30 % and 48 %, respectively, for 24 h (Fig. 6C). After 48 h, the reductions were 41 % and 57 % at 0.01 and 0.1 μg/mL LpEVs, respectively. In a separate experiment, the potential of LpEVs for food preservation and quality retention was evaluated. No significant visual differences were observed between the control (distilled water only) and the LpEV-treated samples (Fig. 7A). However, the total aerobic mesophilic bacterial count was significantly decreased by 60 % in the LpEV-treated samples compared with that in the control (Fig. 7B). Discussion

LAB exhibit an antibiofilm activity against various foodborne pathogens (H. B. Lee et al., 2022; J. S. Lee et al., 2021). Although the mechanisms of the antibiofilm activity vary depending on genus and species, live LAB can inhibit biofilm formation through competition, displacement, or exclusion (Carvalho et al., 2021). In addition to live LAB, metabolites and compounds derived from LAB such as bacteriocins demonstrate an effective antibiofilm activity against a range of foodborne pathogens (N.-N. Kim et al., 2019; Seo and Kang, 2020). Specifically, bacteriocins are regarded as safe and natural preservatives to maintain food quality and prevent microbial contamination in food processing. However, their application in food processing is limited because of their sensitivity to pH, heat, and proteolytic enzymes (Verma et al., 2022). Unlike bacteriocins, EVs are stable under harsh conditions, including those in the gastrointestinal tract (Yi et al., 2025). Therefore, EVs derived from lactobacilli offer unique advantages that can enhance the applications of natural agents for improving microbial safety in the food industry. A previous study reported that membrane vesicles from Lacticaseibacillus casei BL23 suppressed Salmonella Enteritidis biofilm formation without affecting bacterial growth (da Silva Barreira et al., 2023), which is consistent with our findings on LpEVs. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate antibiofilm activity of EVs derived from L. plantarum. L. monocytogenes is known for its ability to persist and form biofilm on food contact surfaces, posing a remarkable challenge to food safety due to its association with the transmission of foodborne illness, contamination of food products, and microbial spoilage (Manville et al., 2023). Therefore, in the present study, LpEVs suppressed L. monocytogenes biofilm formation by regulating QS associated with bacterial motility, adhesion, and virulence. Furthermore, LpEVs could control microbial contamination on fresh produce without affecting its sensory qualities.

QS plays a critical role in the biofilm formation of foodborne pathogens; as such, targeting the QS system has emerged as an effective strategy for biofilm control (Melian et al., 2019; Tomé et al., 2023). Several types of metabolites and compounds produced by LAB affect the QS activity in foodborne pathogens. For example, biosurfactants extracted from Pediococcus acidilactici and L. plantarum inhibit the biofilm formation of Staphylococcus aureus by disrupting the release of AI-2, a key QS signaling molecule (Yan et al., 2019). AI-2 production is regulated by luxS, which in turn modulates L. monocytogenes biofilm formation by affecting the QS activity (Melian et al., 2019; Oloketuyi and Khan, 2017). Moreover, a purified fraction known as Z102-E from L. plantarum suppresses the QS-related gene expression in L. monocytogenes, effectively reducing its biofilm formation (Wei et al., 2024). Consistent with these findings, LpEVs inhibited luxS expression and AI-2 production, thereby decreasing the QS activity. Thus, the QS activity downregulation hindered L. monocytogenes biofilm formation, indicating that LpEVs could control biofilm development by disrupting QS-mediated signaling networks essential for biofilm formation.

The luxS/AI-2 bacterial signaling system affects the motility, adhesion, and virulence, which are key factors associated with biofilm formation (Che et al., 2024). Flagellar motility-related genes, including motB and degU, promote initial adhesion and surface colonization, thereby contributing to biofilm formation (Banerji et al., 2022; Kerekes et al., 2013). Furthermore, degU is related to flagellar synthesis and regulates genes such as flaA, flgE, and flgG (Alejandro-Navarreto and Freitag, 2024). Our findings suggested that the treatment with LpEVs downregulated the genes involved in flagellar synthesis and motility. Thus, impaired flagellar synthesis and reduced motility may contribute to the decreased biofilm formation of L. monocytogenes. Metabolites and compounds derived from lactobacilli also decrease the expression of motility- and virulence-related genes in L. monocytogenes; as a result, biofilm formation decreases on surfaces such as rubber gloves, plastics, and silicon rubber commonly used in the food industry (Hossain et al., 2021). The proteins encoded by fbpA, lapB, actA, and inlA bind to fibronectin, heat shock protein 60, proteoglycan, and E-cadherin, respectively; consequently, they promote bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation and contribute to listeriosis (Kannan et al., 2020; Oloketuyi and Khan, 2017; Osanai et al., 2013; Wampler et al., 2004; Xayarath et al., 2009). Our study demonstrated that LpEV treatment significantly suppressed the expression of genes involved in flagellar motility and adhesion; therefore, the downregulation of motility- and adhesion-related genes regulated by the luxS/AI-2 bacterial signaling system helped inhibit L. monocytogenes biofilm formation.

EVs comprise various biologically active molecules, such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, and their stability and quality are affected by environmental conditions (Qin et al., 2020). Low and high storage temperatures can negatively affect the integrity and function of EVs. For example, the storage of plant-derived EVs at low temperatures is associated with a decrease in the total protein content; conversely, high temperatures facilitate protein degradation (K. Kim et al., 2022; Leng et al., 2023). For food preservation, storage environments are highly variable and affected by several factors, such as temperature and relative humidity (Nunes et al., 2009). Thus, the stability and functionality of EVs under these conditions should be maintained when considering their application in food systems. Although studies have increasingly explored the biological roles of EVs derived from lactobacilli, their antibiofilm activity under typical food storage temperatures has yet to be investigated. The present study demonstrated that LpEVs exhibited the most effective antibiofilm activity against L. monocytogenes at 4 °C, a common storage temperature for fresh food products. Therefore, LpEVs could be used as a natural antimicrobial agent to maintain the microbial safety and quality of fresh food products. However, although LpEVs appear to contain several biologically active molecules, it remains unclear whether a single key molecule is directly responsible for the observed activity against foodborne pathogens. Further studies, including proteomic and metabolic analyses, are necessary to identify and characterize the specific molecules involved in antibiofilm activity.

Generally, common antimicrobial agents are less effective against biofilms than against planktonic cells because of the protective matrix of extracellular polysaccharides that protect the cells within the biofilm (Hossain et al., 2021). Chlorine, a widely used washing and sanitizing agent for fruits and vegetables, is restricted in several European countries because of concerns on the formation of potentially harmful chlorinated organic compounds (Asare et al., 2018). Its efficacy is also often diminished when used against biofilm-forming bacteria (Olaimat and Holley, 2012). Conversely, natural sanitizers, particularly postbiotics such as EVs, have emerged as promising alternatives. They offer antimicrobial benefits with fewer safety concerns than conventional sanitizers (Che et al., 2024). They can be applied as liquid antibiofilm agents and routinely sprayed onto surfaces of fresh produces. For example, clove essential oil effectively reduces microbial contamination on cucumber and lettuce without adversely affecting their sensory qualities (Zhang et al., 2020). Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrated that spraying LpEVs on fresh romaine lettuce effectively reduced microbial contamination by preserving the sensory quality. Therefore, LpEVs might serve as a safe and effective natural antimicrobial agent for maintaining the quality and safety of fresh food products during storage and processing. However, further studies should assess the effectiveness of LpEVs on various produce types under different storage conditions and elucidate the spectrum of antimicrobial activities against diverse foodborne pathogens.

In conclusion, our results suggested that LpEVs effectively inhibited the biofilm formation of L. monocytogenes on abiotic and biotic surfaces potentially by regulating the luxS/AI-2 signaling system. Furthermore, LpEV treatment effectively reduced microbial contamination without affecting the visual sensory quality of romaine lettuce; this finding suggested that it could be used for applications in fresh food products. Although previous studies primarily focused on the immunomodulatory effects of EVs derived from lactobacilli, the present study is the first to demonstrate the potential of LpEVs as a natural antimicrobial agent for food safety and preservation. Although further studies should investigate the efficacy of LpEVs against a broader range of foodborne pathogens within diverse food matrices, our findings demonstrated that LpEVs are a promising, non-chemical intervention strategy in the food industry to enhance food safety.
