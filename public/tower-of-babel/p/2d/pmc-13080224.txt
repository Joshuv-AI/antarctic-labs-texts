Real‐World Effectiveness and Safety of Pembrolizumab–Lenvatinib Versus Axitinib‐Based Regimens in Metastatic Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Kohei Kobatake, Keisuke Goto, Akira Fujita, Takashi Babasaki, Takeshi Ueno, Yoshimasa Kurimura, Kenichiro Fukuoka, Fumiaki Kirishima, Kensuke Nishida, Tetsutaro Hayashi, Nobuyuki Hinata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo compare the real‐world effectiveness and safety of pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib with axitinib‐based regimens (avelumab plus axitinib or pembrolizumab plus axitinib) as first‐line therapy for metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 107 patients treated with immuno‐oncology plus vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor combinations between 2017 and 2025. Propensity score matching (1:1, 0.2 caliper) generated 42 matched pairs. Objective response rate, progression‐free survival, overall survival, and adverse events were assessed. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox regression analyses were performed in the overall cohort, and conditional logistic regression and stratified Cox regression analyses were applied in the matched cohort.ResultsIn the matched cohort, pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib demonstrated a significantly higher objective response rate than axitinib‐based regimens. The incidence of any‐grade and grade ≥ 3 adverse events was similar between regimens. However, pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib was associated with higher rates of hypertension (including grade ≥ 3 hypertension), fatigue, appetite loss, and rash in the full‐dose subgroup. Progression‐free and overall survival did not differ significantly. Multivariate analyses identified pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib treatment as an independent predictor of objective response, whereas survival outcomes were associated with clinical factors rather than the regimen type.ConclusionsPembrolizumab plus lenvatinib achieved a superior tumor response to axitinib‐based regimens, whereas survival outcomes were comparable. Treatment selection should balance enhanced antitumor activity with differences in toxicity profiles.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the sixth and eighth most common cancers in men and women, respectively [1]. Up to 30% of patients with RCC present with metastatic disease at diagnosis, and approximately 20%–30% of those undergoing surgery experience recurrence [2]. The treatment landscape of metastatic RCC (mRCC) has changed considerably, and improvements in survival outcomes have been driven by the development of novel agents, including vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (VEGFRis) and immuno‐oncology (IO) therapies [3, 4, 5]. To date, IO combination therapies, such as IO/IO (ipilimumab and nivolumab) and IO/VEGFRi (IOVE), are available as first‐line (1L) treatment options for mRCC with clear cell histology (mccRCC) regardless of the risk classification in the International mRCC Database Consortium (IMDC) [6, 7]. IOVE regimens consist of avelumab plus axitinib (AA), pembrolizumab plus axitinib (PA), nivolumab plus cabozantinib (NC), and pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib (PL). However, clear distinctions in the patient selection criteria among these regimens have not been established, and evidence remains insufficient for guiding optimal regimen selection.

The phase III CLEAR trial showed that PL achieved particularly robust antitumor activity, with an objective response rate (ORR) of approximately 71% and marked improvements in progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to sunitinib [8, 9]. Recent systematic reviews and network meta‐analyses that indirectly compared 1L IO‐based combination regimens ranked PL among the most active therapies in terms of ORR and PFS, including durable or extremely durable responses, which are defined as PFS ≥ 24 months or ≥ 36 months, respectively; furthermore, these studies often identified PL as the regimen with the highest probability of providing maximal response benefit across IMDC risk groups [10, 11, 12].

Real‐world evidence (RWE) for PL has begun to accumulate. Retrospective cohort studies and registry‐based analyses suggest that PL maintains clinically meaningful effectiveness in routine practice; however, most available RWE focuses on PL as a single regimen, and only a few studies have included multiple IOVE regimens within the same cohort [7, 13].

Axitinib‐based regimens (AA and PA) were the earliest IOVE regimens to be approved and have been widely adopted in clinical practice1 [11, 14, 15]. However, given that no head‐to‐head trials have compared axitinib‐based regimens with PL, the optimal regimen for individual patients remains unknown. In addition, all pivotal phase III trials of IOVE regimens exclusively enrolled patients with RCC with a clear cell component, whereas many RWE studies included mixed histological subtypes. Given this evidence gap, comparative RWE is needed to better inform treatment selection among the currently available IOVE regimens. Thus, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with mccRCC from our institution and affiliated centers to compare the real‐world effectiveness of PL with that of axitinib‐based combination regimens.

Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This retrospective study was conducted as part of the Hiroshima Cancer Registry Project (H‐CARP), which includes Hiroshima University Hospital and its affiliated institutions. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hiroshima University Hospital, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study (approval no. E‐45‐3). The study data were collected and managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture system hosted at Hiroshima University.

Patients who initiated 1L IOVE therapy for RCC at the participating institutions between October 2017 and February 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Among the 191 patients who received 1L IOVE therapy, 157 had mccRCC. Patients treated with the NC regimen were excluded from the analysis. Patients with unavailable or unknown radiological response data, missing survival data, or unknown IMDC risk classification were excluded. In total, 107 patients were included in the final analysis. The selection of the 1L IOVE regimen was based on institutional policies and physician discretion, with individual patient conditions taken into account.

Outcome Measurements

PFS was defined as the time from initiation of 1L IOVE therapy to disease progression or censoring at the last follow‐up during 1L therapy. OS was defined as the time from the initiation of 1L systemic therapy to death from any cause or censoring at the last follow‐up. Eligible patients had evaluable imaging responses, which were defined as baseline computed tomography findings and at least one follow‐up imaging assessment after the initiation of 1L IOVE therapy. Information on distant metastases was collected from the lungs, liver, bones, and non‐regional lymph nodes. In all cases, patients with non‐regional lymph node metastases also had metastases to multiple organs.

Tumor responses were assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 and were classified as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Objective response (OR) was defined as CR or PR, and the ORR was calculated as the proportion of patients achieving an OR. Treatment‐related adverse events (AEs) were assessed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

A propensity score (PS)‐matched model was created with a caliper width of 0.2 and with age, prior nephrectomy, and brain metastasis as covariates on the basis of variations in background factors between patients who underwent AA/PA and PL. A nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm with a 1:1 ratio was used.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate PFS and OS, and differences between groups were assessed using the log‐rank test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., RRID:SCR_002798). Unpaired t‐tests and chi‐square (χ2) tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables between groups, respectively.

In the overall cohort, nominal logistic regression analysis and Cox proportional hazards analysis were performed to identify the predictors of OR, PFS, and OS. The number of variables included in each multivariate model was limited to seven in accordance with the commonly accepted events‐per‐variable principle to minimize overfitting and ensure model stability. To avoid potential bias associated with univariate screening and ensure model objectivity, all predefined clinically relevant variables were entered into the multivariate models regardless of their significance in univariate analyses [16]. These statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (SAS Institute Inc., RRID:SCR_008567).

In the PS‐matched cohort, additional analyses accounting for the matched design were performed. Conditional logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate factors associated with OR, and stratified Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess PFS and OS. These analyses were conducted using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R.

Treatment Outcomes After PSM

Table 2 summarizes the clinical outcomes after PSM. Among all matched patients (n = 42 per group), the overall incidence of AEs was similar between the AA/PA and PL groups (90.5% vs. 92.9%, p = 0.693), as well as the incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs (50.0% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.332). However, ORR was significantly higher in the PL group than in the AA/PA group (76.2% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.013). Similar trends were observed in the subgroup of patients in whom treatment with VEGFRis was initiated at the full dose (n = 41 in the AA/PA group and n = 25 in the PL group). The incidence of any‐grade AEs and grade ≥ 3 AEs did not differ significantly between groups. By contrast, the PL group demonstrated a significantly higher ORR than the AA/PA group (80.0% vs. 51.2%, p = 0.019).

Although the overall incidence of AEs and grade ≥ 3 AEs did not differ significantly between treatment groups (Table 2), the analysis of individual toxicities revealed distinct patterns (Table S2). In the subgroup of patients who initiated VEGFRis at the full dose, hypertension was significantly more frequent in the PL group than in the AA/PA group (p = 0.014), including a higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 hypertension (p = 0.008). Furthermore, fatigue (p = 0.001), appetite loss (p = 0.043), and rashes (p = 0.008) were more frequently observed in the PL group.

Survival Outcomes

Table S4 shows the results of Cox proportional hazards analyses performed to identify the predictors of PFS and OS in the overall cohort. In the multivariate analysis for PFS, age < 75 years and the occurrence of AEs were independently associated with prolonged PFS (the left side of the Table S4). In the multivariate analysis for OS, prior nephrectomy was independently associated with improved OS (the right side of the Table S4). The treatment regimen was not significantly associated with both PFS and OS.

Discussion

In this retrospective real‐world study of patients with mccRCC, PL demonstrated a significantly higher ORR than AA/PA in the PS‐matched cohort. Multivariate analyses further confirmed that treatment with PL was the only independent predictor of OR both in the overall and the PS‐matched cohort. Survival outcomes were more strongly associated with clinical factors such as age, prior nephrectomy, and AE occurrence rather than the treatment regimen itself.

The clinical outcomes shown in Table 2 are consistent with those of previous RWE studies. Studies have shown that a reduced starting dose of VEGFRi in IOVE therapy does not compromise treatment efficacy while improving tolerability [17]. In addition, elderly patients aged ≥ 75 years receiving IOVE therapy have been reported to have significantly lower ORR and higher treatment discontinuation rates because of AEs than those aged < 75 years [18]. The higher ORR observed with PL may partly reflect the differences in the target spectrum of the partner VEGFRi. Lenvatinib inhibits fibroblast growth factor receptors 1–4, in addition to VEGFRs; by contrast, axitinib is more selective for VEGFRs [19]. Preclinical studies have suggested that broader kinase inhibition by lenvatinib can favorably modulate the tumor microenvironment through vascular normalization and immune activation, thereby enhancing the antitumor efficacy of programmed cell death protein 1 blockade [20]. Several studies have reported that the occurrence of immune‐related AEs (irAEs) during IO‐based therapy, including nivolumab plus ipilimumab, AA, or PA, is associated with favorable PFS in patients with mRCC [21, 22]. However, because the AEs evaluated in the present study were not limited to irAEs, further investigations are required to clarify the relationship between AE occurrence and treatment efficacy in the context of IOVE therapy.

Detailed analyses revealed distinct toxicity profiles, as shown in Table S2. Previous pharmacovigilance analyses showed that PL should be selected cautiously because it is significantly associated with the blood and lymphatic system, metabolism and nutrition, and vascular disorders; however, when selecting the PA combination for treatment, the risk of cardiac and hepatobiliary disorders should be noted [23]. We observed a higher incidence of hypertension, including grade ≥ 3 events, in the PL group. Fatigue and appetite loss were also more frequent in patients with PL, thus suggesting distinct toxicity patterns that warrant careful patient selection and monitoring.

In this study, PL achieved a superior ORR to axitinib‐based regimens, whereas the survival outcomes were not different. However, the shorter follow‐up period in the PL group, attributable to its later approval in Japan, should be considered when interpreting survival outcomes, particularly OS. A systematic review and meta‐analysis suggested that response‐based endpoints, such as ORR and CR, may not be reliable surrogates for PFS or OS [24, 25].

Previous RWE evaluations of the PL, AA, and PA have demonstrated clinically meaningful outcomes. Across these reports, median PFS has ranged from 25.6 to 26.8 months for PL and 16 to 18 months for AA/PA, whereas median OS has often not been reached because of limited follow‐up. Similarly, the ORR has been reported to be approximately 50%–60% for AA/PA and 59%–69.5% for PL [7, 13, 26, 27, 28]. The survival and ORR observed for PL and AA/PA in the present study are largely consistent with these reports. Notably, the ORR for PL was 76.2%, representing a high response rate in this cohort. However, our analysis was restricted to patients with mccRCC, whereas earlier studies included mixed histological subtypes. To date, only one prior study directly comparing IOVE regimens has reported superior ORR and longer PFS with NC than with PA in patients with mRCC [29]. Therefore, a real‐world comparative evaluation of all four IOVE regimens, including cabozantinib and nivolumab, is warranted.

This study has a few limitations. First, the retrospective design and relatively small sample size from a regional registry may have introduced selection bias, although the multi‐institutional nature of the cohort partially mitigated this concern. Second, the selection of the 1L IOVE regimen was at the discretion of the treating physicians, thus potentially contributing to baseline imbalances. Third, the AE data in the H‐CARP cohort were based on spontaneous reporting with partially limited details, thus restricting a more granular analysis of the relationship between AEs and treatment outcomes. Finally, the follow‐up period was relatively short‐ and long‐term survival outcomes, particularly OS, required confirmation in larger prospective studies. Furthermore, we did not evaluate the relative dose intensity or the total treatment duration for each systemic therapy, which might have influenced the survival outcomes.

In conclusion, PL was associated with an improved objective tumor response compared with axitinib‐based regimens after adjusting for baseline imbalances, whereas survival outcomes were comparable. Therefore, treatment selection should balance enhanced tumor response against differences in toxicity profiles.
