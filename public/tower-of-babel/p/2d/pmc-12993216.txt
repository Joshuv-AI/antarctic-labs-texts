Developmental human brain connectome from fetal stage to early childhood
Authors: Wentao Wu, Hao Huang, Tianjia Zhu, Shufang Tan, Minhui Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human brain undergoes the most rapid maturation across the lifespan from the fetal stage through early childhood. Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) enable noninvasive mapping of the emerging brain structural and functional connections, which can subsequently be examined using various network-based analytic approaches to characterize how brain network topology evolves during development. Here, we review developmental connectome studies spanning mid-gestation to early childhood using dMRI and rs-fMRI. During this critical early period, unique and dynamic short- and long-range connectivity changes continually reshape the brain connectome. Structural and functional brain networks achieve highly efficient topological architectures in early life, with small-world organization emerging prenatally and adult-like hub distributions observed at birth. Importantly, early connectome development is characterized by a shift from segregation to integration, facilitated by initially faster growth of short-range connections followed by the later strengthening of long-range connections, and demonstrates a hierarchical axis from primary to higher-order regions. Structural connectome maturation is underpinned by the microstructural enhancement of certain white matter fibers and the pruning of others, while functional network emergence is supported by increased cerebral blood flow. Moreover, neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism and schizophrenia are associated with aberrant patterns of hyper- and hypo-connectivity, respectively, and exhibit atypical maturation of brain connectivity, underscoring the need for a developmental perspective. Collectively, this review outlines the spatiotemporal principles of early connectome development, discusses key challenges and methodological considerations in studying the baby brain connectome, setting the stage for understanding aberrant brain development during this vulnerable period.

Introduction

Neural connectivity is a fundamental feature of brain organization, defined as the structural or functional linkages between one brain region and others (NIH RFA-MH-010–020 2010). The human connectome comprises the network of elements (e.g., neurons, brain regions) and connections (e.g., synapses, fiber pathways, temporal signal correlations) that form the brain (Sporns et al., 2005). Early efforts to characterize brain connectivity relied on microscopic histological techniques such as Golgi staining, resulting in Cajal’s neuron doctrine, which first described individual neurons and their arborizations, establishing foundational principles of cortical circuitry (see López-Muñoz et al., 2006; Mesulam, 2012 for reviews). By the mid-20th century, invasive tract-tracing and degeneration methods enabled systematic charting of anatomical wiring and synaptic pathways in animal models (Waller, 1850, Mesulam, 2012, Lanciego and Wouterlood, 2020), producing increasingly refined mesoscale circuit diagrams. In parallel, clinicopathological studies of ‘disconnection syndromes’ in humans inferred large-scale network architecture from focal white matter and cortical lesions (Catani and ffytche, 2005, Geschwind, 1965a, Geschwind, 1965b), presaging modern network-based interpretations of behavior and cognition. In recent decades, advanced noninvasive neuroimaging has revolutionized in vivo, whole-brain mapping of structural and functional connectivity across development and disease, extending earlier invasive and postmortem observations into quantitatively defined, large-scale connectome datasets that can be measured longitudinally. Mapping brain connectivity continues to provide unique insights into how genes, molecules, cells, circuits, and behavior interact, thereby motivating hypotheses about human brain function. Modern connectome analysis offers a unified framework for testing these hypotheses, enabling characterization of macroscale connectivity and advancing our understanding of brain function in health and illness across development.

The human brain undergoes its most rapid development from the fetal period through early childhood, driven by an intensive and precisely regulated spatiotemporal sequence of cellular and molecular processes (e.g., Silbereis et al., 2016), including neurogenesis, neuronal migration (Sidman and Rakic, 1973), synaptogenesis (Huttenlocher and Dabholkar, 1997), dendritic arborization (Bystron et al., 2008, Sidman and Rakic, 1973), axonal growth, pruning (Innocenti and Price, 2005), and myelination (Miller et al., 2012). These developmental processes continuously shape the brain’s microstructural scaffold, supporting the emergence of large-scale network organization that enables increasingly complex information transfer (Cao et al., 2017b, Huang and Vasung, 2014, Ouyang et al., 2019a, Sydnor et al., 2021). At the macroscale, network architecture reflects a trade-off between minimizing wiring costs through dense short-range connections and allowing the formation of costly but efficient topological patterns through long-range connections (e.g., Bassett et al., 2010; Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Ouyang et al., 2017). Perturbations during early life that disrupt the balance between local and distant brain connections have been linked to neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and schizophrenia (SCZ) (e.g., Courchesne and Pierce, 2005; Gogtay et al., 2011; Innocenti and Price, 2005; Ouyang et al., 2017; Paus et al., 2008; Vasa et al., 2016). Thus, mapping the connectome from the fetal stage to childhood is essential for understanding both typical and atypical neurodevelopment.

Here, we review recent advances in understanding the development of the human brain connectome from fetal stages through early childhood. First, we briefly introduce the construction of structural and functional connectomes using modern neuroimaging techniques. Next, we discuss how short- and long-range connections dynamically reshape both structural and functional networks. We then consolidate key findings on structural connectome development using diffusion MRI (dMRI), as well as its relationship with the underlying white matter microstructural maturation. We also summarize findings on functional connectome development, focusing on the emergence of large-scale networks and gradients revealed by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), and highlight their physiological underpinnings. We further review how connectome maturation diverges from typical trajectories in neurodevelopmental disorders, focusing primarily on ASD and SCZ. Finally, we discuss key challenges and methodological considerations in studying the developmental connectome. Through this review, we aim to synthesize current findings that chart the spatiotemporal dynamics of structural and functional connectome maturation and provide a foundation for understanding atypical brain connectomic development.

How to construct structural and functional connectomes

Structural connectivity describes the physical wiring of the brain, ranging from axonal pathways linking neurons at the microscale to white matter fiber tracts connecting large-scale brain regions at the macroscale, together with their associated biophysical properties (Johansen-Berg and Rushworth, 2009; Sporns, 2005). DMRI and dMRI-based tractography enable the characterization of structural connectivity features such as length and strength (upper left panels of Fig. 1). Structural connectivity strength is commonly quantified using tractography-based attributes, including the number of streamlines or tract volume, as well as dMRI-derived measures such as fractional anisotropy (FA) along the reconstructed tracts. Notably, dMRI-based fiber generation itself depends strongly on the chosen tractography algorithm (e.g., Behrens et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2004; Mori et al., 1999; Tournier et al., 2004), which can further influence the resulting characterized connectivity architecture. Specifically, deterministic tractography typically reduces false-positive bundles but at the cost of more false negatives, whereas probabilistic methods lower false negatives but tend to produce more false positives (Maier-Hein et al., 2017). Structural connectivity obtained by dMRI-based tractography, especially for the long-range connections, can also be used to construct white matter atlases (e.g., Feng et al., 2019; Khan et al., 2019; Mori et al., 2008; Oishi et al., 2011; Song et al., 2024). Additionally, structural covariance derived from structural MRI capturing coordinated morphological changes by identifying spatial correlations in anatomical morphology, such as cortical volume, cortical thickness, and surface area, across regions has also been used to study the structural connectome, although it infers connectivity indirectly and does not reflect white matter fiber pathways (Evans, 2013, Lerch et al., 2006).Fig. 1Construction and developmental trajectories of the structural and functional connectomes. Functional connectivity refers to the statistical dependence of neural activity between brain regions (David et al., 2004, Friston, 1994). It is most commonly measured through the correlation of temporal fluctuations of blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signals across brain regions using rs-fMRI (Biswal et al., 1995, Biswal and Uddin, 2025), with functional connectivity strength typically represented by the Pearson correlation coefficient of BOLD signals between region pairs (bottom left panels in Fig. 1). Beyond rs-fMRI, functional connectivity can also be assessed through electroencephalogram (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG) coherence analysis, which quantifies synchronization of neural oscillations in greater temporal precision (e.g., Engel and Singer, 2001; Lachaux et al., 1999) but has limited spatial resolution and depth sensitivity as a result of recording from over the scalp (Gross et al., 2013, Hämäläinen et al., 1993). Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), which enables functional connectivity estimation through temporal correlations of hemodynamic signals detected by near-infrared light, is portable and well-suited for awake infants, yet limited to the superficial cortex (Gervain et al., 2023). Additionally, arterial spin labeled perfusion MRI measures, which quantify cerebral blood flow (CBF) as a surrogate measure of brain metabolism, provide insights into vascular functional networks by assessing cerebral blood flow covariance (e.g., Melie-Garcia et al., 2013; Ngo et al., 2024). Similarly, functional connectivity can be used to derive functional network atlases in adults (e.g., Power et al., 2011; Yeo et al., 2011) and across development (e.g., Mohapatra et al., 2025; Myers et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023a). Establishing functional atlases for neonatal and pediatric populations is particularly important for studying early development, as their network architectures are still evolving and differ markedly from those of adults.

Given the rapid morphological changes occurring from the fetal stage through childhood, age-specific brain atlases (e.g., Ahmad et al., 2023; Bozek et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2019; Gholipour et al., 2017; Oishi et al., 2011; Song et al., 2024) are often essential for delineating accurate anatomical regions when constructing structural and functional connectomes in the developing brain. Once structural or functional connectivity matrices (middle panels of Fig. 1) are established, representative methods such as graph-theoretical analyses (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009, Rubinov and Sporns, 2010) and diffusion embedding approaches (Coifman et al., 2005) can be used to quantify network organization at both local and global levels, and are elaborated in the sections below.

Short- and long-range connectivity dynamically reshape the connectome

Macroscopic brain connectivity relies on both local links between neighboring regions, referred to as short-range connections, and long-distance projections connecting remote areas, referred to as long-range connections (middle panels in Fig. 1) (Ouyang et al., 2017). Specifically, short-range structural connections usually refer to white matter fiber tracts connecting adjacent gyri located in superficial white matter regions (e.g., U fibers), and short-range functional connections indicate functional connectivity between the regions close in space, measured by Euclidean distance (e.g., less than 75 mm). In contrast, long-range structural connections are represented by long axonal fiber bundles located in deep white matter, and long-range functional connections indicate functional connectivity between the distant regions in space (e.g., larger than 75 mm). Short- and long-range connections follow unique developmental patterns during early life (Cao et al., 2017a, Damaraju et al., 2014b, Ouyang et al., 2016b, Thomason et al., 2015, Zhao et al., 2019) that dynamically reshape the connectome to achieve an economic trade-off between wiring cost and efficiency, supporting the optimization of network segregation and integration (see Ouyang et al., 2017 for review). Achieving an appropriate balance between long- and short-range connectivity is crucial for efficient information processing, as well as cognitive and behavioral maturation in the developing brain (Cao et al., 2017b, Ouyang et al., 2017). Disruptions in this balance have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD and SCZ (e.g., Courchesne and Pierce, 2005; Gogtay et al., 2011; Innocenti and Price, 2005; Paus et al., 2008; Vasa et al., 2016).

Developmental structural connectome

From the fetal stage to childhood, short-range fibers emerge first and dominate in the brain connectome (Hagmann et al., 2010, Ouyang et al., 2017), followed by the emergence of long-range fibers starting from 13 postmenstrual weeks (PMW) and their subsequent maturation into childhood, revealed by dMRI tractography and microstructure studies (Huang et al., 2006, Huang et al., 2009, Lebel et al., 2008, Ouyang et al., 2019a, Takahashi et al., 2012). At 20 PMW, the fetal brain is dominated by short-range fibers with almost no long-range connections, and from 20 to 40 PMW, long-range pathways progressively increase (Song et al., 2017). Moreover, observations from dMRI tractography suggest that long-range fibers emerge in a sequential manner: with limbic tracts emerging first, followed by commissural and projection fibers that develop in an anterior-to-posterior gradient, and finally, the association fibers (Huang et al., 2006, Huang et al., 2009, Song et al., 2017, Takahashi et al., 2012; see review Ouyang et al., 2019a). Throughout development, the balance of short- and long-range white matter tracts dynamically changes, with the portion of short-range fiber decreasing from birth to adolescence, and then gradually increasing to adulthood (Ouyang et al., 2016b). While there are limited studies on short-range fiber microstructure, many studies on long-range fiber microstructure complement tractography studies and show continued and gradual maturation of long-range fibers (e.g., Dubois et al., 2008; Huppi et al., 1998; Lebel et al., 2008; Sadeghi et al., 2013; Wilson et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2020). For example, a dMRI study found that FA in the long-range major WM tracts follows a characteristic three‑stage developmental trajectory from 0 to 8 years old: an initial phase of rapid maturation, followed by a period of slower, more gradual change, and finally a plateau phase toward the end of this age range (Yu et al., 2020). These developmental trajectories also vary across white matter tract groups (Lebel et al., 2008, Sadeghi et al., 2013, Yu et al., 2020), with commissural tracts connecting primary sensorimotor regions typically exhibiting the earliest maturation, while association tracts projecting to the prefrontal cortex are among the slowest to develop (Yu et al., 2020). This pattern aligns with the well-established developmental gradient from early-maturing primary sensorimotor systems to later-developing higher-order association and executive functional systems underlined by long-range connectivity (Cao et al., 2017a, Gao et al., 2009, Li et al., 2024).

The dynamic changes of short- and long-range fibers during early developmental stages facilitate brain network segregation and integration (Ball et al., 2014, Calixto et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Hagmann et al., 2010, Huang et al., 2015, Song et al., 2017, Tymofiyeva et al., 2013, Zhao et al., 2019). Network segregation is enhanced during the fetal stage, as suggested by the emergence of small-world, modular, and rich club architectures in fetal structural networks (Calixto et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Song et al., 2017, Tymofiyeva et al., 2013). During the third trimester, short-range rich-club connectivity also exhibits higher developmental rates than long-range connections (Fig. 2A-B), indicating the focus on developing network segregation (Zhao et al., 2019). The strengthening of network segregation, likely underpinned by the enhancement of dominant short-range connections, is also reflected by the changing pattern of structural hubs (Fig. 3A). The degree distribution of structural hubs was found to follow an exponentially truncated power law, reflecting the existence of a small number of highly efficient hubs as early as 30 PMW (Ball et al., 2014, Van Den Heuvel et al., 2015, Zhao et al., 2019). From 32–42 PMW, different hub subtypes follow asynchronous maturation trajectories, and nodal efficiency increases vary markedly across regions (Fig. 3B) (Zhao et al., 2019), with higher developmental rates observed in provincial hubs that mediate within-module communication (i.e., short-range connections) compared to connector hubs that enable between-module communication (i.e., long-range connections) (Zhao et al., 2019). After birth, network segregation begins to decrease, and the strengthening of integration takes over. Measures of network segregation, such as modularity, clustering coefficient, and characteristic path length, are lower in 6-month-old infants compared to term-age neonates (Tymofiyeva et al., 2013). By around two years of age, toddlers show decreased modularity but increased inter-module connections and global efficiency compared to neonates (Huang et al., 2015, Yap et al., 2011). These findings collectively indicate an ongoing decrease in network segregation and a progressive enhancement of network integration, underscoring the network transition from locally specialized to globally integrated throughout early childhood. This transition is also supported by the relocation and changes of structural hubs. Despite the relatively consistent spatial organization of hubs from birth through early childhood (Fig. 3A) (Huang et al., 2015), structural hubs are progressively strengthening their efficiency as the brain continues to mature, thereby enhancing network global integration (Ball et al., 2014, Huang et al., 2015, Zhao et al., 2019, Zhao et al., 2026). Furthermore, some of these hubs, which are initially concentrated in primary motor and sensory cortices, gradually shift toward higher-order association regions over development, supporting increasingly integrated, large-scale information processing (Betzel et al., 2014, Hwang et al., 2013, Tomasi and Volkow, 2011). It is noteworthy that the structural connectome development pattern described here is restricted by factors that limit dMRI tractography (see Jbabdi and Johansen-Berg, 2011 for review). For example, dMRI tractography of the fetal brain cannot distinguish white matter fibers from the transient radial glial scaffold. Although most developmental connectome studies focus on corticocortical connectivity, thalamocortical connectivity also plays an important role in brain development. Thalamic outputs are topographically organized, linking specific nuclei to functionally specialized cortical regions and forming the structural basis for both unimodal and transmodal cortical circuits (Oldham and Ball, 2023, Park et al., 2024). These pathways lay the foundation for cognitive and behavioral development, and their connectivity has been shown to be significantly correlated with behavioral outcomes in infants and children (Alcauter et al., 2014, Ball et al., 2015). Structural thalamocortical connections mature following distinct temporal courses. Prior dMRI studies have shown that thalamocortical projections to primary sensory areas mature earlier than those targeting transmodal regions (Wilson et al., 2023, Zheng et al., 2023), a pattern consistent with corticocortical connectivity maturation (Zhao et al., 2019). The accelerated maturation of sensorimotor circuits likely reflects the need to support early developing behaviors such as feeding and basic motor control, which are essential immediately after birth.

Developmental functional connectome

From the fetal stage to childhood, short-range functional connectivity develops first, followed by the enhancement of long-range functional connectivity and its continued maturation throughout childhood (Cao et al., 2017a, Damaraju et al., 2014b, Fair et al., 2009, Jakab et al., 2014, Smyser et al., 2010, Thomason et al., 2014, Thomason et al., 2015). Dense local connectivity is evident in the fetal brain (Thomason et al., 2014), and short-range connectivity has been shown to increase with gestational age in neonates (Cao et al., 2017a, Jakab et al., 2014, Smyser et al., 2010). More pronounced age-related increases in short- to middle-range functional connectivity strength than in long-range connectivity from 31 to 42 PMW were observed (Fig. 2C-D) (Cao et al., 2017a). Similarly, a prevalence of short-range functional connectivity is also found in preterm infants aged from 26 PMW through term equivalent age, with long-range functional connectivity identifiable only in older infants (Smyser et al., 2010). Notably, the dominant development of primary regions prior to birth, supported by the drastic increase in short- to mid-range connections (Fig. 2C), likely underpins early sensory and motor functions essential for postnatal survival (Cao et al., 2017a). In early infancy, from 4 to 9 months, short-range functional connectivity decreases, which may reflect the synaptic pruning and refinement of local circuits (Damaraju et al., 2014b). In contrast, long-range functional connections remain relatively immature during the prenatal stage and continue to strengthen during the first two years of life (Cao et al., 2017a, Damaraju et al., 2014b, Li et al., 2024, Thomason et al., 2015). This pattern of decreased short-range connectivity and increased long-range connectivity persists from toddlerhood through adulthood (Fair et al., 2009, Grayson and Fair, 2017).

The unique maturation pattern of short- and long-range functional connectivity facilitates functional network development from primary to higher-order systems, and from segregation to integration (Cao et al., 2017a, Gao et al., 2009, Li et al., 2024, Sun et al., 2025). Many resting-state networks emerge during the preterm period and are largely present by term (e.g., Cao et al., 2017a; Doria et al., 2010; Fitzgibbon et al., 2020), with primary sensorimotor, visual, and auditory networks most clearly appreciated (Fig. 4A). From 31–41 PMW, functional hubs are predominantly located in primary cortical regions, such as the sensorimotor and visual cortices, and continuously expand and enhance with development (Fig. 4A) (Cao et al., 2017a, Li et al., 2024). Concurrently, nodal degree in the functional connectome significantly increases in primary regions during the third trimester, but much less so in higher-order functional regions (Fig. 4B) (Cao et al., 2017a). Interestingly, primary sensorimotor and visual areas show low individual variability in functional connectivity in the neonate brain, while association regions demonstrate high variability (Stoecklein et al., 2020, Xu et al., 2019). This pattern persists, but the magnitude of variability decreases globally until around one year of age before increasing again by age two, suggesting an interaction of genetic and environmental influences (Gao et al., 2014, Stoecklein et al., 2020). Moreover, these primary networks, including visual, auditory, and motor networks, integrated portions of the thalamus by term, the time the whole thalamus has developed, suggesting the potential link between spatially distributed resting-state networks and thalamocortical connectivity (Doria et al., 2010, Zhang et al., 2008). Additionally, the early rapid maturation of primary networks gradually enhances network segregation. Distinct small-world and modular organization, as well as emerging rich-club architectures, were detected prior to the term (Cao et al., 2017a, Li et al., 2024, Thomason et al., 2014, Turk et al., 2019). These network properties represent the enhanced network segregation due to dominant short-range connectivity. Higher-order functional networks, such as the default mode network (DMN) (Buckner et al., 2008) and executive control network, remain immature and topologically incomplete at birth compared with those in the adult brain (Doria et al., 2010, Fransson et al., 2011, Gao et al., 2009, Karolis et al., 2023, Smyser et al., 2010), likely due to the immature long-range connections. The DMN begins to emerge from birth to two years of life, along with the maturation of long-range connections, characterized by increasing functional connectivity between the posterior cingulate gyrus (PCG) (a vital hub of DMN) and other DMN regions (Fig. 4A) (Yu et al., 2023). The DMN evolves into a relatively mature architecture at around one year of age, while the executive control network continues to develop throughout the first few years of life (Gao et al., 2015). It is worth noting that thalamocortical connectivity development also follows this hierarchy, with thalamus-sensorimotor and thalamus-salience connectivity networks already present in neonates, whereas the thalamus-medial visual and thalamus-DMN connectivity emerged later, at 1 year of age (Alcauter et al., 2014). By toddlerhood, functional network complexity continues to increase. Nodal efficiency increases, reflecting enhanced network integration, and hub regions begin to shift from primary to higher-order association areas (Li et al., 2024), likely underlying the emergence of higher cognitive and socio-emotional capabilities during childhood. Interestingly, the DMN and frontoparietal networks undergo faster development of network segregation during infancy and childhood, probably due to their initially immature functional architecture present in utero and the subsequent need for rapid postnatal maturation to support the emergence of higher-order cognitive capacities (Sun et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2023). Cortical gradients derived from diffusion embedding analysis (Coifman et al., 2005) also support this hierarchical maturation pattern (Huntenburg et al., 2018, Margulies et al., 2016). A principal sensorimotor-to-visual gradient has been found to emerge during the third trimester (Fig. 4C) (Xia et al., 2024). Both anterior-to-posterior and sensorimotor-to-visual gradients are first observed at this stage (Xia et al., 2024). While sensorimotor-to-visual gradient remains stable throughout childhood, it gradually transforms into the adult-like configuration during adolescence (Dong et al., 2021), characterized by two core functional gradients, including a principal gradient from primary sensorimotor and visual cortices to transmodal association cortices, and a secondary gradient separating sensorimotor and visual cortices (Margulies et al., 2016).

Summary of developmental connectome

To summarize, the unique developmental patterns of short- and long-range connectivity from the fetal stage to childhood are underpinned by a sequence of cellular and molecular processes, including synaptogenesis, dendritic arborization, myelination, and synaptic pruning. The dynamic balance between the short- and long-range connectivity reflects both strengthening (including myelination) of some fibers and pruning of others, continuously shaping the maturation of the brain connectome. This maturation is characterized by a progression from network segregation to integration, and hierarchically from primary to higher-order association systems. The transition from segregation to integration is likely driven by the rapid strengthening of short-range connections early in development, followed by the later enhancement of long-range connections (middle right panel in Fig. 1). The hierarchical maturation from primary to association regions is supported by the earlier beginning of neurobiological processes in primary cortices compared to higher-order regions. The close synchrony observed between structural and functional developmental trajectories suggests that the maturation of structural connectivity provides the foundational scaffold for the emergence and refinement of functional network organization (upper right panel in Fig. 1).

Underpinnings of structural and functional connectome maturation

White matter fiber bundles, connecting different brain regions (Hagmann et al., 2008, Huang et al., 2015), constitute the structural connectivity that forms the complex structural brain network. The heterogeneous microstructural maturation of different white matter fibers underlies the formation and evolution of human brain networks during development. The maturation of white matter microstructure from the fetal stage through early childhood has been extensively studied using dMRI-derived measurements such as FA, a sensitive indicator of the underlying white matter microstructural organization (e.g., Dubois et al., 2008; Huang et al., 2009; Huang et al., 2006; Jeon et al., 2015; Lebel et al., 2008; Lebel and Beaulieu, 2011; Mishra et al., 2013; Ouyang et al., 2019a; Yu et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2020). The general trend of FA increases in white matter regions during this early developmental stage usually reflects underlying cellular processes such as axonal growth, axonal packing, and myelination (Dubois et al., 2008, Ouyang et al., 2019a). Concurrently, the structural brain network becomes stronger, more efficient, and more robust (e.g., Huang et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2026). The tight relationship between network topology and underlying white matter microstructural organization is supported by the significant positive correlations between network metrics (e.g., global and local efficiency) and white matter FA at the whole brain and regional levels during development (Fig. 3C), suggesting the emergence of the maturing brain networks is underpinned by microstructural enhancement of white matter tracts (Huang et al., 2015). However, these correlations are also not uniform across network nodes (Fig. 3C). While the right posterior cingulate gyrus (PCG-R) shows the strong and positive nodal efficiency-white matter FA correlation and the most rapid age-related increase in nodal efficiency through infancy and childhood (Huang et al., 2015), its primary connecting tract, the cingulate bundle, does not undergo the most rapid increase in FA among all white matter tracts during this period (e.g., Huang et al., 2006; Lebel et al., 2008; Ouyang et al., 2019a; Yu et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2020). Therefore, the most rapid developmental rise in nodal efficiency at PCG-R cannot be explained solely by microstructural enhancement of its primary connecting fiber bundles. Instead, other processes, such as the pruning of small fibers or axonal branches, may also contribute to the regional variability of nodal efficiency change rates (Huang et al., 2015).

Hierarchical functional network maturation in early brain development has been extensively characterized with rs-fMRI studies (e.g., Cao et al., 2017a; Doria et al., 2010; Fitzgibbon et al., 2020; Fransson et al., 2011; Smyser et al., 2010). The differential emergence and maturation pattern of functional networks is reflected in their distinct onset times and the varying maturation rates in a given developmental period. In parallel, rapid brain maturation during early life demands substantial energy to support the growth (Pontzer et al., 2021, Kuzawa et al., 2014). Because neurons lack internal energy reserves, these increased metabolic demands must be met by extra oxygen and glucose delivery through CBF. CBF is tightly related to cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO₂) and glucose (CMRGlu), and is often used as a surrogate measure of local brain metabolism (Gur et al., 2009, Ouyang et al., 2024, Vaishnavi et al., 2010). The differentiated cerebral metabolic pattern reflected by regional CBF distribution and differential increase rates of the regional CBF across different developmental stages (e.g., Ouyang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023) is strikingly consistent with the spatiotemporally differentiated functional maturation processes (e.g., Cao et al., 2017; Doria et al., 2010; Smyser et al., 2010; Yu et al., 2023). Tight coupling between the maturation of the functional connectome and CBF changes has been reported across developmental periods (Baller et al., 2022, Liang et al., 2013, Yu et al., 2023). For example, in parallel to the DMN emergence during infancy, indicated by increases of functional connectivity within DWN regions (Fig. 5A), regional CBF also rises rapidly and unevenly across cortical regions from birth to 24 months (Fig. 5B) (Ouyang et al., 2024, Yu et al., 2023). Crucially, the functional emergence of the DMN, represented by increases of functional connectivity within it, was found to be coupled with regional CBF increases specifically within the DMN regions, but not in primary visual or sensorimotor regions (Fig. 5C) (Yu et al., 2023). As synaptogenesis proceeds, neurons within a functional network become more synchronized in their activity (e.g., Kerschensteiner, 2014). During infancy, a likely cascade of events was hypothesized (Fig. 5D), beginning with rising regional CBF, which facilitates increased CMRO₂ and CMRGlu, supporting synaptogenesis and synaptic efficacy increase, enhancing BOLD signal synchronization, and ultimately strengthening functional connectivity (Yu et al., 2023). Furthermore, the continued strengthening of higher-order networks from ages 8–22 years is supported by the coupling between CBF and the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations in the frontoparietal network (Baller et al., 2022). The coupling between CBF and functional connectivity is stronger in higher-order regions with dense long-range connections, indicating efficient information processing at the cost of high energy consumption (Liang et al., 2013). Atypical development of the connectome

Disrupted or aberrant connectivity has been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD and SCZ (Belmonte et al., 2004, Courchesne and Pierce, 2005, Gogtay et al., 2011, Vasa et al., 2016). For ASD, postmortem histology studies have reported an increased number of minicolumns and dendritic spine density in the cortex (Courchesne and Pierce, 2005, Hutsler and Zhang, 2010, Minshew and Williams, 2007, Penzes et al., 2011, Tang et al., 2014). Evidence from MRI-based connectome studies typically suggests ‘hyperconnectivity’ during childhood and ‘hypoconnectivity’ during adolescence and adulthood in ASD (bottom right panel of Fig. 1) (e.g., Nomi and Uddin, 2015; Rudie and Dapretto, 2013; Ouyang et al., 2022). Likewise, postmortem histology studies in SCZ have found an exaggeration of synaptic elimination or pruning (Feinberg, 1982, McGlashan and Hoffman, 2000, Paus et al., 2008), and MRI-based connectome studies have frequently observed a ‘hypoconnectivity’ pattern (bottom right panel of Fig. 1). In this section, we review macroscale evidence of how the brain connectome deviates from normal development in atypical populations using ASD and SCZ as examples.

ASD is characterized by difficulties in social communication and interaction, along with repetitive behaviors and restricted interests (Constantino and Charman, 2016). Numerous prior studies have reported heterogeneous patterns of aberrant brain connectivity in ASD, encompassing both hyperconnectivity and hypoconnectivity (e.g., Dajani and Uddin, 2016; Keown et al., 2013; Maximo et al., 2013; Nomi and Uddin, 2015; Ouyang et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rudie et al., 2013; Shukla et al., 2010; Supekar et al., 2013; Uddin et al., 2013). Brain hyperconnectivity is more commonly observed in younger children with ASD (e.g., Keown et al., 2013; Ouyang et al., 2022; Supekar et al., 2013; Uddin et al., 2013). For example, FA increases, and radial diffusivity decreases have been observed in widespread white matter tracts in children with ASD before the age of 5 (e.g., Ben Bashat et al., 2007; Ouyang et al., 2016a). Both whole-brain and subsystem analyses have reported hyperconnectivity across short- and long-range structural and functional connections (Ouyang et al., 2022, Supekar et al., 2013). Regional homogeneity analyses using rs-fMRI have shown increased short-range functional connectivity within the DMN, visual, and motor networks in ASD (Washington et al., 2014). Such a hyperconnectivity pattern in local circuits observed in ASD is likely due to the increased dendritic spine density with reduced developmental spine pruning (Hutsler and Zhang, 2010, Penzes et al., 2011, Tang et al., 2014). In contrast, older populations with ASD more commonly exhibit hypoconnectivity (e.g., Cherkassky et al., 2006; Shukla et al., 2010; Uddin et al., 2013). For example, older children with ASD showed decreased functional connectivity in superior parietal and prefrontal regions (Shukla et al., 2010), and adolescents and adults with ASD showed hypoconnectivity in anterior-posterior connections (Cherkassky et al., 2006). The progression from hyperconnectivity to hypoconnectivity during development is likely due to the slower maturation rate of brain connectivity in ASD (e.g., Edgar et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2016a; Ouyang et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2020; Nomi and Uddin, 2015; Rudie and Dapretto, 2013), highlighting the importance of studying the aberrant connectome in ASD from a developmental perspective. Moreover, the disrupted balance between short- and long-range connectivity also contributes to impaired network segregation and integration observed in ASD. Reduced network integration with weaker within-network connectivity and impaired segregation with abnormally strong inter-network connectivity were found in individuals with ASD (Fishman et al., 2015, Keown et al., 2017, Ouyang et al., 2022, Rudie et al., 2012). Furthermore, hyperconnectivity in children with ASD is positively associated with their symptom severity (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2021; Ouyang et al., 2022; Supekar et al., 2013) and brain connectivity in high-risk infants robustly predicted later ASD diagnosis (Emerson et al., 2017, Dickinson et al., 2021). Integrating these connectivity-based markers with advanced machine learning techniques holds immense potential for understanding the complexities of early brain development and identifying targets in high-risk individuals for early interventions.

SCZ is characterized by mental impairments, including hallucinations, delusions, avolition, apathy, anhedonia, and loss of cognitive functions across multiple domains (Tandon et al., 2009). A widespread pattern of hypoconnectivity has been found in both structural and functional connectome studies (e.g., Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013; Jiang et al., 2015; Phillips et al., 2011; Nazeri et al., 2013). DMRI studies have reported reduced FA across major association and projection white matter tracts, corpus callosum, and superficial white matter regions in individuals with SCZ, indicating widespread impairments in structural connectivity (Phillips et al., 2011; Nazeri et al., 2013). Similarly, fMRI studies have demonstrated reduced short-range functional connectivity in regions such as the right postcentral gyrus and the left middle occipital cortex (Jiang et al., 2015), as well as globally across the brain (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013). Importantly, reductions in short-range functional connectivity appear more prominent in early-onset compared to late-onset SCZ (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013, Jiang et al., 2015), suggesting the role of the developmental time window in SCZ connectivity disruptions. Decreased network modularity, reflecting reduced segregation of functional systems, has also been observed in SCZ (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013), which may be attributed to the short-range hypoconnectivity. These hypoconnectivity patterns are likely related to the exaggerated synaptic pruning during development in SCZ (Feinberg, 1982, McGlashan and Hoffman, 2000, Murray and Lewis, 1987, Weinberger, 1987). However, hyperconnectivity has also been observed in SCZ, particularly in functional connectivity. For instance, increased functional connectivity has been reported in the superior frontal gyrus (Jiang et al., 2015), DMN (Repovs et al., 2011), the subcortical system, and interhemispheric links (Guo et al., 2014). Part of this heterogeneity is thought to arise from the predominant use of static functional connectivity measures, which provide valuable information but may not fully capture the inherently dynamic and time-varying nature of interactions between brain networks (Damaraju et al., 2014a). Moreover, the abnormal functional connectivity in SCZ is associated with symptom severity (Jiang et al., 2015, Shukla et al., 2019). Specifically, short-range functional connectivity strength, but not long-range connectivity, has been shown to correlate with clinical symptom severity (Jiang et al., 2015). The reduced medial orbitofrontal cortex-striatum functional connectivity in SCZ is found to be associated with the severity of negative symptoms (Shukla et al., 2019). Together, these findings highlight the potential of brain connectivity as a sensitive biomarker for tracking disease progression in SCZ.

Challenges and methodological considerations

Acquisition challenges and their mitigations

MRI acquisition in the developing brain is challenging due to motion caused by spontaneous movement, respiration, and restlessness, which produces interscan and intrascan artifacts (Dubois et al., 2014, Huang and Roberts, 2021). Prospective (e.g., navigated MR sequences, see Tisdall et al., 2012, White et al., 2010; optical motion tracking systems such as TracInnovations, Denmark) and retrospective correction methods (e.g., segmenting scans into shorter units; Kochunov et al., 2006) can help mitigate motion related artifacts. For fMRI, novel sequences such as multiecho echo planar imaging (EPI) were developed to differentiate neurally related BOLD signal from motion and physiology artifacts (Kundu et al., 2012). Pipelines integrating both prospective and retrospective motion correction techniques, such as PROPELLER, synergize advantages of each technique (Pipe, 1999). More recently, deep-learning-based retrospective correction approaches such as BME-X (Sun et al., 2024) have been developed to recover motion-corrupted structural MRI scans. To tackle the unique challenge of fetal motion, fast acquisitions integrated with modern slice-to-volume reconstruction methods (Ebner et al., 2020, Gholipour et al., 2010, Kuklisova-Murgasova et al., 2012, Rousseau et al., 2006) allow for both motion-robust and high-resolution MRI.

Apart from motion, the smaller size of the pediatric brain necessitates higher spatial resolution. However, increasing spatial resolution reduces voxel volume, leading to a proportional decrease in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). To compensate for this SNR loss, additional signal averaging is typically required, which in turn lengthens acquisition time. Advances in data acquisition, such as simultaneous multislice acquisition (Sotiropoulos et al., 2013), can dramatically speed up image acquisition. This permits greater signal averaging for a given scan time, and the associated improvement in SNR can be used to acquire data with higher spatial resolution. More recently, emerging dMRI technologies (e.g., Setsompop et al., 2018) allow submillimeter resolution. However, smaller voxels reduce SNR, lengthen scan times, and cause more severe B0 inhomogeneity and motion artifacts. Dedicated neonatal and pediatric coils (e.g., Hughes et al., 2017; Somerville et al., 2018) boost SNR by improving coil filling. Increased artifacts and decreased SNR in developmental MRI datasets also demand tailored processing pipelines (e.g., Dean et al., 2024; Fitzgibbon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2019a; Somerville et al., 2018), incorporating advanced techniques such as within-volume motion correction and denoising. Moreover, due to rapidly changing tissue properties such as relaxation properties and diffusivity, optimized acquisition pipelines are necessary. Rapid developmental changes in T1/T2 relaxation cause poor gray-white matter contrast (e.g., Barkovich, 2000), demanding age-specific acquisition parameters (e.g., Harms et al., 2018). Specialized processing techniques were also developed to segment gray and white matter from early-life structural MRI with poor contrast (e.g., Billot et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023b; Zollei et al., 2020). For dMRI, acquisition parameters also need to be adjusted (e.g., Feng et al., 2019; Harms et al., 2018; Howell et al., 2019; Hutter et al., 2018; Ouyang et al., 2015). The rule-of-thumb (b-value × Apparent Diffusivity Coefficient ≈ 1) suggests lower b-values (≈ 700 s/mm²) for young brains with high water diffusivity, and at least 30 uniformly distributed gradient directions are required for robust tensor fitting (Huang and Roberts, 2021).

Data harmonization

Substantial variability arises from preprocessing and site-specific factors. Large developmental cohorts such as the NIH-sponsored HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) study (Dean et al., 2024, Nelson et al., 2024), the Baby Connectome Project (BCP) (Howell et al., 2019), the Human Connectome Project: Development (HCP-D) (Somerville et al., 2018), the fetal-neonate and infant brain development dataset (www.brainmrimap.org) (Cao et al., 2017a, Ouyang et al., 2019b), and the ERC-sponsored developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) (Edwards et al., 2022), provide statistical power to delineate spatiotemporal changes of brain connectivity, but highlight the difficulty of harmonizing data across differences in hardware, pulse sequences, acquisition parameters, and preprocessing choices. These discrepancies can alter connectivity and network topology, undermining cross-study comparability and limiting generalizability. Technical standardization through shared pulse sequences, phantoms, and reconstruction tools (e.g., Somerville et al., 2018) helps reduce scanner-dependent variation. Statistical harmonization approaches, including mixed-effects models (Bethlehem et al., 2022, Sun et al., 2025) and empirical Bayes frameworks such as ComBat (Fortin et al., 2017, Johnson et al., 2007), ComBat-GAM (Pomponio et al., 2020), and CovBat (Chen et al., 2022), mitigate site effects while preserving biological effects, though their underlying assumptions require careful validation in pediatric populations.

Network node and edge definition challenges

Defining network nodes and edges is a particularly delicate task in the immature brain. Since brain morphology and tissue contrast change rapidly in early life, age-specific structural and dMRI-based atlases (e.g., Ahmad et al., 2023; Bozek et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2019; Gholipour et al., 2017; Oishi et al., 2011; Song et al., 2024) that better capture developmental anatomy can be used to improve node definitions. The edge estimations in structural networks are affected by the used dMRI tractography algorithms (Maier-Hein et al., 2017). Probabilistic tractography methods (e.g., Behrens et al., 2007; Tournier et al., 2004) increase sensitivity to complex fibers and yield dense connectomes, but with false positives. Deterministic tractography methods (e.g., Huang et al., 2004; Mori et al., 1999) improve specificity and yield sparse connectomes, but with false negatives. DMRI tractography cannot distinguish radial glial cells in the fetal brain from white matter fibers (e.g., Huang et al., 2006; Huang et al., 2009; Huang and Vasung, 2014; Song et al., 2017; Takahashi et al., 2012; Wilson et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2014). Hence, it is often necessary to apply a length filter to the streamlines to filter out the effect of radial glial cells (e.g., Song et al., 2017). Limited angular resolution, low SNR, and immature white matter further increase errors in fetal and neonatal dMRI tractography. Consequently, dMRI tractography often diverges from tracer-derived ground truth (Thomas et al., 2014). Functional connectivity also requires age-appropriate modeling of the hemodynamic response, which shows delayed and lower-amplitude hemodynamic response functions (HRFs) and altered resting-state frequency spectra (e.g., Arichi et al., 2012; Denisova, 2019; Zhang et al., 2019), necessitating developmentally tuned filtering and band selection.

Interpretation considerations

Interpretation of connectome analysis requires careful consideration. Graph-theory derived metrics are powerful mathematical abstractions (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009, Rubinov and Sporns, 2010), but they depend heavily on methodological choices. Their absolute values are not directly comparable across preprocessing pipelines and studies. The differences in topological measures across studies may reflect the influence of imaging resolution. For example, the number of streamlines in dMRI tractography is proportional to the number of seed voxels and hence imaging resolution. BOLD-fMRI is an indirect neural signal dependent on an assumed relationship between neuronal activity and regional hemodynamics (Heeger and Ress, 2002, Logothetis et al., 2001). In early development, neurovascular coupling and neural energy use differ substantially from that of adults, leading to nonstationary and region-dependent relationships between neural firing and the BOLD signal (see Harris et al., 2011 for review). Thus, changes in functional connectivity could partly reflect evolving neurovascular coupling and energy use. Despite these considerations, ‘increases’ or ‘decreases’ in those metrics from consistent methodology within each study can still reflect the general developmental changes of brain network integration or segregation.

Validation of structural and functional connectivity

Validating MRI-based structural connectivity in early development is challenging because there is no direct biological ground truth available for human fetuses and infants. Tracer studies and histology offer gold-standard references in animals (Calabrese et al., 2015, Jbabdi et al., 2013), but human early-life histology remains scarce (Yendiki et al., 2022). Non-human primates (NHPs) provide an unparalleled window to understand human brain development due to their close evolutionary distance (García-Cabezas and Zikopoulos, 2019, Van Essen et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2018). Datasets from the Brain Initiative Cell Atlas Network (BICAN) provide a promising solution, enabling developmental and evolutionary validation using high-resolution NHP histology and dMRI (Feng et al., 2017, Van Essen et al., 2019). On the other hand, functional networks can be validated through task-based fMRI or electrophysiology, where rs-fMRI-derived parcels that show coherent task activations or sharp electrophysiological boundaries are considered biologically meaningful (Mantini et al., 2007, Smith et al., 2009, Wang et al., 2015). However, developmental validation is more complex because neurovascular coupling, neural energy consumption, and HRF properties shift with age (Arichi et al., 2012, Kozberg and Hillman, 2016), requiring developmental models that take these factors into account.

Conclusion

We have consolidated key findings on brain connectome development from fetal stage to early childhood using neuroimaging techniques. The unique developmental patterns and the dynamic balance of short- and long-range connectivity during early life are critical for the segregation and integration processes of developmental connectome. It also supports the hierarchical maturation of the connectome from primary to higher-order function systems, consistent with the spatiotemporal dynamics of underlying cellular processes observed from histologic studies. The highly synchronized development of structural and functional connectomes suggests that the maturation of structural connectivity provides a foundation for the emergence and refinement of functional networks. White matter microstructural maturation likely underpins the evolving structural connectome, while increased CBF may support the metabolic demands necessary for the emergence and maturation of functional networks during early life. Disruptions in the balance between short- and long-range connectivity are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD and SCZ, which are respectively characterized by short-range hyperconnectivity and hypoconnectivity. Research in developmental connectomics will benefit from advanced, age-specific imaging techniques and analytic tools, as well as harmonized large-scale datasets. Furthermore, future mechanistic studies linking microscale histological evidence with macroscale connectome data and behavioral phenotypes are essential to deepen our understanding of neurodevelopmental disorders.
