Impact of COVID‐19 and CFTR Modulators on Cystic Fibrosis: A Real‐World Analysis of Care Patterns
Authors: Alexandra C. Hinton, Edmund H. Sears, Sara Lopez‐Pintado, Jonathan B. Zuckerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionNovel therapeutics and rapid expansion of telehealth have reshaped cystic fibrosis (CF) care; however, the impact on visit patterns and equitable access across the CF population remains unclear. We characterized changes in visit patterns from 2017 to 2022 and the association of sociodemographic and clinical factors with visit frequency in 2022.MethodsThis observational cohort study analyzed 2017–2022 US CF Foundation Patient Registry data for people aged 6–65 years. We described trends in care utilization and used adjusted longitudinal mixed‐effects models to evaluate predictors of between‐visit interval (BVI).ResultsThe study included 28,340 people and 463,745 encounters. Average BVI increased 22.6% from 2019 to 2022, with larger increases among adults. The effect of COVID‐related changes and highly effective modulator use on BVI (average increase in BVI of 13.5 and 9.3 days, respectively) were roughly additive for those with concomitant exposures. Starting elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) initially shortened BVI, followed by sustained increases. Non‐White and Hispanic individuals had shorter BVI than their White, non‐Hispanic counterparts. After adjustment, insurance changes and greater residential distance from CF centers were associated with longer BVI.ConclusionAdvances in CF therapeutics and COVID‐19‐related care adaptations have both markedly influenced visit frequency, indicating evolving care models responsive to the needs of people with CF. Persistent disparities by race, insurance status, and geographic location reveal ongoing challenges in achieving equitable access to routine CF care. Targeted strategies to address these inequities and enhance integrated care are essential to optimize outcomes for people with CF.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) care is evolving rapidly due to two significant developments: approval of the triple combination modulator elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) in 2019 and widespread adoption of telehealth during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 1). ETI use led to an average increase in lung function of 14% within a year [1], with lung function remaining stable in open‐label extension studies [2]. Despite consistent guidance to attend routine visits quarterly, the average annual number of CF care encounters per individual decreased from 4.8 in 2019 to 3.9 in 2022. In 2022 and 2023, telehealth accounted for 20% of all care encounters [3, 4].

The impact of recent shifts, including widespread ETI use and telehealth adoption, on care engagement, visit frequency, and disparities in access has not been comprehensively characterized at the national level. Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding how individuals with CF engage with their care teams across diverse sociodemographic and clinical subgroups in real‐world settings [5]. Prior studies have been limited by pre‐pandemic timeframes, short follow‐up periods, single‐center designs, or a lack of detailed longitudinal data.

Moreover, few studies have rigorously accounted for confounding factors in large, representative cohorts, leaving uncertainty about which populations may be most at risk for reduced care access or suboptimal outcomes as CF management evolves. The interplay between clinician preferences, sociodemographic factors (such as socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity, and access to technology), clinical stability, and mental health is complex and can complicate the interpretation of care engagement patterns. Additionally, prior receipt of recommended CF care strongly predicts continued engagement, underscoring the need to consider historical care patterns when evaluating current visit frequency [6].

In response to these major shifts in how people with CF (pwCF) engage with their care teams [7], the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation (CFF) published a position paper in 2024, providing recommendations for refinements in CF care [8]. Although the paper acknowledges that research in this area is limited, it asserts that expert opinion supports reduced visit frequency for those with uncomplicated and clinically stable disease and supports integration of telehealth into routine care [8, 9]. The paper emphasizes important caveats, however, highlighting that reduced visit frequency may not be suitable for all patients and stresses the need for individualized care plans to ensure comprehensive support, especially for those facing barriers related to technology, socioeconomic status, or mental health challenges [10].

Our study leverages 2017–2022 United States Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Patient Registry (USCFFPR) data to fill this gap by providing a broad and nuanced analysis of clinic visit patterns over time. The study timeline captures the sequential introduction of highly effective modulator therapies (HEMT), including ivacaftor only during 2017 to late 2019, the addition of ETI in late 2019 for those age ≥ 12 years, and in 2021 for those age 6–11 years, as well as the rapid adoption of telehealth during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 1) [1, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. This unique period permits detailed analysis of changes in visit frequency resulting from successive disruptions in both CF therapeutics and healthcare delivery, a set of circumstances unprecedented in the history of CF [16, 17, 18].

Additionally, we investigate trends by sociodemographic factors, health status, and prior care utilization to identify subgroups that may be underserved or at risk of fragmented care amid evolving CF management paradigms.

Methods

Data Source

Our observational cohort study was approved by the USCFFPR Committee and deemed exempt by the MaineHealth Institutional Review Board (protocol no. 1944298‐2) because it involved secondary analysis of de‐identified registry data with established privacy safeguards. Each CF program obtains written informed consent and assent, as appropriate, from participants and/or their legal guardians prior to inclusion in the registry, and all procedures adhere to recognized ethical standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the US Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects. Data are entered prospectively into a secure database by care center staff. Using USCFFPR data from January 2016 to December 2022, we included pwCF aged 6–65 with ≥ 1 year of baseline data before their index visit (to assess prior engagement with care). Data from 2016 were used only for baseline covariate identification. Missing predictor data were imputed using last observation carried forward (LOCF), and subsequently, if necessary, next observation carried backward (NOCB) methods. “Missing” variable categories were created to include individuals lacking values across all encounters. Individuals were censored in the year prior to transplant, and subsequent data were excluded from analysis. Hospital and home intravenous therapy encounters and visits with between‐visit intervals (BVI) of < 30 days were excluded to focus on routine longitudinal care. Cohort exclusions are summarized in Figure 2. Predictors of BVI were assessed in the subset of encounters which took place in 2022. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Flowchart of the study cohort, derived from people with cystic fibrosis in the United States Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Patient Registry (USCFFPR) between 2017 and 2022. The figure includes a description of encounter‐level exclusions.

Variables of Interest

We assessed 30 individual‐level predictors, including sociodemographic factors, health‐status, and prior care utilization. See Supporting Information S1: Table A for full variable definition.

Outcome Definitions

Between‐visit interval (BVI) (continuous): The BVI was calculated for each index encounter as days since the prior encounter.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Longitudinal mixed‐effects regression models evaluated relationships between prior‐encounter predictors and BVI, accounting for time‐varying covariates and individual‐ and center‐level random effects. BVI was log‐transformed to normalize the distribution. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs, Supporting Information S1: Figure A and Table B), informed by prior literature and clinical experience, guided adjustment set selection separately for each predictor to ensure accurate, predictor‐specific effect estimates. For example, age and income may influence insurance type and BVI. To accurately quantify the effect of insurance type on BVI, we therefore adjust for age and income. Predictors which cannot be altered require no adjustment (e.g., sex at birth) and are therefore modeled without covariates. When age was included in models, it was incorporated using natural splines. Predictor effects on BVI, reported as percent differences from the referent group, were calculated as 100(eβ–1)%, where β is the regression coefficient. Pairwise comparisons of factor levels used estimated marginal means from the fitted model. Analyses were performed in R version v4.2.1 (R Core Team 2021), with analytical details and sensitivity analyses described in the supplement (Supporting Information S1: Text A) [6, 19, 20, 21].

Results

The study included 28,340 pwCF and 463,745 encounters from 2017 to 2022 (Figure 2). Demographics reflect the US CF population (Table 1). In 2022, there were 77,606 encounters among 25,014 pwCF. A comparison of the study cohort to the 1737 pwCF (5.8% of the original cohort) excluded due to insufficient follow‐up (Supporting Information S1: Table C) reveals similar profiles, though excluded individuals were more likely to be very young (thus less likely to be prescribed ETI or have complications) and have missing data. Of 4929 non‐White individuals in the original cohort, we excluded 380 (7.7%) and retained 4549 (92.3%).

Patterns of Care Among pwCF, 2017–2022

Average BVI was relatively steady from 2017 to 2019, a period preceding approval of ETI and the COVID‐19 pandemic's triggering of widespread telehealth adoption. BVI increased from 2019 to 2022, climbing 22.1% (95% CI 20.7%, 23.6%, p < 0.001; Figure 3). The difference between pediatric and adult BVI widened considerably from 2019 to 2022, beginning in the teens and continuing through most of adulthood (Figure 3).

From 2017–2019 to 2022, adjusted BVI estimated at age 25 lengthened for those who were on ETI therapy in the 2022 time period, but not 2017–2019 (21.7%; 95% CI 20.5%, 22.9%; p < 0.001; 20.3 days; Figure 4B; Supporting Information S1: Table D), while those who were ineligible for HEMT maintained similar visit frequencies (Supporting Information S1: Figure B). To isolate the effect of COVID‐related changes, we assessed changes in BVI over time for those who were on HEMT (ivacaftor) during the entire study period; those on HEMT at age 25 experienced an average BVI increase of 13.2% (95% CI 10.2%, 16.2%; p < 0.001; 13.5 days) from 2017–2019 to 2022 (Figure 4A). To estimate the isolated effect of HEMT use on BVI, we compared BVI for those not on HEMT to those on HEMT prior to 2020 (ivacaftor was the only HEMT available at this time). As shown in Supporting Information S1: Table D and visualized in Figure 4C, in 2017–2019, the difference in BVI between these groups at age 25 was 9.9% (95% CI 6.8%, 13.2%; p < 0.001; 9.3 days). Together, these results illustrate the effect of COVID‐related changes and modulator use on BVI (i.e., an increase in BVI of 13.5 and 9.3 days, respectively). Effects were roughly additive for those with concomitant exposures (20.3 days for those experiencing both COVID‐19 and HEMT in quick succession; Figure 4B).

Average BVI plotted relative to ETI initiation showed a consistent temporal trend for both pediatric (12–17 years) and adult (18–65 years) pwCF. After ETI initiation, BVI remained stable or decreased slightly for 3–5 months, reflecting consistent or increased healthcare utilization. Following this period, BVI increased progressively and remained elevated above pre‐ETI levels over long‐term follow‐up. This pattern held for people who initiated ETI in both 2019 and 2020 (Supporting Information S1: Figure C).

Quantitatively, within the first year post‐ETI, BVI increased by approximately 19 days in the 2019 pediatric cohort (rising from 75 to 94 days), 8 days in the 2020 pediatric cohort (88–96 days), 17 days in the 2019 adults (76–93 days), and 7 days in the 2020 adults (94–100 days). In Year 3 post‐initiation, pediatric BVI stabilized near 97–104 days and adults between 103 and 112, indicating progressively less frequent healthcare visits compared to baseline.

Two major sociodemographic factors predicted longer BVI (i.e., less frequent visits). Compared to private insurance, having unknown or no insurance was associated with substantially longer BVI (56.5%; 49.4%, 64.0%; p < 0.001; BVI = 176 days), as was experiencing a change in insurance type (35.3%; 95% CI 33.2%, 37.5%; p < 0.001; BVI = 163 days). Living farther from the treating CF center was also a key predictor of longer BVI, with those living 76–125 miles away experiencing a 4% increase in BVI, 125–300 miles a 5% increase, and 300+ miles a 7% increase (p < 0.001). These associations were adjusted for rurality (RUCA categories) and center effects, and the results remained consistent irrespective of model adjustment for rurality.

Conversely, a number of sociodemographic and health status‐associated predictors of reduced BVI were identified. BVI was shorter for those who were unemployed or disabled (–7.0%; 95% CI –8.3%, –5.6%; p < 0.001), those who were pregnant (−10.7%; 95% CI −13.3%, −7.9%; p < 0.001), and those with a diagnosis of depression (−6.7%; 95% CI −7.9%, −5.6%; p < 0.001) or anxiety (−7.8%; 95% CI −8.9%, −6.7%; p < 0.001). CF‐specific factors associated with shorter BVI included moderate (−7.9%; 95% CI −9.0%, −6.7; p < 0.001) and severe lung impairment (−15.5%; 95% CI −17.2%, −13.7%; p < 0.001), CF‐related diabetes (−11.3%; 95% CI −12.3%, −10.2%; p < 0.001), and greater number of pathogens identified in respiratory cultures (Figure 5; Supporting Information S1: Table E). After adjustment for sociodemographic, health‐status, and care utilization factors (Supporting Information S1: Table B), telehealth visits were strongly associated with shorter BVI (−14.6%; 95% CI −15.4%, −13.8%; p < 0.001).

Modestly lower BVI was also seen in non‐White compared to White individuals, both in unadjusted analyses (−3.8%; 95% CI −5.2%, −2.4%; p < 0.001) and after adjustment for genotype, age, rurality, distance from CF center, insurance type, education, employment, income, and smoke exposure (−2.7%; 95% CI −4.1%, −1.2%; p < 0.001). Among young adults (18–35 years) in 2022 without a modulator, non‐White F508del homozygotes had significantly longer BVIs than White counterparts (249 vs. 231 days, p = 0.001), while non‐White F508del heterozygotes (239 vs. 278 days, p < 0.001) and other genotypes had shorter intervals (158 vs. 203 days, p < 0.001). Among ETI users, visit spacing by race was similar across all genotypes (all p > 0.5; Supporting Information S1: Table I). In addition, BVI was somewhat lower in those with public versus private insurance (−2.5%; 95% CI −3.6%, −1.5%; p < 0.001) and for those without cigarette smoke exposure (−4.2%; 95% CI −5.9%, −2.5%; p < 0.001). Although statistically significant, these represent small differences in magnitude, amounting to changes of less than a week.

Discussion

This study analyzes CF care patterns from 2017 to 2022, a period of major change in CF therapeutics and care delivery. We examine the impact of widespread HEMT use and shifts in healthcare provision following the initial COVID‐19 pandemic response. Building on prior work, we explore the effect of sociodemographic and health‐status factors on healthcare system interactions.

Our analysis shows that HEMT and pandemic‐related factors had distinct effects on BVI at the population level, effects that were additive for those who experienced both synchronously (Figure 4), leading to fewer overall visits in 2020–2022 compared with 2017–2019. These findings mirror those described in the USCFFPR [3, 4, 22] and the 2023 CFF Adapting CF Care Survey, which revealed patient preference for more frequent visits for children and less frequent appointments for adults [23]. The alignment between personal preferences and actual visit patterns suggests that care practices are evolving to meet the changing needs of pwCF across the lifespan.

The sharp BVI increase during young adulthood transition aligns with established literature on decreased adherence and care gaps [21, 24, 25]. However, the dramatic rise in BVI in this age group over this 5‐year period cannot be attributed to developmental factors alone. ETI‐related clinical improvements have likely fostered reduced concern about CF progression [24]. Without evidence to support the safety of reduced clinical engagement during this vulnerable period, strategies to improve engagement with transition programs should be further explored.

The effects of ETI on visit frequency across the CF population are intriguing, and several patterns emerged from our analysis. In the short term, visit frequency increased following the prescription of ETI, likely reflecting the standard practice of monitoring for side effects and documenting improvements in lung function. Over 3 years post‐ETI, BVI increased by about 22 days in 2019 for pediatric patients, 16 days in 2020 for pediatric patients, 27 days in 2019 for adults, and 18 days in 2020 for adults. The 2020 groups started with higher baseline BVI values, meaning they had fewer visits even before ETI initiation. Still, all groups showed meaningful increases over time, indicating fewer CF clinic visits after starting ETI. By 2022, both F508del homozygotes and heterozygotes experienced longer BVI than pre‐pandemic. The nearly identical BVI in these two groups in 2022 contrasted with our previous findings of shorter BVI for F508del homozygotes [26], consistent with the equalizing health effects of HEMT across genotypes.

Sociodemographic factors influenced visit frequency in 2022. Non‐White and Hispanic pwCF attended clinic visits more frequently relative to White pwCF. On average, BVI increased for both non‐White and White pwCF compared to prior years, though the increase was less pronounced among non‐White pwCF—a change from previous patterns [26]. The higher proportion of genotypes ineligible for ETI among non‐White pwCF may have contributed to relatively more frequent visits in this group [27]. In contrast to previous reports [6], geographic factors also influenced care utilization, with greater residential distance from CF centers associated with progressively longer times between visits.

We found no clinically important differences in BVI between public and private insurance holders, but those without insurance or with unknown/other insurance unsurprisingly had much longer BVI, consistent with previous findings [26]. Changes in insurance coverage were associated with meaningful delays in care, with the first visit under new insurance occurring, on average, about 2 months later than for those with uninterrupted policies. The impact of this is amplified by the frequency of insurance changes which affected 9.6% of pwCF in 2022 and 29% over the 2017–2022 period. Given the complexity of the US healthcare system and the persistently high healthcare needs of this population, insurance‐related delays in care are not just administrative hurdles but carry real risks for unrecognized disease progression and costly complications, making healthcare coverage fragmentation a key and enduring challenge.

The observed disparities in visit frequency by race/ethnicity and insurance coverage emphasize the need for ongoing efforts to ensure equitable access and tailored care approaches. Appropriate adjustment for confounding variables elucidated a number of important predictors of visit frequency. Future research should address structural factors driving disparities among underserved subgroups. It will also be essential to investigate the impact of visit type and frequency, including the hybrid use of telehealth and in‐person care, on both process outcomes (meeting CF clinical practice guidelines) and health outcomes. Appraising the quality and comprehensiveness of care delivered within different care models will have substantial implications and merit future investigation.

A key strength of our study is the incorporation of longitudinal mixed‐effects modeling, which enabled capture of within‐person changes over time while accounting for variation in practice patterns and center‐level influences that shape BVI. By leveraging DAGs for predictor‐specific adjustment, we minimized bias from measured confounders, generating more robust effect estimates. The USCFFPR is a comprehensive, longitudinal data set with reliable demographic, encounter, anthropometric, and microbiologic information [28]; however, some elements, including insurance and socioeconomic variables, are less well characterized. Factors that may affect visit frequency, such as transfer between centers or from pediatric to adult care, are not consistently documented, and other potentially relevant variables may also be unavailable.

Additionally, our investigation uncovered documentation bias during this period, particularly with telehealth encounters. Supporting Information S1: Table F shows that telehealth encounters had substantial clinical data gaps (FEV1 missing: 76%−88% vs. 10%−14% in‐person; BMI missing: 69%−77% vs. 3%−4%), while certain sociodemographics were consistently complete (< 1% missing race or insurance). Inconsistencies in reported anxiety, depression, and ETI use across visits mirrored irregular recording of pancreatic enzyme use (a treatment that typically remains stable over time), suggesting systemic data entry gaps rather than true clinical variation (Supporting Information S1: Tables G and H). To address this, we treated these variables as annual measures rather than encounter‐level indicators. Since the quality of the data depends on accurate and complete clinical documentation, residual confounding remains possible, and our findings should be interpreted in this context.

The study population, derived from the USCFFPR, is representative of the US CF community; however, differences in healthcare systems and treatment access limit the international generalizability of these findings. Similarly, our selected age range (6−65 years) does not encompass the full CF population; however, it was chosen to capture patterns in care across the lifespan, focusing on ages where visit frequency recommendations are less well defined. Patients < 6 years were excluded given clear ongoing guidance for quarterly visits [8]. Those > 65 years were excluded due to their relatively small numbers and enrichment of this subgroup with rare genotypes and late diagnoses, limiting the generalizability of findings from these individuals to the broader CF population.

Furthermore, this study characterizes visit frequency confined to CF care centers; however, as life expectancy continues to improve for pwCF, the role of primary care and non‐CF specialists in supporting comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, and renal disease [29] will warrant increased scrutiny in future studies.

To maximize the use of longitudinal data while minimizing bias, we employed LOCF and NOCB imputations. While these carry limitations, they are widely used in registry‐based studies and are recommended methods for USCFFPR analyses [30]. Encounters with < 30‐day BVI were excluded to study longitudinal rather than acute or follow‐up care. Including them would have captured urgent or follow‐up visits, tending to underestimate routine visit intervals. While not the focus of this study, urgent care encounters may reflect a shift toward reactive rather than preventive care, an issue worthy of further study.

Our analysis focused specifically on visit frequency, providing detailed insights into care utilization, but did not directly address other important aspects, such as visit quality, encounter duration, treatment adherence, or patient experience. Understanding the quality and comprehensiveness of care delivered within different care models will have important implications and merit future investigation. Additionally, while longer BVIs were associated with healthier clinical profiles (e.g., higher FEV1 and fewer chronic infections), this study did not examine downstream health consequences such as lung function trajectories or exacerbation rates. Shorter BVIs may reflect appropriate intensive monitoring during periods of acute illness or treatment changes (such as ETI initiation), but the visit interval that best balances timely monitoring, care burden, and resource use is not yet known. Future work, including a separate planned analysis from this cohort, will explicitly evaluate how BVI relates to clinical outcomes using longitudinal outcome modeling. Importantly, given the large sample size and the number of hypotheses tested, we have interpreted results with attention to effect sizes and clinical relevance, rather than solely relying on statistical significance.

In summary, this analysis provides a comprehensive view of how therapeutic advances, the COVID‐19 pandemic, and evolving healthcare delivery models have reshaped the frequency of care for pwCF in recent years. By examining visit frequency across diverse subgroups and adjusting for key demographic and clinical factors, we highlight both the benefits of HEMT as well as the structural vulnerabilities that continue to affect access. The findings reveal progress in aligning care practices with patient needs while also emphasizing persistent disparities related to insurance status, race and ethnicity, and geography. Looking ahead, optimizing CF care will require more than innovation in medical therapeutics [10]. It will depend on targeted strategies that reduce inequities, integrate multispecialty support for an aging population, and refine telehealth practices to ensure reliable clinical data collection and communication among key stakeholders. This study offers an evidence‐based foundation for these next steps and positions visit utilization patterns as both a marker of progress and a guide for future improvements in policy and practice.
