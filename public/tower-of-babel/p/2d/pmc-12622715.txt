Rheumatoid arthritis increases risk of atherosclerosis: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Qi-Pei Liu, Lei-Xiao Zhang, Hao-Ze Gao, Jie-Hua Luo, Sheng-Ting Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding of the relationship between rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and atherosclerosis (AS) remains limited by the shortcomings of previous observational studies. This study investigated the causal relationship between RA and AS risks using Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Genetic data related to RA and AS were sourced from the genome-wide association studies database and FinnGen Biobank gene database, respectively. Standard screening metrics were applied and palindromic single nucleotide polymorphisms were processed. The initial analysis employed the inverse-variance weighted method for MR analysis, with other methods used as supplementary methods. Multiple sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO, were used to ensure the robustness of the study. The RA-related dataset included a total of 13,108,512 single nucleotide polymorphisms, involving a sample size of 58,284, of which 14,361 were case groups. The datasets of coronary AS, peripheral AS, cerebral AS, and AS excluding coronary AS, cerebral AS, and peripheral atherosclerosis (ASE) involved in the study contained sample sizes of 296,542, 236,794, 284,385, and 299,827, respectively. The MR results indicated a positive causal relationship between genetically determined RA and the risk of coronary AS (odds ratio [OR] = 1.025, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.003–1.047, P = .023), peripheral AS (OR = 1.071, 95% CI: 1.034–1.110, P < .001), and ASE (OR = 1.045, 95% CI: 1.013–1.078, P = .006), whereas no significant causal relationship with cerebral AS was observed. This study provided new evidence from a genetic evidence perspective that RA increases the risk of AS, revealing insights into early screening and preventive interventions for cardiovascular disease in RA patients.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovitis, cartilage degradation, and bone degradation. Its main clinical manifestations include joint pain, swelling, stiffness, deformity, and sleep disturbances.[1,2] The prevalence of RA in most countries worldwide typically ranges from 0.25% to 3.4%.[3] The socioeconomic cost of RA in European countries exceeds €4.5 billion per year.[4]

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by lipid deposition beneath the arterial intima.[5] Mechanisms such as inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, abnormal immune responses, and disrupted lipid metabolism can lead to the development of AS or atheromatous plaques.[6] Under the influence of metalloproteinases, inflammatory cell infiltration, and other factors, plaques are prone to rupture, thereby triggering acute cardiovascular diseases such as ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction.[7]

Previous studies have pointed out the correlation between RA and increased risk of cardiovascular disease, especially AS, in terms of chronic inflammation, immune infiltration and other aspects.[8] For example, long-term chronic inflammation in RA patients contributes to organismal oxidative stress, abnormal lipid levels and vascular endothelial dysfunction, which increases the risk of AS.[9] In addition, immune cell types (including macrophages, dendritic cells, and other antigen-presenting cells) and various T- and B-lymphocyte subpopulations that play a key role in the pathology of RA, together with their secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, have been shown to mediate pathological alterations in the vascular wall in the early stages of AS, through pathways such as the promotion of endothelial dysfunction and activation of inflammatory cascade responses.[10] However, attempts to reveal the relationship through experimental or observational studies are inevitably susceptible to bias caused by confounding factors and reverse causal associations. Therefore, this study introduces the Mendelian randomization (MR) method, which has the same random assignment characteristics as randomized controlled trials, to provide evidence from a genetic point of view.

MR is an epidemiological method for causal analysis that uses genetic variation as instrumental variables (IVs).[11] Because environmental factors and disease processes do not influence genetic variations, MR can mitigate reverse causality and reduce the influence of confounding factors, thereby strengthening the relationship between exposure and outcome.[12] The advantages of MR in investigating the long-term effects of RA on AS beyond what clinical trials can feasibly assess. There is a lack of research on other more widespread atherosclerotic conditions such as peripheral artery AS, cerebral artery AS, etc. Therefore, in our study, a 2-sample bidirectional MR analysis was implemented to explore the correlation between RA and various types of AS, including coronary AS, cerebral AS, peripheral AS, and AS excluding coronary AS, cerebral AS, and peripheral atherosclerosis (ASE), to further enrich and supplement the theoretical basis.

2. Methods and materials

2.1. Study design

To investigate the relationship between RA and AS risk, a 2-sample bidirectional MR analysis was conducted using single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs.[13] A flowchart of the study design is presented in Figure 1. Three crucial assumptions were made to ensure the accuracy of the study.[14] First, the SNPs included in this study were found to be significantly associated with RA. Second, the SNPs were independent of the confounding factors related to both RA and AS. Third, all SNPs acted on AS only through RA (Fig. 1).

A working flow chart to explore the potential relationship between RA and AS risk. AS = atherosclerosis, RA = rheumatoid arthritis.

2.2. Data sources

The summary-level statistical data of genetic variations related to RA included in our study were obtained from the publicly available integrative epidemiology unit (IEU) open-genome-wide association studies (OpenGWAS) database, which includes 58,284 samples involving both males and females and 13,108,512 SNPs[15] (Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614). These included 43,923 controls and 14,361 cases. RA representative genes involved in European populations were pooled from a meta-analysis of information from populations recruited in Boston, Sweden, Toronto, United Kingdom, Netherlands, and North America.[16] FinnGen database is a large Finnish national biomedical research project that aims to collect genomic and health data from 500,000 Finns, covering about 10 per cent of the total Finnish population. Statistical data for genetically determined AS from the FinnGen database involves samples that are all of Finnish origin[17] (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614). The datasets of coronary AS, peripheral AS, cerebral AS, and ASE involved in the study contained sample sizes of 296,542, 236,794, 284,385, and 299,827, respectively.

2.3. IVs selection

First, we selected SNPs that were significantly associated with the entire RA exposure genome (range of P < 5 × 10−8). For reverse MR, a threshold of P < 1 × 10−5 was set to include more SNPs related to AS. Second, linkage disequilibrium bias between the included SNPs was avoided by setting R2 = 0.001 and clustering distance = 10,000 kb.[18] Third, this study avoided misidentification of the effector allele and other alleles by deleting palindromic SNPs with exposed-end allele frequencies in the intermediate range (40–70%) and thus avoiding misidentification of the orientation of the effector allele and other alleles. Finally, to avoid weak instrument bias, we calculated and excluded SNPs with an F-value of <10.

EAF represents the estimated genetic effect, beta denotes the effect allele frequency, K indicates the sample size, and SE(beta) represents the standard error of the genetic effect.[19]

2.4. Statistical analysis

2.4.1. MR analysis

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method is typically used as the most reliable MR analysis method to explore causal effects.[20] Because the IVW method assumes that all SNPs are valid instruments, the weighted median (WM) and MR-Egger were also included as supplementary methods for MR analysis to assess the reliability of the results. MR-Egger is susceptible to the influence of pleiotropic genetic variations, allowing for causal effect estimates of lower precision under weaker assumptions for included genetic variations.[21] The WM method requires more than 50% effectiveness among the included IVs and estimates causal effects based on the weights of different genetic variations, making it more accurate than estimates based on the MR-Egger regression method.[22] The results based on the IVW method were statistically significant and consistent in direction with those based on the WM or MR-Egger method, indicating significant causal effects in our study.

All data were analyzed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.7) in R (version 4.4.1).

2.4.2. Sensitivity analysis

First, the presence of significant horizontal pleiotropy in the IVs and other potential confounding factors was assessed using MR-Egger regression.[23] Second, the Cochrane Q-test of base versus IVW and MR-Egger methods were used to test the significance of heterogeneity between SNPs included in the study.[24] A fixed-effect model was used by default, and the random-effects model was used when the heterogeneity was significant (P < .05). Third, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) was used to assess whether there was bias due to outliers. Finally, leave-one-out analysis and funnel plots were used in the sensitivity analysis to ensure reliability of the results.

2.4.3. Ethical review statement

The data involved in this study were taken from publicly available databases and the relevant ethics had been previously obtained, therefore ethical approval was no longer required for this study.

3. Results

3.1. Results of the selection of IVs

For detailed information about the SNPs used in the genetic IVs as well as their associations with RA and AS, please refer to Data S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q613.

3.2. Effects of RA on AS risk

MR analysis based on the IVW approach revealed that RA increased the risk of coronary AS (odds ratio [OR] = 1.025, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.003–1.047, P = .023), peripheral AS (OR = 1.071, 95% CI: 1.034–1.110, P < .001) and ASE (OR = 1.045, 95% CI: 1.013–1.078, P = .006) (Fig. 2). MR-Egger regression and WM also showed similar trends (Fig. 2). No significant causal relationship was found between RA and cerebral AS risk (P > .05) (Fig. 2). Visualization of results was performed using scatter plots and forest plots (Figure S1 and Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614).

Forest plot of causal association between rheumatoid arthritis and atherosclerosis risk.

3.3. Results of reverse MR analysis

Based on reverse MR analysis using the IVW, MR-Egger regression, and WM methods, no significant causal effect was detected between coronary AS, cerebral AS, peripheral AS, ASE, and RA risk (Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614).

3.4. Results of the sensitivity analysis

MR-Egger regression was not statistically significant (Data S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q613). The results of heterogeneity are presented in Data S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q613. It can be seen from the funnel plot that all IVs included in the study were basically symmetrically distributed around the IVW value (Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614). Leave-one-out analysis did not identify any IVs as outliers (Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q614). No significant outliers were identified using MR-PRESSO. All SNPs included in our study had an F-statistic of > 10 (Data S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q613).

4. Discussion

In this study, RA was positively correlated with the risk of coronary AS, peripheral AS, and ASE; however, no significant association was observed between RA and cerebral AS risk. This study support the need for early cardiovascular risk assessment and monitoring in patients with RA.

Previous studies have shown that the onset of AS precedes that of RA,[25] and there is an increasing belief that AS is becoming a primary cause of RA.[26] In contrast, several cross-sectional studies have found that the incidence of coronary AS, peripheral AS and carotid AS in patients with RA is greater than that in the general population, sometimes even doubling.[27–30] These results support the hypothesis that RA increases the susceptibility to various forms of AS. Therefore, primary prevention of AS in patients with RA holds significant importance.

RA is an autoimmune disease that can lead to disability. However, the mechanism by which it causes AS remains unclear. Research has revealed that inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-17, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) are widely present in RA patients.[31,32] These cytokines not only participate in synovial development but also promote the activation of endothelial cells, leading to dysfunction and accelerating the progression of AS through the modulation of inflammatory cascades.[31,33] Moreover, the RA chronic inflammation also has procoagulant and prooxidant effects, increasing the expression of tissue factor adhesion molecules, reducing the synthesis of nitric oxide and thrombomodulin, and inducing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase-mediated mechanisms.[34,35] This further leads to endothelial dysfunction and shifts the hemostatic balance of RA toward a prothrombotic state. In addition, in patients with RA, abnormal activation of T and B cells occurs, and CD4 + and Th17 cells can lead to abnormal secretion of inflammatory factors such as IL-17 and IL-6, triggering abnormal inflammatory responses and causing endothelial cell damage and dyslipidemia, ultimately leading to AS.[36,37] Furthermore, studies have proposed the “lipid paradox” in RA patients, wherein serum levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and total cholesterol decrease due to excessive degradation.[35,38] Additionally, a pro-inflammatory form of HDL, known as piHDL, is produced, which promotes foam cell formation and LDL oxidation, thus accelerating the progression of AS plaque enlargement.[39] In summary, RA may trigger and exacerbate AS from various aspects, including inflammation, lipid disorders, endothelial dysfunction, immune dysfunction, and genetic factors.

RA medications such as disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs methotrexate[40,41]and the TNF-α inhibitors Infliximab,[42–45] adalimumab,[45] and rituximab[46] have been clinically proven to reduce proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6 in RA patients and to consistently improve endothelial function, exerting beneficial effects on the development of AS in patients with RA, thereby delaying the onset and mortality of cardiovascular disease. Moreover, the angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor ramipril can significantly improve endothelial function in patients with RA, offering a potential new strategy for preventing cardiovascular events in RA patients.[47] Moreover, clinical studies have demonstrated that long-term lipid-lowering therapy with statins (such as rosuvastatin and atorvastatin) can improve endothelial function in patients with RA by alleviating arterial stiffness and reducing carotid plaque height, thereby reducing the risk of AS.[48,49]

No association between RA and cerebral AS was observed in this study. The cerebral vascular system may be subject to different regulatory mechanisms compared to peripheral or coronary arteries, such as the blood–brain barrier, which may allow less exposure to systemic immune cells and inflammatory factors. In peripheral arteries (e.g., coronary arteries) in AS, massive macrophage infiltration, foam cell formation, lipid deposition, and fibrous cap rupture are common, triggering typical acute coronary events.[50] In addition, plaques in cerebral arteries are relatively stable, have less lipid content, are less infiltrated by inflammatory cells, and exhibit more chronic ischemia and luminal narrowing. further large-scale and well-phenotyped studies are warranted to investigate this observation, and to determine whether it reflects a true absence of association or methodological limitations.

First, this MR study comprehensively covered all types of atherosclerotic disease and strictly adhered to STROBE-MR guidelines. Second, to ensure the robustness of the results, various sensitivity analyses were performed to minimize potential confounding bias. This study support the need for early cardiovascular risk assessment and monitoring in patients with RA, even in the absence of overt cardiovascular symptoms. Clinicians may consider integrating cardiovascular screening protocols into RA management strategies. The identification of a genetic causal link underscores the importance of addressing shared inflammatory pathways, which may guide the development of targeted therapies to reduce both joint and vascular inflammation.

However, this study has some limitations. First, owing to the lack of detailed demographic information in the included data, This study was unable to perform subgroup analyses on the age and sex of the population. Although this MR analysis provided a stark causal analysis, this study did not validate it through animal experiments or clinical studies. We recommend large-scale longitudinal cohort studies and clinical trials focusing on RA patients with integrated cardiovascular outcomes, to validate the MR findings in real-world clinical settings. In addition, multi-ethnic MR studies could help assess the generalizability of our results beyond European populations.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study revealed a positive correlation between RA and AS risks. This information can aid clinicians in identifying patients with RA who are at a greater risk of AS, facilitating early diagnosis and comprehensive treatment. Patients with RA may benefit from earlier cardiovascular screening and more aggressive management of modifiable risk factors.
