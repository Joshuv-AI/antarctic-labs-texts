Climate-adaptive and low-carbon multi-objective water resources optimization in arid oasis cities
Authors: Hejing Shao, Fulong Chen, Liutianjiao Hong, Fan Wu, Chaofei He, Fengnian Zhao, Tongxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo address the intensifying water supply-demand imbalance in arid regions under climate change and low-carbon development, this study uses Shihezi City, Xinjiang, as a case study. CMIP6 climate data were used to drive an LSTM model for basin-scale runoff projection, and a multi-objective water resources optimization framework was developed by integrating NSGA-III with CRITIC-TOPSIS, considering water shortage, economic benefits, pollutant emissions, and water-use carbon emissions. Results show that future runoff in the Manas River Basin generally declines, with the most pronounced reduction in September; by 2030, water scarcity in Shihezi City remains substantial. The optimal scheme under SSP1-2.6 performs well in water conservation and carbon reduction; SSP2-4.5 balances economic development and environmental protection, whereas SSP5-8.5 delivers higher economic benefits but greater environmental pressure. The results provide support for efficient water resources allocation and low-carbon utilization in arid oasis cities.Subject areas: Environmental science, Environmental management, Environmental resource, Urban planning, Water resources engineering

Introduction

Water resources are critical strategic assets that underpin the sustainable development of economies and societies. Their scientific allocation and efficient utilization are essential for maintaining ecosystem stability and enhancing regional resilience.1,2 In recent years, global climate change has intensified the uncertainty of hydrological processes. Rising temperatures have altered precipitation regimes and increased the frequency of extreme hydrological events, posing unprecedented challenges to conventional water management systems that rely heavily on historical observations.3,4,5 In China’s arid northwestern region, oasis cities serve as critical hubs for regional economic and social development. However, under the dual pressures of rapid urbanization and agricultural intensification, the contradiction between water supply and demand has become increasingly acute.6,7 With the implementation of China’s “dual carbon” strategy, which aims to achieve carbon peaking by 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060, water resource management is now required not only to ensure water supply-demand security but also to balance economic growth, ecological protection, and carbon mitigation. Developing an allocation framework that embodies both climate adaptability and a low-carbon orientation has thus become a strategic imperative.8,9,10,11

In this context, numerous studies worldwide have explored water resource optimization from multiple perspectives. Physically based models, such as the Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT), have been widely employed for simulating watershed hydrological processes and conducting scenario analyses.12,13,14 However, these models often require complex parameterization and extensive datasets, which limit their applicability in data-scarce regions.15 In recent years, deep learning models have emerged as a promising alternative. Among them, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network effectively captures the nonlinear temporal relationships between meteorological variables and runoff through its gated architecture, mitigating the vanishing gradient problem and maintaining high predictive accuracy even under limited data availability.16,17 Empirical evidence suggests that LSTM models outperform physically based approaches such as SWAT in terms of both accuracy and generalization capability, particularly for runoff prediction in arid regions under future climate scenarios.18,19

The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), as the latest generation of global climate models, has substantially improved spatial resolution and the accuracy of simulated climatic variables compared with CMIP5.20,21,22 By integrating Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) with Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), CMIP6 provides a more comprehensive representation of future climate change characteristics across diverse emission and development scenarios.23,24 The combination of CMIP6 climate data with the LSTM model enables high-precision runoff simulation driven by climate variability, offering a robust basis for predicting water resource availability.19 Li et al.25 analyzed precipitation sequences using an LSTM model and optimized urban water allocation through an improved INSGA-II algorithm, effectively enhancing water use efficiency. Zhou et al.26 incorporated climate scenarios to develop an integrated framework that combines water supply-demand forecasting and optimal allocation decision-making. Collectively, these studies provide critical theoretical and methodological support for establishing climate-adaptive water resource management and allocation systems.

In the field of water resources optimization, the objective function has gradually evolved from a single indicator to a multi-objective collaborative framework.27,28 Compared with traditional approaches that primarily emphasize economic benefits or supply-demand balance, recent studies have integrated ecological and carbon emission constraints within a unified framework to achieve comprehensive optimization across economic, social, and environmental dimensions.25,29,30 Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms based on Pareto theory can simultaneously identify sets of non-dominated solutions, thereby providing a scientific basis for trade-off analysis among alternative schemes.31 Among these algorithms, NSGA-III, through its reference-point mechanism and improved crowding distance sorting, demonstrates superior convergence and solution diversity. Compared with NSGA-II,32 NSGA-III is more suitable for multi-dimensional optimization involving economic, ecological, and carbon emission objectives.33 To enhance the objectivity of optimization results and the reliability of decision-making, the CRITIC-TOPSIS method is often employed for comprehensive evaluation. This approach systematically and objectively ranks alternative schemes by quantifying the amount of information and the degree of conflict among evaluation indicators.34,35

Although notable progress has been achieved in model development and methodological advancements, studies addressing water resource allocation in arid oasis cities under the dual constraints of climate change and low-carbon development remain limited. In this study, Shihezi City of the Eighth Division, Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, is selected as the case study, and CMIP6 climate scenario data are employed to drive an LSTM model for projecting future runoff, based on which the regional water supply-demand balance is analyzed. A four-objective optimization model is further developed to minimize total water shortage, maximize economic benefits, minimize pollutant emissions, and minimize water-use carbon emissions, and is solved using the NSGA-III algorithm for coordinated multi-sectoral water allocation, with the CRITIC-TOPSIS method integrated for scheme evaluation and selection. This study aims to elucidate the patterns of water resource allocation in arid oasis cities under the combined constraints of climate change and carbon mitigation, thereby providing a scientific basis for enhancing the efficiency and sustainability of regional water resource management.

Results

Runoff simulation and prediction results

A monthly runoff prediction model for the Manas River Basin was developed using an LSTM network, with data from 1957 to 2000 used for training and 2001–2014 used for validation. The model adopts a three-layer hidden architecture with 80, 190, and 80 neurons, respectively, and is trained using the Adam optimizer with mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function. The maximum number of training epochs is set to 400, with an initial learning rate of 0.05. To mitigate overfitting, dropout rates of 20%, 30%, and 20% are applied to the three hidden layers, respectively, in conjunction with gradient clipping and a dynamic learning rate adjustment strategy.

The validation results demonstrate that the model achieved high accuracy in simulating runoff dynamics, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.869, a Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) of 0.8636, a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 18.8767, and a total percentage error (TPE) of 0.2412. These metrics indicate that the model effectively captures both the temporal dependence and nonlinear variation characteristics of the runoff series.

Subsequently, the model was retrained using processed CMIP6 climate scenario data, with the period 1957–2014 serving as the training phase and 2021–2039 as the prediction phase. The performance metrics further improved, with R2 increasing to 0.889, NSE rising to 0.887, RMSE decreasing to 17.1628, and TPE reducing to 0.2046. These results confirm the strong predictive capability and robustness of the model under future climate scenarios (Figure 1).

Runoff simulation results based on the LSTM model

The projected runoff for 2021–2039 is shown in Figure 2. Across all three climate scenarios, streamflow exhibits pronounced seasonality: The flood season is concentrated in June-August, with peak discharges typically between 120 and 140 m3/s, while low-flow periods decline to approximately 7–20 m3/s. Overall, the interannual trends are broadly consistent among scenarios, but differences emerge during high-flow periods. Under SSP1-2.6, the runoff curve is relatively stable with limited interannual variability; under SSP2-4.5, slightly higher peaks occur in some years (e.g., 2033 and 2035); and under SSP5-8.5, runoff shows an overall increasing tendency with persistently elevated peaks.

Runoff prediction results for 2021-2039

From the perspective of intra-annual distribution (Figure 3), future scenarios maintain a unimodal pattern of monthly average runoff, consistent with that of the historical period, with the peak occurring in July. However, all three scenarios exhibit a general decline in runoff magnitude compared to the baseline. Notably, the reduction in September is pronounced, decreasing from approximately 50 m3/s during the historical period to about 20–25 m3/s under future scenarios—a decline exceeding 50%. This suggests that climate change may significantly alter the temporal distribution of runoff in the Manas River Basin, thereby posing potential challenges to regional water resource management and supply stability.

Prediction results of the water supply

The surface water resources in the Division-City are primarily derived from the Manas River, Jingou River, Bayingou River, and Ningjia River. According to the Regulations on the Management of the Manas River, the Regulations on the Management of the Jingou River, and the water allocation agreement with Shawan County, the diversion proportions of the four rivers are 68.86%, 12.82%, 17.42%, and 0.90%, respectively. To reduce the uncertainty associated with interannual variability, the projected runoff of the Manas River was combined with data from the Integrated Annual Report on Water Diversion and Supply of the Eighth Division, and the five-year average runoff over 2028–2032 was adopted as a representative value for 2030 to estimate the available surface water under different climate scenarios.

Based on local groundwater exploitation control targets, the allowable groundwater extraction in 2030 is set at 4.27 × 108 m3. The SSP2-4.5 scenario is adopted as the baseline, as it is most consistent with the current regional development level and water management policies. Under different climate scenarios, groundwater is incorporated as a supplementary water source in the allocation system, with extraction reduced during periods of abundant surface water availability and moderately increased during low-flow conditions. Under the SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, the scale of groundwater supply is accordingly adjusted in response to variations in surface water availability.

The supply of reclaimed water is determined according to the design capacity of the wastewater treatment facilities and is set at 0.96 × 108 m3. As no expansion of the local wastewater treatment infrastructure is planned, this value is assumed to remain unchanged across all scenarios. The projected results are summarized in Table 1.

Water demand prediction

This study adopts the SSP2-4.5 scenario as the baseline condition for Shihezi City in 2030. The SSP2-4.5 scenario represents a middle-of-the-road development pathway, characterized by moderate levels of socio-economic growth, technological progress, and resource utilization, as well as a medium degree of climate change. The assumptions underlying this scenario are largely consistent with the region’s existing economic structure, population growth rate, and water management policies, thereby reflecting a realistic development trend that is neither overly conservative nor excessively expansionary. On this basis, sectoral water demand projections are developed by incorporating both climate correction coefficients and socio-economic adjustment factors to account for future variability across different development scenarios.

Water demand projection under the baseline scenario (SSP2-4.5)

Agricultural water demand: In 2024, the irrigated area of Shihezi City was 4.7562 million mu. According to the Total Water Consumption Control Plan, the irrigated area is expected to decrease by 0.181 million mu by 2030, resulting in a total of 4.5752 million mu. With the improvement of on-farm irrigation infrastructure and the widespread adoption of efficient water-saving technologies, the comprehensive irrigation quota is projected to decline to 350 m3/mu by 2030. Accordingly, the agricultural irrigation water demand under the baseline scenario is estimated to be 16.01 × 108 m3.

Industrial water demand: Based on the Shihezi City Industrial Development Plan, the annual growth rate of industrial added value is projected to reach 7.9% by 2030, with a targeted reduction of 15.16% in water consumption per 104 CNY of industrial output. Under these assumptions, the industrial added value in 2030 is projected to reach 43.742 billion CNY, corresponding to a baseline industrial water demand of 1.03 × 108 m3.

Domestic water demand: Considering an annual population growth rate of 5.5‰ and the 2030 domestic water consumption quotas of 215, 155, and 105 L·person-1·d-1 for urban, township, and rural residents respectively, the total domestic water demand under the baseline scenario is estimated to be 0.55 × 108 m3.

Ecological water demand: According to the 14th Five-Year Plan and 2035 Long-term Development Outline for the 8th Division of Shihezi City, the ecological water demand is determined to be 0.52 × 108 m3 under the baseline scenario.

Water demand projections under different climate scenarios

Considering both climate scenarios and socio-economic correction coefficients (Table 2), the water demand of each sector was comprehensively calculated under three climate scenarios, followed by a water supply-demand balance analysis (Table 3). The results indicate that under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, limited temperature rise, slightly increased precipitation, and the promotion of green transformation and water-saving technologies lead to a total water demand approximately 4–5% lower than that of the baseline scenario. In contrast, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, higher temperatures and intensified evapotranspiration, combined with rapid economic growth, result in an overall increase in total water demand by approximately 6% compared to the baseline. Agriculture remains the dominant water-consuming sector and is the most sensitive to climatic variations, while industrial water use is strongly influenced by socio-economic development, exhibiting an increase of over 18% under the high-emission scenario. Overall, substantial water resource deficits are observed under all scenarios, and the imbalance between supply and demand tends to intensify with continued socio-economic expansion.

Algorithmic results of the water resources optimization model

Based on the NSGA-III algorithm, the water resources allocation model for Shihezi City in 2030 was solved under three climate scenarios, yielding 55, 50, and 71 non-dominated feasible solutions, respectively (Figure 5). Each point in Figure 5 is jointly determined by four objectives—water shortage, economic benefit, pollutant discharge, and carbon emissions from water use—and represents a feasible allocation scheme.

Trade-offs among minimum water deficit (f1), maximum economic benefits (f2), minimum pollutant discharge (f3), and minimum water-related carbon emissions (f4) for all Pareto-optimal solutions under different climate scenarios in 2030

Given that the Pareto solution set contains a large number of feasible schemes, representative solutions consistent with the region’s actual development conditions were identified by excluding single-objective extreme solutions. This selection was conducted under the dual considerations of maximizing water-use economic efficiency and minimizing water resource constraints. Under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, ten candidate schemes were selected, characterized by total water shortages ranging from 3.015 × 108 to 3.019 × 108 m3, economic benefits from 16.62×108 to 18.72×108 CNY, pollutant discharges from 7.52 × 106 to 9.37 × 106 kg, and carbon emissions from water use between 12.13×108 and 13.57 × 108 kg (Figure 5A). Similarly, ten representative schemes were selected for subsequent comprehensive evaluation under both the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios (Figures 5B and 5C).

Selection of optimal allocation schemes

To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance and stability of different schemes, the CRITIC-TOPSIS integrated evaluation model was employed to rank ten candidate schemes under each climate scenario, as presented in Table S1. The weight distribution results indicate that, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, water shortage carries the highest weight (ω=0.3223), while the combined weights of pollutant discharge and carbon emissions from water use account for approximately 50% of the total. This finding highlights the region’s intensified emphasis on improving water-use efficiency and ecological protection under the high-emission scenario.

According to the comprehensive evaluation results, candidate scheme 5 under the SSP1-2.6 scenario (c=0.6246), candidate scheme 1 under the SSP2-4.5 scenario (c=0.6972), and candidate scheme 4 under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (c=0.6737) exhibit the highest integrated benefits, thereby representing the optimal allocation schemes for their respective scenarios.

Based on the optimal selection results, the optimal water allocation schemes for each sector under different climate scenarios were further determined (Table 4), and the flow characteristics between water sources and user sectors were illustrated in Figure 6. Overall, as the climate scenario progresses from low-emission (SSP1-2.(6) to high-emission (SSP5-8.5), regional water allocation exhibits a simultaneous increase in total water use and economic benefits, accompanied by an intensifying environmental burden. Meanwhile, the water-use structure gradually shifts from a surface-water-dominated and agriculture-oriented pattern toward a multi-source, complementary, and economically driven configuration.

Water allocation among water sources and water-use sectors under three climate scenarios (104 m3)

Under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, surface water serves as the main water source, agriculture remains the dominant water-use sector, and reclaimed water is mainly allocated to the industrial and ecological sectors. The total water allocation is 14.16 × 108 m3, with the lowest water deficit and relatively low pollutant discharge and water-use carbon emissions. These results indicate that this scenario is more conducive to a coordinated allocation pattern that emphasizes water conservation and ecological protection.

Under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, the total water allocation increases to 14.78 × 108 m3, with agriculture still accounting for the largest share and industry receiving a relatively small allocation. Although the economic benefit is slightly lower than that under the other scenarios, pollutant discharge and water-use carbon emissions reach their lowest levels. This suggests that SSP2-4.5 provides a relatively balanced performance among water supply security, environmental quality, and low-carbon objectives.

Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, the total water allocation reaches the highest level, at 15.05 × 108 m3. Industrial water use increases markedly, while the share of agricultural water use declines. This scenario generates the highest economic benefit, but it is also accompanied by increased pollutant discharge and water-use carbon emissions, indicating that higher economic output is achieved at the cost of greater environmental pressure and weaker overall sustainability.

Overall, the SSP1-2.6 scenario emphasizes water conservation and ecological harmony; the SSP2-4.5 scenario achieves a balanced performance between agricultural assurance and environmental quality; and the SSP5-8.5 scenario is characterized by maximized economic output but weaker sustainability. These findings indicate that the optimal schemes under different climate pathways exhibit distinct trade-offs among economic, ecological, and resource objectives, underscoring the multi-objective coordination nature of water resource allocation and its sensitivity to climate pathway variations.

Discussion

The carbon emission coefficients used to estimate water-use-related emissions were primarily derived from existing studies and regional emission factors, and therefore remain subject to a certain degree of uncertainty. Taking the SSP2-4.5 scenario as the baseline, a ±10% perturbation was applied to the carbon emission coefficients, followed by re-optimization and scheme selection. As shown in Table 5, within this perturbation range, the variation in water shortage for the optimal scheme is minimal, whereas the other objective values exhibit moderate fluctuations. This suggests that changes in carbon emission parameters mainly affect the trade-off relationships among multiple objectives, while exerting a limited influence on the final scheme selection. Furthermore, Figure 7 shows that the overall water allocation patterns of the optimal schemes remain largely consistent across different perturbation scenarios, with agriculture consistently serving as the dominant water-use sector, surface water remaining the primary supply source, and groundwater functioning as a supplementary source. These results demonstrate the strong structural robustness of the optimal schemes.

Overall, uncertainty during the baseline period is primarily attributable to internal climate variability, whereas uncertainty during the projection period becomes increasingly dominated by inter-model differences and scenario pathways, consistent with previous studies.19 Bias correction improves both the correlation and error performance of the simulations.36 In addition, multi-model ensembles generally provide higher simulation accuracy than individual models37,38 and can effectively reduce systematic biases.39,40,41

This study employs a runoff prediction approach driven by multiple climate pathways, which, compared with the traditional static assumption based on historical averages, more effectively captures the dynamic variability of regional water availability under climate change conditions.26 The LSTM-based projections indicate that the intra-annual distribution of runoff in the Manas River Basin retains a pronounced seasonal pattern, while the overall runoff volume declines, with a more significant reduction observed during the late flood season. This pattern may be attributed to climate warming, which advances the peak timing of glacier meltwater and reduces runoff recharge during the late flood season,42,43 thereby increasing the instability of water supply in autumn. Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, a slight increase in runoff is observed, suggesting that elevated temperature and precipitation under high radiative forcing conditions may partially compensate for runoff reduction in the short term. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Liang et al.44

Under all three climate scenarios, water availability and water demand in Shihezi City are jointly shaped by runoff variation, resource constraints, and socioeconomic development, leading to different degrees of water shortage risk.26 These results indicate that water security in arid regions continues to be jointly influenced by climate change and socioeconomic development.45

The multi-objective optimization results indicate that, given water resource constraints, the optimal schemes require trade-offs among water shortage, economic benefits, pollutant discharge, and water-use carbon emissions, because continuous optimization of a single objective often leads to deterioration in others.46 As shown in Tables 3 and 5 and Figure 6, differences in allocation outcomes across scenarios arise not only from the impacts of climate change on water availability but also from variations in sectoral water demand under different socioeconomic pathways. Under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, corresponding to a green and low-carbon development trajectory, constraints on agricultural and industrial expansion lead to allocation schemes that prioritize water conservation and carbon reduction, resulting in improved ecological performance but relatively limited economic benefits. Under SSP2-4.5, a more balanced outcome is achieved among economic growth, water supply security, and environmental performance.47 In contrast, under SSP5-8.5, rapid economic expansion drives increased industrial and domestic water demand, yielding higher economic outputs but also leading to elevated carbon emissions and pollutant loads, while the substitution role of reclaimed water in industrial and ecological sectors becomes more pronounced. Thus, differences in climate pathways not only affect water availability but also reshape water demand and supply structures, thereby influencing water use patterns and allocation strategies.

To enhance system resilience and policy applicability, future oasis cities in arid regions should establish a water resource management framework centered on multi-source complementarity and low-carbon efficiency.48 Agricultural water use should be optimized by improving irrigation efficiency through advanced water-saving technologies and intelligent scheduling,49 increasing the irrigation water-use efficiency coefficient from 0.62 to 0.70–0.75. The reuse of reclaimed water should be expanded, with the recycling rate increased to 25%–30% by 2030 and prioritized for industrial cooling and ecological replenishment.50 Groundwater exploitation should be strictly controlled to maintain its role as a supplementary resource and to prevent long-term overextraction and groundwater depletion.51 Meanwhile, water use per unit of GDP and carbon emission intensity should be incorporated into a unified performance evaluation framework to promote coordinated water conservation and carbon reduction.52

Taken together, this study develops a climate-adaptive and low-carbon-oriented framework for optimal water resources allocation. The results indicate that, for the 2030 planning horizon, runoff in the Manas River Basin is projected to generally decline, with the most pronounced reduction occurring in September. In Shihezi City, water shortage rates range from 17.54% to 19.13% across scenarios, indicating that water scarcity remains a major challenge. The preferred allocation schemes suggest that SSP1-2.6 favors water conservation and carbon reduction; SSP2-4.5 provides the most balanced overall performance, whereas SSP5-8.5 delivers higher economic benefits but at greater environmental cost. These findings highlight the need to jointly consider water supply security, economic development, ecological protection, and carbon mitigation in future water resources allocation for arid oasis cities.

Limitations of the study

Uncertainties in CMIP6 scenario pathways and differences in model structure may still affect water availability projections, thereby influencing the resulting allocation schemes.53,54 In addition, LSTM models are prone to error accumulation in medium-to long-term prediction and remain limited in their ability to capture extreme events and physical constraints.55,56 Future research could incorporate probabilistic distributions, scenario combinations, or parameter perturbation methods to quantitatively assess the influence of climate scenario uncertainty on water resources allocation results.

Method details

Study area

Shihezi City of the Eighth Division, Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (hereafter referred to as the “Division-City”), is located in the Manas River Basin at the northern foothills of the Tianshan Mountains and the southern margin of the Junggar Basin (84°58′-86°24′E, 43°26′-45°20′N), covering a total area of 6,007.29 km2, and constitutes a core region of the Tianshan North Slope Economic Belt.57 The Division-City is characterized by a temperate continental arid climate and represents a typical oasis city in the arid region of northwestern China.

The regional hydrological system consists of the Manas River, Ningjia River, Jingou River, and Bayingou River. Among them, the Manas River serves as the primary surface water source for the Division-City, with a total length of 504.3 km, a drainage area of approximately 2.43 × 104 km,2 and a multi-year average runoff of 12.21 × 108 m3, primarily supplied by glacial meltwater from the northern slopes of the Tianshan Mountains.58 Climate change is altering the spatiotemporal distribution of runoff, increasing the uncertainty of hydrological processes and posing challenges to regional water resource management.59

The economic structure of the Division-City is dominated by industry, with agriculture serving as a supporting sector. With ongoing socioeconomic development, water demand continues to rise, intensifying the imbalance between water supply and demand. In the context of the “Dual Carbon” strategy, research on multi-objective optimal allocation of water resources is of considerable practical significance for improving regional water resource management.

Data sources

This study employs climate projection data from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). Building upon CMIP5, CMIP6 integrates Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) with Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), thereby establishing a more comprehensive framework for future climate change scenarios.60 Compared with CMIP5, CMIP6 fills gaps in certain emission pathway scenarios and provides a more extensive and robust simulation basis for assessing the impacts of climate change on socioeconomic systems.

Six global climate models (GCMs) (Table S2) and three representative scenarios—SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 (Table S3) —were selected, corresponding to low, medium, and high radiative forcing levels, respectively, to characterize future climate conditions under varying socioeconomic development and carbon emission pathways and to provide a basis for assessing the potential impacts of climate change on regional water resource dynamics.61

The Kensiwate Hydrological Station, located at the outlet of the mountainous section of the Manas River, was selected as a representative monitoring site for this study. The observed dataset includes monthly time series of runoff, precipitation, and temperature recorded at the station from 1957 to 2014. The data cover both the historical simulation period (1957-2014) and the projection period (2021-2039), with key variables comprising precipitation, mean temperature, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature.

Long Short-Term Memory neural network (LSTM)

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is a deep recurrent neural network equipped with a memory mechanism. By introducing a forget gate, input gate, and output gate, it enables dynamic selection and state updating of temporal information. This design effectively mitigates the gradient vanishing and exploding issues commonly encountered in traditional recurrent neural networks (RNNs) when modeling long sequences, thereby enhancing the stability and representational capacity of the model in complex temporal pattern recognition tasks.62

NSGA-III fundamental principles

The third-generation Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm (NSGA-III) is a reference-point–guided multi-objective optimization algorithm. Building upon NSGA-II, it introduces predefined reference points to replace the conventional crowding-distance mechanism, thereby enhancing the diversity and distribution uniformity of solution sets in high-dimensional objective spaces.33 The algorithm performs population ranking through fast non-dominated sorting and selects individuals based on their association with reference points, improving the distribution of solutions while maintaining convergence.68 Owing to these advantages, NSGA-III is well suited for solving optimization problems involving high-dimensional and strongly conflicting objectives.69

The CRITIC-TOPSIS evaluation method

The CRITIC-TOPSIS evaluation method is a comprehensive assessment framework that integrates objective weighting with multi-attribute decision analysis.70 The CRITIC (Criteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation) technique objectively determines indicator weights by quantifying the information content and conflict intensity of each criterion based on its standard deviation and correlation coefficients, thereby minimizing the influence of subjective judgment.71

Subsequently, according to the TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) principle, a weighted and normalized decision matrix is constructed to identify the positive and negative ideal solutions and to compute the relative closeness coefficient of each alternative, enabling comprehensive ranking of the candidate schemes.72

By combining the objectivity of CRITIC with the systematic structure of TOPSIS, this hybrid approach ensures an objective process for both weight determination and scheme ranking in multi-criteria evaluations, thereby enhancing the scientific rigor and robustness of decision-making outcomes.

The NSGA-III algorithm was implemented using the Pymoo optimization framework, with a population size of 600 and a maximum of 30,000 evolutionary generations. The initial population was generated via Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS). The simulated binary crossover (SBX) operator was adopted with a crossover probability of 0.9 and a distribution index of 12, while polynomial mutation (PM) was employed with a mutation probability of 0.0625 and a distribution index of 18.
