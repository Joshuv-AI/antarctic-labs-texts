Unraveling the risk factors, prognostic predictors, and evolving therapeutic approaches for phlegmasia cerulea dolens over 30 years
Authors: Yangyang Feng, Gaoxiang Fan, Wei Song, Xuehu Wang, Fenghe Li, Zhui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePhlegmasia cerulea dolens (PCD) is the most severe form of deep vein thrombosis in the lower extremities. Clinical guidelines have not systematically outlined its risk factors, diagnostic methods, or treatment strategies. In this study, the literature published over the past 30 years is reviewed to summarize the clinical features of, diagnostic approaches to, prognostic factors of, and evolving management strategies for PCD.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 144 PCD patients from 1990 to 2024. Demographic data, clinical features, comorbidities, diagnostic methods, treatments, and prognostic outcomes were extracted. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to evaluate the impact of clinical factors on prognosis.ResultsThe majority of PCD cases involved the left lower limb, with 93.00% of patients having thrombosis extending into the iliocaval vein. Key risk factors included venous structural abnormalities, malignant tumors, and prior venous thromboembolism, with reproductive system cancers being the most common malignancy. Endovenous debulking techniques emerged as the preferred treatment method, reducing the risk of amputation and death by 70% compared with anticoagulation or thrombectomy. Patients with bilateral limb involvement, stage III PCD, or who underwent fasciotomy had significantly greater risks of amputation and death. The overall mortality rate was 18.75%, which was due primarily to metabolic acidosis and multiple organ failure.ConclusionMultimodal imaging should be recommended for the systematic evaluation of PCD to identify underlying malignancies and venous structural abnormalities. Endovenous debulking has demonstrated advantages in improving prognosis and should be recommended as a first-line treatment for PCD.Keywords: Phlegmasia cerulea dolens, deep venous thrombosis, May–Thurner syndrome, malignancy, endovascular debulking

Introduction

Phlegmasia cerulea dolens (PCD) is a rare but severe form of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) marked by rapid venous outflow obstruction leading to limb congestion, cyanosis, ischemia, and potentially shock, amputation, or death. Owing to its rarity, its exact incidence is unknown, but reports show that 40–60% of cases may progress to venous gangrene, with major amputation occurring in 14% of cases and death occurring in 26% [1,2]. Although significant progress has been made in the diagnosis and treatment of lower extremity DVT in recent decades, PCD remains under-recognized and under-researched, with most available knowledge being derived from case reports and small case series. The pathogenesis of PCD is complex, and a variety of factors, such as inferior vena cava filter (IVCF) placement, May–Thurner syndrome (MTS), malignancy, and immune diseases, put patients at high risk of developing PCD. However, the specific effects of these risk factors on prognosis are not yet fully understood [3].

Currently, anticoagulation is the standard treatment for DVT [4]. However, in patients with PCD, anticoagulation alone is insufficient to prevent severe complications. Existing guidelines include general recommendations for managing severe or limb-threatening DVT, but disease-specific diagnostic criteria and standardized treatment strategies tailored to PCD are limited. Emerging endovascular debulking (ED) techniques, such as catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) and percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy (PMT), offer promising alternatives [5,6], but robust evidence supporting their efficacy and long-term outcomes in patients with PCD is still lacking. Given the rarity of PCD and its high morbidity and mortality, more targeted clinical data are urgently needed to guide effective decision-making.

To address these challenges, the literature concerning PCD from 1990 to 2024 was systematically reviewed, with the goal of evaluating high-risk factors for PCD among patients of different epidemiological backgrounds as well as the prognostic significance of these factors. Additionally, the evolution of PCD treatment strategies over the past 30 years was analysed to explore the critical role of emerging therapeutic technologies in improving the prognosis of patients with PCD. These findings provide a stronger scientific foundation for future clinical practice and the development of treatment guidelines.

Methods

Search strategy

The literature screening process followed the PRISMA guidelines to ensure a structured and reproducible approach to study inclusion and exclusion. A systematic literature search was conducted in the MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases using the keyword ‘Phlegmasia cerulea dolens’ to retrospectively analyse the risk factors, clinical features, diagnosis and treatment of patients with PCD and to assess the effects of these factors on patient prognosis. Additionally, we manually retrieved relevant articles from the references of the identified studies. When necessary, we contacted the authors to obtain additional data or clarifications. This study is based entirely on previously published literature and does not involve any new data with human participants. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University determined that ethical approval was not needed.

Eligibility criteria

First, we performed a preliminary screen of case reports, case series, letters to the editor, and clinical images with detailed case information published in English. We further screened the collected literature according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) complete demographic information, (2) identifiable risk factors, (3) typical clinical manifestations, (4) detailed diagnostic methods and therapeutic interventions, and (5) final prognosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) published in a language other than English, (2) upper limb PCD and (3) incomplete PCD case records.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by two investigators (W. XH. and L. FH.) using standardized data extraction forms. The extracted data included the following: (1) patient-related variables, including age, sex, risk factors and affected site; (2) clinical symptoms and signs; (3) diagnostic methods and treatment strategies; and (5) final prognosis. Cases with incomplete information were excluded from the analysis to ensure the integrity of the dataset. Variables that were not included in the multivariable analysis were described categorically, and the final analysis was based on available data. On the basis of the symptoms and signs, the severity of PCD in the affected limb was categorized into three grades, as shown in Table 1. Patients who underwent both venous thrombectomy and PMT were classified according to the primary technique that was used on the basis of descriptions in the literature. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus or by discussion with a third reviewer (L.Z.). Cohen’s kappa was used to assess the agreement between the investigators. A kappa value between 0.6 and 0.7 indicates good agreement, and a value of ≥0.75 indicates excellent agreement. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for Cohen’s kappa was calculated to assess the reliability of the agreement.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs), and categorical data are presented as counts and proportions. To determine the relationships between clinical factors and patient outcomes, univariate stepwise logistic regression analysis was performed. For univariate analysis, continuous variables were analysed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, and categorical variables were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Factors that showed statistical significance in the univariate analysis were included in the logistic regression analysis. Owing to sample size limitations, only binary logistic regression analysis was feasible. Therefore, we categorized patients into two groups according to outcomes, namely the adverse outcome group and the recovery group. Adverse outcomes included both disability and death, which allowed us to analyse the influence of clinical factors on adverse outcomes. To further investigate high-risk factors for mortality, outcomes were also categorized into survival and death categories, and a separate analysis was performed. After ensuring that there was no multicollinearity between independent variables, odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% CIs were used to assess the strength of the associations between the independent and dependent variables. A two-sided P value <0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. All the analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Mac V.25.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics; Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Basic characteristics of the included studies

A total of 457 articles related to PCD were retrieved. After removing duplicate articles, articles that were published in a language other than English, and articles without abstracts or full texts, 280 articles were selected for further screening. Three independent researchers reached a consensus regarding article inclusion according to the eligibility criteria, with a Cohen’s kappa value of 0.7 (95% CI: 0.618–0.782), indicating good agreement. This process resulted in a dataset of 124 articles involving 144 patients. This dataset included 107 case reports, five case series, 11 clinical image reports with detailed clinical information and five letters or correspondences. The included articles were published from 1990 to 2024; publications from 1990–2000 described 21 patients, publications from 2001–2010 described 16 patients, and publications from 2011–2024 described 107 patients. Additionally, these patients originated from six continents: North America (82 patients), Asia (19 patients), Europe (34 patients), Africa (4 patients), Oceania (3 patients), and South America (2 patients). General clinical characteristics and high-risk factors of PCD patients

The ages of the PCD patients ranged from 16 to 94 years, with a mean age of 55.38 ± 16.81 years. The age distribution peaked between 41 and 80 years; the 51–60 years age group included 25% of all patients, and was the largest age group (Figure 3). In terms of sex, male patients slightly outnumbered female patients, with 78 males (54.16%) and 66 females (45.83%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.18:1.

Age distribution of the PCD patients.

Among the 144 patients, 11 had no documented or identifiable high-risk factors for PCD. This study analyzed 10 major high-risk factors for PCD (Table 2). Among patients with known risk factors, 93 had a single high-risk factor, 41 had two major risk factors, and 10 had three risk factors. Vascular structural abnormalities were the most common high-risk factor, and these abnormalities were present in 42 patients; in 36 patients, these abnormalities arose because of MTS. Malignancies were the second most common risk factor and were observed in 37 patients. Additionally, 25 patients had a prior history of venous thromboembolism (VTE). These factors represented the primary high-risk contributors to PCD, accounting for 20.68%, 18.22%, and 12.80% of the total cases, respectively.

Among the 37 patients with malignancies, 15 had reproductive system cancers—six patients with ovarian cancer, five patients with prostate cancer, two patients with cervical cancer and one patient each with seminoma and vulvar squamous cell carcinoma. Among male patients, prostate cancer and lung cancer were the leading risk factors, accounting for six cases each. Among female patients, ovarian cancer and breast cancer were the predominant risk factors, accounting for six and three cases, respectively. Notably, in 10 patients, PCD was the initial presentation of the underlying malignancy, including four patients with reproductive system tumours, three patients with lung tumours, and one patient each with hepatocellular carcinoma, renal carcinoma and breast cancer.

Clinical manifestations of PCD in patients

PCD predominantly affected the left lower limb: 92 patients had lower left limb involvement (63.88%), followed by 35 patients (25.00%) with right lower limb involvement and 17 patients (11.80%) with involvement of both limbs. The ratio of left-sided to right-sided involvement was 2.63:1, whereas the ratio of left-sided to bilateral involvement was 5.41:1. Among the thrombus-involved levels, excluding one unrecorded case, the highest level of thrombus involvement was the inferior vena cava (IVC) in 22 patients and the iliac veins in 111 patients. In contrast, the highest level of involvement in the femoropopliteal vein occurred in 8 patients.

All the patients presented with cyanosis of the affected limb. Tension blisters were observed in 22 patients (15.27%, 22/144), gangrene was observed in 24 patients (16.67%, 24/144) and diminished or absent distal arterial pulses were observed in 98 patients (68.05%, 98/144). Additionally, 77 patients (53.47%, 77/144) were documented as having sensory or motor nerve deficits. On the basis of these clinical manifestations, the severity was classified into three grades: 29 patients were classified as having Grade I PCD, 91 patients were classified as having Grade II PCD, and 24 patients were classified as having Grade III PCD. Among the 96 patients who underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) or pulmonary perfusion scans, only 27 patients were diagnosed with pulmonary embolism (PE) (28.12%, 27/96), of whom 17 had symptomatic PE (17.71%, 17/96) and 10 had asymptomatic PE.

Diagnosis and treatment of PCD patients

A total of 113 patients (80.15%) underwent lower limb venous ultrasound as the first-choice diagnostic method. CT was performed for 62 patients (45.03%) and was used as the primary diagnostic tool for 21 of these patients (13.74%). Forty patients (30.53%) underwent combined diagnosis with both ultrasound and contrast-enhanced CT, and most of these patients had a history of IVC filter placement. Four patients also underwent MRI to assess ischemia in the lower limbs, and venography was the first-choice diagnostic method for 5 patients.

In this study, 38 patients received conservative treatment consisting of anticoagulation therapy combined with hydration and limb elevation. ED therapy was administered to 80 patients, 56 of whom received CDT. However, only 4 patients were treated with CDT alone. PMT was performed in 60 patients, and venous stents were placed in 33 patients. Additionally, 30 patients underwent venous thrombectomy, and 36 patients underwent fasciotomy for decompression. An IVC filter was used in 62 patients during treatment.

Over the past decade, CDT and/or PMT have increasingly become mainstream treatments for PCD. From 2020 to 2024, 60% of patients chose PMT as the primary treatment, while the rate of venous thrombectomy use decreased from 37.0% between 2015 and 2019 to 13.8%. Moreover, the rate of fasciotomy use decreased from 40.7% to 21.5%. The changes in treatment methods for PCD are shown in Figure 4. Among patients with similar disease severity, the rate of fasciotomy use was significantly lower in the ED group than in the venous thrombectomy group (17.1% vs. 40.0%, p = 0.025). Additionally, the incidence of adverse outcomes was significantly lower in patients who received an ED than in those who received anticoagulation therapy alone (19.7% vs. 52.6%, p = 0.007). These results suggest that ED is associated with a lower need for fasciotomy and a reduced rate of adverse outcomes in patients with comparable disease severity. Clinical outcomes of PCD patients

A total of 110 patients achieved full recovery after treatment. Sixteen patients experienced disability due to amputation or severe neuromuscular impairment. Twenty-seven patients died during hospitalization, with an overall mortality rate of 18.75%. Among the deaths, 17 were caused by complications related to muscle necrosis, such as acute renal failure, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia and multiple organ failure, resulting in a mortality rate of 9.03%. Notably, only one patient died from PE, yielding a PE-related mortality rate of 0.69%. Nine other patients died from comorbid conditions, including malignancies, myocardial infarction, severe infection, complications from kidney transplantation, and stroke. The follow-up records of 71 patients were available, and 44 patients had follow-up periods of 6 months or longer, with an average follow-up duration of 17.52 ± 18.69 months. Twelve patients experienced varying degrees of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), such as chronic limb swelling, resulting in a PTS incidence of 27.27% (12/44) among PCD patients.

Clinical predictors of adverse outcomes and mortality in PCD patients

Adverse outcomes among PCD patients included disability and death. According to the univariate analysis, the laterality of thrombus involvement, PCD grade, device-related factors, history of VTE, ED and fasciotomy significantly affected patient prognosis. Furthermore, logistic regression analysis revealed that patients with bilateral limb involvement had a significantly increased risk of death or disability (OR = 15.848; 95% CI: 2.631–95.449; p = 0.003), and those with Grade III PCD had a 6.4-fold greater risk of poor outcomes than patients with Grade I PCD did (OR = 6.416; 95% CI: 1.346–30.571; p = 0.021). Additionally, compared with patients who did not undergo fasciotomy, patients who underwent fasciotomy had a more than threefold increased risk of death or disability (OR = 3.176, 95% CI: 1.056–9.552; p = 0.040). In contrast, ED significantly reduced the risk of adverse outcomes by approximately 70% (OR = 0.214, 95% CI: 0.056–0.815; p = 0.024) (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study represents the largest retrospective analysis of PCD over the past 30 years. The peak incidence of PCD occurred between the ages of 41 and 80, and vascular structural abnormalities, particularly MTS, malignancies, and a history of prior VTE, were identified as the most significant risk factors. On the basis of the available case reports, we have shown that reproductive system malignancies are the most common type of cancer associated with PCD. A combination of lower limb venous ultrasound and abdominal/pulmonary CT provided more comprehensive information for the diagnosis of PCD. In terms of treatment, ED has gradually emerged as the first-line therapy for PCD and has advantages in reducing mortality and disability rates. However, patients with bilateral limb involvement, those with Grade III PCD, and those who underwent fasciotomy still experienced poor outcomes, with significantly increased risks of disability and death.

MTS is a known high-risk factor for lower extremity DVT, which explains the significantly greater incidence of left-sided DVT than right-sided DVT [7]. This risk factor was similarly replicated in PCD, with MTS accounting for 25% of PCD cases. Left-sided PCD was 2.63 times more common than right-sided PCD. Additionally, lesions originating from the IVC, such as congenital anomalies, occlusions, or aneurysms, also contribute to PCD development in critical ways. Studies have shown that congenital IVC anomalies can increase the risk of thrombosis by more than 60%. In this study, 15 of the 17 device-related risk factors were caused by IVCF, which also explains the role of a previous history of VTE as a major risk factor for PCD. Previous research has reported that IVCF-induced thrombosis can occur in up to 31% of patients, and thrombosis in the IVC, with its large clot burden, blocks both iliac vein outflow and pelvic collateral circulation, leading to worse outcomes, especially in patients with bilateral limb involvement.

Malignancies significantly increase the risk of VTE. Fernandes et al. reported that among 10,315 VTE patients, those with haematological malignancies had a relative risk of 26.2, whereas those with lung and gastrointestinal malignancies had relative risks of 24.8 and 18.9, respectively. However, patients with reproductive system cancers, such as prostate, cervical, and ovarian cancer, have lower risks, at 3.4, 3.3 and 2.3, respectively [8]. Our study revealed that reproductive system malignancies are the most common cancer-related high-risk factor for PCD, followed by lung cancer. Reproductive system malignancies account for 45.45% of all cancer cases, and three most common malignancies are ovarian, prostate, and cervical cancers. In male patients, prostate cancer and lung cancer were predominant, whereas in female patients, ovarian and breast cancers were most common. Among cancer patients who present with PCD as the initial manifestation, reproductive system tumors and lung cancer are the most common. In this study, most malignancies associated with PCD were in advanced or late stages. Blom et al.’s study of 66,329 cancer patients revealed that patients with distant metastasis, those who were receiving chemotherapy, and those who were receiving hormonal therapy had significantly greater risks of thrombosis, with relative risks of 1.9, 2.2, and 1.6, respectively [9]. Mechanical compression of the pelvic veins by reproductive system malignancies is more common among patients with advanced-stage disease, leading to venous outflow obstruction and exacerbating the severity of DVT. In clinical practice, the severity of DVT associated with reproductive system malignancies may be underestimated, and VTE management in these patients should be emphasized. In our study, 10 cancer patients presented with PCD as the initial symptom, accounting for 6.94% (10/144) of all PCD cases, with occult reproductive system cancers and pulmonary malignancies being most prevalent. Previous studies have indicated that in patients with unprovoked VTE, the 12-month incidence of cancer is 5.2% [10]. Although undifferentiated screening significantly increases cancer detection rates by approximately 2.17 times, it does not substantially improve overall survival or reduce cancer-related mortality [11]. Most experts recommend limited screening for patients with unprovoked VTE, including medical history, physical examination, basic blood tests, and age- and sex-specific cancer screenings (e.g. mammography, Papanicolaou test, and prostate-specific antigen testing), while routine use of CT or PET scans is not recommended [12–14]. Among patients with PCD, 18.22% were found to have concurrent malignancies, suggesting a greater likelihood of underlying cancer in this group. Therefore, screening for lung cancer and genital system malignancies may be warranted. Additionally, approximately 20.68% of PCD patients have iliac vein structural abnormalities, which can be identified alongside malignancies using abdominal and pelvic CT scans, providing a more comprehensive diagnostic approach. Therefore, screening may provide clinical benefits for this patient population.

We found that 133 patients experienced thrombus involvement above the iliac veins, accounting for 92.36% of the total patient population, which emphasized that extensive thrombosis is a key factor in the progression from DVT to PCD. Importantly, 8 PCD patients had thrombi limited to the femoropopliteal veins. Khamin et al. reported that 6% of PCD patients had thrombosis confined to the popliteal vein [2]. Therefore, clinicians should not underestimate the potential for both proximal and distal DVT to progress to PCD. The hallmark of PCD is blue discoloration of the limb, which is caused by extensive thrombosis leading to decompensation of superficial veins and collateral circulation, increased capillary permeability, severe tissue oedema, and hypoxia, resulting in deoxygenated haemoglobin accumulation. As tissue pressure increases, tension blisters develop, accompanied by arterial spasms and decreased peripheral pulses, indicating progression to Grade II PCD. When venous gangrene occurs, the disease progresses to Grade III, which carried a sixfold higher risk of disability or death than Grade I PCD in our study; this finding is consistent with those of previous studies showing that more severe symptoms lead to worse outcomes [2].

The overall mortality rate among PCD patients was 20.83%. Muhlberger et al.’s study of 17 PCD patients reported a mortality rate of 22%, which increased to 50% when PCD was associated with cancer or PE [15]. In this study, 17 patients died from PCD-related complications, such as metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, multiorgan dysfunction, and PE. Although 27 patients were diagnosed with PE, only 1 died from a PE, resulting in a PE-related mortality rate of 0.69%. This rate is significantly lower than the mortality rates associated with limb ischemia and primary conditions such as cancer, severe infections, stroke, and myocardial infarction. Previous studies have reported that PE mortality rates in DVT patients with cancer range from 1.36% to 1.96% [16], and the significantly lower PE mortality in this study may be due to the rapid progression and severity of PCD, prompting treatment to prevent PE. Additionally, some PCD cases involve thrombosis secondary to IVCF, further reducing PE-related mortality. Clinically, although aggressive management of the primary disease is important, attention should be given to managing limb ischemia in patients with PCD, including timely thrombectomy or endovascular interventions to restore blood flow and close monitoring for signs of metabolic abnormalities and multiorgan failure.

In this study, 80.15% of the PCD patients underwent Doppler ultrasound monitoring as the initial diagnostic approach, reflecting the common use of this tool in clinical practice as the first-line method for diagnosing DVT [17–19]. However, PCD often involves the iliocaval venous system, and ultrasound may not provide a complete picture because of attenuation of deep structures or interference from bowel gas. Given the frequent associations of PCD with vascular abnormalities, tumours and a history of IVCF placement, CT should be a routine method for assessing vascular anatomy, extent of thrombus, degree of obstruction, and IVCF position. CT has a sensitivity of 89%–100% and a specificity of 94%–100% for detecting DVT [20]. Although this study did not include a specific analysis of the rate of PE detection by CTPA in PCD patients, the ACCP and ESC guidelines recommend CTPA for patients with iliac–femoral thrombosis or respiratory symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, hypoxia, or hemodynamic instability [21,22]. For patients with renal insufficiency, V/Q scans may be considered [23]. MRI can be used to assess the severity of limb ischemia, but its longer image acquisition time and inability to reflect dynamic changes in compartment pressures limit its use [24]. In accordance with the BOAST guidelines, severe limb swelling with passive stretch pain should suggest compartment syndrome, and fasciotomy should be performed promptly, especially for patients with rapidly progressing symptoms [25]. Some studies suggest that a compartment pressure exceeding 30 mmHg is an indication for fasciotomy [26], but most consensus recommendations advise against delaying decompression surgery on the basis solely of ICP or imaging findings [27,28]. In summary, multimodal imaging combining ultrasound and CT provides comprehensive information for the diagnosis of PCD and helps detect early signs of compartment syndrome through the dynamic monitoring of limb symptoms and signs.

Timely removal of thrombi and restoration of lower limb circulation are the core goals of PCD treatment [29]. In accordance with the ESC and ACCP guidelines, CDT and PMT are recommended for patients with iliac–femoral vein thrombosis or limb-threatening PCD [21,22]. This study revealed that PMT and CDT have increasingly become the primary methods of thrombectomy in PCD patients. Over the past five years, PMT has been used as the primary treatment method for 60% of patients, whereas the use of traditional venous thrombectomy or fasciotomy has gradually decreased, reflecting a reduced reliance on invasive surgeries. Previous studies have shown that CDT significantly increases the risk of bleeding in DVT patients [30], whereas combining CDT with PMT reduces this risk by minimizing thrombolytic agent use and improving thrombus clearance rates [31,32]. In clinical practice, PCD patients should consider the need for fasciotomy when selecting a specific ED technique. To reduce the risk of bleeding, CDT should be avoided in patients who require fasciotomy, and PMT should be prioritized. Compared with venous thrombectomy and anticoagulation alone, ED significantly improved adverse outcomes and reduced mortality and disability rates. ED rapidly clears thrombi, improves circulation, and reduces tissue necrosis, aligning with guidelines emphasizing the importance of restoring blood flow early. This study also revealed that with comparable PCD severity, ED significantly reduces adverse outcomes, possibly because of its early application, thus reducing the need for fasciotomy. Patients who underwent fasciotomy had a 3.1-fold greater rate of adverse outcomes than those who did not undergo fasciotomy. The widespread adoption of ED is gradually transforming PCD treatment practices. As this technique becomes more common, the indications and applications for fasciotomy are being optimized to reduce the need for invasive surgeries and improve outcomes.

Despite being the largest retrospective analysis of PCD to date and providing valuable insights into the risk factors, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, treatment strategies, and prognostic factors of PCD, this study has certain limitations. First, increasing the sample size would increase the statistical power, especially when evaluating the effectiveness of complex treatment methods such as fasciotomy and ED. Second, this study was retrospective, and the completeness of the data is inevitably affected by incomplete case records or missing clinical details, which may influence the interpretation of specific risk factors and outcomes. Furthermore, given the limited available literature on PCD, more severe or atypical cases may be more likely to be published, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Despite these limitations, the key conclusions revealed in this study about the overall pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment trends of PCD, particularly the significant benefits of ED in reducing adverse outcomes, remain valuable. Future prospective randomized controlled trials will further validate and optimize these findings, informing the development of more robust treatment strategies for PCD patients.

In conclusion, this study systematically analysed the risk factors, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, treatment strategies, and prognostic factors of PCD, highlighting the critical roles of vascular structural abnormalities, reproductive system malignancies, and a previous history of VTE as key risk factors for developing PCD. ED techniques have demonstrated significant advantages in reducing adverse outcomes. However, patients with bilateral limb involvement, Grade III PCD, and those who undergo a fasciotomy still face high risks of adverse outcomes and death. With the increasing application of ED, the indications for fasciotomy are being optimized to reduce the need for invasive surgeries. Given the increased likelihood of underlying malignancies in PCD patients, targeted screening for lung cancer and reproductive system malignancies may be beneficial, especially for patients with significant risk factors. Future prospective studies are needed to validate and optimize these treatment strategies, with the aim of reducing adverse outcomes and mortality rates, providing more effective, individualized treatment plans for PCD patients, and incorporating relevant cancer screening into routine diagnostics for at-risk populations.
