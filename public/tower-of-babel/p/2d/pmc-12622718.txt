Thoracotomy is better than minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy in the lymph node dissection of lung cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ying Liu, Zengfeng Sun, Yangyang Deng, Hui Li, Yanlin Li, Dong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Currently, the predominant method for surgical intervention in lung cancer is minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy (MITL). This study, however, seeks to evaluate and compare various operative techniques to determine which approach offers superior efficacy for lymph node (LN) dissection during pulmonary resection.Methods:We conducted a comprehensive systematic search across several electronic databases, including Embase, the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Medline, and Web of Science, to identify studies that compared MITL (either robotic-assisted or video-assisted) with conventional thoracotomy in the context of lung cancer resection. For the meta-analysis, pooled standard mean differences (SMDs) and relative risks were computed, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251147327.Results:The analysis incorporated data from 42 studies, involving a total of 8947 participants. When compared to MITL, the thoracotomy group exhibited a significantly higher total lymph node number (LNN) (SMD −0.22; 95% CI −0.28 to −0.16; I² = 81.6%, P = .000). This trend remained consistent in the video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy (VATL) subgroup, where thoracotomy was associated with increased total LNN (SMD −0.27; 95% CI −0.33 to −0.21; I² = 80.5%, P = .000). In contrast, no significant difference in total LNN was observed between thoracotomy and robotic-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy (RATL) (SMD 0.12; 95% CI −0.04 to 0.27; I² = 16.1%, P = .133). Similarly, thoracotomy yielded a greater number of total lymph node stations (LNS) relative to MITL (SMD −0.16; 95% CI −0.21 to −0.11; I² = 88.9%, P = .000). The VATL subgroup analysis also showed advantage for thoracotomy in total LNS (SMD −0.18; 95% CI −0.23 to −0.12; I² = 88.8%, P = .000), whereas no notable difference was found within the RATL subgroup (SMD 0.21; 95% CI −0.04 to 0.47; I² = 90.0%, P = .098). Furthermore, patients receiving thoracotomy demonstrated higher N1 LNN (SMD −0.22; 95% CI −0.32 to −0.12; I² = 35.3%, P = .000) and more N1 LNS (SMD −0.24; 95% CI −0.41 to −0.07; I² = 43.8%, P = .005) compared to the MITL group. An increase in N2 LNN was also observed with thoracotomy (SMD −0.12; 95% CI −0.20 to −0.03; I² = 50.4%, P = .006). This significance persisted in the VATL subgroup for N2 LNN (SMD −0.18; 95% CI −0.28 to −0.08; I² = 43.1%, P = .000), but not in the RATL subgroup, where no statistical difference was detected (SMD 0.07; 95% CI −0.09 to 0.24; I² = 0%, P = .395).Conclusion:Thoracotomy demonstrates a significant advantage over MITL in LN dissection for lung cancer. Subgroup analyses further indicate the superiority of thoracotomy compared to VATL. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between thoracotomy and RATL in terms of total LNN, total LNS, or N2 LNN. Future studies should incorporate additional high-quality literature to enhance the robustness of these findings.

1. Introduction

Lung cancer continues to be among the most commonly diagnosed cancers globally. According to current evidence, approximately 27.5% of individuals with clinical stage I lung cancer who undergo lobectomy are found to have metastatic involvement in N1 or N2 lymph nodes (LN).[1] Nonetheless, widely used noninvasive staging techniques such as computed tomography and positron emission tomography/computed tomography exhibit constraints in both sensitivity and specificity for precise nodal assessment.[2] Therefore, accurately determining LN metastasis through surgical means remains essential. Surgical resection continues to represent the standard of care for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In comparison to conventional open thoracotomy, minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy (MITL) better meets cosmetic and minimal access objectives, and is associated with benefits including shorter recovery time, less postoperative pain, and reduced length of hospitalization.[3,4] However, considerable debate remains as to whether MITL can provide LN dissection outcomes comparable to those of thoracotomy in the surgical management of lung cancer.[4,5]

Therefore, this study analyzed the number of LN dissection in lung cancer surgery, aimed to systematically evaluate the effect of MITL and traditional thoracotomy on LN dissection, and provide the basis for the clinical use of the best treatment.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol and guidance

The conduct and reporting of this investigation adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) Guidelines.[6] Furthermore, the review protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251147327.

2.2. Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis included relevant literature retrieved from Embase, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Medline, and Web of Science, with the search cutoff set on September 07, 2023. The inclusion criteria were structured according to the PICOS framework: P (Population): patients undergoing LN dissection for lung cancer; I (Intervention): minimally invasive segmentectomy or lobectomy; C (Comparator): open thoracotomy; O (Outcomes): at least one of the following: total number of LN, number of lymph node stations (LNS), N1 LN count, N1 station count, N2 LN count, or N2 station count.

The search strategy incorporated Medical Subject Headings and keywords such as “lung cancer,” “lung neoplasm,” “thoracotomy,” “open surgery,” “robotic surgery,” “robotic thoracic surgery,” “video-assisted thoracic surgery,” and “VATS,” along with comparative study filters. No restrictions were imposed on language or publication date. As this study is a systematic review and meta-analysis based on published literature, ethical approval or institutional review board evaluation was not required.

2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected based on the following eligibility criteria: comparative data must be available between thoracotomy and MITL regarding LN dissection in lung cancer. Required outcome measures included at least one of the following: total LN count, total number of LNS, N1 lymph node number (LNN), N1 stations, N2 LNN, or N2 stations. Articles that did not meet all the inclusion criteria outlined above were excluded.

2.4. Data extraction and outcome measures

Two investigators (L.Y. and S.Z.F.) independently assessed the retrieved studies based on the predetermined eligibility criteria. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (L.D.) was consulted to facilitate discussion and reach a consensus regarding inclusion. The following data were extracted from each study: 1st author, year of publication, country of origin, study design, patient group characteristics, age, clinical tumor stage, and relevant outcomes (where available), as summarized in Table 1.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 12.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station). In this meta-analysis, the random-effects model was applied when heterogeneity measures indicated I² > 50% and P < .1; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Pooled effect values along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for each study, and results were visualized using forest plots. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Publication bias was evaluated using Begg and Egger tests. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Eligible studies and study characteristics

A total of 1065 records were initially identified through the search strategy. After removing 486 duplicate publications, 494 articles were excluded based on title and abstract screening. The remaining 85 articles underwent full-text retrieval and quality assessment, resulting in the final inclusion of 42 studies,[3–5,7–40] encompassing 8947 participants (minimally invasive surgery: 4003; open surgery: 4971). 3.2. Outcomes

3.2.1. Total LNN and LNS

Data on total LNN were reported in 31 studies.[3–5,7,8,10–13,15,17,20,21,23,25,27–33,37–39,41,43–45] Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more in total LNN (standard mean difference [SMD] −0.22; CI −0.28 to −0.16; I2 = 81.6%, P = .000) (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, for video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy (VATL) or robotic-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy (RATL) subgroup analysis, in VATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more in total LNN (SMD −0.27; CI −0.33 to −0.21; I2 = 80.5%, P = .000) (Fig. 2B). However, in the RATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy no difference in total LNN (SMD 0.12; CI −0.04 to 0.27; I2 = 16.1%, P = .133) (Fig. 2B).

Meta-analysis of thoracotomy versus MITL for in the lymph node dissection of lung cancer: (A) total LNN; (B) subgroup analysis of total LNN; (C) total LNS; (D) subgroup analysis of total LNS. LNN = lymph node number, LNS = lymph node stations, MITL = minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy.

We included 18 studies[7–9,11,12,14,15,18,23,26,27,29,31,35,39,41,42,45] about total LNS. Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more in total LNS (SMD −0.16; CI −0.21 to −0.11; I2 = 88.9%, P = .000) (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, for VATL or RATL subgroup analysis, in VATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more in total LNS (SMD −0.18; CI −0.23 to −0.12; I2 = 88.8%, P = .000) (Fig. 2D). However, in the RATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy no difference in total LNS (SMD 0.21; CI −0.04 to 0.47; I2 = 90.0%, P = .098) (Fig. 2D).

3.2.2. N1 LNN and N1 LNS

Data on N1 LNN were reported in 8 studies.[10,15,19,29,34,36,41] Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more N1 LNN (SMD −0.22; CI −0.32 to −0.12; I2 = 35.3%, P = .000) (Fig. 3A). We included 3 studies[15,19,29] about N1 LNS. Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more N1 LNS (SMD −0.20; CI −0.36 to −0.04; I2 = 51.1%, P = .016). Owing to high heterogeneity (I2 = 51.1%), sensitivity analysis was carried out by Stata 12.0. After removing the studies by Liu et al[19] as the sample that was “left out,” the pooled results did change substantially but the heterogeneity was significantly reduced (SMD −0.24; CI −0.41 to −0.07; I2 = 43.8%, P = .005) (Fig. 3B).

Meta-analysis of thoracotomy versus MITL for in the lymph node dissection of lung cancer: (A) N1 LNN; (B) N1 LNS. LNN = lymph node number, LNS = lymph node stations, MITL = minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy.

3.2.3. N2 LNN and N2 LNS

Data on N2 LNN were reported in 15 studies.[5,10,15,16,19,22,24,28,29,34,36,38,39,41,45] Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more N2 LNN (SMD −0.12; CI −0.20 to −0.03; I2 = 50.4%, P = .006) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, for VATL or RATL subgroup analysis, in VATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more in N2 LNN (SMD −0.18; CI −0.28 to −0.08; I2 = 43.1%, P = .000) (Fig. 4B). However, in the RATL subgroup analysis, patients who underwent thoracotomy no difference in N2 LNN (SMD 0.07; CI −0.09 to 0.24; I2 = 0%, P = .395) (Fig. 4B).

Meta-analysis of thoracotomy versus MITL for in the lymph node dissection of lung cancer: (A) N2 LNN; (B) subgroup analysis of N2 LNN; (C) N2 LNS. LNN = lymph node number, LNS = lymph node stations, MITL = minimally invasive thoracoscopic lobectomy.

We included 8 studies[3,9,15,16,29,39,45] about N2 LNS. Compared with MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy had more total LNS (SMD −0.17; CI −0.28 to −0.06; I2 = 87.3%, P = .003). Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis cannot reduce heterogeneity. Therefore, we choose random effect model results (SMD −0.16; CI −0.27 to −0.04; I2 = 87.5%, P = .008) (Fig. 4C).

3.3. Publication bias

We conducted publication bias on more than 15 included studies using Begg test. For total LNN, Begg test results revealed that t = 1.77, P = .088 in Figure S1a, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q572. For total LNS, Begg test results revealed that t = 0.71, P = .49 in Figure S1b, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q572. For N2 LNN, Begg test results revealed that t = 1.89, P = .082 in Figure S1c, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q572. There is no publication bias in the above.

4. Discussion

Lung cancer is one of the most malignant tumors that poses a significant threat to health and life.[1] Surgical treatment remains the preferred approach for NSCLC. Internationally, lobectomy combined with mediastinal lymph node dissection is regarded as the standard surgical procedure for NSCLC.[46,47] A critical factor influencing both staging and prognosis in lung cancer is the involvement of LN, particularly mediastinal LN. Some literature indicates that when the tumor exceeds 2 cm in size, there exists a certain degree of mediastinal LN metastasis.[48] The overall postoperative survival rates and tumor-free survival outcomes for patients are positively correlated with the number of LN assessments conducted during surgery.[49,50] Consequently, both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network treatment guidelines for NSCLC and recommendations from the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons advocate for systematic LN dissection during early-stage NSCLC surgeries, emphasizing that mediastinal LN dissection should encompass no fewer than 3 stations.[51,52] In comparison to traditional open thoracotomy, MITL aligns more closely with noninvasive characteristics and aesthetic requirements while offering advantages such as rapid recovery, reduced pain levels, and shorter hospitalization durations. However, whether thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery can achieve equivalent efficacy in LN dissection compared to thoracotomy remains a subject of debate.[3,4] Luo et al[21] suggest that MITL may provide an enhanced surgical field view which facilitates better separation of LN adjacent to blood vessels and trachea; it also allows for detection of smaller LN thereby achieving superior results in terms of LN dissection compared to thoracotomy. Conversely, Whitson et al[40] report indicates that both the quantity of dissected LN and positivity rates associated with MITL are significantly lower than those observed following thoracotomy. Other reports have indicated no significant differences in the number of LN dissections and the number of dissection stations between thoracoscopic surgery and thoracotomy, with both methods demonstrating equally accurate assessments of LN metastasis.[5,29,32]

This study analyzed a total of 42 articles encompassing 8947 patients. Compared to MITL, patients who underwent thoracotomy exhibited a greater total number of LN. In the subgroup analysis for VATL, those undergoing thoracotomy also had a higher total LN count. However, in the RATL subgroup analysis, there was no difference in total LNN among patients who underwent thoracotomy. Additionally, when compared to MITL, patients undergoing thoracotomy had more N1 LN and N2 LN overall. Specifically, within the VATL subgroup analysis, those who underwent thoracotomy showed an increased count of N2 LNS; however, this was not observed in the RATL subgroup analysis where no differences were noted regarding N2 LNN. In RATL procedures, surgeons possess enhanced capabilities for systematic LN dissection due to its wide 3-dimensional field of view and flexible wrist movements that allow for 7 degrees of freedom. The ergonomic control console further reduces operator fatigue while enhancing sensitivity during operations.[53]

Upon analyzing the included literature and data from this study, several limitations were identified: the research subjects primarily originated from Europe, America, and East Asia which may introduce certain selection biases; there is a limited body of literature on RATL, with the majority consisting of retrospective studies. The experimental design, data extraction, and procedural rigor in these studies are often lacking. Notably, only 4 randomized controlled trials have been conducted to date, which undermines the robustness of meta-analysis conclusions; in our data analysis, while we performed a sensitivity analysis on highly heterogeneous outcome indicators, some results did not elucidate the sources of their heterogeneity. Variability in perspectives among different surgeons regarding LN dissection techniques, varying stages of the learning curve, and differing levels of experience among pathologists mean that some included data from certain studies do not meet the fundamental criteria outlined by National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines for systematic LN dissection in lung cancer surgery. This variability affects the reliability of the reported results; there exists a significant risk of LN fragmentation during dissection procedures. Additionally, discrepancies between pathologists and variations in counting methods considerably influence statistical outcomes related to LN. This contributes to increased heterogeneity across included studies and may impact final results.

5. Conclusion

Thoracotomy is significantly superior to LN dissection than MITL for lung cancer. Subgroup analysis shows that thoracotomy is superior to VATL. However, there is no difference between thoracotomy and RATL in total LNN, total LNS, and N2 LNN. More high-quality literature is needed to be included in the research in the future.
