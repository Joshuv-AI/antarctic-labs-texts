The anti-inflammatory drug celecoxib is metabolized by Pseudomonas aeruginosa CYP107S1 in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Sylvie E. Kandel, Jed N. Lampe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The significance of the gut microbiome on drug metabolism has been demonstrated, yet much less is known about the pathobiome’s potential impact on systemic drug metabolism outside of the β-lactam antibiotics, especially for bacterial species prone to multidrug resistance, which often leads to acute or chronic infections. CYP107S1, a cytochrome P450 (P450) from the opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which exhibits substrate promiscuity and allosteric features, was able to tightly bind (Kd, app of 0.755 μM) and rapidly metabolize with high affinity (Km of 1.63 μM) the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug celecoxib. It formed the same hydroxy metabolite as human CYP2C9, the primary enzyme responsible for the metabolism of this selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor. In liquid cultures of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain expressing a relatively high level CYP107S1 during the initial bacterial growth phase, dosing of celecoxib resulted in an increase in the hydroxyl product formation over time, attesting to translation from the P450 in vitro recombinant drug-metabolizing activity to live bacterial cultures. Furthermore, the celecoxib metabolite formation by the CYP107S1 recombinant enzyme or in PAO1 culture was partially inhibited by the pan-CYP inhibitor 1-aminobenzotriazole and exhibited preincubation time-dependency characteristics. Thus, P. aeruginosa CYP107S1 capability to metabolize drugs continues to expand, driving new knowledge and potential for new useful substrate probes to study P450 function and regulation in P. aeruginosa.Significance StatementThis study provides further insights into the metabolic ability of CYP107S1, a cytochrome P450 enzyme belonging to the azetidine biosynthetic gene cluster of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is capable of metabolizing the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug celecoxib, further widening the promiscuity feature of the enzyme and offering a novel probe to study its regulation in the PAO1 strain of P. aeruginosa.

Introduction

The concept of the ability of the “pathobiome” to contribute to drug metabolism in the host is driving new scientific research to obtain a better understanding of the drug metabolic capability and adaptability of human pathogenic microorganisms, especially the multidrug-resistant pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, or Pseudomonas aeruginosa.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Drug metabolism at the site of infection can decrease drug concentration and therefore impact drug efficacy locally, which can lead to the emergence of new drug resistance traits in the pathogen. Metabolism of β-lactam antibiotics by bacterial β-lactamases is a prime example of this phenomenon, greatly affecting the emergence of bacterial antibiotic resistant strains.3,8,9 Coincidentally, our previous characterization of the P. aeruginosa cytochrome P450 (P450) CYP107S1 revealed a promiscuous drug-metabolizing enzyme behaving analogously to the human hepatic P450 drug-metabolizing and detoxification enzymes, like the well-known CYP3A4.10 CYP107S1 can metabolize drugs from structurally diverse classes, including the fluoroquinolone antibiotic ciprofloxacin, the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator potentiator ivacaftor, and the selective estrogen receptor modulator raloxifene. In fact, these compounds have been employed either as a primary treatment for bacterial infections or as a comedication to minimize the severity of the infection in humans or animal models.11, 12, 13, 14

During an infection, the inflammatory responses of the host are activated as a defense mechanism against the invading pathogen, and fever, pain, or swelling symptoms may arise.15 Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are often used to reduce inflammation and relieve undesirable symptoms, taken either under a prescription or over-the-counter.16,17 Although some controversy exists about the benefits of NSAID medications during an infection,18, 19, 20 several clinical trials have assessed the effects of anti-inflammatory agents (eg, ibuprofen) either by themselves or in combination with antibiotics for the treatment of uncomplicated urinary tract infections, and these have demonstrated a general improved response time, and occasionally, a lesser need for antibiotic treatment.21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Unfortunately, complications deriving from the NSAID treatment limited their systematic recommendations. Interestingly, multiple NSAIDs have previously shown inherent antimicrobial properties against Gram positive and Gram negative bacteria and fungi, with aspirin, celecoxib (CXB), diclofenac, and ibuprofen displaying in vitro antimicrobial activities in repetitive studies.26, 27, 28, 29 These 4 NSAIDs have also been reported for their antibiofilm activity, disrupting the quorum-sensing signaling pathway in methicillin-resistant S. aureus or P. aeruginosa.29, 30, 31, 32 In addition, CXB, by inhibiting bacterial efflux pumps, demonstrated synergistic effects with antibiotics, improving bacterial sensitivity.33

The susceptibility of the bacterial pathogens to NSAIDs is intriguing, questioning not only the inherent origin of the response but also whether some of these microorganisms have developed further metabolic mechanisms to address it. The PAO1 genome contains 3 P450 genes, CYP107S1 (PA3331), CYP168A1 (PA2475), and CYP169A1 (PA3679), with only CYP107S1 and CYP169A1 genes found in an operon structure format.34,35 Currently, very limited information exists about the endogenous functions of the P. aeruginosa P450 enzymes. The majority of the available data have been accumulated for the CYP107S1 gene, which belongs to the conserved aze biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC), reportedly under regulation of the acyl homoserine lactone (AHL)-mediated quorum-sensing mechanism. Additionally, the CYP168A1 gene has been identified as a fatty acid hydroxylase.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 Importantly, few studies have examined the P450 metabolism potential in P. aeruginosa PAO1 cultures with activity measurement requiring either overexpression of the operon genes through mutation or induction via AHLs or promoter engineering.36,37,39 Here, we evaluated 2 P. aeruginosa P450 enzymes, CYP107S1 and CYP168A1, for their ability to bind and metabolize one of these active antimicrobial NSAIDs, CXB. Furthermore, P. aeruginosa planktonic cultures of the laboratory strain PAO1 were dosed with CXB, and the metabolism was assessed after 2 and 20 hours. The pan-CYP inhibitor 1-aminobenzotriazole (1-ABT) was tested with the recombinant CYP107S1 enzyme and the PAO1 cultures to substantiate P450 involvement in the CXB hydroxylation reaction.41

Materials and methods

Experimental procedures

Materials and test systems

CXB (CAS: 169590-42-5), hydroxy celecoxib (CAS: 170571-00-3), celecoxib-d7 (CAS: 544686-21-7), and 1-ABT (CAS: 1614-12-6) were purchased from Cayman Chemical. D-Glucose-6-phosphate (CAS: 3671-99-6) and NADP+ (CAS: 4292-60-2) were obtained from Alfa Aesar. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (CAS: 9001-40-5) and spinach ferredoxin (Fdx, CAS: 9040-09-9) and ferredoxin reductase (FdR, CAS: 9029-33-8) were purchased from MilliporeSigma. All other chemicals were obtained from standard suppliers and were of reagent or analytical grade.

The P. aeruginosa P450 recombinant enzymes and bacterial strain originated from the following sources. The cDNA sequence, Escherichia coli expression, and Ni-NTA purification of the P. aeruginosa (His)-tagged CYP107S1 and CYP168A1 enzymes were previously described in Kandel et al,10 and Tooker et al,40 respectively. The P. aeruginosa (Schroeter) Migula PAO1 strain was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC BAA-47) (Manassas, VA).

Ligand Kd determination by optical difference spectroscopy

Binding of CXB to recombinant CYP107S1 or CYP168A1 was determined using UV-visible spectroscopy. The spectra were acquired on a Varian Cary 50 Bio UV-visible scanning spectrophotometer (Agilent) between 350 and 500 nm. CXB stock (4 mM) and working solutions were prepared in methanol. Titration of CXB was carried out using 1 μM CYP107S1 or 2 μM CYP168A1 sample prepared in 100 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, and methanol was used as a solvent control. After the acquisition of a baseline, aliquots of CXB were added to the sample cuvette, whereas an equal volume of methanol was added to the reference cuvette. Spectra were recorded at varying ligand concentrations, and the calculated difference spectrum was obtained by subtracting the reference spectrum to the corresponding ligand-bound protein spectrum as described in Mohamed et al.42 If binding of the ligand triggered characteristic shift in the Soret spectrum, the absolute changes in absorbance deriving from the triplicate titrations were measured, plotted as a function of ligand concentration and fitted to the best binding model using the GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.2).

Recombinant P450 CXB in vitro metabolic assays

Incubations of CXB (1 μM) with recombinant CYP107S1 or CYP168A1 (1 μM) were carried out in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and 3 mM MgCl2 using the spinach redox partners as follows: 1 μM P450, 10 μM Fdx, and 0.1 U/mL FdR. CXB was dissolved in methanol or DMSO with a maximum of 0.2% (v/v) solvent in the final incubation reactions. After an equilibration at 37 °C for 3 minutes, all reactions (200 μL final volume) were initiated by the addition of the NADPH-regeneration system mix consisting of NADP+ (1 mM), D-glucose-6-phosphate (10 mM), and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (2 IU/mL). The reactions were incubated for up to 20 minutes at 37 °C under gentle agitation and stopped by the addition of ice-cold methanol containing 40 to 60 ng/mL celecoxib-d7 as internal standard. Incubations without the NADPH-regenerating system mix served as negative controls. Precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation of the stopped samples for 20 minutes at 2500g and 4 °C. Supernatants were transferred to high-performance liquid chromatography vials for metabolite formation analysis by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

For the CYP107S1 kinetic assessment, similar assay conditions were followed with concentrations of CXB ranging from 0.1 to 5 μM and up to 8 minutes of incubation time. The hydroxy celecoxib metabolite was quantified based on a calibration curve prepared in matrix and ranging from 0.005 to 2.5 μM, including matrix blank and standard zero samples. The mean metabolite formation rates were obtained from triplicate determinations and best fitted the Michaelis-Menten model (GraphPad Prism software, version 10.4.2).

For the inhibition assays, the pan-CYP inhibitor 1-ABT,41 prepared in DMSO, was incubated at 1 mM for up to 8 minutes with CYP107S1 (1 μM) and CXB (1 μM) under the same incubation conditions described above. Furthermore, to assess the mechanism-based inactivation potential of 1-ABT for P. aeruginosa CYP107S1, a 20-minute preincubation step in the presence of the NADPH-regenerating system mix was included. CXB was added after the preincubation step, and the substrate-P450-inhibitor mixtures were incubated for a total of 8 minutes. After protein precipitation, the methanol-stopped supernatant samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS for the formation of the hydroxy celecoxib metabolite.

P. aeruginosa PAO1 planktonic cultures

One isolated colony of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain grown on Luria broth agar was used to inoculate a 50 mL starting culture in Luria broth medium. After overnight growth at 37 °C and 250 rpm, an aliquot of 0.25 mL of the PAO1 starting culture was used to inoculate 50 mL of M9 minimal medium supplemented with 0.4% glucose. A total of 6 cultures in M9 minimal medium with 0.4% glucose were prepared. The optical density (OD) of the PAO1 cultures was measured at 600 nm wavelength on a Shimadzu BioSpec-mini spectrophotometer. When the OD600nm reached 0.3 to 0.4, after about 6 hours of growth time at 37 °C and 250 rpm, CXB (25 μM) was dosed in all PAO1 planktonic cultures. Among those, 3 cultures were further treated with 1 mM 1-ABT, a P450 pan-inhibitor.41 The remaining cultures received an equal volume of DMSO (remaining under 0.2% v/v). Aliquots of 0.75 mL from the different PAO1 cultures were taken at 8 and 26 hours of growth, corresponding to 2 and 20 hours of CXB dosing time points, respectively. Proteins were precipitated by the addition of 1 mL of methanol. After thorough mixing, samples were transferred to ice and spiked with 10 μL of 12 μg/mL celecoxib-d7 (prepared in methanol). Following a centrifugation step of 20 minutes at 3000g and 4 °C, the supernatants were collected in high-performance liquid chromatography vials and analyzed for hydroxy celecoxib formation by LC-MS/MS.

In parallel, 2 mL aliquots were collected at the 5-, 6-, 8-, and 10-hour time points for RNA extraction using the RNAprotect Bacteria reagent and the RNeasy kit from Qiagen. The aliquots at the 6-hour time point were collected before the addition of CXB. Manufacturer protocols were followed, with the inclusion of the DNase step. The RNA was eluted in nuclease-free water, and concentration and purity were determined based on the absorbance at 260 and 280 nm using a NanoQuant plate with the Tecan Infinite M Plex plate reader. The cDNA was prepared from purified RNA (800 ng) using the iScript Reverse Transcriptase Supermix kit from BioRad and following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed in technical duplicate on a BioRad CFX96 instrument with specific qPCR primer sets for CYP107S1, CYP168A1, and CYP169A1 genes and including the rpoD gene as reference (Supplemental Table 1). Each primer set was previously validated for qPCR efficiency. Gene expression was normalized to the rpoD gene, and the 2(–ΔCq) method was used to determine the relative P. aeruginosa P450 gene expression in planktonic cultures.43,44

LC-MS method for CXB metabolism study

CXB samples from recombinant P450 incubations and planktonic cultures were analyzed by LC-MS/MS with a Waters Acquity ultra-performance liquid chromatography system interfaced by electrospray ionization with a Waters Xevo TQ-S micro tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS) (Waters Corp). The MS source parameters were as follows: 1.0 kV for capillary voltage, 150 °C for source temperature, 500 °C for desolvation temperature, and 900 L/h for desolvation gas flow. Negative ionization was employed for multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) and product ion scan modes. The following mass transitions, including cone voltage (CV) and collision energy (CE), were used for the MRM scan mode: 380 >316 (CV = –50 V, CE = 20 V) for CXB, 396 >302 (CV = –75 V; CE = 20 V) for hydroxy celecoxib, 387 >323 (CV = –50 V, CE = 20 V) for celecoxib-d7. The analytes were separated on a Waters Acquity BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) by flowing water and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid at 0.4 mL/min and using the following gradient: 5% organic held for 0.5 minutes, increased to 98% over 2.5 minutes, held at 98% for 0.9 minutes. For product ion scans, CE was optimized to maximize ion fragmentation. The MS peaks were integrated using QuanLynx software (version 4.1, Waters Corp), and the analyte/internal standard peak area ratios were used for relative quantification. For determination of the hydroxy celecoxib concentration, the regression fit was based on the analyte/internal standard peak area ratios calculated from the calibration standards, and the analyte concentration in the incubations was back-calculated using the weighted (1/x) linear least-squares regression.

CXB molecular docking to CYP107S1

The CYP107S1 homology model previously developed by Kandel et al10 was used to help understand the molecular interactions between CYP107S1 and the NSAID CXB. The CXB 3D structure, CID 2662, was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used for all subsequent docking studies. For molecular docking, the procedure described in Kandel et al10 was followed using the same docking grid parameters, except for the exhaustiveness being set to 20. Docking results were visualized by using the ViewDock function on the UCSF Chimera interface (version 1.15). Hydrogen bonds were identified using the “Add H-Bonds” function, and pseudo-bonds were identified using the “Find Contacts” function.

Results

The anti-inflammatory drug CXB is a substrate of CYP107S1

The purified CYP107S1 recombinant enzyme from the P. aeruginosa PAO1 laboratory reference strain, characterized by Kandel et al,10 was assessed for its ability to bind the NSAID CXB. Spectral titrations with CXB gave rise to a type I spectral shift of the P450 Soret band (Fig. 1, inset). The maxima and minima of the calculated difference binding spectra were used to plot the binding isotherm and determine the apparent dissociation constant (Kd, app) (Fig. 1). From the statistical Akaike information criterion (AIC) method, the best fit of the binding isotherm was obtained with the Hill equation (the difference in AICc was positive with a value of 20.3 between the one site-specific binding, the simpler model, and the specific binding with Hill slope, the alternative model). CXB bound to CYP107S1 with a submicromolar Kd, app of 0.755 μM, and a nHill coefficient of 1.5, further supporting the concept of a large and flexible active site with positive cooperative features. Conversely, titration of CXB with purified CYP168A1 recombinant enzyme, 1 of the 3 P. aeruginosa PAO1 P450 isoforms cloned and expressed in E. coli,40 generated a very limited and unquantifiable change in the Soret band (data not shown).Fig. 1Binding isotherm of celecoxib for CYP107S1 with representative difference spectra. To ascertain that CXB may be a P450 substrate for the P. aeruginosa enzymes, particularly CYP107S1, in vitro metabolism studies were carried out with the CYP107S1 and CYP168A1 recombinant enzymes. For CYP168A1, no hydroxy derivative was detected after 20-minute incubation of CXB in the presence of the NADPH cofactor (data not shown), and therefore further reinforcing the negative binding results. However, for CYP107S1, CXB incubations led to the formation of one NADPH-dependent hydroxy metabolite (Fig. 2). Molecular docking of CXB to CYP107S1

The interaction of CXB with CYP107S1 was further explored through molecular docking. The CYP107S1 homology model developed by Kandel et al10 was used for the docking study, and the most representative pose was selected based on the proximity of the phenyl methyl moiety near the heme iron to enable hydroxylation. Although the energetically most favorable pose scored –9.6 kcal/mol, it directed the hydrophobic trifluoromethyl group of CXB toward the heme. The optimal positioning for the phenyl methyl for attack was only found in the 19th ranked pose with an energy score of –7.5 kcal/mol and the methyl moiety positioned at about 6.62 Å above the heme iron (Fig. 3). In this model, the amine of the sulfonamide group is predicted to form a hydrogen bond with H87, a histidine residue that belongs to the B-C loop region. Several other pseudo-bonds were predicted between CXB and the following CYP107S1 residues: M287, L288, F387, L388, and I385. Kinetics of CXB hydroxylation by recombinant CYP107S1 and inhibition by 1-ABT

To further explore the kinetic characteristics of CXB hydroxylation by CYP107S1, steady-state kinetic experiments were carried out using the spinach redox partners Fdx and FdR. The Michaelis-Menten model, representing the simplest model, fit best according to the statistical AIC analysis (Fig. 4A). From the fit, CYP107S1 exhibited a Km of 1.63 μM and a Vmax of 51.7 pmol/min/nmol P450 for CXB. Interestingly, the CXB dissociation and affinity constants for CYP107S1 remained very close in the concentration range. Concurrent to our kinetic assessment, inhibition of the recombinant CYP107S1 by the nonspecific P450 inhibitor 1-ABT, known to be a mechanism-based inactivator (MBI) for P450 enzymes, was evaluated using CXB as substrate and with 0- and 20-minute preincubation (Fig. 4B). Significant inhibition of CYP107S1 was observed in the presence of 1 mM 1-ABT. P. aeruginosa P450 gene expression in planktonic PAO1 cultures

To better understand the metabolic capability of P. aeruginosa to hydroxylate the anti-inflammatory drug CXB, the laboratory reference strain PAO1 was grown as planktonic cultures in M9 minimal medium supplemented with 0.4% glucose as the sole source of carbon and spiked after 6 hours of growth with either CXB (25 μM) or CXB (25 μM) with 1-ABT (1 mM). The OD of the cultures was measured at 600 nm up to 10 hours of growth (Fig. 5A), and aliquots of the bacterial cultures spiked with only CXB were taken at 5, 6, 8, and 10 hours for P450 gene expression analysis. Overall, both growth curves showed high variability in their OD measurements starting at the 6-hour timepoint (before any analyte being spiked), thus limiting further interpretation on the effect of CXB or 1-ABT on bacterial growth. The expression level for the CYP107S1, CYP168A1, and CYP169A1 genes was assessed based on the 2(–ΔCq) method and using the rpoD gene encoding for the sigma 70 factor of the RNA polymerase as the housekeeping gene (Fig. 5B).43 CYP107S1 was expressed in the early exponential phase of the planktonic culture with a substantial and transient increase at the 6-hour time point whereas the other 2 P450 genes showed relatively limited expression level. Although aliquots at the 5- and 6-hour timepoints were taken before spiking CXB and aliquots at the 8- and 10-hour timepoints were taken after spiking the anti-inflammatory drug, the decrease in CYP107S1 expression level at the 8-hour timepoint is not related to CXB dosing. CXB metabolism in PAO1 cultures and partial inhibition by 1-ABT

The PAO1 planktonic cultures were further analyzed by LC-MS for CXB and formation of the hydroxy celecoxib metabolite (Fig. 6). The extraction procedure of the bacterial culture was simple, with an organic solvent addition for stopping the reaction, a centrifugation step for protein precipitation, followed by direct LC-MS injection of the resulting supernatant. After 2-hour incubation of CXB in P. aeruginosa PAO1 culture, a low intensity peak was detected at the retention time of the hydroxy celecoxib standard. The signal intensity of hydroxy celecoxib rose 2.6-fold after an incubation time of 20 hours. To ascertain that formation of hydroxy celecoxib in PAO1 was CYP107S1-dependent, 1 mM of the 1-ABT inactivator was added to the bacterial cultures at the same time as the CXB spike. At 2 hours, about 38% inhibition of the hydroxy celecoxib formed in bacterial culture was determined (Fig. 6D). A similar percent inhibition was calculated at the 20-hour incubation time point. Discussion

BGCs in P. aeruginosa contribute to the bacterial adaptability and persistence to challenging environments and to the pathogenicity during opportunistic infections in humans.46,47 CYP107S1, also referred to as AzeF, belongs to one of these conserved P. aeruginosa BGCs, involved in the biosynthesis of secondary azetidine metabolites via a multimodular nonribosomal peptide synthetase complex.36,37,39 In addition, the hydroxy derivative, azetidomonamide A, formed by CYP107S1, acts as the main mediator for the reported stimulatory effect on bacterial biofilm formation and virulence factor production.39 Meanwhile, CYP107S1 demonstrates substrate promiscuity for multiple drug classes.10 It is somewhat surprising that a bacterial enzyme involved in the production of specialized bioactive metabolites that require a high amount of energy for production exhibits this type of catalytic versatility. This arguably echoes the promiscuous hepatic drug-metabolizing P450 enzymes in humans, responsible for phase I drug metabolism reactions but also involved in hepatic hormone homeostasis of the steroid (eg, testosterone, bile acids) and retinoic acid signaling pathways.48, 49, 50, 51, 52 This suggests that the promiscuous behavior of this bacterial P450 enzyme may confer a similar function as the human drug-metabolizing enzymes, ie, xenobiotic detoxification, empowering this unique metabolic versatility to drive antibiotic drug resistance. It is interesting to note that P. aeruginosa is not an obligate bacterial pathogen, but rather an opportunistic one. It can live freely in the soil or water in the absence of a plant or animal host. In these environments, it is likely to be exposed to antibiotics and other toxic secondary metabolites produced by bacterial and fungal competitors that must be dispatched for the organism to survive and maintain genetic fitness. In this context, it is reasonable to assume that the P450s of P. aeruginosa PAO1 may have, over time, developed the ability to metabolize novel environmental toxicants in order to improve the organism’s fitness for its environment. This is a direct analogy to the xenobiotic detoxification abilities of P450s expressed in the mammalian liver and intestine.

In this study, we demonstrated the capability of CYP107S1 to tightly bind, at submicromolar concentration (Fig. 1), and readily metabolize the NSAID CXB (Fig. 4A). The hydroxylation of CXB was specific to CYP107S1, since no binding or metabolism was observed with the P. aeruginosa CYP168A1 recombinant enzyme.40 CYP107S1 attacked the phenyl methyl moiety of CXB similarly to the human hepatic CYP2C9 enzyme, forming an identical hydroxy product (Fig. 2).45 Furthermore, we used molecular docking to predict the CXB conformation within the CYP107S1 active site and identify the residues important for the formation of a productive CXB-CYP107S1 complex. Compared to ciprofloxacin, ivacaftor, or raloxifene, other known substrates of CYP107S1,10 the optimal pose for CXB oxidation at the phenyl methyl moiety positioned the NSAID in an extended conformation alongside the I helix (Fig. 3). With residue H87 anchoring the molecule at its sulfonamide group via a hydrogen bond, the phenyl methyl is hovering perpendicularly to the heme iron at 6.62 Å, conceivably close enough for oxidation by the activated iron oxime species. In addition, residue M287 is repetitively predicted as an interacting residue for all four drugs docked in the CYP107S1 active site,10 its hydrophobic feature probably strongly influencing ligand fit and orientation in the active site.53 The 2 amino acid residues, L288 and F387, were also projected to interact with raloxifene, a compound rapidly metabolized by CYP107S1 according to its UV metabolic profile.10 Interestingly, the optimal selected pose for CXB productive attack at the phenyl methyl did not have the most favorable binding energy, with a score of –7.5 kcal/mol, but nearly matched the energy score obtained for ciprofloxacin (–7.6 kcal/mol), a low-affinity ligand for CYP107S1 with a Kd, app of 52 μM.10 Thus, relying solely on computational docking energy scores to predict ligand binding affinity to P450 enzymes could be misleading, probably due in part to the lack of dynamics represented in the molecular docking algorithms. The high affinity, turnover rate, and specificity for CYP107S1 portray CXB as an ideal probe substrate,54 with a complementary simple metabolic pathway and commercial availability of the metabolite. In human drug metabolism studies, the availability of a specific probe substrate for a P450 drug-metabolizing enzyme allows the assessment of enzyme induction or inhibition in a complex cellular test system.55 The identification of CXB as a probe substrate for CYP107S1 provides a new biochemical tool to further examine CYP107S1 function and regulation in P. aeruginosa.

High recombinant enzyme metabolic activity is not consistently associated with measurable activity in bacterial cultures. Difficulty in the substrate entry and crossing of the bacterial wall or the gene expression level and profile of the enzyme responsible for the metabolic activity are examples of potential challenges that can be encountered with live cultures. Hence, being able to measure metabolic P450 activity in PAO1 cultures first required us to understand the expression profiles of the P. aeruginosa P450 enzymes, and more specifically, that of CYP107S1. In general, bacterial gene expression is best assessed in minimal medium with a controlled carbon source and a simple matrix type, and preferably, with sampling done during the exponential growth phase. Rarely is the gene expression profile done over time during the logarithmic stage; more often, a single timepoint or a single OD (eg, OD600nm of 0.6) is chosen for sampling, and the change in expression level is measured and compared between solvent control and treatment. Our initial characterization of P. aeruginosa P450 gene expression proved to be very informative (Fig. 5), highlighting a significant regulatory increase in CYP107S1 expression level early in the exponential phase, followed by a rapid return to basal expression level at 8 hours. This increase in expression level was significant and reproducible (Supplemental Fig. 1) and happened before any test chemical dosing. However, control of this transient escalation in expression is yet to be determined. When considered as part of the entire gene cluster, we surmise that an increase in CYP107S1 expression suggests an increase in gene expression for the entire aze BGC operon (PA3327 to PA3334), which has been reported to be upregulated by the quorum-sensing AHLs.37,38 Effectively, N-(3-oxododecanoyl)-L-homoserine lactone (3-o-C12-HSL) has been measured during the early logarithmic phase; however, its level remained constant during the late exponential and stationary phases,56 somewhat conflicting with the prompt return to basal expression level of CYP107S1. At last, the relative gene expression level of CYP107S1 gave us further confidence in the feasibility of its metabolic activity measurement in PAO1 planktonic cultures.

At 6 hours of growth, when the CYP107S1 gene expression level is at its highest, CXB was added at 25 μM to PAO1 cultures. Note that the CXB concentration used here is below the minimum inhibitory concentration (∼42 μM) reported for a P. aeruginosa clinical isolate when treated in conjunction with a subinhibitory concentration of colistin to facilitate drug entry.57 Using a simple sample processing method, hydroxy celecoxib was detected at 2 hours and its signal intensity further increased after 20 hours incubation (Fig. 6). The early detection of the hydroxy metabolite positively substantiates the involvement of CYP107S1, whereas the increase in metabolite formation at the later timepoint is more perplexing and requires a better understanding of the stability of the CYP107S1 protein in bacterial cells, the possible change in CYP107S1 gene expression at the stationary phase, or the processes allowing CXB entry. Finally, CYP107S1’s contribution in CXB hydroxylation by the recombinant enzyme (Fig. 4B) or upon dosing of PAO1 cultures (Fig. 6D) was validated with the nonspecific P450 MBI, 1-ABT. The slight increase in inhibition observed after the 20-minute preincubation further supported the time-dependency associated with a P450 MBI like 1-ABT (Fig. 4B). Unfortunately, as for the human CYP2C9 enzyme,58 1-ABT was not a strong inactivator of CYP107S1, potentially due to low affinity or slow metabolic rate. The inhibitor 1-ABT, with a molecular weight of 134 g/mol, finds a large active site to occupy within CYP107S1 (calculated volume of 1526 Å3).10 In addition, CYP107S1 has demonstrated a slow metabolic rate for certain substrates (eg, ivacaftor; data not shown). Partial P450 inhibition is certainly not preferential; hence, the search for specific and selective inhibitors of CYP107S1 and other P. aeruginosa P450 enzymes is currently underway and a subject of active investigation in our laboratory.

Our investigation of CXB metabolism by CYP107S1 opens queries for other NSAIDs and their interactions with bacterial P450 enzymes. Although CXB has not been shown to directly impact biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa and only appeared to have antibacterial effects on Gram negative bacteria when combined with antibiotic agents,57 its tight interaction with CYP107S1 is intriguing in the sense that the CYP107S1 hydroxy azetidine derivative mediates production of biofilm and other virulence factors. The potential for CXB to inhibit CYP107S1 and impact the formation of the active hydroxy azetidine product remains to be determined. Thus, continuing to study the intricacy of P450 involvement in the metabolic and virulent pathways of these resistant bacterial pathogens has the possibility to provide new avenues for the treatment of acute and chronic bacterial infections.
