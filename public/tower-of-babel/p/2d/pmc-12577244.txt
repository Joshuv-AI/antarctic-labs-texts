Risk factors for perinatal intimate partner violence: a global systematic review and meta-analyses
Authors: Le Chen, Norhasmah Mohd Zain, Qin Qin, Raishan Shafini Bakar, Pan Li, Afang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerinatal intimate partner violence (IPV) is highly prevalent in different regions worldwide. However, there is still insufficient systematic evidence on the risk factors for perinatal IPV across various regions and socioeconomic levels, which presents challenges in developing targeted prevention programs. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analyses was to identify the risk factors for perinatal IPV across various regions and socioeconomic levels worldwide.MethodsA comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science, encompassing all available studies from each database’s inception date to June 2024. Potential risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists. Subgroup analyses were also performed by region and level of economic development.ResultsA total of 83 studies were included in the review, involving 1,609,201 participants, of whom 90,479 (5.6%) experienced perinatal IPV. For the type of violence, 64 studies reported overall IPV, 21 reported physical violence, 12 reported psychological violence, and 10 reported sexual violence. The meta-analyses showed that major risk factors for these types of violence included woman’s education level, woman’s working status, pregnancy intention, social or family support, partner’s education level, and partner’s alcohol consumption. However, some factors varied across different regions and levels of economic development, suggesting contextual influences on risk factors for perinatal IPV.ConclusionsThis is a comprehensive review on the risk factors for perinatal IPV across regions with varying levels of economic development. These factors vary across different regions and economic contexts. In high-income countries, IPV is more commonly associated with mental health status and addictive behaviors, while in middle- and low-income countries, it is primarily associated with low education levels, economic dependence, and partner’s risk behaviors. This finding highlights the necessity of fully considering contextual factors in future risk assessments for perinatal IPV and establishing corresponding prevention and intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04078-3.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is defined as ongoing or past behaviors/acts by an intimate partner or ex-partner, such as a spouse, cohabiting partner or lover, that cause(s) physical, psychological/emotional or sexual harm(s) [1]. It is estimated that 30% of women have experienced IPV worldwide in 2018. In particular, Globally, an estimated 852 million women aged 15 years and older (nearly one-third of the adult female population) have experienced at least one form of violence in their lifetime [2].

Perinatal IPV has emerged as a critical public health concern in recent years and garnered widespread attention. Given that IPV measurement or screening tools typically assess violence occurring over the past 12 months or across the lifetime [3], perinatal IPV in this study is operationally defined as violence occurring from one year prior to conception, during pregnancy, and up to one year after delivery, to be consistent with previous studies [4, 5]. In this review, perinatal IPV is categorized into four types: (1) overall IPV, defined as any type of IPV [6]; (2) physical violence; (3) psychological or emotional abuse; and (4) sexual violence.

A study by Román-Gálvez et al. [7] reported that the global prevalence of IPV during pregnancy is 25.0%. However, evidence on the prevalence of IPV across the full perinatal period remains limited. Perinatal IPV not only causes serious harm to pregnant women, such as uterine rupture and hemorrhage, but also leads to adverse mental health outcomes, including postpartum depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and suicidal ideation. In addition, it has detrimental effects on fetus and child, including preterm birth, low birth weight, stillbirth, and an increased risk of physical, mental, and behavioral problems during childhood [5, 8]. Furthermore, perinatal IPV is associated with reduced utilization of maternal health care services. For example, women exposed to IPV have 25% lower odds of receiving adequate antenatal care and 20% lower odds of utilizing skilled delivery care [9]. Emerging evidence also suggests that IPV is associated with reduced breastfeeding intention, delayed initiation of breastfeeding, and early termination of exclusive breastfeeding during the first six postnatal months [10]. Therefore, some researchers have explored the risk populations and risk factors for perinatal IPV to develop targeted prevention strategies.

Although existing reviews have reported the epidemiological characteristics of IPV during pregnancy, such as the review by Román-Gálvez et al. (2021) [7], which focused on the prevalence of different types of IPV, and the review by Hahn et al. (2018) [11], which focused on health consequences, none of these reviews have systematically integrated the risk factors for perinatal IPV. Furthermore, although reviews conducted in specific regions (e.g., Turkey [12], Ethiopia [13, 14]) or under particular circumstances (e.g., COVID-19 pandemic [15]) have reported findings related to risk factors, they primarily focus on IPV risk factors faced by women during pregnancy and are limited in the range of study.

The level of economic development significantly affects both the incidence and characteristics of IPV, and the factors associated with IPV vary across different economic contexts [16, 17]. Although the aforementioned studies have made significant contributions to raising awareness of perinatal IPV and its harms, there is currently a lack of systematic reviews and meta-analyses that comprehensively explore the risk factors for perinatal IPV globally, as stratified by economic context. In this review, risk factors are defined as characteristics or variables that increase the likelihood of experiencing IPV during the perinatal period. This study aimed to fill this gap by systematically identifying and analyzing the risk factors for perinatal IPV across various regions and socioeconomic levels worldwide, to provide evidence-based guidance for screening high-risk populations and formulating targeted prevention programs.

Study registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [18] and was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024554107).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science, encompassing all available studies from each database’s inception date to February 26, 2024, using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree, and free-text terms, without geographical restrictions. A supplementary search was subsequently conducted in June 2024 to ensure that no newly published studies were missed. The detailed search strategies are presented in Table S1.

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNoteX9 to remove duplicates. Titles and abstracts of the articles were screened to preliminarily identify eligible studies, and then full-text articles were thoroughly reviewed to confirm eligibility for inclusion.

Data were extracted into a customized standard data extraction spreadsheet, including the title, PubMed ID (PMID) or Digital Object Identifier (DOI), first author, publication year, study design, country of authors, number of observations, number of persons exposed to violence, country of study, region, economic level, sample source, sampling time, IPV type, IPV assessment, IPV duration, age, and statistical methods.

Literature screening and data extraction were independently performed and cross-checked by two researchers (LC, QQ). In case of inconsistency, a third researcher (NMZ) would be consulted.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies was evaluated and cross-checked by two researchers (LC, QQ) using the corresponding JBI critical appraisal checklists [19]. Literature screening was conducted independently by two researchers, followed by a cross-checking performed upon completion. In case of inconsistency, a third researcher (NMZ) would be consulted. The reliability of the quality assessments performed by the two researchers was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC is a reliability index that reflects both the degree of correlation and agreement between measurements, and it is calculated from analysis of variance (ANOVA) mean squares as the ratio of between-group variance to total variance [20]. The questions contained in the JBI checklists (eight for cross-sectional, ten for case-control, and eleven for cohort studies) are shown in Supplementary Tables S3a–S3c. Each question should be answered as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear” or “Not applicable”.

Synthesis methods

The meta-analyses were performed using R 4.4.1. Effect size was presented as odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI%95). The inter-study heterogeneity was evaluated using the Q test, and quantified by the I2 statistic. If I2 < 50%, a fixed effects model was employed for meta-analyses; otherwise, a random effects model was used. The subgroup analyses were stratified by geographic region and level of economic development, to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Specifically, the economic development status of a country was further categorized as high-income, upper-middle-income, lower-middle-income, and low-income according to the World Bank income categories [21]. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and the Egger’s test for risk factors included in more than ten studies. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

Of the 5,001 initially retrieved articles, 1,481 were removed due to duplication, and 3,397 were excluded due to their irrelevance. Following full-text review of the remaining 123 articles, we excluded the following studies based on the predefined criteria: (1) One study with ambiguous IPV definition, (2) Two non-peer-reviewed conference abstracts, (3) Twelve studies with indistinguishable gestational and non-gestational IPV, (4) Twenty-two studies with only univariate analyses performed or multivariate analyses performed but without proper adjustment for confounding factors, and (5) Three studies with potentially questionable outcome data from multivariate analyses. After this rigorous screening process, 83 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 83 included studies were published between 2000 and 2023, including 75 cross-sectional studies [6, 22–95], 1 case-control studies [96], and 7 cohort studies [97–103]. These studies were geographically distributed across several continents, with 37 conducted in Asia [24, 26, 28, 33, 36, 38, 43, 47, 48, 50, 52–54, 56–60, 64, 65, 70–73, 77–80, 82–85, 91, 94–96, 103], 31 in Africa [6, 22, 23, 25, 27, 29, 31, 32, 34, 39, 42, 44–46, 49, 51, 55, 61–63, 66, 69, 74–76, 81, 86–88, 90, 93], 12 in North America [35, 37, 40, 41, 68, 89, 92, 97, 99–102], 1 in South America [30], 1 in Europe [98], and 1 in Oceania [67]. The retrieved studies originated from 30 countries. According to the World Bank classification [21], 18 studies were from high-income countries [24, 35, 37, 40, 41, 57, 59, 60, 65, 68, 89, 92, 96–100, 102], 19 from upper-middle-income countries [23, 25, 26, 28, 30, 33, 36, 47, 54, 56, 58, 64, 66, 76, 82, 83, 94, 95, 101], 31 from lower-middle-income countries [27, 34, 38, 42, 43, 48–53, 63, 67, 70–78, 80, 84–88, 90, 91, 103], and 15 from low-income countries [6, 22, 29, 31, 32, 39, 44–46, 55, 61, 62, 69, 81, 93]. Of the 1,609,201 participants involved, approximately 90,479 experienced violence. Sixty-four studies reported overall IPV [6, 22, 25–29, 31, 33–36, 38, 40–42, 44, 45, 48, 49, 51–55, 57–62, 64, 65, 67–80, 82–89, 91, 93, 95–98, 100–102], 21 studies physical violence [6, 23, 24, 37, 39, 46, 47, 50, 54, 56, 64, 66, 76, 81, 86, 90, 91, 94, 99, 102, 103], 12 studies psychological violence [6, 24, 30, 50, 54, 56, 63, 64, 76, 81, 86, 99], and 10 studies sexual violence [6, 32, 50, 54, 56, 64, 76, 81, 86, 103]. The overall characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

IPV assessment tools

A variety of tools were used to assess IPV in these 83 included studies. The most frequently used assessment instruments included: the WHO Multi-Country Study Questionnaire (used in 19 studies), the WHO Violence Against Women Questionnaire (5 studies), and two other WHO-derived tools; the Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS) and its revised version CTS2 (11 studies), and the Abuse Assessment Screen (AAS) (6 studies). Other commonly used standardized tools included the Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream (HITS) scale (4 studies), the Violence Against Women Screen (VAWS) (3 studies), NorVold Abuse Questionnaire (NorAQ) (2 studies), and the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) Questionnaire (2 studies). Furthermore, 5 studies employed the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Domestic Violence Module, a validated instrument specifically designed for national population-based surveys. A total of 20 studies employed study-specific measurements, including self-developed tools or instruments adapted from existing questionnaires. No IPV assessment tool was clearly reported in 4 studies. Details of the IPV assessment tools used are presented by study in Supplementary Table S2.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the 75 included cross-sectional studies was assessed using the JBI Checklist for Analytical Cross Sectional Studies. All included studies (1) explicitly specified participant inclusion/exclusion criteria, and (2) comprehensively described key methodological characteristics including participant recruitment sources, study settings, and study periods. Exposure factors were systematically measured using validated structured survey instruments, ensuring methodological rigor in data collection and reliability of the data. The majority of studies employed standardized tools to identify IPV; three studies failed to clearly specify their IPV assessment methods and tools. All included studies systematically identified potential confounding factors, and appropriately adjusted these factors through multivariable regression analyses, which was a reasonable approach to control potential confounding factors and ensure sound analytical methodology. Most studies showed robust performance in outcome measurement; however, nine studies did not clearly report how IPV outcomes were assessed, and seven studies failed to measure outcomes in a valid and reliable manner. All studies employed multivariate logistic regression analyses to ensure methodological rigor in statistical approaches. Based on the standardized quality assessment criteria, the cross-sectional studies exhibited consistently moderate-to-high methodological quality, with summary scores ranging from 13 to 16 points (proportion: 0.81–1.0.81.0). The risk of bias assessment is detailed in Supplementary Table S3a.

The quality score was 18 for the case-control study (proportion: 0.90) and ranged from 13 to 19 (proportion: 0.59–0.86) across the seven cohort studies. The subjects and exposure measurements were clearly described in most studies, and the overall outcome assessment was appropriate and reliable. However, most studies did not report whether participants were free of the outcome at study initiation. Additionally, some studies had inadequate follow-up time, incomplete follow-up, or failed to report the reasons for loss to follow-up or the strategies to address it. Several studies did not assess the validity and reliability of the outcome measures. The assessment results are detailed in Supplementary Tables S3b-S3c. The reliability of the quality assessments performed by the two researchers was confirmed using ICC, which showed excellent agreement (ICC = 0.901, 95% CI: 0.846–0.936).

Publication bias evaluation

Publication bias was evaluated for 17 risk factors. Both funnel plot and Egger’s test indicated no publication bias in women’s education level (p = 0.690), marital status (p = 0.720), woman’s age > 30 (overall IPV: p = 0.735; physical violence: p = 0.345), woman’s age < 20 (p = 0.056), residence (p = 0.459), pregnancy intention (p = 0.065), parity (p = 0.733), partner’s education level (p = 0.747), partner’s alcohol consumption (overall IPV: p = 0.293; physical violence: p = 0.695), partner’s working status (p = 0.087), and partner’s age (p = 0.107) (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Our review examined the risk factors for overall IPV, as well as physical, psychological, and sexual violence, by performing meta-analyses on data from 83 studies across six continents with varying social backgrounds and levels of economic development, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Our analyses identified a wide range of risk factors for perinatal IPV, with variations in regions and levels of economic development.

Risk factors for overall IPV

This study has identified multiple risk factors associated with perinatal IPV, covering four dimensions: demographic characteristics, health and reproductive characteristics, social and relationship factors, and partner characteristics.

In terms of demographic characteristics, women’s economic disadvantage and lower education level emerged as major predictors, consistent with previous studies [12, 104–111]. Marital status further increases IPV risk, with married women reporting lower rates compared to single or separated women [110, 112, 113], possibly due to greater social stigma and lack of support during pregnancy [114]. Overall, these demographic risk factors reveal that perinatal women are more likely to be exposed to IPV under conditions of economic dependency, insufficient social support, and marital instability. This suggests that related interventions, prevention, and screening strategies should be designed to prioritize this high-risk group.

Regarding health and reproductive characteristics, previous experiences of violence were the strongest predictor for IPV, as the cyclical nature of abuse makes previously abused women particularly vulnerable during pregnancy [115, 116]. Other risk factors include unintended pregnancy, substance abuse, and health problems, which highlight the multidimensional vulnerabilities of perinatal women in terms of reproductive autonomy, mental health, and access to healthcare services [117]. Therefore, prevention and intervention strategies for perinatal IPV should prioritize mental health services, substance abuse prevention/treatment, and comprehensive reproductive health services.

In terms of social and relationship factors, the lack of social support and the polygamous marriage model can significantly increase the risk of IPV for perinatal women. Existing studies have shown that women lacking reliable social networks are more susceptible to coercive control and harm, particularly during the physiologically and psychologically vulnerable perinatal period [55]. Therefore, improving women’s social support systems and strengthening emotional and relational connections are key strategies to prevent and reduce perinatal IPV.

In terms of partner characteristics, low education levels, unemployment, alcohol consumption, drug abuse, infidelity, and criminal history are highly associated with perinatal IPV. Therefore, intervention strategies should focus on modifiable factors at the partner level. Evidence demonstrates that integrated interventions in combination with behavior therapy and substance abuse treatment can effectively reduce alcohol- and drug-related intimate partner violence [118]. Furthermore, community-level intervention programs, such as communication skills training for partners, relationship education, and marriage counseling, can help improve partner interactions and reduce the risk of conflict escalation into violence [119].

Economic context moderates the risk of perinatal IPV, and prevention strategies should be tailored accordingly. This study suggests that high-income countries (HICs) should focus on women’s mental health support (such as depression screening) and interventions for addiction behaviors (such as alcohol consumption and drug abuse), strengthen social support networks and relationship counseling services, and establish more comprehensive violence identification and protection mechanisms for economically independent women. Lower-middle-income and low-income countries (LMICs and LICs) should focus on promoting female education and economic empowerment, as well as ensuring the implementation of women’s employment policies. In terms of high-risk behaviors of partners, such as alcohol abuse, drug abuse, and infidelity, community-level interventions should strengthened. Particularly in contexts with inadequate social support, it is crucial to build community-based support systems to enhance women’s sense of safety and coping capacity during the perinatal period.

Risk factors for physical violence

Among the various types of violence analyzed in this study, physical violence is the most commonly reported type by perinatal women. The major demographic, health, and reproductive risk factors identified in this study suggest that women with limited educational resources, unstable marital status, insufficient pregnancy preparation, and poor health status are more vulnerable to physical violence [120]. Therefore, it is recommended to include safety education into routine prenatal care, and meanwhile improve accessibility to legal assistance, primarily focusing on supporting women with unstable marriage or high parenting pressure; it is also recommended to systematically assess pregnancy intention and health conditions during prenatal screenings, to promote early identification and intervention and further reduce the risk of physical violence [121].

In terms of social and relationship factors, women’s fear of her partner is most significantly associated with perinatal physical violence, suggesting that assessment of women’s subjective sense of safety should be prioritized during prenatal screening to identify high-risk cases in a timely manner. Additionally, mother’s history of suffering violence and her tolerance toward violence significantly increase the risk of physical violence, indicating the necessity of conducting violence awareness education and preventing the intergenerational transmission of domestic violence [122].

In terms of partner characteristics, alcohol consumption and controlling behaviors are common risk factors for perinatal physical violence. Therefore, the prevention and control strategies for perinatal physical violence should focus on identifying and intervening in high-risk behaviors of the partner. Evidence suggests that intervening in the partner’s alcohol consumption can effectively reduce the risk of violence [123]; the controlling behaviors of the partner identified during pregnancy should form a high-risk signal, and in this case, interventions should be initiated timely [124].

Economic context plays a critical role in the risk structure of perinatal physical violence. In HICs and UMICs, risk factors are more related to women’s health vulnerabilities, while in LMICs and LICs, partner alcohol consumption is a major trigger. Therefore, the prevention and control strategies for physical violence should be tailored to local contexts: In economically developed countries, physical violence screening and support for health-vulnerable women should be strengthened; in LMICs and LICs, partner alcohol consumption should be included in prenatal assessments, and community-level behavioral interventions and health education should be promoted [118, 125].

Risk factors for psychological violence

Psychological violence is often overlooked due to its covert nature but is a major contributor to perinatal depression, maternal anxiety, and adverse neonatal outcomes [126–128]. This study shows that the risk factors for psychological violence are primarily characterized by individual characteristics and reflected at intimate partner level. Factors such as advanced age, large number of children, low education level, unintended pregnancy, poor health status, and childhood violence experience are all significantly associated with the elevated risks of experiencing psychological violence. Low education level of the partner is also an important risk factor; and no significant associations are found in terms of social and relationship factors. Based on these findings, the identification of psychological violence should focus on high-risk individuals, and educational and support interventions should be conducted for their partners, such as implementing educational programs centered on non-violent communication, mental health literacy, and relationship management [129].

Subgroup analysis further reveals the moderating effect of economic context on the risk factors for psychological violence. In HICs and upper-middle-income countries, there are fewer risk factors that are significantly associated with psychological violence, while most significant associations are concentrated in lower-middle-income countries. This finding suggests that, in LMICs and LICs, routine psychological violence screening should be strengthened in prenatal care systems, with priority given on high-risk perinatal women, such as those with factors of advanced maternal age, multiple children and lower education level. Meanwhile, tailored health education programs targeting psychological violence should be developed with a focus on education, a modifiable factor, while considering characteristics of local economic and social structure.

Risk factors for sexual violence

The reported rate of perinatal sexual violence in this study is relatively low, which may be possibly influenced by cultural taboos and women’s avoidance of sexuality-related topics. Some women, even when experiencing sexual coercion, may not perceive it as violence [130]. Nevertheless, the study has identified several significant risk factors. Advanced maternal age over 30 years was the only significant demographic risk factor, while the low education level and alcohol consumption of the partner are significant risks at partner level. This suggests that the partner’s behavior and cognition play a critical role in the occurrence of sexual violence, particularly alcohol-induced controlling and coercing behaviors. Based on these findings, prevention and identification of perinatal sexual violence should focus on those with advanced maternal age. Sexual violence awareness and alcohol intervention education should be conducted for high-risk partners; and women’s self-protection awareness and resource access capabilities should be enhanced through community empowerment programs [32].

This study suggests that economic context may influence the exposure to and identification of risk factors for perinatal sexual violence. Subgroup analysis indicates that significant risk factors were identified exclusively in lower-middle-income countries, including advanced maternal age (over 30 years), low education level of women, and low education level of the partner. No significant associations were found in high-income, upper-middle-income, and low-income countries, possibly influenced by cultural taboos or limitations in the sample studies [131]. Therefore, lower-middle-income countries should focus on high-risk groups such as women with advanced maternal age and/or low education level, and strengthen gender education and violence prevention interventions for men. Other countries with different economic contexts should also improve their sexual violence identification and reporting mechanisms, to avoid underestimating or overlooking risk factors.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This is the first systematic review to explore risk factors for perinatal IPV and analyze their differences across regions and levels of economic development. The findings of this review provides new insights into developing tailored prevention strategies. This review included samples from 30 countries across various continents, representing different levels of socioeconomic development.

However, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, although the review included a substantial number of studies (n = 83), the meta-analyses were restricted to risk factors reported in a significant number of studies to ensure statistical reliability; this necessarily excluded rarely examined factors that may have clinical or theoretical relevance. Second, methodological heterogeneity in the classification of certain risk factors across studies necessitated our conservative approach of pooling only the most severe or extreme categories, potentially introducing bias into the findings. Third, although this study quantitatively synthesized multiple risk factors for perinatal IPV, it should be noted that some subgroup analyses included only a very limited number of studies (e.g., 1–2), which consequently imposes certain limitations on the interpretation of the findings. Lastly, although this study included region as a variable in the subgroup analysis, which partially reflected geographic and cultural diversity, the discussion section did not provide an in-depth analysis of how the cultural differences underlying these regions might potentially influence risk factors for perinatal IPV.

Conclusions and implications

This study has identified multiple significant factors associated with the risk of perinatal IPV, covering multiple aspects such as demographics characteristics, health and reproductive characteristics, social and relationship factors, and partner characteristics. Specifically, low education level, unstable employment status, unintended pregnancy, and insufficient social support of women, as well as low education level and alcohol consumption of the partner, are the most commonly reported significant risk factors. The study has revealed heterogeneity in risk factors for perinatal IPV across different economic contexts: In high-income countries, mental health and addiction behaviors require prioritized attention, while in low- and middle-income countries, educational deficits, economic dependency, and high-risk behaviors of the partner are the primary risk determinants.

Based on these findings, it is recommended to incorporate routine IPV screening into antenatal and postnatal care as a foundational strategy for preventing and controlling perinatal violence. For example, IPV identification and safety planning can be integrated into perinatal home visit services through family visit programs, to provide continuous and accessible support to women in home environment [122]. Meanwhile, the prevention and control strategies for perinatal IPV should focus on modifiable high-risk factors and adopt individualized strategies based on economic contexts. In high-income countries, mental health assessments and addiction behavior management should be included in perinatal care services, and social support systems and relationship counseling services should be strengthened concurrently. In low- and middle-income countries, priority should be given to promoting female education and economic empowerment, enhancing community-based interventions for high-risk partner behaviors, and improving the social support system during pregnancy, so as to reduce the occurrence of perinatal IPV at multiple levels.

Given the predominance of cross-sectional studies in current review, future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies, to better elucidate the causal pathways of the risk factors for perinatal IPV. In addition, research on psychological violence, sexual violence, and multiple forms of violence remains insufficient and further studies are required to explore their potential risk factors. Meanwhile, standardized assessment tools should be promoted, to enhance the scientific rigor of the studies and the comparability of the study results.
