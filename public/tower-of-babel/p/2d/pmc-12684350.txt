Enzyme Immobilization in Porous Crystals via Cage Encapsulation
Authors: Hong‐Kun Liu, Takahiro Nakama, Shingo Funami, Risa Ebihara, Takaaki Mitsuhashi, Makoto Fujita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnzyme immobilization within solid or polymer matrices continues to face challenges, such as inhomogeneous protein environments, low loading efficiency, and limited versatility. Here, we present a pre‐encapsulation strategy for enzyme immobilization in porous crystals via coordination cage encapsulation. In this approach, enzymes are first confined within the well‐defined cavities of spherical coordination cages prior to crystallization. The host cage framework facilitates crystal formation under almost uniform conditions, independently of the surface properties of the enzymes. This isomorphous crystallization enabled the successful immobilization of nine proteins with diverse sizes and properties, achieving high loading efficiencies. The immobilized enzymes retained their native structures and catalytic activities within the crystal, functioning as heterogeneous catalysts with enhanced stability. Furthermore, the co‐immobilization of two enzymes in close proximity facilitated cascade reactions, underscoring the potential of coordination cages as effective hosts for enzyme immobilization.

Introduction

Immobilization of enzymes in solid support materials is a promising approach to enhancing their performance as heterogeneous catalysts.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] While various matrices, such as organic polymers,[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] mesoporous silica,[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] hydrogels,[ 12 , 13 ] nanoparticles,[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] and metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs)[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] have been employed, the immobilization often suffers from limited substrate scope, low loading efficiency, and heterogeneous chemical environments within their cavities.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 14 , 25 ] These obstacles cannot be fully addressed by conventional strategies, in which enzymes are either encapsulated in pre‐synthesized cavities of solid supports[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 15 , 16 , 20 , 22 ] or embedded in host matrices assembled around them.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 12 , 13 , 21 , 23 , 24 ]

Here, we present a versatile method for immobilizing proteins in structurally ordered porous crystals via encapsulation within spherical coordination cages. M12L24 coordination cages,[ 26 , 27 ] self‐assembled from palladium(II) ions (M) and bent bridging ligands (L), feature well‐defined cavities large enough to accommodate a single protein.[ 28 − 33 ] These large symmetrical cages readily crystallize, even after encapsulating a protein (Figure 1). Using this pre‐encapsulation approach, where proteins are confined within the cage cavities in solution prior to crystallization, we successfully immobilized nine proteins of varying sizes and physicochemical properties within the M12L24 cage crystals. Since the large windows of the cage are oriented to give a porous host framework, the immobilized enzymes function as heterogeneous catalysts with enhanced stability. Notably, the co‐immobilization of two different enzymes within a single cage crystal facilitated proximity‐promoted cascade reactions, in which the two enzymes sequentially transform the absorbed guest into a product, highlighting the potential of this strategy for developing functional biomaterials.

Enzyme immobilization in a porous crystal. a) Pre‐encapsulation of a protein in M12L24 coordination cages: First, by mixing a target protein with ligand L (24 equivalents), the protein is conjugated with a portion of L through Schiff base formation, followed by imidazolidinone ring formation.[ 34 ] Subsequently, adding 12 equivalents of Pd(II) salt (M) induces the self‐assembly of the M12L24 complex encapsulating the protein, as previously reported.[ 28 , 29 ] b) Formation of protein‐immobilized porous crystals via isomorphous crystallization: The protein‐encapsulating M12L24 complex prepared in solution crystallizes under nearly constant conditions, regardless of the protein type. This yields porous crystals that readily permit the diffusion of external small molecules in and out.

Results and Discussion

Enzyme Immobilization in Coordination Cage Crystals

Superoxide Dismutase 1 (SOD1) Immobilization

Superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) was first encapsulated in the cage crystal because of its high enzymatic performance to disproportionate superoxide O2 •– and its unique bimetallic Cu/Zn catalytic site (Figures 1a and 4d,f).[ 35 ] We employed two kinds of cages 2a and 2b, with a cavity of 3.8 nm and 5.5 nm diameter, for protein immobilization (Figures S1–S3). To encapsulate an enzyme in the cages, the 2‐formylpyridyl group[ 34 ] of ligand 1a or 1b (Figure 2a) was conjugated selectively to the N‐terminal amino group of the protein, and subsequent complexation with Pd(II) ions self‐assembled M12L24 cages (Scheme S1).[ 28 , 29 ]

Immobilization of proteins in M12L24 cage crystals. a) Chemical structures of 1a and 1b as the L component of the M12L24 cages. b) Pictures of the crystals of the empty cage 2a (left) and SOD1@2a (right). c) Crystal structure of SOD1@2a complex. SOD1 in the cage cavity could not be modeled because of the random orientation of SOD1 in the cage. d) Another view emphasizing the square windows (2.0 × 2.0 Å) of the SOD1@2a crystals.

The encapsulation of SOD1 in cage 2a (SOD1@2a) was primarily confirmed by 1H diffusion‐ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR analysis (Figures S4, S6, and S7). Complexation with Pd(II) ions significantly decreased the diffusion coefficient (D) of SOD1, with this value matching that of the cage (D = 3.9 × 10−11 m2 s). Furthermore, SOD1 encapsulated in the larger cage 2b (SOD1@2b) exhibited an even smaller diffusion coefficient (D = 2.5 × 10−11 m2/s, Figures S5 and S8). Mixing the unencapsulated enzyme with an empty M12L24 cage resulted in distinct D values for each (Figure S21). These results suggest that SOD1 diffuses together with the cage framework, confirming its encapsulation within the cage. The salting‐out of the caged protein[ 28 ] also demonstrated the encapsulation (Figures S22 and S45–S48). The complete co‐precipitation of the proteins with NaBF4 indicates a high encapsulation efficiency within the cage.

A simple vapor diffusion method afforded single crystals of cage 2a encapsulating SOD1 (Figures 2b,c, and S23). We first examined the crystallization of cage 2a without proteins using several different poor solvents. Among them, vapor diffusion of 1,4‐dioxane or tert‐butyl acetate into a N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF)‐water mixed solution of cage 2a at 20 °C resulted in twisted‐octahedral crystals of cage 2a with a size of 100 µm × 100 µm (Figure 2b, left). Then, the same crystallization conditions were applied to SOD1@2a, resulting in crystals of similar shape and size (Figure 2b, right). Crystals were also obtained from SOD1@2b under the crystallization conditions for the larger cage 2b (Figure S24). These results suggest that the cages can crystallize independently of the guest proteins in their cavities.

Single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of SOD1@2a revealed that the M12L24 cage framework crystallized in the same space group with nearly identical lattice parameters as the protein‐free cage alone, featuring isomorphous crystallization to give the identical alignment of SOD1 within the crystal (Figures 2c,d, S33, S35, S37, and S38). However, despite our numerous efforts to refine the structures using different data sets, the structure of SOD1 could not be modeled in the crystallographic analysis. This is likely due to the random orientation of the enzyme within the symmetric cage cavity, resulting in static disorder in the crystal structure. This phenomenon is thought to stem from two factors: the high symmetry of the host framework, which causes cages to crystallize in highly symmetric space groups (prone to information loss), and the inherent tendency for static disorder when guest molecules are confined in a symmetric host.

Although X‐ray observation of the encapsulated SOD1 was unfortunately not possible, it is noteworthy that we successfully immobilized SOD1 in an ordered manner within the single crystals. The encapsulation of SOD1 within the cage cavities of the crystal was confirmed by SDS‐PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) analysis (Figures S25, S26, S49, and S50). X‐ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy provided more quantitative insight into the protein loading in the cage crystals (Figure S27). X‐ray irradiation at 25.5 keV to the SOD1@2a crystal provided the fluorescence signals from Cu and Zn located at the catalytic center of SOD1, as well as from Pd in the cage. The emission intensities of Cu Kα and Zn Kα relative to Pd Kα indicated that SOD1 was incorporated into 70% ± 23% and 67% ± 24% of the cage cavity of the crystal, respectively.

Versatility

We have previously demonstrated the solution‐phase encapsulation of various proteins within M12L24 cages.[ 28 ] These cages can accommodate a given protein regardless of its isoelectric point (pI) or other properties, as long as the protein fits into the cage cavity. If these protein‐encapsulating cages can form single crystals under nearly identical conditions, diverse enzyme immobilization in the cage crystals is expected. We thus investigated nine different types of proteins, using two different sizes of cages 2a (3.8 nm in diameter) and 2b (5.5 nm in diameter), depending on the proteins' size (Figure 3 and Table S1).

Scope of protein immobilization in cage 2a and 2b crystals.

After confirming their encapsulation in solution by 1H DOSY NMR and the salting‐out experiments, we attempted to crystallize them under conditions similar to those used for SOD1@2a (Figures S9–S22). As a result, we obtained single crystals encapsulating individual proteins from all samples (Figures 3, S23, and S24). SDS‐PAGE analysis confirmed the inclusion of proteins within the crystal (Figures S25, S26, S49, and S50). The band intensities suggested that the proteins were loaded into more than half of the crystal cavity, consistent with the XRF analysis.

Notably, these crystals exhibited the same space group and cell parameters as SOD1@2a and were also crystallized by vapor diffusion under the same conditions as the empty cages, highlighting the isomorphous crystallization of the protein encapsulating cages (Figures S33–S36 and Tables S2–S5). Without individual condition optimization, all caged protein crystals grew to a size greater than 100 µm. These results clearly demonstrate the excellent versatility of our pre‐encapsulation strategy using coordination cages.

Characterization in the Solid State

Since enzymes are immobilized as single crystals within the well‐defined cavity of M12L24 cages, they are expected to maintain their native conformations within these homogeneous internal environments. This allows for the structural characterization of these caged enzymes. We investigated the structure of SOD1 with a Cu/Zn active center (Figure 4d), which is well‐suited for solid‐state spectroscopic studies. X‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis revealed the coordination structure of SOD1 encapsulated within the cage crystal. X‐ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra at the Cu K‐edge showed that the absorption edges (8975 eV) of both the SOD1@2a crystal and the uncaged SOD1 powder were identical (Figure 4a). These spectra are comparable to those of [Cu(Im)4](NO3)2 (Im = imidazole), indicating that the oxidation state of Cu remained + 2 after immobilization. The XANES spectrum of SOD1@2a at the Zn K‐edge was also consistent with that of uncaged SOD1 powder and [Zn(Im)4](NO3)2 (Figure S28).

The spectra of [CuII(Im)4](NO3)2 (Im: imidazole, pale blue) and [CuI(MeCN)4](BF4)2 (brown) are also shown. d) SOD1 catalytic center in the reported crystal structure (PDB: 2ZOW) and corresponding chemical structure. e) FTIR spectra of SOD1 powder (green), the SOD1@2a crystal (red), and the cage 2a crystal (gray). f) Scheme of SOD1 assay. g) Reaction profiles the SOD1@2a crystal (red) and uncaged SOD1 (green).

The extended XAFS (EXAFS) region elucidated the coordination structure of the SOD1 active site (Figure 4b). The Cu K‐edge k 3‐weighted EXAFS spectrum of SOD1@2a overlapped with that of uncaged SOD1, indicating similar coordination structures. Both spectra in R space showed a main peak at 1.53 Å, corresponding to the Cu–N (Cu–imidazole) coordination bonds. The R patterns are also comparable to those of [Cu(Im)4](NO3)2 and the previous reports on SOD1,[ 36 ] supporting the coordination structure of SOD1@2a (Figure 4c). The Zn K‐edge EXFAS spectra also show that the structure of the first coordination shell is preserved after the immobilization (Figures S29 and S30). These results indicate that SOD1 was encapsulated in the cage crystal with its active center structure intact.

Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectra showed the overall structure of the caged proteins in the crystals (Figures 4e, S31, and S32). When a single crystal of SOD1@2a was analyzed using FT‐IR microscopy, distinct peaks appeared at 2941, 1633, and 1530 cm−1, which were absent from the spectra of the empty cage of 2a. These peaks correspond to the C─H stretching vibration, amide I absorption, and amide II absorption bands of SOD1, respectively. A slight shift in the amide I band suggests minor structural changes in the intermolecular antiparallel β‐sheets of SOD1.[ 37 ] Meanwhile, the shifted peak differed from that of the random coil, and the amide II band remained unchanged. Together with the XAFS data, these results indicate that the overall SOD1 structure was retained in the cage crystal, with only minor changes in β‐sheet conformations. Other proteins in the cage crystals, such as cytochrome c (cyt c)@2a, exhibited characteristic IR bands from the protein (Figure S32).

Enzymatic Performance of Immobilized Enzymes

The porous nature of the host framework allowed for free diffusion of small molecules into the crystals, where the immobilized enzymes maintained their native active structure. Consequently, immobilized SOD1 exhibited high catalytic activity despite being in a crystalline state. The enzymatic activity of SOD1@2a was evaluated by monitoring the absorption spectrum of WST‐1, a tetrazolium dye that absorbs at 450 nm when reduced by superoxide (Figure 4f).[ 38 ] The results revealed that SOD1 within the single crystal catalyzed the superoxide disproportionation reaction with 46% residual activity compared to the free enzyme. This value takes into account the encapsulation ratio estimated by X‐ray fluorescence (XRF) spectra (Figure S27). This residual activity is significantly higher than that observed after immobilization in conventional host matrices, where limited substrate diffusion and denaturation typically reduce enzymatic activity.[ 12 , 14 ] Cyt c@2a and RNase A@2b crystals also demonstrated remarkable enzymatic performance (Figures S39 and S42 and Scheme S2).

The enhanced stability of the immobilized enzymes is also noteworthy. Cyt c@2a exhibited significant tolerance to organic solvents, such as acetonitrile (Figure S40). The catalyst promoted an oxidation reaction not only in aqueous media but also in acetone (Figure S41). This stands in stark contrast to uncaged cyt c, which showed limited enzymatic performance due to severe aggregation in these organic media.[ 39 ] This stabilization against organic media can broaden the reaction scope of enzymes,[ 40 , 41 , 42 ] thereby increasing their potential as heterogeneous biocatalysts, which are important for both industrial and laboratory applications. These results collectively demonstrate the high performance of the cage crystal as an immobilization matrix.

Cascade Enzymatic Reaction in a Coordination Cage Crystal

The cascade reaction catalyzed by multiple enzymes immobilized within the cage crystal is particularly noteworthy because it significantly enhanced the performance and utility of these crystalline immobilized enzyme catalysts (Figure 5). First, we mixed two types of protein‐encapsulating cages, SOD1@2a and cyt c@2a (Figure S23), in solution at a 1:1 ratio, and subsequently obtained single crystals of immobilized SOD1–cyt c@2a from this mixture. In these crystals, the two enzymes are randomly arranged within the crystal (Figure 5a). When this single crystal was added to a solution containing superoxide and Amplex Red (substrates), the two enzymes catalyzed a cascade oxidation, yielding resorufin from Amplex Red (Figure 5b).[ 43 ] The reaction rate was 4.2 times faster than that observed when two separate crystals, each immobilizing an individual enzyme (SOD1@2a and cyt c@2a), were added (Figures 5c,d, S43, and S44). This remarkable acceleration is attributed to the proximity effect between the two enzymes within the cage crystal. Specifically, SOD1 increased the local concentration of hydrogen peroxide, a reactive intermediate. This reactive species then promoted the efficient tandem oxidation of Amplex Red by cyt c without diffusing out of the crystal.

The reaction was monitored using the fluorescence from resorufin (λ ex = 540 nm, λ em = 585 nm). d) The relative enzyme performance at a 0.5 h reaction time (n = 6). Error bars: standard deviation.

Compared to conventional multi‐enzyme immobilization systems,[ 4 , 9 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ] our system allows enzymes to be packed into the clearly aligned cavities of the cage, enabling highly effective proximity between the enzymes. Furthermore, this pre‐encapsulation approach is applicable to many types of enzymes, which should lead to the development of unique multi‐enzyme systems.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated a pre‐encapsulation approach to address the challenge of enzyme immobilization in solid crystals. This approach involves preparing enzyme‐encapsulated cages in solution beforehand and subsequently crystallizing them, which enabled the immobilization of nine different types of proteins, regardless of their surface charge or size. Furthermore, we showed that the proteins retained their active structures within the cage crystals.

From the perspective of enzyme immobilization in solid materials, our method has a decisive advantage over conventional techniques such as encapsulation within polymeric matrices[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 12 , 13 ] or the post‐synthetic introduction of enzymes into MOFs[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] or other porous frameworks.[ 49 , 52 ] In polymer matrix encapsulation, enzymes are significantly influenced by the matrix, and the free diffusion of substrates within the matrix is often considerably restricted, which generally leads to a substantial decrease in enzyme activity.[ 3 , 4 , 6 , 9 , 10 , 17 , 18 , 25 , 44 , 52 ] Moreover, the heterogeneous nature of polymers hinders precise structural characterization of enzymes and detailed analysis of enzymatic reactions.[ 2 , 5 , 8 , 14 , 19 , 25 ] However, our single‐crystalline protein‐encapsulating cage complexes showed no such significant decrease in activity. The structural homogeneity of the host also allowed for the acquisition of precise structural information, such as through EXAFS. Furthermore, post‐loading of proteins into MOFs often results in low encapsulation efficiencies and difficulties in characterization; there are currently few known examples demonstrating high versatility across a diverse range of proteins.[ 2 , 10 ] The high encapsulation efficiency and versatility of our approach significantly surpass methods utilizing post‐encapsulation in MOF complexes.

Additionally, the ability to introduce multiple types of enzymes at high loading into the crystal is a groundbreaking new method for easily synthesizing crystal‐immobilized cascade catalysts, as evidenced by the 4.2‐fold acceleration of the enzymatic cascade reaction. These results lead us to conclude that coordination cages can serve as an exceptionally versatile host for immobilizing enzymes in crystalline materials.
