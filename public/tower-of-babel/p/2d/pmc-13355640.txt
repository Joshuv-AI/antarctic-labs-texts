Association Between Low-Dose Aspirin Use and Early Renal Injury Biomarkers in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Retrospective Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Yu-Shan Hsieh, Yan-Yu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMicroalbuminuria and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) are important markers for early diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid; ASP) has been associated with anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects in patients with T2DM. This study investigated the association between low-dose ASP use and renal injury markers in patients with T2DM.MethodsA retrospective matched cohort study was conducted in 60 Taiwanese patients with T2DM. Patients receiving low-dose ASP (100 mg/day) were matched 1:1 with controls according to sex, age, height, weight, body mass index, and duration of diabetes. Metabolic outcomes and renal injury markers, including serum creatinine, urine microalbumin, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), were evaluated during follow-up.ResultsCompared with the DM group, the ASP group demonstrated lower HbA1c and total cholesterol levels during follow-up. In renal outcome analysis, urine microalbumin levels were significantly lower in the ASP group than in the DM group at follow-up (p = 0.039). A trend toward improved UACR was also observed in the ASP group.ConclusionLow-dose ASP use was associated with favorable changes in glycemic control and early renal injury markers in patients with T2DM. Lower urine microalbumin levels and a trend toward improved UACR were observed in the ASP group during follow-up. Although microalbuminuria may be influenced by multiple metabolic and cardiovascular factors, the present findings suggest that low-dose ASP therapy may have potential metabolic and renal associations in patients with T2DM. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to explore the underlying mechanisms and their clinical relevance.Keywords: aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid, microalbuminuria, type 2 diabetes mellitus, renal injury markers

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the leading causes of chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular morbidity worldwide. Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), a major microvascular complication of T2DM, is associated with increased mortality and progression to end-stage renal disease. Although multiple glucose-lowering therapies are currently available, the prevention of early renal injury in patients with T2DM remains a major clinical challenge.1,2

Inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are important contributors to the development and progression of DKD. Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid; ASP), a widely used anti-inflammatory agent, has been reported to exert metabolic3 and vascular,4 and anti-inflammatory effects beyond platelet inhibition.5 Previous studies have suggested potential associations between ASP use and glycemic control in patients with T2DM6,7 although the findings remain inconsistent. However, the relationship between low-dose ASP use and early renal injury markers in T2DM has not been fully clarified.

Microalbuminuria and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) are clinically important markers for the early detection of DKD and are associated with increased renal and cardiovascular risk in patients with T2DM.8 CKD is a common complication of T2DM and is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events, end-stage renal disease, and mortality.9 This underscores the importance of measuring urinary microalbumin as a crucial indicator for the early prediction of diabetic nephropathy.

Therefore, this retrospective matched cohort study investigated the association between low-dose ASP use and metabolic as well as renal injury markers in patients with T2DM. We analyzed changes in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), serum lipid profiles, including triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and total cholesterol, as well as renal injury markers including serum creatinine, urine creatinine, urine microalbumin, and UACR.

Methods and Materials

Study Population

Patients with T2DM who received regular follow-up at the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism at a medical center in Taiwan between July 2021 and October 2023 were screened for eligibility in this retrospective matched cohort study.

Inclusion criteria for the ASP group were as follows: (1) treatment with low-dose ASP (100 mg/day) for at least 6 months; and (2) regular outpatient follow-up during the study period. The control group consisted of patients with T2DM who had never received ASP therapy.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included: (1) age <18 years or >90 years; (2) diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus or gestational diabetes mellitus; (3) chronic liver disease, including nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD); and (4) treatment with statin therapy. To minimize potential confounding effects on lipid profiles, patients receiving statin therapy were excluded.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

Study Design and Setting

A retrospective matched cohort design was used in this study. Patients receiving ASP therapy were matched 1:1 with patients not receiving ASP according to sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and duration of diabetes to improve comparability between groups (Table 1).

Baseline laboratory data before ASP exposure were retrospectively obtained from medical records. Follow-up laboratory measurements were collected during routine outpatient follow-up visits after at least 6 months of ASP therapy. Metabolic and renal outcomes were subsequently compared between the ASP and DM groups.

Initially, a retrospective review of medical records was conducted to document the previous blood test results of each participant before starting ASP medication. Subsequently, blood tests were performed concurrently to assess post-medication cross-section outcomes. All pre-medication blood test results were obtained retrospectively from case reviews, while post-medication results were analyzed from blood samples taken immediately after patients enrolled in the study. Following this, matched analyses were conducted both within groups (pre-post analysis) and between groups (analysis with or without ASP usage). Medication use was confirmed through medical records and pharmacy refill documentation during routine follow-up visits.

Main Outcomes

Fasting blood samples were collected during routine outpatient follow-up visits. HbA1c levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (Bio-Rad Variant II Turbo 2.0 System, Hercules, CA, USA). Serum lipid profiles, serum creatinine, urine creatinine, and urine microalbumin were analyzed using an automated chemistry analyzer (ADVIA Chemistry XPT, Siemens, Forchheim, Germany). UACR was calculated by dividing urine microalbumin concentration (mg/L) by urine creatinine concentration (mg/dL) and multiplying by 100 to express the result as mg/g creatinine.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and data management were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Within-group comparisons between baseline and follow-up measurements were performed using paired t-tests for normally distributed variables or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for skewed variables. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests according to data distribution. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Patients

A total of 100 patients with T2DM were screened for eligibility, and 60 patients were ultimately included after 1:1 matching between the ASP and DM groups according to sex, age, height, weight, BMI, and duration of diabetes. Patients who could not be adequately matched were excluded from the final analysis. The study population consisted of 30 patients in the ASP group and 30 patients in the DM group. No significant differences were observed between groups regarding age, sex, BMI, height, weight, or duration of diabetes after matching (Table 1).

The distributions of antidiabetic medications were generally comparable between groups. ACEI/ARB use was similar between the ASP and DM groups, whereas calcium channel blocker use was more frequent in the ASP group. Concomitant medication use in both groups is summarized (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective matched cohort study, patients with T2DM receiving low-dose ASP therapy demonstrated favorable changes in metabolic and renal biomarkers compared with matched controls. The ASP group showed lower HbA1c and total cholesterol levels during follow-up, along with lower urine microalbumin levels and a trend toward improved UACR. These findings suggest that low-dose ASP use was associated with favorable changes in metabolic control and early renal injury biomarkers in patients with T2DM.

Systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are important contributors to diabetes-related complications and glycemic control.10 ASP has been reported to possess antiglycation and anti-inflammatory properties, including inhibition of albumin and hemoglobin glycation in both in vivo and in vitro studies.11 Previous studies have also suggested that COX inhibition and protein acetylation may contribute to the metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects of ASP.5,12,13 In the present study, patients receiving ASP demonstrated lower HbA1c levels and reduced urine microalbumin levels during follow-up. However, published evidence regarding the metabolic and renal effects of aspirin remains inconsistent, which may be attributable to differences in aspirin dosage, study populations, and outcome measures. Although high-dose aspirin has been reported to improve insulin sensitivity and glycemic control, evidence supporting the long-term benefits of low-dose aspirin remains limited. Therefore, the favorable changes observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.

The dosage of ASP may be an important factor influencing its metabolic and renal effects. Previous studies have used a wide range of ASP dosages and reported inconsistent findings. Lou et al reported that low-dose ASP did not significantly affect serum glucose or lipid profiles in patients with T2DM.14 In contrast, studies using high-dose ASP, such as 3 g/day for several days or 7 g/day for two weeks, demonstrated improvements in fasting glucose, hepatic glucose production, fatty acid levels, and triglyceride metabolism.15,16 These discrepancies may be partially related to differences in ASP dosage across studies.

However, the ASP dosages used in these high-dose studies are substantially greater than those commonly prescribed in routine clinical practice and may raise concerns regarding gastrointestinal adverse effects, bleeding risk, and platelet-related safety issues. In contrast, low-dose ASP, commonly prescribed at 81–100 mg/day, is widely used for cardiovascular prevention in patients with diabetes and has a more clinically acceptable safety profile. Therefore, the present study focused on ASP 100 mg/day, which reflects a real-world dosage commonly used in patients with T2DM rather than a pharmacological high-dose intervention.

Although the metabolic effects of low-dose ASP may be less pronounced than those observed with high-dose regimens, our findings suggest that long-term low-dose ASP use may still be associated with favorable changes in HbA1c and renal injury markers. Further studies are needed to determine whether these effects are dose dependent.

Dyslipidemia is an important cardiovascular risk factor in patients with T2DM and is closely associated with insulin resistance and glycemic dysregulation. Previous studies evaluating the effects of ASP on lipid metabolism have reported inconsistent findings. The JPAD (Japanese Primary Prevention of Atherosclerosis with Aspirin for Diabetes) trial reported that long-term low-dose ASP therapy did not significantly affect serum lipid profiles in patients with T2DM,17 whereas another study using ASP 300 mg/day observed reductions in serum cholesterol levels.18

In the present study, total cholesterol levels were lower in the ASP group during follow-up, and LDL-C levels also demonstrated a decreasing trend, although the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance. Because all participants continued routine diabetes management during follow-up, the observed lipid changes may not represent a direct lipid-lowering effect of ASP itself. Instead, improved glycemic control and overall metabolic status may partially contribute to these findings.

Because liver dysfunction may influence lipid metabolism and metabolic profiles, GPT levels were evaluated in both groups.19 GPT was selected because it is considered a relatively liver-specific biochemical marker compared with other transaminases. No significant between-group differences in GPT levels were observed during follow-up, suggesting that hepatic dysfunction was unlikely to be a major contributor to the observed metabolic findings.

Previous studies investigating the renal effects of ASP in patients with T2DM have reported inconsistent findings. Several studies evaluating renal function using eGFR decline or urine dipstick albuminuria did not observe significant renoprotective effects associated with low-dose ASP therapy.20–22 In contrast, the present study focused on early renal injury markers, including urine microalbumin and UACR, which may be more sensitive indicators of early diabetic kidney injury.

Microalbuminuria is considered an important marker for early DKD and is associated with both renal and cardiovascular risk in patients with T2DM.23 The development of microalbuminuria is influenced by multiple factors, including glycemic control, blood pressure, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and BMI.24–29 Therefore, the lower urine microalbumin levels observed in the ASP group may not necessarily represent a direct renoprotective effect of ASP itself. Instead, the observed renal findings may be partially associated with overall improvements in metabolic status and disease management.

In the present study, UACR also demonstrated a trend toward improvement in the ASP group. Although serum creatinine levels remained comparable between groups, this finding may reflect the relatively early stage of renal injury in the study population. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the potential relationship between low-dose ASP use and early renal injury markers in patients with T2DM.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings may provide additional insight into the potential relationship between low-dose ASP use and early renal injury markers in patients with T2DM. Because low-dose ASP is already widely prescribed for cardiovascular prevention in high-risk diabetic populations, understanding its possible associations with metabolic and renal parameters may be clinically relevant in routine diabetes management.

Previous studies evaluating ASP in patients with T2DM have primarily focused on cardiovascular outcomes or long-term renal endpoints such as eGFR decline. In contrast, the present study evaluated early renal injury markers, including urine microalbumin and UACR, which may provide additional information regarding early diabetic kidney injury. Although the observed improvements in renal biomarkers should be interpreted cautiously, the present findings suggest that early renal markers may warrant further evaluation in future studies investigating the pleiotropic effects of ASP in patients with T2DM. Nevertheless, the current findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting ASP use specifically for renal protection. Instead, the present study may provide preliminary clinical evidence supporting further investigation of the metabolic and renal associations of low-dose ASP therapy in patients with T2DM. This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and retrospective observational design may have limited the statistical power and generalizability of the findings and may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding despite the matching procedures. Second, blood pressure data were not systematically analyzed, although hypertension is an important factor associated with diabetic kidney disease and microalbuminuria. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive clinical assessments are warranted to validate and extend the present findings.

In addition, the ASP group showed a higher proportion of calcium channel blocker use compared with the DM group, which may reflect a population receiving more intensive cardiovascular management. Although ACEI/ARB use was comparable between groups, the imbalance in antihypertensive medication use may still represent residual confounding. Third, renal function was primarily evaluated using early renal injury markers, including urine microalbumin and UACR, rather than long-term renal outcomes such as eGFR decline or progression to chronic kidney disease.

Therefore, larger prospective studies are needed to further clarify the relationship between low-dose ASP use and renal injury markers in patients with T2DM.

Conclusion

In conclusion, low-dose ASP use was associated with favorable changes in glycemic control and early renal injury markers in patients with T2DM. Lower urine microalbumin levels and a trend toward improved UACR were observed in the ASP group during follow-up. However, because microalbuminuria is influenced by multiple metabolic and cardiovascular factors, the observed renal findings should be interpreted cautiously and may not represent a direct renoprotective effect of ASP. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive clinical assessments are warranted to validate these findings, clarify the underlying mechanisms, and further explore the potential metabolic and renal effects of low-dose aspirin in patients with T2DM.
