The value of One-Minute Preceptor combined with Flipped Classroom teaching model in clinical teaching of general practice
Authors: Chai Yueyang, Wang Li, Chen Haiyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of combining the One-Minute Preceptor (OMP) and Flipped Classroom (FC) methods in clinical teaching for general medicine residents during their standardized training.MethodsA total of 80 general medicine residents undergoing standardized training at our hospital between June 2023 and December 2024 were selected as study participants. These residents were randomly assigned to either an experimental group (n = 40) receiving the OMP/FC teaching method or a control group (n = 40) following traditional teaching methods. The effectiveness of the teaching methods and resident satisfaction were assessed through exit examinations and a questionnaire survey.ResultsThere was no significant difference in gender, age and other general data between the two groups. The experimental group showed significantly higher performance in the exit examination compared to the control group, with superior scores in theoretical assessments, first-round medical record writing, major case documentation, and clinical reasoning (P < 0.05). Additionally, survey results indicated that the experimental group exhibited better abilities in disease history taking, physical examination, and interpreting auxiliary examinations (P < 0.05). While improvements in theoretical knowledge, medical record documentation, and clinical reasoning were statistically significant (P < 0.05), the size of the improvement for some measures, such as satisfaction ratings, were modest and may not be clinically meaningful.ConclusionThe combination of the OMP and FC methods significantly improved the clinical training outcomes for general medicine residents. These methods enhanced key clinical skills, increased teaching effectiveness, and fostered higher levels of resident satisfaction, making them a promising approach for clinical education in primary care.Keywords: One minute teaching, Flipped classroom, General practice, Entrustable professional activities, Standardized training of residents, Effect of teaching

Introduction

The standardized residency training phase for general medicine residents is a critical stage in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and the development of comprehensive clinical skills. Traditional lecture-based teaching, which primarily involves instructors delivering content through pre-prepared slides, often overlaps with theoretical coursework. This results in limited student engagement and fewer opportunities for hands-on practice, ultimately hindering the translation of theoretical knowledge into clinical application, especially in real-world scenarios [1]. To address these challenges, it is crucial to enhance residents’ initiative and participation in learning, while also fostering their ability to apply theoretical concepts in practical settings. The One-Minute Preceptor (OMP) method is a time-efficient, learner-centered approach that focuses on immediate feedback from instructors to enhance clinical decision-making. By concentrating on specific cases, instructors can quickly identify the learners’ needs and provide targeted suggestions, promoting problem-solving skills and clinical reasoning [2, 3]. On the other hand, the Flipped Classroom (FC) model encourages active learning by shifting the traditional lecture content outside the classroom, enabling residents to engage in more interactive, hands-on learning during class time [4]. Together, these methods offer promising solutions to address the limitations of traditional teaching and improve clinical training outcomes.

Materials and methods

General information

A total of 80 general medicine residents undergoing standardized training at our hospital from June 2023 to December 2024 were selected as the study subjects. After matching the participants based on grade, age, and gender, they were randomly assigned into an experimental group (OMP/FC combined teaching model, n = 40) and a control group (traditional teaching model, n = 40) using a random number table. In the experimental group, there were 26 male and 14 female participants, 19 in the first grade and 21 in the second grade, with an average age of (22.20 ± 2.41) years. In the control group, there were 25 male and 15 female participants, with 20 in the first grade and 20 in the second grade, with an average age of (22.42 ± 2.58) years.

Research methods

The study design employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) method to evaluate the impact of the OMP and FC teaching methods on clinical training outcomes for residents. Residents were randomly assigned to either the experimental group or the control group to conduct an intervention comparison, ensuring the reliability and scientific validity of the results. Randomization process

At the start of the study, 80 general medicine residents were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (OMP/FC combined teaching model) or the control group (traditional teaching model) using a random number table. The residents were matched based on key characteristics, such as age, gender, and grade, to ensure comparability between the groups. This process helped eliminate potential biases and ensured the reliability of the comparison between the two groups.

Teacher preparation

All the instructors are clinical physicians with at least a bachelor’s degree and a mid-level professional title or higher, and have received systematic teaching training and assessment. The training content includes mastering the OMP/FC teaching models, instructional methods, evaluation skills, improving clinical operation and communication abilities, as well as gaining hands-on experience in clinical teaching. Additionally, the instructors regularly participate in teaching seminars and feedback mechanisms to ensure continuous improvement in teaching quality. These training programs not only enhance the instructors’ clinical teaching abilities but also enable them to provide personalized guidance based on the needs of the trainees, effectively promoting the development of residents’ skills.

Teaching process

Both groups participated in departmental teaching rounds and short lectures as part of their learning activities. The content for these rounds and lectures was developed based on the “Assistant General Practitioner Training Standards” and included the following [5]:

Observation indicators

Exit examination scores

At the end of the teaching phase, each student will take an exit exam, which includes assessments on theoretical examination, basic skills operation, first course writing, medical record writing, and clinical thinking. These results will serve as objective evaluation metrics, comparing student performance across different teaching models to assess their relative effectiveness. The exit examination was designed based on well-established competency frameworks in medical education. To ensure the reliability of the exam, we conducted a pilot test prior to the study, involving a small group of residents to assess the clarity, difficulty level, and overall structure of the exam. The reliability of the examination was measured using Cronbach’s alpha, which showed good internal consistency (α = 0.84). Additionally, the theoretical component of the exam was validated through expert reviews, ensuring that it adequately assessed core medical knowledge relevant to the residency program.

Assessment of resident physicians’ entrustable professional activities

At the end of the teaching, the clinical ability of the residents was evaluated by the clinical teachers through entrustable professional activities(EPAs).The evaluation covers various aspects of resident physicians’ skills, including disease history collection, physical examination, auxiliary examination interpretation, clinical thinking, teamwork, humanistic quality and self-learning ability [6]. A five-point scale is used for scoring (Table 1). The EPA evaluation tool was reviewed by a panel of clinical experts, who provided feedback on the relevance and comprehensiveness of the included activities. Additionally, the inter-rater reliability was evaluated by having multiple clinical teachers independently assess the same residents. The results indicated good agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC = 0.79), suggesting that the evaluation tool is reliable in assessing various clinical skills. This scale was used by the examiners to rate residents’ performance on each activity, with descriptions for each level provided in the rubrics to guide the assessments. These rubrics ensured that examiners applied consistent standards across both groups.

Resident physicians satisfaction survey

Feedback from resident physicians was collected through a questionnaire, which assessed their satisfaction in five areas: course content, course resources, classroom interaction, teaching methods, and learning burden. A five-point scale was used for scoring: 1—Very Dissatisfied, 2—Dissatisfied, 3—Neutral, 4—Satisfied, and 5—Very Satisfied. The satisfaction survey was tailored to assess the specific aspects of this study, such as the effectiveness of the OMP/FC approach. Prior to implementation, a pilot test was conducted with a small group of residents to assess the clarity and relevance of the items. The internal consistency of the survey was measured using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.86, indicating good reliability. Additionally, exploratory factor analysis was performed, which confirmed that the survey items load on the intended factors (course content, classroom interaction, etc.), supporting its construct validity.

For this study, a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80 were assumed. We calculated a sample size of at least 40 participants per group by estimating the overall variance and expected effect sizes on the basis of previous studies. The exit examination scores and survey results were statistically analyzed using the SPSS 21.0 software(SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (Mean ± Standard), and unpaired t-tests were conducted for comparisons between the two groups. For categorical data, chi-square (χ2) tests were used for group comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The goal of general medicine education is to cultivate resident physicians’ comprehensive clinical thinking abilities, such as problem-solving, diagnostic reasoning, and decision-making, in order to meet the complex and evolving needs of patients. In particular, during residency training, it is essential not only to consolidate resident physicians’ theoretical knowledge but also to strengthen their clinical practice skills, enabling them to apply knowledge effectively in real-world settings. While traditional teaching models have advantages in knowledge dissemination, their limitations-particularly in fostering student engagement, self-directed learning, and the translation of theoretical knowledge into practical skills-are becoming increasingly evident. Given these limitations, exploring teaching models that better align with the needs of modern medical education is imperative. This study proposes and validates the effectiveness of combining the OMP with the FC model in general medicine teaching, and provides an in-depth discussion of its impact on teaching outcomes.

In terms of integrating theory with practice, the OMP model offers short, frequent teaching feedback, enabling instructors to pinpoint resident physicians’ knowledge gaps and provide personalized guidance. This feedback mechanism proves particularly effective in real clinical scenarios, where instructors use case-based questions and immediate responses to foster active student engagement, thereby enhancing clinical diagnostic and decision-making skills [7]. In contrast to OMP, the FC model emphasizes independent learning before class, allowing resident physicians to construct a knowledge framework on their own. In class, they engage in in-depth case discussions with instructors and peers, which ensures that theory and practice are seamlessly integrated [8, 9]. Furthermore, the OMP/FC combined model provides the flexibility to dynamically adjust teaching content according to the resident physicians’ knowledge level. In this study, the experimental group using the OMP/FC model outperformed the control group in both theoretical scores and practical abilities, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving learning efficiency and helping resident physicians adapt to clinical practice more quickly. Additionally, no significant differences were found in basic skill operations, suggesting that hands-on procedural training may require alternative instructional methods. Compared to individual teaching models, the OMP/FC combination better addresses the needs of resident physicians at various stages of learning, offering a novel approach to the refined management of medical education.

EPA evaluation results indicate that the OMP/FC model significantly enhances resident physicians’ clinical skills, such as history-taking, physical examination, and case documentation. Additionally, it fosters clinical thinking by utilizing problem-based learning and group discussions, helping resident physicians develop systematic and logical diagnostic approaches. This enhancement of clinical thinking is essential for resident physicians to independently manage complex cases in the future, preparing them for real-world medical challenges. While the FC model promotes autonomy by providing more opportunities for independent learning, the OMP model complements this by offering targeted guidance and real-time feedback during class. Together, these models create a closed-loop learning design that enhances student initiative and fosters teamwork through group activities. Research indicates that the OMP/FC model significantly boosts resident physicians’ interest, engagement, and overall abilities [10]. In addition to enhancing clinical skills and engagement, the model places a strong emphasis on developing critical thinking. Through guided questions and literature-based tasks, instructors stimulate deep thinking, helping resident physicians develop essential research skills. resident physicians in the experimental group also demonstrated improved self-directed learning abilities, gradually becoming more independent in their learning process. Furthermore, the learning experience of the residents is one of the key indicators for evaluating a teaching model. This study showed that the improvement in course content, classroom interaction, and teaching methods in the experimental group was not significantly greater than that in the control group, and may not have clinical significance. Additionally, there was no difference in “learning burden” between the two groups. Although the Flipped Classroom (FC) model requires students to engage in a certain amount of self-directed learning before class, its well-structured task design and classroom activity arrangements helped prevent an increase in learning pressure.

Although this study demonstrates that the OMP and FC teaching methods significantly enhance the clinical training outcomes of residents in the short term, it is essential to consider their potential long-term impact on clinical competence and patient outcomes. Early-stage training is well-documented as a critical factor in shaping a physician’s ability to perform independently in clinical practice. By enhancing fundamental skills such as clinical reasoning, decision-making, and diagnostic abilities, the OMP/FC model may establish a strong foundation for residents to confidently manage complex cases with greater expertise. Over time, this development can lead to improved patient care, as physicians with better clinical judgment are more likely to make accurate diagnoses, select appropriate treatments, and manage patients effectively. Furthermore, the OMP/FC model fosters key competencies such as critical thinking, problem-solving, and adaptability—skills that are essential for success in dynamic and fast-paced clinical environments. These attributes are vital for physicians to remain effective as they face new medical challenges and adapt to evolving treatment protocols throughout their careers. Thus, the improvements observed in short-term assessments could have a lasting effect on residents’ ability to provide high-quality care and make sound clinical decisions in a variety of patient scenarios.

An important consideration is the scalability of the combined OMP/FC approach. While this study was conducted in the context of general medicine training, the flexibility and adaptability of the OMP and FC methods make this model suitable for other specialties and educational settings. Both methods emphasize active learning, self-directed study, and direct feedback—principles that are relevant across all medical disciplines. Therefore, this model could be effectively applied in fields such as surgery, pediatrics, and emergency medicine, where clinical skills, critical thinking, and decision-making are similarly emphasized. The scalability of the OMP/FC approach is further supported by its versatility. The model can be tailored to the specific needs of different specialties, incorporating relevant content and case studies while maintaining core principles of interactive, learner-centered teaching. Moreover, this adaptability extends beyond individual institutions. Medical schools and teaching hospitals globally could integrate the OMP/FC model into their existing curricula, making it a valuable framework for improving clinical education and fostering long-term professional development in a wide range of educational contexts. Finally, although this study focused on a relatively small cohort of general medicine residents, future research could explore the broader applicability of the OMP/FC model through multicenter trials across various specialties and regions. Such studies would provide valuable insights into its effectiveness and feasibility in diverse educational settings and potentially pave the way for its widespread adoption.

While this study demonstrated significant teaching effects, it also has several limitations. Given that this study included only 80 residents and focused solely on general medicine rotations, its findings may lack broader applicability. To address this limitation, future studies should increase the sample size and expand the scope to include residents from various medical departments, which would help assess the broader applicability and scalability of the OMP/FC model. A significant limitation of this study is the absence of separate groups for OMP and FC, which prevents the isolation and quantification of each method’s individual impact. This makes it difficult to discern the specific contributions of each teaching method in the combined model. To address this gap, future research should introduce additional control groups (e.g., OMP-only, FC-only, or other alternative teaching models) to enable a more detailed and direct comparison of the effects of each approach. A further limitation is the study’s short observation period, during which teaching effectiveness was assessed only through exit exams and questionnaires. To overcome this limitation, future research should include longitudinal tracking to evaluate the OMP/FC model’s long-term impact on resident physicians’ professional development, clinical practice efficiency, and patient satisfaction.

In conclusion, the combined OMP/FC model has demonstrated significant teaching effectiveness in enhancing clinical skills and fostering critical thinking. However, to fully understand its long-term impact and potential across diverse medical contexts, further research with larger sample sizes, more diverse control groups, and extended observation periods is needed.
