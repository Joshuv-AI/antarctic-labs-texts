Integrative Omics Reveals the Metabolic Patterns During Oocyte Growth
Authors: Xiang Zhang, Juan Ge, Yue Wang, Minjian Chen, Xuejiang Guo, Shuai Zhu, Hui Wang, Qiang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWell-controlled metabolism is associated with high-quality oocytes and optimal development of a healthy embryo. However, the metabolic framework that controls mammalian oocyte growth remains unknown. In the present study, we comprehensively depict the temporal metabolic dynamics of mouse oocytes during in vivo growth through the integrated analysis of metabolomics and proteomics. Many novel metabolic features are discovered during this process. Of note, glycolysis is enhanced, and oxidative phosphorylation capacity is reduced in the growing oocytes, presenting a Warburg-like metabolic program. For nucleotide biosynthesis, the salvage pathway is markedly activated during oocyte growth, whereas the de novo pathway is evidently suppressed. Fatty acid synthesis and channeling into phosphoinositides are specifically elevated in oocytes accompanying primordial follicle activation; nevertheless, fatty acid oxidation is reduced in these oocytes simultaneously. Our data establish the metabolic landscape during in vivo oocyte growth and serve as a broad resource for probing mammalian oocyte metabolism.Keywords: metabolism, oocyte, proteomics, follicle, reproduction

In mammals, oogenesis is a complex and orderly process that involves mitosis and proliferation of oogonia, as well as growth and meiotic maturation of oocytes. Oogonia undergo meiosis and become primary oocytes, which then progress to meiotic prophase I and arrest at the diplotene stage around birth. After this period, oocyte development occurs in a follicle unit, which comprises the oocyte itself and granulosa cells that surround the oocyte (1, 2, 3). In mice, primordial follicle formation begins at embryonic day 17.5 and is completed within the first 3 days after birth (4). Then, oocytes remain in a quiescent state until receiving the appropriate hormonal cues to initiate growth and maturation (5, 6, 7). Between 5 and 6 days post-birth (P5-P6), primordial follicles are activated and transform into primary follicles, in which the oocytes begin to grow and synthesize the zona pellucida and the granulosa cells undergo a morphological shift from squamous to cuboidal (8). During the postnatal period of 10 to 12 days (P10-P12), a cohort of secondary follicles develops, characterized by oocytes that are encased in multiple layers of granulosa cells (9). By postnatal day 15 (P15), the oocytes are present in early antral follicles and acquire meiotic competence (10). Large antral follicles are present on postnatal day 21 (P21), which in the first wave of growth and development can progress to and arrest at metaphase II, with emission of the first polar body. Eventually, the mature oocyte is released during ovulation, ready for fertilization.

Metabolism plays an important role in female reproduction, particularly in providing the necessary energy and substrates for oocyte growth and maturation (11, 12). Over the past few decades, researchers have laid the foundations for our understanding of the metabolic dynamics during oocyte meiotic maturation and embryonic development across various species (13, 14, 15, 16). For instance, oocytes have a limited capacity to utilize glucose via glycolysis and rely on cumulus cells for nutrient supply (17, 18, 19). The activity of pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) influences meiotic resumption in cumulus-oocyte complexes (20, 21, 22). In the perinatal mouse ovary, the uptake of pyruvate to mitochondria is essential for early folliculogenesis via regulation of a TGF-β-related factor (23). However, to date, the global picture of metabolic patterns during mammalian oocyte growth has not been systematically characterized.

In the present study, to understand the developmental control of oogenesis, mouse oocytes at different corresponding stages of follicular growth were isolated in vivo and processed for trace-sample/low-input metabolomic and proteomic analysis. Through these analyses, a comprehensive metabolic landscape was established and an array of novel metabolic features was discovered, as reported below.

Experimental Procedures

Mouse

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC: 1703017) and were conducted in compliance with the rules and guidelines of the local animal ethical committee. Female ICR mice were obtained from the experimental animal center of Nanjing Medical University and were housed in ventilated cages. The environment was maintained at a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, room temperature (22 °C), and controlled humidity.

Collection of Mouse Oocytes

Oocyte collection was conducted based on methods detailed in previous studies (24). The process began with the isolation of follicles from ovarian tissue. Ovaries were collected from mice at different postnatal days (P2, P5, P10, P15, and P21). Each ovary was then placed in α-MEM medium (Gibco #12571048), supplemented with 1x penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco #15140122). For P2, P5, and P10 ovaries, mechanical dissection was performed using an insulin needle, followed by treatment with 1 mg/ml collagenase IV (Glpbio #GC19591) at 37 °C for 20 min. Then preantral follicles (primordial, primary, and secondary follicles) which have intact structures, were manually picked and cultured in 30-μL of α-MEM medium. Following isolation, these follicles were then treated with TrypLE Express enzyme (Gibco #12604021) at 37 °C for 20 min to release the oocytes. For the P15 and P21 ovaries, early antral and antral follicles (sized at 230 μm and 350 μm, respectively) were mechanically separated using fine watchmaker forceps and ruptured to collect cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs). The cumulus cells were then removed through repeated pipetting. The diameters of oocytes from different stages were recorded as follows: 18 μm for P2, 30 μm for P5, 45 μm for P10, 60 μm for P15, and 80 μm for P21. Different numbers of oocytes were collected for each stage. Specifically, 500 oocytes from primordial follicles constituted one sample, 42 oocytes from primary follicles constituted a single sample, 22 oocytes from secondary follicles were assembled as one sample, 10 oocytes were collected from each early antral follicle for a single sample, and eight oocytes from antral follicles were sampled. In this study, an average of 600 (P2), 50 (P5), 43 (P10), 36 (P15), and 30 (P21) oocytes were obtained from an ICR mouse, respectively. Finally, these samples were washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for the metabolomics and proteomics experiments.

Metabolomics

All samples were collected and transferred to Eppendorf tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C. Metabolite extraction was performed by resuspending samples in 60 μl of 80% methanol/water (volume/volume) followed by ultrasonication using a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode). After cooling on ice for 10 min, samples were centrifuged at 16,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected in new tubes, and the dried samples were stored at −80 °C until instrumental analysis. Metabolite profiling was performed on an UPLC Ultimate 3000 system (Dionex, Germering) coupled with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To prevent issues associated with the order of injection, each sample was randomly injected for analysis. Using a Hypersil GOLD C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the chromatographic separation was performed at a temperature of 40 °C. The separation of metabolites was achieved using a mobile phase consisting of 0.1% formic acid in ultra-pure water (Phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in pure acetonitrile (Phase B). The flow rate was set at 0.4 ml/min. Initially, there was a 3-min elution with 99% A (volume/volume), followed by a decrease in solvent A to 1% at t = 10 min. This 1% composition was maintained for 5 min (t = 15 min), and then rapidly increased to 99% for 2 min (t = 17 min). For mass spectrometric detection, a full-scan mode from 70 to 1050 m/z was employed at a resolution of 70,000 in both positive and negative ionization modes. TraceFinder (version 5.1) was utilized for processing the mass spectrometry raw data. Metabolites were identified by comparing their accurate mass and retention times against commercial standard compounds using the author-constructed library. This methodology was also applied to identify specific metabolites like serine, glycine, and SAM.

Sample Preparation for Proteomics

For the TMT 6-plex, the samples were incubated in 100% acetonitrile at 25 °C for 1 h, labeled by TMT reagents, stopped by 5% hydroxylamine, and pooled together. The peptides were then dried by a vacuum concentrator, and acidified by 0.1% formic acid for further LC-MS analysis. To construct a spectral library for DIA analysis, 1000 GV oocytes, 1000 MII oocytes, and 1000 zygotes were collected for protein extraction, reduction, alkylation, trypsin digestion, and desalting by an OASIS HLB 1 cc Vac cartridge (Waters). The purified peptides were separated into 12 fractions based on HP-RP fractionation technology using the ACQUITY UPLC M-class system (Waters) with a BEH C18 Column (300 μm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters). The oocytes from five developmental stages were collected for DIA-based protein quantification. Each developmental stage included six biological replicates. The oocytes were lysed and digested in a solution of 0.1% n-Dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM), 10 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), 25 mM cysteamine (CAA), 130 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), and 10 ng/μl trypsin at 37 °C for 5 h. The peptide digests were desalted by C18 StageTips and dried in a SpeedVac for further analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

For TMT6-plex analysis, precursor ions of peptides were scanned from 350 to 1500 m/z at a resolution of 60,000 with an ion injection time (IT) of 50 ms and a standard automatic gain control (AGC) target. During a cycle time of 1.5 s, precursor ions with > +2 charges were isolated with a window of 0.8 m/z. The isolated ions were fragmented by a higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) level of 36%, and the fragments were scanned in an Orbitrap at a resolution of 50,000. For DIA-MS, samples were sent to an LC column (75 μm i.d., 1.7 μm × 15 cm, USA) on an easy-nLC 1200 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a flow rate of 300 nl/min. A Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer was used for all analyses. The isolated ions between 350 to 1500 m/z were scanned for MS1 spectra with a resolution of 120,000, an Automatic Gain Control (AGC) target set at 250%, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Fragmentation of precursor ions was performed by an HCD level of 30%, with an AGC target of 100% and dynamic maximum injection time. Subsequently, the fragments were analyzed in an Orbitrap at a resolution of 30,000, employing an isolation window of m/z = 50 for the first two windows and m/z = 15 for the remaining.

Protein Identification and Quantification

The TMT raw files were analyzed by Proteome Discoverer (V1.3) with UniProt mouse proteome database (Jun 2011, 60,010 entries) with precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm, fragment ion mass tolerance of 20 mmu, Trypsin/P enzyme specificity, and two maximum missed cleavages sites. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residue was set as fixed modifications. Variable modifications included oxidation of methionine, TMT6-plex of protein N-termini, and the amino groups (-NH2) on the side chains of lysine residues. False discovery rate (FDR) q-values were controlled at 1% at peptide and protein group levels. The quantification of each channel relative to the baseline channel (Channel 126, representing the P2 samples) was determined by calculating the ratio of the median value of total protein intensities per channel to that of channel 126. The DIA raw files were analyzed with Spectronaut software (version 17.0) in library-based search mode using a hybrid library by combining the common library from UniProt mouse proteome database (Sept 2022, 55,315 entries) and DDA library of GV oocytes, MII oocytes and zygotes. The DDA results used for the generation of the spectral library are now provided in Supplemental Table S3. The mass tolerance for fragment ions for DIA data analysis was set as ± 40 ppm, the default parameter of the Spectronaut 17.0 software. Carbamidomethyl (C) was set as the fixed modification, and acetylation (Protein N-term) and oxidation(M) as variable modifications. Peptides with lengths of 7 to 52 amino acids were considered for the search, and the FDR q-values were controlled at 1% at precursor and protein group levels. The information on the proteins identified in the DIA analysis has been provided in Supplemental Table S4. Furthermore, proteins identified based on a single unique peptide are listed in Supplemental Table S5, and the annotated spectra have been exported and are provided in Supplemental Fig. S10.

Bioinformatic Analysis

For proteomic data analysis, we only considered oocyte samples identifying greater than 60% of the total number of identified proteins in all samples, resulting in the removal of one replicate of P21 oocytes. The quantitative values of proteins were log2 transformed and median normalized. The missing values were imputed using the imputePCA algorithm (R package embedded in R). The differences in abundances among different stages were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc test. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on oocytes across five developmental stages as well as heatmap analysis were performed to depict protein accumulation similarity among the samples. The heatmaps were produced using the ComplexHeatmap package (25), employing Z-score to standardize data across samples. KEGG pathway Enrichment Analysis of differentially accumulated proteins and metabolites was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (26).

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

Achieving a good egg requires a precisely regulated and well-timed metabolism. To depict the temporal metabolic dynamics of oocytes, low-input metabolomic and proteomic analyses were performed on mouse oocytes during in vivo growth (P2, P5, P10, P15, and P21). A total of six biological replicates were used for each stage. Due to the different sizes of growing oocytes, we first need to determine the number of oocytes used in each group. Due to the limited materials, it is difficult to accurately measure the amount of protein in each sample. So, we used TMT-based labels to tag oocyte peptides at different developmental stages. A ratio close to 1:1:1:1:1 indicates an equal protein amount in different samples. Meanwhile, to mitigate the impact of varied amounts of sample input, cross-run normalization was performed by spectronaut software. In the present study, 500 (P2), 42 (P5), 22 (P10), 10 (P15), and 8 (P21) oocytes were used for quantitative proteomics and metabolomic analysis in each replicate, respectively.

To identify differentially accumulated proteins, the statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc test. Proteins with a fold change ≥1.5 and a p-value <0.05 were considered significant. For metabolomic data analysis, OPLS-DA was performed using SIMCA-P (version 14.1). A VIP value > 1.00 and a p-value (Student's t test) < 0.05 were considered significant. Data are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Metabolomic and Proteomic Profiling of Mouse Oocyte Growth

Although the effects of metabolism on oocyte development have been widely implicated, a comprehensive investigation of metabolic signatures in growing oocytes is lacking. Oogenesis is closely associated with the development of follicles. Here, we isolated mouse oocytes from follicles at five key time points during growth (Fig. 1A). Specifically, non-growing oocytes were collected from primordial follicles in female mice on postnatal day 2 (termed P2 oocyte in this paper, 18 μm in diameter). Once the primordial follicles are formed, some oocytes are recruited to grow. Growing oocytes were retrieved from primary follicles on postnatal day 5 (termed P5 oocyte, 30 μm in diameter), secondary follicles on postnatal day 10 (termed P10 oocyte, 45 μm in diameter), and early antral follicles on postnatal day 15 (termed P15 oocyte, 60 μm in diameter), respectively. Fully grown immature oocytes were obtained from antral follicles on postnatal day 21 (termed P21 oocyte, 80 μm in diameter). A limited number of experimental materials have been a challenge for proteomic and metabolomic profiling of mammalian oocytes. Hence, the sensitive analytical techniques we developed recently have been employed in the present study. As the follicle grows, the oocyte experiences a dramatic increase in volume (27) and accumulates metabolites, proteins, and mRNA. To achieve consistent protein content across multiple groups, different numbers of oocytes were collected from each stage for the proteomics experiments (Fig. 1, A and B). Amine-reactive tandem mass tag 6-plex (TMT6-plex) was used to standardize protein levels, and more than 5000 proteins were quantified (Supplemental Fig. S1, A and B). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed good reproducibility of biological replicates (Supplemental Fig. S1C). The dynamics of differentially accumulated proteins (DAPs) during mouse oocyte growth are depicted as a heatmap (Fig. 1C). In total, 4287 DAPs were identified (Supplemental Table S1), and 656 of them are enzymes related to diverse metabolic pathways including glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and nucleotide synthesis, etc. (Fig. 1D).

Next, we conducted an analysis of intracellular metabolome using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS) (Fig. 1E). Five groups of oocytes were clearly separated according to robust orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) (Fig. 1F and Supplemental Fig. S2, A–D). Based on a Student's t test (p < 0.05) coupled with a variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis (VIP >1.00), a total of 155 differential metabolites were detected (Supplemental Table S2). Changes in metabolite level during oocyte growth are represented in Figure 1, G and H, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed the metabolic processes that were significantly altered. Collectively, these data provide a broad resource of metabolomic and proteomic profiles during in vivo oocyte growth.

Carbohydrate Metabolism During Oocyte Growth

Although carbohydrate metabolism plays an essential role in oogenesis (28), the dynamics of carbohydrates during oocyte growth remain largely unknown. From the temporal proteomic and metabolomic profiles (Fig. 1, D and G), we observed the dramatic alterations in metabolic pathways in growing oocytes, such as glycolysis, citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), etc., as detailed below.

Sustained Increase in Glycolysis During Oocyte Growth

Glycolysis is a series of metabolic processes in which glucose is broken down into pyruvate, producing ATP (29). Remarkably, our proteomic analysis showed that the levels of several key glycolysis-related enzymes were all elevated during oocyte growth (Fig. 2, A and B). For instance, a ∼4-fold-increase in phosphofructokinase-1 liver type (PFKL), a ∼9-fold-increase in glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and a ∼17-fold-increase in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate mutase (BPGM) were detected in oocytes from P2 to P21 (Fig. 2, C–G and Supplemental Fig. S3, A–C), indicative of the active glycolysis. Depending on the microcellular environment, pyruvate has separate fates. Under anaerobic conditions, the cytosolic enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) converts pyruvate to lactate, and thereupon oxidizes NADH to NAD+, which is essential for the continued ATP production (30, 31). Intriguingly, we found that LDH was dramatically accumulated (LDHB: ∼30-fold; LDHC: ∼35-fold) in growing oocytes (Fig. 2, H and I), implying the significant efficient anaerobic glycolysis.

As an important branch of glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) is essential for the production of ribonucleotides. It is worth noting that glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), the rate-limiting enzyme of PPP, exhibited a 60-fold increase during oocyte growth (Fig. 2J). The hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) serves as a glucose sensor and converts glucose into uridine 5′-diphosphate-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), which then acts as a substrate for O-glycosylation (Supplemental Fig. S4A). In striking contrast, HBP-related enzymes (GNPNAT1, PGM3, UAP1, OGT) we detected experienced the reduced accumulation in oocytes from P2 to P5 (Supplemental Fig. S4, B–E), suggesting the potential suppression of HBP pathway during primordial follicle activation.

Downregulated TCA Cycle and Oxidative Phosphorylation during Oocyte Growth

Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate can diffuse into mitochondria, where it enters the citric acid cycle and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation. The enzymes of TCA cycle are in the mitochondrial matrix, and the complexes of the electron transport chain are embedded within the inner mitochondrial membrane. Of note, our temporal proteomic profiles clearly showed that all TCA cycle enzymes (ACO2, SUCLA2, SDHA, MDH2, CS, LDH3, DIST, and FH1) we detected experienced consistent downregulation during the entire process of oocyte growth (Fig. 3, A–E and Supplemental Fig. S5, A–H). Furthermore, the abundance of isocitric acid, a TCA cycle intermediate, was decreased by around 74% in fully-grown oocytes compared to non-growing oocytes (Fig. 3F).

The OXPHOS is a key pathway involved in numerous cellular processes, including electron transport chain (ECT) and ATP synthase (Complex V). The ETC is a collection of membrane-embedded proteins and organic molecules, most of them organized into four large complexes labeled I to IV. NADH and FADH2 generated by the TCA cycle donate electrons to the ECT for OXPHOS, which pumps protons out across the mitochondrial inner membrane and generates a proton gradient. Protons enter the mitochondrial matrix through complex V, driving ATP synthesis (Fig. 3G). Similar to the TCA cycle, those enzymes involved in OXPHOS unexceptionally showed a significant reduction in growing oocytes from P2 to P21 (Fig. 3, H–P and Supplemental Fig. S6, A–Q). In particular, NADH dehydrogenase 3 (ND3) in complex I decreased by 73% (Fig. 3I), and cytochrome c-1 (CYC1) in complex III decreased by 67% (Fig. 3L) compared to non-growing oocytes. These results strongly suggest that the activity of the TCA cycle and OXPHOS was inhibited in growing oocytes.

The Warburg effect is characterized by a transition from oxidative phosphorylation to rapid glycolysis, accompanied by an increased rate of lactate production (32). Markedly, growing mouse oocytes displayed similar characteristic alterations in glucose metabolism (Figs. 2 and 3), metabolizing glucose into lactate instead of channeling glucose into the typical aerobic pathway that more efficiently captures energy. Hence, in the present study, we refer to such a specific glucose metabolism pattern during oocyte growth as a Warburg-like metabolic program.

Nucleotide Synthesis During Oocyte Growth

The biosynthesis of purine and pyrimidine nucleotides is carried out via two pathways: de novo synthesis from small molecules, and salvage pathways, which use existing purine or pyrimidine bases or nucleosides. Synthesis of nucleotides requires a source of ribose 5-phosphate, which is derived from glucose 6-phosphate through the pentose phosphate pathway. The de novo nucleotide biosynthesis begins with the activation of ribulose 5-phosphate to phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP), and ends with the production of inosine monophosphate (IMP). As shown in Figure 4, A–F and Supplemental Fig. S7, A–D, the accumulation of all enzymes (PPAT, GART, PFAS, PAICS, ADSL, ATIC, CAD, and UMPS) we detected in de novo pathway was gradually decreased during oocyte growth (P2-P21). Nevertheless, the abundance of three key metabolites of nucleotide synthesis underwent a dramatic increase (IMP: ∼11-fold; Inosine: ∼3-fold; UMP: ∼2-fold) in oocytes from P5 to P15 (Fig. 4, G–J), indicating the existence of an alternative synthetic pathway. In support of this notion, the content of metabolites in nucleotide salvage synthesis (i.e. CMP, xanthosine, and uracil) was increased in oocytes from P5 to P15 (Fig. 4, K–M). Moreover, proteomics data revealed that the levels of six enzymes (IMPDH2, NT5C2, GDA, UPP1, CTPS2, and NME3) involved in the salvage pathway were also elevated in oocytes from P5-P15 (Fig. 4, N–S). Collectively, these data indicate that nucleotide levels during oocyte growth are likely maintained by salvage pathways instead of by de novo synthesis.

Metabolic Characteristics of Amino Acid During Oocyte Growth

Amino acids are essential for protein synthesis, energy production, and epigenetic modiﬁcations (33, 34, 35). However, very little is known about the metabolic features of amino acids during oocyte growth. Through a combined metabolome and proteome analysis, our study reveals the diversity of amino acid metabolism in oocytes. Two metabolic pathways that significantly changed in growing oocytes, specifically from P5 to P15, were presented below.

Enhanced Catabolism of Arginine and Proline in Growing Oocytes

In mammals, arginine can be synthesized from citrulline in the urea cycle and then serves as a precursor for synthesis of proline and glutamate (Fig. 5A). Our metabolomic profiling identified a 1.5-3-fold increase in the content of citrulline, arginine, glutamate, and proline in oocytes from P5 to P15 (Fig. 5, B–E). Meanwhile, the key enzymes (ARG2, ALDH4A1, and PYCR1) responsible for arginine and proline metabolism were evidently accumulated in these oocytes (Fig. 5, F–H), indicative of the enhanced catabolism of arginine and proline accompanying oocyte growth.

Upregulated One-Carbon Metabolism in Growing Oocyte

One-carbon metabolism involves a network of linked pathways, including the crucial methionine and folate cycles, essential for cellular function (Fig. 5I). We found that in the methionine cycle and transsulfuration pathway, two key metabolites, s-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and cystathionine, were elevated in oocytes from P5 to P15 (Fig. 5, J and K), corresponding to the process of primary follicle development into early antral follicles. Furthermore, as important cellular antioxidants synthesized from cysteine in the transsulfuration pathway, the levels of glutathione and oxidized glutathione were upregulated during the oocyte growth (Fig. 5, L and M). Consistent with this observation, proteomic profiles showed the increased accumulation of enzymes (MAT1A, AHCYL1, and DHFR) related to the folate cycle, methionine cycle, and transsulfuration pathway in these oocytes (Fig. 5, N–P). The results imply the upregulated one-carbon metabolism in growing oocytes.

As a crucial output of one-carbon metabolism, SAM serves as the exclusive methyl donor for all methyltransferase reactions in mammalian cells. In the female germline, DNA methylation occurs in oocytes after the initiation of growth, and the most dramatic increase in DNA methylation level is observed during the midgrowth stage (24, 36). The process of DNA methylation is carried out by enzymes known as DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs). It is worth noting that temporal proteomic data showed that the accumulation of DNMT1 and DNMT3A exhibits about 300-fold and 3-fold increase during oocyte growth (Fig. 5, Q–R), respectively. Combined with the metabolite changes, our data provide an explanation for the epigenetic establishment in oogenesis from the metabolic perspective.

Lipid Metabolism in Oogenesis

Through the integrative analysis of temporal proteomic and metabolic profiles, we discovered several important features of lipid metabolism in oogenesis, specifically during primordial follicle activation (from P2 to P5), as reported below. Such a precisely regulated metabolic flux indicates the well-controlled function of lipid metabolism in oocyte development.

Enhanced Fatty Acid Biosynthesis and Phosphatidylinositol Metabolism in Oocyte During Follicle Activation

Fatty acids are key components of most cell membranes and serve as long-term energy storage compounds. Their biosynthesis follows an iterative pathway that two-carbon carboxylic acid building blocks that contribute to the stepwise elongation of precursors through numerous individual reactions (37). As key enzymes in fatty acid synthesis, acetyl-coenzyme A carboxylase beta (ACACB) and fatty acid synthase (FASN) showed a significant accumulation in oocytes from P2 to P5, implicating an increased demand for fatty acids during follicle activation (Fig. 6, A and B). In line with this observation, the enzymes in the long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase family (i.e. ACSL1, ACSL3, and ACSL4), responsible for activating fatty acids to generate long-chain fatty acyl CoA, were also significantly accumulated in similar stage oocytes (Fig. 6, C–E). Fatty acyl CoA, in combination with phosphatidic acid, could be utilized for the synthesis of CDP-diacyl-glycerol, eventually transformed into phosphoinositides (PPIns) (Fig. 6F). An increased level of glycerol was detected (Fig. 6G), which can be converted into phosphatidic acid through multiple reactions. As important lipid signaling molecules, PPIns (i.e. PI4P and PIP3) participate in regulating membrane trafficking and cell signaling in eukaryotic cells (38). Of note, our proteomic data revealed that those enzymes involved in phosphatidylinositol metabolism (GK, CDS1, PI4K2A, PIK3CD, and CDIPT) were all elevated in oocytes from P2 to P5 (Fig. 6, I–L and Supplemental Fig. S8A). Research has shown that increased PIP3 in oocytes could recruit and activate the phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1)/AKT pathway to stimulate follicle growth (39, 40). These data suggest that the enhanced synthesis and transformation of fatty acids into phosphoinositides in oocytes are probably the metabolic basis for primordial follicle activation.

Reduced Fatty Acid Beta Oxidation in Oocyte During Follicle Activation

Fatty acid beta oxidation (FAO) is a major pathway for the degradation of fatty acyl CoA (Fig. 6F). Carnitine palmitoyltransferase I transforms fatty acyl-CoAs into fatty acylcarnitines, which are then translocated to the inner mitochondrial membrane via a carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase. Subsequently, carnitine palmitoyltransferase II (CPTII) converts these fatty acylcarnitines back into fatty acyl-CoAs for beta-oxidation within the mitochondrial matrix. FAO has been considered to be important for oocyte maturation (41, 42); however, its dynamic changes during oocyte growth remain poorly understood. Notably, metabolome profiles identified a significant decline in carnitine levels in oocytes from P2 to P5 (Fig. 6H). Consistent with this phenomenon, proteomic data showed that the accumulation of eight key enzymes (CPT2, HADH, HADHB, HADHA, ACADL, ACADM, ACAA2, ACADVL) in FAO was markedly decreased (Fig. 6, M–R and Supplemental Fig. S8, B and C). These findings imply that the activity of FAO is suppressed in oocytes during follicle activation. In addition, we noticed that the abundance of steroid hormones underwent a remarkable increase (5-Androstenediol: ∼17-fold; Estrone: ∼4-fold; 17alpha-Estradiol: ∼2-fold; Estriol: ∼1.5-fold) in oocytes from P5 to P10 (Supplemental Fig. S9, A–E), indicating that the elevated steroid hormones might be crucial for oocyte development along with the transition from primary follicle to secondary follicle.

Discussion

Well-balanced and timed metabolism is required to make a good egg. Although recent studies have characterized the global metabolic patterns during oocyte maturation and early embryonic development (13, 15), how intracellular metabolites and metabolic pathways change in growing oocytes remains a longstanding unanswered question owing to the scarcity of materials. Advancements in MS technologies have enabled us to profile cellular proteomics and metabolomics with a limited number of oocyte samples.

In the present study, we isolated mouse oocytes in vivo at five key time points and investigated the global metabolic patterns of oocytes during growth. In parallel, we conducted metabolome studies to bolster the proteomic data. Each specific stage has its unique metabolic signature, reflecting that the physiological requirements for metabolites and enzymes vary greatly across different growth phases (Fig. 7). For instance, we observed the progressively increased accumulation of glycolysis-related enzymes during the entire process of oocyte growth (Fig. 2). Therefore, increased glycolysis and fermentation of glucose to lactate are likely to be critical energy resources for supporting oogenesis. On the contrary, the enzymes involved in the TCA cycle and OXPHOS exhibited a gradual decrease during oocyte growth (Fig. 3). It has been well accepted that, in contrast to normal cells, many cancer cells exhibit increased glycolysis and decreased oxidative phosphorylation capacity (Warburg effects, also known as aerobic glycolysis) (43, 44). What we observed in growing oocytes is very similar to this phenotype; thereupon, we call it “Warburg-like metabolic program” in this paper. Glycolysis is a major pathway that generates ATP molecules in the cytosol without the requirement of molecular oxygen. While oxidative phosphorylation produces more ATP compared to glycolysis, the latter generates ATP at a faster rate (45). This evidence may provide the potential explanation for why growing oocytes present the Warburg-like metabolic program. In addition, metabolites of glycolysis can also serve as substrates for generating NADPH and the building blocks needed for glycogen, lipid, nucleotide, and protein synthesis via other anabolic pathways (46). Glucose-6-phosphate can be channeled into the pentose phosphate pathway by G6PD, generating ribulose 5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis and NADPH. A marked increase in G6PD accumulation indicates the importance of the pentose phosphate pathway in oocyte growth. In sum, these data reveal the metabolic characteristics and utilization pathway of glucose during oogenesis. Nevertheless, the mechanistic regulation and developmental significance of these metabolic changes deserve future study.

Characteristics of global metabolic patterns during mouse oocyte growth. Diagram illustrating the dynamic changes in metabolites and enzymes during mouse oocyte growth.

Besides, we also discovered several novel metabolic features in growing oocytes, including (i) diminished de novo pathway (P2 to P21) and enhanced salvage pathway (P5 to P15) for nucleotides synthesis; (ii) active arginine and one-carbon metabolism (P5 to P15); and (iii) increased fatty acid synthesis and reduced fatty acid oxidation (P2 to P5). These metabolic kinetics not only lay a foundation for comparative studies of abnormal oocyte growth but also provide opportunities for predicting oocyte quality and improving the success rate of in vitro fertilization procedures. Studies have shown that DNA methylation levels increase with oocyte growth, with a slight increase observed before day 10, followed by significant increases until the GV stage (47). Between days 10 and 15, DNA methylation for imprinting genes is established in oocytes (48). In the current study, we observed that S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), an exclusive methyl donor for all methyltransferase reactions, was markedly elevated at the same time (Fig. 5J). Moreover, our proteomics analysis revealed that two key DNA methyltransferases, DNMT1 and DNMT3A, were also dramatically upregulated during this stage, consistent with the trend of changes in DNA methylation levels. These findings suggest that the upregulation of one-carbon metabolism is closely linked to DNA methylation. The simultaneous elevation of both the enzymes and metabolites involved in this pathway supports the hypothesis that metabolic changes provide a basis for epigenetic modifications during oocyte growth. A strength of our research is the delineation of in vivo oocyte metabolome landscape during growth. However, the full understanding of oocytes cannot be isolated from their surrounding granulosa cells and follicular microenvironment. The metabolic co-dependence of oocytes and granulosa cells is essential for their functional regulation and facilitation of oocyte maturation (16). Thus, metabolomic analysis of granulosa cells will be helpful for uncovering the metabolic network controlling oocyte growth. In addition, two important questions need to be addressed in the future: (i) the developmental significance of those altered metabolic pathways during oocyte growth (i.e. glycolysis/fatty acid utilization/nucleotide synthesis); (ii) the profound effects of metabolic perturbation in growing oocytes on the subsequent oocyte maturation and embryo development. The potential weaknesses of this study primarily include: (i) the total number of metabolites detected here is relatively small due to the use of low-input metabolomics analysis. To cover more metabolites involved in different pathways, an improved technique will be needed in the future study. (ii) conditional-knockout and/or transgenic animal model is necessary to dissect the metabolic control of distinct-stage oocyte growth.

Taken together, we established the metabolome and proteome profiles of mouse oocyte growth. The novel metabolic features discovered in the current study will drive further research on their potential physiological impact. In addition, these findings provide insights into the mechanisms governing oocyte development and fertility in women.
