Sexually explicit and violent media use among high school students in Vietnam: Gender-differentiated links with sexual misconduct victimization, perpetration, and health
Authors: Kathryn M Yount, Meghan Macaulay, Kim Tu Tran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:In lower-income countries, research is lacking on levels and associations of using sexually explicit, physically non-violent media (SEM) or sexually explicit, physically violent media (SVM) with well-being outcomes in high-school students.Objective:We tested theories of gendered aggression and moral incongruence to explain the associations of SEM use and/or SVM use versus non-use with sexual misconduct perpetration/victimization and other well-being outcomes among students attending three high schools in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.Method:712 students completed surveys about their general health, mental health, alcohol use, academic disengagement, sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization, beliefs about pro-violence peer norms, and any SEM/SVM use in the prior six months. Unadjusted and adjusted regression models were estimated.Results:Most reported SEM (47 %) or SVM (32 %) use. SVM use was higher among boys (39 %) than girls (27 %) (χ2 p = 0.01). Among boys, compared to non-users, users reported worse self-rated health (SEM acoef = 0.44 (0.10, 0.78); SVM acoef = 0.58 (0.23, 0.93)), higher alcohol use (SEM aOR = 2.42 (1.06, 5.58); SVM aOR = 2.44 (1.05, 5.62)), and higher sexual misconduct perpetration involving physical dating violence, stalking, or sexual violence (SVM aOR = 5.02 (1.39, 18.06)). Among girls, compared to non-users, SVM users reported higher sexual misconduct perpetration (aOR = 2.59 (1.13, 5.92)) and sexual violence victimization (aOR = 5.74 (1.81, 18.22)); SEM users reported higher sexual misconduct victimization involving physical dating violence, sexual harassment, stalking, or sexual violence (OR = 2.32 (1.33, 4.04)).Conclusion:Most high-school students in this study reported sexualized media use, and boys reported SVM use more often than girls. Corroborating gendered aggression theory, sexualized media use tended to predict violence perpetration among boys and victimization among girls. Corroborating moral incongruence theory, boys’ use was associated with worse self-rated health and alcohol use. Contextualized programs may reduce student access to common modalities of sexualized media use and educate school communities about the harms of use.Keywords: Academic disengagement, Adolescents, High school, Mental health, Peer norms, Perpetration, Sexually explicit/violent media, Sexual misconduct, Victimization, Vietnam

Introduction

Sexual misconduct encompasses “physical or non-physical conduct of a sexual nature, including sexual harassment, stalking, dating violence, and sexual violence in the absence of clear, knowing, affirmative consent” (Swartout et al., 2019, p. 9; emphasis added). With this definition, sexual violence refers to sexual behaviors involving contact without consent. Among middle- and high-school-aged students globally, sexual misconduct victimization, or exposure to an act of sexual misconduct committed by another person, is common, especially for girls (Woolweaver et al., 2024) and is associated with multiple adverse health and social outcomes into adulthood (Exner-Cortens et al., 2013). An understudied risk factor for sexual misconduct perpetration by one person against a victim is using sexually explicit media (SEM) or sexually violent media (SVM). Sexually explicit media depicts sexual intercourse, contact, or objectification, including “domination, degradation, subordination, or humiliation” most often of women (Rodenhizer and Edwards, 2019) and other vulnerable groups (Burnay et al., 2022). Thus, SEM includes sexualized media that depicts individuals attempting to inflict or inflicting psychological but not physical harm on others. Sexually violent media (SVM) is SEM that also depicts individuals attempting to inflict or inflicting physical harm on others (Burnay et al., 2022). Thus, SVM is a discrete form of SEM that involves the attempted or actual perpetration of physical harm against others.

Meta-analyses have found higher rape-myth acceptance with SEM use and SVM use, and higher aggression with SEM use (r = 0.15 [0.13, 0.17]) and especially SVM use (r = 0.25 [0.19, 0.31]) (Burnay et al., 2022). Yet, meta-analyses have included mostly male samples from higher-income Western settings (Burnay et al., 2022; Hedrick, 2021). Thus, our understanding of potential gender-differentiated effects of SEM use and SVM use, as well as use patterns and effects among young people in non-Western settings, remains limited.

Research on the effects of SEM use and SVM use in young people is especially rare in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Bergenfeld et al., 2022; Burnay et al., 2022; Hedrick, 2021). In Vietnam, undergraduate men have reported high rates of any sexualized media use (86 %), any SVM use (41 %), and at least monthly SVM use (17 %) in the prior six months (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Men reporting any or at least monthly SVM use have had 2-4 times higher adjusted odds than their counterparts of sexually violent behavior (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Similar research among high-school students is even less common in LMICs. Among eleventh and twelfth graders in three high schools in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, a majority (55 %) have experienced some form of sexual misconduct victimization since attending high school (Tran et al., 2025); yet, the relationship of SEM use and SVM use with sexual misconduct victimization, perpetration, and other well-being outcomes has not been assessed.

Here, we analyzed data from the above-mentioned study of students in Ho Chi Minh City to test theories of gendered aggression and moral incongruence to explain associations of SEM use and SVM use with sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration; self-reported health and risk behaviors; academic disengagement; and beliefs that peers accept violence against women. Findings may provide insights about the kinds of interventions in high schools that may be needed to address the gender-differentiated harms of SEM use and SVM use among young people in Vietnam.

Background

Sexual misconduct victimization and well-being among young people

A global systematic review of sexual misconduct in middle- and high-school-aged students found that prevalence rates were higher among female students than male students for specific forms of sexual harassment victimization (9 %–47 % versus 12 %–35 %), sexual assault victimization (7 %–12 % versus 2 %–6 %), any dating violence victimization (21 % versus 10 %), physical dating violence victimization (11 %–30 % versus 14 %), and sexual dating violence victimization (8 % versus 3 %) (Woolweaver et al., 2024). Exposure to sexual misconduct victimization at this age has been associated with poorer mental health, self-harm and suicide ideation and attempts, drug and alcohol use, aggression and violence, poor sexual health, and poor academic performance, including academic disengagement (Exner-Cortens et al., 2013; Woolweaver et al., 2024).

Effects of sexualized media use on sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization: gendered aggression theory

An understudied risk factor for sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration is the use of SEM and/or SVM. Mimicry, sexual scripts, social cognition, and cultivation theories are relevant frameworks (Anderson et al., 2024; Huesmann, 2007). Mimicry refers to imitating behaviors, including those seen in media (Huesmann, 2007). Sexual script theory asserts that individuals model their sexual expectations, norms, desires, and actions after portrayals of sex in their culture, such as in media (Wiederman, 2015). Social cognitive theory employs the concept of observational learning (Bandura, 2001), including about sexual activity and violence from media (Huesmann, 2007). Young people routinely employ observational learning (Fryling et al., 2011), including in cases of observed violence (Vazsonyi et al., 2018)4 Observational learning often occurs over time, with enactment continuing beyond the observed event (Fryling et al., 2011), Cultivation theory, also applied to media-violence research (Hedrick, 2021), posits that greater and repeated use of media contributes to greater acceptance of the norms and beliefs it conveys (Morgan et al., 2014). The repeated observation of arousing and violent content can cause desensitization, diminishing negative affect when viewing or enacting violence (Huesmann, 2007).

General aggression theory integrates the above theories into a meta-theory for understanding aggressive behavior (Anderson and Bushman, 2018). Accordingly, behavior is based on the learning, activation, and application of related knowledge structures, such as scripts stored in memory that may be learned from observing characters in media (Bandura et al., 1963). Once learned, the script may guide the behavior of individuals when similar situations are encountered in daily life. The individual first selects a script from memory to represent the situation encountered, assumes a role in the script, and behaves accordingly (Schank and Abelson, 1977). As knowledge structures are practiced, they may become more complex, differentiated, and resistant to change.

A gendered interpretation of this general aggression meta-theory links SEM use and SVM use with higher risks of sexual misconduct perpetration by men and higher risks of sexual misconduct victimization among women. Specifically, media contain many reinforcing scripts of how men should treat women. In “mainstream pornography” in the U.S., sex scenes often portray verbal (49 %) and physical (88 %) aggression, usually by men against women (Bridges et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2008). As such, viewing SEM and SVM may lead the user to objectify women and accept violence against them (Wright et al., 2016). When sexual scripts in media largely depict men’s objectification of and verbal aggression toward women (in SEM), men’s physical aggression toward women (in SVM), and women’s subordination to men (in SEM and SVM), a gendered aggression model would predict that: men who repeatedly use such content risk becoming more habitual perpetrators, and women who repeatedly use such content risk becoming more habitual victims (Tomaszewska and Krahé, 2018) because both users tend to assume the role of their gender in the script.

Evidence for this hypothesis remains inconclusive. In a convenience sample of mainly white college students in the U.S., SEM-use frequency was positively associated with aggressor, target, and uncommon/degrading sexual behavior for men and women (Bridges et al., 2016). In meta-analyses, SEM use and SVM use have shown positive effects on rape-myth acceptance (Burnay et al., 2022; Hedrick, 2021) and aggression (SEM use r = 0.15 (0.13, 0.17); SVM use r = 0.25 (0.19, 0.31)) and similar effect sizes across gender, age, and student/non-student samples (Burnay et al., 2022). Yet, these analyses could not test for the gender-differentiated effects of SEM use and SVM use on aggression due to insufficient female samples. These analyses also could not generalize findings globally because most studies came from higher-income, Western settings (Burnay et al., 2022).

Effects of sexualized media use on health: moral incongruence theory

Other literature has examined the effects of sexualized media use on broader measures of adolescent psychosocial well-being (Short et al., 2012). One consistent finding is that adolescent users of sexualized media more often exhibit emotional problems (Alexandraki et al., 2018). On the one hand, adolescents suffering from emotional problems may use sexualized media as a coping strategy; on the other hand, an adolescent’s use of sexualized media may contribute to their psychological distress (Perry, 2018). Extending the second argument, the link between sexualized media use and emotional problems may depend on moral congruence, or the extent of alignment between a person’s moral beliefs about sexualized media and the person’s media use behavior (Perry, 2018). In the aggregate, the use of SEM and SVM has risen more quickly than approval of their use, and younger users may experience moral incongruence, or misalignment between their moral beliefs about SEM and SVM and their use behavior. The strength of the association between SEM use and SVM use with psychosocial well-being may depend on the extent of the user’s cognitive dissonance—or the psychological distress that the user feels when their moral beliefs do not align with their actions. Social values about what is “good” or “bad” also may influence the stress-formation process because these values define how actors interpret their own behavior (Pearlin, 1989). This theory and associated research comes mostly from higher-income Western countries, and to our knowledge, the theory has not been tested among adolescents in LMICs.

Relevance of gendered aggression and moral incongruence theories in Vietnam

Gendered aggression theory for the effects of SEM use and SVM use may be salient in Vietnam, where historical and prevailing gender norms may reinforce gendered sexual scripts in media. In Vietnam’s vertical patrilineal kinship system, descent is traced through fathers (Rydstrøm, 2003), men often are viewed as superior to women (Rydstrøm, 2003), and senior men are socially defined as “pillars” of the household (Rydstrøm, 2003). Confucianism aligns femininity with compliance and endurance to uphold family harmony; whereas, Confucianism aligns masculinity with “hot anger” and violence (Rydstrøm, 2003). Gendered scripts in mainstream and sexualized media reinforce these gender norms. For example, 25 % of 400 news-media samples more often have depicted women in stereotypically domestic roles and more often have depicted women as victims and men as perpetrators of domestic violence (Nguyet, 2022). Seventy studies in Asia, including three in Vietnam, have addressed the phenomenon of deep-fake porn, in which the creators use artificial intelligence to overlay mostly women’s faces onto sexually explicit or violent images or videos (Bansal et al., 2024). Increases in problematic internet use among 18-25 year-olds in recent years may have increased exposure to these gendered sexual scripts (Gjoneska et al., 2022). Thus, observing gendered scripts in daily life and gendered sexual scripts in various media may increase a male viewer’s tendency to perpetrate sexual misconduct and a female viewer’s tendency to be victimized (Bergenfeld et al., 2020).

Moral incongruence theory of sexualized media use and personal health also may be relevant in Vietnam. In this context, the State has defined commercial sex a social evil, and many parents have defined teenage sexuality as a social evil (Do et al., 2017). Amidst these norms, premarital sex and the use of sexualized media have been increasing among young people (Ghuman et al., 2006). Consequently, young people are interpreting their own behavior amidst parental expectations of premarital chastity, especially for girls (Do et al., 2017; Bergenfeld et al., 2021), peer expectations for sex, and prevailing norms of feminine obedience, masculine dominance, and men’s entitlement to sex (Bergenfeld et al., 2020, 2021). A young person’s interpretation of their behavior amidst these complex social value systems may contribute to stress among SEM users and SVM users, leading to poorer well-being, especially among boys, who are the more frequent users of sexualized media (Alexandraki et al., 2018).

First-year undergraduate men in northern Vietnam report high rates of any SEM or SVM use (86 %), any SVM use (41 %), and SVM use at least monthly (17 %) in a six-month period (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Men exposed to any or at least monthly SVM in that period have had 2-4 times higher adjusted odds than their counterparts of engaging in sexually violent behavior (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Among eleventh and twelfth-graders attending three high schools in Ho Chi Minh City, 55 % have reported any sexual misconduct victimization since starting high school (Tran et al., 2025). During that period, the prevalence rates of sexual harassment victimization perpetrated by students was 34 %., stalking victimization was 18 %, and physical or emotional dating violence victimization was 13 % (Tran et al., 2025). Nearly one in ten students reported any sexual violence victimization. Boys reported more often than girls unwanted sexual attention (19 % versus 11 %) and sexual coercion (10 % versus 5 %) by staff. Girls reported more often than boys physical and emotional dating violence victimization (15 % versus 10 %). The authors did not test the gender-differentiated effects of SEM use and SVM use on sexual misconduct victimization, perpetration, or broader psychosocial well-being.

Hypotheses

Using novel data from high school students in Ho Chi Minh City, we tested the associations of SEM use and SVM use with sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration, self-rated general health and mental health, alcohol use, academic disengagement, and perceptions that peers were accepting of violence against women. According to general aggression theory, SEM use and SVM use would be associated with:

higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration in the pooled sample.

higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration in the pooled sample.

According to gendered aggression theory, SEM use and SVM use would be associated with:

higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence victimization among girls and higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration among boys.

higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence victimization among girls and higher sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration among boys.

According to moral incongruence theory, SEM use and SVM use would be associated with:

poorer self-rated health, poorer mental health, lower quality of life, higher academic disengagement, and alcohol use;

poorer self-rated health, poorer mental health, lower quality of life, higher academic disengagement, and alcohol use;

especially among boys, being more common users of sexualized media; and

especially among boys, being more common users of sexualized media; and

especially when beliefs are stronger that peers are accepting of violence against women, as such beliefs are thought to heighten the cognitive dissonance between personal values and sexualized media use.

especially when beliefs are stronger that peers are accepting of violence against women, as such beliefs are thought to heighten the cognitive dissonance between personal values and sexualized media use.

Method

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (821/DHYD-HDDD).

Sample

Three high schools in Ho Chi Minh City were selected randomly from high-, middle-, and low-ranked school strata, defined by students’ scores on the national entrance test. This selection ensured a demographically diverse sample of students. Eleventh and twelfth graders attending selected high schools were eligible to ensure that exposures and outcomes covered the periods of high-school attendance only.

Data collection

The questionnaire included modules from the GlobalConsent efficacy trial (Yount et al., 2020) and the Administrative Researcher Campus Climate Collaborative (ARC3) survey (Swartout et al., 2019). The GlobalConsent online survey included nine modules with scales validated in Western populations that were translated, cognitively tested, adapted, and validated among undergraduate men in Vietnam (Yount et al., 2020, 2022, 2023). Fit statistics and loadings from factor analyses for all scales used in the GlobalConsent survey are detailed elsewhere (Yount et al., 2022), and all study forms are publicly available in the published study protocol (Yount et al., 2020). The GlobalConsent survey module on media use (Bergenfeld et al., 2022; Yount et al., 2023), adapted from the Kids Online survey (Livingstone et al., 2011), provided the exposure measures for the present study, and findings on sexualized media use from participants in the efficacy trial are detailed elsewhere (Bergenfeld et al., 2022).

The ARC3 survey, a free campus-climate survey designed by sexual assault researchers and administrators for U.S. institutions of higher education, assesses forms of sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration identified as Title IX violations. The survey includes 19 modules covering types of sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration since enrollment, climate-related factors, and possible outcomes of sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration (Swartout et al., 2019). Included measures have shown strong psychometric properties in prior research, ARC3 pilot research, and research across seven U.S. universities (Swartout et al., 2019; Tilley et al., 2020). Across the seven universities, internal consistency exceeded 0.70 for almost all item sets, loadings from exploratory factor analyses exceeded 0.30 with few cross-loadings, and model fit statistics for confirmatory factor analyses of most item sets were acceptable (SRMR<0.06; CFI>0.95) (Tilley et al., 2020). The ARC3 survey provided the outcome measures for the present study. Local experts reviewed these modules for feasibility and suitability with high-school students in Vietnam.

The final questionnaire included 20 modules (Appendix A Supplementary data). A demographics module included questions on sex assigned at birth, gender identity, sexual orientation, ethnicity, age, grade, organizational membership, and living arrangements (Swartout et al., 2019; Yount et al., 2023). The sexualized media module asked about the frequency of using five types of sexualized media (text-based, partial nudity, full nudity, non-violent sexual acts, violent sexual acts) through four types of modalities (internet, TV/movie/DVD, book/-magazine, social media) (Yount et al., 2023). The outcomes modules included measures of sexual harassment victimization by faculty/staff and students (Fitzgerald et al., 1999), stalking victimization and perpetration (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2011), dating violence victimization and perpetration (Hamby, 2014; Smith et al., 1995), sexual violence victimization and perpetration (Koss et al., 1987), perceived peer norms about sex, dating violence, and sexual violence (DeKeseredy and Kelly, 1995), self-rated health and general mental health (Ware and Sherbourne, 1992), satisfaction with life (Diener et al., 1985), and academic disengagement (Hanisch and Hulin, 1990; Ramos, 2000). The questions on sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration, which may be more sensitive, were asked in later modules.

The team developed an online survey form using REDCap (Harris et al., 2009). REDCap is a customizable data system that handles the secure (encrypted) collection, transfer, storage, and analysis of project data. The team has successfully developed and implemented a customized REDCap data system for the web-based collection, transfer, and storage of data on knowledge, attitudes, and sexually violent behavior from 18-year-old undergraduate men in Vietnam (Yount et al., 2023).

Parents were invited to provide written informed consent for their children to participate. Students were invited to provide written informed assent to participate. Assenting students whose parents had consented convened in classrooms and received a link to the online survey through their mobile phones with 3G connectivity. The survey was anonymous, meaning no identifiers were collected, and a randomly generated identification number was assigned to each participant. Two non-study staff members were present in the classroom to assist participants with any technological issues that students encountered while entering and completing the survey. The survey-link address was altered after each data collection to prevent students from re-accessing the data system. Students who completed the survey received the equivalent of a $3 voucher in Vietnamese dong, the local currency.

Outcomes

Self-rated health was measured with one question about perceived health (0 = Excellent; 1 = Above Average; 2 = Average; 3 = Fair; 4 = Bad), such that a higher score denoted poorer self-rated health. Frequency of depression or anxiety symptoms was measured as the mean frequency (0 = Never; 1 = Sometimes; 2 = A few times; 3 = Most of the time; 4 = Always) in the prior four weeks of experiencing five emotional states, including feeling calm and peaceful, nervous, down and nothing could cheer them up, down-hearted and blue, and happy (α = 0.71 in this sample). Positively valenced items were reverse-coded so that a higher combined score denoted a higher mean frequency of experiencing five negative symptoms. For example, a mean of 2.5 for a participant would indicate that the participant experienced negative symptoms, on average, between a few times and most of the time in the prior four weeks. Self-reported satisfaction with life was measured with one question about perceived satisfaction with life (0 = Strongly Agree; 1 = Agree; 2 = Neutral; 3 = Disagree; 4 = Strongly Disagree), such that a higher score denoted lower life satisfaction. Alcohol consumption was measured originally as the frequency (1 = Never; 2 = Monthly or less; 3 = 2–4 times a month; 4 = 2–3 times a week; 5 = 4 or more times a week) at the time of the survey of consuming alcohol. Due to the skewed nature of the responses to the original measure (Never = 65.2 %; Monthly or less = 25.1 %; 2–4 times a month = 7.2 %; 2–3 times a week = 1.8 %; 4 or more times a week = 0.4 %), response options were recoded to capture any (=1) versus no (=0) alcohol consumption. Academic disengagement, an important measure of psychosocial well-being among high-school students,2 was measured as the mean frequency in the prior semester (0 = Almost Never; 1 = Seldom; 2 = Sometimes; 3 = Frequently; 4 = Almost Always) of eight behaviors: skipping class, making an excuse to leave class, being late for school, poor academic performance, coming to class drunk or high, falling asleep in class, thinking about dropping out of a class, and thinking about dropping out of school (α = 0.81 in this sample). A higher combined score denoted a higher mean frequency of academic disengagement. For example, a mean of 3.0 for a participant would indicate frequent academic disengagement, across all behaviors measured, in the prior semester.

Sexual misconduct perpetration measured reports of having ever (versus never) committed, since attending high school, any of six physical dating violence items (e.g., “I hit the person”), any of 10 stalking items (e.g., “made unwanted calls to them”), or any of 25 act-by-tactic sexually violent behaviors (e.g., “had oral sex with someone … without their consent”) (α = 0.97 in this sample). Thus, sexual misconduct perpetration was a binary composite measure of any dating-violence, stalking, or sexually violent behavior. Sexual violence perpetration measured reports of having ever (versus never) committed any of the 25 act-by-tactic sexually violent behaviors since attending high school (α = 0.98 in this sample). Thus, sexual violence perpetration was a binary composite measure of a subset of sexual misconduct perpetration behaviors.

Sexual misconduct victimization measured reports of having ever (versus never) experienced, since attending high school, any of 16 sexual harassment items by faculty/staff or students (e.g., “treated you differently because of your sex”), any of six physical dating violence items (e.g., “hit you”), any of 10 stalking items (e.g., “made unwanted phone calls to you”), or any of 25 act-by-tactic sexual violence items (e.g., “had oral sex with you … without your consent”) (α = 0.98 in this sample). Thus, sexual misconduct victimization was a binary composite measure of any sexually harassing, physical dating-violence, stalking, or sexually violent behaviors. Sexual violence victimization measured reports of having ever (versus never) experienced any of the 25 act-by-tactic items on sexual violence since attending high school (α = 0.96 in this sample). Thus, sexual violence victimization was a binary composite measure of a subset of sexual misconduct victimization experiences.

Exposure

The use of sexualized media was measured as any use in the prior six months of three mutually exclusive categories of media: neither SEM nor SVM (=0), any SEM (=1), or any SVM (=2). SVM was defined as “images or video of a woman performing a sex act in which she was choked, hit, humiliated, or forced.”

Moderator

The moderator variable was beliefs that peers approved of violence against women. It was measured as the mean response to seven questions about the participant’s level of agreement (0 = Strongly Disagree; 1 = Disagree; 2 = Neutral; 3 = Agree; 4 = Strongly Agree) that his or her friends would approve of 12 behaviors: having many sexual partners, telling stories about sexual experiences, getting someone drunk or high to have sex with them, lying to someone to have sex with them, forcing someone to have sex, using physical force such as beating to resolve conflicts with dates, insulting or swearing at dates, hitting a date in certain situations, expecting sex from a date when you want, expecting sex from a date when you spend money on them, insulting or putting a date down when they challenge your authority, and physically forcing a person to have sex under certain conditions (α = 0.89 in this sample). Higher scores denoted higher perceived peer support for risky and violent sexual behaviors. For example, a mean of 1.5 would indicate that the participant, on average across the item set, disagreed with or had a neutral opinion about their peers’ support for risky and violent sexual behaviors.

Covariates

Several covariates were considered potential confounders of the relationships of primary interest and were controlled for in adjusted regression models. These covariates included school ranking (low [reference], middle, high), grade level (eleventh [reference], twelfth), gender identity (female [reference], male), sexual orientation (heterosexual [reference], non-heterosexual), living situation (without parents [reference], with parents), feeling safe on campus (agree [reference]; neutral; disagree), and perceived school climate (likely [reference]; neutral; unlikely to support victims).

Analysis

As a first step, we performed descriptive analysis to understand the unweighted distributions of outcomes, sexualized media use, and covariates to explore potential confounders of the relationships of interest. For covariates, we examined their distributions in the pooled sample and tested for differences in their distributions across media-use groups (none, any SEM, any SVM). For categorical covariates, we computed and reported the exact p-value for the χ2 test statistic for differences in their distributions across media-use groups. For continuous outcomes measured as the mean response across an item set, we assessed the internal consistency of the items, or the alpha reliability, for the full sample (See the Outcomes section of the Methods.). For internally consistent item sets, we estimated the mean score (or average response) across the items for each participant. In the pooled sample, we estimated mean scores for continuous outcome measures and prevalence rates for binary outcome measures. For continuous outcomes, we performed bivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test for differences in mean scores across media-use groups. For binary outcomes (alcohol use), we computed the χ2 statistic to test for differences in the prevalence rates of binary outcomes across media-use groups. Differences across sub-groups with p-value less than 0.05 were considered significant and were reported as such in the text, and readers are referred to the Tables for all exact p-values.

As a second step in the analysis, we estimated linear regressions for continuous outcomes and logistic regressions for binary outcomes to test their associations with SEM use and SVM use exposures, relative to no use in the prior six months. For each outcome, we estimated an unadjusted bivariate model and a multivariable model adjusted for all covariates, described above, for the full sample, female-only sample, and male-only sample (Model fit statistics for each model are available upon request.). For media-use exposures, unadjusted and adjusted coefficients for linear regressions and unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios for logistic regressions were presented with their 95 % confidence intervals.

To test general aggression theory (hypothesis 1), lower confidence bounds >1.00 for adjusted odds ratios denoted significant associations of SEM use and separately SVM use with sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration and victimization in the pooled sample. To test gendered aggression theory (hypothesis 2), lower confidence bounds >1.00 for adjusted odds ratios denoted significant associations of SEM use and separately SVM use with sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence victimization in the female sample and with sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence perpetration in the male sample.

To test moral incongruence theory (hypotheses 3–5), lower confidence bounds >0.00 for adjusted coefficients and >1.00 for adjusted odds ratios denoted significant associations of SEM use and separately SVM use with health outcomes (hypotheses 3–4). Finally, to test hypothesis 5, we added a measure for participants’ beliefs about peer approval of violence against women, a beliefs-by-SEM-use interaction term, and a beliefs-by-SVM-use interaction term to all adjusted regression models for health outcomes. Lower confidence bounds >0.00 for the coefficients of the interaction terms in the pooled sample and the male-only sample provided tests for hypothesis 5.

For all analyses, point estimates were provided in the text, and significant differences across groups and associations of SEM use and SVM use that met the criteria for significance, as defined above, were denoted as such in the text. Readers are referred to the Tables for exact p-values and confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp, 2023).

Results

Participation rate

Of 1012 eligible students from grades 11 and 12 across the three high schools, 754 students had consent from parents and assented to participate in the study, for an initial participation rate of 74.5 %. Of assenting participants, 712 or 94.4 % had complete data for outcomes of interest, for a final participation rate of 70.4 % for this analysis.

Sample characteristics

Approximately 20.5 % of participants had not used either SEM or SVM in the prior six months (Table 1). Just under half (47.3 %) had used SEM, and almost one third (32.2 %) had used SVM in that period. Among female students, 24.0 % had not used either SEM or SVM in the prior six months, 48.7 % had used SEM, and 27.3 % had used SVM. Among male students, 15.4 % had not used either SEM or SVM in the prior six months, 45.5 % had used SEM, and 39.2 % had used SVM. The pooled sample was distributed evenly across the three schools (See Table 1 for exact p-value); however, students attending the lower-ranked school were significantly more prevalent among non-users (37.0 %) and SVM users (40.6 %) than among SEM users (26.7 %) in the prior six months. The sample was approximately evenly distributed across grades (46.6 % eleventh graders; 53.4 % twelfth graders), and this distribution did not differ significantly across media-user groups. Overall, a higher percentage of participants identified as female (59.1 %) than as male (40.2 %), and females were significantly less prevalent among participants who had used SVM (50.2 %) than among those who had not used sexualized media (69.2 %) or had used SEM (60.8 %) in the prior six months. About 21.9 % of the sample self-identified as non-heterosexual (gay, lesbian, bisexual, asexual, pansexual, queer, or other orientation), and non-heterosexual participants were significantly more prevalent among SVM users (25.8 %) and SEM users (22.9 %) than among non-users (13.7 %) in the prior six months. Living without parents was rare overall (6.5 %), and this percentage did not differ significantly across media-user groups. A minority (41.0 %) of participants agreed that they felt safe on campus, and this percentage did not differ significantly across all media-user groups; however, SVM users agreed significantly less often than SEM users with feeling safe on campus (35.8 % versus 44.5 %). A majority of participants felt it was likely that their school would support victims of sexual misconduct; however, the percentage of participants thinking school support for victims was unlikely was significantly higher among SVM users (3.9 %) than among SEM users (0.9 %) and non-users (0.7 %). As the distributions of most covariates differed significantly across sexualized media use groups and were potential confounders of the associations of SEM use and SVM use with outcomes of interest, we retained all covariates for inclusion in adjusted regression models.

Social and health status by media use

In the pooled sample, mean self-rated health at the time of the survey was 1.65 or “above average” (response options 0–4, with a higher score indicating worse self-rated health). Mean self-rated health did not differ significantly by media-use in the prior six months (See Table 2 for estimates and exact p-value). On average, participants “agreed” or were “neutral” about their life satisfaction (mean 1.71; possible range 0–4, with a higher mean score denoting lower perceived satisfaction with life). However, life satisfaction was significantly closer to “neutral” among SVM users (mean 1.85) than SEM users and non-users. On average, participants “rarely” or “sometimes” experienced symptoms of anxiety or depression in the prior four weeks (mean 1.75; possible range 0–4, with a higher mean score denoting a higher mean frequency of experiencing symptoms); however, the mean frequency of negative symptoms was significantly closer to “a few times” in the prior four weeks among SVM users (mean 1.84) than among SEM users. Over one-third (34.6 %) of participants reported consuming any alcohol, and alcohol use was significantly higher among SVM users than non-users (SVM users: 41.2 %; SEM users: 33.3 %; non-users: 27.6 %).

The mean score for academic disengagement was 0.85 or “seldom” overall (possible score 0–4, with a higher score denoting a higher mean frequency of academic disengagement). However, the mean frequency of academic disengagement behaviors was significantly higher among SVM users (0.94) than among SEM users (0.82) or non-users (0.78). Similarly, participants tended to disagree with statements reflecting pro-violence peer norms (mean score 0.57 on a 0–4 scale, with higher scores indicating higher perceived peer support for risky and violent sexual behaviors); yet, the mean score for beliefs in pro-violence peer norms was significantly higher among SVM users (0.68) than among SEM users (0.52) and non-users (0.51).

The prevalence of any sexual misconduct perpetration was 18.0 % overall and was significantly higher among SVM users (26.2 %), followed by SEM users (16.3 %) and non-users (8.9 %). The prevalence rate of sexual violence perpetration was 4.8 % overall and was significantly higher among SVM users (10.5 %) than among SEM users (2.7 %) and non-users (0.7 %). The prevalence of sexual misconduct victimization was 66.4 % overall and was significantly higher among SEM users (70.1 %) and SVM users (68.8 %) than among non-users (52.2 %). The prevalence rate of sexual violence victimization was 10.1 % overall and was significantly higher among SVM users (18.0 %) than among SEM users (8.0 %) and non-users (2.8 %).

Regression results

In the pooled sample, neither SEM use nor SVM use in the prior six months was significantly associated with self-rated health or the frequency of symptoms of anxiety and depression (Table 3). The significant unadjusted association of SVM use with poorer life satisfaction (coef. = 0.24 (0.04, 0.45)) was fully attenuated in adjusted models (Table 3). Compared to SEM/SVM non-use in the prior six months, SVM use was associated with 1.8 times higher odds of any alcohol use (uOR = 1.84 (1.17, 2.89); aOR = 1.77 (1.10, 2.84)) and more frequent academic disengagement (coef. = 0.16 (0.03, 0.29); coef. = 0.13 (0.002, 0.26)). The significant unadjusted association of prior SVM use with beliefs in pro-violence peer norms (coef. = 0.16 (0.04, 0.29)) was fully attenuated in adjusted models (Table 3). Finally, compared to non-use, prior SVM use was associated with significantly higher adjusted odds of sexual misconduct perpetration (aOR 3.14 (1.60, 6.14)), sexual violence perpetration (aOR = 11.21, (1.46, 86.25)), sexual misconduct victimization (aOR = 2.04 (1.25, 3.32)), and sexual violence victimization (aOR = 7.07 (2.38, 20.95)); Table 3). Compared to non-use in adjusted models, SEM use was associated with 2.1 times higher odds of sexual misconduct victimization (95 % CI 1.35, 3.32).

Several significant gender-differentiated associations were observed between sexualized media use exposures and outcomes (Table 3). Among boys, compared to non-use in adjusted models, SEM use and SVM use, respectively, were associated with significantly poorer self-rated health (coef. = 0.44 (0.10, 0.78); coef. = 0.58 (0.23, 0.93)) and significantly higher odds of any alcohol use (aOR = 2.42 (1.06, 5.58); aOR = 2.44 (1.05, 5.62)). Compared to non-use, SVM use was associated with significantly higher adjusted odds of any sexual misconduct perpetration (aOR = 5.02 (1.39, 18.06)) and significantly higher adjusted odds of sexual misconduct victimization (aOR = 2.64 (1.15, 6.10)).

Among girls, compared to non-use of sexualized media, SVM use was associated with significantly higher adjusted odds of sexual misconduct perpetration (aOR = 2.59 (1.13, 5.92)) and sexual violence victimization (aOR = 5.74 (1.81, 18.22)). SEM use was associated with significantly higher adjusted odds of sexual misconduct victimization (aOR = 2.32 (1.33, 4.04)). Neither form of media use was significantly associated with other health or social outcomes (Table 3).

Lastly, several interactions between SEM use and SVM use with beliefs in pro-violence peer norms were significant, especially among male participants (Table 4). Among boys, stronger beliefs that peers accepted violence against women strengthened the associations of SEM use with poorer self-rated health and negative mental health symptoms and the associations of SVM use with negative mental health symptoms and academic disengagement. Among girls, stronger beliefs that peers accepted violence against women did not alter most associations of media use with well-being outcomes; but, stronger beliefs attenuated the associations of SVM use with lower life satisfaction and SEM use with higher academic disengagement (Table 4).

Discussion

The use of sexually explicit media and sexually violent media among young people is a potential risk factor for many adverse health and psychosocial outcomes, including sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization (Burnay et al., 2022; Hedrick, 2021; Rodenhizer and Edwards, 2019). This risk may be due in part to increases in young people’s time spent using media and shifts to new media forms, which have increased access, especially among boys, to sexualized content that typically subordinates women and depicts violence against them and other vulnerable groups.4 Several shortcomings of the prior literature include unclear definitions of sexualized media; its assessment with few, unvalidated items that do not measure the modality (e.g., online) or content (e.g., physical violence) of sexualized media; potential biases of self-reported assessments; and the multifactorial nature of sexualized media use and violence victimization and perpetration. Other short-comings include cross-sectional designs that preclude causal inferences; small, unrepresentative samples often of white men from the U.S. that preclude generalizations across cultures and genders; and atheoretical research on sexualized media use and violence, hindering comparisons of findings across studies (Mestre-Bach et al., 2024).

The present study addressed many of these limitations. This study was the first conducted in a large and diverse sample of male and female high-school students in Vietnam to collect detailed data on all major forms of sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization, other potential health outcomes, and measures of SEM use and SVM use based on clear definitions and scales adapted previously for undergraduate men in Vietnam (Bergenfeld et al., 2022) and shown in the current sample to have high internal consistency (α = 0.98 in this sample). The analysis was grounded in two theories—gendered aggression5 and moral incongruence (Perry, 2018)—for the effects of SEM use and SVM use on sexual misconduct victimization, perpetration, and psychosocial well-being in high-school students. The analysis tested these theories by comparing SEM use and SVM use levels among male and female students and associations of their use versus non-use with sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization (combining sexual harassment, sexual violence, physical dating violence, and stalking); sexual violence victimization and perpetration, self-reported health, mental health, alcohol use, and school disengagement.

Summary and interpretation

Findings revealed that most (79.5 % of) eleventh- and twelfth-grade students reported using any sexualized media in the prior six months. Nearly half (47.3 %) reported using SEM, and nearly one third (32.2 %) reported using SVM during this period. These use levels corroborate those among first-year undergraduate men in Hanoi, Vietnam, in which 86.2 % reported any sexualized media use, 45.2 % reported SEM use, and 41.0 % reported SVM use in a six-month period.7 Similarly, a study of exposure to sexually explicit internet material among students attending public high schools in four provinces in Vietnam showed that 84.1 % of participants had been exposed, with near-universal use among male students (89.8 %).52 The latter study did not separate SEM use and SVM use, so to our knowledge, our study is the first to provide separate estimates of these exposures for high-school students in Vietnam. Prevalence rates of sexualized media use in Vietnam are higher than those among high schoolers in parts of the U.S. (Rivas-Koehl et al., 2023).

Findings also confirmed expected differences in SEM use and SVM use between male and female students. In the prior six months, girls more often reported non-use of sexualized media (24.0 % versus 15.4 % among boys), and boys more often reported SVM use (39.2 % versus 27.3 % among girls). That said, SEM use was reported in just under half of female (48.7 %) and male (45.5 %) students. Prior studies in Vietnam did not differentiate the type of sexualized media that was used by male and female high-school students nor their relationships with a range of health and psychosocial outcomes (Nguyen et al., 2021). Therefore, the present study is the first in Vietnam to be able to assess how applicable Western theories of general aggression, gendered aggression, and moral incongruence are for understanding the associations of SEM use and SVM use with psychosocial health among high-school students in this setting.

Findings supported general aggression theory in the pooled sample (Anderson and Bushman, 2018) (hypothesis 1) and partially supported gendered aggression theory in samples stratified by gender (hypothesis 2). In the pooled sample, SVM use was positively associated with any sexual-misconduct and any sexual-violence perpetration and victimization, and SEM use was positively associated with sexual misconduct victimization. Supporting gendered aggression theory (hypothesis 2), positive relationships of SEM use with sexual misconduct victimization and SVM use with sexual violence victimization were observed only among girls. Thus, female users may learn, activate, and apply in their daily lives scripts in sexualized media that depict the objectification and physical violence of women (Tomaszewska and Krahé, 2018). Prevailing gender norms among young people in Vietnam that normalize women’s compliance and men’s dominance, violence, and entitlement to sex may reinforce observed gendered sexual scripts in sexualized media (Bergenfeld et al., 2020, 2021).

That said, a positive relationship between SVM use and sexual misconduct victimization was also observed among boys. In Vietnam, the gendered scripts learned by some male SVM users may align with experiences of emotional, physical, and sexual violence in childhood (Yount et al., 2023), including corporal punishment by male adult relatives (Rydstrøm, 2006) and unwanted sexual attention and coercion by school staff (Tran et al., 2025). In parts of Vietnam, ethnographic research has suggested that corporal punishment by senior male relatives pushes the “… beaten boy into an inferior (feminine) position” (Rydstrøm, 2006, p. 336). Thus, the violent scripts that boys learn in SVM may reinforce scripts learned from their own exposures in boyhood to adult male-perpetrated violence, making male high-school student SVM users more vulnerable to sexual misconduct victimization in their daily lives.

Finally, SVM use was positively associated with sexual misconduct perpetration among boys and girls. This finding for boys corroborates gendered aggression theory (hypothesis 2) and findings from a prior study of first-year undergraduate men in Vietnam; in that study, any SVM use in the prior six months was associated with higher adjusted odds of any non-contact sexually violent behavior and contact sexually violent behavior using physical and non-physical tactics (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Both findings support the possibility that male SVM users in Vietnam observe gendered sexual scripts in media amidst prevailing gender norms that normalize masculine dominance and violence toward others. However, the finding that SVM use was associated with misconduct perpetration among girls was unexpected. Here, a general aggression model may apply to some girls who use SVM in Vietnam. Alternatively, the interpretation of perpetration among girls may be self-defense. According to young people in Vietnam, girls are expected to show “fierce resistance” to communicate their non-consent of unwanted sexual advances (Bergenfeld et al., 2020; Bergenfeld et al., 2021; Lewis et al., 2021). Thus, girls may be conditioned by prevailing gender norms and sexual scripts in SVM to use “fierce resistance” as a form of self-defense to unwanted sexual advances, and girls may report this behavior as perpetration. Finally, the target of misconduct perpetration may differ for girls and boys; however, these data were not collected in the original study.

Findings from this study supported moral incongruence theory regarding the associations of SEM use and SVM use, especially with boys’ health and well-being (hypotheses 3–5). Among boys, compared to non-use, SEM use and SVM use were associated with poorer self-rated health and higher adjusted odds of alcohol use. These relationships may be bidirectional (Perry, 2018), and causality cannot be inferred from the present cross-sectional study. Still, questions about SEM use and SVM use were asked with respect to “the prior six months,” and questions about self-rated health and alcohol use were asked with respect to the time of the survey; thus, findings support the idea that use of sexualized media may influence subsequent well-being outcomes (Mehmood Qadri et al., 2023).

Moreover, models with interaction terms revealed that boys who used SEM had worse self-rated health and worse mental health when they believed more strongly that peers supported violence against women. Similarly, boys who used SVM had worse mental health and higher academic disengagement when they believed more strongly that peers supported violence against women. Consistent with hypothesis 5, these findings suggest that boys who use SEM and SVM and who more strongly perceive pro-violence norms among peers may feel the strongest cognitive dissonance between their own moral values and sexualized media use, exacerbating poor well-being outcomes. Among girls, as expected, stronger beliefs about pro-violence peer norms did not alter most associations of SVM use with well-being outcomes, but weakened the association of SVM use with negative life satisfaction and SEM use with academic disengagement.

Limitations and Strengths

Some limitations of the study warrant discussion. First, some non-participation may have occurred due to data collection in May, the examination period for high schools in Ho Chi Minh city. Still, the participation rate (74.5 %) far exceeded rates in surveys of undergraduates in the U.S. (20.4 %) (Cantor et al., 2020). Second, higher levels of missingness in later modules about sexual violence victimization (16 %–23 %) than in earlier modules about sexual harassment (11 %–14 %) may have been due to respondent burden, the greater sensitivity of questions about sexual violence, and some duplicate records for students who lost internet connectivity and re-entered REDCap with a new ID to retake the survey. Still, levels of missingness in the present study and in surveys of undergraduates in the U.S. were similar (Krebs et al., 2016), and estimates of sexual violence victimization and perpetration are shown to be similar across different response rates (Jeffrey et al., 2022). Third, the original study was not specifically powered to analyze the relationship of sexualized media use, including “frequent” SVM use (n = 149) and “any” SVM use (n = 229), with specific forms of sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration,8 and the frequency of use is relevant for aggression theory. Still, research with undergraduate men in Vietnam showed that the adjusted odds of sexually violent behavior were similar for men reporting any and at least monthly SVM use (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). Fourth, the higher internal consistencies (α > 0.95) for measures of sexual misconduct/sexual violence perpetration and sexual misconduct/sexual violence victimization may appear to be a result of item redundancy; however, they were instead due to the high percentages of participants reporting “no” perpetration or victimization. Item removal was not considered warranted for several reasons: 1) each item captures a different behavior that, in theory, can occur among young people, 2) including more items helps to reduce under-reporting of perpetration and victimization, and 3) we created binary outcomes of these measures anyway for the analysis. For these reasons, we retained all items to capture perpetration and victimization outcomes. Finally, this study addressed sexual misconduct perpetration and victimization among students; in this sample, 40 % of students reported sexual harassment victimization by staff (Tran et al., 2025); thus, research is needed to understand the context of staff-on-student sexual miscondcut and avenues for prevention intervention.

The study design addressed several limitations of prior studies (Mestre-Bach et al., 2024), lending credibility to the findings. The survey collected detailed data on multiple forms of sexual misconduct victimization and perpetration. Measures relied on previously validated scales (Swartout et al., 2019; Tilley et al., 2020) that had good internal consistency in the present sample (Tran et al., 2025). The survey included contextualized demographic questions from prior surveys of young people in Vietnam (Yount et al., 2020) and a module on sexualized media use that was previously self-administered online among young men in Vietnam (Bergenfeld et al., 2022). The anonymous online self-administered survey format likely increased participation rates compared to similar surveys of students in the U.S. (Cantor et al., 2020) and disclosures to sensitive questions.

Implications for research and policy

Findings have important implications for future research and policy. First, survey experiments are recommended to understand how design elements may affect survey participation and completion. Randomizing the sampling strategy (e.g., full census versus random sample); number and order of survey modules; delivery mode; compensation level and type; and/or number, frequency, and format of participation and completion reminders would help to identify the design elements that optimize data quality in future surveys. Second, a national high-school climate survey with a module on media use is warranted. Third, qualitative research is needed to understand some of the more nuanced findings, such as SVM use and sexual misconduct perpetration among girls and sexual misconduct victimization among boys. Fourth, longitudinal studies among high-school students could confirm or disconfirm the cross-sectional findings presented here. Fifth, further research is needed to understand the modalities by which high-school students access SEM and SVM in Vietnam. If, for example, SEM use and SVM use are common via smartphone, a modality to which 38 % of young people are reportedly addicted in Vietnam (Nguyen et al., 2025), a multi-arm randomized controlled trial would be useful to test the effects of phone-free schools, alone and with evidence-based educational programming with teachers, parents, and students, on the health and behavioral harms of sexualized media use among students. Finally, findings should be disseminated locally to raise awareness among policymakers, school leaders, and parents about the potential benefits of contextualized school policies and programs to reduce harmful media use as one means to improve the well-being of all students.

Conclusion

In this first climate survey of high-school students in Vietnam, a majority reported using sexualized media, and SVM use was more common among boys than girls. Among boys, use was more strongly associated with sexual misconduct perpetration and with poorer self-rated health and alcohol use, especially when boys more strongly perceived peer norms to be accepting of pro-violence against women. Among girls, sexually violent media use was more strongly associated with sexual-misconduct and sexual-violence victimization. Future contextualized programming may involve reducing student access to common modalities of sexualized media use and evidence-based education of staff, parents, and students about sexual misconduct and the harms of sexualized media use in young people.
