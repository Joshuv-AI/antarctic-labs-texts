The metabolic and physiologic impairments underlying long COVID associated exercise intolerance
Authors: Brooks P. Leitner, Phillip Joseph, Andres Figueroa Quast, Maria Alejandra Ramirez, Paul M. Heerdt, Jose G. Villalobos, Inderjit Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractData from invasive CPET (iCPET) revealed long COVID patients have impaired systemic oxygen extraction (EO2), suggesting impaired mitochondrial ATP production. However, it remains uncertain whether the initial severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection has implications on EO2 and exercise capacity (VO2) nor has there been assessment of anerobic ATP generation in long COVID patients. iCPET was performed on 47 long COVID patients (i.e., full cohort; n = 8 with severe SARS‐CoV‐2 infection). In a subset of patients (i.e., metabolomic cohort; n = 26) metabolomics on venous and arterial blood samples during iCPET was performed. In the full cohort, long COVID patients exhibited reduced peak EO2 with reduced peak VO2 (90 ± 17% predicted) relative to cardiac output (118 ± 23% predicted). Peak VO2 [88% predicted (IQR 81% ‐ 108%) vs. 70% predicted (IQR 64% ‐ 89%); p = 0.02] and EO2 [0.59(IQR 0.53–0.62) vs. 0.53(IQR 0.50–0.48); p = 0.01) were lower in severe versus mild infection. In the metabolomic cohort, 12 metabolites were significantly consumed, and 41 metabolites were significantly released (p‐values < 0.05). Quantitative metabolomics demonstrated significant increases in inosine and succinate arteriovenous gradients during exercise. Peak VO2 was significantly correlated with peak venous succinate (r = 0.68; p = 0.0008) and peak venous lactate (r = 0.49; p = 0.0004). Peak EO2 and consequently peak VO2 impact long COVID patients in a severity dependent manner. Exercise intolerance associated with long COVID is defined by impaired aerobic and anaerobic energy production. Peak venous succinate may serve as a potential biomarker in long COVID.

INTRODUCTION

Acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection continues to be prevalent 4 years following the pandemic onset despite the advent of vaccinations, targeted antiviral therapies, and preventative measures. 1 Beyond the acute clinical implications of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, the post‐viral syndrome, colloquially referred to as either long COVID or post‐acute sequalae of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (PASC) has become more common. In the United States, the prevalence of long COVID is estimated to be approximately 7% amongst noninstitutionalized individuals. 2

Exertional intolerance represents one of the most common presentations of long COVID, 3 which has prompted a multitude of noninvasive and invasive cardio‐pulmonary exercise test (CPET) studies to objectively assess whether the cause(s) of exercise limitation is either a central cardiac and/or peripheral limitation. 4 With an invasive CPET, the combination of real‐time blood sampling from arterial and mixed venous sites along with measurements of oxygen consumption at the whole‐body level helps delineate whether oxygen delivery (DO2) and/or oxygen extraction (EO2) are impaired in exercising patients. Using invasive CPET, our group previously demonstrated that long COVID patients continue to experience exertional intolerance from impaired EO2 well over a year following their mild self‐limiting acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. 5 , 6 Currently, it remains unclear whether long COVID patients who required in‐patient care for severe acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection experience greater EO2 impairment and therefore worse aerobic exercise capacity compared to those with mild self‐limiting acute infection.

The finding of impaired EO2 observed during invasive CPET indicates a primary peripheral limitation to exercise and suggests that oxygen‐dependent adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production in skeletal muscle mitochondria is impaired in these patients. 7 , 8 However, both aerobic and anaerobic metabolic pathways contribute to ATP generation to meet the energy demands required by exercising skeletal muscle. 9 Skeletal muscle glycolysis (from intramuscular glycogen or circulating glucose) is the predominant source of ATP at high and moderate intensity exercise and occurs outside of the mitochondria. Also, in the cytosol of contracting skeletal muscles, particularly at rates of high ATP turnover or during hypoxia, the purine nucleotide cycle is upregulated to provide adenine nucleotides to meet increased ATP demands. 10 , 11 , 12 It is therefore plausible that a metabolic underpinning of long COVID exertional intolerance extends beyond oxygen‐dependent catabolism of fatty acids and glucose in the mitochondria. Ultimately, identifying specific metabolic defects is important not only for understanding mechanism(s), but also for elucidating potential treatment targets.

In the current study, we sought to characterize the physiologic and metabolic aberrancies in patients with long COVID associated exertional intolerance by integrating our invasive CPET infrastructure with deep metabolomic profiling. In addition, we examined if there exists an abnormal exercise pathophysiology and metabolite expression in a severity dependent manner between long COVID patients who experienced mild (i.e., nonhospitalized) versus severe (i.e., hospitalized) acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

METHODS

This study was conducted after we obtained ethics approval from our institutional review board (IRB) (Yale HIC #2000024783 and Yale HIC #2000024570). Written informed consent was obtained from 47 consecutive long COVID patients with persistent unexplained exertional intolerance referred to the Yale New Haven Hospital Pulmonary Vascular Disease Clinic (New Haven, CT) for invasive CPET between February 2021 and March 2023 (Figure 1). All subjects had previously experienced acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection confirmed by polymerase chain reaction testing and undergone investigative testing that did not explain their persistent exertional intolerance (i.e., unremarkable pulmonary function test, computed tomography chest, noninvasive CPET, cardiac stress testing, and echocardiogram). In a subset of patients (n = 26, i.e., metabolomic cohort), targeted metabolomics from venous and arterial samples obtained at rest and peak exercise was performed. Subjects within this cohort had fasted for at least 8 h before their blood draws.

Invasive CPET data collection

Our methods for resting RHC 13 , 14 and invasive CPET 5 , 6 have been described previously. Briefly, RHC was performed in the supine position with a five‐port pacing PA catheter (Edwards LifeSciences) inserted percutaneously under fluoroscopic and ultrasound guidance into the internal jugular vein, and a catheter concurrently placed in the radial artery. Patients underwent a symptom‐limited incremental CPET using an upright cycle ergometer with a breath‐by‐breath assessment of gas exchange (ULTIMA CPX; Medical Graphics Corporation) along with continuous 12‐lead electrocardiography monitoring. Patients underwent 2 min of rest followed by 2 min of unloaded cycling at 40 to 60 revolutions per minute. Work rate then was increased continuously using a ramp protocol at 5, 10, 15, or 20 Watts/min depending on the patient's functional status, until peak exercise, defined either by peak respiratory exchange ratio (RER) of >1.10 or peak heart rate of >85% predicted, was achieved. Pulmonary and systemic hemodynamics were monitored continuously during exercise (Xper Cardio Physiomonitoring System; Phillips). Pulmonary pressures were recorded at the end of passive exhalation unless there were marked respirophasic changes, at which time, the electronic average over three respiratory cycles was used. 15 Arterial and mixed venous blood gases and pH were collected during each minute of exercise, and the arterial‐mixed venous oxygen content difference was calculated. EO2 was calculated as arterial oxygen content (CaO2) minus mixed venous oxygen content (CvO2) divided by CaO2. Direct Fick cardiac output (CO) was calculated as VO2/(CaO2 – CvO2) with peak CO estimated as the predicted maximal VO2 as defined by the Wasserman‐Hansen reference equations 16 divided by the assume arterio‐venous (AV) content difference. The assumed A‐V content difference is 140.7 based on the following equation: 1.34 x Hemoglobin x (SaO2‐SvO2) x correction factor, where the hemoglobin is assumed to be 14 g/dL and the SaO2‐SvO2 is 0.75 based on the assumption that a normal extraction is 75%. The correction factor is 10.

Direct Fick CO and stroke volume (SV) were determined every minute. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was calculated as: (mean PA pressure ‐ PA wedge pressure)/CO, expressed in Wood units. SV was calculated as CO/heart rate. Oxygen delivery (DO2) was calculated as: CO x CaO2. PA compliance was calculated as the ratio of SV to PA pulse pressure and expressed as milliliters per millimeter of mercury. Total pulmonary resistance (TPR) was calculated as the mean PA pressure/CO and expressed in Wood units.

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed on mixed venous and radial arterial blood plasma obtained at rest and peak exercise during invasive CPET. Each collected sample was centrifuged, and the resulting plasma supernatant aliquoted then frozen at −80°C. Plasma samples were run on an LC/MS/MS on two different liquid chromatography columns (Hypercarb and Reverse Phase) in both positive and negative modes.

Statistical analysis

For the invasive CPET clinical data set, values are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (inter‐quartile range). Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9.7) using the SciPy (version 1.7.3) and NumPy (version 1.22.0) packages, and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.1). Comparisons between long COVID patients with initially mild (i.e., nonhospitalized) versus severe (i.e., hospitalized) acute SARS‐CoV‐infection were conducted as students t‐tests or Mann‐Whitney U Tests in comparisons with less than 10 patients in a group.

For the metabolomic analyses, targeted mass spectrometry peaks were curated in El‐MAVEN software, and metabolites were annotated using IROA Technologies metabolite library. For differential expression analyses, data were normalized by computing each metabolite relative to the sum of all metabolites in each run, then log‐transformed to generate a normal distribution for parametric statistical analysis. Targeted metabolomics data were uploaded into a Jupyter Notebook for analysis in Python version 3.9.7. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Stats package from SciPy (version 1.7.3), and log base 2 normalization using NumPy (version 1.22.0). Benjamani‐hochberg correction for multiple comparisons was performed using the Multiple Tests package from StatsModels (version 0.13.2). Plots were made with Seaborn (version 0.11.2). Pearson correlation analyses were performed using Pandas (version 1.3.5) using peak ion abundance values for metabolites on one of the four LC/MS/MS modes where the metabolite was captured with the highest abundance across all samples.

The exercise response of patients with long COVID

The invasive CPET data of the full cohort are presented in Table 1. Overall, patients demonstrated “preserved” peak aerobic exercise capacity (i.e., peak VO2 > 80% predicted). However, the peak VO2 (90 ± 17% predicted) was reduced relative to what was essentially a supra‐normal peak CO (118 ± 23% predicted). Consistent with prior reports, 5 , 6 peak EO2 was reduced when compared to prior published normative values (0.57 ± 0.1 vs. 0.78 ± 0.1). 5 , 15

Peak VO2 (%predicted) was significantly lower in patients with severe versus mild acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection [88% predicted (IQR 81% ‐ 108%) vs. 70% predicted (IQR 64% ‐ 89%); p = 0.02]. This impairment in peak VO2 is not due to a lower peak DO2 but rather from a reduction in peak EO2 ([0.59(IQR 0.53–0.62) vs. 0.53(IQR 0.50–0.48]); [p = 0.01]

There was no difference between maximum watts achieved [133(IQR 92–176) vs. 132(IQR 105–148); p = 0.90)] or peak respiratory exchange ratio (RER) [1.2(IQR 1.15–1.25) vs. 1.19(IQR 1.14–.127); p = 0.94)] between mild and severe groups. The peak heart rate (% predicted) differed in a severity‐dependent manner [92 (IQR 85–96) vs. 78(IQR 76–83); p = 0.02)], with no difference between age (p = 0.87) or body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.08) amongst patients with mild or severe acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (Table S2).

We investigated the relative increase (fold change) in EO2 as exercise intensity increased [measured in metabolic equivalents (METs)] to examine if EO2 is impaired at rest or during incremental exercise between severe versus mild acute infection. METs calculated as VO2/resting VO2 reflects the oxygen requirements of activity as a multiple of basal metabolism and can be used to compare physiologic demand a variety of activities of daily living. 17 EO2 rose steadily as exercise intensity increased to about four METs (Figure 2), at which point the EO2 rise plateaued up until the maximal METs (ranging from six to ten) were achieved. We observed no apparent differences in EO2 kinetics between the mild versus severe groups.

The metabolic underpinnings of patients with long COVID: Arteriovenous differences in aerobic and anaerobic metabolic pathways at rest and peak exercise

To understand which circulating metabolites were significantly altered at peak exercise compared to resting condition, the arteriovenous (a‐v) difference in metabolite expression at peak exercise versus at rest was determined (Figure 3). Of the 368 targeted metabolites identified by mass spectrometry, 12 metabolites were significantly consumed, and 41 metabolites were significantly released at the whole‐body level (corrected p values < 0.05). Hierarchical clustering analysis was unable to identify a significant relationship between covid severity, age, sex, or BMI (Figure 3). We then plotted our data to examine trends in the major energy systems known to contribute to maximal exercise. There was a significant net consumption of isocitrate, and a large net succinate release that did not meet significance (Figure S1). There was also observed a significant net consumption of inosine (Figure S1). There was no significant net extraction or production of glycolytic intermediates or creatine metabolites (Figure S1). There was a significant net extraction of nine amino acids, including all three branched chain amino acids (BCAAs), three of the four aromatic amino acids (tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine), and arginine, methionine, and aspartate (Figure S1). There was a net release of long chain fatty acids.

The metabolic underpinnings of patients with long COVID: Quantitative metabolomics on anaerobic metabolic pathways

Quantitative metabolomics was performed to follow up on several metabolites to better understand the non‐oxygen dependent changes observed in global metabolomics analysis. There was a significant increase in the inosine arteriovenous gradient from rest to peak exercise (Figure S2). There was a significant rise in venous lactate during peak exercise but no significant differences between mild and severe patients (Figure S2). Peak venous lactate was significantly correlated with peak VO2 (Figure S2). There was a significant increase in peak venous succinate (Figure S2). There was a significant correlation between peak VO2 with peak venous succinate (Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

While long COVID associated exercise intolerance has been documented, a more specific mechanistic basis is necessary to guide treatments. In the current paper, we describe the following physiological and metabolic underpinnings of Long COVID associated exercise intolerance: (1) Reduction in peak VO2 explained by reduced peak EO2, with conserved systemic O2 delivery. This reduction in peak EO2 and consequently peak VO2 impacts long COVID patients in a severity dependent manner with patients who acquired severe acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection exhibiting worse peak EO2 and VO2. (2) A plateau in the ability to further increase EO2 as exercise intensity reaches 4 METs suggesting a reliance on anaerobic metabolic pathways for energy production at lower exercise intensities. (3) A significant correlation between peak venous succinate and peak VO2. (4) Reduced capacity for anaerobic ATP production, including reduced capacity for purine nucleotide cycle function.

Primary peripheral limit to exercise

According to the Fick principle, a depressed peak VO2 is the result of reduced CO and/or from impaired EO2. In the current study, consistent with prior reports, 5 , 6 our long COVID cohort exhibited a discordance between the peak VO2 and CO. The reduced peak VO2 relative to peak CO is therefore a manifestation of impaired EO2. 18 Impaired peak EO2 is further supported by the elevated peak exercise mixed venous O2 saturation (MvO2) of 41 ± 7%. While the current iCPET study did not have a healthy comparator group, this level of peak MvO2 is significantly higher than previously reported for healthy controls (26.5 ± 3.6%). 19

This reduction in peak EO2 was more pronounced in long COVID patients who acquired initial severe acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection with resultant worse peak VO2 compared to those with initial mild SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Although the peak heart rate response (% predicted) was reduced in the severe group, this did not impact the systemic DO2. Therefore, the significantly worse peak VO2 (% predicted) observed in long COVID patients with severe infection is attributed primarily to impaired peak EO2. Prior studies have suggested that mitochondrial dysfunction underlies this the physiologic finding of reduced peak EO2 on iCPET. 20 , 21 , 22 Most recently, comprehensive metabolic analyses have examined the pathology of long COVID during resting sample acquisition and demonstrated lower O2 phosphorylation capacity and an increase in muscle amyloid deposition. 7 However, to date, this level of granularity has been unable to link molecular pathways to identify potential therapeutic targets for long COVID patients. To meet the demands of increasing exercise intensity, O2‐dependent (aerobic) metabolic pathways that occur in the mitochondria, and O2‐independent (anaerobic) pathways that occurs in the cytosol are upregulated to generate ATP to fuel muscular contraction. Peak EO2 is higher in exercise trained versus sedentary individuals, and in healthy subjects, EO2 increases steadily as exercise intensity and VO2 increases. 23 We found that patients with long COVID following both mild and severe initial infections experience a plateau in EO2 as VO2 continues to increase (Figure 2). We quantified this increase in VO2 as metabolic equivalents (METs) to identify units that would be meaningful for clinical practice. The EO2 plateau occurred at around 4 METs, which typically occurs when an individual walks up a flight of stairs or conducts heavy household chores. At above 4 METs, as long COVID patients approached peak exercise at 6–10 METs, it is likely that primarily anaerobic metabolism increases to meet the increased exercise demand.

While fatty acid and amino acid metabolism is maintained, purine and succinate metabolism May be impaired

During maximal exercise, CO is directed almost exclusively to contracting skeletal with approximately 80–90% of the O2 consumed during exercise is used by the contracting skeletal muscles. 24 Long chain fatty acids are released from adipocytes via lipolysis as exercise intensity increases, 25 and the majority of amino acids are extracted at the whole body level during exercise in healthy individuals. 26 We recapitulated these findings in our patients demonstrating perseveration of these metabolic pathways. 27

Glucose also contributes to ATP production via skeletal muscle derived glycogen, liver derived glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, or circulating glucose. 28 , 29 Glucose can be further metabolized via aerobic glycolysis to pyruvate, and continue into the mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation, or be converted to lactate in the cytosol. Venous lactate content can therefore serve as a proxy for enhanced glycolytic capacity for anerobic metabolism as this would reflect that lactate exited the cell following glycolysis rather than glucose participating in aerobic mitochondrial metabolism. While we did not observe any differences in glycolytic capacity as measured by arteriovenous lactate differences, peak lactate production was significantly correlated with peak exercise O2 consumption (peak VO2) (r = 0.49; p= 0.0004. Figure S2). O2 consumption represents an important measure for aerobic metabolism, as it is the final electron acceptor of the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane (i.e., aerobic mitochondrial metabolism). We observed that patients with low peak venous lactate also exhibit lower peak VO2. Conversely, the correlation of peak venous lactate and peak VO2 also suggests that long COVID patients with high peak VO2 have the highest capacity for lactogenic glycolysis at peak exercise. Accordingly, since both of these metabolic traits occur simultaneously, this suggests that impairment in exercise capacity experienced by long COVID patients is a combination of blunted aerobic and anaerobic metabolic capacity. This provides nuance to previous reports suggesting increased reliance on glycolysis in long COVID patients. 25

We observed a large increase in the consumption of the purine inosine, during our global metabolic analysis Figure S2. The purine nucleotide cycle plays a significant role in ATP provision during very intense or hypoxic skeletal muscle contraction. 10 , 30 , 31 , 32 While purines likely do not contribute significantly to ATP provision at low to moderate intensity exercise, it is possible that this metabolic pathway plays a more significant role in patients with long COVID, where reliance on anaerobic pathways appears to occur at lower exercise intensity. To begin to answer this, we demonstrated that arteriovenous inosine content is increased at peak exercise, suggesting net consumption of inosine, and thus its potential role in meeting the peak exercise demands. This increase in net inosine consumption may in part account for ATP generation beyond 4 METs to meet the increasing exercise demands as the EO2 plateaus (Figure 2).

Overall, the findings of the current study suggest that the increased reliance on anerobic metabolism during exercise extends beyond our current understanding of Long COVID pathobiology, which thus far, has primarily implicated abnormal mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. 20 , 21 , 22 These findings also have important implication towards the development of potential pharmacotherapeutic interventions directed towards mitigating early reliance of anerobic metabolism which may include shifting, restoring, and/or enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity during exercise.

We also demonstrated that peak venous succinate is significantly correlated with peak VO2 in long COVID patients (r = 0.68; p= 0.0008). Figure S2. While succinate release during exercise has been previously demonstrated, it's mechanistic or prognostic significance is poorly understood. Few groups have provided a rationale and supportive data demonstrating this. 33 Reddy et al 34 demonstrated that succinate is significantly correlated with post‐exercise glucose tolerance in humans, which has important implications for linking succinate release at maximal exercise to glucose tolerance— suggesting a potential exercise‐related biomarker for severity of glucose intolerance. To our knowledge, our study demonstrates for the first time, the significant correlation between maximal exercise capacity and peak succinate release. This provides potential evidence for an exercise responsive, blood‐based biomarker for long COVID associated exercise intolerance.

LIMITATIONS

Study results need to be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, our comparator groups (i.e., mild vs. severe group) consisted of a small number of patients which may have potentially introduced a type 1 error. We also did not observe significant changes in dynamic expression of different metabolites during exercise between the groups. This in part, maybe reflective of a type 2 error owing to the small number of patients, specifically within the severe group. Second, the current study did not include a healthy control group which would ideally consist of individuals with known prior SARS‐CoV‐2 exposure but without exertional intolerance. Recruitment of a control cohort was therefore limited, as in our institution, the iCPET represents a clinically indicated study performed in symptomatic patients only. Nonetheless, the impaired EO2 and resultant peak VO2 exhibited by PASC patients are in keeping with prior reports 5 , 6 and by combining our iCPET infrastructure with metabolomic profiling, we provided a comprehensive and unparalleled insight into the pathophysiological hallmark of exertional intolerance in PASC, that is otherwise not apparent on conventional investigative testing. Further studies consisting of a larger cohort of Long COVID patients are warranted to help understand the impaired metabolomic profiling that begets the aberrant exercise physiological observed in Long COVID patients.

CONCLUSION

In summary, exercise intolerance associated with long COVID is defined by a combined impairment in aerobic and anaerobic energy production during physical activity, in part due to an increased reliance on anaerobic pathways at lower exercise intensities. Additionally, purine metabolism (through modulation of purine nucleotide cycle and/or transport enzymes) may serve as a potential novel treatment target, while peak venous succinate as a potential biomarker for long COVID patients with associated exercise intolerance. Further studies exploring the mechanistic role of these aberrant metabolite expressions in Long COVID may help reveal novel pathways which can be leveraged for potential therapeutic opportunities.
