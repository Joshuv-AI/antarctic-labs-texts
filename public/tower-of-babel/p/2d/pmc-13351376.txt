Tumor Spread through Air Spaces in Early-Stage Lung Adenocarcinoma: Surgical Implications and Patient Management Strategies
Authors: Jin-Haeng Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor spread through air spaces (STAS) is a recently recognized, clinically relevant invasive pattern in lung adenocarcinoma with important prognostic implications. Recognition of STAS has influenced ongoing discussions regarding the surgical management of non-small cell lung cancer. The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer Staging Project recently recommended adopting STAS as a histologic T descriptor in the 9th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification. Moving beyond its role as a morphological risk factor, recent studies suggest that STAS may serve not only as a prognostic marker but also as a potential indicator of divergent evolutionary trajectories shaped by underlying driver mutations. This review summarizes the morphological definitions of STAS, its observed impact on outcomes after sublobar resection, optimization of surgical margin distance, challenges in intraoperative diagnosis, differential prevalence according to genetic alterations, and proposed integration into the 9th edition of the TNM classification. Potential clinical considerations for risk stratification and patient management based on STAS are also discussed.

Introduction

Lobectomy has traditionally been the standard surgical procedure for clinical stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). More recently, the increased detection of small, peripheral ground-glass nodules by high-resolution computed tomography has led to more frequent use of intentional limited resection, including wedge resection and segmentectomy, for early-stage disease [1,2]. However, tumor size alone may be insufficient for accurately predicting patient outcomes [3-5]. Tumor spread through air spaces (STAS) is increasingly recognized as a risk factor associated with higher recurrence rates and has therefore influenced discussions regarding the necessary extent of surgery [3,5,6]. STAS is an invasive pattern of lung adenocarcinoma that was described relatively recently and was formally recognized as a mechanism of invasion in the 2015 World Health Organization Classification of Lung Tumors [7]. STAS is defined as the spread of micropapillary clusters, solid nests, and/or single cancer cells into air spaces in the lung parenchyma beyond the edge of the main tumor (Fig. 1). Morphologically, STAS presents as 3 distinct patterns: micropapillary structures composed of papillary clusters without central fibrovascular cores; solid nests or tumor islands composed of solid collections of tumor cells filling air spaces; and scattered discohesive single cells [7]. STAS is considered present when these tumor cells are identified beyond the edge of the main tumor, even if they are present only in the first alveolar layer adjacent to the tumor edge. The prognostic significance of this phenomenon has led to the designation of STAS as an exclusion criterion for adenocarcinoma in situ and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma [7].

With accumulating evidence from multiple meta-analyses identifying STAS as a consistent negative prognostic factor in early-stage disease [8-12], the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) Staging Project has recommended consideration of STAS as a histologic T descriptor, similar to visceral pleural invasion [6]. The IASLC committee has provided a consensus view regarding the nature of STAS, favoring its recognition as an invasive feature. Although the committee acknowledged ongoing debate and the perspective that STAS may represent a potential artifact, this position marks an important step toward distinguishing STAS from ex vivo phenomena [6]. This review evaluates recent academic milestones, addresses current debates surrounding STAS, and outlines future directions for investigating STAS as a potential dynamic mechanism of tumor dissemination in lung cancer.

STAS: ex vivo artifact versus in vivo biological phenomenon

Since its formal recognition, the conceptual validity of STAS has been questioned, with some researchers proposing that it may represent an ex vivo artifact rather than an in vivo invasive pattern [13-16]. Thunnissen et al. [13] described 4 potential mechanisms by which such artifacts may arise in lung tissue: surgical collapse, ex vivo contraction of bronchial and bronchiolar smooth muscle, clamping-induced edema, and mechanical dragging of tissue fragments across a scalpel blade, referred to as the “spread through a knife surface” hypothesis [14]. Despite ongoing debate regarding the extent to which STAS may reflect ex vivo processing artifacts, accumulating evidence from meta-analyses identifying STAS as a negative prognostic factor in lung cancer [8-12] and from studies using spatial analyses supports a potential in vivo biological role [17,18]. Although distance-based topological definitions have historically been criticized for introducing diagnostic subjectivity, 3-dimensional pathological reconstructions have suggested that apparently isolated tumor islands are not freely floating, mechanically dislodged anomalies. Rather, they appear to comprise interconnected neoplastic networks that interact with pre-existing alveolar capillary beds, suggesting a potential biological survival mechanism [17,18]. Furthermore, the identification of viable STAS cells in independently processed secondary resections provides evidence against the single-knife-cut artifact hypothesis and further supports STAS as a biological mechanism of pulmonary tumor dissemination [19].

Evolution and clinical implications of STAS grading systems in lung adenocarcinoma

Although STAS is now widely recognized as a key pattern of invasion in lung adenocarcinoma, methodological standardization for defining and grading its extent remains an active area of pathological research. The literature has evolved from binary morphological assessment toward semiquantitative grading systems, each offering potential insight into tumor biology and patient prognosis.

Initial distance and volumetric paradigms

Early efforts to stratify STAS severity relied on anatomical landmarks and cluster quantification. Warth et al. proposed a distance-based metric that defined “limited” STAS as detachment within 3 alveoli of the main tumor mass and “extensive” STAS as spread beyond this boundary [20]. Although the presence of STAS was associated with reduced overall and disease-free survival, this alveolar threshold did not yield a statistically significant survival difference between the limited and extensive cohorts in a series of 569 patients. This finding suggested that, under an alveolar-count model, the biological event of cellular detachment itself may serve as the primary prognostic determinant, independent of travel distance. In contrast, Uruga et al. [21] introduced a volumetric, cluster-count method that categorized STAS as “low” (1–4 single cells or clusters) or “high” (≥5 clusters) within the most prominent microscopic area. This quantitative approach demonstrated a prognostic gradient, with higher STAS cluster burdens predicting shorter recurrence-free survival in 208 patients with small (≤2 cm) stage I lung adenocarcinoma. However, manual cluster counting may introduce interobserver variability and limit diagnostic efficiency in high-volume routine pathology practice.

Distance grading system using objective microscopic thresholds

To establish a reproducible metric, Han et al. evaluated a 2-tiered distance grading system using a standardized 2,500-µm radius, equivalent to a single microscopic field with a ×10 objective lens (Fig. 2). In a prospectively collected cohort of 1,869 patients with NSCLC, tumors exhibiting localized spread (STAS I: <2,500 µm) were not independently associated with prognosis. In contrast, extensive distal dissemination (STAS II: ≥2,500 µm) was identified as an independent poor prognostic factor for both recurrence-free survival and lung cancer-specific survival [5]. The 2,500-µm criterion was subsequently evaluated by Lee et al. [22] in a cohort of 1,660 patients with stage I invasive non-mucinous lung adenocarcinoma who underwent radical lobectomy. The 5-year cumulative incidence of any recurrence was 3.9% in the STAS-negative group, 8.4% in the STAS grade I group, and 17.0% in the STAS grade II group (p<0.001). In subgroup analysis stratified by pathological stage, the cumulative incidence of any recurrence in the pathological stage IA/STAS grade II subgroup was comparable to that in the pathological stage IB subgroup (stage IA/STAS grade II, 13.0%; stage IB overall, 16.0%; p=0.8). These findings indicated that STAS grade II in pathological stage IA lung adenocarcinoma was associated with a recurrence risk similar to that observed in pathological stage IB disease. However, the clinical utility and reproducibility of STAS grading systems remain under evaluation, and cross-institutional validation may further support their integration into routine diagnostic practices.

Surgical extent: lobectomy versus sublobar resection according to STAS

The prognostic impact of STAS is frequently discussed in relation to the selected surgical procedure. In 2015, Kadota et al. [23] investigated 411 patients with resected small (≤2 cm) stage I lung adenocarcinoma and identified STAS in 155 cases (38%). In the limited resection group, recurrence risk was significantly higher among patients with STAS-positive tumors than among those with STAS-negative tumors (5-year cumulative incidence of recurrence [CIR], 42.6% vs. 10.9%). STAS was strongly associated with higher risks of both distant and locoregional recurrence. In contrast, among patients who underwent standard lobectomy, STAS was not significantly associated with recurrence. In multivariate analyses, STAS remained an independent predictor associated with an approximately 3-fold increase in recurrence risk (hazard ratio [HR], 3.08), but only within the limited resection cohort. These findings suggest that lobectomy may mitigate the recurrence risk associated with STAS and support lobectomy as the standard treatment for patients with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma exhibiting this feature. Similar findings have been reported in other studies showing that STAS is associated with elevated recurrence rates in patients undergoing limited resection [24-26].

In contrast, Han et al. [5] and Lee et al. [22] demonstrated that, although STAS predominantly increases recurrence risk after sublobar resection, extensive STAS (STAS grade II) remains an independent predictor of recurrence even among patients undergoing standard lobectomy. A recent meta-analysis of 4,061 patients with pathological stage I NSCLC demonstrated that patients with STAS had significantly worse recurrence-free and overall survival in both univariable and multivariable analyses, regardless of histological subtype or extent of resection (lobar vs. sublobar) [6]. These findings provide a framework for stratifying prognostic outcomes according to the spatial distribution of STAS.

Independent prognostic value of STAS grade II according to surgical extent

STAS II was associated with a significantly higher recurrence risk than STAS-negative status in the lobectomy group (HR, 4.41). Similarly, STAS II was associated with higher recurrence risk in the limited resection group (HR, 4.88). These findings indicate that elevated recurrence risk may persist when extensive STAS (STAS grade II) is present, regardless of surgical extent [5].

Relationship between surgical extent and biological invasiveness

These findings suggest that STAS reflects not only surgical-margin adequacy but also the biological invasiveness of the tumor itself. Although previous studies proposed that lobectomy may offset the recurrence risk associated with STAS [23-26], Han et al. [5] and Lee et al. [22] showed that standard lobectomy alone may be insufficient to fully address tumor dissemination in cases with extensive spread (STAS II, ≥2,500 µm).

Potential clinical considerations

These analyses may inform future treatment strategies for early-stage lung cancer.

Consideration of adjuvant therapy

For patients with stage IA disease who have undergone lobectomy, pathological confirmation of STAS II may identify a higher-risk subgroup. This finding may warrant closer postoperative surveillance and consideration of adjuvant therapies [27-29].

Importance of preoperative and intraoperative evaluation

If STAS II is suspected preoperatively or identified intraoperatively in a patient scheduled for limited resection, a multidisciplinary approach may help determine the final extent of surgery. Once preoperative prediction or intraoperative diagnosis becomes reliable, future integrated surgical strategies could incorporate the following steps (Fig. 3).

When planning limited resection

STAS could be assessed intraoperatively or predicted using clinicopathological and preoperative radiological features [30]. If STAS is detected intraoperatively, lobectomy may be favored over limited resection, consistent with the findings of Kadota et al. [23] in 2015, Masai et al. [26] in 2017, Eguchi et al. [24] in 2019, and Chae et al. [25] in 2021.

Consideration of STAS extent (distance)

Once STAS is identified, assessment of its spatial extent may provide prognostic information. STAS II (≥2,500 µm) [5,22] or high STAS (≥5 clusters) can [21] indicate poor prognosis.

Postoperative management

STAS I (<2,500 μm) or low STAS (1–4 clusters): Adequate local control is generally achievable with lobectomy.

STAS II (≥2,500 μm) or high STAS (≥5 clusters): These findings may identify a higher-risk subgroup for whom careful follow-up and individualized clinical management should be considered.

Nevertheless, clinical application of this proposed algorithm will require further improvements in the accuracy, sensitivity, and interobserver agreement of intraoperative frozen-section diagnosis, supported by more extensive pathological evidence.

Optimization of surgical margin distance

When limited resection is performed, securing an adequate tumor margin is important for local control. Masai et al. [26] evaluated 508 cases of limited resection for primary lung cancer to define the relationship between surgical margin distance and STAS. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that local recurrence risk was significantly associated with STAS (HR, 12.24) and a tumor margin of less than 1.0 cm (HR, 6.36). A resection margin greater than 2.0 cm was associated with a lower risk of local recurrence and mitigation of other risk factors.

Intraoperative diagnosis of STAS and gross handling challenges

Because STAS in a sublobar resection specimen may indicate the need for completion lobectomy, accurate intraoperative diagnosis is clinically relevant. However, reliable diagnosis of STAS on frozen sections remains a major logistical and interpretive challenge. Lung deflation during surgical atelectasis collapses the alveolar architecture, limiting the ability of pathologists to identify detached tumor clusters reliably. Studies examining STAS on frozen sections [31-33] have demonstrated moderate interobserver agreement (kappa values, 0.34–0.67) and variable sensitivity (44%–86%), indicating that more standardized criteria are needed before STAS can be routinely assessed intraoperatively. Although reporting STAS on frozen section can inform surgeons’ intraoperative decision-making, refined diagnostic approaches are needed. Intraoperative inflation methods may improve the accuracy of frozen-section diagnosis for detecting STAS in small lung cancers [32,34,35]. Preanalytical variables also influence the accuracy of frozen-section diagnosis and subsequent pathological staging. Radonic et al. [36] emphasized the importance of maintaining a 3-dimensional orientation during gross handling of pulmonary resection specimens. Such careful handling preserves the anatomical integrity of the airways and alveoli, reduces structural artifacts during frozen-section assessment [32,34,35], and enables precise correlation with presurgical imaging for STAS prediction [30].

Differential prevalence of STAS according to genetic alterations

Recent evidence suggests that the prevalence and prognostic significance of STAS vary according to oncogenic driver mutations [3,5,20,37,38]. Lee et al. [3] evaluated this context-dependent behavior in lung adenocarcinoma using a prospectively collected cohort. The investigators analyzed 4,027 patients and focused the survival analysis on 1,117 patients with pathological stage IA disease who underwent R0 resection. In the primary study cohort, the overall prevalence of STAS was 40.2%, with variation according to the underlying molecular profile (Fig. 4). Although STAS was prevalent in tumors harboring ALK or ROS1 rearrangements, it occurred less frequently in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated cancers, particularly in the EGFR L858R subset. This finding suggests that STAS may represent an intrinsic biological characteristic linked to specific molecular drivers. In the stage IA survival cohort, STAS-positive patients had a 5-year CIR of 12.2%, compared with 2.3% in STAS-negative patients (p<0.001). Furthermore, multivariable subdistribution hazard models (p for interaction=0.013) showed that, despite the lower overall prevalence of STAS in EGFR-mutated tumors, STAS was a significant independent predictor of recurrence in this subgroup (subdistribution HR [SHR], 5.04; p=0.004) (Fig. 5). Conversely, in the EGFR-wild-type cohort, STAS was not a statistically significant independent predictor of recurrence (SHR, 0.72; p=0.490). These findings suggest a potential evolution in how STAS is interpreted and applied in clinical practice.

Refined risk stratification: Pathological assessment of STAS could be integrated with molecular profiling to improve risk stratification in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma.

Potential therapeutic implications: Identification of STAS in a stage IA EGFR-mutated tumor may define a patient subgroup at higher risk of recurrence. For this subset, these findings could inform future multidisciplinary discussions regarding the optimal extent of surgery and the potential role of adjuvant targeted therapy in early-stage disease [27-29].

Tumor-node-metastasis staging and clinical upstaging

The biological behavior of STAS suggests a potentially elevated risk profile among early-stage tumors and supports ongoing discussions regarding clinical upstaging. Several research groups have demonstrated that stage IA adenocarcinomas with STAS have recurrence-free and overall survival outcomes comparable to those of stage IB adenocarcinomas [5,22,39,40]. Furthermore, a global analysis of 4,061 pathological stage I NSCLC cases found that STAS was an independent predictor of outcomes and demonstrated broader applicability than visceral pleural invasion [6]. Although visceral pleural invasion is inherently limited to peripherally located tumors, STAS can occur in tumors located deep within the lung parenchyma and can affect prognosis in these tumors. Based on these international data, the IASLC has officially recommended that STAS be introduced as a new histologic descriptor in the 9th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification of lung cancer [6].

Evolutionary trajectories and molecular context of STAS

Emerging evidence suggests that STAS may serve as a morphological marker of divergent evolutionary trajectories; consequently, its clinical interpretation may be context dependent. According to Hong et al. [4], the prognostic significance of EGFR mutations in early-stage resected lung adenocarcinoma should be interpreted within the framework of the IASLC histological grading system. Patients with EGFR-mutated and histological G3 tumors had a lower 5-year recurrence-free survival rate (58.4%) than their EGFR-wild-type counterparts (71.5%). These findings are consistent with the studies by Lee et al. [3] and Han et al. [5]. STAS is significantly associated with G3 histology in lung adenocarcinoma, and although it has the lowest prevalence among EGFR-mutant tumors, its presence may indicate an aggressive biological phenotype [3,5]. Within the typically more indolent genomic landscapes driven by L858R or exon 19 deletions, the emergence of STAS may represent a critical evolutionary bottleneck. ALK- or ROS1- rearranged tumors may harbor an intrinsic invasive program, such as epithelial–mesenchymal transition [3,5,37, 38]. This may explain the high prevalence of STAS even in small (<1.5 cm) tumors, whereas EGFR-mutant tumors may require a distinct biological transition to acquire an invasive phenotype [3,4]. When this phenotypic switch occurs and manifests morphologically as STAS or histological G3 disease, it may indicate that the malignancy has overcome biological barriers to dissemination.

Currently, adjuvant osimertinib or chemotherapy is standard for stage IB–IIIA EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma [28,29]. However, recent findings suggest that patients with STAS-positive stage IA EGFR-mutant tumors may represent a subset that could derive survival benefit from adjuvant intervention [3,27]. Therefore, evaluation of STAS as a predictive biomarker in future adjuvant trials for early- stage disease warrants further investigation. Current staging paradigms often assume a linear relationship between tumor size and biological behavior [41]. However, longitudinal trajectory modeling indicates that tumor size dynamics may reflect nonlinear, discrete transitions in biological aggressiveness [3]. This nonlinear, phenotype-contingent framework may inform future clinical management of early-stage lung cancer. First, it may help delineate the optimal temporal window for surgical intervention, in which resection may be most effective before phenotypic expansion. Second, it highlights the potential role of adjuvant systemic therapy once this biological threshold has been crossed. Once STAS or histological G3 disease is evident, an EGFR-mutant tumor may have adapted to the biomechanical constraints of the dynamic alveolar microenvironment. In such instances, the malignancy may have undergone biological upstaging irrespective of anatomical dimensions, suggesting that adjuvant EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy could be explored as a targeted intervention against active micrometastatic dissemination [27-29]. Therefore, the presence of STAS should be interpreted in the context of the tumor’s specific molecular drivers to inform surgical and adjuvant oncological decision-making.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that STAS may function as a dynamic marker of divergent tumor evolution, underscoring the potential value of interpreting STAS within specific molecular contexts. The presence of STAS may serve as an additional stratification variable when considering patients with early-stage EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma for adjuvant EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy after curative-intent resection [27-29]. Regarding optimal therapeutic timing, treating the tumor before extensive phenotypic expansion may yield better clinical outcomes, although this concept requires further prospective validation. Moving forward, well-designed clinical trials and comprehensive real-world evidence analyses will be essential to clarify how STAS can be optimally integrated into treatment paradigms for early-stage lung adenocarcinoma.
