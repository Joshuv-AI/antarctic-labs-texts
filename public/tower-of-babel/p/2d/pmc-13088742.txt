Evaluation of cephalometric landmarks using artificial intelligence compared to the manual method
Authors: Alfonso Alvarado-Lorenzo, Laura Criado-Pérez, Daniele Garcovich, Sabina Romero, Giuseppe Scuzzo, Giacomo Scuzzo, Adrià Jorba-García, Adrián Curto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is growing interest in cephalometric methods based on artificial intelligence (AI), as they can significantly reduce clinicians’ workload and increase productivity. However, the literature indicates that further research is required to bridge the gap between operator-performed and AI-performed cephalometry. The aim of this study was to compare and evaluate the reproducibility and accuracy of three cephalometric methods: Manual, WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™.MethodsThirty cephalograms from 30 patients (50% male, 50% female) were analyzed. Thirty-five landmarks were recorded using three different methodologies (Manual, WebCeph™, and AngelAligner™) at two time points (T1 and T2).ResultsThe method with the highest number of cephalometric landmarks showing an excellent successful detection rate (SDR) was the manual method, in which 18 of the 35 points demonstrated an SDR < 1 mm in 80% of the analyzed cephalograms, thus being the most accurate. However, greater T1–T2 variations were observed with the manual method. The AI methods showed high and statistically significant correlations for all landmarks (p < 0.01), except for Pg’. The manual method showed low and non-significant correlations for all points except Na’ (p < 0.05) and UL (p < 0.01).ConclusionsFully automated, AI-based cephalometric methods are more reproducible than manual methods. In terms of accuracy, certain AI-based point detections may be comparable to those of an expert, although supervision remains necessary to ensure precise and reliable results.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cephalometric analysis, Manual tracing, Digital tracing, Orthodontics, Treatment planning

Background

Cephalometry represents one of the most significant scientific advances in modern orthodontics. Playing a fundamental role in the diagnosis, planning, and monitoring of dental malocclusions and bone dysplasias, it enables the evaluation of craniofacial structures and growth [1, 2]. A crucial component of this analysis is the precise localization of cephalometric points and planes to accurately measure the positions and relationships between craniofacial and dental structures [1].

Traditionally, conventional cephalometric tracing has involved manually identifying anatomical points on acetate paper placed over a radiograph. Although this remains the most commonly used approach, it is time-consuming [3] and prone to intra-operator errors [4, 5]. Factors such as the observer’s training and clinical experience [6], as well as radiograph quality, can affect tracing accuracy [5].

Rapid technological progress has led to the digitization of cephalometric analysis in orthodontics and the development of artificial intelligence (AI)-based software capable of storing, transmitting, and processing imported images [3, 7, 8]. AI systems perform tasks using algorithms that enable them to learn from data, identify patterns, and make decisions with minimal human intervention [9, 10]. This technology has the potential to automate and enhance accuracy by facilitating image acquisition, improving usability, and allowing faster measurements [3, 11].

Consequently, various AI-based methods have emerged. WebCeph™ is an online platform capable of performing AI-based cephalometric tracings and treatment simulations, among other functions. AngelAligner™ uses an Intelligent Cephalometry System (ICS), which also performs AI-based cephalometric tracing and allows superimpositions and skeletal age determinations, among other features. Both programs are free of charge and can perform cephalometric analyses within seconds, saving substantial time. Several studies have reported that automated cephalometric analysis can be as stable and repeatable as manual methods [3, 7]; therefore, there is growing interest in analyzing and comparing different AI-based cephalometric systems [3, 11, 12].

However, AI is a complex tool that must be used cautiously, with regular verification of its results [3, 13]. The transition from manual to automatic cephalometric landmark identification has not yet reached a consensus for clinical application [14]. Thus, additional studies are required to bridge the gap between clinician-performed and AI-performed cephalometric diagnosis [3, 12, 15]. A deeper understanding of this issue could accelerate technological improvements and support the safe replacement of manual tracing in the future.

The aim of this study was to compare the reproducibility and accuracy of three cephalometric analysis methods (Manual, WebCeph™, and AngelAligner™). The null hypothesis stated that there are no differences in the localization of cephalometric points traced by automated AI systems compared with manual methods.

Methods

Study design

Cephalometric landmark detection was performed using three methods for each patient (manual, WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™) at two different time points (T1 and T2). The same radiograph was used for both time points, with the initial tracing at T1 and a second tracing performed 20 days later, without additional radiation exposure. Description of the cephalometric landmarks used in the present study

All cephalograms were taken prior to treatment for treatment planning purposes [16]. Radiographs were obtained by the same technician using the same X-ray equipment (Planmeca ProMax®, Helsinki, Finland) to ensure consistent resolution and quality. Each patient was positioned standing with feet together, holding the hand support of the machine and biting on a plastic groove. Positioning was controlled using light-alignment lines, keeping the Frankfurt plane parallel to the floor and the tongue against the palate to minimize artifacts. Each cephalogram was analyzed manually and with two AI-based software programs (WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™). These programs were selected because they are freely available, easy to access, and have been used in previous studies, facilitating comparison with existing data.

Manual method

Radiographs were printed on photo paper to provide sufficient resolution and contrast for identifying anatomical landmarks, while allowing standardization of image size, brightness, and contrast before printing. The 30 mm ruler included in the cephalograms was used to ensure the printed images were reproduced at their actual size. Before printing, digital images were inserted into Microsoft Word and adjusted so that the on-screen ruler matched exactly 30 mm when measured with a physical ruler. After printing, the calibration was rechecked to confirm a 1:1 scale.

Manual tracing was performed by two operators on A4-sized acetate sheets using different colored permanent markers. Cephalometric tracing was conducted at two separate times (T1 and T2), and a millimeter ruler was used to assess intra-operator differences (in mm). A maximum of six tracings per day were performed to avoid clinician fatigue and potential errors. The second tracing was completed 20 days later, following the protocol of previous studies [8, 17]. The tracings obtained by Operator A, an orthodontist with three years of experience, were reviewed by Operator B, an orthodontist with twelve years of experience, to assess inter-operator reliability.

WebCeph™ method

An account was created at https://webceph.com. Each image was imported twice—once for each tracing session—with a 20-day interval between imports. The images were uploaded individually for each tracing session and stored in their original, uncompressed format (JPEG) on a secure laboratory computer, without any archive compression or alteration that could affect image quality. All images were calibrated using the 30 mm ruler included in the cephalograms. Cephalometric points were generated by pressing a single button. Operator B subsequently reviewed the cephalograms at both time points, and discrepancies (in mm) were determined between the program-generated points and the anatomical structures.

AngelAligner™ method

Tracing was performed in the same manner as with WebCeph™, ensuring that all images were calibrated beforehand.

Both AI methods were fully automated; no manual adjustments were made after the AI-generated tracings, and the results were saved exactly as generated by the software.

Sample

The study included 30 patients from the Dental Clinic of the University of Salamanca, with an equal distribution of males and females (50% each). The mean age of the patients was 28.53 years.

A formal sample size calculation was performed using G*Power to determine the appropriate number of participants for a study with the present design. Based on standard parameters for this type of analysis, the resulting study power was 0.75, slightly below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.80, but still considered moderately acceptable. To reach a power of 0.80, a total of 34 participants would have been required. Although the current sample size (n = 30) is slightly smaller than this ideal value, it is consistent with comparable studies in the literature [6, 18–22] and sufficient to support the main conclusions of this research.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) complete permanent dentition (excluding third molars); (2) cephalograms taken in a standing position with the Frankfurt plane parallel to the floor; (3) cephalograms obtained using the same equipment (Planmeca ProMax®, Helsinki, Finland); (4) cephalograms including all relevant structures; and (5) cephalograms acquired with a calibration ruler to determine magnification.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) artifacts that could interfere with landmark localization; (2) positional errors indicated by the cephalostat; (3) unerupted or (4) impacted teeth; (5) hypodontia; (6) prosthetic restorations on incisors or molars; (7) presence of intraoral devices; (8) prior orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery; (9) history of facial trauma or bone fracture; (10) cleft lip or palate; (11) diagnosis of systemic disease; (12) craniofacial syndromes; (13) presence of cysts or other bone disorders; and (14) poor-quality cephalograms.

All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Organic Law 3/2018 of December 5 on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Salamanca (code: 1074/2023). All participants received detailed information about the study and provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0, IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), obtaining the main descriptive variables for each method and time point.

The relative percentage frequencies for each point were taken to determine the successful detection rate (SDR) for each method and time point, comparing point detection of the three methods with Operator B. SDR for each landmark was assessed according to errors of < 1 mm (Excellent), 1–1.5 mm (Good), 1.5–2 mm (Fair), 2-2.5 (Acceptable) and > 2.5 mm (Unacceptable). Pearson correlations between methods were also analyzed.

The Mean Radical Error (MRE) in point detection between operators and between AI methods and Operator B were determined to assess inter-rater reliability. The final MRE data reflect the difference in millimeters between the measured value and the expected value for the three methods analyzed.

The reproducibility or consistency of the methods was analyzed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for values at T1 and T2 for Operator A and AI methods’ point detection. To examine the variance components for each point, and the variation of each source of measurement error, Gage R&R analysis was conducted in Minitab.

A repeated measures ANOVA was also performed to determine the interaction between methods and time points.

For the manual method, 18 of the 35 landmarks showed an SDR value of < 1 mm (Excellent) in more than 80% of cases. This level of accuracy (< 1 mm) was also achieved for 10 landmarks with the AngelAligner™ method and for 7 landmarks with WebCeph™.

The cephalometric landmarks with the highest SDR < 1 mm values in the manual method (above 90%) were S, Or, A, Pm, Pg, DU6, ML6, DL6, A’, UL, Stm, and B’. For AngelAligner™, these were S, Pm, Sn, A’, and UL. For WebCeph™, the highest SDR < 1 mm values were found for Pm, Pn, and UL.

High percentages of SDR values > 2.5 mm (Unacceptable) were observed for all methods at Ba, Go, and Xi. Additional high-error points were Po (manual and WebCeph™), Or (WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™), Me and Co (manual), and A’ (WebCeph™). In contrast, AngelAligner™ showed no landmarks with more than 50% of SDR values > 2.5 mm.

Correlations between manual and AI-based methods were found for OR, A, and MU6 in the case of AngelAligner™, and for Pm and Stm in the case of WebCeph™ (Table 3).

MRE analysis

The landmarks with the highest Mean Radial Error (MRE) values were Xi, Co, and Ba in manual tracing; ANS and Ba in AngelAligner™; and A’ and Xi in WebCeph™.

The lowest MRE values were found at Stm, UL, Pg, and B in the manual method; at A’, UL, and Sn in AngelAligner™; and at Pn, UL, and Pm in WebCeph™. Overall, Pm and UL showed very low mean errors across all methods, indicating that these points are easier to detect. In contrast, landmarks with higher MRE values (such as Ba or Xi) proved more challenging to locate (Table 4).

Temporal consistency analysis

Temporal consistency was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) between T1 and T2 for each landmark and method, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results indicated high and statistically significant correlations for all landmarks in the AI-based methods, except for Pg’ in AngelAligner™. The manual method, however, exhibited low or non-significant correlations for most points, except for UL, which did show a significant correlation (Table 4).

To further evaluate variance components for each landmark and identify sources of measurement error, a Gage R&R analysis was performed (Table 5). Repeatability refers to variability within each method, while reproducibility reflects variability across methods. The data indicated minimal variation within each method but substantial variation when both methods were compared, suggesting limited correlation between them (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

Before the broad implementation of AI, it is essential to ensure that automated methods are as accurate and reproducible as conventional techniques. The results of this study reject the null hypothesis that there are no differences in cephalometric landmark localization between automated AI systems and manual methods. Significant differences were observed among the three tracing methods, consistent with previous reports [11, 18, 20, 23, 24].

Regarding reproducibility, greater variations in standard deviation between T1 and T2 were observed in the manual method, indicating lower consistency. This method also showed low or non-significant correlations, reflecting greater variability between time points. In contrast, AI-based methods demonstrated high and statistically significant correlations for nearly all landmarks and stable standard deviations. The Gage R&R results confirmed lower intra-method variation in AI systems. These findings suggest that AI-based landmark detection is more consistent and repeatable, as supported by previous studies [7]. AI consistently detects the same point on identical images, whereas human operators rarely do [7]. This can be attributed to the small size of anatomical points and the limitations of visual detection. Moreover, conventional tracing is affected by intra-operator variability [4, 5, 8, 18, 25] and influenced by factors such as experience and fatigue. In this study, the operators had different experience levels, which may have contributed to these discrepancies.

In terms of accuracy, the manual method was the most precise, followed by AngelAligner™ and WebCeph™. Previous studies [24, 26] reported high percentages of points with SDR < 1 mm, particularly S, U1, L1, Sn, and A’, when using AI-based systems. In our study, U1, L1, and LL also showed high SDR < 1 mm values with AngelAligner™. Conversely, Jeon and Lee [19] described difficulties in detecting dental points due to overlapping anatomical structures, especially L1, which can be obscured by the maxillary incisor in cases of overbite, overjet, crowding, or malocclusion [11]. In contrast, in our study, L1 showed SDR < 1 mm values ranging from 63.3% to 86.7%, and it was not among the most difficult points to detect. MU6 and ML6 appeared easier for human operators than for AI, likely because AI systems struggle with overlapping adjacent structures such as premolars [7, 11, 19, 25, 27].

While earlier studies identified A and Po as the least accurately detected points using manual tracing [4, 28, 29], our findings indicate that Me, Go, Xi, and Co were the most challenging. Xi was particularly difficult across methods, probably due to its involvement with multiple structures and the need for additional measurements, which increase the risk of error accumulation. AngelAligner™ showed no landmarks in the “unacceptable” accuracy range (> 2.5 mm). Some studies have reported higher percentages of points with SDR < 2 mm (82.11–85%) when using AI, but did not report SDR values beyond that threshold [3, 4, 30].

Several landmarks, such as Po, Or, Go, and PNS, are inherently difficult to detect [18, 26] because of overlapping cranial structures [4, 25, 26], and bilateral points add complexity in determining the midpoint [25]. Our results support these findings. Ba also exhibited high MRE across methods, likely due to overlap with the styloid process. In these cases, human operators can better differentiate overlapping structures. Furthermore, AI performance depends on radiograph quality and resolution [18] and may vary with different radiographic systems [24]. Consequently, AI may struggle to identify bilateral or superimposed structures accurately [7, 11, 19, 25, 27, 31].

From a clinical perspective, small differences in landmark identification (< 2 mm) may have relevant implications depending on the clinical context. In orthodontic diagnosis, growth assessment, or treatment simulation, such discrepancies usually fall within acceptable clinical tolerance [3, 7, 32, 33]. However, they may influence the assessment of therapeutic outcomes [34], and in orthognathic surgery or treatment simulations, even millimetric variations may affect surgical planning and postoperative predictions [35, 36]. Therefore, clinicians must consider the magnitude of measurement errors when interpreting cephalometric results and making diagnostic or treatment decisions.

In this study, some landmarks showed comparable results between AI and manual methods. Nonetheless, the Gage R&R report revealed considerable variation between the methods. Previous research indicates that AI alone is not sufficiently accurate for landmark identification and that manual supervision is required to improve reliability [8, 14, 37–40]. These findings align with ours. However, effective supervision demands additional time and training, which may affect clinical efficiency. Thus, strategies are needed to balance automation and human oversight while maintaining diagnostic quality and minimizing operational costs.

AI use in orthodontic diagnosis is an emerging field with encouraging results [3, 11, 13, 15, 24, 41]. It has the potential to reduce clinicians’ workload, improve diagnostic accuracy, enhance decision-making, increase treatment predictability, and save both time and energy [13, 17, 23, 31]. Nevertheless, further research is needed to support its reliable clinical implementation [39, 40, 42]. Broader datasets that represent diverse populations are required to increase the robustness and generalizability of AI models [40]. Current evidence suggests that AI methods can be used cautiously in clinical practice [8, 23]: they are sufficiently reliable for certain applications but cannot yet replace manual tracing entirely [11]. Because AI still shows higher error rates for specific landmarks, recent studies [8, 14, 40] emphasize the need for collaborative research to strengthen AI model reliability and ensure their safe integration into clinical workflows.

Image quality in lateral cephalometric radiographs also plays a crucial role in AI-assisted landmark detection and can significantly influence diagnostic reliability [31, 39]. Therefore, AI integration in clinical practice must take image quality into account to ensure valid diagnostic outcomes. Further studies should systematically evaluate how variations in radiographic quality affect AI performance in real clinical settings, with the goal of achieving safe and precise orthodontic diagnostics.

Although the sample size in this study was limited, it was adequate to detect significant effects with moderate statistical power. Larger samples would improve statistical robustness and strengthen future conclusions. Future research could also explore differences across ethnic groups rather than a solely Caucasian population. To better assess operator-related variability in the manual method, it may be useful to have operators trace points on separate acetates to reduce potential bias. While intra-operator reproducibility and MRE provide an indirect measure of accuracy, future studies could include formal diagnostic accuracy metrics (e.g., specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value) to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic performance of AI-based tracing. Expanding the range of software tested could further help identify sources of point-selection error and inform the development of correction algorithms for future AI systems.

Conclusions

Fully automated, AI-based cephalometric methods are more consistent than manual tracing techniques. Among the AI systems analyzed, AngelAligner™ demonstrated greater accuracy than WebCeph™. Although manual tracing remains the most accurate method overall, certain AI-based point detections can achieve levels of precision comparable to those of an experienced clinician. Expert verification of AI-generated landmarks is therefore recommended to ensure optimal diagnostic accuracy and reliability.
