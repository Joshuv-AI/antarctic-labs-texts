A Nomogram for Predicting Metachronous Gastric Cancer After Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection of Early Gastric Cancer Following Successful Helicobacter pylori Eradication
Authors: Shangtao Mao, Miao Liu, Tao Zhao, Qiong Yan, Ying Xiang, Hai Wu, Wenjun Li, Hongji Tao, Duanming Zhuang, Lei Wang, Guifang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDue to the preservation of the entire stomach after endoscopic resection, the occurrence of metachronous gastric cancer (MGC) remains a possibility. In this study, we investigated the incidence and risk factors for MGC in patients with early gastric cancer who underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and successfully eradicated Helicobacter pylori.Materials and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted of 1,191 patients who underwent ESD and successfully eradicated H. pylori at the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University. Endoscopic surveillance was performed at 3, 6, and 12 months post-resection, and annually thereafter. MGC was defined as the development of a new cancer at a site other than the primary gastric cancer site, at least 1 year after the initial endoscopic resection.ResultsA total of 77 patients were diagnosed with MGC during a median follow-up of 41.5 months. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a 5-year cumulative incidence of MGC of 9.4% after successful H. pylori eradication. Multivariate analysis of the training set using Cox proportional hazards models identified male sex, severe atrophic gastritis, multiple gastric cancers before H. pylori eradication, and smoking history as independent risk factors for MGC. The nomogram exhibited favorable discrimination, with area under the curves of 0.767 and 0.822 in the training set and 0.724 and 0.745 in the testing set at 3 and 5 years, respectively.ConclusionsPatients with gastric cancer who undergo endoscopic resection, even after successful H. pylori eradication, should undergo annual and continuous endoscopic surveillance for MGC.Keywords: Stomach neoplasms; Neoplasms, second primary; Helicobacter pylori; Endoscopic submucosal dissection; Comorbidity

INTRODUCTION

Early gastric cancer (EGC) refers to gastric cancer that is confined to the mucosa and submucosa, regardless of the presence of lymph node metastasis. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become a widely recognized and effective minimally invasive treatment for EGC, with advantages including minimal trauma, rapid postoperative wound healing, and a favorable prognosis [1,2]. However, because the entire stomach is preserved after endoscopic resection, metachronous gastric cancer (MGC) remains a potential risk [3,4,5]. The incidence of metachronous tumors after ESD for EGC ranges from 2.5% to 4.5% [6,7]. The cumulative incidence of MGC at 5, 7, and 10 years is 9.5%, 13.1%, and 22.7%, respectively [8].

The eradication of Helicobacter pylori has been shown to reduce the incidence of MGC after endoscopic resection of EGC [9]. However, despite the successful eradication of H. pylori, MGC may still occur, with an annual incidence of 0.8%–4.1% [4,5,10,11,12,13,14]. This persistence of risk is often attributed to irreversible histological changes, including atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, or the concept of a point of no return in carcinogenesis. Although previous studies have identified male sex, severe atrophic gastritis, and multiple gastric tumors before H. pylori eradication as independent risk factors for MGC [11,12,15], most of these investigations were limited by relatively short follow-up periods or a primary focus on comparing eradicated versus non-eradicated groups. However, these studies largely overlooked the potential impact of patient comorbidities. Emerging evidence suggests that comorbidities, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), obesity, type 2 diabetes, and Parkinson’s disease, are associated with an increased risk of MGC [16,17,18]. Additionally, a strong positive correlation has been observed between hypertension, stroke, and gastric cancer globally [19,20]. Nevertheless, the specific role of comorbidities in MGC development after H. pylori eradication remains unclear.

Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to develop a novel predictive nomogram for patients with EGC who underwent ESD to achieve successful H. pylori eradication. Beyond identifying risk factors, we specifically evaluated integrating metabolic comorbidities into the prediction model to establish a more accurate risk-stratification system for this distinct population with residual risk.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted at the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University, China. Between January 2014 and June 2020, 2,039 patients with EGC underwent ESD. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) loss to follow-up, 2) postoperative follow-up period of less than 12 months, 3) history of gastrectomy, 4) scheduled surgery within a year, 5) patients without available H. pylori infection status, and 6) patients with previously negative H. pylori infection or those who had successfully eradicated H. pylori prior to initial gastric cancer diagnosis. Ultimately, 1,191 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patients included in this study.

ESD = endoscopic submucosal dissection; EGC = early gastric cancer.

Given the retrospective nature of this study, the need for informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (IRB No. 2024-395-01). Informed consent was obtained from all participants via telephonic communication. The ethics committee approved this form of consent, exempting the need for signed written consent, as the study was a retrospective analysis based on existing medical records. The participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits during telephone conversations, and their verbal consent was recorded. This process complies with national and institutional regulations.

Detection and eradication of H. pylori infection

H. pylori infection was assessed using one or more of the following tests: urea breath test, serum anti-H. pylori antibody test, or rapid urease test. Evidence of H. pylori infection was defined as at least one positive test result. For eradication therapy, patients received oral lansoprazole (30 mg), amoxicillin (750 mg), and clarithromycin (200 mg) twice daily for 1 week. Successful eradication was confirmed by a negative urea breath test at least one month after treatment completion. In cases of eradication failure, the patients underwent second-line therapy. The decision to administer third-line or subsequent eradication regimens was made at the discretion of the attending physician. Strict adherence to these eradication and confirmation protocols was mandatory for study inclusion to ensure the reliability of H. pylori-negative status.

Data collection

The following data were collected: age, sex, tumor size, histological type, invasion depth, lymphatic involvement, vascular involvement, degree of gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and comorbidities. Histological types were classified as follows [21]: 1) well-differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma (tub1), 2) moderately differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma (tub2), 3) papillary adenocarcinoma (pap), 4) poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma (por), 5) signet-ring cell carcinoma (sig), and 6) mucinous adenocarcinoma (muc). When more than one histological type was present within a tumor, the two dominant types were recorded in descending order according to the hierarchy (tub2 > tub1). Tub1, tub2, and pap were categorized as differentiated types, while por, sig, and muc were classified as undifferentiated types. In cases where the lesions contained two histological components, the classification was based on the component comprising the larger proportion. The tumor invasion depth was divided into two categories: confined to the mucosal layer (M) and invading the submucosal layer (SM). The degree of gastric mucosal atrophy was graded into 3 levels—mild (C0–C1), moderate (C2–C3), and severe (O1–O3)—according to the Kimura-Takemoto classification [22]. The degree of intestinal metaplasia was assessed using the Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia staging system [20,23] and classified as none, mild, moderate, or severe. The pathological and endoscopic features were independently evaluated by 2 experienced pathologists and endoscopists. A smoking history was defined as a cumulative smoking exposure of ≥26.7 pack-years, based on the categorization criteria used in previous studies [24,25]. Alcohol consumption was defined as the consumption of alcohol at least twice per week.

Surveillance endoscopy and definition of metachronous gastric cancer

Follow-up endoscopic examinations were performed at 3, 6, and 12 months after the initial ESD, and annually thereafter. MGC was defined as a newly developed gastric cancer detected at least one year after the initial ESD. To distinguish MGC from local recurrence, the new lesion was required to be clearly separated from the ulcer scar of the previous endoscopic resection by normal mucosa [26]. The diagnosis of MGC was confirmed histologically based on specimens obtained by biopsy, endoscopic resection, or gastrectomy. The follow-up period was defined as the time from the initial ESD to the date of the last endoscopic examination or histological confirmation of MGC occurrence.

Nomogram development and testing

The dataset was randomly divided into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio. The baseline characteristics of the patients in both sets were well balanced and are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. A nomogram was subsequently developed. The validation set was used solely for the external evaluation of the model. Statistically significant risk factors were identified using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses performed exclusively on the training set. Subsequently, a nomogram was developed based on significant risk factors derived from the final multivariate model.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify independent risk factors for MGC. Covariates with clinical or statistical significance (P<0.05) in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. To address the potential bias arising from interval-censored data, we performed a sensitivity analysis using an interval-censored Cox proportional hazards model. The cumulative incidence of MGC was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The discrimination and calibration of the nomogram were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calibration plots, respectively. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net benefits at different threshold probabilities for the training and test datasets. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.0.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

During a median follow-up time of 41.51 months, MGC was detected in 77 patients (6.5%, 77/1,191). The Kaplan-Meier curve showed a 5-year cumulative incidence of MGC of 9.4% after successful eradication of H. pylori (Fig. 2). Table 1 presents the clinicopathological characteristics of the 1,191 patients included in this study. The cohort consisted predominantly of males (64.8%, 816/1,191). Prior to successful H. pylori eradication, severe gastric mucosal atrophy was observed endoscopically in 24.2% (288/1,191) of patients, and 14.1% (168/1,191) had multiple gastric cancers. The age distributions of the MGC and Non-MGC groups were roughly the same. There were no significant differences in tumor location, histopathological type, invasion depth, or ulceration between the 2 groups.

The multivariate analysis identified male sex, severe atrophic gastritis, multiple gastric cancers before H. pylori eradication, and smoking history as independent risk factors for the development of MGC (Table 2). Nomogram for predicting MGC occurrence

The nomogram for predicting MGC occurrence is shown in Fig. 4. This nomogram estimates the probability of MGC occurrence at 3 and 5 years after ESD in patients with successful H. pylori eradication. The area under the curve (AUC) in the training set was 0.767 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.695–0.833) at 3 years and 0.822 (95% CI, 0.759–0.881) at 5 years (Fig. 5A). In the testing set, the AUC values were 0.724 (95% CI, 0.551–0.871) and 0.745 (95% CI, 0.603–0.874) at 3 and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 5B). Calibration of the nomogram in both the training and test sets was assessed, and the calibration curves (P>0.05) demonstrated good agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 5C and D). DCA demonstrated the clinical utility of the nomogram (Fig. 6). In the test set, the model provided a positive net benefit superior to both the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a threshold probability range of approximately 3%–15%.

To illustrate the clinical application of the nomogram, we selected two male patients with MGC from the test set. One male patient was diagnosed with EGC in 2019, along with O1-grade atrophic gastritis, a history of smoking, and MASLD. Based on the nomogram, the patient’s total score was 239 points, with predicted 3- and 5-year MGC probabilities of 56% and 68%, respectively. A follow-up endoscopy revealed MGC in 2021 (Fig. 7A). The other male patient had a total score of 268 points, with predicted 3- and 5-year MGC probabilities of 65% and 70%, respectively (Fig. 7B).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to incorporate metabolic comorbidities, specifically MASLD and diabetes, into a predictive model for MGC in post-ESD patients with successful H. pylori eradication. While previous studies have focused heavily on mucosal changes, our findings demonstrate that systemic metabolic status plays a pivotal role in the development of MGC in the post-eradication setting.

A multicenter prospective study in Japan reported a 5-year cumulative incidence of MGC of 15.0% after successful H. pylori eradication [15]. Previous studies have reported an annual incidence of MGC ranging from 0.8% to 4.1%. In our study, the 5-year cumulative incidence of MGC after successful H. pylori eradication was 9.4%. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in patient characteristics, with studies showing a higher incidence generally including a greater proportion of males and patients with severe atrophic gastritis, both of which are independent risk factors for MGC.

Our study identified several independent risk factors for MGC occurrence, including male sex, severe atrophic gastritis, smoking history, and multiple gastric cancers before successful H. pylori eradication. Sex differences in gastric cancer incidence have been well established in epidemiological studies [26]. Consistent with prior research, smoking is associated with an increased risk of MGC [10,12,13]. In China, the smoking rate among men ranges from 40% to 60% [27,28], second only to Indonesia. In our study population, the smoking rate among male individuals was 48.1%, which increased to 66.6% in those who developed MGC. Therefore, after H. pylori eradication, smoking cessation becomes an important modifiable factor in reducing the risk of MGC. Physicians should emphasize the importance and benefits of smoking cessation during patient follow-ups.

Several studies have also reported that severe atrophic gastritis is a risk factor for MGC development [12,13,14,29]. The underlying mechanism may involve H. pylori-induced inflammatory responses, leading to aberrant DNA methylation in gastric mucosal cells, and the accumulation of such methylation is strongly linked to gastric cancer within the epigenetic domain [30,31]. Patients with severe atrophic gastritis resulting from prolonged H. pylori infection and chronic inflammation exhibit elevated methylation levels. A Japanese multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trial demonstrated that, over a median 3-year follow-up, the cumulative incidence of MGC was reduced in the eradication group [10]. However, conflicting results have been reported. For example, Choi et al. [13] found that H. pylori eradication after endoscopic resection did not reduce the incidence of MGC over a median 3-year follow-up. This may be explained by irreversible gastric mucosal atrophy caused by H. pylori. Most patients in China undergo gastrointestinal endoscopy because of digestive symptoms. By the time EGC is diagnosed, patients are often older and have had H. pylori infection for many years. Prolonged infection can lead to severe gastric atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, and the gastric mucosal damage cannot be reversed despite the H. pylori eradication. Not only does mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia persist, but more importantly, the methylation level increases. Therefore, the eradication of H. pylori prior to the progression of mucosal atrophy may be beneficial.

According to epidemiological surveys in China, the prevalence of MASLD is 36.8% [32], and it is closely associated with various gastrointestinal cancers [33]. Our previous study found a correlation with MGC [29]. This study further confirmed that after H. pylori eradication, MASLD remained an important factor for MGC. After successful H. pylori eradication, reducing the BMI through aerobic exercise and improving the metabolic status are crucial for reducing the incidence of MGC.

Furthermore, the potential effect of long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use on MGC development has attracted increasing attention. Some studies have suggested that long-term PPI use is associated with an increased risk of gastric cancer, even after H. pylori eradication [34], possibly because of PPI-induced hypergastrinemia, worsening of corporal atrophic gastritis, or alterations in the gastric microbiota [35]. However, this association remains controversial because other large-scale cohort studies and meta-analyses failed to find a significant correlation [36]. In the present study, we could not evaluate this factor because data on the precise duration and adherence to maintenance PPI therapy in an outpatient setting were not systematically recorded in our retrospective cohort. Future prospective studies with rigorous medication tracking are warranted to clarify this issue.

Several nomograms have been developed to predict MGC. The FAMISH score, which includes six clinical predictors (male sex, intestinal metaplasia, positive family history of gastric cancer, advanced age, concurrent gastric lesions, and persistent H. pylori infection), helps identify patients at low to moderate risk for MGC recurrence [37]. However, the AUC for predicting 5-year MGC was 0.708, suggesting that relying solely on clinicopathological features may be insufficient for this specific population with a residual risk. A distinguishing feature of our study is the integration of systemic comorbidities into the risk prediction model. We incorporated MASLD and diabetes mellitus, recognizing the growing epidemiological evidence linking metabolic syndrome to gastrointestinal carcinogenesis [18,33,38,39]. Although these factors showed statistical significance in the univariate Cox analysis and not in the multivariate analysis, their inclusion was critical for optimizing the model’s overall performance. This aligns with the concept that MGC pathogenesis in the post-eradication setting is multifactorial and driven by the cumulative burden of metabolic and environmental stressors rather than a single independent factor. Consequently, our novel nomogram demonstrated superior discrimination with a 5-year AUC of 0.822, which significantly outperformed the standard FAMISH model (AUC, 0.708). This improvement underscores the value of a more holistic, individualized surveillance approach that extends beyond the gastric mucosa to the patient’s systemic metabolic health.

Assessing the net benefits of a model is crucial for its translation into clinical practice. DCA confirmed that the nomogram achieved a net benefit within a threshold probability range of 3%–15%. Although this range is concentrated at the lower end of the probability spectrum, it corresponds to the low incidence of MGC (9.4%) observed in our study. In the context of postoperative surveillance of gastric cancer, strategies typically prioritize early detection. A predicted MGC risk of 5%–10% may often be regarded as a sufficient indicator to justify intensive endoscopic follow-up. Therefore, despite the relatively narrow threshold range, these findings suggest that the nomogram has potential clinical utility in assisting clinicians in identifying high-risk individuals for targeted surveillance.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and 6.4% (114/1,780) of potentially eligible patients were lost to follow-up immediately after ESD. However, this low attrition rate suggests that the potential impact on hazard estimates is likely minimal. Another potential concern regarding selection bias involves the exclusion of patients with persistent H. pylori infection, which might be considered a source of selection bias. This design strictly aligns with current clinical guidelines, which strongly recommend eradication for all post-ESD patients. Therefore, our cohort reflects a real-world population of patients receiving standard care who continue to face a residual risk of MGC despite H. pylori eradication. Second, not all patients adhered to the endoscopic surveillance schedule, and the actual onset time of MGC may lag behind its actual biological onset. Third, potential risk factors such as long-term PPI use were not included in the nomogram because these data were not systematically recorded in this retrospective cohort. Fourth, genetic and epigenetic markers associated with MGC were not assessed in the study population. Fifth, the nomogram model lacks external validation in a multicenter cohort. Therefore, future studies incorporating large-scale multicenter data are warranted to further evaluate MGC risk factors and surveillance strategies after successful H. pylori eradication.

In summary, even after the successful eradication of H. pylori, patients who underwent endoscopic resection for EGC still required annual, continuous endoscopic surveillance for MGC. The nomogram, including male sex, smoking history, MASLD, diabetes, multiple tumors, and atrophic gastritis as predictive factors, shows favorable discrimination and calibration for individualized MGC risk prediction and surveillance guidance. The performance of this predictive nomogram model should be validated in future prospective studies.
