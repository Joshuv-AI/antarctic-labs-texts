A Facile Heat-Up Strategy for Synthesizing InP/ZnSe/ZnS Quantum Dots
Authors: Krishna P. Acharya, Jiamin Huang, Amelia Waters, Binod K. Rai, Corey R. Martin, Mikhail Zamkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colloidal InP/ZnSe/ZnS core/shell/shell quantum dots (QDs) have emerged as a leading alternative to Cd-based QDs. However, their synthesis is more complex because of the susceptibility of InP QDs to oxidation and surface defects. This necessitates a multistep, labor-intensive, layer-by-layer shell growth approach with intermediate surface treatment or etching steps to maintain interface quality and optical performance. Here, we present a simplified approach to the synthesis of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs, wherein all zinc precursors are introduced at the beginning along with InP QDs, followed by the sequential addition of chalcogenide precursors. It is demonstrated that heterogeneous shell growth is achieved via the consumption of monomers by the cores. Such a simplified strategy eliminates the need for successive zinc precursor injection during shell growth and yields highly photoluminescent (PL) green QDs (PL quantum yield = 75%, peak at 532 nm) with a narrow full width at half maximum (41 nm). The synthesized InP/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS QDs exhibited stable multiexciton performance under high excitation fluence. We expect that, by simplifying the workflow and improving reproducibility, this strategy could become broadly applicable across InP-based and other core/shell chalcogenide systems.

Introduction

Colloidal semiconductor quantum dots (QDs) have emerged as transformative materials for optoelectronic technologies ranging from energy conversion to quantum information processing, , owing to their exceptional optical properties and low-cost solution processability. , Among promising material systems, indium phosphide-based QDs have gained significant attention as a leading, less-toxic alternative to Cd-based semiconductors. Beyond their environmental advantages, indium phosphide (InP) also benefits from a relatively large exciton Bohr radius (∼10 nm), which enables band gap tuning across a broad spectral range from blue to near-infrared.

A persistent challenge with as-synthesized InP QDs is their high density of surface defects, arising from indium and phosphorus dangling bonds, as well as oxidized species. − These defect sites can be removed by surface etching followed by passivation with a wider band gap shell, such as ZnSe and/or ZnS, which effectively neutralizes surface traps and yields high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs). − The resulting InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs show excellent optical properties, enabling performance comparable to Cd-based QDs in a broad range of applications, including down-conversion phosphors in displays and solid-state lighting, electroluminescent quantum dot light-emitting diodes, luminescent solar concentrators, bioimaging, sensors, hydrogen-generation photocatalysts, and other advanced technologies. ,,−

Conventional syntheses of InP/ZnSe/ZnS core/shell/shell QDs typically involve multiple injections of zinc and chalcogen precursors during the shell growth phase. − The synthetic challenges increase sharply for large-diameter QDs (≥10 nm), which exhibit suppressed Auger recombination, longer exciton lifetimes, and improved photostabilityproperties that are critical for high-performance optoelectronic applications. , A widely used approach for growing such large core/shell QDs is the layer-by-layer (LBL) or closely related successive ionic layer adsorption and reaction (SILAR) methods, in which cation and anion precursors are sequentially added and annealed after each injection. ,, For example, growing ∼10 nm InP/ZnSe QDs from a ∼2.2 nm InP core using SILAR involves on the order of ∼12 sequential additions of each Zn and Se precursor (with a monolayer approximated as half the ZnSe lattice constant, a/2 = 0.283 nm), resulting in a lengthy, labor-intensive process that accumulates dosing errors. In addition, LBL and SILAR protocols require accurate estimates of core sizes and molar extinction coefficients to calculate per-monolayer dosing, and synthesis often includes in situ etching to remove surface defects, which shrinks the core and further complicates stoichiometry. These limitations underscore the need for simpler, faster methods to synthesize high-quality InP-based core/shell QDs.

In this work, we report a simplified heat-up method for synthesizing InP/ZnSe/ZnS and InP/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS core/shell QDs. All zinc precursors are introduced at the beginning of the reaction along with InP cores, followed by in situ etching of InP using aqueous HF and a sequential addition of chalcogen precursors. This approach eliminates repeated precursor injections, shortens total synthesis time relative to LBL or SILAR, and yields high-quality, large-diameter core/shell QDs. Our study presents detailed synthetic procedures and a full characterization of the resulting QD product, including optical, structural, morphological, thermal, and carrier dynamics properties.

Experimental Section

Materials

Indium­(III) acetate (anhydrous, 99.99%, Thermo Scientific), anhydrous zinc acetate (99.98%, Thermo Scientific), oleic acid (tech, 90%, Thermo Scientific), trioctylamine (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), 1-octadecene (ODE, technical grade, 90%, Thermo Scientific), trioctyl phosphine (TOP, >85%, TCI), tris­(trimethylsilyl)­phosphine (TMSP, 95%, Thermo Scientific), acetone (99.5%, Aldrich), hexane (≥95%, Sigma-Aldrich), isopropanol (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), hydrofluoric acid (HF, ≈49.5%, Thermo Fisher), selenium powder (Se, 200 mesh, 99.999%, Thermo Scientific), and sulfur flakes (S, 99.998%, Aldrich) were used as received without further purification.

InP QDs Synthesis

A mixture containing 1 mmol (292 mg) indium acetate, 3 mmol (847 mg) oleic acid, and 10 mL ODE was degassed under vacuum at 120 °C in a 3-neck 50 mL flask under the Schlenk line until the bubbling of the reaction product, acetic acid, ceased (∼40 min). Then, the flask was switched to a nitrogen environment, and a solution containing 0.74 mmol (215 μL TMSP) in 1.0 mL TOP was injected into the flask and heated (note: TMSP is a very reactive and pyrophoric compound; therefore, it should be handled with care). The temperature was increased from 120 to 270 °C over approximately 12 min. Once the temperature reached 270 °C, the flask was removed from the heating mantle and allowed to cool under an air stream. InP QD was purified in a glovebox by precipitating with acetone (crude QD solution to acetone ratio 1:4). The solid was separated by centrifugation and dissolved in hexane. This process was repeated one more time for additional purification. The QD solid was dispersed in 5.0 mL hexane. This method typically results in InP QDs with the first excitonic peak at around 460 nm (also see Figure S1). TEM measurement of this core is challenging due to its very small size, so the diameter of the InP core is estimated to be 2.23 nm using the sizing curve equation. 1 Eg=1.401+3.493/d1.172

Here, E g and d are the band gap energy and the diameter of the InP QDs, respectively.

Preparation of Shell Precursors

A 1.0 M trioctylphosphine selenide (TOP-Se) solution was prepared by dissolving 15 mmol (1.18 g) of Se powder in 15.0 mL of TOP. Similarly, a 1.0 M trioctylphosphine sulfide (TOP-S) solution was prepared by dissolving 15 mmol (481 mg) of sulfur flakes in 15.0 mL of TOP. Both solutions were stirred at room temperature until visibly transparent solutions were obtained.

Core/Shell QD Synthesis

A mixture containing 4.0 mmol zinc acetate (734 mg), 8.0 mmol oleic acid (2.26 g), and 5.0 mL ODE in a 50 mL 3-neck flask was connected to a Schlenk line and degassed under vacuum at 120 °C. When the bubbling ceased (about 40 min), 5.0 mL of trioctylamine was injected and further degassed until bubbles ceased (∼5 min), followed by the injection of 0.70 mL InP QDs (∼35 mg) from the stock solution. Then, the solution was degassed under vacuum to remove hexane for ∼5 min, switched to nitrogen, and 50 μL of aqueous HF and 1.0 mL 1.0 M TOP-Se were injected under vigorous stirring. We observed that the immediate application of high vacuum following the addition of HF caused the reaction mixture to reflux vigorously. To mitigate this, the mixture was gently degassed under vacuum at 120 °C. After the reflux subsided, the temperature was gradually increased to 300 °C. During this process, the mixture was maintained under high vacuum up to 150 °C to effectively remove water and volatile organic compounds. At 150 °C, the system was transitioned to a nitrogen atmosphere, and the reaction mixture was annealed at 300 °C under nitrogen for 45 min. The optimum annealing time for the shell growth was found to be 45 min because the InP/ZnSe QDs exhibited the least scattering at higher wavelengths, as revealed in the absorbance spectra (see Figure S1). In addition, the intensity of photoluminescence (PL) was at its maximum and did not change significantly after 45 min of annealing. The flask was removed from the heating mantle and cooled to 150 °C using an air stream. At this temperature (150 °C), 1.0 mL of 1.0 M TOP-S was injected. Then, the reaction mixture was heated further and annealed at 300 °C for 45 min. For InP/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS (InP-CS QDs), all processes and reagents used were the same, except a 1.0 mL mixture containing 0.5 mL S and Se each was injected at 150 °C between the ZnSe and ZnS layers. For the continuous injection method, the shell was grown by injecting the same amounts of chalcogenide precursors at the rate of 22 μL/min at 300 °C using a syringe pump, maintaining the same annealing time. The core/shell QDs were purified two times by precipitation using isopropanol (QDs to isopropanol 1:5 ratio), centrifuged, and the QD solid was dissolved in hexane for characterization.

Characterization

Absorbance and PL spectra were measured with an Ocean Optics USB 4000 spectrometer with Ocean View software. The PL spectrum of only InP QDs, which exhibit very weak emission, was measured by excitation with a pulsed 405 nm laser diode (PDL 800-D, Picoquant), and emitted photons were collected by a fiber optic cable connected to an Andor Shamrock 303i spectrograph and measured using an Andor Newton 970 EMCCD. PL decay dynamics were acquired via 405 nm pulsed excitation (Picoquant), and photons were detected with an ID100–50 single-photon detector (ID Quantique) and processed by an SPC-130 TCSPC module (Becker & Hickl). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired at 200 kV on carbon films coated with a copper grid (300 mesh, obtained from Electron Microscope Sciences) using a Thermo Fisher Talos F200X G2 S/TEM. Absolute PL quantum yields were measured using a Quantaurus-QY absolute PL quantum yield spectrometer C11347 (Hamamatsu, Inc., Japan).

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) experiments were conducted to confirm the crystallinity and phase purity of the core and core/shell QDs. Powder samples were mounted in a MiTeGen microloop dipped in epoxy. Data collection was performed at 300 K using a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy-S X-ray diffractometer equipped with a Mo source (λ = 0.71073 Å, V = 50 kV, and I = 1 mA), a HyPix-3000 hybrid pixel array detector, and a PhotonJet-i microfocus X-ray source. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the QDs was performed using a NETZSCH TG 209 analyzer in ambient air. The samples were dried under vacuum until the mass of the dried QDs was constant. Then, a ∼10 mg sample was transferred into an alumina crucible and heated to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrum was recorded using a Hitachi TM3000 benchtop scanning electron microscope (SEM). Solid QDs were placed on an SEM stub with carbon tape and analyzed using a 15 kV accelerating voltage.

Results and Discussion

Shell Growth and Defects Passivation

Considering the lattice constants of cubic InP, ZnSe, and ZnS (5.87 Å (ICDD PDF 00–032–0452), 5.66 Å (ICDD PDF-00-037-1463), and 5.41 Å (ICDD PDF-04-006-2561)), the lattice mismatches between InP and ZnS, and InP and ZnSe, are 7.80% and 3.66%, respectively. Therefore, a ZnSe layer is usually introduced between InP and ZnS to relieve the strain. The schematic diagram of the synthesis of the InP/ZnSe/ZnS core/shell morphology used in this work is illustrated in Figure a. The zinc oleate precursor, in the amount necessary for the growth of all the shell layers, is prepared in the three-neck flask at the beginning. Selenium and sulfur precursors are then injected sequentially and annealed as described in the Experimental Section.

Schematic diagram illustrating the (a) synthesis of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs using the heat-up method. (b) Energetic origin of electron, hole, and deep trap states in InP QDs. The valence band and the conduction band arise from the bonding and antibonding orbitals of sp3-hybridized In (5s/5p) and P­(3s/3p) atomic orbitals. Surface-related defects, including In/P dangling bonds and interfacial InPO x species, introduce localized electron, hole, and deep trap states within the bandgap. The bonding and antibonding orbitals are represented by merged and split ovals, respectively. The colors in the diagram are used only to represent the origin of defects.

Within this framework, controlled etching of the InP core proved to be a crucial step in forming core/shell QDs with a high PLQY. In general, surface fluorination and HF etching of InP are widely used to mitigate surface defects. − As summarized in Figure b, phosphorus dangling bonds, arising from uncoordinated P orbitals located above the valence band, act as hole traps, while indium dangling bonds from uncoordinated In orbitals serve as electron traps near the conduction band. , In addition, oxidized phosphorus species (PO x ) introduce deep trap defect states that adversely impact the luminescence properties of the InP core and core–shell interface. , The fluorination passivates dangling indium bonds via the formation of In–F bonds, while chemical etching removes the surface oxides, , yielding an oxide-free, clean InP surface suitable for epitaxial shell growth. Diluted HF in organic solvents, as well as in situ creation of HF using organic and inorganic fluorides, have been typically employed for InP surface etching. , In a previous work, the presence of and the etching of phosphorus oxide were confirmed through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and primarily described by the following chemical equation. 2 InPOx+3HF=InF3+H3POx

To evaluate the role of etching in this work, we also synthesized a control batch of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs without etching the InP core. As expected, the resulting nanocrystals exhibited a lower PLQY and broader emission peaks (Figure S2b). In contrast, aqueous HF etching of the InP core in the presence of zinc oleate, followed by the growth of ZnSe and ZnS shells, yielded a PLQY of about 75%. The comparison of absorption spectra between core/shell QDs synthesized with and without the etching step (Figure S2a) reveals an expected blue shift of the InP excitonic feature when etching is performed. This is also corroborated by a blue shift of the PL peak in etched core/shell QDs (from 542 to 532 nm) and narrowing of the PL full width at half-maximum (FWHM) from 46 to 41 nm. Consequently, we can confirm that HF etching removes InPOx, leading to enhanced PLQY and a narrower PL line width. Notably, etching of InP QDs with HF in the absence of zinc oleate reduces PLQY in both the core and the subsequent core/shell structures. A plausible explanation is that Zn2+ coordinates to surface P dangling bonds, modulating the HF accessibility to enable controlled surface modification. This interpretation is consistent with prior observations that zinc additives enhance the PLQY in InP QDs and predominantly reside at the surface as zinc carboxylate species.

Optical Properties and Shell Growth Mechanism

Figure a shows the absorbance spectra at successive shell-growth stages. After etching, the lowest-energy excitonic feature blue-shifts from ∼452 nm to ∼446 nm, consistent with a reduced InP core size. Interestingly, the second excitonic transition becomes more pronounced, which can potentially indicate an improved core facet quality. Meanwhile, growth of the ZnSe shell is evidenced by a marked increase in absorbance intensity below ∼460 nm, which increases further after ZnS overcoating. The ZnSe shell growth causes the first excitonic transition in the absorbance spectrum to red-shift from ∼446 nm to ∼500 nm. This red shift is consistent with quasi-type II band alignment, in which the electron wave function is delocalized to both the InP core and ZnSe shell, while the hole remains in the InP core. This red shift can be explained by the rise of the conduction band edge energy in smaller InP QDs, as previously reported for InP/ZnSe systems. , The spatial separation of charge carriers in resulting quasi-type II heterostructures leads to reduced radiative recombination rates. This property has potential benefits in applications such as photovoltaics, photocatalysis, and photodetection, where efficient charge separation and long carrier lifetimes are essential. For instance, the formation of a ZnSe shell on InP QDs has been shown to induce delocalization of electrons and holes, resulting in a 288% improvement in photocurrent density, reaching 6.2 mA/cm2, compared to photoelectrochemical cells based on bare InP quantum dots for hydrogen generation.

Figure b shows the PL spectra of InP before and after the ZnSe and ZnS shell growth. As-synthesized InP cores exhibit a high density of surface defects, manifested as spectrally broad trap-state emission. Following HF etching, these surface defects were largely removed, and the emission band narrows with a peak centered at 495 nm. Upon deposition of a very thin ZnSe shell, the emission peak red-shifts from 495 to 532 nm, whereas further growth of the ZnSe and ZnS shells does not produce additional spectral shifts, indicating only minimal modification of the band structure at larger shell thicknesses. The absence of PL signal below 460 nm further suggests that isolated ZnSe nanocrystals are not formed via homogeneous nucleation under these conditions.

These observations support the monomer-mediated seeded epitaxial growth mechanism. We hypothesize that during shell formation, zinc oleate reacts with TOP-Se or TOP-S to generate ZnSe and ZnS monomers, which are subsequently consumed at the InP core surface, as schematically illustrated in Figure c. Because zinc is present in excess and multiple monolayer equivalents of Se (and/or S) are introduced, small ZnSe or ZnS nuclei or nanocrystals may transiently form during the initial temperature ramp. Owing to their high surface energy and solubility, these small particles redissolve and redeposit onto the larger InP cores, ultimately yielding a single nanocrystal domain. The absorbance and PL spectra of QDs synthesized via the heat-up method are comparable to those produced using the continuous injection method, in which a syringe pump with injection duration and chalcogenides dose matched to the heat-up protocol (see Figure S3), with all the zinc precursors present at the onset. Other techniques, such as asymmetric strain engineering, cation-exchange-assisted synthesis, and multistage heating strategies, have been employed to significantly improve the color purity, monodispersity, and PLQY in colloidal QDs. − Several reviews have summarized these advances, as well as the synthesis, properties, and applications of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs. , Selected examples of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QD synthesis, precursor injection methods, synthesis times, and optical properties are summarized alongside our results in Table S1. To further increase particle size, InP/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS was grown, whose absorbance and PL spectra are shown in Figure S4. The PLQY of these QDs was observed to be similar to that of InP/ZnSe/ZnS QDs (∼75%), which could be due to the benefits of improved lattice-matched growth of ZnS on ZnSeS being counterbalanced by the increase in particle size. Further characterization of these InP-CS QDs was conducted, as discussed in the following sections.

Structure, Morphology, and Composition Analysis

Figure a compares the PXRD patterns of the InP core and the InP-CS QDs. The InP QDs show broad reflections at ∼12°, 19.9°, and 23°, indexed to the (111), (220), and (311) planes of the zinc-blende phase, consistent with their small crystallite size. After ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS overcoating, these peaks become markedly sharper, reflecting the growth of larger, more ordered crystals. As expected, the reflections shift to higher angles due to incorporation of ZnSe and ZnS, whose lattice constants are smaller than that of InP. A weak feature near ∼14° in the core/shell QDs is likely from the InP core itself. Only under high-temperature (340 °C), thermodynamically driven conditions can the shells adopt the wurtzite structure, which shares similar peak positions, whereas ligand-assisted, kinetically controlled growth in this work favors the zinc blende phase.

The TEM image of the InP-CS QDs (Figure b) reveals predominantly irregular particle shapes. The size distribution was determined by measuring the longest dimension of 146 nanoparticles, yielding an average size of 9.7 ± 1.9 nm. A large-area TEM of these structures is also provided in Figure S5. These particle shapes are consistent with the previously reported InP/ZnSe/ZnS core/shell/shell structures synthesized using a layer-by-layer approach. The irregular morphology primarily arises from the intricate interplay among lattice strain, surface chemistry, and growth kinetics, leading to facet energy-dependent growth of the shell material. When ZnSe was deposited on cubic InP QDs at 300 °C, a multibranched cubic ZnSe shell was formed, driven by kinetically controlled growth. Conversely, increasing the growth temperature to 340 °C resulted in spherical wurtzite-phase InP/ZnSe QDs due to thermodynamically controlled growth conditions, which facilitated surface reconstruction via the migration of Zn and Se atoms, leading to isotropic growth of the shell. Kinetically controlled QD growth typically produces irregular, high-energy facets, which may compromise chemical stability and electronic performance compared to more symmetric shapes. On the other hand, such faceted QDs could be advantageous for various applications, including phosphor layers, which require light scattering, or photocatalysis, which favors exposed high-energy facets to enhance surface reactions. In contrast, devices such as QLEDs or photoanodes benefit from monodisperse, spherical QDs that enable close packing, facilitating efficient charge transport. Achieving this size (∼10 nm) typically requires many iterative injections of cation and anion precursors with extended annealing in layer-by-layer methods. In contrast, the current approach achieves comparable dimensions with minimal precursor additions and good PL efficiency. The composition of the core/shell QDs was measured using SEM (see Figure S6). The EDX spectrum and the elemental mapping confirm the presence of In, P, Zn, Se, and S elements as expected. The results indicate the presence of near-equal amounts of S and Se, reflecting the stoichiometric addition of these precursors to the shell.

Thermal Analysis and Stability

The thermal analysis of the InP QDs and InP-CS QDs was carried out using TGA in air (see Figure a). The results indicate that from room temperature to 300 °C, both samples exhibit minimal mass loss, attributed to the evaporation of solvent and physisorbed species. The InP core QDs show a pronounced mass loss between approximately 300 and 425 °C, corresponding to the rapid combustion of ligands and the volatilization of phosphorus as its oxides, followed by the formation of a more stable indium oxide (In2O3) residue above ≈450 °C. At temperatures exceeding 600 °C, any remaining or partially oxidized indium species undergo further oxidation to form In2O3, which is heavier than the initial material. In contrast, InP-CS QDs exhibit a broader region of ligand loss with additional features at higher temperatures. The bump near 510 °C could be attributed to the oxidation of the ZnSe shell, wherein competing processes of SeO2 volatilization and oxygen uptake result in a nonmonotonic mass change, with complete volatilization by 730 °C.

The stability of InP-CS QDs under long-term storage conditions was assessed by comparing the PL properties of a fresh sample with those of an aged (414th day) sample. The sample was stored at 4 °C in a refrigerated environment. During the storage period, the vial cap was opened multiple times, exposing the sample to air. After aging, the sample was still very bright under ultraviolet light; a slight blue shift in the PL peak was observed, from 543 to 540 nm, alongside a reduction in the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) from 42.9 to 40.3 nm (Figure S7). This change could be attributed to exposure to oxygen and moisture, which likely induced subtle alterations in the chemical environment, such as variations in pH and solvent polarity. The aged InP-CS QDs film deposited on a glass substrate and kept in ambient air demonstrated bright fluorescence under UV light for at least 2 weeks. However, under continuous intense illumination in air with a 440 nm, 45 W source, the sample exhibited a significant decline in emission brightness within 17 min (Figure S6). This degradation is attributed to rapid photo-oxidation of the QDs, resulting in the formation of surface trap states that quench emission. Consequently, proper encapsulation strategies are necessary to maintain the stability of InP-CS QDs for various practical applications.

At the lowest fluence, <N ep> ∼0.1, the decay exhibits a long average lifetime (127.7 ns), consistent with single-exciton recombination. With increasing pump power, the effective PL lifetime decreases systematically. For the highest excitation fluence (<Nep> ∼ 2.5), the average lifetime is shortened by more than a factor of 2 compared to the single-exciton regime. This is consistent with multiexciton populations (biexcitons and higher) that undergo rapid nonradiative Auger recombination, in which exciton energy is transferred to a third carrier rather than emitted as a photon. Thus, even with a thick passivating shell, Auger processes remain a dominant relaxation channel under intense excitations. However, from the application standpoint, the onset of multiexcitons indicates transient access to multiple carriers per particle under high illumination. This regime can be beneficial for light harvesting and photocatalysis, provided that carrier extraction to surfaces or catalytic sites occurs faster than Auger recombination. Correlating TRPL power dependence with catalytic metrics could, therefore, provide a practical route to identify illumination regimes that maximize multicarrier harvesting.

Conclusions

We developed a streamlined synthetic route for fabricating bright InP/ZnSe/ZnS and InP/ZnSe/ZnSeS/ZnS QDs. The method is based on introducing zinc precursors at the beginning of the reaction along with InP QDs, followed by the sequential addition of chalcogenide precursors. A brief aqueous HF treatment is used to remove core surface defects, with the subsequent annealing step driving epitaxial shell growth. The red shift of the absorption and emission features after shell deposition is consistent with a quasi-type II carrier distribution. TRPL measured over varying excitation fluences shows clear multiexciton signatures at higher powers, suggesting operating regimes where carrier extraction is faster than Auger recombination could maximize light-harvesting efficiency.

In contrast to SILAR or LBL growth methods, the present heat-up approach eliminates successive cation and anion dosing for each monolayer. It reliably yields InP-ZnSeS-based core/shell QDs without evidence of isolated ZnSe or ZnS byproducts, and the resulting structural and optical characteristics are comparable to those achieved by using conventional LBL protocols. In addition, this strategy circumvents practical challenges associated with continuous injection, such as syringe and needle clogging due to zinc oleate solidification. Overall, these features significantly reduce synthesis time and establish the heat-up protocol as a practical and scalable route for producing high-quality InP-based core/shell QD systems suitable for solid-state lighting, displays, bioimaging, sensing, and related technologies.
