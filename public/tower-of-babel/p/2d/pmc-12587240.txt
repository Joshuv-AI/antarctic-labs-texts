Treat to target in weight management with semaglutide: Real‐world evidence from an eHealth clinic
Authors: Søren Seier, Kine Stamp‐Larsen, Simon Birk Kjær Jensen, Signe Sørensen Torekov, Henrik Gudbergsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the effectiveness of an online multidisciplinary weight loss service combining individualised semaglutide dosing with intensive behavioural therapy (IBT) in adults living with overweight or obesity.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study included data from Danish participants enrolled in a digital weight loss programme via the Embla app between January 2022 and September 2024. The programme offered semaglutide with personalised dosing alongside evidence‐based recommendations on nutrition, exercise and habits, delivered through IBT. All data collection and monitoring was conducted via the Embla app. The primary outcome was percentage change in body weight at week 64. Secondary outcomes included semaglutide dose, proportions achieving ≥5%, ≥10%, ≥15%, or ≥20% weight loss, and weight change by baseline BMI and dose.ResultsThe study included 2694 participants (78% women, mean age 46.8 ± 10.8 years, mean BMI 34.3 ± 4.8). At weeks 26, 52, and 64, the number of participants that remained in the study was: 1580, 712, and 465. Mean weight loss at week 64 was 16.7% (95% CI: −17.4 to −16.0) with a mean semaglutide dose of 1.08 mg/week (SD 0.54). A total of 98% achieved ≥5% and 82% achieved ≥10% weight loss. Weight change was consistent across BMI classes and medication doses.ConclusionsParticipants achieved clinically meaningful weight loss comparable to that seen in randomised trials, despite using lower semaglutide doses. These findings support the effectiveness of a personalised, treat‐to‐target approach integrating medical and behavioural obesity management in a digital setting.Trial registration https://osf.io/86kmc/ Keywords: cohort study, GLP‐1, obesity care, real‐world evidence, weight management

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing, and progressive disease characterised by excess adiposity, which affects more than one billion people worldwide. 1 Obesity is associated with several health complications, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, various cancers, musculoskeletal disorders, and decreased quality of life. 2

Newly developed gut hormone‐based medications are effective for weight loss and treatment of obesity‐related diseases and are therefore approved for obesity treatment as an adjunct to increased physical activity and reduced calorie diets. Particularly, the glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonist, semaglutide, and the GLP‐1/glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist (RA), tirzepatide, have shown large weight losses of 15%–16% 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 and up to 19%–22.5% 7 respectively, in randomised controlled trials (RCT). GLP‐1 receptor agonists act by enhancing glucose‐dependent insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon release, while also reducing appetite and energy intake through delayed gastric emptying and increased satiety signalling. 8 Several other gut hormone‐based obesity treatments are currently being investigated in clinical trials, such as GLP‐1/GIP/Glucagon triple receptor agonist (retatrutide), 9 GLP‐1/Glucagon receptor dual agonist (survodutide and mazdutide) 10 , 11 and Amylin/GLP‐1 receptor agonist (CagriSema and Amycretin). 12 , 13 with early data suggesting even larger weight losses. In contrast to controlled trials, real‐world effectiveness may be influenced by medication costs, diverse populations, and the fact that many stop medications already within the first year. 14 , 15 Thus, real‐world evidence (RWE) studies are necessary to elucidate the treatment effect and implementation possibilities in clinical practice. Unlike RCTs which assess efficacy under controlled conditions, RWE studies evaluate effectiveness in everyday settings and more diverse patient populations. 16 , 17

Previous, retrospective RWE studies on semaglutide for obesity have shown smaller weight losses (8%–14%) than RCTs. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 These RWE studies are generally limited by a lack of detailed description of the concomitant lifestyle intervention and medication use. A population‐based study showed that only 10% of semaglutide users follow the original titration plan 24 highlighting the discrepancy between controlled trials and real‐world usage. The effectiveness of weight loss depends on the lifestyle intervention together with medical treatment. For instance, exercise combined with GLP‐1 RA reduced body fat more than GLP‐1 RA alone. 25 Obesity medication is predominantly prescribed by general practitioners, 24 which may make it difficult to provide the concomitant high‐quality, non‐pharmacological lifestyle component important for optimal health outcomes. Digital health services may offer a platform for such lifestyle components to be implemented scalably and effectively with enhanced access through self‐monitoring and online calls. 26 , 27 , 28 Indeed self‐monitoring through digital platforms has been associated with weight loss 29 , 30 and could support long‐term weight loss and maintenance. 31 , 32 Additionally, medication usage should be taken into consideration in the evaluation of weight loss efficacy, as lower doses may reduce the severity of side effects 33 as well as costs, 34 and thereby potentially improve long‐term treatment adherence. In this study, we therefore aimed to explore the effect of a digital, multidisciplinary weight loss programme consisting of online intensive behavioural therapy (IBT) with diet, exercise, and coaching combined with personalised doses of semaglutide in a large real‐world cohort. Specifically, our primary objective was to describe changes in body weight and the use of medication.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This is a retrospective analysis of real‐world data obtained from Danish adults engaging in an online multi‐disciplinary weight management program (Embla) between January 2022 and September 2024. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Participants and recruitment

The study included all participants who had completed a screening visit and were considered eligible for the weight loss program. All included participants were motivated for weight loss, agreed to engage in IBT and initiate obesity medication, and were financially able to afford expenses (membership to the program of 995 DKK pr. month (approximately 140 USD) + medication expenses). To be included in the program, the participants completed a medical screening call with a healthcare professional and were approved for semaglutide treatment, based on the following criteria: (1) a self‐reported BMI ≥30 kg/m2 or between 27 and 29.9 kg/m2 with at least one obesity‐related condition (hypertension, dyslipidemia, prediabetes, sleep apnoea and/or known cardiovascular disease); (2) age between 18 and 75 years; (3) at least one reported previous failed attempt to lose weight or maintain a weight loss. Exclusion criteria included unstable diabetes (type 1 or type 2), pregnancy, breastfeeding, planning to conceive, or not using contraception (fertile women), and active eating disorder (except mild to moderate binge eating disorder). A full list of exclusion criteria can be found in Appendix S1.

During an initial video call, potential participants were introduced to the program's content, and it was agreed between the participant and health coach whether the treatment program was appropriate for the individual in terms of personal weight loss and overall health goals, as well as general life situation. This was followed by a medical screening by a certified physician to access eligibility for medical treatment. The decision to initiate semaglutide treatment was based not only on whether the patient meets the European Medicines Agency's (EMA) official guidelines but also on a thorough case‐by‐case evaluation. This evaluation considered the safety of remote semaglutide prescriptions, the limitations of the program's online format, and addressed individual weight management needs. If there were any doubts about a potential participant's weight, the healthcare professional would request weight verification, and the participant documented their body weight through pictures or video on body weight scales.

Setting

Participant contact, data collection and monitoring was all digitally conducted via the Embla app, an electronic health record system, and online video calls. This is in accordance with the consent provided by the users, allowing the company to process their data for the purpose of generating statistics, ensuring quality, and developing as well as analysing the course of treatment, in compliance with the Data Protection Act.

Procedures

The online weight management program consisted of patient‐centred lifestyle interventions in combination with personalised dosages of semaglutide. A dedicated health team consisting of physicians, nurses, psychologists, and health coaches was available daily through the Embla app to support and help with sustainable lifestyle changes involving evidence‐based recommendations on nutrition, habits, and exercise. The coaching program combined psychosocial learning principles, including Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) with personalised diet and exercise coaching. Through a dedicated app, the health coaches could, in collaboration with a nurse, psychologist, and physician, tailor the program to each participant's unique needs and circumstances. The program offered a 3‐week, 3‐month, and 12‐month consultation with a nurse to discuss semaglutide treatment, side effects, dose adjustments, and a potential tapering plan. In addition, the participants could book a session with either a nurse or physician at any time to discuss the above or other health‐related questions. For a detailed description of the weight loss program, see Appendix S1.

Semaglutide was taken as once‐weekly subcutaneous injections. Dose escalations were generally tapered to a tolerable level at the discretion of the managing healthcare professional and were continuously assessed during the programme. However, dose escalations were generally not advocated by the clinic if the patient maintained a weekly weight reduction >0.5% of their body weight and was able to manage the experienced satiety/hunger while on the medication. If satiety and hunger were perceived as unmanageable and/or sufficient weight loss was not achieved, the dose could be escalated. Regular follow‐ups and dose adjustments were based on individual response and tolerability. A titration chart used by the treating healthcare professional can be found in the Appendix S1. In the case of adverse events, depending on the severity of the adverse event report, the participant could either be maintained at the current dose, reduced to a dose in between standard doses, reduced to the previous titration dose, or terminated until the adverse event was properly handled. In general, there was an ongoing dialogue between the participant and health coach in collaboration with the treating nurse and physician about weekly dose and treatment effects. Participants were maintained at an effective dose level until their treatment goal was reached or the patient reached a BMI of 25. At this time, if motivated for tapering of medication, the participant was provided with a personalised tapering plan designed to support a gradual lowering of medicine, with dose reductions every 4–8weeks, depending on weight fluctuations and satiety/hunger changes reported by the participant. If a significant weight increase occurred (>2 kg) or hunger became overwhelming after a dose reduction, the dose could be adjusted upward to prevent substantial weight gain while maintaining the overall goal of gradually tapering the patient to the lowest efficient dose level of semaglutide or cessation of semaglutide.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was percentage change in body weight at week 64. The secondary outcomes were the proportion of participants achieving ≥5%, ≥10%, ≥15%, or ≥20% weight loss, and mean weekly semaglutide dose (mg/week). Body weight was based on self‐reported weights in kilograms (kg) registered in the app. Participants were required to record their weight at least once a month together with weekly logging of medication, including dosage and timing. BMI at enrolment was calculated using a weight and height measurement recorded closest to the enrolment date, using the most recent pre‐enrolment weight or a measurement taken within the first 7 days of the programme. Demographic variables, including sex (assigned at birth) and age, were determined based on Det centrale Personregister (CPR) numbers (personal identification numbers used in Denmark). Additional weight data, use of medication, physical activity, and app engagement were prospectively logged by the patients in the app from enrolment, defined as the day of first payment, to week 64. Engagement was calculated as the total number of activities in the app, including appointments, messages, and logged foods (see Figures S3–S7 and Tables S2–S4).

Statistical analysis

A statistical analysis plan was made before data was extracted and sent to the statistician for the analysis (see Appendix S1, Statistical Analysis Plan). The primary outcome, percentage change in body weight from enrolment to week 64, was analysed using a linear mixed effects model. Time (week), baseline BMI, age, and sex were included as covariates. Time was modelled using splines with inner knots placed at weeks 12, 26, 39, and 52 and outer knots weeks 1 and 64. To account for serial correlation, the autoregressive order 1 (AR1) correlation structure was employed. Percentage change in body weight is presented as model estimated mean with 95% CIs at week 64. For the proportions of participants achieving >5, >10, >15, and >20% body weight reduction, results are presented descriptively for all participants with available weight data between weeks 52 and 64. For the mean dose of semaglutide, all available dose measurements for each week was plotted to show mean weekly dose and were also summarised descriptively at weeks 12, 26, 39, 52, and 64. A linear mixed effects model similar to the one used for the primary outcome was used for subgroup analysis of percentage change in body weight according to initial BMI class (setting BMI to the centre of each class for predictions), mean medication dose (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 mg/week), and sex (male/female). Missing weight was assumed to be missing at random and handled implicitly in the mixed model. To explore whether participants with missing data responded differently to treatment, a post‐hoc analysis was made to compare percentage change in body weight at week 26 for participants with available weight data after week 26 and participants without available weight data after week 26. In this post‐hoc analysis, we also included a comparison of different baseline characteristics between the same participants including age, BMI, sex, and initial dose. Furthermore, to examine how app engagement influenced the outcomes, we compared different dose groups and engagement levels and how they affected weight loss. For all the analyses, the widths of the 95% CIs were not adjusted for multiplicity. Analysis and reporting statistics were performed in R version 4.5.0.

From January 2022 to September 2024, a total of 2694 participants were enrolled in the Embla program. After 26, 52, and 64 weeks, respectively, 1580, 712, and 465 participants were still active. Changes in body weight

The estimated mean (95% CI) change in body weight from enrollment to week 64 was −16.7% (−17.4, −16.0) (Figure 1A). The estimated mean (95% CI) change in body weight at week 26 and 52 was −12.2% (−12.6, −11.9) and −16.2% (−16.7, −15.7), (Figure 1A) respectively. Among participants with an available body weight measurement at week 52–64, 98%, 82%, 51%, and 23% achieved at least 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% weight loss, respectively (Figure 1B).

Females had a higher weight loss compared to male participants –17.6% (−18.4, −16.9) versus –13.4% (−14.3, −12.5) (Table S1).

Similar weight losses were obtained in all baseline BMI‐based obesity categories (Figure 2C) and also across different age groups (Table S1). Participants with BMI below 30 showed similar weight loss until week 26, after which weight was stable in contrast to continued weight loss for obesity classes I–III (Figure 2C).

Mean percentage change in body weight was similar regardless of the mean average medication doses (Figure 2D, Table S1). An analysis of dose escalations after week 26 among participants with >5%, >10%, and >15% weight loss at week 26, respectively only showed minor differences (Table S6).

To test the assumption that data is missing at random, a post hoc analysis of participants enrolled for 26 weeks or less and those enrolled for longer revealed no difference between groups, as illustrated in Figure S1. Furthermore, the two groups did not differ in baseline characteristics, including age, BMI, sex, and initial dose (Table S5). Observed percentage median (IQR) weight changes with individual‐participant data are shown in Figure S2. Observed mean weight losses were similar to the model‐based estimates: 16.7% (SD = 6.86) and 12.6% (SD = 4.78) at week 64 and week 26, respectively (Figure S2).

App engagement

Engagement subgroups were defined as the participants total of activities in the app above the median (high) and below the median (low). After 64 weeks, high engagement resulted in a larger weight loss (−17.0% vs. −13.7%) compared to low engagement (Table 2).

High engagement groups showed larger weight loss regardless of low dose or high dose during the study (Figure S3 and Table S4).

In general, higher engagement in the program, reflected by more consultations, messages, and activity logging in the app, was associated with greater weight loss compared to lower levels of engagement. This association remained evident even after stratifying for baseline BMI (Figures S8 and S9).

An overview of different engagement types in the study and its influence on weight loss can be found in Figures S3–S9.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world cohort, adults living with overweight or obesity who engaged in a digital program combining online IBT and personalised doses of semaglutide achieved a mean weight loss of −16.7% after 64 weeks. This weight loss was achieved with a mean dose of subcutaneous semaglutide reaching approximately 1 mg/week and across all BMI‐based obesity classes. A weight loss of at least 5% was achieved for 98% of the participants, suggesting that most people eligible for treatment through a digital solution could obtain clinically meaningful weight loss.

The mean weight loss was larger than the 5%–13% weight loss typically reported after 1 year in real‐world evidence studies on injectable semaglutide based on data originating from electronic medical records. 20 , 22 , 23 , 35 However, real‐world studies based on adults specifically on higher semaglutide doses indicated for obesity (1.7 or 2.4 mg/week) show weight loss of 10%–12% after six months 18 of treatment, which is similar to the 12.2% weight loss obtained on average after 6 months in the present study. Notably, the mean semaglutide dose after half a year in the present study was 0.85 mg/week, illustrating that the non‐pharmacological component of the program made it possible to reach substantial weight loss but with less medication. Most of the above‐mentioned real‐world evidence studies provide no information about any non‐pharmacological component. In the study by Ghusn et al., it was noted whether patients met with a dietician or psychologist. It showed that 25% and 10% met with a dietician and psychologist during the 6‐month study duration, which was not associated with greater weight loss. The Juniper weight loss service provides GLP‐1RA therapy combined with personalised diet and health coaching via an app or e‐mail. This program has been associated with a mean weight loss of 9.5% after 5 months of treatment 36 supporting the effectiveness of obesity medication‐supported digital weight loss services.

In the phase 3 STEP program, a set of RCTs which evaluated semaglutide 2.4 mg/week for treatment of obesity, semaglutide together with lifestyle intervention led to a mean weight loss of 15%–16% after 68 weeks in the analysis of all randomised participants irrespective of treatment discontinuation and rescue medication. 3 , 4 , 6 This is slightly less than the 16.7% observed in the present study. Notably, 86%–90% of the participants in these studies reached 2.4 mg/week 4 , 6 in contrast to only 28.8% titrating to above 1 mg/week in our study. Our results are more similar to the ~17% weight loss in the STEP program estimated by a hypothetical approach analysing the effect if all participants took the drug as intended. The study populations were generally similar between our study and the STEP program regarding age and sex, but the present cohort had a lower initial BMI (34.3 kg/m2 vs. 37.8 kg/m2 in STEP 1). A separate RWE study 37 with a comparable lifestyle intervention to this study showed a mean weight loss of 19.4% after just 12 months, but without a detailed description of how much medication was used during the study. Thus, some of the high efficacy in the current study with less than half the medication is likely a result of the IBT involving cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and a designated health coach available through the digital app solution.

The observation of similar weight losses across semaglutide doses in our study is in contrast with prior studies 19 , 35 showing that higher maintenance doses, compared to lower doses, increased weight loss. This discrepancy most likely reflects the personalised medication strategy in the present study, where participants were encouraged to stay on the lowest effective dose associated with continued weight loss and a manageable satiety/hunger. Interestingly, an exploratory analysis of dose escalations after week 26 among participants with different weight loss outcomes at week 26 showed only minor overall differences, suggesting that non‐responders were not substantially more likely to escalate their dose. However, it remains unknown how outcomes might have differed under a standardised titration of semaglutide. Therefore, the observation that weight loss was similar despite medication dose reflects the implication of a personalised dosing strategy and shows that different patients may require different titration strategies for optimal weight loss and support a more personalised, treat‐to‐target strategy where dose escalation is tailored to the individual.

Ultimately, participants used less medication, and future studies may explore whether such strategy improves long‐term adherence to pharmacological treatment due to lower costs, shared decision making, and potentially fewer side effects, while also addressing the need for ongoing efforts to reduce the net prices of new anti‐obesity medications to ensure equitable access to these highly effective treatments. 38 In subgroup analyses, similar weight losses were observed across age groups. However, our results demonstrated that females lost a larger percentage of body weight compared to male participants. Overall, the population in Denmark initiating GLP‐1 RA (semaglutide) treatment is predominantly female (approximately 70%). 24 Furthermore, data from the SURMOUNT‐5 trial also observed that mean percentage weight loss was greater in women than in men undergoing semaglutide treatment. 39 These findings may reflect biological differences. Notably, similar to previous findings 40 , 41 high engagement in the online app was associated with larger weight loss. Indeed, engagement in the current program, measured by the number of consultations and messages, was associated with greater weight loss in both the low‐dose and high‐dose groups. This association remained after adjusting for baseline BMI and highlights the potential for digital behavioural support to enhance clinical outcomes alongside pharmacological treatment. Interestingly, similar to previous literature, 25 , 29 , 30 self‐monitoring and exercise played an important role in the magnitude of weight loss in this study. This suggests an easily accessible solution where structured and personalised coaching can ensure that each patient receives the needed support simultaneously with individualised medical treatment.

This study has several strengths. It is the first study to explore personalised semaglutide dosing in the treatment of overweight and obesity in a large and recent cohort of participants. Comprehensive tracking of medication adherence and dosing patterns allowed for dose adjustments based on individual response, side effects, and shared decision‐making. The treatment approach integrated coaching and IBT alongside the medical treatment, which is a strength because the lifestyle component has typically not been the focus in previous semaglutide studies. Finally, the digital delivery of the treatment through an app offers a scalable solution for the medical treatment of people living with overweight or obesity.

Our study also has several limitations. Firstly, the study did not have a control group, which is an inherent limitation of the study design. Thus, the absence of a comparator group restricts the ability to control for unmeasured confounders and establish causality. Secondly, participants had the financial resources to afford treatment and were, by design, individuals who were very motivated to engage in IBT. This could limit the generalisability of the findings to lower‐income populations and in general to the broader population. Thirdly, another limitation is that 80% of the participants did not have a registered body weight at week 64, the primary endpoint. The reasons for missing body weight included that participants transitioned to other non‐medicine programmes within the Embla treatment regimen, participants who had not been enrolled for long enough at data extraction, and participants who stopped subscription for various reasons. Importantly, there was no difference in weight loss or in baseline characteristics between those who stopped early, before or after 26 weeks, and those who continued for more than 26 weeks, suggesting attrition was not systematically related to treatment response. The loss to follow‐up rate in the study was similar to other RWE studies. 36 , 37 Finally, weight and use of medication data were self‐reported via the Embla app, which introduces the risk of recall bias, social desirability bias, reporting bias, and data entry errors. The absence of validation by healthcare professionals and objective measurement tools should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this is the first study to show weight loss of a magnitude similar to those reported in RCTs, but with less than half the usually applied medication used. Clinically significant weight loss was observed across different BMI classes, sex, age groups, and medication doses. These results highlight the effectiveness of a personalised, treat‐to‐target approach to medical obesity management when combining this with easily accessible IBT. Furthermore, this approach demonstrated that lower doses of semaglutide can still yield strong outcomes on weight loss, offering new insights into the role of personalised dosing strategies and implementation.
