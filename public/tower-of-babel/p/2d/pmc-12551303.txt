Competency-driven hemostasis training reduces vascular complications in transradial hemodynamic monitoring: a self-controlled trial with early-career residents
Authors: Qin Hou, Bin Zhou, Jing-Ping Kang, Yun-Xia Zuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe manual compression approach is still widely used to achieve hemostasis of the radial artery. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of competency-driven hemostasis training for radial artery among early-career residents.MethodsThis prospective, self-controlled study included 10 residents who had not previously received standard compression hemostasis training (Trial Registration: ChiCTR1900025140, 13/08/2019). Radial artery compression was performed in 100 patients both before and after training. The primary endpoints were 5 min hemostasis success rate, incidence of hematoma/bruise in puncture site, and the secondary endpoints were compression-related neurological sequelae and allergic responses.ResultsThe analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in the 5-min hemostasis success rate (84.94% vs. 93.62%, P = 0.05) and a substantial reduction in the occurrence of hematoma/bruise (29.03% vs. 13.82%, P = 0.011). There were no significant differences in the frequencies of neurological complications (3.22% vs. 1.06%, P = 0.65) or allergic reactions (4.30% vs. 3.19%, P = 0.32) between the pre- and post-training phases.ConclusionsThe competency-driven hemostasis training for radial artery enhanced hemostatic effectiveness while ensuring procedural safety, as demonstrated by a reduction in hematoma/bruise formation, without elevating the risk of neurological sequelae or allergic responses. The findings established an evidence-based foundation for competency-driven hemostasis training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08048-z.Keywords: Radial artery hemostasis training, Vascular access complications, Residents

Introduction

Arterial puncture and cannulation are widely used for continuous hemodynamic monitoring and arterial blood gas sampling in the operating room and intensive care unit (ICU) [1]. The radial artery has become the preferred site for this procedure due to its consistent anatomic accessibility, technical feasibility of cannulation, and low complication rates [2]. However, past literature has extensively reported the puncture and cannulation techniques for radial artery, including the palpation-based approach and ultrasound-guided approach [3–6]. Specifically, although hemostasis strategies for radial artery have been thoroughly studied, most of these investigations concentrate on post-cardiac interventional settings [7–11]. In contrast, a significant knowledge gap persists concerning the optimal hemostatic practices following arterial catheter removal under anesthetic conditions. Of particular concern is the lack of clinical or educational research addressing hemostasis protocols for this critical perioperative practice.

Residents in the anesthesiology department need acquire proficiency in various vascular access techniques, including cannulation and removal of catheters in peripheral veins, central veins, and peripheral arteries. Current clinical practice reveals that residents usually rely on observational learning or empirical approaches to achieve artery hemostasis, which often results in inadequate compression efficacy [12]. The lack of hemostasis training may contribute to complications, such as postoperative hematoma formation and subcutaneous ecchymosis, which may potentially impact patient outcomes and satisfaction.

To address this training deficiency, we developed a competency-driven hemostasis training (CDHT) comprising three progressive components: (1) baseline assessment of residents’ current hemostatic techniques and clinical outcomes; (2) didactic instruction emphasizing radial artery anatomical considerations and evidence-based compression parameters (including methods of gauze folding, direct manual pressing time, ratio of the gauze’s retained width to wrist circumference, and duration of gauze remaining in situ); and (3) supervised hands-on hemostasis training with competency verification. This study aimed to investigate whether this training might boost the first-attempt hemostasis success rates among trainees, hence reducing post-procedural complications and eventually improving patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective, self-controlled educational study was conducted at West China Hospital of Sichuan University from November 1, 2024, to February 28, 2025. Ten first-year anesthesiology residents (6 males, 4 females, mean age 24.3 ± 1.2 years) with limited arterial catheter management expertise were enrolled sequentially.

CDHT protocol

Pre-training assessment

The residents performed radial artery compression on 100 non-cardiac surgical patients (10 cases per resident) based on their personal experience. The folds of gauze used for hemostasis, time of manual compression, percentage of patients’ wrists covered by tape for fixation, and ratio of the width of the compression gauze to the wrist circumference were all recorded. In addition, the incidence of hematoma/bruise in the puncture site was recorded within 24 h.

Catheter removal

Using an aseptic technique, the arterial catheter was gently withdrawn while simultaneously applying firm and focused pressure to the puncture site using sterile rolled gauze. The catheter tip was carefully inspected to ensure structural integrity and absence of fragmentation.

Secondary endpoints

Complications included nerve injuries and allergies. Subjective symptoms, such as hand numbness and weakness, may indicate nerve damage. Skin itching, erythema, rashes, or blisters were documented as potential allergic reactions to the gauze tape.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp). Chi-square tests were performed for categorical variables (hemostasis success rates and complication incidence). Fisher’s exact test was utilized when the expected cell frequencies were less than 5. Continuous variables were assessed using Student’s t-tests. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of α = 0.05 (two-tailed), with statistical significance defined as P ≤ 0.05. Effect sizes were calculated using Phi coefficients for 2 × 2 contingency tables.

Results

Prior to the CDHT, the 10 residents required 3–10 min (5.23 ± 2.64) to achieve arterial hemostasis via manual compression. The gauze was folded either once or twice or left unfolded, with a retained width corresponding to 20–50% of patients’ wrist circumference. Post-training all participants uniformly implemented cylindrical gauze folding, standardized the width-to-circumference ratio to 15–20%, and sustained pressure application for at least 5 min. Regardless of whether it was before or after CDHT, the precise retention duration of the compression dressing remained untraceable in most instances because the dressing gauze was removed by nurses in the ward. Even when the retention times were documented, most instances exceeded 6 h (Table 1).

This training demonstrated significant clinical improvement in hemostatic outcomes. Before training, the 5-min primary hemostasis success rate was 84.9%, while after training, the 5-min primary hemostasis success rate increased to 93.6% (P = 0.05, Table 2). Before CDHT, the 24-h postoperative hematoma/bruise incidence was 29.0% among 93 patients. Following hemostasis training, this rate decreased to 13.8% in 94 consecutive cases (χ² = 6.42, P = 0.01, two-sided; Table 2). A consistent finding was that the size of the hematoma remained within the same range before and after training, with all patients exhibiting a diameter of less than 5 cm (Table 2). Notably, a reduction in hemorrhagic complications occurred without compromising CDHT safety. Both neurological rates (pre: 3.2% vs. post: 1.0%, P = 0.36) and allergic reaction incidence (pre: 4.3% vs. post: 3.2%, P = 0.72) remained statistically comparable before and after training, as analyzed using Fisher’s exact test.

Discussion

Notably, CDHT achieved its primary objective of an increase in the success rate of achieving hemostasis within 5 min from 84.94% to 93.62% and significantly reduction in post-procedural hemorrhagic complications, with the hematoma/bruise incidence decreasing from 29.03% to 13.82%. This improvement likely stems from three synergistic mechanisms. Firstly, the three-dimensional vascular demonstrations enhanced residents’ spatial understanding of radial artery anatomy, facilitating precise compression, a critical factor often neglected in empirical practice. Secondly, by applying pressure with a rolled gauze and ensuring occlusion of the radial artery pulse, this approach directly addresses the common issue of insufficient compression. Thirdly, prior to the implementation of CDHT, residents usually ceased applying continuous pressure with the compression gauze after achieving hemostasis within 5 min and subsequently removed the gauze. Nevertheless, patients’ hand movements may result in micro-bleeding at the puncture site, potentially leading to subcutaneous bleeding that manifests as delayed ecchymosis 24 h later. In contrast, the compression technique in the CDHT protocol involves securing cylindrical gauze in place with tape for 6 h. This approach effectively minimizes the incidence of delayed subcutaneous hematoma attributable to wrist movement.

Despite the marked improvement, the post-training hematoma/bruise rate of 13.8% remains higher than the 5–8% benchmark reported in cardiology literature [9, 15–18]. This discrepancy may reflect fundamental differences in compression methods, operator variability, cannula indwelling times and individuals performing the hematoma documentation. Mechanical compression devices, which are widely used to achieve hemostasis in transradial coronary intervention procedures, can maintain consistent pressure for continuous compression without variability in force. Manual compression depends on the operator applying the hand pressure, making it very variable and not reproducible owing to subjective fatigue, which is a limitation observed in human operators [19]. Moreover, in this study, the cannula indwelling time consistently exceeded 2 h, which was markedly longer than the typical duration observed in most cardiac interventional procedures (average 20–60 min) [20–22]. Besides, the nurse-conducted documentation of hematoma, which may be more sensitive than operator-performed assessments utilized in interventional trials.

The observed heterogeneous outcomes revealed that one resident performed worse after CDHT, another maintaining baseline proficiency. Potential explanations include individual variability in skill acquisition and procedural execution (e.g., pressure control) despite standardized teaching, as well as patient-related factors such as anatomical variations in radial artery, which may influence hemostasis effectiveness. Another resident maintained only baseline proficiency following CDHT. This was evident as the top performance metric—the incidence of hematoma in the pre-training period was zero—was already optimal and could not be further improved.

During clinical bedside teaching, attending physicians usually provide guidance on puncture techniques. However, tasks such as the removal of arterial cannulas and the appropriate hemostasis methods might be perceived as less critical. As a result, residents may not receive standardized training in these specific procedures. This study emphasizes the importance of such training for ensuring comprehensive medical skills development among residents. Therefore, this competency- driven training could also be applied to other clinical settings such as arterial cannulation training, hemostasis protocols following central venous catheter removal, and hemostasis management following percutaneous vascular interventions. Moreover, our findings indicate that systematic training in manual hemostasis techniques can significantly improve hemostatic efficacy without imposing additional economic burden or increasing resource utilization for patients. This is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings where access to advanced hemostasis devices or closure technologies remains restricted.

Limitations

This study had a few limitations that should be considered. First, the single-center design study exclusively focusing on anesthesiology residents may limit its applicability to other specialties or healthcare settings with different training regimens. Second, despite a reduction in hematoma incidence, significant inter-operator variability remained (success rate: 10.0–40.0%), underscoring the persistent biological heterogeneity in manual compression techniques that remains unaddressed by current training metrics. Third, our outcome measures failed to account for potential confounding factors, such as anticoagulant pharmacokinetics or vascular elasticity. Finally, the short-term follow-up (24 h) precluded the assessment of delayed complications.

Conclusions

The CDHT significantly improved hemostasis outcomes in anesthesiology residents, resulting in a clinically meaningful 15.2% absolute risk reduction. While individual heterogeneity and pharmacological complexity necessitate tailored remediation strategies, the core methodology establishes an evidence-based foundation for hemostasis skill training. Future research should explore augmented reality platforms for real-time pressure feedback and longitudinal competency-maintenance programs.
