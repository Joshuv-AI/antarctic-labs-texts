Subcortical neural basis of malevolent creativity
Authors: Zhenni Gao, Xiaojin Liu, Jing Teng, Ning Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMalevolent creativity (MC) involves generating original ideas to harm others, and it not only relies on cognitive flexibility but may also be related to the activities of emotional and motivational brain regions known as the subcortical regions. However, the relationship between the subcortical regions and MC performance remains unclear. We calculated dynamic graph-based analyses using resting-state fMRI. We found that MC originality was negatively correlated with functional connectivity (FC) between the right nucleus accumbens (NAcc) and cortical regions such as the right medial superior frontal gyrus (mSFG) and supplementary motor area (SMA). Similarly, benevolent creative (BC) originality was negatively correlated with FC between the right NAcc and SMA/superior frontal gyrus (SFG). MC malevolence was positively correlated with FC between the left caudate and postcentral gyrus and negatively correlated with FC between the right amygdala and SFG. These findings suggest that MC is associated with a complex interaction between the subcortical and cortical regions.Subject areas: Natural sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Clinical neuroscience

Introduction

Creativity is often associated with innovation, problem-solving, and artistic expression, all of which contribute to societal progress. However, creativity is not inherently positive; it can also be directed toward harmful, manipulative, or even destructive purposes. For example, an individual may propose an original strategy designed to prevent a competitor. This form of creativity, known as malevolent creativity (MC), refers to the ability to generate novel, harmful, and useful ideas or products that violate social norms, manipulate others, or prioritize personal gain over ethical considerations.1,2 While previous studies on creativity have concentrated on positive outcomes, there is a growing awareness that creativity can also be used for harmful purposes. Nevertheless, the brain mechanisms behind MC remain poorly understood.

Neuroscience studies on creativity have traditionally emphasized the role of cortical brain regions, particularly the prefrontal cortex (PFC).3,4,5 However, subcortical regions such as the striatum, hippocampus, and thalamus also play significant roles in shaping creative behavior, working closely with the PFC to support the broader creative process.6,7,8 These regions contribute to cognitive functions such as emotional processing, motivation regulation, motor control, perceptual processing, and memory integration.9,10,11,12 For example, the basal ganglia, often referred to as the reward center of the brain, reinforce feelings of success and accomplishment.13 However, when overactive, the reward system may lead individuals to prioritize short-term gains or rationalize unethical behaviors, fueling malevolent intentions.14,15,16 Similarly, the limbic system, particularly the amygdala, is involved in emotion regulation but may be related to impulsive or destructive creative ideas when overwhelmed by emotional intensity.13,17 An imbalance in these subcortical systems may be associated with creativity toward malevolent outcomes, emphasizing the need to explore their relationship with MC. The subcortical regions are critical for processing emotion and motivation,17,18 which are central to the neural mechanisms underlying MC. In addition, the reward system is associated with MC, as reward-driven thinking can be related to individuals to justify unethical actions as being “worth it”.16,19,20

The dopaminergic system, especially within the fronto-striatal pathway, is also associated with creativity.8,21 These circuitries regulate the balance between flexibility and persistence—two key cognitive processes that facilitate creativity.22 Higher dopamine levels in the fronto-striatal circuit promote goal-directed behavior and persistence in creative tasks,10 broadening attention, enhancing problem-solving capacity, and fostering persistence in difficult challenges.22 However, the pathway connecting the PFC to the striatum, especially the nucleus accumbens (NAcc), is central to reward processing and motivation.23 The influence of the fronto-striatal circuits on how individuals feel rewarded for their creative efforts—whether through intrinsic satisfaction or external validation—may influence MC.

Another critical circuit involves the limbic system, especially the amygdala, which is responsible for emotional processing. The amygdala-PFC pathway has been shown to modulate emotional regulation, and altered activity in this circuit can result in increased emotional reactivity, potentially helping to harmful creative thoughts.24,25 The thalamus, which serves as a hub for sensory and emotional information, further connects subcortical structures with cortical regions, enhancing the integration of emotional and cognitive processing in creative tasks.7,9 These circuits play an essential role in emotional regulation, motivation, and reward processing, indicating that MC may be associated with imbalances in these areas.16,19,26

Although MC can potentially cause significant harm, there are few studies on its brain mechanisms. Most creativity studies have focused on the brain regions involved in general creativity, particularly within the cortical areas.27,28,29 However, MC may involve different brain networks, especially those related to emotion regulation, motivation, and reward processing, which are primarily located in the subcortical regions. Exploring the relationship between these regions and MC performance is critical to understanding the neural correlates when individuals use creativity for harmful purposes.

This study analyzed functional connectivity (FC) and dynamic graph-based analyses from resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data collected from 47 healthy right-handed participants. We aimed to explore the relationship between the subcortical regions and MC performance. Specifically, our study focused on the following questions. (1) How are the subcortical regions, such as the basal ganglia and limbic system, associated with MC performance? Are these relationships distinct when compared to benevolent creativity (BC) performance? (2) How are connections between the subcortical regions and the PFC associated with MC performance? To address these questions, we propose the following hypotheses. (1) The subcortical regions, particularly the NAcc, amygdala, and thalamus, are significantly associated with MC performance. (2) Connections between the subcortical regions and PFC are associated with MC performance, with stronger connectivity potentially related to greater malevolence. (3) Neural connectivity within the subcortical regions is negatively correlated with MC performance, suggesting that a less integrated subcortical network may be associated with higher MC performance.

Correlation analyses between behavior and dynamic topological properties

We calculated the variance of dynamic topological properties, including the clustering coefficient (Cp), characteristic path length (Lp), local efficiency (Eloc), and global efficiency (Eglob). We then computed each participant’s dynamic FC (dFC) variance. We estimated the partial correlation between the subcortical network’s variance of dynamic topological properties (Cp, Lp, Eloc, and Eglob) and behavior scores while controlling for age and gender. Results showed that the variance of Cp had a significant negative correlation with MC originality (r = −0.31, p = 0.04) and BC originality (r = −0.33, p = 0.03) (Figure 3) and no significant correlation with MC malevolence (r = −0.21, p = 0.17) and BC benevolence (r = −0.11, p = 0.46). The variance of Lp had no significant correlations with MC originality (r = −0.07, p = 0.66), MC malevolence (r = 0.02, p = 0.91), BC originality (r = −0.20, p = 0.18), and BC benevolence (r = 0.16, p = 0.28). The variance of Eloc had a significant negative correlation with MC originality (r = −0.32, p = 0.04) and BC originality (r = −0.31, p = 0.04) (Figure 3) and no significant correlation with MC malevolence (r = −0.25, p = 0.10) and BC benevolence (r = −0.02, p = 0.92). The variance of Eglob had no significant correlations with MC originality (r = −0.18, p = 0.24), MC malevolence (r = −0.04, p = 0.80), BC originality (r = −0.24, p = 0.12), and BC benevolence (r = −0.02, p = 0.90).

We found a significant negative correlation between MC malevolence and the dFC variance between the left pallidum and left brainstem (r = −0.42, p = 0.0038, Figure 4A). Discussion

This study explored the neural mechanisms underlying MC, particularly the relationship with the subcortical brain regions such as the NAcc, pallidum, and amygdala. Our main findings are as follows. (1) MC originality was negatively correlated with the FC between the right NAcc and cortical regions, including the right SMA and mSFG. Similarly, BC originality was negatively correlated with the FC between the right NAcc and the right SMA/SFG and positively correlated with the FC between the right pallidum and left SOG. (2) MC malevolence showed positive correlations with the FC between the left caudate and right PCL/left PoCG. It was also negatively correlated with the FC between the right amygdala and both the right thalamus and SFG. (3) BC benevolence was negatively correlated with the FC between the right amygdala and right LING and between the left amygdala and cortical regions, including the left/right LING, right PreCG, and others. (4) In the subcortical regions, the variance of Cp and Eloc showed negative correlations with both MC and BC originality. MC malevolence was negatively correlated with the dFC variance between the left pallidum and left brainstem, while BC benevolence showed positive correlations with the dFC variance between the left NAcc and both the right brainstem and pallidum.

Originality in MC and BC

We observed that the connectivity between certain brain regions, specifically the right NAcc, right SMA, and right SFG, was negatively correlated with both MC and BC originality. The pathway connecting the PFC (i.e., SFG) to the striatum (i.e., NAcc) is central to reward processing and motivation.23 The originality of MC and BC and low connectivity of NAcc may indicate that, in both forms of creativity, there is less reliance on immediate rewards, and creators rely more on intrinsic drives or complex cognitive processing. The SFG is responsible for higher cognitive functions, including attention control,30 working memory,31,32 and planning.33 In contrast to the greater originality of MC and BC, low SFG connectivity may reflect the “decentralized” nature of creative divergent thinking, where individuals are not limited to a single solution but rely more on broad associations and inspiration-driven ideas. The SMA plays a key role in motor planning and complex behaviors,34,35 and it is also related to cognitive control.36,37 Low connectivity in the SMA may suggest a reduced relevance of planning and automatic behaviors, allowing for greater flexibility in divergent thinking during MC and BC. Both MC and BC are associated with the generation of original ideas; the reduced connectivity observed in the right NAcc, SMA, and SFG may be associated with creative generation. This may indicate that a lower focus on rewards is related to the development of highly original ideas.

Malevolence in MC

MC malevolence showed significant positive correlations with the FC between the left caudate and right PCL/left PoCG. The caudate, a key component of the striatum, is involved in cognitive planning and behavioral control,38,39 while the PCL and PoCG are associated with sensory processing, motor control, and somatosensory integration.40,41 This increased connectivity may reflect enhanced physical perception and response control during malevolent creative thinking, which may be related to rapid and decisive actions in high-pressure or confrontational situations.42,43 The interaction between the caudate, PCL, and PoCG is likely related to the execution of complex behaviors, such as aggression and motor planning,44,45,46 and may be involved in generating malevolent creative ideas, including destructive designs. The observed increase in FC suggests improved coordination between these regions, enabling more detailed and targeted behavioral strategies.

In contrast, MC malevolence was negatively correlated with the FC between the right amygdala and both the right thalamus and right SFG. The amygdala, a core region for emotional processing and threat perception,47 and the thalamus, which facilitates information flow and multisensory integration,48 typically interact with the SFG to support higher cognitive functions such as attention control,30 working memory,31,32 and planning.33 Reduced connectivity in this network may indicate diminished coordination between emotional regulation (amygdala) and cognitive control (thalamus and SFG), correlating with impulsive and emotionally driven decisions rather than calm, deliberate actions.49 This weakened connectivity could characterize malevolent creative behavior, where reduced emotional regulation and cognitive control may be related to individuals toward more extreme or destructive creative acts. These findings suggest that malevolent behavior is not merely an emotional response but involves the integration of multiple functional systems, including sensory-motor coordination, cognitive control, and emotion regulation.

Benevolence in BC

BC benevolence was negatively correlated with the FC between the right amygdala and right LING and between the left amygdala and several cortical regions, including the left/right LING, right PreCG, and others. The LING is an important area for visual processing50,51 and is particularly associated with emotional expression and perception.52 BC benevolence is associated with reduced FC between the right amygdala and right LING. This reduction in connectivity may reflect an adjustment in the relationship between emotional regulation and visual perception during the generation of BC. Individuals may rely more on emotional cognition rather than visual input to generate creative ideas with good intentions. This could also be linked to the adaptive nature of BC in social interactions, where less emotional overreaction or visual distraction allows individuals to focus on creating positive and beneficial ideas. The PreCG is related to movement control and speech motor planning or execution.53,54 BC benevolence is associated with reduced FC between the left amygdala and several cortical regions, such as the LING and PreCG. This may suggest that, during the process of generating BC, the connection between emotional processing and movement/behavior control decreases. It may reflect that individuals rely more on internal emotional regulation rather than specific behavioral control when generating kind and creative ideas. This reduced connectivity may indicate smoother emotional expression and creative associations, correlating with ideas that are compassionate and socially valuable.

Network dynamics in subcortical regions

We found that the variance of Cp and Eloc within the subcortical regions showed negative correlations with the originality of both MC and BC. Cp and Eloc reflect the organization and efficiency of brain networks; Cp measures the tightness of connections between nodes in the brain network, while Eloc indicates how efficiently the local network processes information.55 The variance provides a more dynamic perspective, indicating the variability of the brain network over time, reflecting the brain’s flexibility or instability.56 The negative correlation between originality and Cp or Eloc variance suggests that individuals with higher creativity exhibit less efficient information flow in certain subcortical regions, such as the basal ganglia. This may indicate that higher creativity is associated with cross-regional interactions and broader network connections rather than tightly connected or highly efficient local networks, consistent with previous findings that creative performance involves communication between spatially separated brain regions.21,57

Specifically, MC malevolence was negatively correlated with the variance of dFC between the left pallidum and left brainstem. The pallidum is involved in motor control and emotional responses,58,59 while the brainstem regulates autonomic functions and physiological responses.60 This reduced connectivity may suggest that MC is less associated with emotional regulation and physiological control, which may relate to individuals toward more impulsive and emotionally reactive behaviors.16,19 In contrast, BC benevolence showed positive correlations with the variance of dFC between the left NAcc and both the right brainstem and pallidum. The NAcc, a key node in the mesolimbic dopaminergic pathway, is linked to motivation, reward prediction, and social rewards.61,62,63,64 The increased connectivity variance in BC may reflect stronger collaboration between reward and physiological control systems, facilitating the generation of socially beneficial and compassionate ideas.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that MC and BC share some neural foundations, such as reduced NAcc-prefrontal connectivity, which may be associated with divergent thinking and originality. However, they also exhibit distinct patterns: MC is associated with enhanced sensory-motor coordination and reduced emotional regulation, while BC is more related to internal emotional processing and reward-driven motivation. These results suggest that MC is associated with a complex interaction between cognitive planning, emotional impulsivity, and sensory-motor integration, whereas BC is related to emotional regulation and social reward systems. These findings suggest that MC is linked to a complex interaction between the subcortical and cortical regions, with distinct patterns of connectivity associated with generating harmful ideas and their malevolent intent.

Limitations of the study

This study explored the neural mechanisms underlying MC and BC performance, focusing on the relationship with the subcortical regions such as the NAcc, pallidum, and amygdala. Several limitations require further exploration. First, the relationship between cognitive functions (such as emotional processing) and both MC and BC needs to be further explored. Future research should incorporate task-based measures that engage neural mechanisms. Second, creativity is a complex and multifaceted construct. While this study distinguishes between MC and BC, the specific cognitive and emotional processes driving these forms of creativity are not yet fully understood and need further exploration. Third, weaker effects may have gone undetected due to limited power. Larger sample sizes in future studies will be important to confirm and extend these findings. Fourth, although the MC task provides standardized, ethically permissible scenarios for eliciting malevolent ideas, it may not fully capture the complexity of real-world malevolent behaviors. Future research should validate this task against established measures such as moral dilemmas or unethical decision-making paradigms. Finally, both general creative ability and moral reasoning may affect resting-state connectivity and performance in the MC task. Future studies should include standardized assessments of these factors to address potential confounds.

Experimental model and study participant details

We recruited 54 healthy, right-handed college students. Data from 47 participants (27 females, mean age = 22.55 ± 2.34 years, age range: 19–28 years) who completed all tasks were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the University Committee on Human Research Protection (UCHRP) of East China Normal University (ECNU) (Approval No. HR 319–2019), ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. Before the experiment, all participants provided written informed consent. They were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any penalty or negative consequences. None of the participants reported a history of drug or alcohol abuse, neurological or psychiatric conditions, claustrophobia, or head injury.

In this study, we analyzed resting-state data from a sample that overlaps with participants used in our previous study,16 which used task-state data to explore dynamic brain network patterns during MC. The earlier study examined task-based fMRI data focusing on dynamic brain network patterns during malevolent creativity, whereas the present study investigates resting-state FC and subcortical network properties in relation to both malevolent and benevolent creative performance.

Method details

Behavioral data

The MC task was adapted from the realistic presented problem (RPP) task, which involves solving open-ended problems creatively.65,66 For example: “Your friend, who dislikes wearing the same clothes as others, discovers that a classmate owns the same coat. Think of a novel way to secretly destroy the classmate’s coat.” To contrast, we included a BC task, also adapted from the RPP task. An example prompt is: “Your friend is too shy to approach the girl he likes. Think of a novel way to help him naturally meet her.” Participants were instructed to generate as many responses as possible in MRI scanners during each 30-second trial and then recall the most novel solution from their perspective after leaving the scanner.

We evaluated task performance by scoring originality in the MC and BC tasks, as well as malevolence (MC task) and benevolence (BC task). Four trained raters independently assessed the responses on a 1–5 scale (1 = not at all; 5 = very much).67 The individual ratings were averaged into a single score for each participant. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the internal consistency coefficient (ICC): MC originality = 0.79, MC malevolence = 0.68, BC originality = 0.83, and BC benevolence = 0.68.

Image acquisition

Whole-brain imaging was conducted using a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical images were obtained using a T1-weighted 3D MP-RAGE sequence (TR = 2530 ms; TE = 2.98 ms; slice thickness = 1 mm; image matrix = 256 × 224; field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm2; flip angle = 7°). Functional images were acquired using gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence, covering the whole brain (TR = 1000 ms, TE = 30 ms, image matrix = 64 × 64, FOV = 224 × 224 mm2, flip angle = 60°, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, 40 slices, spatial resolution = 3.5 × 3.5 × 3.5 mm3, slice gap = 3.8 mm). Participants were instructed to lie quietly with their eyes closed during the scan. Each participant completed 480 volumes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Preprocessing

The resting-state functional images were processed using DPABI software (http://www.rfmri.org/dpabi) based on SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). The preprocessing steps included: (1) discarding the first 10 volumes to allow for signal stabilization; (2) correcting for acquisition time differences between slices; (3) realigning images to remove motion artifacts, ensuring displacement ≤ 1.5 mm and rotation ≤ 1.5°; (4) segmenting individual T1 images and transforming them to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using the DARTEL tool, with resampling to 3 × 3 × 3 mm3; (5) regressing out nuisance signals, including 24-parameter head motion profiles,68 mean white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals; (6) spatially smoothing the data with a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel; and (7) The data were then band-pass filtered (0.01 – 0.1 Hz) to decrease the effects of low-frequency drift and high-frequency physiological noise.

Regions of interest (ROIs)

We extracted 16 subcortical regions of interest (ROIs) from the Harvard-Oxford Subcortical Structural Probabilistic Atlas to comprehensively cover emotion-, motivation-, and cognition-related subcortical structures. ROIs were based on a 25% probability map and included bilateral NAcc, amygdala, caudate, brainstem, hippocampus, pallidum, putamen, and thalamus. Given the limited prior work on MC, this broad ROI selection was exploratory, aiming to avoid missing potentially relevant regions. All ROIs were resampled to a voxel size of 3 mm3 for further analysis. Figure S1 illustrates the anatomical orientations of the subcortical areas used in this study.

Functional connectivity maps

FC maps for each subcortical region were generated using a standard seed-voxel method, and each ROI served as a seed. For each participant, we extracted the mean time series of all voxels within the seed region and the time series for each voxel across the whole brain. Pearson correlation coefficient r was then computed to assess FC between the seed region’s time series and those of all brain voxels. This process produced a whole-brain FC map for each subcortical region of each participant. Subsequently, we applied Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to convert these maps into z-value maps to facilitate further statistical analysis.

Dynamic measures of the subcortical network

We assessed dFC and dynamic topological properties using the sliding-window approach69 via the DynamicBC toolbox.70 For each of the 16 subcortical ROIs, we computed subject-level seed-to-voxel dFC maps using a sliding-window approach (window = 44 TR, step = 1 TR). The temporal variance of this correlation across windows was used as the dFC metric at each voxel. We calculated the variance of dynamic topological properties, including the Cp, Lp, Eloc, and Eglob. We then computed each participant’s dFC variance.

Statistical analysis

We calculated Pearson correlation between MC, BC scores, age, and gender, applying Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05). Then we estimated the partial correlation between the variance of dynamic topological properties (Cp, Lp, Eloc, and Eglob) and behavior scores while controlling for age and gender. We estimated the partial correlation between the variance of dFC and behavior scores while controlling for age and gender (1/n correction). P-values reported here are a false positive correction to n-node statistical comparison by taking 1/(number of nodes) = 1/120 = 0.008 as the significance threshold, which is equivalent to saying that nodes with p < 0.008 are “significant”71,72,73; given the exploratory nature of this analysis, we did not apply more conservative whole-family corrections. We also estimated the partial correlation between the whole brain’s FC maps and behavior scores while controlling for age and gender using the TFCE74 with 5,000 permutations to estimate significance levels. The FWE method based on TFCE was used to control for multiple comparisons correction. The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (FWE-corrected) and cluster size > 30 voxels. We did not apply an additional multiple-comparison correction across the 16 subcortical seed ROIs. This decision was based on two considerations: (1) The seeds were defined a priori using the Harvard–Oxford subcortical atlas based on strong anatomical and theoretical reasons, rather than being chosen through data-driven exploration screening; and (2) the present study is partly exploratory, aiming to identify potential patterns of brain–behavior associations for future confirmatory research.
