The Relationship between Biological Aging and Cognitive Function: A Machine Learning Model
Authors: Chongkang Ren, Zean Li, Jinyi Cai, Jiuzhou Li, Xinyu Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe relationship between cognitive impairment and biological age (BA) is unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between BA and cognitive impairment.Materials and MethodsA total of 2202 participants from the 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were included. Klemera-Doubal method age (KDM-Age), phenotypic age (PhenoAge), and their acceleration values were calculated based on laboratory parameters. Six machine learning models were constructed to compare performance metrics such as area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and accuracy, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) was used to interpret feature contributionsResultsAfter fully adjusting for confounders, each 1-year increase in PhenoAge was associated with a 6%, 8%, and 5% higher risk of cognitive impairment on the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD), Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), and Animal Fluency Test (AFT) tests, respectively (all p<0.001); the highest quartile group exhibited a 5.98–17.48-fold increase in risk compared to the lowest. KDM-Age showed only a marginal association with DSST [odds ratio (OR)=1.02, p=0.049]. Among all models, the random forest (RF) performed best for CERAD prediction (AUC-ROC=0.997, sensitivity=99.5%), significantly outperforming the other algorithms (p<0.001). INTRODUCTION

Cognitive impairment has increasingly become a significant public health issue worldwide, severely affecting the quality of life of older adults.1 Mild cognitive impairment, a transitional state between normal cognition and dementia, is rapidly increasing in prevalence, and individuals in this group face a markedly higher risk of progression to dementia.2 Declines in cognitive function not only severely undermine individuals’ independence but also impose a substantial burden on society. Studies have shown that early detection of cognitive impairment and timely intervention can effectively delay or even prevent the progression of cognitive decline, thereby improving long-term prognosis.3 At present, clinical assessment of cognitive function primarily relies on standardized scales and evaluation of clinical symptoms, among which standardized cognitive scales remain the core tools for determining cognitive status.4 In recent years, novel biomarkers have gradually been incorporated into research to enable early prediction of cognitive disorders. Research indicates that gray matter volume loss is closely associated with cognitive dysfunction.5 Furthermore, widespread DNA methylation abnormalities have been observed in the peripheral blood of patients with mild cognitive impairment. Some of these methylation sites change in accordance with cognitive status progression, suggesting a potential role in the onset and progression of cognitive impairment.6 However, these biomarkers are currently applied mainly in mechanistic research and have not yet been widely adopted in clinical assessment practice. Therefore, exploring biological indicators that can complement clinical evaluations to enhance risk identification and facilitate population stratification holds significant research value.

With accelerating global population aging, healthcare systems face significant pressure. Aging is a complex biological process driven by the accumulation of molecular and cellular damage.7 It leads to functional decline and increased disease risk. To effectively address age-related health issues, accurate assessment of individual aging status is required. Traditional chronological age (CA) measures only the passage of time and cannot reflect inter-individual heterogeneity in aging rates. Therefore, its use as an aging indicator is limited.8 In contrast, biological age (BA) integrates the functional status of multiple physiological systems. Previous studies have suggested that biomarkers of BA are closely associated with various health outcomes, including metabolic diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and overall mortality risk.9 BA is estimated based on physiological and biochemical indicators. Examples include the Klemera-Doubal method age (KDM-Age) and phenotypic age (PhenoAge), which are quantified using routine clinical laboratory data. Compared to CA, BA more accurately reflects an individual’s physiological condition.8 BA provides a crucial clinical basis for disease prevention and health management.10 However, studies examining its association with cognitive function remain relatively scarce, particularly lacking systematic validation and analysis in large population cohorts.

This study systematically investigated the association between BA and clinical cognitive assessment outcomes using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). We also developed predictive models using multiple machine learning algorithms and employed Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values to elucidate the relative contributions of sociodemographic factors and BA metrics to cognitive function. By exploring this relationship, the present study provides novel scientific evidence for the early identification of cognitive impairment. It further offers potential intervention strategies to alleviate the burden of cognitive disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sources

NHANES, which is conducted by the U.S. National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a comprehensive survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to collect data via interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. The NHANES protocol has received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants from the 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 NHANES cycles were considered eligible for this study. Exclusion criteria included missing key data: lack of core neurocognitive test data [16808 participants excluded for Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD), 149 for Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), and 40 for Animal Fluency Test (AFT)]; inability to calculate PhenoAge (182 excluded) and KDM-Age (17 excluded); and missing sociodemographic and health behavior covariates [2 excluded for education level, 228 for family income to poverty ratio, 35 for body mass index (BMI), and 2 and 266 for smoking and drinking status, respectively]. Ultimately, 2202 participants (11.05% of the initial sample) were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online).

Calculation of BA

BA was measured using KDM-Age and PhenoAge. The KDM-Age method calculates BA based on the following clinical biomarkers: C-reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine, glycosylated haemoglobin, serum albumin, total serum cholesterol, blood urea nitrogen, serum alkaline phosphatase, and systolic blood pressure. PhenoAge is determined using a multivariable mortality risk model that includes the following clinical biomarkers: albumin, creatinine, glucose, CRP concentration, lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red blood cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count. Two points should be noted: 1) in the NHANES data, systolic blood pressure is calculated as the average of two measurements; 2) due to database limitations, CRP information is not publicly available in NHANES and was therefore excluded from the development and calculation of the BA models. However, previous studies have demonstrated a very high correlation (r=0.99) between PhenoAge values calculated with and without CRP, indicating the robustness of this measure even in the absence of CRP.11 In addition, several other studies have successfully developed BA models using clinical biomarker sets that did not include CRP.12 KDM-Age acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration were defined as the difference between each BA measure (KDM-Age or PhenoAge) and the CA at the time of biomarker measurement. BA acceleration was defined as KDM-Age acceleration >0 or PhenoAge acceleration >0.

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive assessment was conducted via home interviews or at mobile examination centers (MEC) using three tests: CERAD, AFT, and DSST. The CERAD Word Learning Test (CERAD-WL) is designed to evaluate immediate and delayed learning of novel verbal information. It was administered three times with a different word order each trial. Participants were asked to read aloud 10 unrelated words and then recall as many as possible. The score was the total number of words recalled across all three trials (0–30 points). The delayed word recall task (CERAD-DR) was conducted after completion of the AFT and DSST, and the score was the total number of words recalled (0–10 points). The AFT is a key component of executive function underlying language fluency. Participants were asked to name as many animals as possible within 1 minute, and the total number of correct responses was recorded as the score. The DSST, a subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, assesses sustained attention, processing speed, and working memory. It is administered on a paper form that displays digits 1–9 alongside their corresponding symbols in a key at the top of the page. Participants have 2 minutes to copy the appropriate symbols into 133 boxes next to the corresponding digits. The total number of correct matches constitutes the score. To date, no formal cutoff criteria have been established for CERAD tests (including CERAD-WL and CERAD-DR), AFT, or DSST to identify cognitive impairment. Therefore, following previous literature, this study defined low cognitive function as performance below the 25th percentile of each cognitive test score distribution. This threshold has been widely applied in large population-based studies and is supported by robust empirical evidence.1314

Collection of baseline features

Covariates included in this study were selected based on their association with cognitive impairment and their use in previous similar studies.15 Covariate information was primarily obtained through structured questionnaires administered at the study baseline (2011–2012 and 2013–2014 cycles). Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex (male or female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and other races), income level [poverty income ratio (PIR), defined as the ratio of family income to the poverty threshold and used as a proxy for socioeconomic status], and education level (less than 9th grade, 9–11th grade, high school graduate, some college, and college graduate). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never vs. current smoker), alcohol consumption (≥12 alcoholic drinks per year vs. none), and BMI. BMI was calculated from directly measured height and weight. History of major chronic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, was collected and defined based on self-reported and measured data. In addition, stratified associations between PhenoAge/KDM-Age and cognitive impairment defined by CERAD, DSST, and AFT are presented in Supplementary Figs. 2–7 (only online).

Pre-processing of machine learning features

To address the class imbalance between the cognitively normal and cognitive decline groups, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). SMOTE balances the dataset by generating new synthetic samples from existing minority class instances. Supplementary Fig. 8 (only online) presents the detailed class distribution of cognitively normal and cognitive decline participants in the dataset. The Boruta algorithm is an all-relevant feature selection method that iteratively evaluates the importance of actual features by creating shadow features. We applied the random forest (RF)-based Boruta algorithm to SMOTE-balanced data to select the variables most associated with cognitive risk from the candidate predictors, thereby reducing model complexity and enhancing generalizability. The results and details of the variable selection process are presented in Supplementary Fig. 9 (only online).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of all participants were described and stratified by cognitive impairment status. Given the complex multistage clustered design of NHANES, we applied the MEC sampling weights according to recommendations from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Weighted analysis of variance was used for continuous variables, and the Rao–Scott χ2 test was applied for categorical variables. For variables with less than 10% missing data, single imputation using the mode was performed; for those with 10%–25% missingness, multiple imputation was conducted using the mice package in R. In multiple imputation, five datasets were generated and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Associations between KDM-Age, PhenoAge, and cognitive risk were analyzed using weighted logistic regression models, yielding odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three adjustment levels were fitted: Model 1 served as the baseline; Model 2 included demographic covariates (race, education level, household income); and Model 3 further adjusted for health behavior and metabolic status covariates (BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, hypertension, and diabetes). To assess dose–response relationships across the three models, KDM-Age and PhenoAge were modeled as log-transformed continuous variables and categorized into quartiles (Q1: ≤25th percentile; Q2: 25th–50th percentile; Q3: 50th–75th percentile; Q4: 75th–100th percentile). Restricted cubic spline models were applied to examine the relationships between KDM-Age, PhenoAge, and the three cognitive function scores. Stratified analyses by sex, race, and other key covariates were then conducted in the fully adjusted models to evaluate the associations of KDM-Age and PhenoAge with the cognitive function scores.

Six predictive models were subsequently developed based on the key variables identified by the Boruta algorithm: RF, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Kernel k-Nearest Neighbors (K-KNN), Naive Bayes (NB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). RF, an ensemble learning method, significantly reduces the risk of overfitting by constructing multiple decision trees and averaging their predictions.16 It also effectively handles complex interactions among features. LightGBM is an efficient implementation of gradient boosting that offers significant computational advantages when processing large-scale datasets.17 The K-KNN model classifies samples based on similarity between instances, making it suitable for data with uneven sample distributions.18 NB achieves high computational efficiency when handling large datasets.19 SVM, using kernel techniques and a maximum-margin classification strategy, excels at handling high-dimensional and nonlinearly separable problems, demonstrating superior generalization performance in small-sample settings.20 XGBoost, an ensemble algorithm based on gradient boosting, efficiently supports parallel computation and regularization, is robust to missing values, and achieves excellent predictive accuracy on structured and large-scale tasks.21 These models have also been successfully applied in previous studies analyzing NHANES data, demonstrating their applicability.

Model comparison is a crucial method for evaluating the performance of machine learning models. This process involves evaluating multiple models on a standardized dataset and using consistent evaluation metrics to ensure a fair comparison. Depending on the nature of the task, metrics such as accuracy, F-beta score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), sensitivity, specificity, and the precision-recall (PR) curve are selected. A higher AUC-ROC is a key indicator for selecting the best model, while the other metrics serve as supplementary tools for performance assessment. Analysis of variance and Kruskal–Wallis tests are used to examine differences in metrics between models.

Additionally, we employed SHAP values to assess overall feature importance in the top-performing machine learning model. SHAP is a significant recent advancement in tree model interpretability, based on a game-theoretic framework that aggregates local contributions of individual features to enable an interpretable analysis of global model behavior. Compared to other global approximation methods, SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to individual predictions, providing insights into the decision-making process of the optimal model.

Data analysis for the study was performed using R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Basic characteristics of participants

A total of 2202 participants (weighted sample size: 21250242) were included in this study, with a cognitive impairment prevalence of 1.74%, 12.41%, and 7.00% as assessed by CERAD, DSST, and AFT, respectively. The overall population had a mean age of 68.87 years, was 52% female, comprised 82% non-Hispanic whites, and had a mean BMI of 29.10. Overall, participants with cognitive impairment were more likely to be older, of non-Hispanic black race, have lower educational attainment, lower income, and exhibit higher prevalence of diabetes (30% vs. 17% in the DSST group) and hypertension (p<0.001). Additionally, BA metrics revealed accelerated KDM-Age and a 6–8-year increase in mean PhenoAge in the cognitive impairment group, with 46% of participants in the highest PhenoAge quartile (Table 1).

Analysis of the association between biological aging and cognitive function

In the fully adjusted models (Table 2), PhenoAge exhibited a significant dose–response relationship with cognitive impairment: each one-unit increase in PhenoAge was associated with higher odds of impairment on CERAD (OR=1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.09), DSST (OR=1.08, 95% CI 1.06–1.10), and AFT (OR=1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07) (p<0.001). Quartile analysis of PhenoAge showed that, compared with Q1, participants in Q4 had 17.48-fold higher odds of CERAD impairment, and 7.69- and 5.98-fold higher odds of DSST and AFT impairment, respectively (p for trend <0.001) (Supplementary Table 1, only online). In contrast, associations with KDM-Age were weaker and inconsistent: only a marginal association was observed with DSST (OR=1.02, p=0.049), and no acceleration subgroup reached statistical significance (all p>0.05) (Supplementary Table 2, only online). Notably, the PhenoAge acceleration group exhibited a 2.17-fold increased risk of DSST impairment (p=0.017) and a significant nonlinear dose–response relationship with DSST (U-shaped curve with inflection at the 60th percentile; p for nonlinearity=0.003), whereas KDM-Age showed weak linear negative associations with CERAD and AFT (p<0.05) (Fig. 1).

Further subgroup analyses (Supplementary Figs. 2–7, only online) revealed significant heterogeneity: in the CERAD test, the association between PhenoAge acceleration and cognitive impairment exhibited an interaction effect in participants with diabetes (p for interaction=0.049); DSST analyses indicated a significant interaction by education level, with college graduates showing an OR of 1.11 (95% CI 1.06–1.16; p for interaction=0.044); in the AFT model, alcohol consumption status and BMI significantly modified the strength of the association (p for interaction <0.05). Notably, non-Hispanic black participants were more sensitive to PhenoAge acceleration (OR=1.12 for CERAD and 1.06 for DSST), whereas KDM-Age showed no significant effect in this subgroup. Threshold effect analysis (Supplementary Table 3, only online) further suggested that when PhenoAge acceleration exceeded 72.75 units, its association with DSST performance was substantially attenuated (likelihood ratio test p=0.002).

Development and validation of machine learning models

Table 3 and Fig. 2 present detailed evaluations of six machine learning methods—RF, LightGBM, K-KNN, NB, SVM, and XGBoost—across multiple performance metrics: AUC-ROC (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 10, only online), calibration curves (Fig. 2B), decision curve analysis (Fig. 2C), AUC-PR (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 11, only online), sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 12, only online), specificity (Supplementary Fig. 13, only online), F-beta score (Supplementary Fig. 14, only online), and accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 15, only online). In predicting different cognitive domains, RF demonstrated overall superiority across models. In the AFT model, it achieved 92.0% accuracy (AUC-ROC=0.968, AUC-PR=0.973), while in the CERAD model it reached clinical-grade performance with 98.5% accuracy, 99.5% sensitivity, and an AUC-ROC of 0.997, significantly outperforming other algorithms (all p<0.001). Despite the increased complexity of DSST data leading to generally lower performance metrics, RF remained robust with 88.0% accuracy and 81.6% specificity, significantly higher than XGBoost and LightGBM. Notably, LightGBM excelled in sensitivity (97.4% for AFT) and AUC-ROC (0.995 for CERAD) but exhibited wide variability in specificity (80.0%–97.0%); among traditional algorithms, K-KNN achieved 99.7% specificity for CERAD but had lower sensitivity (94.1%). NB showed the lowest AUC-ROC (0.828) in the DSST model. Calibration curves showed high concordance between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes for LightGBM and XGBoost, while decision curve analysis confirmed their superior standardized net benefit over traditional models (K-KNN and NB) within the high-risk threshold range (0.2–0.8). Furthermore, performance metrics differed significantly among the models. A 20% test set was used for final model evaluation. After multiple adjustments, the model demonstrated consistent performance across different data split strategies, further confirming the stability of the RF model (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17, only online).

SHAP analysis of social determinants and biological aging metrics

Multidimensional analysis of the association between accelerated BA and cognitive function was conducted using SHAP models (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 18, 19, only online). SHAP plots (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 18, only online) show the importance of each feature in the machine learning model for cognitive decline. Across the CERAD, DSST, and AFT assessment systems, sociodemographic variables (education level, race/ethnicity, and household income) exhibited protective effects on cognitive function (SHAP values 0.014–0.135) that substantially exceeded those of BA metrics (all SHAP values <0.011). Across cognitive dimensions, education consistently emerged as a key predictor (SHAP values of 0.131, 0.120, and 0.135 for CERAD, DSST, and AFT, respectively), whereas the negative association strength of advanced age, female sex, and metabolic risks (BMI and smoking) varied heterogeneously across assessment tools. Additionally, Fig. 3 displays the rank contributions to cognitive function prediction and cumulative predictive performance, with final predictive values of 0.537, 0.524, and 0.538, respectively. Sociodemographic factors dominated across models: in CERAD (race contribution 47.5%), AFT (race contribution 52.2%), and DSST (education contribution 32.3%), race/ethnicity (CERAD +0.255; AFT +0.281), sex (CERAD +0.156; AFT +0.141), and education level (CERAD +0.0557; AFT +0.0549; DSST +0.169) were identified as core predictors of cognitive function, with their combined contributions significantly surpassing those of other factors. BA metrics had weak and heterogeneous effects: total contributions of PhenoAge and KDM-Age were markedly lower than those of sociodemographic factors. The SHAP scatter plot matrix (Supplementary Fig. 19, only online) provided an in-depth analysis of complex inter-variable relationships. Education level exhibited a dense positive distribution at high SHAP values (>0.1) and showed significant correlations with age and BMI. Moreover, the explanatory power of KDM-Age was substantially enhanced after adjusting for socioeconomic confounders such as the PIR. To further clarify the incremental value of BA metrics beyond sociodemographic factors, a nested model comparison was conducted (Supplementary Tables 4–6, only online). The results showed that PhenoAge provided additional explanatory power for cognitive performance, particularly in the DSST model, whereas KDM-Age exhibited weaker effects, consistent with the SHAP findings.

DISCUSSION

This study used large-scale NHANES data to systematically examine the associations between two BA measures, KDM-Age and PhenoAge, and cognitive impairment. Machine learning model construction combined with interpretability analyses revealed the main influencing factors. The results revealed a statistically significant dose–response trend between PhenoAge and cognitive decline: each unit increase was associated with 6%, 8%, and 5% higher risks of impairment on the CERAD, DSST, and AFT tests, respectively (all p<0.001), and participants in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited 5.98 to 17.48 times greater risk compared to the lowest quartile (Q1). By comparison, KDM-Age showed only a marginal association with DSST performance (OR=1.02, p=0.049). The RF model performed exceptionally on the CERAD test (AUC-ROC=0.997, sensitivity 99.5%), significantly outperforming other machine learning algorithms (p<0.001). The SHAP analysis further revealed the important role of sociodemographic factors in distinguishing the risk of cognitive impairment and illustrated the complex interactions between PhenoAge and social determinants. The protective effects of sociodemographic factors—such as education level, race, and household income (SHAP values 0.014–0.135)—far exceeded those of BA indicators (SHAP values <0.011), underscoring the critical importance of social determinants in cognitive decline. These findings support the potential utility of PhenoAge in risk stratification for cognitive impairment and provide a reference for developing socially oriented precision prevention strategies.

Subgroup analyses further revealed significant heterogeneity in the associations between BA markers and cognitive function. Specifically, PhenoAge acceleration exhibited a stronger effect on the risk of cognitive impairment in individuals with diabetes (interaction p=0.049). This finding reflects a synergistic mechanism between glucose metabolism abnormalities and biological BA. Hyperglycemia may exacerbate neuronal oxidative stress and microglial activation via the advanced glycation end products pathway, thereby intensifying cognitive decline.2223 In addition, education level significantly modified the association between PhenoAge and DSST performance (interaction p=0.044). A more pronounced risk increase was observed among highly educated individuals, suggesting a dual role of cognitive reserve.24 Cognitive reserve may both protect neural function and increase sensitivity to subtle impairments induced by BA. These results underscore the necessity of personalized intervention strategies.

This study further complements the theoretical foundation regarding the association between BA and cognitive impairment. Previously, Li, et al.12 conducted a cross-sectional study in an elderly population and reported a synergistic effect of serum 25(OH)D3 levels and cognitive function on biological aging progression. Lower cognitive performance was significantly associated with accelerated biological aging. In comparison, the present study systematically evaluated the dose–response relationships between two BA metrics, PhenoAge and KDM-Age, and the risk of cognitive impairment. It also incorporated SHAP analysis to examine their relative contributions alongside sociodemographic variables, thereby expanding upon existing research. PhenoAge incorporates multiple biomarkers which reflect systemic inflammation and metabolic status. These features have been widely implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease.2526 In addition to inflammation-driven processes, aging of the neurovascular unit and dysfunction of the blood–brain barrier may impair the clearance of metabolic waste in the brain, leading to disturbances in synaptic homeostasis.27 Moreover, imbalances in adipokines and metabolic abnormalities may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby contributing to the neurodegenerative cascade.28 Building on earlier studies, the present analysis revealed a consistent association between accelerated PhenoAge and cognitive impairment. In contrast, KDM-Age reflects aging primarily in peripheral organs such as the cardiovascular and renal systems, as evidenced by its core indicators including creatinine and total cholesterol. These indicators are predominantly associated with cardiovascular risk and may have limited relevance to the pathological processes within the central nervous system. This interpretation is consistent with the relatively lower predictive performance of KDM-Age observed in our study.29

This study optimized model performance by combining the Boruta feature selection method with SMOTE oversampling, yielding results that significantly outperformed a conventional logistic regression model. These findings align with those of Duckworth, et al.,30 who applied machine learning to management of chronic conditions, confirming the superior capability of such techniques in handling multivariable, high-dimensional predictive tasks. In addition, the dominant role of sociodemographic factors in the association with cognitive function suggests that older adults with low education levels and low income may be more vulnerable to cognitive decline, potentially due to higher cumulative exposure to chronic stress.31

In model performance comparisons, the RF model demonstrated significant advantages. The superior performance of RF can be attributed to its robust ensemble learning capabilities. By constructing many decision trees and aggregating their predictions, RF significantly reduces the risk of overfitting. This approach is particularly well suited for complex data analyses. RF efficiently captures complex nonlinear relationships, as extensively validated in previous studies, especially in the medical field for detecting intricate biological interaction effects.32 Moreover, RF’s built-in bootstrap sampling and random feature selection mechanisms effectively address class imbalance in the data, thereby enhancing predictive performance. Although LightGBM excels in sensitivity and training speed, particularly in large-scale environments, its specificity exhibits considerable variability, which may affect its reliability in clinical applications.33 Traditional methods such as KNN perform well in terms of specificity. However, they rely on simple distance calculations, which are insensitive to nonlinear features and thus limit their applicability to complex datasets.34 This study demonstrates that the RF model, with its combined performance and stability, has broader potential applications in prediction and clinical risk assessment.

SHAP analysis revealed the hierarchy of variable contributions in cognitive risk prediction, further confirming the significance of social factors in cognitive decline. Sociodemographic factors such as education level, race, and household income predominated in the predictive models, with education level and race having the most pronounced effects on cognitive protection or impairment. Previous studies have shown that higher education can effectively mitigate the risk of cognitive impairment by enhancing neural plasticity.35 Among the BA indicators, PhenoAge contributed markedly more than KDM-Age, reflecting a broader range of BA characteristics. Patient interventions should account for individual biological factors while also addressing social determinants in order to develop more precise cognitive protection strategies.

BA reflects multiple interrelated physiological processes, including chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and oxidative stress, all of which are closely linked to neural dysfunction and ultimately contribute to cognitive impairment. Studies have shown that the brains of patients with Alzheimer’s disease exhibit chronic inflammation and microglial activation, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which disrupt synaptic function and promote β-amyloid deposition and Tau protein phosphorylation.36 In addition, metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance can impair neuronal repair capacity and are considered an important determinant of Tau phosphorylation.37 Moreover, excessive free radical production has been reported to disrupt neuronal membrane lipids and DNA structure, accelerating the progression of cognitive impairment.38 The biomarkers integrated into PhenoAge, including glucose levels and leukocyte count, reflect systemic alterations in inflammation and metabolism. This suggests that PhenoAge can capture systemic aging features associated with dementia-related pathology. Furthermore, compared with KDM-Age, which primarily reflects peripheral organ aging such as cardiovascular and renal function, PhenoAge may have more evident clinical and biological relevance in predicting cognitive impairment.

This study offers several advantages. It is the first to examine the relationship between BA and cognitive impairment. By utilizing the representative, large-scale NHANES dataset, the generalizability of the findings was effectively enhanced. We rigorously applied complex sampling weight adjustments and multiple imputation to minimize potential errors and increase the reliability of our conclusions. Additionally, the Boruta algorithm was used for feature selection, and SMOTE was applied to address class imbalance, thereby optimizing model performance and ensuring accuracy in practical applications. Furthermore, we applied SHAP analysis to explore the relative contribution of each variable to the model outputs associated with cognitive function. This enhanced model interpretability and provided a clear scientific basis for future mechanistic research.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Since a cross-sectional design was used, the findings reveal associations between BA and cognitive function but cannot establish causality. Future longitudinal cohort studies are needed to clarify the dynamic predictive value of BA indicators for changes in cognitive performance. This study did not include neuroimaging data or key biomarkers, which limits the exploration of mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment. Subsequent research should integrate multidimensional data to enhance model predictive performance. Although SMOTE effectively addresses class imbalance, the synthetic data it generates may introduce bias; therefore, further validation in independent cohorts is particularly important. Furthermore, although various tests were used to assess cognitive function in older adults, they cannot fully capture all aspects of cognition. Moreover, assessments based on questionnaires or self-administration may be subject to bias. Future studies should further refine these methods to provide clearer strategies for practical implementation.

This study offers new insights into the relationship between BA and cognitive impairment. The study systematically demonstrated a significant dose–response association between the PhenoAge metric and cognitive impairment, suggesting that PhenoAge may have greater indicative value than KDM-Age in identifying individuals at risk of cognitive decline. The RF model performed best in cognitive function assessments and further highlighted the important role of sociodemographic factors in discriminating cognitive impairment. These findings provide a reference for understanding the potential role of BA in cognitive function changes.
