Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry in the Ugandan Link Cohort
Authors: Mariam Hammad, Faustine Ceasor, Madeline Helwig, Bbuye Mudarshiru, Trishul Siddharthan, Bruce Kirenga, Amanda Robinson, Peter D Jackson
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Research on obstructive lung diseases in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) has expanded, but other respiratory diseases remain understudied. Preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) is defined by absence of defined lung disease and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with normal FEV1/FVC ratio (> 0.70) and FEV1 < 80% predicted. In high-income countries, PRISm is associated with increased mortality, cardiac disease, hospitalizations, and progression to obstructive lung disease.1,2

The prevalence of PRISm varies. In Denmark, the prevalence of PRISm is 7.6%; however, in Malawi and Uganda (LMICs), the prevalence is 36.8% and 16%, respectively.2, 3, 4 The reasons for these geographic variations is unclear; however, the high prevalence and known risks of PRISm underscore the need for longitudinal studies of PRISm in LMICs. We conducted a 7-year follow-up to the Ugandan Lung function in Nakaseke and Kampala (LiNK) study, with repeat spirometry and the addition of diffusion capacity (Dlco), total lung capacity (TLC) and quality of life (QofL) measurements at our follow-up visit.4,5 We hypothesized that patients with PRISm would have greater lung function decline and higher rates of obstruction on longitudinal spirometry, and lower TLC, lower Dlco, and worse QofL at the follow-up visit than age- and sex-matched patients.

Methods

This study leveraged the previously described LiNK cohort.5 The primary outcome was the change in spirometry at 7-year follow-up. Ethical approval was obtained from the Makerere Research and Ethics Committee (No. REC-Ref 2019-157), the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (No. HS-2722), and the Virginia Commonwealth University institutional review board (No. HM20024381). From September 2022 to September 2023, we recruited participants with PRISm using a random number generator to identify participants from the LiNK database. Control patients were sex- and age-matched in 10-year blocks and selected in an identical fashion. Patients were contacted by phone and invited to a research visit. Consenting patients completed repeat spirometry, Dlco, and TLC by nitrogen washout using the EasyOne-Pro platform (ndd Medical Technologies) in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines.6 FEV1 and FVC % predicted of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) African American reference values are reported for consistency with original LiNK study; Dlco and TLC % predicted of Global Lung Initiative (GLI) race-neutral reference are reported at follow-up. Inclusion criteria included prior enrollment in the LiNK cohort and ability to perform PFTs. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, inability to consent, and active pulmonary infection.

Statistical Analysis

Between-group comparisons used Welch t test for continuous parametric data and Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric data (eg, income, follow-up time); χ2 tests were used for categorical data (eg, smoking, new obstruction defined by FEV1/FVC < 0.70). Multiple linear regression evaluated associations of follow-up FEV1 and FVC adjusting for sex, age, smoking, baseline lung function, and self-reported biomass exposure years. Absolute changes in FEV1 and FVC volume and NHANES % predicted and annualized change (mL/y, % predicted/y) were compared. Adjusted values used analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for baseline values and time of follow-up; Dlco and TLC were compared using percent of race-neutral GLI reference values using the Welch t test.7 Proportion with new obstruction, defined as FEV1/FVC < 0.70, was compared in patients with PRISm and control patients using the χ2 test. QofL measures at follow-up to assess symptomatic burden associated with PRISm were compared using appropriate statistical tests and are presented. Analysis was conducted in IBM SPSS 29.0.2.0 and R Studio 4.5.2; Posit Team, 2025.

Results

We recruited 76 participants; 75 completed acceptable spirometry (PRISm: n = 38; control patients: n = 37). Demographic data and lung function for both groups are detailed in Table 1. FEV1 and FVC values were significantly lower in the PRISm group at both baseline and follow-up (P < .01). Unexpectedly, both FEV1 and FVC values increased in both groups at follow-up. The absolute change in lung function was similar in the PRISm vs control groups and is detailed in Table 1. Dlco and TLC % predicted was lower in the PRISm group vs control group (83.0% ± 19.1% vs 92.8% ± 10.4%; P = .008; 100.3% ± 16.0% vs 113.2% ± 18.0%; P = .002, respectively) (Fig 1A).

Annual change for FEV1 and FVC volume and % predicted was similar among PRISm and control groups before adjustment for baseline values (Table 1). Annualized FEV1 and FVC change with adjustment for baseline was compared with ANCOVA; the adjusted group effect on FEV1 and FVC change (PRISm – control) was 23.84 mL/y (P = .137) and 38.75 mL/y (P = .020), respectively. For ANCOVA for % predicted change, adjusted group effects were not statistically significant for FEV1 (0.658 percentage points/y; P = .403) or FVC (0.345 percentage points/y; P = .658).

BMI and mean biomass years were similar in PRISm and control groups (P > .05); the change in BMI at follow-up (0.21 ± 2.61) was similar between groups (Table 1). Adjusted regression models showed no significant association of FVC and FEV1 with self-reported biomass-years (β, −0.007 L/biomass-year; P = .238; β, −0.0097 L/biomass-year; P = .115, respectively). Annual income and smoking exposure were not associated with follow-up FVC (P = .837 and P = .804, respectively) or follow-up FEV1 (P = .629 and P = .883, respectively); the FVC and FEV1 model fit indices was adequate (R2 = 0.78; adjusted R2 = 0.757; and R2 = 0.726; adjusted R2 = 0.698, respectively).

QofL was assessed with St. George’s Respiratory questionnaire (SGRQ) and EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level (EQ-5D-3L), both validated in these settings. Patients with PRISm had significantly worse QofL in the SGRQ symptom (18.01 ± 14.26 vs 12.26 ± 7.02; P = .028), impact (11.10 ± 16.02 vs 3.29 ± 4.40; P = .005), and total scores (18.41 ± 17.64 vs 9.85 ± 9.47; P = .010). EQ-5D-3L showed a greater proportion of participants with PRISm reporting problems with mobility (26.3% vs 0.0%; P < .001), pain/discomfort (26.3% vs 7.9%; P = .035), and anxiety/depression (26.3% vs 0.0%; P < .001) (Fig 1C). Additionally, patients with PRISm reported worse overall health quality on the EQ-5D-3L visual assessment scale (72.5 ± 15.7 vs 85.4 ± 14.5; P < .01) (Fig 1C). Mean modified Medical Respiratory Council (mMRC) score was not significantly different in the PRISm group (3.31 vs 2.47; P = .054); however, more patients with PRISm reported significant limitations (mMRC score ≥ 3) (42% vs 16%; P = .008).

Discussion

Contrary to our hypothesis, there was not more significant decline in lung function over 7 years in patients with PRISm in a Ugandan cohort. Both groups showed an increase in FEV1 and FVC from baseline, and the increase in FVC was significantly greater in patients with PRISm when controlling for follow-up duration and baseline lung function with ANCOVA. This was unexpected given the average FEV1 decline of 17.7 to 46.4 mL/y.8 The improvement from baseline may be related to differences in PFT devices; the Easy-One-PC spirometer was used in LiNK, whereas the Easy-OnePro platform to perform TLC and Dlco testing was used at follow-up. Both platforms are US Food and Drug Administration-approved for lung function testing; however, subtle differences may exist. Another explanation for our findings is the dynamic nature of PRISm; some patients may transition out of this category over time, as seen in the Genetic Epidemiology of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD-GENE) study where 19.5% of patients with PRISm normalized spirometry at 5 years.9 Changes in body habitus were unlikely to account for these changes given insignificant changes in BMI at follow-up; other explanations include reversible lung disease (pre-bronchodilator results were used because these were used in LiNK), changes in air quality, or selection bias for those with improving lung function having greater rates of follow-up. Our regression models did not note significant contribution of smoking, biomass exposure, or income on follow-up spirometry when adjusting for age, baseline lung function, and sex. Patients with PRISm had lower Dlco and TLC, which was expected. Although mean Dlco and TLC in the PRISm group was normal (> 80% predicted), significantly lower measurements in patients with PRISm, without significant differences in BMI, suggest underlying physiological differences or early disease. This interpretation is supported by worse QofL with higher SGRQ scores and more problems reported in EQ-5D-3L mobility, anxiety/depression, and visual health assessments in the PRISm group. We found no clear association between biomass exposure or socioeconomic status and PRISm, despite other studies suggesting biomass may contribute.2

Limitations of this study included small sample size and lack of consistency between spirometry devices. Our study contributes to the literature on PRISm, particularly its trajectory and impact in LMICs. Further investigations of PRISm with larger populations are warranted to understand the trajectory and impacts on QofL in high-prevalence LMIC settings.
