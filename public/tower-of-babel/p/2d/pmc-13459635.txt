Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells synergize with fusobacterium nucleatum to drive colorectal tumorigenesis via gut microbiome dysbiosis
Authors: Keyi Zhang, Caihan Duan, Jiawei Chen, Qi He, Yu Jin, Jun Liu, Rong Lin, Chaoqun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe oncogenic role of F. nucleatum (Fn) in colorectal cancer (CRC) is increasingly recognized, yet its interaction with host stromal components, such as bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), remains poorly understood. Building on our previous discovery that BMSC-derived Wnt3a promotes Fn-driven tumorigenesis, this study aims to investigate the synergistic interplay between BMSCs and F. nucleatum in CRC pathogenesis via the gut microbiome.MethodsBased on the established ApcMin/+ mouse model of CRC, animals were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: control, Fn-only, BMSCs-only, and Fn+BMSCs co-treatment group. Gut microbiota composition was continuously analyzed over 8 weeks by metagenomic sequencing. Metagenomic functions were predicted using PICRUSt2.ResultsThe Fn+BMSCs co-treatment group exhibited the highest enrichment of F. nucleatum and the greatest reduction in microbial diversity. Fn+BMSCs co-treatment induced a distinct pro-tumorigenic shift, marked by a decline in symbiont Lactobacillus and an increase in pathobiont Escherichia-Shigella. Metagenomic analysis revealed a unique enhancement of butanoate metabolism in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group. Furthermore, a profoundly elevated LPS level was discovered in the Fn+BMSCs co-treatment group, indicating hyperactivation of the pro-inflammatory and proliferative TLR4/NF-κB pathway.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that BMSCs synergize with F. nucleatum to create a tumorigenicmicroenvironment by driving microbial dysbiosis, reprogramming metabolic pathways, and amplifying pro-inflammatory signaling. Our findings reveal that BMSCs fuel CRC progression via multiple mechanisms: by altering the gut microbiome ecology and, as previously discovered, by providing oncogenic Wnt3a signals. Targeting the synergistic BMSC-Fn axis may thus offer a novel therapeutic strategy for CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), as the third most common malignancy worldwide, continues to present challenges in early detection and has a complex etiology [1, 2]. Beyond genetic predispositions, the gut ecosystem has emerged as a pivotal player in intestinal homeostasis and disease pathogenesis. Alterations in the gut ecosystem have been implicated in changes in human health and disease, including inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), CRC, and other malignancies of the digestive system [3–6].

In recent years, fecal metagenomics and metabolomic analyses have become a powerful tool for the quantification of the gut microbiome and have shown promising diagnostic potential. In this context, Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum, Fn) has been thrust into the spotlight. In human studies, this anaerobic bacterium is not merely a passenger but is increasingly recognized as a key oncogenic driver, consistently enriched in CRC tissues and associated with tumor progression and metastasis [4, 7, 8]. Studies in murine models have partially elucidated its pathogenic mechanisms, including the activation of pro-inflammatory and pro-proliferative signaling pathways such as TLR4/NF-κB and E-cadherin/β-catenin [9, 10]. Furthermore, the functional output of the microbiota, its metabolites, is equally critical. Both human and animal studies have shown that microbial metabolites, such as bile acids and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), exhibit complex, context-dependent roles in CRC tumorigenesis [11].

While the microbial community and its metabolites are crucial, the host stromal response constitutes an equally important dimension of the tumor microenvironment. Bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), multipotent stromal cells with homing capabilities, exhibit a paradoxical behavior in gastrointestinal pathophysiology. On one hand, BMSCs were reported to ameliorate colitis-associated tumorigenesis in mice [12]. On the other hand, studies have indicated that BMSCs can be recruited to the stroma and contribute significantly to cancer progression [13]. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for F. nucleatum and immunohistochemical staining for BMSC markers GFP, we previously confirmed that Fn infection actively recruits BMSCs to the intestinal submucosa and that the two entities co‑localize in the tumor microenvironment [14]. The precise role and mechanisms of BMSCs in CRC, particularly their interaction with oncogenic microbes, remain a significant knowledge gap.

Our previous work provided critical insights into this interaction by establishing an Fn-infected BMSC transplantation model in ApcMin/+ mice [14]. We demonstrated that Fn infection recruits BMSCs to the intestinal submucosa, which then migrate to the mucosal layer and exacerbate tumorigenicity by enhancing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, we identified BMSCs as the major source of Wnt3a, a key ligand of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway that functions as an oncogenic driver in CRC [15, 16]. Notably, knockdown of BMSC-derived Wnt3a or administration of its antagonist analogs significantly attenuated Fn-induced colorectal carcinogenesis, confirming Wnt3a as a pivotal mediator in the pro-tumorigenic crosstalk between BMSCs and F. nucleatum.

Critically, while our prior study established the central role of BMSC-derived Wnt3a in this pathogenic axis [14], the downstream mechanisms by which BMSCs and F. nucleatum synergize to drive CRC progression remain largely unexplored. Specifically, how their interaction shapes the gut microbial community, reprograms metabolic pathways, and amplifies pro-inflammatory signaling cascades, which are intimately associated with CRC development, has not been fully elucidated. We hypothesize that BMSCs and Fn do not act in isolation but rather synergize to create a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Therefore, in this study, by using the established Apc Min/+ mouse model of CRC, we investigated how the co-action of BMSCs and F. nucleatum drives microbial dysbiosis, reprograms metabolic pathways, and amplifies pro-inflammatory signaling, thereby accelerating CRC tumorigenesis.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J-ApcMin/+ mice, a well-established animal model for studies of human CRC, were used in this study [17]. Eight-week-old male mice (Nanjing Biomedical Research Institute, China) were obtained and mated with female C57BL/6 mice to generate ApcMin/+ offspring. A total of 120 eight-week-old male ApcMin/+mice were used in this study. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the animal facility of Wuhan Union Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, with free access to food and water, a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and controlled temperature and humidity (40%–60%). Pregnant females were housed individually, and the offspring were genotyped at 3 weeks of age to confirm the presence of the ApcMin allele as described previously [14]. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Wuhan Union Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. IORG0003571), and performed in accordance with national and EU guidelines.

Experimental group distribution

The mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 30 each group): (1) F. nucleatum group: Experimental mice received daily oral gavage of F. nucleatum (1 × 109 CFU suspended in 0.1 mL PBS) per day; (2) Control group: Experimental mice received daily oral gavage of an equal volume (0.1mL) of PBS; (3) BMSCs group: Experimental mice received weekly intravenous injections of BMSCs (1 × 107 cells) via the tail vein; (4) F. nucleatum + BMSCs co-treatment group: Experimental mice were treated with a combination of F. nucleatum (as in group 1) and BMSCs (as in group 3).

Successful colonization of F. nucleatum was confirmed seven days post-inoculation by quantifying Fn-specific CFU per gram of fecal sample.

Bacterial strain

F. nucleatum (ATCC25586) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and cultured by the Wuhan Research Institute of First Light Industry (Wuhan, Hubei Province, China). F. nucleatum was cultured overnight at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions in fluid thioglycollate medium (HB5191, Hopebio, China).

Cell culture

BMSCs were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, Hubei Province, China) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing a lower concentration of glucose (1,000 mg/l), 15% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin.

Sample collection

Fresh fecal samples were collected from individual mice at weeks 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80℃ until processing. Experimental mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. At each of the five time points (week 0, 2, 4, 6, 8), six mice per group were euthanized, and the blood and tissue samples were collected for the following experiments.

Metagenomic sequencing and microbiota analysis

Genomic DNA of 200 mg stool samples were extracted using QIAamp® DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (catalog number: 51604). DNA concentration and purity were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Library preparation was performed at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit 2.0 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with an average insert size of 350 bp. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA), with a target sequencing depth of 10 Gb per sample. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.23.2). Taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken2 (version 2.1.2) against the RefSeq database. Bracken (version 2.6.2) was used to estimate relative abundances at the species level. Alpha diversity (Shannon index) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, Unweighted UniFrac distance) were calculated using the phyloseq package (version 1.42.0) in R (version 4.4.2) and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed to identify differentially abundant taxa across groups and time points using the online LEfSe tool (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/lefse/) with a default alpha value of 0.05 and a logarithmic LDA score threshold of 2.0.

Metagenomic functional prediction and metabolic pathway analysis

The functional potential of the gut microbiota was predicted by PICRUSt2 (version 2.5.1; Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States). The predicted metagenomes were analyzed for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthologs and pathways using the KEGG database. Differences in butanoate metabolism and other relevant pathways among groups were compared based on the LEfSe analysis of the predicted KEGG pathways with the same parameters as described above.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) quantification

The concentration of LPS in the colorectal tissue samples was measured using a commercial Pierce Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (catalog number: A39552). Absorbance was read at 405 nm, and LPS concentrations were calculated against a standard curve.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Differences between groups across multiple time points were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

BMSCs facilitate recruitment and enrichment of F.nucleatum

In our previous study, we performed immunofluorescence staining for BMSC marker GFP and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for F. nucleatum on colonic tissue. These histological analyses confirmed that F. nucleatum infection recruits BMSCs to the intestinal submucosa and that the two entities colocalize within the mucosal layer, establishing a direct physical interaction [14]. Based on the previous findings, we first assessed the temporal dynamics of F. nucleatum abundance to investigate its interaction with BMSCs. Longitudinal profiling revealed that while the Fn-only group showed a moderate increase in F.nucleatum levels, the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group exhibited a dramatic and sustained elevation at later stages (weeks 4, 6, and 8), consistently higher than the Fn-only group. This finding extends our previous observation that F. nucleatum infection actively recruits BMSCs to the intestinal submucosa, and now suggests that BMSCs, in turn, create a microenvironment that potently enhances F. nucleatum colonization. Co-treatment of F. nucleatum and BMSCs results in a reduction of microbial diversity and community structural shifts

To assess the overall impact of F. nucleatum and BMSCs on the gut microbiota, we analyzed both alpha and beta diversity metrics. The Fn+BMSC co-treatment group exhibited a sustained decline in microbial diversity, as measured by the Shannon index at weeks 4, 6, and 8, where the Shannon index was significantly lower than that of the other experimental groups, indicating a significant loss of community richness and evenness (Fig. 2.A).

These findings collectively illustrate that co-treatment of F. nucleatum and BMSCs induces marked reductions in microbial diversity and profound structural shifts in the gut microbiota ecosystem.

F. nucleatum and BMSCs co-treatment reshape the gut microbiota community conducive to tumorigenesis

To characterize the taxonomic shifts induced by F. nucleatum and BMSCs, we first analyzed microbial composition at the phylum level. As shown in the phylum-level stacked bar plots, a time-dependent increase in the abundance of Fusobacteriota was observed in the Fn-only and Fn+BMSC co-treatment groups from week 2 to 8, concurrent with a progressive decrease in Bacteroidota and Firmicutes, the two dominant bacterial phyla of the normal human gut microbiota [18]. (Fig. 3.A) This phylum-level reshaping, characterized by the expansion of Fusobacteriota at the expense of core symbiotic communities, suggests a fundamental and deleterious alteration of the gut ecosystem.

To further dissect taxonomic changes, genus-level heatmap analysis (Fig. 3.B) revealed a unique pro-inflammatory ecology exclusive to the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group. This profile was defined by a progressive expansion of the pathobiont Escherichia-Shigella, and a concurrent, significant decline in the symbiont Lactobacillus (known for reinforcing the intestinal epithelial barrier) [19]. The temporal dynamics of these key genera were quantified: Fusobacterium exhibited the highest relative abundance in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group, with a sharp increase from week 4 to 8 (Fig. 3.C), significantly exceeding that of the Fn-only group. Conversely, Lactobacillus abundance was consistently lowest in the Fn+BMSC group across all time points, particularly at week 6 and 8 (Fig. 3.D), in sharp contrast to its relatively stable levels in the control and BMSC-only groups.

Collectively, these taxonomic shifts illustrate that BMSCs synergistically promote the recruitment of F. nucleatum and reshape the gut microbiota toward a pro-tumorigenic ecology.

Co-treatment of F. nucleatum and BMSCs synergistically activates a pro-tumorigenic butanoate metabolism

To elucidate the functional consequences of microbial dysbiosis, we next analyzed the metagenomic profiles of the gut microbiota. PCA analysis of predicted gene families revealed that the functional structure in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group was distinctly segregated from all other groups across all time points, as visualized in multiple PCA dimensions (Fig. 4. A-E, week 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, respectively). This consistent, time-dependent segregation across all analyzed time points indicates a profound and unique functional shift induced by the co-treatment of Fn and BMSCs, reflecting extensive remodeling of microbial gene families.

BMSCs promote Fn-associated Oncogenic Signaling

Having established that BMSCs cooperate with F. nucleatum to drive profound microbial and metabolic dysbiosis, we investigated their combined impact on a key molecular pathway in Fn-mediated tumorigenesis.

Our data demonstrate that BMSCs critically amplify this pathogenic cascade. Measurement of total luminal LPS, a master driver of TLR4 activation [20], revealed that the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group exhibited significantly higher overall LPS levels than all other groups at weeks 4, 6, and 8, with the magnitude of increase growing over time (Fig. 6. A-E, week 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, respectively). For example, at week 4 (Fig. 6.C), the Fn+BMSC group showed a marked elevation in LPS abundance compared to other groups (with control group: p < 0.001, with BMSC-only: p < 0.001, with Fn-only: p = 0.002). The observed increase in total luminal LPS likely reflects the combined effect of F. nucleatum enrichment and the broader dysbiotic shifts in the microbial community (such as the expansion of other LPS-producing pathobionts like Escherichia-Shigella), collectively contributing to an elevated overall load of this pro-inflammatory molecule. This synergistic amplification of the LPS-TLR4 pro-inflammatory axis by BMSCs provides a critical link between the observed microbial dysbiosis and the activation of oncogenic signaling within the host epithelium. Importantly, this aligns with our prior mechanistic work [14], wherein the pro-tumorigenic crosstalk between BMSCs and Fn was critically dependent on BMSC-derived Wnt3a, the ablation of which significantly attenuated CRC carcinogenesis. ApcMin/+ mice treated with BMSCs knockdown for Wnt3a showed slower solid tumor growth, a reduction in mean tumor volume, and an amelioration of clinical symptoms, including rectocele and hematuria. The current data suggest that BMSCs fuel a feed-forward cycle, simultaneously amplifying microbiota-derived pro-inflammatory signals (LPS) and delivering a direct proliferative signal (Wnt3a) to the epithelium, thereby collaboratively driving malignant progression.

Critically, these synergistic molecular and microbial disruptions culminated in severe pathological outcomes as described in our previous study [14]. In the present study, we did not repeat tumor burden analysis, as our previous work has already established that the Fn+BMSC co-treatment significantly increases tumor numbers (4.91 ± 0.25 vs. 3.09 ± 0.21, P = 0.001) and tumor volumes (2.80 ± 0.53 mm³ vs. 1.60 ± 0.62 mm³, P = 0.011) in this model. Instead, we focused on the underlying microbial and inflammatory mechanisms. This enhanced oncogenic phenotype was further accompanied by serious clinical complications, including hematochezia and rectocele, alongside a significant exacerbation of body weight loss beginning at 15 weeks post-BMSC treatment initiation. These macroscopic findings definitively demonstrate that the BMSC-Fn synergy, operating through the interconnected mechanisms of microbial dysbiosis, metabolic reprogramming, and innate immune activation, ultimately translates into accelerated and more aggressive disease pathogenesis.

Discussion

Our study identifies a previously unrecognized synergistic alliance between BMSCs and F. nucleatum that establishes a pro-tumorigenic environment in the gut. Building upon our prior work, which demonstrated that BMSCs migrate to the intestinal mucosa upon F.nucleatum infection and exacerbate tumorigenicity in ApcMin/+ mice through the secretion of Wnt3a [14], we now extend these findings by delineating the multifaceted mechanisms underlying this pathogenic interaction. We demonstrate that BMSCs are not passive bystanders but active facilitators of Fn-driven CRC tumorigenesis, acting through a continuous cascade of ecological reshaping, metabolic reprogramming, and innate immune hyperactivation, all of which likely converge with the BMSC-derived Wnt3a signal to promote malignant progression.

The initial and pivotal event in this cascade is the BMSCs-mediated facilitation of F. nucleatum recruitment and enrichment. The sustained highest abundance of F. nucleatum in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group, as confirmed by both taxonomic and LEfSe analyses, suggests that BMSCs create a microenvironment conducive to F. nucleatum colonization. This is consistent with our previous observation that BMSCs can modulate the tumor microenvironment to support oncogenic processes. The co-action of BMSCs and F. nucleatum resulted in profound erosion of microbial diversity, a hallmark of dysbiosis linked to CRC [21]. The selective depletion of beneficial Lactobacillus, a crucial genus for barrier integrity, coupled with the expansion of pathobionts like Escherichia-Shigella (which includes genotoxic pks+ strains capable of damaging host DNA) [22, 23], results in a gut ecosystem deprived of protective members and invaded by genotoxic and pro-inflammatory agents. This dysbiotic shift, fostered by BMSCs, likely further enhances the oncogenic potential of the gut microenvironment.

Beyond taxonomic shifts, our functional metagenomics data reveal a consistent and unique enhancement of butanoate metabolism in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group. The role of butyrate in CRC is complex and context-dependent, often referred to as the “butyrate paradox” [24]. While it can be tumor-suppressive at high concentrations, perhaps via HDAC inhibition [25], it may fuel tumorigenesis in specific microenvironments [26]. Our results support the latter scenario in the context of Fn-dominated dysbiosis. The enhanced butanoate metabolism we observed may be co-opted to promote carcinogenesis. This aligns our findings with the “driver-passenger” model [27, 28], where F. nucleatum drives tumorigenesis and creates a tumor-supportive microenvironment through altered metabolic pathways.

The significantly elevated total LPS levels in colonic tissue in the Fn+BMSC co-treatment group provide a direct molecular connection between this synergistic dysbiosis and sustained TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation, a cornerstone of Fn-mediated tumorigenesis. While F. nucleatum is a known source of LPS capable of activating TLR4 signaling [9, 29], our data on total luminal LPS levels indicate that the BMSC-Fn synergy creates a dysbiotic environment that leads to a significantly increased overall burden of this potent microbial product. This could be due to both the enrichment of F. nucleatum itself and the co-expansion of other LPS-producing bacteria (such as the Escherichia-Shigella) within the restructured microbial community. Notably, this LPS-TLR4/NF-κB axis may synergize with the Wnt3a/β-catenin pathway identified in our previous study, as well as with other F. nucleatum virulence mechanisms like FadA-mediated E-cadherin/β-catenin signaling [30], to create a potent pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Importantly, the specificity of BMSCs in mediating this synergistic effect was validated in our previous study, where other cell types (e.g., NIH/3T3 fibroblasts) failed to recapitulate the pro-tumorigenic phenotype induced by BMSCs, and knockdown of BMSC-derived Wnt3a abolished the synergistic effect [14]. Thus, BMSCs not only initiate oncogenic signaling via Wnt3a but also amplify inflammatory signals through modulation of the microbiota.

Despite the novel findings presented herein, our study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the research was conducted primarily in the ApcMin/+ mouse model, which may not fully recapitulate the complex, multi-stage pathogenesis of human CRC. Second, although we established a synergistic interaction between BMSCs and F. nucleatum, the precise molecular pathways through which BMSCs facilitate F. nucleatum recruitment and enrichment remain to be further elucidated. A key limitation is that we did not perform interventional studies, such as the application of probiotics or TLR4 antagonists to directly test causality, due to constraints in time and animal resources. Finally, our focus was on the LPS-TLR4 axis and its connection to prior Wnt3a findings, and we cannot rule out the involvement of other inflammatory or oncogenic pathways in this collaborative process.

To summarize, our study positions BMSCs as a critical ally in Fn-associated CRC, extending our previous identification of BMSC-derived Wnt3a as a key mediator. They together create a vicious cycle by fostering a dysbiotic environment that supports F. nucleatum expansion, reshaping microbial metabolism, and amplifying a key host pro-inflammatory and proliferative pathway. This sequential cascade underscores the complexity of the host-microbe interaction in cancer and suggests that targeting this synergistic BMSC-Fn interaction could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for CRC.

Conclusion

This study, together with our previous work [14], demonstrates that BMSCs act as a critical driver that synergizes with F. nucleatum to promote CRC tumorigenesis. We have delineated a sequential pathogenic cascade wherein BMSCs first facilitate the recruitment and enrichment of F. nucleatum, which in turn triggers a profound dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. This dysbiosis is characterized by a loss of microbial diversity, a decline in protective symbionts such as Lactobacillus, and an expansion of pathobionts such as Escherichia-Shigella. Furthermore, this altered microbial community undergoes a functional reprogramming, notably enhancing butanoate metabolism, which may fuel the pro-inflammatory and proliferative microenvironment. Crucially, we demonstrate that this BMSC-Fn synergy culminates in the hyperactivation of the LPS/TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis, suggesting a feed-forward loop of chronic inflammation and epithelial injury. Importantly, these microbiota-centered mechanisms operate collectively with the previously established pathway wherein BMSC-derived Wnt3a directly activates oncogenic Wnt/β-catenin signaling in epithelial cells [14]. Collectively, our findings delineate a novel mechanism by which the tumor microenvironment, represented by BMSCs, can co-opt an oncogenic bacterium such as F. nucleatum to drive tumor progression through dysbiotic disruptions, metabolic reprogramming, and pro-inflammatory disruptions. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that concurrently target the BMSC-Fn axis and the BMSC-derived Wnt3a/β-catenin signaling pathway may represent a promising approach to intercept CRC progression.
