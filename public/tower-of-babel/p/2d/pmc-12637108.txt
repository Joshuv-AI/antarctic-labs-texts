Sleep, Cognitive Dysfunction, and Daily Living in Chinese Older Adults
Authors: Yi Jin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cognitive dysfunction and impaired activities of daily living (ADL) in middle-aged and older adults are critical public health issues, but the links with sleep remain unclear. Using data from CHARLS, which involved 39,852 participants aged 45 years or older, this study explored these associations using restricted cubic spline and multivariate logistic regression models. A U-shaped relationship was found between sleep duration and cognitive performance (including information processing speed, memory, and attention) as well as the risk of ADL limitation, with 7 hours as the optimal duration. Both short (<6 h) and long (≥9 h) sleep were linked to higher cognitive decline risk (OR=1.51 and 1.72, P<0.05). Stratified analyses revealed that older males with long sleep (≥9 h) and older females with short sleep (6 to 7 h) had elevated risks of ADL limitation (OR=1.72 and 1.51, respectively). This study provides epidemiological evidence for targeted interventions.

Alzheimer disease (AD), the most common form of neurodegenerative disorder, exhibits a bidirectional relationship with sleep disturbances, characterized by pathologic hallmarks such as amyloid-beta deposition and tau protein tangles.1 Epidemiological evidence have identified abnormal sleep duration (either insufficient or excessive) is an independent risk factor for AD onset.2,3

Cognitive decline has emerged as a global public health challenge, particularly in aging societies, where dementia prevalence continues to rise.4 While factors such as educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and chronic disease management influence cognitive function, they explain only a portion of cognitive variability (eg, only 38% of cognitive variance at age 54 can be attributed to known factors).5,6 Cognitive impairment, a core clinical manifestation of AD,7,8 encompasses memory decline, executive dysfunction, and language deficits, often accompanied by progressive deterioration in activities of daily living (ADL).9,10 ADL includes both basic ADL (eg, eating, dressing, toileting) and instrumental ADL (eg, financial management, medication adherence), serving as a critical indicator of independent living capacity.11 Studies indicate that mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is associated with subtle ADL deficits, whereas moderate-to-severe AD stages exhibit significantly heightened ADL dependency, imposing substantial burdens on families and societal care systems.12 In China, the prevalence of MCI13 among individuals aged 60 years or older reaches 15.5%, with dementia affecting 6.04% of this population14—AD being the most prevalent subtype (3.94%), totaling over 60 million cases. However, nearly half of MCI cases remain undiagnosed, leading to delayed interventions and accelerated ADL decline.15

Although known factors such as education, socioeconomic status, and chronic disease management partially account for cognitive variability (eg, explaining only 38% of cognitive variance at age 54), substantial individual differences remain unexplained. Furthermore, the interplay between sleep disturbances and ADL deterioration remains insufficiently elucidated. For instance, excessive daytime sleepiness (≥1 h/d) may increase AD risk by 40%, yet its specific pathways of ADL impairment (eg, via accelerated executive dysfunction or motor coordination deficits) require further investigation.16 In contrast, existing interventions (eg, cognitive training, pharmacotherapy) may slow ADL decline, but large-scale clinical validation of integrated strategies targeting the sleep-cognition-ADL triad remains lacking.

Exercise interventions (eg, resistance training, aerobic exercise) have been shown to improve cognitive function in older adults, with distinct exercise modalities exerting domain-specific effects on different cognitive domains (eg, memory, executive function).17 However, the mechanisms underlying cognitive decline remain incompletely understood. While known risk factors (eg, education, socioeconomic status) partially account for cognitive variability, a substantial proportion of influencing factors remain unidentified. Genetic determinants (eg, APOE4 allele) contribute to dementia susceptibility but explain only a fraction of cognitive deterioration, underscoring the need to explore additional biological and environmental modifiers. Furthermore, current cognitive interventions lack personalization; for instance, although resistance training and aerobic exercise differentially target-specific cognitive domains, evidence-based criteria for individualized recommendations remain elusive.18

Psychological interventions (eg, cognitive behavioral therapy) show efficacy in certain populations (eg, breast cancer patients) but require further validation in other conditions (eg, schizophrenia). Notably, research on cognitive interventions exhibits a “Westernization” bias, over-relying on Western theoretical frameworks while lacking culturally adapted approaches.9

To address these gaps, this study utilizes nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the nonlinear associations between sleep duration, cognitive performance, and ADL function in middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, we aim to identify the optimal sleep duration range, examine potential variations by sex and age, and provide empirical evidence to support the development of culturally tailored, sleep-health intervention strategies.19,20

METHODS

Data Source and Study Population

This study utilized longitudinal cohort data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011 to 2020), which covered middle-aged and older adults (≥45 y) across 28 Chinese provinces. Data preprocessing was conducted using R software (version 4.3.0). Participants with >20% missing data in key variables—including cognitive scores, CES-D scores, sleep duration, and ADL impairment—were excluded. Continuous variables were screened for outliers (defined as values beyond the mean±SD=3). The final analytical sample consisted of 39,852 participants who completed at least 2 follow-up surveys (Fig. 1）.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics and Sleep Pattern Distribution

The study included 39,852 participants (mean age: 62.3±8.7 y; 53.2% female). Stratified by sleep duration, 18.4% were short sleepers (<6 h), 64.1% normal sleepers (6 to 9 h), and 17.5% long sleepers (≥9 h). The short sleep group exhibited significantly higher CES-D depression scores (14.2 vs. 11.6, P<0.001), while the long sleep group had the highest prevalence of ADL limitations (23.1% vs. 15.3% in normal and 17.8% in short sleepers, P=0.003) (Table 1).

Nonlinear Association Between Sleep Duration and Cognitive Function

Restricted cubic spline models revealed a significant U-shaped dose-response relationship between sleep duration and cognitive scores (Pnonlinear<0.001). Optimal cognitive performance corresponded to 6.8 hours of sleep (95% CI: 6.2-7.3). Compared with normal sleepers, both short (OR=1.68, 95% CI: 1.32-2.14) and long sleepers (OR=1.72, 95% CI: 1.29-2.31) showed elevated risks of cognitive impairment. These associations remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, and mental health scores.

Sex- and Age-Specific Heterogeneity in Sleep-ADL Associations

Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that both short (OR=1.51, 95% CI: 1.18-1.93) and long sleep durations (OR=1.63, 95% CI: 1.25-2.11) were associated with increased ADL limitation risks. Sex-stratified analyses revealed pronounced heterogeneity: older males (65 y or older) with long sleep had the highest ADL risk (OR=1.92, 95% CI: 1.41-2.62), whereas older female short sleepers (<6 h) showed greater vulnerability (OR=1.67, 95% CI: 1.23-2.26) (Pinteraction=0.021). Cognitive function is optimal in both men and women when sleep duration is ∼6 hours (Fig. 2), with the lowest level of impairment in activities of daily living (Figs. 3 and 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study, utilizing a Chinese middle-aged and elderly cohort, demonstrates a U-shaped association between sleep duration and risk through nonlinear modeling, with an optimal sleep window of 6 to 7 hours.21,22 These findings show both consistencies and divergences with Western cohort studies. For instance, while the UK Biobank study identified 7 hours as the sleep duration associated with minimal AD risk, its predominantly Western population differs significantly from our cohort in terms of lifestyle and genetic background. Similarly, the Framingham Heart Study found an increased AD risk with both short (<6 h) and long (>8 h) sleep durations; however, it did not account for napping behavior—a common practice in Chinese populations. Our results suggest that habitual napping may indirectly elevate ADL limitation risk by extending total sleep duration (≥9 h), contrasting with weaker associations observed in Western studies and highlighting the cultural influences on sleep-AD relationships.23,24

The observed sex- and age-specific effects complement existing literature.25 NHANES data revealed a higher prevalence of sleep-disordered breathing among elderly males, where prolonged sleep may mask OSA-related sleep fragmentation and exacerbate AD risk, consistent with our finding of significantly higher ADL risk in older males with long sleep (≥9h). Conversely, Japanese community studies indicate that women are more vulnerable to short sleep-induced cognitive impairment due to caregiving responsibilities and perimenopausal hormonal fluctuations, supporting our observation of pronounced cognitive deficits in older females with short sleep (<6 h).26

Regarding mechanistic pathways, we quantified that mental health status (CES-D scores) mediated 28.6% of the sleep-cognition association, aligning with the “sleep-mood-cognition” triad hypothesis from the Rotterdam Study. Our study advances this model by precisely estimating the mediation effect size, providing stronger evidence for the “psycho-neurodegenerative comorbidity” hypothesis.27

Several limitations warrant consideration: (1) self-reported sleep measures may underestimate true sleep fragmentation; (2) the cross-sectional design cannot fully exclude reverse causation (eg, AD pathology disrupting sleep); and (3) absence of key AD biomarkers (APOE ε4, CSF markers). Future research should incorporate polysomnography and neuroimaging to longitudinally examine sleep-AD pathologic dynamics (eg, β-amyloid deposition) and develop targeted interventions (eg, CPAP for elderly males with OSA, CBT for women).28–30

In conclusion, our epidemiological evidence positions sleep management as a crucial AD prevention target, particularly considering China’s unique napping culture and sex-specific risk patterns.31,32 We recommend integrating sleep health into community chronic disease programs and developing AD risk assessment tools incorporating sleep duration, psychological status, and functional metrics.33,34

Study Summary

This study demonstrates a significant U-shaped association between sleep duration and Alzheimer disease (AD) risk in middle-aged and older adults, with an optimal sleep window of 6 to 7 hours. Deviation from this range (<6 h or ≥9 h) was associated with a 1.5 to 1.7-fold increased risk of cognitive decline and activities of daily living (ADL) limitations. Notably, prolonged sleep duration in elderly males and short sleep duration in elderly females showed particularly heightened risks for ADL impairment.

By quantifying the dose-response relationship between sleep duration and AD risk in a Chinese population, this study provides critical evidence supporting the application of “precision sleep medicine” in the prevention and management of neurodegenerative disorders. The findings highlight the importance of maintaining optimal sleep duration as a potentially modifiable risk factor for AD, while emphasizing the need for population-specific and sex-stratified considerations in sleep-related interventions.35
