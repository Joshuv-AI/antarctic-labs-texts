Downregulation of SPRED1 drives keratinocyte hyperproliferation in psoriasis via activation of the ERK-RSK1 signaling axis
Authors: Ruixia Hou, Xinhua Li, Ling Zhou, Zehong Wang, Xuping Niu, Hui Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPsoriasis features excessive epidermal keratinocyte proliferation, and our previous work identified a non-synonymous mutation in SPRED1 in psoriasis, specifically an enrichment of c.A881T (p.Y294F) in exon 7 (chr15:38351210). Combining public database data and clinical skin specimens, this study confirmed reduced SPRED1 expression in psoriatic lesions. Functional investigations were performed using HaCaT/NHEK keratinocyte transfection models, imiquimod-induced psoriasis-like mouse models, pathway agonist/inhibitor rescue assays, co-immunoprecipitation and dual-luciferase detection. SPRED1 indirectly interacts with nuclear ERK to block ERK-RSK1 activation and restrain keratinocyte overgrowth, whereas miR-126 downregulates SPRED1 by direct targeting. Intradermal supplementation of recombinant SPRED1 alleviates skin pathological changes in mice. These findings define the miR-126/SPRED1/ERK-RSK1 regulatory cascade and support SPRED1 as a promising candidate target for psoriasis treatment.Keywords: SPRED1, ERK, psoriasis, keratinocytes, proliferation

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by uncontrolled keratinocyte (KC) hyperproliferation. As the dominant cell type of the epidermis, KCs constitute the skin’s stratum corneum, fulfilling a vital barrier-protective role.1 KCs are central to psoriasis pathogenesis, with their functions regulated by multiple factors including genetics, cytokines/receptors, metabolism, cell signaling pathways, transcription factors, non-coding RNAs, and antimicrobial peptides.2 Previous studies from our group have demonstrated aberrations in the in vitro proliferative capacity, cytokine secretion profiles, gene expression patterns, and immune regulatory functions of psoriatic KCs.3,4,5 However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these abnormalities remain to be elucidated.

SPREDs (Sprouty-related EVH1 domain-containing proteins) are tyrosine kinase-binding proteins homologous to Sprouty, comprising four family members: SPRED1, SPRED2, SPRED3, and Eve3.6 While the potential roles of SPRED genes in various diseases have been increasingly elucidated, SPRED1 remains the most extensively studied. The human SPRED1 gene localizes to chromosome 15q13.2 and encodes a 444-amino acid protein.7 Structurally, SPRED1 comprises three distinct domains: the N-terminal EVH1 domain (Enabled/VASP homology 1), the C-terminal Sprouty-related SPR domain, and the central c-Kit binding domain (KBD).8 Each domain mediates unique functions: The EVH1 domain interacts with neurofilament protein (NFP), forming a ternary complex with the GAPex and GAPc regions (GRD) of NF1 via hydrophobic bonds. Following growth factor stimulation, EVH1 exerts negative regulation on ERK signaling by recruiting NFP to the plasma membrane.9 The SPR domain governs SPRED1 membrane localization, facilitating NFP recruitment into plasma membrane cavities.10 The KBD is essential for SPRED1 phosphorylation, a critical step for its functional activity.11

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that SPRED1 regulates critical cellular processes including development, migration, and proliferation, and is implicated in pathophysiological mechanisms of psoriasis, tumor metastasis, and hematopoietic disorders. In wound healing and chemotaxis models, SPRED1 overexpression disrupts actin stress fibers and inhibits chemokine-induced cell migration.12 As a key regulator of RhoA-mediated cell motility and signal transduction, SPRED1 suppresses both serum growth factor- and chemokine-induced migration.13 In melanoma, SPRED1 functions as a tumor suppressor: SPRED1 knockdown activates ERK signaling and enhances cell proliferation.14 Similarly, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), SPRED1 expression is markedly reduced in neonatal cases but increases post-treatment. SPRED1 overexpression inhibits ERK signaling and AML cell proliferation, whereas silencing SPRED1 promotes ERK activation and cellular expansion.15 In lung adenocarcinoma, SPRED1 suppresses fibroblast growth factor signaling, governing lung branching morphogenesis and tissue development. Lower SPRED1 expression correlates with poorer prognosis, while high SPRED1 levels predict extended survival.16 In hepatocellular carcinoma, SPRED1 acts as a tumor suppressor: Its downregulation promotes tumor cell invasion/metastasis, whereas overexpression reduces proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, accompanied by decreased MMP2/MMP9 levels.17 Mechanistically, SPRED1 inhibits ERK activation by forming a complex with Raf: SPRED1 inhibition induces ERK phosphorylation in HepG2 cells, while overexpression decreases ERK phosphorylation in MHCC97H cells.18 Notably, the ERK pathway is central to KC proliferation, migration, and apoptosis in psoriasis lesion formation.19 SPRED1’s SPR domain binds Caveolin-1 to regulate plasma membrane translocation and ERK signaling.20 Despite its role as a major ERK pathway negative regulator, how SPRED1 modulates KC hyperproliferation in psoriasis remains undefined.

Our prior studies demonstrated that co-culturing healthy KCs with mesenchymal stem cells from patients with psoriasis induced KC hyperproliferation, accompanied by downregulated SPRED1 mRNA/protein expression and enhanced ERK phosphorylation.21 Additionally, psoriasis, characterized by KC overproliferation, exhibits non-synonymous mutations in SPRED1, with the enrichment of c.A881T (p.Y294F) in exon 7 (chr15:38351210).22 These findings suggest that aberrant SPRED1 expression contributes to psoriasis pathogenesis by modulating ERK activation. While extensive research has explored SPRED1’s molecular mechanisms, its role in psoriasis remains uncharacterized. In this study, we confirmed significant SPRED1 downregulation in psoriatic epidermis. Mechanistically, low-expressed SPRED1 in psoriasis interacts with caveolin at the nuclear outer membrane-endoplasmic reticulum contact site, concurrently inducing the phosphorylation of ERK. This activates the ERK-RSK1 signaling pathway, promoting KC hyperproliferation and exacerbating psoriatic pathogenesis. Using an imiquimod-induced psoriasis mouse model with SPRED1 overexpression, we showed that SPRED1 suppresses epidermal hyperplasia by inhibiting the ERK-RSK1 pathway. Collectively, these results establish SPRED1 as a critical regulator in psoriasis pathogenesis.

Results

SPRED1 expression is downregulated in psoriatic lesions

Analysis of the GSE30999 dataset (170 samples: 85 psoriatic lesions vs. 85 non-lesional skin) from the GEO database revealed significantly reduced SPRED1 expression in psoriatic epidermis (Figure 1A, p < 0.05). Clinical validation in six psoriatic and six healthy epidermal samples showed typical epidermal hyperplasia in psoriasis (H&E staining, Figures 1B and 1C, p < 0.001), with SPRED1 primarily localized in KC nuclei of the spinous and granular layers (immunohistochemistry, Figure 1D). Tissue immunofluorescence confirmed a marked decrease in SPRED1 expression within the psoriatic epidermis compared to healthy controls (Figures 1E and 1F, p < 0.001). Consistently, qRT-PCR and western blot analyses demonstrated downregulated SPRED1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels in psoriatic epidermis (Figures 1G–1I, p < 0.01). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential role for SPRED1 in psoriasis pathogenesis.

SPRED1 overexpression inhibits keratinocyte proliferation by suppressing ERK phosphorylation

To investigate the role of SPRED1 in KCs, we overexpressed SPRED1 in HaCaT and NHEK cells using the pLVX-spred1 vector. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed significant upregulation of SPRED1 mRNA and protein levels compared to empty vector controls (Figures 2A–2C, p < 0.001). CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that SPRED1 overexpression significantly reduced cell viability (Figures 2D–2G) and proliferation (Figures 2H and 2I, p < 0.001), consistent with results from cell counting experiments (Figures 2J and 2K, p < 0.001). Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle demonstrated that SPRED1 overexpression decreased the proportion of cells in S phase and increased G0/G1 arrest (Figures 2L and 2M, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively). qRT-PCR and western blot analyses further showed downregulation of proliferation-related genes PCNA, Cdc42, Ki67, and c-Myc (Figures 2N–2P). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed nuclear localization of SPRED1 (excluding nucleoli) and reduced PCNA expression in SPRED1-overexpressing cells (Figures 2Q–2S, p < 0.01).

Previous studies have demonstrated that SPRED1 inhibits cell proliferation by suppressing ERK phosphorylation, but its mechanism in NHEK cells remains unreported. We therefore investigated the expression of SPRED1 downstream effectors ERK and RSK1. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that SPRED1 overexpression significantly reduced mRNA levels of ERK and RSK1 (Figure 2T, p < 0.01). Western blot analysis further showed suppressed ERK phosphorylation and reduced RSK1 protein expression (Figures 2U and 2V, p < 0.05). Immunofluorescence staining of phospho-ERK (p-ERK) revealed its nuclear localization (excluding nucleoli) in NHEK cells, with significantly decreased fluorescence intensity in SPRED1-overexpressing cells (Figures 2W and 2X, p < 0.01). Collectively, these findings indicate that SPRED1 inhibits KC proliferation by suppressing ERK phosphorylation, providing new insights into its role in KC biology.

SPRED1 knockdown promotes keratinocyte proliferation via enhanced ERK phosphorylation

To determine whether SPRED1 knockdown exerts opposing effects to its overexpression in KCs, we silenced SPRED1 in HaCaT and NHEK cells using pLKO-spred1-shRNA. qRT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed significant reduction of SPRED1 mRNA and protein levels compared to pLKO-NC controls (Figures 3A–3C). CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that SPRED1 knockdown significantly increased cell viability (Figures 3D–3G) and proliferation (Figures 3H and 3I, p < 0.05), consistent with cell counting results (Figures 3J and 3K, p < 0.001). Flow cytometry analysis showed a significant increase in the S-phase population following SPRED1 knockdown (Figures 3L and 3M, p < 0.001). qRT-PCR and western blot further demonstrated the upregulation of proliferation markers PCNA, Cdc42, Ki67, and c-Myc (Figures 3N–3P). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed reduced SPRED1 and increased PCNA fluorescence intensity in knockdown cells (Figures 3Q–3S, p < 0.05). These data indicate that SPRED1 depletion enhances KC proliferation, mirroring the phenotypic reversal observed in overexpression experiments.

To explore the molecular mechanism of SPRED1 in KC proliferation, we analyzed the expression of ERK and RSK1, key downstream effectors of the SPRED1 pathway. qRT-PCR confirmed increased mRNA levels of ERK and RSK1 in SPRED1-knockdown cells (Figure 3T), while western blot analysis showed enhanced ERK phosphorylation and RSK1 protein expression (Figures 3U and 3V, p < 0.05). Immunofluorescence staining of p-ERK revealed elevated nuclear fluorescence intensity in SPRED1-depleted cells (Figures 3W and 3X, p < 0.01), consistent with activated ERK signaling. Collectively, these data demonstrate that SPRED1 loss promotes KC proliferation by upregulating the ERK/RSK1 pathway, reinforcing its role as a negative regulator of MAPK signaling in epidermal cell homeostasis.

Intradermal injection of recombinant SPRED1 protein alleviates psoriasis-like skin lesions in IMQ-induced mice

To investigate the in vivo role of SPRED1 in epidermal hyperplasia, we established IMQ-induced psoriasis-like skin lesions in C57BL/6 mice. IMQ treatment induced obvious psoriatic plaques on the dorsal skin, while SPRED1 significantly ameliorated lesion severity (Figure 4A). Histopathological analysis revealed that IMQ increased epidermal thickness, which was reversed by SPRED1 (Figures 4B and 4C, p < 0.05).

Molecular analysis showed that IMQ treatment upregulated mRNA levels of proliferation markers PCNA, Cdc42, Ki67, and c-Myc, accompanied by decreased SPRED1 expression in lesional skin (Figure 4D). Consistently, western blotting confirmed increased PCNA protein (Figures 4E and 4F, p < 0.001) and reduced SPRED1 (Figures 4E and 4F, p < 0.05). In contrast, SPRED1 overexpression downregulated proliferation genes (Figure 4D), decreased PCNA protein (Figures 4E and 4F, p < 0.01), and restored SPRED1 expression (Figures 4E and 4F, p < 0.001). Immunofluorescence staining of SPRED1 and BrdU further validated reduced KC proliferation after intradermal injection of recombinant SPRED1 protein (Figures 4G and 4H). Mechanistically, IMQ induced ERK phosphorylation and decreased RSK1 expression in skin lesions, while SPRED1 suppressed ERK phosphorylation and rescued RSK1 levels (Figures 4I–4M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SPRED1 inhibits epidermal hyperplasia and alleviates psoriasis-like phenotypes in vivo, highlighting its therapeutic potential in psoriasis.

SPRED1 regulates NHEK cell proliferation via indirect interaction with ERK

Given the nuclear co-localization of SPRED1 and ERK observed in previous studies, we hypothesized an interaction between these proteins. Co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed physical associations between SPRED1 and ERK, as well as SPRED1 and caveolin (Figure 5A). To dissect the role of ERK signaling in SPRED1-mediated proliferation, we performed rescue experiments using the ERK agonist Ro67-7476 and inhibitors PD98059/SCH772984. qRT-PCR showed no effect of these reagents on ERK mRNA levels (Figure 5B), while western blotting revealed that the agonist reversed SPRED1-overexpression-induced suppression of ERK phosphorylation (Figures 5C and 5D, p < 0.05). Conversely, PD98059/SCH772984 abrogated ERK activation caused by SPRED1 knockdown (Figures 5C and 5D, p < 0.001), with immunofluorescence staining of p-ERK confirming these results (Figures 5E and 5F, p < 0.05).

Functionally, the agonist rescued KC viability/proliferation inhibited by SPRED1 overexpression, whereas PD98059/SCH772984 suppressed the enhanced proliferation in SPRED1-knockdown cells (Figures 5G–5J). miR-126–3p promotes NHEK cell proliferation by targeting SPRED1

Bioinformatic analysis predicted that miR-126–3p directly targets the SPRED1 3′-UTR at nucleotides 525–531 (Figure 6A). Luciferase reporter assays were conducted to verify the interaction between SPRED1 and miR-126. Co-transfection of miR-126 mimic and dual-luciferase reporters harboring the wild-type SPRED1 3′UTR significantly suppressed luciferase activity, whereas transfection with reporters containing a miR-126 target site-mutated SPRED1 3′UTR had no impact (Figure 6B, p < 0.001). qRT-PCR confirmed elevated miR-126 expression in psoriatic skin lesions compared to healthy controls (Figure 6C, p < 0.01). To investigate miR-126 function in NHEK cells, we transfected cells with miR-126 mimic or inhibitor. miR-126 overexpression significantly increased miR-126 levels (Figure 6D, p < 0.001) and reduced SPRED1 mRNA/protein expression (Figures 6E–6G, p < 0.01). Conversely, miR-126 inhibition decreased miR-126 levels (Figure 6D, p < 0.001) and upregulated SPRED1 (Figures 6E–6G).

Functional assays revealed that miR-126 overexpression enhanced KC viability and proliferation (Figures 6H–6K), accompanied by increased mRNA levels of Ki67/PCNA and elevated PCNA protein (Figures 6L–6N). Conversely, miR-126 knockdown reduced proliferation and downregulated proliferation markers (Figures 6H–6N). We generated a SPRED1 overexpression vector with 3′UTR deletion (3′UTR-deleted SPRED1) to block miR-126-induced targeted repression, aiming to clarify whether the biological functions of miR-126 rely specifically on SPRED1. Then, miR-126 mimic and the 3′UTR-deleted SPRED1 vector were co-transfected into NHEK cells. Functional experiments showed that the ectopic expression of 3′UTR-deleted SPRED1 significantly rescued the abnormal cellular phenotype caused by miR-126 mimic. In NHEK cells transfected with miR-126 mimic combined with 3′UTR-deleted pLVX-SPRED1, SPRED1 mRNA (Figure 6O) and protein levels (Figures 6P and 6Q) were notably upregulated. Meanwhile, cell viability (Figure 6R) and proliferation ability (Figures 6S and 6T) were markedly declined in this experimental group. In summary, these rescue assays strongly validate that miR-126 executes its regulatory function by specifically targeting SPRED1.

To elucidate the mechanism responsible for miR-126–3p upregulation in psoriasis pathogenesis, primary human epidermal KCs (NHEK) were treated with the M5 pro-inflammatory cytokine mixture (IL-1α, IL-17, IL-22, TNF-α, OSM). Stimulation with M5 significantly upregulated miR-126–3p expression (Figure S1A), and concurrently suppressed SPRED1 mRNA (Figure S1B) and protein expression (Figures S1C and S1D). Moreover, M5 treatment remarkably improved cell viability (Figures S1E and S1F) and promoted cell proliferation (Figures S1G and S1H) in NHEK cells. These results collectively suggest that the high expression of miR-126–3p in psoriasis is driven by inflammatory factors, and this upregulation of miR-126–3p further promotes NHEK cell proliferation, which is consistent with the pathological characteristics of psoriasis.

Discussion

Psoriasis is a complex chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent scaly erythema and severe pruritus, which substantially impacts patients’ physical-mental health and quality of life.23 Although its pathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated, excessive proliferation of epidermal KCs is a hallmark of disease onset. Psoriatic skin is defined by uncontrolled KC proliferation and aberrant differentiation, processes that drive the formation of lesional microenvironments. Emerging evidence indicates that KCs are not passive bystanders in psoriasis but actively orchestrate disease progression at multiple pathogenic nodes, significantly influencing disease initiation and exacerbation.2 Functionally, KCs act as both effector and promoter cells, secreting cytokines, adhesion molecules, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides in response to external stimuli. These mediators facilitate intercellular communication with neighboring cells, promoting T cell activation, neutrophil chemotaxis, and subsequent inflammatory cascades.24

The Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK cascade is a canonical MAPK pathway that mediates diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, migration, differentiation, cytoskeletal organization, and cell morphology maintenance. It also contributes to inflammatory responses, immune regulation, and tissue repair.25 ERK functions as a central regulator in this pathway, activated by growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) via the Ras-Raf-MEK signaling axis. Activated ERK phosphorylates downstream substrates, including transcription factors (e.g., c-Jun, c-Fos, ATF2) and protein kinases (e.g., myosin light-chain kinase, calpain, FAK).26 Under physiological conditions, controlled ERK activation ensures normal cell growth and metabolism. Pathologically, hyperactivation of the ERK pathway contributes to cellular damage, highlighting the importance of its tight regulation for cellular homeostasis.27 In KCs, ERK signaling is activated upon cellular damage or loss of integrity, with peak ERK phosphorylation coinciding with the proliferative phase of wound healing. Activated ERK promotes downstream gene expression to orchestrate the inflammatory and proliferative phases of tissue repair.28

Studies have established caveolin as a negative regulator of the ERK pathway across diverse cell types, a role shared by SPRED family proteins.29 SPREDs contain a C-terminal cysteine-rich (CR) domain, analogous to caveolin, which undergoes palmitoylation for membrane anchoring.30 Notably, SPRED1 localizes to cholesterol-rich membrane rafts and interacts with caveolin-1; ectopic expression of SPRED1 in caveolin-expressing cells synergistically suppresses ERK activation and cell proliferation, indicating a cooperative inhibitory mechanism.20 As an integral membrane protein, caveolin-1 is inserted into the endoplasmic reticulum, transported via the Golgi apparatus, and incorporated into lipid domains for cell-surface trafficking.31 According to public database annotations, SPRED1 is localized to either the cell membrane or the nucleus, whereas our results demonstrate that SPRED1 is predominantly distributed in the nucleus in NHEK cells. Immunohistochemical analysis in NHEK cells revealed nuclear co-localization of SPRED1 and ERK (excluding nucleoli). MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are evolutionarily conserved non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by partial binding to mRNA targets, offering promising therapeutic potential for psoriasis.32 While miR-126 promotes angiogenesis by repressing SPRED1, its role in regulating SPRED1-ERK signaling in psoriatic KCs remains uninvestigated.33

In conclusion, our study establishes that low SPRED1 expression drives its pathogenic role in psoriasis. Specifically, we demonstrate that low-expressed SPRED1 in psoriasis interacts indirectly with ERK within the nucleus, concurrently inducing the phosphorylation of ERK. This event activates the ERK-RSK1 signaling cascade, driving aberrant KCs proliferation and exacerbating psoriatic epidermal hyperplasia. Additionally, we identify miR-126 as a key post-transcriptional regulator of SPRED1 in NHEK cells. Using an imiquimod-induced psoriasis mouse model, we show that SPRED1 overexpression significantly alleviates psoriasis-like phenotypes by suppressing the ERK-RSK1 pathway (Figure 7). The pathogenic relevance of this pathway extends beyond epidermal cell-autonomous effects, likely intertwining with the IL-23/IL-17 immune axis, the central inflammatory driver of psoriasis. On one hand, SPRED1 downregulation-induced ERK-RSK1 activation in NHEK cells may augment the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-23, IL-6), which promote dermal Th17 cell polarization and IL-17 production. This establishes a positive feedback loop wherein KCs-derived inflammation further exacerbates epidermal hyperplasia via the SPRED1-ERK pathway. On the other hand, IL-17 signaling may reciprocally amplify this axis by upregulating miR-126–3p expression in KCs, thereby suppressing SPRED1 and disinhibiting ERK phosphorylation. The miR-126–3p/SPRED1/ERK/RSK1 and IL-23/IL-17 immune axes may form an integrated regulatory network that orchestrates psoriasis pathogenesis. Thus, targeting the crosstalk between these pathways could yield synergistic therapeutic benefits for psoriasis management. Although the present study has elucidated the regulatory role of SPRED1 in KC proliferation and the ERK signaling pathway, several limitations still need to be acknowledged. The clinical sample size enrolled in this study was relatively limited, with only 10 cases included. A larger independent patient cohort is required to further validate the clinical expression pattern of SPRED1. Future work will focus on expanding the clinical sample size to confirm the clinical significance and broader applicability of our findings.

Schematic model of SPRED1 downregulation-mediated keratinocyte hyperproliferation in psoriasis

In psoriatic keratinocytes, reduced SPRED1 expression promotes ERK phosphorylation and activation of the ERK-RSK1 signaling cascade. This process drives aberrant keratinocyte proliferation and exacerbates psoriatic epidermal hyperplasia. Concurrently, miR-126 post-transcriptionally regulates SPRED1 expression to modulate keratinocyte hyperproliferation in psoriasis.

Our findings reveal that reduced SPRED1 expression drives its pathogenic function in psoriasis. Specifically, we demonstrate that low-expressed SPRED1 in psoriasis interacts indirectly with ERK within the nucleus, concurrently inducing the phosphorylation of ERK. This event activates the ERK-RSK1 signaling cascade, driving aberrant KC proliferation and exacerbating psoriatic epidermal hyperplasia. Additionally, we demonstrate that miR-126 post-transcriptionally regulates SPRED1 to modulate KC proliferation. In an imiquimod-induced psoriasis mouse model, SPRED1 overexpression significantly suppresses epidermal hyperplasia by inhibiting the ERK-RSK1 pathway (Figure 7). Collectively, these results establish SPRED1 as a critical mediator of psoriasis pathogenesis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for psoriasis treatment.

Limitations of the study

Although the present study has elucidated the regulatory role of SPRED1 in KC proliferation and the ERK signaling pathway, several limitations still need to be acknowledged. The clinical sample size enrolled in this study was relatively limited, with only 10 cases included. A larger independent patient cohort is required to further validate the clinical expression pattern of SPRED1. Future work will focus on expanding the clinical sample size to confirm the clinical significance and broader applicability of our findings. This study mainly focused on KCs and failed to explore the crosstalk between the SPRED1 pathway and dermal fibroblasts, dendritic cells, as well as Th17 cells, lacking systematic analysis of multicellular pathogenic networks in the skin microenvironment and resulting in incomplete interpretation of epidermal-dermal dysregulation in psoriasis. For molecular regulation, only the targeted inhibition of SPRED1 by miR-126 was validated. Other dysregulated non-coding RNAs in psoriatic lesions remain uninvestigated, and the upstream multi-layered regulatory and epigenetic mechanisms of SPRED1 need further exploration.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human data

Ten Han Chinese patients with vulgaris psoriasis and ten healthy controls aged between 18 and 60 years were recruited from Taiyuan Central Hospital. Detailed information of all volunteers is summarized in Table S1. The subjects were assigned to each group in sequence. No significant association between biological sex and the study outcomes was observed in human participants. All participants provided written informed consent and had refrained from using immunosuppressants, corticosteroids, or retinoids for at least 3 months prior to sampling. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital Taiyuan Hospital, and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (approval no. KY2023005). Following sampling, the epidermis was separated from the dermis using trypsin for subsequent analyses. SPRED1 and miR-126–3p expression was determined in 10 clinical tissue samples. HaCaT and NHEK cells were used for cellular assays (N = 6).

Animals

The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Taiyuan Central Hospital (approval no. SYXK2023-0014). C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Shanxi Medical University. Male mice were used to avoid confounding effects from cyclic sex hormones in female mice, reduce data variability, ensure experimental reproducibility. All the procedures for the care of the mice were in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal use in research. Mice were housed under controlled conditions: a constant temperature of 25 ± 1°C, a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) had their dorsal skin shaved and were topically applied with 62.5 mg of 5% imiquimod (IMQ) cream daily for 5 consecutive days (IMQ group). Control mice received daily applications of vaseline to the shaved dorsal skin (vaseline group). Lesion samples were collected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or stored at −80°C for subsequent qRT-PCR and western blot analyses. For all histological scoring (H&E staining, epidermal thickness measurement), immunofluorescence imaging quantification, and Western blot/PCR data analysis in animal experiments, all assessments were performed by two independent researchers who were blinded to the group assignments. Blinding was strictly implemented to minimize observer bias.

Cell culture

The human immortalized epidermal keratinocyte cell line HaCaT and Primary normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEK) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). HaCaT cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Invitrogen). NHEK cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% FBS under the same culture conditions. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell passaging was performed using 0.05% trypsin with 0.5 mM EDTA solution. The cell lines used in this study were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling to confirm their identity and genetic consistency. Additionally, all cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection method, which ensures the purity of the cultures and the reliability of the experimental results.

Method details

Plasmids and oligonucleotides

The following plasmids were purchased from PPL (Shanghai, China): pLVX-Flag-spred1, pLVX-vector, pLVX-spred1, pLKO-NC, and pLKO-spred1-shRNA. miR-126–3p mimic-NC, miR-126–3p mimic, miR-126–3p inhibitor-NC, and miR-126–3p inhibitor were obtained from RIBOBIO (Guangzhou, China). The ERK agonist Ro67-7476 and ERK inhibitors PD98059/SCH772984 were purchased from MCE (New Jersey, USA). Antibodies used in this study were sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), including: SPRED1 (ab271191), PCNA (ab92552), ERK (ab184699), phospho-ERK (ab201015), RSK1(ab32114), phospho-RSK1 (ab32413), caveolin (ab2910), β-actin (ab8227).

Establishment of stable cell lines

To generate SPRED1-overexpressing stable keratinocyte lines, HaCaT and NHEK cells were transfected with control plasmid (pLVX-vector) or SPRED1-overexpressing plasmid (pLVX-spred1) (PPL, Nanjing, China), respectively. For SPRED1-knockdown stable lines, HaCaT cells were transfected with control plasmid (pLKO-NC) or spred1-shRNA plasmid (pLKO-spred1-shRNA) (PPL, Shanghai, China). Keratinocytes were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until 80%–90% confluent. Transfection was performed using 2.5 μg/mL plasmid and 7.5 μL Lipofectamine 3000 reagent per well in serum-free medium, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After overnight incubation, the medium was replaced with growth medium containing 5% FBS. Transfected cells were selected with 3 μg/mL puromycin (Solarbio) for 2 weeks to establish stable lines. SPRED1 transfection efficiency was validated by qPCR and Western blot analysis.

For miR-126-3p-modified stable lines, NHEK cells were transfected with miR-126–3p mimic/control (miR-126-3p-mimic/miR-126-3p-mimic-NC) or miR-126–3p inhibitor/control (miR-126-3p-inhibitor/miR-126-3p-inhibitor-NC) (RIBOBIO, Guangzhou, China). miR-126–3p transfection efficiency was verified by qPCR.

Intradermal injection of recombinant SPRED1 protein in psoriasis-like skin lesions

C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) underwent daily application of 62.5 mg 5% IMQ cream to the shaved dorsal skin for 5 consecutive days. A total of experimental mice were randomly assigned into four groups: the Vaseline group, IMQ model group, IMQ + H2O control group, and IMQ + SPRED1 treatment group, with 3 mice in each group. Random grouping was performed to ensure the reliability of subsequent experimental results. Recombinant SPRED1 protein (CUSABIO, China) was diluted with sterile water to a working concentration of 40 ng/uL. Intradermal injection was performed concurrently with IMQ application, once daily for 5 consecutive days. For the IMQ + SPRED1 group, each mouse was injected with 25 μL of protein solution at two separate dorsal skin lesion sites, with a total volume of 50 μL per mouse per day. Under identical experimental conditions, the IMQ + H2O control group received an equal volume of sterile water (50 μL per mice per day) at the same injection sites and with the same treatment frequency.

For miRNA analysis, total miRNA was extracted and purified using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany), then reverse transcribed with the miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from epidermal tissues and transfected keratinocytes using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). Protein lysates were analyzed by ProteinSimple Wes (ProteinSimple, USA), an automated ultramicro protein expression quantification system, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-human SPRED1 (1:50, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-human p-ERK (1:100, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-human ERK (1:100, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-human p-RSK1 (1:100, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-human RSK1 (1:100, Abcam, UK) and rabbit anti-human β-actin (1:100, Abcam, UK). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ProteinSimple, USA) served as the secondary antibody. β-actin was used as an internal standard, and protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Boster, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 3 × 103 cells in 100 μL of complete medium were seeded into 96-well plates, with wells containing no cells serving as blank controls. For four consecutive days, cells were incubated with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution at 37°C for 2 h daily. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, with absorbance intensity positively correlated with viable cell number.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Cells were incubated with 50 μM EdU for 2 h, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with Apollo 567. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (5 mg/mL) for 30 min, and EdU-positive cells were quantified in five random fields under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell cloning assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected from each group. Cell suspension concentration was adjusted using a hemocytometer, and 2 mL of the suspension was seeded into each well of a 6-well plate to achieve a density of 500 cells per well. Cells were cultured in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2, and the medium was refreshed every 3 days while monitoring cell status. After 10 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 1 mL of crystal violet solution for 20 min, and imaged. Clones containing ≥50 cells were counted under a microscope.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected from each experimental group and fixed by adding 500 μL of 70% ice-cold ethanol per sample, followed by incubation at 4°C. Samples were then stained with 500 μL of a working solution containing RNase A and propidium iodide (PI) at a 1:9 ratio. Staining was performed in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Fluorescence emission at 488 nm excitation wavelength was measured using flow cytometry.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Tissue samples were stained with hematoxylin at room temperature for 2 min, rinsed in water for 5 min, and counterstained with eosin. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and mounted with glycerol. Images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Human skin specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Tissues were sectioned, deparaffinized, and rehydrated before antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated with anti-human SPRED1 primary antibody, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. The chromogenic substrate 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used to generate insoluble brown precipitates, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Target antigen distribution and expression in tissues were visualized under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 2 days, and paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 7-μm slices. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using a citric acid-sodium citrate buffer. After blocking with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37°C for 1 h, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies [FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Boster, China) or CY3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Boster, China)] were applied at a 1:500 dilution in PBS, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. Samples were washed and counterstained with diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at room temperature for 20 min. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

NHEK cells were transiently transfected with 2.5 μg of pLVX-Flag-SPRED1 plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and incubated on ice for 30 min. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 5 min, and supernatants were incubated with Anti-FLAG M2 Affinity beads overnight at 4°C. Beads were washed three times with IP lysis buffer to remove non-specific binding. Bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting using standard protocols.

Luciferase reporter assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to verify that SPRED1 is a direct target of miR-126. Recombinant reporter vectors (SPRED1-miR-126-WT and SPRED1-miR-126-MUT), along with miR-126-mimic and miR-126-mimic-NC, were obtained from the Public Protein/Plasmid Library (PPL, Nanjing, China) and co-transfected into NHEK cells, respectively. At 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 17.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0, and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data with equal variances were analyzed using standard parametric tests: the independent samples t test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Normally distributed data with unequal variances were analyzed using corrected parametric tests: Welch’s t test was used for two-group comparisons, and Brown-Forsythe corrected ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. For multiple pairwise multiple comparisons (e.g., multiple genes or cell cycle phases), one-way ANOVA was used to compare the differences and Bonferroni correction was applied to control Type I error inflation. SPRED1 and miR-126–3p expression was determined in 10 clinical tissue samples. HaCaT and NHEK cells were used for cellular assays (N = 6). Technical triplicates were used for all experiments. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
