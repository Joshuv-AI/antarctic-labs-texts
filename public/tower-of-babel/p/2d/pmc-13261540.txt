Biovigilance in Faecal Microbiota Transplantation: 7‐Year Cohort Study and Framework for Microbiological Assessments of Infectious Adverse Events
Authors: V O Chernova, J van Prehn, B Groenewegen, E van Lingen, E J Kuijper, M M C Lambregts, E van Nood, G Choi, M D Hazenberg, J J Keller, E M Terveer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsFaecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is increasingly used. However, no systematic approach exists to assess infectious risks after FMT, leading to underreporting. We evaluated infectious complications at the Netherlands Donor Feces Bank (NDFB) and proposed a structured biovigilance approach aligned with the EU Regulation on Substances of Human Origin (SoHO).MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational cohort study of all patients receiving frozen donor faecal suspensions from the NDFB (May 2016–December 2023) for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI) or via an extended access programme (EAP) for non‐CDI indications. (Serious) adverse events ((S)AEs) were reported by physicians and recorded at 3 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. Serious adverse reactions (SARs) were defined as SAEs probably or definitely related to FMT.ResultsWe included 290 rCDI patients (322 FMTs) and 35 EAP patients (75 FMTs). FMT efficacy was favourable overall: 92% of rCDI patients remained free of rCDI within 8 weeks, and 49% of EAP patients achieved at least a partial response. Sixty‐one per cent of rCDI patients and 34% of EAP patients had mild gastrointestinal AEs. AE incidences were comparable across groups (rCDI: 5.1 vs. EAP: 4.4 per 100 patient–weeks). SAEs were more frequent in EAP patients (0.66 vs. 0.28 per patient), reflecting higher immunosuppression and comorbidity. FMT‐attributable SARs occurred after 6 of 322 FMTs (1.9%) in the rCDI group and after 3 of 75 FMTs (4.0%) in the EAP group. Two donor–derived Escherichia coli infections in EAP patients with predisposing conditions were confirmed, consistent with early donor–strain colonisation rather than a direct FMT effect. Most SAEs were unrelated.ConclusionsIn this 7‐year cohort, donor‐derived infections were rare but present, particularly in non‐CDI patients with substantial comorbidity, whereas overall safety remained favourable. A SoHO‐compliant biovigilance protocol incorporating microbiological investigation and donor/sample traceability is essential for the safe clinical use of FMT and faecal microbiota products.Keywords: biovigilance, Clostridioides difficile infection, extended access programme, faecal microbiota transplantation, infectious adverse events

Introduction

Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) transfers stool from a healthy donor to a recipient's intestinal tract to restore microbial composition and function [1, 2]. It is effective for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI) and shows promise for other disorders linked to microbiota perturbation [1, 2]. Although many FMT recipients have comorbidities, including immunosuppression, the procedure is generally safe [3, 4, 5, 6]. Rare but serious infectious adverse events have been reported [7, 8, 9, 10], including sepsis caused by multidrug‐resistant Escherichia coli (E. coli) [10]. In response to these concerns, several international guidelines and stool‐banking standards have been established over the past decade to harmonise donor screening and stool processing practices aimed at mitigating infectious risk [11, 12, 13].

As clinical use expands, regulatory frameworks continue to evolve. In Europe, new legislation now classifies FMT as SoHO, requiring robust biovigilance and administrative oversight, although practical protocols are still under development. In the United States, recent FDA approval of donor–derived faecal microbiota products (FMPs), such as Rebyota (live‐jslm) and Vowst (live‐brpk), marks a shift towards commercialised, more standardised formulations. Whilst these differ in composition and delivery from conventional FMT, they share the same underlying source: screened donor material. These developments highlight the need for harmonised biovigilance across the spectrum of FMT‐based interventions.

Currently, no structured approach exists for systematic investigation and reporting of infectious complications, limiting insight into associations between FMT preparation, patient‐specific factors, and disease aetiology, contributing to underreporting.

Here, we report over 7 years of prospective monitoring of adverse and serious adverse events (AEs/SAEs) following FMT with frozen faecal suspensions from a centralised stool bank [14]. Based on these data, we propose a structured biovigilance framework to support standardised investigation and reporting of infectious (S)AEs, in line with emerging EU SoHO requirements. This is relevant for both conventional and donor–derived faecal microbiota products.

Methods

Study Design and Cohorts

We conducted a 7‐year prospective observational cohort study (May 2016–December 2023) to assess AEs and SAEs following FMT in routine Dutch practice. Patients were treated with frozen faecal suspensions from the Netherlands Donor Feces Bank (NDFB). Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Leiden University Medical Centre (LUMC METC, P15.145).

Our primary objective was to assess FMT safety in two cohorts:

Recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI) cohort: patients receiving standard‐of‐care FMT for rCDI.

Recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI) cohort: patients receiving standard‐of‐care FMT for rCDI.

Extended access programme (EAP) cohort: patients receiving FMT for non‐CDI indications without approved alternatives.

Extended access programme (EAP) cohort: patients receiving FMT for non‐CDI indications without approved alternatives.

The secondary objective was to describe and formalise the NDFB's structured biovigilance protocol for the investigation of potential infectious complications.

FMT Indications and Procedures

For FMT, frozen screened donor suspensions (60 g faeces, final volume including 10% glycerol, stored at −80°C) were administered to the duodenum (via gastroscope or nasoduodenal tube) or colon (via colonoscopy) [14]. Capsule formulations were not included. Indications were evaluated by the NDFB multidisciplinary team, including at least one infectious disease specialist, gastroenterologist, and medical microbiologist.

Donor material was obtained from healthy volunteers screened through the standardised NDFB protocol, including questionnaire‐ and interview‐based risk assessment, comprehensive stool testing for enteric pathogens and multidrug‐resistant organisms, and serological testing for major blood‐borne infections. Only donors with repeatedly negative results and cleared after quarantine‐based batch release were approved (see Supporting Information S1, Methods S1 [14]).

rCDI patients followed a standardised protocol [14, 15] and received vancomycin or fidaxomicin pretreatment for at least 4 days, which was stopped at least 24 h before FMT. Bowel lavage was performed the day prior to FMT using a macrogol‐based solution: (2 L of Kleanprep or 1 L of Moviprep) for upper GI administration or conventional bowel lavage for colonoscopy.

EAP eligibility was determined on a case‐by‐case basis, in consultation with an independent disease‐specific expert, based on (1) absence of approved therapeutic alternatives, (2) a plausible pathophysiological role of the microbiota, and (3) published evidence suggesting potential FMT benefit. No formal inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied, resulting in heterogeneity in immune status and underlying disease. Individualised treatment plans were proposed by the NDFB multidisciplinary team. These included the number of FMTs (usually one or three from the same donor), pretreatment, and follow‐up strategy. Most patients received vancomycin pretreatment, except those with therapy‐refractory graft‐versus‐host disease (GvHD). In the case of multiple FMTs, any bowel lavage and antibiotic pretreatment were only given before the first FMT.

Safety Monitoring and Data Collection

Treating physicians were requested to inform the NDFB of any (S)AEs occurring within 30 days post‐FMT. High‐impact SAEs (e.g., bacteraemia) triggered additional active follow‐up by the NDFB. Standardised follow‐up questionnaires were performed at 3 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months post‐FMT, using patient‐ or physician‐completed questionnaires and/or medical record review (Supporting Information S1: Methods S2). Immunocompromised status was classified a priori as severely immunocompromised (neutropenia, ≤ 100 days after allogeneic stem cell transplantation, or active graft‐versus‐host disease requiring immunosuppressive therapy), moderately immunocompromised (CD4+ T‐cell count < 200 cells/μL or chronic corticosteroid use ≥ 0.3 mg/kg/day prednisone equivalent; treatment with other recognised T‐cell immunosuppressants during the last 90 days or having an inherited severe immunodeficiency), or not immunocompromised [15]. For EAP patients, treating physicians were contacted via email to provide regular updates on disease‐related outcomes. Data were collected using standardised forms and stored in the Castor EDC database (Amsterdam, Netherlands).

Biovigilance

The description of adverse events follows pharmacovigilance conventions to capture the full spectrum of side effects after FMT, not limited to those likely related to the procedure. As FMT will be regulated as a SoHO under EU legislation, reporting of adverse outcomes should adhere to the principles of biovigilance as outlined in the EDQM Guide to the Quality and Safety of Human Cells and Tissues for Human Application (fifth edition) [16]. According to these principles, SARs were noted in addition to SAEs.

Infectious (S)AEs were assessed for early timing, severity, and a plausible link to FMT. Only severe or clinically suspicious events within 21 days post‐FMT and involving pathogens plausibly originating from donor material or the gastrointestinal tract were investigated. Events with a low likelihood of donor origin (e.g., late‐onset urinary tract infections) were not pursued.

Definitions and Statistical Analysis

CDI relapse was defined as microbiologically confirmed CDI within 8 weeks post‐FMT; in accordance with ESCMID guidelines [17]. Late recurrence was defined as CDI > 8 weeks after FMT. CDI relapse was classified as an SAE when it fulfiled the ESCMID criteria for severe or severe‐complicated CDI. Non‐severe relapses within 8 weeks after FMT were classified as AEs.

AE and SAEs were defined and graded according to the Common Terminology for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0 [18, 19]. Events were classified by type (infectious, gastrointestinal, other), route (procedure or FMT‐related), and causality (not, possibly, probably, definitely related). Causality was initially categorised based on temporal association:

–Probably related (< 7 days)

Probably related (< 7 days)

–Possibly related (≤ 90 days, unless there were significant alternative aetiologies or if the clinical presentation was unlikely to be microbiome‐related)

Possibly related (≤ 90 days, unless there were significant alternative aetiologies or if the clinical presentation was unlikely to be microbiome‐related)

–Not related (> 90 days, except certain SAEs with autoimmune aetiology or gastrointestinal presentations known to emerge later following FMT)

Not related (> 90 days, except certain SAEs with autoimmune aetiology or gastrointestinal presentations known to emerge later following FMT)

Final classification considered not only timing but also alternative explanations, baseline disease activity, prior similar events, and microbiological findings where available. Particular caution was applied to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) flares and urinary tract infection (UTI)–related SAEs, as these may occur independently of FMT in patients with active CDI, underlying IBD, recurrent UTIs, or severe comorbidity. Classification could be revised based on additional microbiological or donor–derived analyses. Mild to moderate gastrointestinal AEs within the first 3 weeks post‐FMT were generally classified as expected side effects, unless clinical features indicated a possible infectious or otherwise unexpected aetiology. SARs were defined as SAEs reasonably associated with FMT, thus SAEs classified as probably and definitely FMT‐related, in line with the EU Regulation on Substances of Human Origin (SoHO; Regulation (EU) 2022/1904), which refers to the biovigilance framework outlined by the EDQM in the Guide to the Quality and Safety of Tissues and Cells for Human Application (so‐called Tissue Guide) [16]. SARs were reported as the proportion of FMT procedures associated with such events.

Additional information on classification criteria is provided in the Supporting information S1: Methods S3.

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2). Continuous variables were reported as median [IQR], categorical as n (%). Rates per FMT were estimated via Poisson regression; 95% CIs are Poisson. Incidence rates (IRs) were calculated per 100 patient‐weeks with 95% Poisson confidence intervals. Rate ratios (RRs) were adjusted for age, sex, number of FMTs, and immunocompromised status. No untreated comparator group was included.

Results

Cohort Characteristics & Follow‐Up

A total of 322 FMTs were administered to 290 patients with rCDI, and 75 FMTs to 35 patients under the EAP for non‐CDI indications (Figure 2). rCDI patients were older (median 71 years [61–80]) and more often female (61.4%) compared with EAP patients (median 55 [36.5–65]; 60% male) (Table 1). EAP patients exhibited greater clinical heterogeneity, with higher rates of haematological disease (45.7%) and severe immunocompromise (40%), particularly graft‐versus‐host disease (GvHD; 43%) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). FMT was predominantly administered via the upper gastrointestinal route (rCDI: 90.7%, EAP: 97.1%). Whilst 91.7% of rCDI patients received a single FMT, 63% of EAP patients underwent 2–4 FMTs.

The median follow‐up duration was longer in the rCDI cohort (24 weeks [8–34]) than in the EAP cohort (8 weeks [4–18.5]). Follow‐up completeness at 3 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months was 93.4%, 63.4%, 40.7% for rCDI, and 94.3%, 42.9%,17.1% for EAP.

Side Effects and Adverse Events

Amongst rCDI patients, 455 AEs and 82 SAEs were reported (mean AE rate 1.57 per patient; 1.45 per FMT). In the EAP cohort, 22 AEs and 23 SAEs were observed (mean AE rate 0.63 per patient; 0.29 per FMT). The mean infectious AE rate per FMT was higher in rCDI 0.14 [0.10–0.19] compared with EAP 0.07 [0.03–0.16]. The AE incidence per 100 patient‐weeks was comparable between groups: 5.12 [4.66–5.62] in rCDI patients and 4.42 [2.77–6.69] in EAP patients (Table 2).

Serious Adverse Events, Serious Infectious Adverse Events, and Reactions

SAEs were more frequent in EAP patients (0.66 SAEs per patient [0.42–0.99]) compared with rCDI (0.28 SAEs per patient [0.22–0.35]) and were often more severe based on CTCAE grading (Figure 3; Supporting Information S1: Tables S2–3). SAE incidence was substantially higher in the EAP cohort (4.62 [2.93–6.93]) compared with rCDI (0.92 [0.73–1.15]) (Table 2). Infectious SAEs occurred in 10 of 35 EAP patients (29%) and 31 of 290 rCDI patients (11%), with bacteraemia more common in EAP (14% vs. 3%). All bacteraemia cases in EAP occurred in GvHD patients.

Most SAEs were deemed unrelated to FMT by the FMT‐expert team (72% in rCDI and 52.1% in EAP). SARs, defined as SAEs classified as probably or definitely related to FMT, occurred after 6 of 322 FMT procedures (1.9%) in the rCDI cohort and after 3 of 75 FMT procedures (4.0%) in the EAP cohort (Figure 4, Supporting Information S1: Table S2–3). FMT efficacy was high in the rCDI cohort and favourable in the EAP cohort: 92% (267/290) of rCDI patients remained free of recurrent CDI within 8 weeks after FMT, whilst 49% (17/35) of EAP patients achieved at least partial clinical improvement. In total, 15 suspected infectious (S)AE underwent detailed microbiological investigation, representing 15/45 (33%) of all infectious events that met predefined criteria for donor–derived investigation. This included 2/32 (6%) events in the rCDI cohort and all 13 infectious (S)AEs in the EAP cohort. Of these, only two cases in the EAP cohort were confirmed as definitely donor–derived: 1 E. coli UTI and 1 E. coli bacteraemia, both confirmed by whole genome sequencing (WGS) (Table 3). Importantly, these isolates were not multidrug‐resistant organisms (MDROs).

In the first case, a patient who received FMT for recurrent multidrug‐resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae UTIs developed E. coli pyelonephritis 30 days post‐FMT [20]. WGS confirmed donor origin. The infection was successfully treated with oral antibiotics. In the second case, an EAP patient with GvHD developed E. coli bacteraemia 9 days after the first of three FMTs. Despite the SAE, the patient improved clinically and completed the FMT treatment, ultimately achieving overall clinical improvement. WGS confirmed donor–derived transmission.

Three additional E. coli infections (bacteraemia, sepsis, and pneumosepsis) following FMT were investigated but showed distinct resistance profiles, indicating no donor link. Other suspected pathogens that were identified in patients (e.g., Streptococcus equinus/gallolyticus/infantarius/lutetiensis, Campylobacter jejuni, sapovirus, and Yersinia enterocolitica) were not detected in the corresponding donor samples used for FMT.

In total, two patients at separate centres received FMT prepared from the same donor. Both developed similar transient gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhoea and vomiting) within 48 h post‐FMT. However, no causative pathogen (e.g., Salmonella ssp., Shigella spp., Yersinia spp., Campylobacter spp., Aeromonas, Plesiomonas, Shiga toxin–producing E. coli, Clostridioides difficile, Bacillus cereus, Listeria monocytogenes, C. perfringens, Cryptosporidium, Giardia lamblia, Entamoeba hystolytica, Microsporidium spp., norovirus, astrovirus, adenovirus type 40/41, sapovirus) was detected in either donor QC samples or recipient clinical samples. Causality was therefore reclassified as not related.

Another patient experienced a gastrointestinal SAE characterised by diarrhoea with dehydration one day after FMT. Microbiological investigations of both the patient and the donor failed to identify any causative microbiological agents.

Discussion

Although infrequently encountered, infectious complications after FMT should be considered a potential risk and require continuous monitoring. To ensure the safety of stool bank‐facilitated FMT, and to identify risks associated with FMT, biovigilance (i.e., structured investigation and reporting of adverse events and reactions after transplantation) is required. In this 7‐year prospective real‐world cohort, FMT was generally safe for rCDI, with mostly mild and transient gastrointestinal adverse events. In contrast, patients in the extended access programme (EAP) had a higher risk for serious adverse events (SAEs) and serious adverse reactions (SARs). Patients with rCDI also have an increased baseline risk of adverse events due to older age, frailty, hospitalisation, repeated antibiotic exposure, and multiple comorbidities. Therefore, the observed SAE rates in the rCDI cohort should not be interpreted as entirely attributable to FMT. This is supported by the finding that most SAEs in the rCDI cohort were judged unrelated to FMT, whereas only 1.9% of FMT procedures were followed by a probable or definite SAR. Of 15 suspected infectious events investigated in detail, only two were confirmed as donor–derived, underscoring both the rarity of donor transmission and the value of systematic microbiological investigation. These findings highlight the need for biovigilance protocols to safeguard patient safety, aligned with emerging EU regulatory frameworks for SoHO. Notably, both confirmed donor–derived infections occurred despite rigorous donor screening and selection, indicating the need for robust post‐procedural surveillance. In both cases, underlying predisposing conditions likely increased susceptibility, with the infections reflecting early donor–strain colonisation rather than a procedure–related complication.

Systematic registration, investigation, and management of infectious complications post‐FMT are critical to optimise safety and outcomes [21, 22, 23]. Biovigilance protocols and outcome registration are also required under the new SoHO regulation, which directly applies to FMT. However, despite expanding global experience, structured biovigilance has not yet been widely implemented, possibly contributing to the underreporting of infectious complications. Our study incorporated prospective, standardised AE reporting tools, predefined follow‐up intervals, and targeted microbiological investigation of suspected infectious events. This approach establishes the safety of FMT in CDI but also identified two microbiologically confirmed, donor‐derived bacterial infections: one UTI and one bacteraemia. Notably, the patient with donor–derived bacteraemia after the first of three planned FMTs for GvHD showed clinical improvement and underwent further FMT, illustrating the need for nuanced clinical decision‐making when balancing infectious risk with potential therapeutic benefit in immunocompromised populations.

Although pathogen transmission through FMT is rare when rigorous donor screening is applied, infectious SAEs were more frequent in non‐CDI indications, particularly in haematological conditions such as GvHD. This likely reflects multifactorial risk, where impaired mucosal barrier function due to the GvHD and immunosuppression contribute to increased susceptibility to bacterial translocation, as previously reported [4, 24]. Neutropenia may also increase susceptibility to bacterial translocation [9]. Post‐FMT bacteraemia therefore appears to have a multifactorial aetiology, with host factors playing a greater role than donor–derived pathogens. Bacteraemia is common in GvHD patients even without FMT, and our observed cases may partly reflect the natural disease course. However, the short interval between FMT and onset of bacteraemia suggests a possible causal link, even where the causative strain was not donor derived. Introduction of donor microbiota into inflamed mucosa may exacerbate existing inflammation and promote bacterial translocation, as also reported in IBD patients undergoing FMT [7, 25, 26, 27]. Interestingly, Ghani et al. observed reduced infection risk in stem cell transplant recipients receiving FMT in the absence of active GvHD, underscoring the interplay between host immunity, gut integrity, and FMT [28]. These observations suggest that barrier–stabilising interventions may warrant further investigation prior to FMT in high‐risk groups. Potential approaches include anti‐inflammatory therapy with corticosteroids [29], as well as antibiotic pretreatment [30] and bowel lavage [31], aimed at reducing pro‐inflammatory bacterial burden. Similar considerations may apply to other high‐risk groups, including patients with advanced cirrhosis, where severe sepsis after FMT has been described [10], although other data suggest infection‐related complications may decrease post‐FMT [32].

Interpretation of causality remained particularly challenging for IBD flares and UTI‐related SAEs. Although these events were classified as probably related because of their close temporal association with FMT, they may also reflect the underlying disease course, particularly in patients with active CDI, pre‐existing IBD, recurrent UTIs, or severe immunocompromise. The effect of FMT on IBD activity remains uncertain, with both beneficial and neutral effects reported in small studies [33, 34].

Our study leveraged structured AE follow‐up and standardised tools, but successful implementation depends on clinician engagement. Enhanced data infrastructure, including national or international registries for donor and patient follow‐up and biological sample storage, would facilitate improved biovigilance. Such registries already exist in North America [35] and are under development in Europe [36]. These initiatives align with the new EU SoHO regulation, which emphasises biovigilance and standardised outcome reporting for medical use of human‐derived substances [36, 37, 38]. Importantly, this regulation mandates the use of EDQM terminology: events with a serious outcome and plausible causal link to donor material are classified as serious adverse reactions (SARs) [12]. In our study, SARs were operationally defined as SAEs probably or definitely related to FMT. Harmonisation and adoption of this nomenclature are essential for future publications on FMT safety. It should also be noted that in EDQM terminology, ‘adverse events’ refer to deviations in donor material processing, whereas ‘adverse reactions’ describe harm in donors or recipients. Our study was limited to patient safety and did not include events related to procurement, processing, or storage of donor faecal material. When interpreted alongside the high efficacy of FMT, the observed SAR frequency supports a favourable overall benefit–risk ratio. In the rCDI cohort, 92% of patients remained free of recurrent CDI within 8 weeks, whilst only a small proportion of FMT procedures (1.9%) were followed by a probable or definite SAR. Similarly, although EAP patients experienced more SAEs, likely reflecting their greater comorbidity and immunosuppression, approximately half achieved at least partial clinical improvement. Limitations include the observational, descriptive design without a control group, loss to follow‐up, and heterogeneity of the EAP cohort, which may limit generalisability. Follow‐up duration also differed between the groups. Early post‐FMT adverse events were assessed using patient‐reported questionnaires at 3 weeks, which may have been subject to recall bias and underreporting of mild or transient events. Nonetheless, the study's key strengths include systematic, real‐world documentation of SAEs and SARs, microbiological confirmation, and an explicit focus on biovigilance and donor safety. This study focused particularly on infectious complications, where microbiological investigation of stored donor and patient material proved essential for causal attribution, as in the two WGS‐confirmed donor–derived cases. Although rare, these events highlight the importance of integrating microbiological testing into safety protocols. Our biovigilance workflow, featuring donor suspension and batch tracking, enabled rapid containment and investigation. Increased awareness, structured data collection, and systematic microbiological investigations will improve the recognition, understanding, and management of infectious events following FMT.

Conclusion

Although FMT is generally safe, caution is required in severely immunocompromised patients, particularly those with GvHD, because of an increased risk of infectious complications. Systematic AE registration in large patient cohorts will eventually enable differentiation between the expected side effects of FMT and true adverse reactions. Biobanking of both donor and patient samples, microbiological investigation, and traceable donor management are essential. International collaboration through registries and the implementation of structured biovigilance frameworks will be key for safe clinical and research use of FMT and microbiota‐based products. Future reporting should align with the SoHO framework, in which adverse reactions with a probable or definite causal link to donor material are classified as SARs. Further research should focus on strategies to reinforce mucosal barrier integrity and customise FMT protocols for high‐risk populations. These efforts will ultimately contribute to the development of safer and more effective microbiota‐based therapies.
