Intergenerational Knowledge Exchange in Japanese Biosphere Reserves: Community Perceptions from Aya and Mount Hakusan
Authors: Aida Mammadova, Bernadette Ekua Bedua Afful, Racheal Fosu Donkoh, Saku Monden, Seira Harada, Denise Margaret Matias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores community perception of how traditional knowledge is transmitted across generations within two Japanese UNESCO Biosphere Reserves (BRs): Aya and Mount Hakusan. Using a mixed-methods approach combining surveys and interviews with elders, youth, community leaders, and local entrepreneurs, the research examines how respondents perceive intergenerational exchange as a support for sustainable practices and ecotourism-based livelihoods. Across both sites, traditional ecological knowledge is widely recognized by participants as essential for cultural continuity and community resilience, and younger generations view elders as key knowledge holders. However, reported differences in social and governance contexts shape how knowledge is perceived to be shared: respondents indicate that Aya’s cohesive, single-town BR facilitates regular, structured exchanges, while the multi-municipality structure of Mount Hakusan creates challenges for coordination and youth engagement. Overall, the findings suggest that fostering effective intergenerational learning strengthens conservation values, supports sustainable entrepreneurship, and fosters cultural bridging within and beyond local communities. The study highlights the importance of policies that cultivate shared learning spaces, mentorship networks, and community-based education to sustain traditional knowledge in rural regions.

Introduction

Intangible cultural heritage, encompassing the knowledge, practices, and beliefs that people inherit from previous generations, is increasingly under pressure in our rapidly changing world. UNESCO Biosphere Reserves (BRs) have a unique mandate to safeguard this heritage alongside ecological conservation and sustainable development goals (Batisse 1982). BRs are designated areas under UNESCO’s Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Program that balance biodiversity protection with the needs of local communities. They are often described as “living laboratories” where they test innovative approaches to sustainable living (UNESCO, 2017). Practically, this means BRs conserve critical habitats and species while simultaneously fostering education, research, and sustainable economic opportunities for local people (Tanaka and Wakamatsu 2018). Local communities are seen as stewards and beneficiaries; by involving residents, who often hold generations of wisdom, BRs aim to enhance both environmental outcomes and human well-being (Berkes 1999; Mammadova et al. 2022). In this paper, traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) is defined as the cumulative body of knowledge, practice, and belief evolving through adaptive processes and passed across generations regarding the relationship between people and their environment (Berkes 1999).

Elders serve as living libraries of practical wisdom, from farming and foraging to rituals that embed conservation ethics (Pretty 1995; Chambers and Reid 2009). If passed effectively to younger generations, this knowledge strengthens community identity and guides sustainable resource use. Our conceptual framework (Fig. 1), informed by social learning theory (Bandura 1977) and concepts of cultural capital and community resilience (Berkes and Ross 2013), posits that such exchange drives innovation in sustainable entrepreneurship. Youth learning from elders may find inspiration for ecotourism, local crafts, or organic farms leveraging traditional know-how (Berkes and Ross 2013). These businesses provide economic incentives to maintain traditional practices, completing a positive feedback loop reinforcing BR goals. Social scientists have observed that communities with strong intergenerational bonds tend to be more resilient and innovative while preserving identity (Berkes and Ross 2013). While studies show elders teaching youths can lead to the revival of traditional crafts or improved agricultural outcomes (Honey 2008; Fennell 2013), challenges persist: modernization can reduce interest in old ways, and commercialization risks commodifying sacred traditions (Wearing and Neil 2009; Cole 2007). Understanding how this sharing unfolds and contributes to sustainable ventures is a valuable empirical endeavor.Fig. 1Conceptual framework linking intergenerational knowledge exchange to sustainable entrepreneurship outcomes in Biosphere Reserve (BR) communities

Research Gap and Rationale

Previous research has established the importance of traditional knowledge for sustainable community development, but less is understood about the processes and outcomes of intergenerational knowledge transmission under different governance and social conditions. In particular, there is a need for studies that investigate how variations in local governance structures (e.g., a single community vs. multiple communities) and demographic contexts affect the sharing of traditional knowledge and its translation into sustainable entrepreneurship. To address this gap, we undertook a comparative case study in Japan, a country with a strong cultural emphasis on heritage that faces acute rural socio-economic challenges (e.g., ageing populations, youth out-migration). Japan currently has ten BRs, all characterized by rich biodiversity and unique cultural landscapes. We focus on two notable examples: Aya Biosphere Reserve in Miyazaki Prefecture (southern Japan) and the Mount Hakusan Biosphere Reserve in central Japan. These two sites were selected for comparison because they share a reputation for active community engagement and biocultural heritage yet differ markedly in their scale, governance, and social-ecological contexts (Mammadova et al. 2021; Tanaka & Wakamatsu, 2018). By comparing a small, single-municipality BR (Aya Town, population 7200, managing the entire Aya BR) with a large, multi-municipality BR (Mount Hakusan spans 199,300 ha across seven municipalities, 17,000 residents, managed by a multi-stakeholder council), we aim to examine how different governance structures and community scales influence intergenerational knowledge exchange and sustainable entrepreneurship. This comparative design allows us to explore a question that a single-case study could not: How do contextual differences shape the ways traditional knowledge is transmitted across generations and leveraged for sustainable development?

In summary, Aya and Hakusan BRs provide two contrasting yet comparable scenarios to examine our research question: How do community members in Aya and Mount Hakusan Biosphere Reserves perceive intergenerational traditional knowledge exchange and its contribution to sustainable entrepreneurship?

By comparing the two BRs, we aim to identify general principles and context-specific factors on how traditional knowledge is mobilized for sustainable development. This study contributes to academic understanding of biocultural sustainability, social learning, and community-based environmental management, and offers practical insights for BR managers and policymakers. In doing so, we aim to clearly position our work within and beyond the existing literature on traditional ecological knowledge in environmental governance, demonstrating how our findings advance understanding of the role of intergenerational knowledge exchange in fostering resilience and innovation in rural communities.

Methodology

Our fieldwork was carried out in the Aya and Mount Hakusan Biosphere Reserves in Japan. Given the comparative nature of the study, we ensured that data collection in both sites covered similar themes and population segments. Aya Biosphere Reserve (Miyazaki Prefecture, Kyushu) is the smallest BR in Japan (total area 14,580 ha; designated in 2012 as the country’s 5th BR). It encompasses a single municipality, Aya Town (population 7200), and is managed entirely by the town’s local government (Fig. 2). Aya is known for Japan’s largest remaining lucidophyllous (evergreen broadleaf) forest and has a long history of grassroots environmentalism. Since the 1980s, the community has championed organic agriculture and community-based forestry guided by elder farmers using traditional methods. Aya is also known for its crafts (woodworking, weaving) and annual harvest festivals celebrating agricultural cycles. These practices form Aya’s rich intangible heritage and have been leveraged for sustainable tourism (e.g., a forest canopy suspension bridge built in 1984, organic wine and shōchū production). By integrating conservation with small-scale enterprise, Aya experienced a tourism boom (peaking at nearly one million visitors per year in the late 1980s). Aya’s governance model—local authorities and residents working hand-in-hand - fosters regular, structured intergenerational exchange of knowledge.Fig. 2Map of Aya Biosphere Reserve (single municipality of Aya town), with three zonation. In contrast, Mount Hakusan Biosphere Reserve (Ishikawa, Fukui, Gifu, and Toyama Prefectures in central Japan) is one of the largest BRs in the country (199,329 ha; designated in 1980 and expanded in 2016 to include human communities) (Fig. 3). It spans seven municipalities around the 2702 m Mount Hakusan (Mount Haku), revered as one of Japan’s “Three Holy Mountains.” Approximately 17,000 people live in the BR’s buffer and transition zones. For over 1300 years, the region has been a site of mountain worship (Hakusan Shinkō), with pilgrimage routes and numerous shrines (e.g., Shirayama Hime Shrine) reflecting its spiritual significance. Residents in the mountain villages historically developed extensive traditional knowledge in areas such as forestry, washi paper-making, charcoal production, and unique adaptations to heavy winter snowfall. Mount Hakusan BR’s governance is coordinated by a multi-municipality, multi-stakeholder council that brings together four prefectural governments, seven city/town/village governments, local communities, and academic institutions (Hakusan BR Council, 2017). This polycentric governance structure requires collaboration across administrative boundaries and stakeholder groups, which can affect how knowledge-sharing initiatives are implemented and whose voices influence management decisions.Fig. 3Area of Mount Hakusan Biosphere Reserve, with four prefectures and seven municipalities. We adopted a mixed-methods research design (Creswell and Clark 2007) to thoroughly investigate the research question from multiple angles. This approach allowed us to cross-validate and enrich our findings: we treated the quantitative component as exploratory and descriptive, providing broad patterns to complement qualitative findings, rather than as a basis for generalizable inference. We focused on descriptive statistics and integrated quantitative results with qualitative insights instead of emphasizing statistical test. In each site, we targeted two groups of participants: (1) general community members for a survey, and (2) key informants for in-depth interviews. For the survey, we used a purposive sampling approach facilitated by local contacts, aiming to include a diverse range of community members. In Aya, survey questionnaires were distributed with the help of the Aya BR Promotion Office and community associations, seeking to include both men and women, representatives of different age groups (including youth and elders), and various occupations (e.g., farmers, craft artisans, local officials, business owners). In Mount Hakusan, we collaborated with the Hakusan BR Council and local non-profit organizations (e.g., in villages like Shiramine) to reach respondents under similar inclusion criteria. The final sample size of valid survey respondents was 20 in Aya and 23 in Hakusan. (Note: One Hakusan respondent did not answer all survey items, resulting in some measures being reported for n = 22 in Hakusan).

For the interviews, we selected key informants using snowball and purposive sampling to capture a mix of ages, genders, and roles. We conducted 38 semi-structured interviews (18 in Aya, 20 in Hakusan), continuing until reaching thematic saturation. Participants included elder knowledge holders (organic farmers, foresters, artisans, a priest-guide), youth innovators (farmers, ecotourism guides, business owners, club members), and middle-aged stakeholders (BR managers, officials, NGO representatives). Held in Japanese, the 30–60 min sessions were recorded with informed consent, transcribed, and translated into English. A consistent interview guide covered intergenerational interactions, knowledge transfer, local entrepreneurship, and integrating tradition with modern activities.

As Table 2 indicates, the Hakusan survey sample skews younger, including students and seasonal or returning residents. Conversely, Aya’s sample features more older, self-employed farmers and craftspeople, with no respondents identifying as students or retired. Given the exploratory nature of the study and logistical constraints, we used non-random sampling to prioritize participants with relevant local experience. We acknowledge these compositional differences and interpret the survey results as reflective of these specific participant cohorts rather than the wider BR populations.

Data collections and analysis

The structured questionnaire (administered in Japanese and analyzed in English) comprised four sections: demographic information (Table 2); intergenerational knowledge exchange (eight 5-point Likert items); sustainable entrepreneurship opportunities (six Likert items); and integration of traditional knowledge with modern practices (six Likert items), supplemented by open-ended questions. Surveys were administered anonymously—in person in Aya, and via local collaborators and student assistants in Hakusan. Given the modest, non-random sample sizes, data are treated as exploratory and descriptive. Results are presented as frequencies and percentages without formal statistical testing or population generalization.

Qualitative data from interviews and open-ended survey responses underwent thematic analysis using a hybrid deductive-inductive coding strategy (Braun and Clarke 2006). We initialized the process with deductive codes based on our research questions (e.g., “knowledge transmission,” “youth engagement”) before allowing inductive sub-themes to emerge. To ensure intercoder consistency, two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts to refine the coding scheme collaboratively. We used a two-step coding process: assigning raw quotes to initial semantic codes (e.g., coding “The young ones leave…” as “Youth out-migration threatening continuity”) and subsequently grouping those codes into broader formal thematic categories (e.g., “Challenges in sustaining intergenerational knowledge”). Data were managed in a spreadsheet detailing themes, sub-themes, exemplary quotes, and participant attributes to evaluate generation- and site-specific variations.

We used triangulation between quantitative trends and qualitative narratives to enhance validity, using the qualitative insights to add depth to survey patterns. Given the dual-case design and non-random sampling, the study aims for analytical rather than statistical generalization (Yin 2014), providing a nuanced, exploratory understanding of the contexts rather than testing formal hypotheses.

Researcher positionality and study integrity

The research team comprised both local Japanese and international researchers, and we were mindful of our positionality as (in part) outsiders to the communities. To build trust and avoid extractive practices, we closely collaborated with local partners at every stage of the study. The Aya BR Promotion Office and the Mount Hakusan BR Council were involved in facilitating introductions and supporting logistics, which helped demonstrate our commitment to the communities’ own interests. All participants were informed about the purpose of the research and gave their voluntary, informed consent to participate. Interviews were conducted in the local language (Japanese) by bilingual team members, and all interview recordings were kept confidential and later transcribed and translated by a native speaker to ensure accuracy. To enhance validity and respect for local perspectives, we carried out informal member-checking: for instance, we shared preliminary findings and interpretations with a few key community members and BR officials to solicit their feedback and ensure our analyses resonated with their experiences. We plan to provide the final results and recommendations to the communities and BR management as a form of knowledge sharing and to support local initiatives. By engaging local collaborators, protecting anonymity, and remaining reflexive about our role, we aimed to minimize bias and align the research with community values.

Results

In this section, we present the key findings from the surveys and interviews, structured around the main themes of interest: (1) community perceptions of intergenerational knowledge exchange, (2) reported sustainable entrepreneurship and ecotourism initiatives, and (3) participant attitudes toward integrating traditional knowledge with modern practices (including synergies and tensions). We integrate quantitative and qualitative results for each theme to provide a cohesive representation of community viewpoints, highlighting both similarities and differences between the Aya and Mount Hakusan BRs.

Intergenerational knowledge exchange in community life

Both quantitative and qualitative data indicate that community members perceive intergenerational transmission of knowledge as active and meaningful, though the forms it is reported vary between Aya and Hakusan. Survey results indicate strong community awareness of the importance of elders in knowledge transmission. In Aya, 11 of 20 respondents (55%) and in Hakusan 14 of 23 (≈61%) agreed with statements indicating that elders play an active role in teaching younger people about local natural resources and traditions (combining related statements; see Table 3). An even larger share—16 of 20 (80%) in Aya and 18 of 23 (78%) in Hakusan – affirmed that community gatherings (festivals, group activities) are perceived to provide crucial spaces for intergenerational learning. Notably, more participants in Hakusan reported that they personally have learned sustainable practices by observing or working with elders (15 of 23, ~65%) compared to Aya (9 of 20, 45%). This descriptive difference suggests that Hakusan’s residents may rely more on family and village traditions for learning. Conversely, in Aya, respondents’ accounts indicate that some knowledge transmission has been systematically incorporated into formal programs, which participants suggest may reduce the necessity for individual, unstructured observation of elders. Nonetheless, a substantial proportion of Aya respondents also acknowledged the influence of elder knowledge, indicating that elders’ wisdom remains highly valued in both communities.

The interviews help explain these numbers and illustrate how knowledge transfer occurs. In Aya, participants described intergenerational exchange as frequently institutionalized. For example, local schools partner with senior residents: “We pair retirees with school kids for nature observation events,” explained an Aya town official, highlighting a program where elders teach students to identify trees and forest plants. Elders in Aya also lead workshops at community centers (e.g., teaching organic farming or traditional crafts). These structured activities mean that even young people who are not from farming families still gain exposure to traditional ecological knowledge. One Aya youth described learning charcoal-making from an elder in such a workshop and now volunteers annually to practice that skill, saying it made her realise “it’s a legacy we should keep.” By contrast interviewees from Mount Hakusan characterized transmission as relying more heavily on informal, community-embedded pathways. In the small villages around Mt. Hakusan, youths often absorb knowledge during festivals, communal farming, or family work. For instance, a young guide in Hakusan recounted how accompanying an older mountain guide on pilgrimages taught him local legends and plant lore beyond what any formal training could offer: “I learned so many things just walking with him and listening to his stories about every stream and shrine,” he said. Similarly, elders take pride in involving youth in cultural rituals; a senior shrine priest noted that after teenagers joined an annual mountain worship ceremony, “they became curious about the meanings and started asking us elders to explain our chants and dances,” thus sparking a transfer of cultural knowledge.

Despite generally positive exchange, both communities face challenges in sustaining intergenerational exchange. In Hakusan, the dispersion of villages and youth out-migration mean fewer young people are present to learn; several Hakusan elders expressed concern that “the young ones leave, so who will carry tradition?” In Aya, which has a smaller but more cohesive population, the main challenge is keeping youth interested amid modern education and entertainment options. Older residents observed that informal storytelling or apprenticeships occur less frequently as lifestyles change and young people spend more time in school or jobs outside traditional sectors. Both BRs have recognised these issues and responded by creating more opportunities for elder–youth interaction. For example, Hakusan’s BR Council in recent years has organized multi-village heritage workshops where elders demonstrate traditional skills (like paper-making or ceremonial dances) to visiting students from across the region. Aya has instituted annual “learning festivals” that celebrate local heritage and explicitly pair younger community members with older mentors in activities like planting ceremonies and craft making. These efforts are viewed as essential to keep the chain of knowledge transmission intact. Overall, intergenerational knowledge exchange remains a strong asset in each community, providing a cultural foundation for local sustainability initiatives.

In summary, the narratives from both Aya and Hakusan BRs demonstrate that residents view the presence of knowledgeable elders as an asset for education and mentorship. From the perspective of the participants, these pathways help ensure that traditional practices—ranging from organic farming techniques to folklore and ritual—are perceived as shared rather than lost between generations. This perceived continuity is framed by respondents as central to community identity and resilience.

Sustainable Entrepreneurship and Ecotourism Initiatives

The second major theme explores how community members perceive the extent and nature of sustainable entrepreneurship, particularly ventures related to ecotourism or local products, and how these enterprises are understood to relate to traditional knowledge. Survey responses show optimism about sustainable business opportunities in both BRs, coupled with recognition of some gaps in youth involvement. Around 16 of 20 respondents in Aya (80%) and 18 of 23 in Hakusan (~78%) agreed that there are good opportunities for locals to start ecotourism or nature-based businesses. An even larger proportion—14 of 20 in Aya (70%) and 21 of 23 in Hakusan (~91%)—felt that such “green” enterprises help preserve the environment by linking livelihoods with conservation incentives. However, relatively few perceived strong interest from local youth in actually pursuing these ventures (only 8 of 20, 40%, in Aya and 7 of 23, ~30%, in Hakusan believed that many local young people are interested in starting eco-friendly businesses). Likewise, roughly one-third or less in each site (7 in Aya, 35%; 8 in Hakusan, ~35%) agreed that sufficient training or financial support exists for would-be sustainable entrepreneurs. These findings (see Table 4) suggest a positive attitude toward the idea of sustainable entrepreneurship, tempered by the reality that only a minority of youth are actively engaged or perceive adequate support.

On the ground, both BRs host a variety of small-scale sustainable enterprises that tie into their natural and cultural heritage. In Aya, several community members have developed businesses that grew organically from traditional practices. For example, local farmers, guided by elder expertise in organic methods, have created value-added products like organic wine, shōchū (a traditional distilled spirit), and herbal teas branded with Aya’s name. One young Aya entrepreneur who opened a farm-to-table café noted that his concept relies on his grandparents’ knowledge of Indigenous crops: “We use old varieties of fruits and vegetables that elders taught us to cultivate without chemicals—visitors appreciate the unique taste and story behind them,” he explained. Aya also capitalises on ecotourism; the town manages forest hiking trails and a suspension bridge canopy walk, which in turn support guided tour services and family-run guesthouses. Interviewees in Aya highlighted that many of these ventures are multigenerational efforts—for instance, a guesthouse owner in her 30 s runs the business side while her retired parents engage guests by teaching traditional crafts and cooking. This intertwining of generations ensures authenticity in the services offered, giving Aya a reputation for genuine cultural experiences.

In Mount Hakusan, sustainable entrepreneurship often takes the form of experiential tourism and niche crafts. The mountainous terrain and dispersed cultural sites lend themselves to guided tours, homestays, and workshops. Several villagers have become licensed nature or culture guides. One Hakusan interviewee, a 45-year-old guide, leads wildflower hikes in spring and snowshoe treks in winter, blending scientific facts with folk tales learned from an elder hunter. “The veteran hunter I apprenticed with knows the mountain like the back of his hand—that authenticity is what people pay for,” he said, illustrating how elders’ knowledge underpins the business. Hakusan communities have also started cottage industries like traditional charcoal-making and washi papermaking geared towards tourists and urban markets. In one case, a young entrepreneur revived a near-forgotten charcoal technique by apprenticing under an 80-year-old craftsman; now their high-quality product is sold to eco-conscious restaurants, and the elder’s skill has become a valued “brand secret.” Similarly, women’s collectives in Hakusan villages offer cooking classes for visitors, teaching recipes such as wild plant tempura and fermented foods that have been passed down locally. These classes not only generate income but also incentivise preserving those culinary traditions. The peer-inspiration effect is evident in both sites: about half of respondents in each BR (10 of 20 in Aya, 50%; 12 of 23 in Hakusan, 52%) agreed that seeing successful local eco-business owners motivates others in the community to consider launching similar ventures (Table 4).

One striking point is the very high consensus in Hakusan (21 of 23 respondents, 91%) on the positive environmental impact of entrepreneurship. In interviews, Hakusan participants elaborated that sustainable businesses (like eco-tours, local specialty products) are seen as a way to create jobs so that people don’t abandon villages, which in turn keeps traditional land use practices alive and prevents farmland from reverting to wild (which can cause more wildlife conflicts).

Despite such examples, participants maintained that significant challenges persist. Interviews in both sites noted that not all youth are inclined or able to pursue local entrepreneurship, often due to financial risk and limited know-how. A Hakusan youth who returned from the city to help with his family’s inn observed that while he had ideas for new tour packages, “I realised I need marketing and business skills – we’ve never done that here.” This lack of business training is reflected in the survey’s low perceived support for entrepreneurs (Table 4). In Aya, a few young adults have moved in from the outside (attracted by Aya’s green reputation) and started businesses, but they mentioned hurdles like obtaining loans for unconventional projects. Moreover, seasonal fluctuations in tourism can make income unstable; as one Aya artisan put it, “Our sales peak in holidays then drop. It’s hard for a young person to rely on this alone, so many treat it as secondary income.” Community leaders in both areas are trying to address these issues: Aya’s town office has begun offering small grants for youth-led organic enterprises, and Mount Hakusan’s BR Council has partnered with a regional university to host entrepreneurship seminars for locals (e.g., how to market crafts online).

Crucially, the role of traditional knowledge is central to the appeal and distinctiveness of these sustainable businesses. Entrepreneurs from both BRs emphasised that their “competitive advantage” is authenticity – something they derive from elders and local culture. For instance, a product like Aya’s organic miso or Hakusan’s hand-carved wooden utensils carries a story of place and heritage that mass-produced goods lack. This dynamic creates a virtuous cycle: valuing elder knowledge gives local products and services a unique market value, and commercial success in turn reinforces the community’s pride in that knowledge. As a young craft-maker in Hakusan summed up, “We sell crafts made from the stories our parents tell – that’s why customers find them special.” However, the communities are cautious to scale up in ways that might dilute this uniqueness. In both sites, the prevalent strategy is small-scale, high-quality production and personalised tourism rather than mass tourism development. This approach aligns with the BR philosophy and has community support, but it means economic impacts, while positive, remain modest in size. In summary, sustainable entrepreneurship in Aya and Hakusan is emerging organically, fueled by intergenerational cooperation: elders provide the wisdom and often the labor or training, while younger adults bring energy and sometimes new technology or business savviness. The initiatives - though still relatively few in number, illustrate a potential pathway for rural revitalization that complements environmental conservation. Looking ahead, increasing the breadth of youth participation (through improved support systems, mentorship, and exposure to role models) will be vital to scaling up these successes in a way that remains sustainable and community-driven.

Integrating Traditional Knowledge with Modern Practices: Synergies and Tensions

The final theme addresses how community members perceive the integration of traditional knowledge with modern life, particularly in the context of starting new businesses or engaging with the broader economy, and what challenges or opportunities arise from this integration. Both communities believe that blending tradition with modernity is not only feasible but beneficial, yet they also recognize challenges in doing so. Survey findings reveal that about two-thirds of respondents in each BR (13 of 20 in Aya, 65%; 14 of 22 in Hakusan, 64%) agreed that “traditional knowledge can be combined with modern business practices to create successful ecotourism” (Table 5). A similar proportion acknowledged that there are significant challenges in applying “old ways” in today’s economy (13 of 20, 65% in Aya; 13 of 22, 59% in Hakusan agreed with a statement about such challenges). In other words, residents are optimistic about integration but also clear-eyed about the potential difficulties. There is broad consensus that dialog and innovation are needed to make integration work: roughly three-quarters of each sample (15 of 20, 75% in Aya; 16 of 22, 73% in Hakusan) agreed that community discussions help bridge traditional values and new business ideas, and that shared learning experiences (e.g., training sessions, exchange visits) are helping people figure out how to adapt heritage to current needs. One survey item in particular yielded the largest difference between the two communities: in Aya, 17 of 20 respondents (85%) agreed that “indigenous/traditional wisdom combined with new technology can help both the environment and the economy,” whereas 13 of 22 (59%) agreed in Hakusan. This 85% vs. 59% gap was the greatest divergence observed on any single item. This might be because Aya, having a stable environment and smaller community, may be more comfortable or experienced with such integration. It should also be noted that the Hakusan survey sample contained a larger proportion of younger respondents than the Aya sample (Table 2). Differences in age composition may have influenced perceptions related to traditional knowledge, entrepreneurship, and technology use; therefore, some observed differences between sites should be interpreted with caution. Indeed, Aya has examples like applying organic certification (a modern process) to traditional farming, or using solar panels to power traditional workshops—things which elders in Aya discussed as positive in interviews. Hakusan’s comparatively lower agreement (59%) on the technology item could reflect a cautious attitude; some in Hakusan may be less certain about the role of modern technology or have had fewer opportunities to witness its benefits in a traditional context.

Interview narratives reveal how residents pragmatically integrate tradition and modernity, retaining core values while using modern tools to enhance them. For example, an Aya elder permitted his son to introduce a drone and smartphone app to monitor organic tea fields, while maintaining traditional planting calendars and manual harvests. The elder noted, “We still grow tea the way my father taught, but my son’s drone helps us know which area needs care. It’s the best of both worlds”.

Similarly, a Mount Hakusan youth uses social media and multilingual websites to attract tourists to traditional, elder-led workshops and seasonal festivals, stating, “modern marketing is just a tool—what we market is 100% our culture.” These examples demonstrate a clear synergy: modern platforms and technology amplify the reach and efficiency of traditional knowledge-based activities without displacing their cultural core.

A significant opportunity area is innovation in products as a way to connect old and new. In Aya, a small cooperative combined an elder’s knowledge of wild herbs with a younger member’s food processing skills to create a line of herbal health teas and jams for urban consumers. In Hakusan, local artisans have started to use traditional techniques to craft contemporary designs—for instance, using centuries-old indigo dye methods on modern fashion accessories, which sell well online. Such initiatives illustrate how tradition can evolve rather than remain static. One young artisan from Hakusan explained that innovation is approached carefully: “We change the surface look for modern taste, but we don’t change the process that gives it meaning. That process is our heritage.” This suggests a guiding principle in both communities: modernise the packaging, not the essence. It helps maintain authenticity, which is crucial for both pride and market differentiation.

Despite these concerns, most participants expressed a belief that these challenges can be negotiated through community dialog and adaptive management. The survey responses reflect this outlook: about 75% of respondents in both sites agreed that ongoing community discussions help bridge traditional values with modern opportunities, and that shared learning experiences (such as cross-generational workshops or innovation labs) help people figure out how to blend heritage with new ideas (Table 5).

Overall, the communities are achieving a working balance. There is a shared understanding that traditional knowledge is a living resource—it can be adapted to contemporary contexts as long as its core values are respected. Interview quotes reflect this balanced perspective. As an Aya youth remarked, “We can’t keep everything exactly as it was 100 years ago if we want to thrive, but we can adapt these traditions so they continue in a new form.” On the other hand, a senior Hakusan craftsperson stressed, “Modern tools help, but craftsmanship needs heart; I tell my apprentice never to rush just because of a machine.” Together, these viewpoints highlight a mutual commitment to “modernizing without losing soul.” Importantly, integrating tradition and innovation has started to yield benefits beyond economic success. Both communities report a sense of renewed pride and confidence. Young people who successfully turn a traditional skill into a profession gain esteem in the eyes of elders, validating the worth of that knowledge. Elders, in turn, feel their lifelong wisdom is contributing to the community’s future, not just its past.

Discussion

Bringing together findings from Aya and Mount Hakusan BRs, this study identifies several common patterns and context-specific differences. Participants in both BRs perceived intergenerational knowledge exchange as important for maintaining cultural continuity and supporting opportunities for sustainable entrepreneurship in ecotourism. Differences between Aya and Hakusan further suggest that community size, governance structures, and socio-economic contexts may shape how knowledge exchange is experienced and valued.

Key Common Insights

Elders as Perceived Key Assets: In both Aya and Hakusan, participants characterize elders as active educators, mentors, and cultural anchors rather than barriers to development. Their insights ensure local projects remain rooted in community values, aligning with research on elders’ roles in community resilience and environmental stewardship (Berkes 1999; Berkes and Ross 2013). High survey agreement and interview data highlight a shared perspective: rural initiatives that neglect elder knowledge miss a foundational cornerstone. Ultimately, leveraging elder wisdom—from farming techniques to folklore—yields authentic products and experiences that outside expertise cannot replicate, transforming cultural heritage into a valuable economic and social asset (Pine and Gilmore 1999).Youth Engagement and Perceived Capacity Building: While overall youth engagement is low (30–40%, Table 4), active participants serve as critical “knowledge brokers” (Wenger 1998) who translate traditional knowledge into contemporary uses. Interviewees highlighted that educated, tech-savvy returnees act as vital links, blending elders’ wisdom with modern digital marketing and business networking. Our findings suggest low participation stems from a lack of awareness and opportunity rather than apathy; many out-migrated youths expressed willingness to return under favorable conditions. Both communities could expand this cohort through targeted interventions like entrepreneurship training, seed funding, and exchange programs.Cultural Heritage as Economic Driver: Local culture and nature function as practical economic assets when mobilized thoughtfully. Aya’s organic products command premium prices due to their heritage narrative, while Hakusan’s guided tours leverage intangible elements—like learning from grandmothers or hearing folktales—to create value in the “experience economy” (Pine and Gilmore 1999). However, selective commercialization can commodify sacred traditions (Wearing and Neil 2009; Cole 2007). Reflecting this concern, community leaders in both sites explicitly limit what is commercialized, safeguarding spiritual ceremonies while sharing peripheral experiences. This ongoing negotiation highlights the necessity for knowledge holders to maintain control over their heritage to prevent the distortion of ancestral symbols in the marketplace (Mbaiwa 2011). Ultimately, community consent and careful management are required to ensure economic mobilization does not erode intrinsic cultural value.Balancing Modernization with Tradition: Both cases illustrate a delicate, achievable balance. Instead of viewing tradition and modernity as conflicting, participants see them as complementary—tradition provides grounding and identity, while modern practices offer reach and efficiency. Survey results reinforce this: roughly two-thirds of residents believe culture and modern entrepreneurship complement each other, and an even higher share agree that combining indigenous wisdom with new technology yields benefits (Table 5). Furthermore, strong support (over 70% in both BRs) for community dialog highlights that residents view collective problem-solving as the key to resolving tensions. Both communities rely heavily on individual initiative; while local governments secured the UNESCO designation, direct economic assistance remains limited, with only 35% of residents viewing current support as sufficient (Table 4). This reflects a common hurdle where rural communities possess assets and ideas but lack capital and technical expertise (Peredo and Chrisman 2006). Addressing this gap requires multi-level partnerships among BR councils, governments, NGOs, and universities to establish tailored micro-grants, mentorship networks, and business workshops. Crucially, these external capacity-building programs must demonstrate political empathy by co-creating initiatives that prioritize the community’s own vision of well-being. While local entrepreneurship cannot independently reverse national demographic decline, targeted local inputs—complemented by supportive regional and national policies (e.g., infrastructure and tax incentives)—can help mitigate rural marginalization and youth out-migration.

Differences and Their Implications

Governance and Scale: Aya’s single-town governance appears to give it an advantage in coherence and speed of implementing ideas (consistent with Tanaka and Wakamatsu 2018, who distinguish single versus multi-municipality BR models). Our research suggests that Aya can mobilize town-wide programs (such as integrating traditional knowledge into school curricula or community events) relatively easily due to its unified administration and close-knit social network. Hakusan’s multi-municipality, multi-stakeholder setup, by contrast, involves greater diversity and geographic spread. We observed that some villages in Hakusan (like the Shiramine area) are very active in BR activities, while others are less engaged or less aware of BR programs. This fragmentation implies several implications: Knowledge exchange in Hakusan thrives on decentralized, village-level hubs tailored to local contexts (e.g., craft vs. adventure tourism), with the central BR council acting as a facilitator for cross-pollination. Geographically compact Aya avoids this fragmentation by functioning effectively as a single, unified hub.Maintaining a unified BR identity across Hakusan’s large multi-municipality structure requires continuous commitment from all local governments. To prevent less-engaged areas from lagging, the council uses regional branding—such as the “Hakusan: Land of Legend and Snow” theme—to package diverse community efforts into multi-day tourist circuits that benefit multiple towns.In Aya, BR management is embedded directly within the local government, granting community knowledge holders a direct role in policy design. Conversely, Hakusan’s multi-authority council risks diluting individual community influence. While Hakusan includes community representatives, assessing their actual decision-making power over zoning or funding was beyond this study’s scope. Moving local stakeholders from downstream tourism implementers to upstream strategic partners is vital for authentic participatory environmental governance and resource co-management (Fraser et al. 2006). Community Demographics and Human Capital: The sites’ demographic differences directly shape their intergenerational initiatives. Geographically compact Aya features a smaller, older population where remaining or incoming youth are highly self-selected—already deeply immersed in the town’s sustainable lifestyle. This concentrated engagement explains why Aya’s fewer youth respondents showed equal or greater enthusiasm for blending technology with tradition (Table 5). Conversely, Hakusan possesses a larger pool of young adults, including part-time residents and returnees, resulting in a broader, more varied spectrum of attitudes ranging from highly engaged to ambivalent. This indicates that small communities can achieve high per-capita engagement (“quality over quantity”), while larger areas possess untapped, latent human capital. Consequently, Hakusan needs targeted incentives—such as local environmental leadership scholarships or sustainable business competitions—to mobilize its latent youth pool. Meanwhile, Aya must focus on retention and attracting young newcomers to expand its small, active base. In both settings, mitigating these demographic challenges requires pairing local initiatives with broader structural policies like improved rural services and regional employment opportunities.Economic Context and Market Access: The two sites operate in distinct economic landscapes. Aya has cultivated a nationwide reputation as a model eco-village, allowing it to leverage its unique identity directly; visitors arrive organically through word-of-mouth and established ecotourism networks. Conversely, Hakusan faces intense regional competition from nearby Kanazawa city and local ski resorts, requiring its entrepreneurs to build external alliances with urban tour operators, travel platforms, universities, and regional boards. To bolster its profile, the Hakusan BR Council has pursued sustainable tourism certifications and joint marketing campaigns. This divergence suggests valuable cross-learning: less centrally branded sites like Hakusan require strategic external marketing and alliances, whereas Aya could benefit from adopting Hakusan’s model of tapping university programs for youth entrepreneurship training.Types of Traditional Knowledge Emphasised: The core nature of traditional knowledge differs between the sites, directly shaping their entrepreneurial outputs. Aya’s knowledge centers on production (farming, forestry), lending itself to tangible goods like tea, sake, and crafts that provide diversified, shippable revenue. Meanwhile, Hakusan’s heritage emphasizes culture and spirituality, manifesting as experiential tourism. This experiential focus requires bringing visitors in situ, leaving Hakusan more vulnerable to tourism disruptions—such as the COVID-19 pandemic, which forced a temporary pivot to online content creation. To build resilience, Hakusan is diversifying into product development using cultural branding (e.g., special sake and charcoal), while Aya can expand into experience-based offerings like immersive farm stays to prolong visitor spending.

Our research suggests that Aya can mobilize town-wide programs (such as integrating traditional knowledge into school curricula or community events) relatively easily due to its unified administration and close-knit social network. Hakusan’s multi-municipality, multi-stakeholder setup, by contrast, involves greater diversity and geographic spread. We observed that some villages in Hakusan (like the Shiramine area) are very active in BR activities, while others are less engaged or less aware of BR programs. This fragmentation implies several implications:

Knowledge exchange in Hakusan thrives on decentralized, village-level hubs tailored to local contexts (e.g., craft vs. adventure tourism), with the central BR council acting as a facilitator for cross-pollination. Community Demographics and Human Capital: The sites’ demographic differences directly shape their intergenerational initiatives. Geographically compact Aya features a smaller, older population where remaining or incoming youth are highly self-selected—already deeply immersed in the town’s sustainable lifestyle. This concentrated engagement explains why Aya’s fewer youth respondents showed equal or greater enthusiasm for blending technology with tradition (Table 5). Conversely, Hakusan possesses a larger pool of young adults, including part-time residents and returnees, resulting in a broader, more varied spectrum of attitudes ranging from highly engaged to ambivalent. This indicates that small communities can achieve high per-capita engagement (“quality over quantity”), while larger areas possess untapped, latent human capital. Consequently, Hakusan needs targeted incentives—such as local environmental leadership scholarships or sustainable business competitions—to mobilize its latent youth pool. Meanwhile, Aya must focus on retention and attracting young newcomers to expand its small, active base. In both settings, mitigating these demographic challenges requires pairing local initiatives with broader structural policies like improved rural services and regional employment opportunities.

Implications for Policy and Practice

The findings offer five practical implications for rural communities, UNESCO, and local governments. First, strengthening intergenerational knowledge transfer is essential. Combining formal initiatives (school curricula, elder–youth councils) with informal pathways (festivals, apprenticeships) proves effective. Allocating modest BR resources to structured mentorship programs, including elder stipends, preserves skills while driving local enterprise.

Second, green entrepreneurs require holistic support. Targeted training in marketing, digital skills, and business planning should be delivered through workshops or exchange visits. Complementary financial support via culturally and environmentally responsible micro-loans or seed funding could significantly lower entry barriers for small eco-friendly businesses.

Third, communities must maintain authenticity while fostering innovation. Development strategies should be co-designed with elders to identify core traditions to preserve, and with youth to find where innovation strengthens livelihoods. Policymakers should help communities develop safeguards, guidelines, or charters against over-commercialization. Aya and Hakusan show that small-scale, community-led development preserves authenticity better than external investments. Policy frameworks should incentivize “slow”, community-based tourism over mass tourism to prevent cultural and environmental overexploitation, ensuring growth remains local rather than market-driven (Timothy and Tosun 2003).

Fourth, the UNESCO Biosphere Reserve designation is an underused asset. Consistent BR branding of local products and services helps communities reach niche markets seeking responsible offerings. This potential aligns directly with the Hangzhou Strategic Action Plan for UNESCO’s MAB and its World Network of BRs (2026–2035), which emphasizes support for local communities, youth engagement, and living in harmony with nature. Our findings advance objectives akin to MAB Target 15 (preserving cultural and heritage resources) and support research and ecotourism goals found in MAB Targets 28 and 33.

Finally, these findings echo global rural trends. Similar patterns of community-based stewardship aiding cultural resilience are documented globally, from Pacific ecotourism bolstering identity (Basham 2005) to African settings where traditional livelihoods evolve alongside tourism (Mbaiwa 2011). Proactively blending heritage with innovation, supported by flexible governance and partnerships, allows rural communities everywhere to counteract marginalization and environmental degradation.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. First, the survey sample sizes were small (20 respondents in Aya and 23 in Hakusan) and were obtained through purposive and convenience sampling. Consequently, the quantitative findings are exploratory and descriptive, illustrating patterns among the participants included in this study rather than supporting statistical generalization to the wider populations of the two Biosphere Reserves.

Second, differences in the demographic composition of the two samples may have influenced some findings. The Hakusan sample included a larger proportion of younger respondents, whereas the Aya sample contained more older participants. As age may shape perceptions of traditional knowledge, entrepreneurship, and technology use, it is difficult to fully disentangle the effects of site-specific contexts from those associated with demographic differences. Therefore, observed differences between the two Biosphere Reserves should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, there is a possibility of selection bias in the qualitative component of the study. Interviews were conducted primarily with individuals identified through local partners and snowball sampling, which may have resulted in greater representation of participants who were actively engaged in knowledge exchange activities or sustainable entrepreneurship initiatives. Consequently, unsuccessful, discontinued, or less visible experiences may be underrepresented despite efforts to include diverse participants and collect open-ended survey responses.

Fourth, this study is based primarily on participants’ perceptions and reported experiences regarding intergenerational knowledge exchange rather than direct observation of transmission processes. Although interviews and surveys provide valuable insights into how community members understand and value knowledge-sharing practices, they do not allow systematic examination of behavioral mechanisms or causal pathways of transmission. Future research employing ethnographic observation, social network analysis, or longitudinal approaches could provide a more detailed understanding of how traditional knowledge is maintained and transferred across generations.

Finally, the study focuses on two Japanese Biosphere Reserves with distinct social, cultural, and governance contexts. While the findings contribute to broader discussions on biocultural sustainability and community resilience, their applicability to other regions should be considered with caution. Comparative studies involving additional Biosphere Reserves in Japan and elsewhere would help assess the extent to which the patterns identified here are transferable to other settings.

Despite these limitations, the study provides useful insights into community perceptions of traditional knowledge exchange and highlights important considerations for supporting biocultural heritage and sustainable development within Biosphere Reserves.

Conclusion

This comparative study demonstrates that intergenerational knowledge exchange serves as a cornerstone for sustainable community development, ecotourism, and green entrepreneurship. In both Biosphere Reserves (BRs), collaboration between elders and youth creates innovative economic opportunities—such as Aya’s branded organic products and Hakusan’s cultural eco-tours—while reinforcing biocultural conservation. Elders contribute time-honed wisdom and identity, while a small group of highly motivated younger innovators act as “knowledge brokers.” These youth bridges translate ancestral knowledge into contemporary contexts, driving small-scale, culturally authentic, and environmentally responsible ventures.

Despite structural differences—Aya’s single-town model versus Hakusan’s multi-town council framework—both reserves reveal identical patterns of success rooted in respect for tradition, open intergenerational communication, and a shared development vision. Persistent challenges include engaging the broader youth population, bolstering external funding and training, and creating safeguards against over-commercialization. Our findings suggest these issues are being actively negotiated through community dialogs and adaptive management.

Globally, Aya and Hakusan demonstrate that empowering local communities as stewards of their heritage yields concrete economic and environmental dividends.
