Virtual reality transition to underground: human responses across sequential spatial transitions
Authors: Koeun Jung, Kyunghyun Cho, Hyeokjin Choi, Sun Ah Bae, Sookyung Chun, Seung Hyun Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the growing demand for the active utilization of underground spaces, understanding their effects on humans is crucial. However, constructing physical underground spaces for research is often costly and inflexible. In response, virtual reality (VR) provides a flexible and cost-effective alternative for simulating such environments and evaluating human responses. Yet, accurately replicating the experience of being underground in VR remains challenging. This study proposes a novel framework—Virtual Reality Transition to Underground (VRTU)—designed to stimulate the experience of descending from aboveground to underground environments. VRTU incorporates a sequential transition process through aboveground, transporting, and underground spaces, each enhanced with multimodal sensory cues to promote immersion. To validate this framework, we conducted an experiment within the VRTU environment, assessing participants’ responses using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ), a cognitive task, a subjective perception questionnaire, and heart rate measurement. Results revealed a significant increase in negative affect state following exposure to the VR-based underground space, along with physiological changes consistent with real-world underground settings. These findings demonstrate that the VRTU framework effectively captures core aspects of the underground space experience and offers a viable tool for evaluating human responses in immersive simulations prior to physical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53532-1.

Introduction

Humans inhabit and interact with various spaces, whose relevance evolves over time to meet changing life demands. For example, several countries are now leveraging underground areas to optimize material transportation and establish efficient transport networks1. Moreover, abandoned underground spaces, once integral to various functions, are being repurposed as data centers2. Additionally, the underground construction of shopping centers and cultural venues is on the rise to mitigate the impact of harsh weather conditions3,4. Recognizing the potential uses of underground spaces as part of human development is therefore necessary. Previous studies have highlighted this potential5–7, showing that the quality of the spatial environment is crucial for the effective utilization of underground spaces. Particularly, minimizing adverse effects stemming from the unique characteristics of underground spaces is imperative8–10.

The quality of a spatial environment and its impact on individuals can be assessed in two primary ways. First, individuals can subjectively evaluate the environment by directly assessing the perceptual aspects or overall satisfaction. Alternatively, the environmental impact can be indirectly measured by evaluating the physiological and psychological states of individuals within the space. For instance, assessing individuals’ emotional states can reveal whether the space elicits positive or negative feelings, while monitoring physiological responses such as heart rate can indicate whether individuals experience tension or relaxation. Comprehensive research methods are required to identify the effects of underground spaces on individuals by combining direct evaluations of the spatial environment with indirect measurements of physiological and psychological states.

An effective approach for comprehensive assessment may involve the physical construction of the target space. However, physical construction can be inefficient owing to substantial construction costs11 and challenges in adapting to changes in research questions12. To address this inefficiency, virtual reality (VR) technology can be employed13. A growing body of research has investigated the impact of architectural design on individuals using VR as a tool to support design decisions. Specifically, previous studies have collected individuals’ subjective evaluation of VR-based experimental environments14–18 and indirectly assessed the effects of spaces on individuals18–20.

However, if VR experimental environments fail to accurately simulate the experience of being in a target space, interpreting the responses measured within them as representative of typical human reactions in an actual target environment becomes challenging. Therefore, constructing a VR experimental environment that authentically replicates the target space experience is essential to effectively examine its impact on individuals. Techniques such as a smooth transition21 to the target space or the incorporation of various cues22,23 can achieve an accurate simulation of the target space. For instance, in the context of studying underground spaces, incorporating a transition process from aboveground to underground spaces in VR can significantly enhance immersion and the fidelity of the experience. This approach mirrors that of a recent study that investigated the effects of connections between aboveground and underground spaces on individuals24. In that study, participants were informed that the venue was located in a basement, and they moved into an underground space using an elevator. Although the primary focus was the effects of different connection types between underground and aboveground spaces on individuals’ psychophysical states, the study procedure offers valuable insights into enhancing immersion in a VR-based underground environment.

Based on these considerations, we propose a framework for constructing a VR experimental environment that authentically replicates the experience of being in an underground environment. The framework involves a systematic transition process from aboveground space to underground space, comprising three distinct areas: aboveground space, transporting space (elevator), and underground space. Each area is designed to incorporate various sensory cues that accurately convey environmental characteristics. Participants progressed sequentially through these areas to experience the transition to underground space. To validate the effectiveness of this framework, we comprehensively assessed participants’ physiological and psychological states, as well as their subjective perceptions, within an experimental environment adhering to this framework.

Related works

Unlike physical construction, VR enables rapid simulation of the target space and its elements, facilitating swift adjustments to changes in research questions25. It also provides a realistic spatial experience by directly engaging their senses and enhancing their concentration in a given scenario26. Additionally, recent advancements in VR technology have improved participants’ immersion compared to earlier iterations27.

Using VR, researchers can investigate the impact of architectural design on individuals by directly assessing their subjective evaluation of VR-based experimental environments. In a previous study, participants evaluated various physical conditions of architectural models and reported their individual preferences for each condition14. Similarly, several studies have manipulated architectural variables, such as furnishings28, curvilinear forms17, boundary types15, and wall textures16, and investigated their effects on individuals’ evaluations of the environment.

Moreover, VR can be used to indirectly assess the effects of architectural components and spaces on individuals. A previous study proposed an integrated VR-based experimental form that quantified the impact of architectural components on individuals’ experiences20. By employing physiological sensors (EEG, GSR, and PPG), this study demonstrated that various architectural configurations influenced participants’ stress and anxiety levels. More recently, Jung et al.19 reported that biophilic elements in indoor spaces affect individuals’ brain activity and emotional responses. By examining changes in participants’ physical and emotional responses within a VR-based experimental environment, researchers can infer the impact of architectural components or spaces on individuals.

Although these studies have been informative regarding the effectiveness of VR as a tool for investigating the effects of architectural components on individuals, questions remain as to whether participants’ responses within VR environments accurately mirror those in actual environments. Several studies have addressed this concern by comparing individuals’ responses in VR and real-world settings. For instance, Kort et al.29 found similar responses to bipolar adjectives in both environments, and Kuliga et al.25 demonstrated comparable behaviors between individuals in VR and actual environments. Additionally, the validity of VR-based experimental environments has been affirmed, with participants exhibiting similar physiological responses in both VR and real-life scenarios30,31.

Moreover, VR has been proven effective in exploring interactions within both underground and aboveground spaces. In aboveground environments, VR has been utilized to investigate individuals’ experiences across various architectural settings, such as window views32, window size and layout33, and the visual appearance of the built environment34. Yao et al.35 developed an underground shopping street model to study the impacts of street width and height on individual activities. Kostakos et al.36 modeled an underground parking lot and demonstrated that landmarks and wayfinding features effectively reduced individuals’ cognitive demands. Additionally, Li et al.18 proposed a model of an underground office space to examine the effects of office colors and green plants on individuals’ subjective comfort and cognitive performance.

Li et al.18 utilized VR, EEG, and laboratory experimental control (LEC) to simulate underground spaces and explore the interaction between participants and the experimental environment. By presenting well-modeled environmental configurations and assessing their effects on participants’ physical, emotional, and cognitive states, as well as subjective perceptions, this study provides a reference design for investigating the impacts of VR-based underground spaces on individuals. In this study, participants were informed that they would experience a VR-based underground space. Similarly, research investigating the interaction between individuals and spaces using VR generally provides such information through simple explanations14,19,20. That is, participants do not experience the process of transition into the target space within VR. However, merely instructing participants or allowing simple entry into the target space may not ensure the desired level of immersion or the fidelity of the experience21.

Development of virtual reality transition to underground (VRTU) framework

We propose a novel framework, Virtual Reality Transition to Underground (VRTU), for investigating the effects of VR-based underground space on individuals. A crucial aspect of VRTU is ensuring that individuals in the VR-based experimental environment genuinely feel as though they are in an underground space. To achieve this, VRTU employs a virtual environment carefully designed to incorporate various sensory and environmental cues that help individuals distinguish between aboveground and underground spaces while facilitating a seamless transition between the two. To support this, the virtual environment was primarily designed using visual and auditory cues, as these modalities are known to play crucial roles in human information processing, including perception37, spatial orientation38, and emotional responses39. Previous studies have shown that visual and auditory stimuli are critical for inducing presence and emotional immersion in virtual environments40. Although multimodal stimulation, such as thermal or olfactory cues, may further enhance realism, the visual and auditory systems can provide direct and immediate inputs for constructing and evaluating the perceived experience of underground spaces. Accordingly, VRTU focuses on these sensory channels to balance ecological validity and experimental feasibility.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, when individuals initially enter the virtual environment, they find themselves in an aboveground space. Thus, they perceive their surroundings as aboveground during the first phase of cognitive processing within this space. Two types of visual cues serve as robust indicators of aboveground space. First, symbolic representations of the sky, such as the sun, clouds, and natural phenomena like rain or snow readily evoke the perception of being aboveground due to their expansive and open characteristics41. Second, horizon-related symbols, often depicted through city spaces with mountains, rivers, or seas, evoke a sense of horizontal infinity grounded in our understanding of the Earth’s curvature. This perception of horizontal infinity reinforces the recognition of aboveground space42. Auditory cues, such as weather-related sounds (e.g., rain, distant wind, and thunder) and natural outdoor sounds (e.g., bird vocalizations and flowing water), serve as salient indicators of aboveground environments. These sounds are typically associated with open, naturally exposed settings, reinforcing the perception of spatial openness and environmental liveliness. In the present study, clear daytime conditions were simulated; accordingly, bird vocalizations were presented as continuous ambient audio to convey an acoustic atmosphere characteristic of an aboveground environment43,44.

In the VRTU sequence, the second phase of cognition in aboveground space focuses on individuals’ awareness of the potential transition to underground space. This phase is characterized by the behavioral sequence in which individuals depart from aboveground space and approach transportation facilities. Visual cues such as the appearance of elevators, escalators, ramps, or staircases clearly signal the possibility of descent45,46. These visual cues help individuals recognize their movement toward a transporting space, allowing them to perceptually prepare for the transition to underground space. Once within the transporting space, a new cognitive phase begins, focusing on initiating movement through this space. This phase involves entry into the transporting space, accompanied by visual cues signaling the onset of downward motion—whether initiated by the individual or the space itself (e.g., elevator doors closing, surroundings descending, or escalators gradually moving downward)47. Auditory cues, such as variations in footstep sounds across different spaces and mechanical sounds indicating the operation of transportation devices (e.g., doors opening and closing), serve as salient indicators of transition from aboveground space to a transporting space. These sounds are commonly associated with entering a new spatial context and initiating movement. In the present study, this transition was primarily conveyed through the sound of elevator doors opening and closing, providing perceptual cues that can be intuitively recognized as signaling entry into a transporting space48.

The next phase in the VRTU framework centers on the awareness of movement within the transporting space—either one’s own or that of the transporting environment—in the intended direction. Visual cues, such as the upward motion of the surroundings (e.g., rising walls outside an elevator or ascending balustrades of escalators), provide strong indications of downward movement. Auditory cues, including sounds produced by the transporting space or individuals’ own actions (e.g., elevator machinery, footsteps on escalator or staircase, the sounds of gripping handrails), further enhance the perception of being in transit49. In the present study, this sense of movement was conveyed through continuous elevator operation sounds combined with subtle, periodically occurring mechanical interaction sounds, such as intermittent jolting noises, providing perceptual cues that can be intuitively recognized as characteristic of ongoing descent.

Upon entering the final stage of VRTU, the first phase of cognition in an underground space focuses on individuals’ recognition of having transitioned to a new environment following movement through the transporting space. The behavior sequence involves leaving the transporting space and entering the underground space, marked by visual cues that differ from those of the transporting space. These cues include changes in spatial form, ceiling height, surface texture, interior color, and overall spatial design, prompting individuals to perceive that they have arrived at a distinct location from which they initially began their descent50. Variations in the spatial atmosphere, such as increased enclosure, reduced or altered exterior views, and variations in ambient brightness, can further reinforce this perception.

The second phase of cognition in an underground space pertains to individuals’ perception and recognition of the environment as underground space. Visual cues, such as the presence of a ceiling that blocks the view of the sky and suggests vertical confinement, combined with prior knowledge of having descended from aboveground, provide compelling evidence of being in an underground space51. Additional factors, including limited openness, reliance on artificial lighting, and distinctive architectural elements contribute to this perception. Auditory cues further emphasize the enclosed nature of the environment, with amplified echoes from wall surfaces and the distinctive resonance characteristic of enclosed spaces52. These acoustic features, in which indirect sounds dominate over direct ones, play a crucial role in establishing the auditory atmosphere typical of underground spaces. In the present study, this auditory condition was implemented by applying subtle reverberation effects to environmental sounds and reducing ambient openness-related sound cues, thereby providing perceptual signals characteristic of an enclosed, underground environment.

Methods

Characteristics of the demonstration model and apparatus

Figure 2 shows a schematic diagram and a real-world photo of the experimental environment, illustrating how the virtual spaces were structured and experienced by participants. The VR environment was composed of three interconnected zones, aboveground space, transporting space, and underground space, as described in Sect. 3. Each space was designed to embody the key sensory and environmental cues outlined in the VRTU framework, enabling a realistic and seamless transition between environments. In the transporting space, the elevator descent was simulated over approximately 180 m within 60 s, corresponding to a constant speed of 180 m/min, consistent with typical high-speed elevator performance. This depth was selected to represent deep underground environments exceeding 30–50 m, as commonly considered in future underground development scenarios53,54. To enhance perceptual realism, smooth acceleration and deceleration phases were implemented at the beginning and end of the descent, respectively.

The VR environments were created using Rhinoceros 7 for spatial modeling and Unity for integration of assets, environmental lighting, and soundscapes. Outdoor landscaping and architectural elements (e.g., high-rise buildings, urban vegetation) were sourced from Unity asset packages. Ambient sounds, environmental transitions, and interactive elements such as the Pinch gesture were implemented within Unity to ensure real-time responsiveness.

The experiment was run on a desktop computer equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 7950 × 3D 16-Core, 64GB RAM, and an RTX 4090 graphics card. Participants experienced the virtual environments through a Meta Quest Pro head-mounted display (HMD), with real-time heart rate data recorded via an EmotiBit sensor attached to the forearm (see Fig. 3 for devices and adaptation). All survey responses and the cognitive task were completed within the VR environment to minimize breaks in presence, in line with the immersive principles of VRTU and the recommendations of Schwind et al.55.

Participants

A total of 50 adults (21 men and 29 women, aged 21–34 years) participated and received monetary compensation. We recruited the participants via campus announcements, student group chats, and email advertisements, which clearly stated the use of virtual reality. Basic information from the participants was collected during recruitment. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST, KH2023-078) and were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurement

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the VRTU framework, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of participants’ physiological, emotional, cognitive, and perceptual responses. These measures aimed to capture how immersion in the VRTU environment influenced both subjective experience and psychological, bodily reactions across different stages of the simulated underground transition.

We used the Positive Affect and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) to assess participants’ emotional states before and after their experience in the VRTU environment. This widely used self-report scale consists of 20 items—10 reflecting positive affect and 10 reflecting negative affect—rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely)56. Higher scores indicated greater emotional intensity.

To assess participants’ cognitive function, we employed the Stroop task within the VRTU environment. In this task, participants typically identified the ink color of presented words while ignoring conflicting semantic content (e.g., the word “BLUE” printed in red ink)57. Administering the task in the virtual environment allowed us to evaluate whether cognitive performance is preserved during and after exposure to underground simulation.

Participants’ perceptions of the VRTU environment were assessed using a custom questionnaire adapted from previous VR and architectural studies18,58. The questionnaire included 8 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (ranging from − 3 to + 3) and addressed both physical (e.g., lighting, color, spaciousness, enclosure) and affective (e.g., comfort, attractiveness, satisfaction, willingness to remain) dimensions (Table 1).

We used heart rate monitoring as a non-invasive physiological measure to capture emotional arousal and environmental responsiveness throughout the VRTU experience36,59,60. Heart rate is a well-established psychophysiological index sensitive to both stress and affective changes61,62.

To evaluate participants’ immersive engagement with the VRTU environment, we administered the Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ)63. This validated instrument comprises 14 items across four factors: general presence (G); spatial presence (SP); involvement (INV); experienced realism (REAL). Items are rated on a 7-point scale and reflect participants’ perceived realism and absorption in the virtual space (S_Table 1)64,65.

Experimental procedure

Figure 3 illustrates the experimental procedure. Prior to the laboratory visit, as part of the recruitment process, participants completed a brief demographic form and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) to assess their baseline emotional state. This pre-exposure measurement allowed us to examine emotional changes following the VRTU experience. Upon arrival, participants first provided informed consent and received a brief explanation of the study (see Fig. 3), including instructions on completing the surveys and cognitive task. They were then equipped with a Meta Quest Pro headset and an EmotiBit sensor. Following an adaptation period—during which participants familiarized themselves with the virtual environment and practiced the pinch gesture—they were allowed to freely explore the aboveground space before descending via elevator to the underground area. Once the exploration was complete, participants filled out the PANAS, IPQ, and a subjective perception questionnaire, and then proceeded to the cognitive task. All tasks were completed within the VRTU environment (see Sect. 4.3 for details). The experiment concluded with the removal of the devices and a brief 5–10 min interview to gather additional feedback.

Demonstration results

This section presents the empirical outcomes from the demonstration of the VRTU framework. Participants’ responses were analyzed across five domains: emotional state (PANAS), cognitive performance (Stroop task), subjective perception, physiological arousal (Heart Rate), and sense of presence (IPQ). Together, these results demonstrate how the VRTU environment influenced affective, cognitive, and embodied experiences during the simulated transition from aboveground to underground space.

PANAS

To evaluate emotional changes induced by the VRTU experience, we analyzed PANAS scores using a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance with affect (negative vs. positive) and time (pre- vs. post-exposure) as within-subject factors. The pre-exposure scores were collected during participant recruitment, and the post-exposure scores were obtained immediately after the VRTU environment experience. The results are shown in Fig. 4. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of affect, F(1, 49) = 132.70, p < .001, η2 = 0.73, indicating that the positive affect scores were higher than negative affect scores. The main effect of time was significant, F(1,49) = 5.64, p = .022, η2 = 0.10, reflecting changes in emotional states over time.

Fig. 4Mean scores of negative and positive affects across time points. Error bars denote within-subject standard error.

Mean scores of negative and positive affects across time points. Error bars denote within-subject standard error.

Although the two-way interaction between factors was not significant, F(1,49) = 0.87, p = .355, η2 = 0.02, we separately examined the negative affect and positive affect data for the following reasons. Previous studies have indicated that the experience of underground spaces commonly elicits negative emotions. Thus, we presume that the magnitude of negative affect was amplified after the participants experienced VR-based underground space. Therefore, previous findings justify the current analyses. Further t-tests showed that negative affect significantly increased after the VRTU experience, t(49) = 2.23, p = .029, d = 0.32, whereas positive affect did not significantly change, t(49) = 0.82, p = .415, d = 0.12. These results suggest that the VRTU underground simulation elicited a measurable increase in negative emotional responses consistent with prior findings on underground environments.

Cognitive task (Stroop Task)

To assess cognitive performance within the VRTU environment, we analyzed accuracy rates in the Stroop task. This task involved both congruent trials (e.g., the word “BLUE” displayed in blue ink) and incongruent trials (e.g., the word “RED” displayed in blue ink), requiring participants to identify the ink color while ignoring the semantic content of the word. Successful performance demands inhibitory control and selective attention.

Because the task was performed via pinch gesture interaction in VR, response times were not emphasized; instead, accuracy rates were used as the main indicator. To compare the accuracy data between congruent and incongruent trials, we conducted a paired t-test on response accuracy data. One participant’s data was excluded from the analysis owing to technical issues in the recording. The results of cognitive task analysis are shown in Fig. 5. Accuracy rates were high in both trial-types, and a paired t-test revealed no significant difference between congruent and incongruent trials, t(48) = 0.77, p = .444, d = 0.11.

Typically, participants are instructed to respond as accurately and quickly as possible when performing the Stroop task. However, in the current study, the interaction between participants and the VR-based experimental environment was carried out through pinch gestures, resulting in longer response times compared to traditional computer-based laboratory studies. Longer response times enabled participants to achieve a high level of accuracy rates. Consequently, detecting the congruent effect, which is commonly observed when participants respond as quickly as possible to experimental stimuli, may be challenging in the current study. Considering this, we emphasized that participants encountered no difficulties in comprehending and executing cognitive tasks within the VR-based experimental environment. Statistical results confirm that the participants sufficiently understood the task and encountered no specific issues in performing the cognitive task in the VR-based experimental environment.

Subjective perception

Participants’ subjective evaluations of the VRTU environment included assessments of physical attributes (e.g., lighting, spaciousness, color, permeability) and affective impressions (e.g., comfort, attractiveness, satisfaction, and willingness to remain). The results of participants’ subjective perception are presented in Fig. 6. On average, participants perceived the VRTU environment as bright, spacious, and moderately comfortable. The color scheme was interpreted as cool, and the space was viewed as relatively open rather than enclosed. These responses support the effectiveness of the sensory and environmental design features embedded within the VRTU framework.

Physiological response (Heart Rate)

To examine how physiological arousal varied throughout the VRTU sequence, heart rate data were segmented into seven stages reflecting the experimental phases: aboveground exploration, elevator descent, underground exploration, measurement preparation, and the three subsequent measurement phases (PANAS/IPQ, cognitive task, and perception survey). Due to technical issues resulting in missing heart rate data or unclear differentiation between stages, we excluded data from 10 participants in the subsequent analysis. To investigate variations in heart rates across stages, we applied a repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with stage as the within-subject factor to the data. The analysis yielded a significant main effect of stage, F(6,234) = 2.54, p = .021, η² = 0.06, reflecting variation in physiological arousal across the stages of the VRTU sequence (Fig. 7).

Acknowledging the variations in heart rate levels across procedural stages, we further explored whether these changes were associated with emotional responses. Specifically, participants were divided into high- and low-difference groups based on their change in negative affect (post-pre PANAS scores). A repeated-measures two-way ANOVA with stage and group as within-subject factors revealed a significant main effect of stage, F(6,228) = 2.56, p = .021, η² =0.06, indicating differences in heart rate levels across stages (see Fig. 7). Additionally, we observed a significant main effect of group, F(1,38) = 6.90, p = .012, η² =0.15, indicating that heart rate levels were lower for the high difference group compared with the low difference group. The two-way interaction between factors was not significant, F(6,228) = 1.24, p = .284, η² =0.03.

A notable finding in the heart rate analysis is that the high difference group exhibited lower heart rate levels compared with the low difference group. This pattern may reflect the engagement of emotional regulation strategies during the more enclosed and immersive portions of the VRTU environment66,67. These results suggest that physiological responses are dynamically modulated by both the environmental conditions simulated in VRTU and individual differences in emotional reactivity (see Discussion for further elaboration on this issue).

Sense of presence (IPQ)

The IPQ was used to assess the degree to which participants felt immersed in the VRTU environment. Table 2 presents IPQ scores from the current study alongside those reported in previous research. Results across the four IPQ dimensions—general presence, spatial presence, involvement, and experienced realism—indicated high levels of perceived presence. General presence scored the highest (M = 4.52), followed by spatial presence (M = 3.43) and involvement (M = 3.10), while experienced realism was lowest (M = 2.80). These values are comparable to or exceed benchmarks reported in prior VR studies, supporting the immersive quality and ecological validity of the VRTU design.

Discussion

The present study proposes an integrated framework, Virtual Reality Transition to Underground (VRTU), to provide an expanded reference design for investigating the effects of VR-based underground space on humans. We note that the primary aim of the present study was not to isolate the causal contributions of individual components (e.g., VR exposure, environmental characteristics, or transition dynamics), but rather to propose and provide an initial validation of a structured framework (VRTU) for simulating the experience of transitioning into underground space. As an initial step, we examined whether such a structured transition paradigm can elicit measurable psychological and physiological responses consistent with prior findings on underground environments.

To construct this framework, we incorporated the concept of the “transition space” along with various sensory cues. The transition space can facilitate participants’ immersion by simulating the transition from aboveground space to underground space, enhancing their perception of the environment45,46. Additionally, multiple cues, including visual and auditory stimuli, further immerse participants in the VR environment41,43,44. Using two measurement approaches, we examined the impact of VR-based underground spaces constructed using this integrated framework. This examination encompassed both direct methods, based on participants’ subjective evaluations of the experimental environment, and indirect methods, through the assessment of participants’ psychological and physiological aspects.

By assessing participants’ affective state before and after participating in the experiment, we confirmed that the VRTU environment influenced their affective states. Specifically, compared with the baseline state before experiencing VR-based underground space, participants reported higher scores for negative emotions afterward. These findings align with previous research indicating that the unique characteristics of underground environments tend to evoke negative emotions in individuals9,73. Moreover, the VRTU framework significantly shaped the participants’ experiences. However, it is essential to approach these results with caution. The design of the current study, which measured affect states before and after the experiment, may not have captured emotional changes during the experience itself. Consequently, whether the observed difference in negative affect states stems from the underground space experience itself or the transition into it remains unclear. Future research aimed at elucidating this aspect would be important.

The present findings regarding heart rate appear to deviate from previous literature, which typically associates negative or claustrophobic environments with increased sympathetic activation and elevated heart rate36,62,74. In contrast, higher levels of negative affect in the current study were associated with decreased heart rate, particularly among participants showing larger changes from baseline. One possible explanation is that participants engaged in emotion regulation strategies in response to increased negative affect. Previous research suggests that effective emotional regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal or expressive suppression, can impact heart rate. Specifically, individuals who employ adaptive emotional regulation strategies tend to show more stable heart rate patterns during emotionally challenging situations compared to those who use maladaptive strategies75,76. Although this study did not provide explicit emotional explanations or instructions to the participants, the heightened negative affect experienced in the VRTU might have prompted individuals to instinctively regulate their emotions to alleviate discomfort.

However, this interpretation should be treated with caution. The observed pattern may instead reflect more complex or context-dependent physiological dynamics, such as an orienting or attentional engagement response in immersive environments77, or parasympathetic-dominant responses (e.g., freezing-like or passive coping mechanisms) under constrained conditions78,79. Importantly, because the present study relied on heart rate as a single physiological index, the precise mechanism underlying this dissociation between affective and physiological responses cannot be determined. Future studies incorporating additional measures (e.g., heart rate variability or skin conductance) are needed to distinguish between these alternative explanations.

The cognitive task results of this study indicate that participants in the VR-based underground space encountered no critical issues in understanding and executing tasks related to measuring information processing. However, the interaction between participants and the VR environment used in this study presented challenges in terms of the participants’ immediate responses to the presented stimuli. Therefore, adopting interactive methods that facilitate real-time response recording would enhance efficiency when investigating the impact of VR-based experimental spaces on individuals using tasks commonly employed in traditional computer-based laboratory research. Furthermore, given the focus of this study on measuring emotional changes resulting from VR-based underground space experiences, employing an emotional Stroop task could help determine whether emotional states modulate the effects of VRTU experience on information processing.

Research on the effects of exposure to underground spaces on individuals has been challenging owing to high construction costs and space demands11. A VR-based experimental environment like VRTU can circumvent this problem by immersing individuals in a realistic underground environment to conduct experiments. Since the experiment is conducted in a virtual environment rather than a real one, the level of realism provided by this environment played a crucial role in participant immersion. The present study included a transition process in its research procedure, allowing participants to clearly perceive their movement into an underground space. Moreover, this transition process incorporated the characteristics commonly associated with each processing stage. Thus, VRTU enables participants to effectively immerse themselves in a VR-based underground experimental environment. This design can contribute to examining the effects of underground spaces on individuals, which is challenging to achieve in a physical experimental environment.

Despite its contribution to underground space research, this study has some limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the current framework focused primarily on the transition from aboveground to underground spaces, exposing participants to underground spaces for relatively short durations. Consequently, assessing the impact of underground space characteristics on participants during prolonged exposure is challenging. Future research is necessary to explore the effects of prolonged exposure and individual adaptation processes to better understand these dynamics. Second, the participant sample was limited to a relatively small group of young adults (aged 21–34), with an uneven gender distribution. This demographic concentration may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Importantly, younger adults may differ from older populations in their familiarity with VR environments, sensitivity to environmental stressors, and emotional regulation strategies, all of which could influence affective and physiological responses observed in the present study. Therefore, the observed increase in negative affect and associated physiological patterns may not directly generalize to older individuals or more diverse populations. To enhance external validity, future studies should include a more diverse participant pool encompassing different age groups, genders, and occupational backgrounds. Third, while the VRTU framework simulated a generic underground space, it did not account for variations in the functional characteristics of different underground environments, such as commercial malls, subway stations, or shelters. These contexts may differentially influence emotional, perceptual, and cognitive responses. Future research should develop scenario-specific virtual environments to investigate the nuanced effects of various underground space types on use experience. Finally, the present design does not allow us to disentangle whether the observed effects were driven by the simulated underground environment itself, the use of VR equipment, or the structured transition process implemented in VRTU. Moreover, similar changes in affective and physiological responses could arise from exposure to any novel or arbitrary environment, rather than being specific to the sequential transition from aboveground to underground space. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that the VRTU framework can elicit relevant psychological and physiological responses, rather than as definitive evidence that it faithfully reproduces real-world underground experiences. This distinction is particularly important given the absence of control conditions that would allow for a clear separation of VR-related and environment-specific effects. Future research should incorporate appropriate control conditions, including non-VR real-world comparisons (e.g., elevator descent), as well as alternative conditions (e.g., non-transition or teleportation), to more rigorously test the uniqueness of the VRTU framework.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the effects of a VR-based underground environment, designed using an integrated framework called Virtual Reality Transition to Underground (VRTU), on individuals. The VRTU environment was carefully designed to simulate the experience of being in an underground space by incorporating diverse sensory and environmental cues to differentiate it from aboveground environments. To evaluate the effectiveness of this VR framework, we assessed participants’ subjective evaluations of the environment and measured their psychophysiological responses.
