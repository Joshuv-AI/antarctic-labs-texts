Sensory-guided human-machine joint learning accelerates the acquisition of motor imagery brain computer interface control
Authors: Hanwen Wang, Yisha Zhang, Maxim Karrenbach, Yidan Ding, Bin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) offer the potential to restore function and augment human capabilities. However, non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG)-based BCIs still face challenges in learning efficiency and control precision, particularly for naïve users performing complex tasks. Here, we present a sensory-guided joint learning framework that integrates human motor learning with adaptive machine learning to improve BCI training and performance. In 31 BCI-naïve participants, the framework enabled rapid skill acquisition, achieving average online discrete accuracies of 86.0% for one-dimensional (1D) and 77.5% for two-dimensional (2D) motor imagery tasks, along with continuous control accuracies of 77.5% (1D) and 66.9% (2D). Mechanistically, tactile guidance reduced user exploration and accelerated neural adaptation, while sample reweighting aligned decoder updates with human learning trajectories. By coupling reinforcement-driven neural plasticity with adaptive algorithmic optimization, this framework advances BCI training from passive calibration to active human–machine joint learning, enabling practical and scalable neural interfaces for communication and rehabilitation.

Introduction

Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) translate neural activity into external control signals, offering avenues for both functional restoration in clinical populations and performance augmentation in healthy users. Recent studies using invasive BCIs have demonstrated impressive results. For example, intracortical systems have enabled individuals with paralysis to achieve fluent text communication via neural decoding of intended speech1–3 and to control robotic arms with high precision4,5, with recent advances further introducing tactile percepts through patterned cortical microstimulation6,7. Despite these advances, invasive BCIs are limited by the need for neurosurgical implantation, long-term maintenance, and patient eligibility. In contrast, non-invasive BCIs based on electroencephalography (EEG) offer a safe and more accessible alternative with potential applicability to a wide population. While EEG has limited spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio, EEG-based BCI systems have demonstrated effectiveness in enabling both communication and robotic control in clinical and healthy populations8–10. Studies have shown that non-invasive motor imagery BCIs can support complex applications such as quadcopter navigation in three-dimensional space11, as well as robotic arm control for reach-and-grasp and pick-and-place tasks12,13. Recent developments in wearable EEG systems and advanced signal processing methods have further improved the usability and scalability of non-invasive BCIs14–16, and enabled fine robotic hand control at individual finger levels15.

Among non-invasive paradigms, motor imagery (MI) remains one of the most widely studied approaches17–19. Users learn to voluntarily modulate sensorimotor rhythms by imagining specific motor actions without executing actual movements20. Compared to paradigms such as steady-state visual evoked potentials (SSVEPs) or P300-based BCIs, MI paradigms do not rely on exogenous stimuli or overt sensory attention, allowing for more autonomous and flexible use21,22. MI-BCIs have shown promise in motor rehabilitation and assistive device control for individuals with impairments23,24, and have also been explored in prosthetics control, gaming, and cognitive training applications due to their potential for hands-free and intention-driven interaction12,25. Despite their promise, MI-BCIs remain difficult to use effectively, as users must learn to generate consistent neural patterns through repetitive training—a process that is often slow and highly variable across individuals26. These challenges are amplified in high-dimensional tasks such as cursor or robotic arm control, underscoring the need for improved training protocols and system adaptability9,18.

To address these limitations, extensive research has focused on improving motor imagery BCI performance through algorithmic advancements. A wide range of methods—including adaptive classifiers, transfer learning, and deep neural networks—have been proposed to mitigate inter-session variability and increase decoding robustness27–29. These approaches have significantly reduced calibration time and improved offline performance. Nevertheless, their efficacy often diminishes in online settings, especially for naïve users who must concurrently learn to generate consistent neural patterns while adapting to the system dynamics30.

In addition to algorithmic advancements, considerable research has focused on developing training paradigms that facilitate user adaptation and enhance MI-BCIs. These paradigms often incorporate feedback mechanisms to assist users in learning to modulate sensorimotor rhythms more effectively. While visual feedback remains the most widely used modality, recent studies have shown that somatosensory and proprioceptive inputs—such as vibrotactile stimulation—can improve sensorimotor engagement and classification accuracy31,32. Moreover, targeted neurofeedback protocols have been demonstrated to strengthen sensorimotor rhythm (SMR) modulation and induce measurable neuroplastic changes in relevant cortical regions33. Beyond feedback strategies, some paradigms have integrated tasks such as movement execution or focused attention meditation to prime the sensorimotor system and improve BCI performance15,34–36. Despite these advances, most training paradigms rely on predefined, one-way feedback that does not dynamically adapt to individual learning trajectories or interact with decoder optimization. This lack of mutual adaptation limits the efficiency and robustness of skill acquisition in MI-BCI systems.

Building on the two aforementioned directions, the concept of co-adaptation has emerged as a theoretical framework to address the dual-agent nature of BCI systems, in which both the user and the machine adapt simultaneously. Early work formalized co-adaptation as a framework for mutual learning, emphasizing the importance of alignment between decoder updates and user strategy formation37,38. Recent experimental efforts have begun to implement human learning mechanisms or closed-loop adaptation using reinforcement learning39,40. However, most implementations remain algorithm-centric, treating the user as a passively adapting signal source. As a result, the core challenge of heterogeneous human–machine learning dynamics—characterized by mismatched optimization methods and representations—remains largely unresolved.

Despite its conceptual merits, the practical realization of effective co-adaptation remains highly challenging. Human and machine learners operate under fundamentally different principles—humans rely on sparse, often implicit feedback and exhibit slow, strategy-driven adaptation37, whereas machines update iteratively through explicit, algorithmic optimization41. These mismatches in learning timescales, representational structures, and adaptation mechanisms make it difficult to establish a stable and synergistic learning process. Moreover, existing frameworks seldom provide coordinated guidance to both agents simultaneously, often leading to misaligned learning trajectories and suboptimal convergence.

To address these challenges, we propose the first sensory-guided joint learning framework that enables simultaneous adaptation of both the user and the decoder, thereby accelerating the training process and improving overall system performance. Unlike existing MI-BCI paradigms, which typically emphasize either user-side adaptation or decoder-side optimization in isolation, the proposed framework explicitly coordinates both processes within a unified learning loop. Conventional feedback-driven approaches primarily guide user learning through visual or auditory error feedback while relying on static or weakly adaptive classifiers. Decoder-centric adaptive methods, in contrast, focus on algorithmic refinement to improve separability, often treating user learning as an implicit or secondary process. More recent sensory- or reinforcement-based paradigms introduce tactile or reward signals to enhance user engagement or machine learning, yet generally operate independently of decoder optimization. By coupling structured tactile guidance with a sample reweighting mechanism that biases decoder updates toward user-learnable neural patterns, the sensory-guided joint learning framework synchronizes human learning and machine adaptation, addressing a fundamental mismatch in learning dynamics that limits the efficiency and robustness of existing MI-BCI systems. In a grouped study involving 31 participants, we test whether our framework can significantly outperform traditional motor imagery BCI training methods in both one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) control tasks. We examine the dual role of tactile stimulation: it not only enhances users’ ability to generate more discriminable neural patterns but also guides the algorithm’s optimization trajectory to align with the stimulation-induced intent. By aligning human learning and machine adaptation toward a shared control objective evoked by sensory cues, the system fosters a synergistic co-adaptive process. This proposed framework substantially enhances the robustness of MI-BCI control and expands the potential for broader applications in assistive robotics, neurorehabilitation, and consumer-grade neural interfaces.

Results

We investigated the sensory-guided joint learning framework for MI-BCI in a six-session longitudinal study involving 31 able-bodied participants. To disentangle the effects of tactile guidance and the joint learning mechanism, participants were randomly assigned to three groups: an experimental joint learning group (n = 15), a BCI2000 control group (n = 8), and a tactile control group (n = 8). All participants completed the first four sessions, which included two left/right (L/R) sessions, one mixed L/R and up/down (U/D) session, and one 2D cursor control session. A subset of participants (8 from the joint learning group, 7 from the BCI2000 control group, and 6 from the tactile control group) completed the final two sessions involving extended 2D control tasks. In addition, a subset of participants (6 from the joint learning group, 5 from the BCI2000 control group, and 5 from the tactile control group) completed the experiment of long-term effects involving one additional L/R session and one additional 2D session.

The BCI system featured a button-sized linear resonant actuator (LRA) placed at the volar aspect of the wrist to deliver localized vibrotactile stimulation. The delivery of tactile cues varied across groups: the tactile control group received vibrotactile stimulation on every training trial; the experimental group received stimulation selectively based on performance in the preceding trial, as determined by the joint learning framework; the BCI2000 group received no tactile stimulation42. For the long-term experiments, all groups of subjects were tested without tactile stimulation and with an interval of over 2 months after previous training sessions.

Each session for the experimental and tactile groups comprised six runs: one initial no-feedback run for decoder initialization, followed by a test run with real-time decoder feedback, three training runs with both feedback and tactile stimulation, and a final test run without stimulation. The BCI2000 group completed six matched feedback runs using a fixed decoder and no tactile cues, with an identical number of trials. All subjects went through one BCI2000 run (Run 0) at the very start of the experiment to show the naïve performance.

Online decoding was performed using EEGNet-8,243. In the joint learning and tactile control groups, decoder parameters were updated after each training run, while the BCI2000 control group employed a fixed autoregressive (AR) model in the first 6 sessions, and in the long-term experiments, EEGNet was adopted. To evaluate performance, we computed multiple metrics: online accuracy, defined as the proportion of correct predictions throughout the trial with a 1000 ms sliding window (stepped every 40 ms); as well as percent valid correct (PVC) and forced percent total correct (fPTC) based on the standard BCI2000 framework.

Statistical comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests between the joint learning group and each control group across sessions and three key metrics. No significant group differences were observed during the initial BCI2000 baseline run (Run 0, p > 0.05, Fig. 1b, c). However, from Session 1 onward, the joint learning group demonstrated progressively stronger performance, with statistically significant advantages (p < 0.01 to p < 0.001) over both the tactile and BCI2000 control groups. These differences became particularly pronounced in the later sessions and 2D tasks.

Further analysis of 2D and L/R performance (Fig. 1d, e) reinforced this trend. In 2D tasks, the joint learning group showed the highest median accuracies across all metrics, with fPTC and PVC both exceeding 80%. The tactile control group performed modestly better than the BCI2000 control group, suggesting that vibrotactile cues alone contribute measurable benefit, though insufficient to match the joint learning framework. A similar pattern was observed in L/R tasks, with the tactile group again slightly outperforming the BCI2000 group, while the joint learning group maintained clear superiority throughout. Consistent with a learn-then-plateau profile in 2D, pairwise contrasts between Sessions 4–6 showed no within-group changes across metrics (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d), indicating that all groups reached a performance plateau once 2D control was acquired.

These results collectively demonstrate that the sensory-guided joint learning framework not only ensures superior MI-BCI control accuracy but also generalizes effectively across increasing task complexity and session progression.

We next examined intra-session learning by comparing online accuracy between the initial and final motor imagery test runs (Run 6—Run 2) within each session. As shown in Fig. 2c, the joint learning group consistently demonstrated larger within-session gains than either control group (ANOVA, p = 0.0007), indicating more effective real-time adaptation. Corresponding results for PVC and fPTC are presented in Supplementary Fig. 4.

To further assess learning efficiency, we analyzed decoder loss across runs. The loss is averaged by online test loss across samples within each run. Conceptually, this loss represents the cross-entropy between the predicted class distribution and the true labels, such that lower values indicate that the decoder assigns a higher probability to the correct motor imagery class with greater confidence. The joint learning group consistently maintained lower loss values than the tactile control group across all sessions (Fig. 2d). These group differences were statistically significant in Sessions 2–6 (p < 0.01 to p < 0.001; Fig. 2e). Moreover, only the joint learning group exhibited a clear decreasing trend in loss over time (Fig. 2f), reflecting effective decoder refinement. In contrast, the tactile control group showed greater variability and lacked consistent loss reduction.

Together, these results highlight the enhanced learning capacity enabled by the sensory-guided joint learning framework, both within sessions and across longitudinal use.

The joint learning framework better aligns decoder updates with user learning

To examine how the joint learning framework supports co-adaptation, we assessed whether the direction of neural adaptation in users aligned with the direction of decoder updates. This alignment was quantified using two complementary measures. Projection length indicates the magnitude of participants’ neural changes along the decoder’s gradient direction, reflecting how much user adaptation occurred in the dimensions most relevant to decoder optimization. Cosine similarity captures the directional agreement between user-driven signal changes and decoder updates, independent of magnitude.

As shown in Fig. 3a, b, participants in the joint learning group exhibited significantly greater alignment between their signal changes and model gradients, as measured by projection length (mean: 0.46 vs. 0.19; p < 0.001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) and cosine similarity (mean: 0.62 vs. 0.49; p < 0.001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). These results indicate that the model updates in the joint learning framework not only track, but also effectively discover and reinforce directions in neural space that are easier for users to learn and adapt to. The visualization in a reduced 2D subspace further confirmed this pattern (Fig. 3c). Here, each participant’s neural activity across runs was projected into a simplified “trajectory” that captures how neural representations evolve during learning. In the joint learning group, these trajectories progressed consistently along the decoder’s gradient direction, forming structured, goal-directed paths. Joint learning enables sustained and long-term MI-BCI performance without external stimulation

To evaluate the persistence of learning effects, we compared decoding performance across multiple MI runs within each session. Specifically, we compared the two non-stimulated test runs: Run 2 (before training) and Run 6 (after training), with Run 4 serving as the training phase with tactile stimulation. As shown in Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 5, participants in the joint learning group exhibited continued performance improvement from Run 2 to Run 6, despite the absence of tactile stimulation in both runs, indicating sustained learning effects. In contrast, the tactile control group—despite receiving identical tactile stimulation during training—showed no significant gain in 2D tasks, and even a significant decline in 1D accuracy after training (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed rank test). Also, the BCI2000 control group did not exhibit significant performance changes, indicating no significant learning effect within the session. To assess the stability of learning outcomes within each session, we compared the maximum accuracy achieved during the session with the final test performance (Run 6). As shown in Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 6, participants in the joint learning group exhibited no significant drop between their peak performance and final test accuracy (p > 0.05, Wilcoxon signed rank test), indicating that they were able to retain and apply their learned strategies throughout the session, even without further stimulation. By contrast, both the tactile control and BCI2000 groups showed a significant decline from their session peak to final performance (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed rank test), suggesting weaker consolidation of learned control strategies.

To determine whether the performance gains induced by sensory-guided joint learning persist beyond the training context, we conducted a long-term evaluation in which participants performed MI-BCI control without tactile stimulation or instructional cues. Long-term performance was compared directly with the accuracy measured during the training phase. As shown in Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 7, participants from the different groups, especially in the joint learning group, exhibited stable long-term retention of MI control. No significant decrease in online accuracy was observed at long-term testing relative to training-phase performance, indicating that learned control strategies remained effective after the withdrawal of stimulation. Notably, accuracy in the L/R task of joint learning group subjects showed a significant improvement at long-term evaluation (p < 0.05, r = 0.89, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), whereas performance in the more demanding 2D task was maintained without a significant change (n.s.). This pattern suggests that joint learning supports durable MI representations that generalize beyond the immediate training session.

We further compared long-term performance across groups to assess whether retention effects were specific to the joint learning framework. As shown in Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 8, the joint learning group achieved significantly higher long-term online accuracy than both the tactile control and BCI2000 control groups in both the L/R and 2D tasks (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; all p < 0.05, all r > 0.58). Importantly, this advantage persisted despite the use of an EEGNet decoder in the BCI2000 control group, indicating that long-term performance differences cannot be attributed solely to decoder architecture. Instead, these results highlight the role of the sensory-guided joint learning paradigm in promoting stable and transferable MI-BCI control.

Finally, we examined whether the adaptive paradigm with pairwise trials in the joint learning group provided behavioral advantages. In this design, the first trial’s outcome determines whether stimulation is delivered in the subsequent same-type trial. As shown in Fig. 4d, participants in the joint learning group achieved significantly higher accuracy in the next trial of each pair (*p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed rank test), whereas the tactile control group showed no such trial-wise difference. This finding suggests that joint learning’s adaptive structure actively reinforces learning during online control.

Together with the within-session retention and trial-wise reinforcement effects (Fig. 4a–d), the long-term results shown in Fig. 4e, f demonstrate that the sensory-guided joint learning framework not only facilitates more effective online training but also enables participants to retain and apply learned motor imagery strategies beyond the training context. Importantly, these performance gains persist after the withdrawal of tactile stimulation, indicating that joint learning supports stable and transferable MI control rather than transient, stimulation-dependent improvements.

The sensory-guided framework facilitates robust and localized cortical activations

To assess how the joint learning framework influences underlying neural dynamics, we examined event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) patterns in the sensorimotor rhythms, which estimate trial-averaged changes in band power relative to baseline in decibel units. Unlike ERD/ERS, which express power decreases or increases as relative percentages within predefined frequency bands, ERSP provides a full time–frequency representation, enabling detection of both transient and sustained modulations across multiple rhythms. Time–frequency ERSP analyses of electrodes C3 and C4 (Fig. 5a, b; baseline relative to −1500-0 ms window with the instruction and tactile stimulation) revealed that MI tasks in the joint learning group elicited robust alpha-band (8–13 Hz) activation over contralateral sensorimotor areas. Compared with pre-training trials, alpha band responses were markedly enhanced during the runs with tactile stimulation. Notably, the runs conducted after tactile stimulation still preserved the same characteristic features, though with reduced strength. This suggests that the paradigm facilitates MI-related neural activation (Fig. 5b; −0.14, 0.68, and 0.20 dB of ERSP for Before, Tactile, and After conditions). In contrast, this phenomenon was not observed in the tactile control group. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9a, b, consistent ERD responses were present across all conditions, but no significant differences emerged before versus after tactile stimulation. To further delineate the differences between joint learning and tactile control, we redefined the baseline as the resting state prior to trial onset (−3500−2000 ms). In both groups (Supplementary Fig. 10a, d), the onset of tactile stimulation ( − 1500 to 0 ms) elicited a pronounced ERD (group-level results in Supplementary Fig. 10b, e; **p < 0.01, * p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed rank test), reflecting heightened attentional engagement in line with prior tactile stimulation studies. However, once motor imagery commenced (0–4000 ms), participants in the joint learning group entered a phase of comparatively weaker ERD, indicative of task execution (Fig. 5a; group-level analysis in Supplementary Fig. 10c showed that no significant signal changes were evoked during the motor imagery period, compared with trials before and after tactile stimulation). By contrast, in the tactile control group, a strong and sustained alpha-band ERD persisted until trial termination (Supplementary Fig. 10e; * p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed rank test). These results highlight distinct electrophysiological signatures between the joint learning and tactile control paradigms: while both groups showed attentional ERD at stimulus onset, only the tactile control group exhibited a prolonged alpha desynchronization during MI. Building on these time–frequency observations, we next quantified the group-level differences in ERD strength across motor imagery runs without stimulation. Group-level comparisons of left and right trials confirmed significantly stronger alpha ERD (8–13 Hz; Fig. 5c; −18.71% vs −2.52%) and beta ERD (13–30 Hz; Fig. 5d; −24.07% vs −11.31%) in the joint learning group compared to the tactile control group (*p < 0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). These results indicate that the sensory-guided framework facilitates more pronounced neural engagement during MI tasks, while subjects in the tactile control group failed to establish the canonical ERD pattern during MI.

To further evaluate how the trial structure modulates ERD strength, we compared alpha ERSP across different trial types in the joint learning group (Fig. 5e). In the proposed framework, trials occurred in pairs. If the first trial reached the accuracy threshold, the next was a copy trial; otherwise, it was a new trial. Accordingly, four types were defined: before copy (trial preceding a copy), before new (trial preceding a new), copy trial, and new trial. New trials with tactile stimulation elicited significantly greater ERSP compared to their preceding trials without stimulation (***p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed rank test), whereas no significant difference was found between copy trials and their respective pre-trial baselines (n.s.). This supports the notion that tactile stimulation in the paradigm specifically enhances MI-related signal features in targeted trials.

Also, we computed the topographical distribution of alpha ERSP by subtracting the preceding non-stimulated trial from the corresponding tactile-stimulated trial within each pair (Fig. 5f). Because ERSP in a narrow frequency band reflects the relative change in spectral power from baseline, reductions in alpha ERSP can be directly interpreted as event-related desynchronization (ERD). The resulting difference maps revealed that tactile stimulation selectively amplified class-specific ERD responses over contralateral sensorimotor regions, demonstrating its effective role in enhancing MI-related neural desynchronization.

Finally, we examined the full-scalp alpha ERSP distribution across all 2D sessions (Supplementary Fig. 11). All three groups exhibited task-related activations, but the joint learning group showed the most consistent and spatially focused patterns, with clear contralateral sensorimotor and parietal involvement. These concentrated activations suggest more efficient recruitment of the motor imagery network compared with the tactile and BCI2000 control groups. The tactile and BCI2000 control group displayed weaker and more diffuse patterns. The stronger and more localized responses in the joint learning group are consistent with prior studies showing that effective MI-BCI training enhances cortical engagement in sensorimotor areas44,45. These findings support the conclusion that the joint learning framework not only improves behavioral accuracy but also facilitates the development of robust, physiologically meaningful neural representations critical for MI-BCI control.

To further evaluate the contribution of the reweighting strategy, we conducted pseudo-online experiments in which datasets from the joint learning, tactile control and EEGNet control groups were trained with both proposed sample reweighting algorithm (Weighted-EEGNet) and conventional EEGNet. Across sessions, Weighted-EEGNet generally achieved higher online accuracy, PVC, and fPTC than conventional EEGNet within the same dataset, although the magnitude of improvement varied across groups and sessions (Supplementary Fig. 12, linear mixed-effects analysis, all p < 0.05). This advantage was most consistent in the joint learning group, underscoring the algorithm’s intrinsic advantage in extracting discriminative features.

However, decoder-side improvements alone were insufficient to explain the full behavioral advantage of the joint learning framework. To directly dissociate the contribution of decoder architecture from that of the sensory-guided paradigm, we conducted an additional EEGNet control experiment. In this condition, participants were trained using a conventional EEGNet decoder with the same run-by-run update schedule as the joint learning group, but without tactile stimulation, pairwise strategic structure, or the sample reweighting mechanism used in joint learning. As shown in Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Fig. 13, the EEGNet control group exhibited a session-wise performance trajectory and learning effect comparable to that of the tactile control group and remained substantially below the joint learning group across online accuracy, PVC and fPTC. These results indicate that the superior performance of joint learning cannot be attributed simply to the use of EEGNet as a more advanced adaptive decoder. At the same time, the similarity between the EEGNet control and tactile control groups suggests that tactile stimulation alone does not directly produce substantial improvements in motor imagery performance when it is not embedded in the full joint learning framework. Together with the pseudo-online analyses, these findings indicate that both decoder-side adaptation and sensory-guided user learning contribute to performance improvement, but neither component alone is sufficient to reproduce the gains observed in the joint learning condition. Instead, the greatest and most consistent improvements emerge only when adaptive decoding is coupled with weighted updating and sensory-guided changes in user-generated neural patterns.

We then examined whether the learned decoder features were neurophysiologically meaningful. Frequency-domain analysis (Fig. 6b) demonstrated that, although the temporal convolutional kernels of the network (Weighted-EEGNet) were distributed across a broad range, only the alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) bands contained a high density of kernels. Importantly, gradient-based band importance (Grad-CAM, gradient-weighted class activation mapping) indicated that alpha and beta-band features contributed strongly to classification, in line with their established role in motor imagery decoding.

Spatial activation mapping further illustrated how the learned features aligned with task-relevant cortical regions. For the proposed algorithm (Fig. 6e), left–right feature differences exhibited the expected lateralized activation over contralateral motor areas (C3/C4), while up–down feature differences produced bilateral central activations. To further characterize the network’s learned representations, Grad-CAM visualizations averaged across all four motor imagery directions in the joint learning framework revealed focused activations over sensorimotor areas (Fig. 6f), indicating that the algorithm captures consistent and spatially meaningful features across multidimensional tasks. Grad-CAM highlights the spatial regions of the input EEG signals that most strongly influence the network’s classification decision, thereby providing an interpretable link between model predictions and neurophysiological features. In contrast, the conventional EEGNet model with tactile control group (Supplementary Fig. 10f) displayed weaker and less spatially specific activations, particularly for the up–down dimension. This underscores the joint learning framework’s capacity to extract spatially and spectrally meaningful features that support robust multidimensional MI-BCI control.

Discussion

We present a sensory-guided human-machine joint learning framework that fundamentally advances motor imagery brain–computer interfaces (MI-BCIs) by uniting tactile reinforcement with co-adaptive decoding. This approach establishes a new framework for non-invasive BCI training: rather than relying on slow, unguided trial-and-error practice, participants rapidly converged on effective control strategies through structured sensory scaffolding. Even BCI-naïve users achieved robust accuracies—exceeding 85% in one-dimensional cursor movement tasks and 75% in two-dimensional cursor movement tasks—within only a few sessions, corresponding to substantially higher learning efficiency than conventional baselines (Figs. 1, 2). These results demonstrate not simply an improvement in training speed, but a conceptual shift toward embedding biologically inspired reinforcement signals into human–machine co-adaptation, offering a powerful route to sustainable and generalizable BCI performance with clear translational potential8,9.

A central innovation of our framework lies in the integration of structured tactile stimulation, which acts as both a reinforcement signal and a guidance mechanism for human learners. In the training paradigm, “new” trials were paired with vibrotactile cues whenever participants’ prior performance fell below threshold, thereby reinforcing effective motor imagery while reducing reliance on unguided trial-and-error exploration. This design aligns closely with established principles of reinforcement learning in the brain, where salient reward-like feedback accelerates the acquisition of motor skills and promotes plasticity in sensorimotor circuits46–48. By providing a goal-directed corrective cue rather than an abstract error signal, tactile stimulation effectively narrows the search space of possible mental strategies, guiding participants toward representations that are both discriminable for the decoder and learnable for the user (Fig. 7b).Fig. 7Human-machine joint learning framework, experimental paradigm, and session structure.a Illustration of the experimental setup. Such scaffolding is especially critical in MI-BCI, where naïve users often struggle to establish consistent neural patterns without clear external guidance26. Behaviorally, this translated to faster convergence and more stable control strategies; at the neural level, as elaborated below, it was paralleled by enhanced alpha-band desynchronization and reinforced cortical representations. Together, these findings illustrate how embedding biologically inspired reinforcement signals into BCI training can transform user learning from unguided exploration into structured, efficient adaptation.

A key strength of the joint learning paradigm is that its benefits extend beyond transient performance gains, supporting the consolidation of motor imagery strategies even in the absence of ongoing tactile cues. Participants in the joint learning group maintained or improved their accuracy between pre- and post-training runs without stimulation (Run 2 vs. Run 6), while the tactile control and BCI2000 control groups showed no such retention, and in some cases declined (Fig. 4a–c). This persistence indicates that tactile stimulation functions not merely as an external aid but as a scaffold that enables the internalization of effective sensorimotor representations. Such consolidation reflects a well-documented property of motor learning, wherein external reinforcement signals initially facilitate performance but gradually give way to internally stabilized control as neural circuits reorganize49,50. In our paradigm, tactile cues were specifically coupled to “new” trials following subthreshold performance, thereby reinforcing the generation of stronger and more separable neural signals. Over time, this repeated pairing allowed participants to learn how to voluntarily reproduce these task-relevant patterns even in the absence of cues. Thus, the tactile stimulation did not create a dependency, but instead accelerated the transition from externally guided to internally sustained motor imagery representations.

Importantly, the adaptive pairing design further amplified this effect: participants showed higher accuracy in second trials of each pair, suggesting that temporally structured reinforcement accelerated the stabilization of learned strategies (Fig. 4d). This is consistent with broader principles of reinforcement learning and motor skill acquisition, where goal-directed feedback accelerates the shift from exploratory to habitual control51,52. By embedding such principles into the paradigm, the joint learning framework effectively converted external sensory guidance into enduring neural adaptations, enabling sustained performance without continuous external support. This property is essential for the translational potential of non-invasive BCIs, which must ultimately function without intensive supervision or constant feedback to be practical in daily use.

In addition to reinforcement-like mechanisms, attentional modulation may also contribute to the neural and behavioral effects observed during sensory-guided training. Attentional modulation is known to influence sensorimotor rhythm dynamics, including alpha- and beta-band desynchronization during motor imagery, by increasing task-relevant cortical engagement and suppressing competing neural activity. In the present framework, tactile stimulation may increase task salience by making the cued motor imagery state more perceptually and behaviorally prominent, thereby directing attention toward the intended sensorimotor representation and transiently enhancing ERD during the learning phase, particularly in training runs accompanied by sensory cues. However, several observations argue against attentional modulation as the sole explanation for the sustained effects observed here. First, the tactile control group received tactile stimulation during training but did not show behavioral improvements comparable to the joint learning group, indicating that stimulation-induced salience alone was insufficient to reproduce the full effect. Second, the primary post-training evaluations were conducted without tactile stimulation or copy/new instructional cues and were preceded by a 5-min rest period following stimulation runs, reducing the likelihood that acute attentional carryover drove the observed performance gains. Third, the neural and behavioral effects were trial-type specific: in the joint learning group, performance-contingent “new” trials showed stronger modulation than their preceding trials, whereas such structured improvement was not observed in the tactile control group. Finally, long-term testing without tactile stimulation showed retained performance advantages in the joint learning group, further supporting a learned and stabilized change in motor imagery control rather than a transient attentional facilitation. Together, these findings suggest that attentional engagement may facilitate the learning phase by increasing feedback salience, but it does not fully account for the sustained behavioral improvements, trial-specific neural modulation, or long-term retention. Instead, the results support a mechanism in which tactile cues act as reinforcement-like sensory scaffolds that promote task-relevant sensorimotor representations, while the sample reweighting algorithm preferentially incorporates these reinforced neural patterns into decoder adaptation. Another contribution of this work is the demonstration that the joint learning framework promotes a coordinated co-adaptive process between user and decoder (Fig. 7b). Unlike traditional approaches, where human adaptation and algorithm updates proceed largely independently, our results show that decoder updates tracked neural adaptation trajectories that participants could naturally reinforce (Fig. 3a, b). Projection length and cosine similarity between trial-to-trial neural signal changes and decoder gradients were significantly higher in the joint learning group, indicating that the decoder was not simply accommodating variability, but actively identifying neural directions that were both discriminative and behaviorally learnable. This property reflects a theoretical solution to the “two-learners problem” of BCI, where the challenge lies in aligning machine learning updates with human neural plasticity38,53. By embedding structured tactile guidance into trial design, our system reduced the dimensionality of user exploration, allowing both the human and the decoder to converge on shared representations more efficiently. Importantly, additional analyses showed that this advantage cannot be explained by decoder sophistication alone. The pseudo-online analyses showed that the weighted EEGNet algorithm provided a measurable decoder-side benefit over conventional EEGNet, indicating that the reweighting strategy itself improves the extraction of discriminative motor imagery features. However, the additional EEGNet control experiment showed that an adaptive EEGNet decoder with the same run-by-run update schedule, but without tactile guidance or pairwise strategic structure, produced performance comparable to the tactile control group and consistently lower than the joint learning group across sessions and metrics. Together, these findings indicate that neither adaptive decoding alone nor tactile stimulation alone is sufficient to account for the observed gains. Rather, robust improvement emerges only when decoder-side adaptation is coupled with sensory-guided user learning, allowing the algorithm and the participant to progressively align with one another. In this sense, the sensory-guided joint learning framework should be understood not as a stronger decoder in isolation, but as a coordinated human–machine co-learning paradigm in which neural signal formation and decoder optimization are jointly shaped over training.

From a neuroscience perspective, this alignment mirrors known mechanisms of motor skill learning, in which consistent sensory feedback shapes synaptic plasticity along behaviorally relevant neural pathways49,54. The visualization of latent trajectories further illustrates this synergy: joint learning participants showed structured, goal-directed neural evolution, while tactile-only controls exhibited more diffuse, unstable patterns (Fig. 3c). Such findings support the view that effective BCI training requires not only adaptive decoders, but also paradigms that actively elicit coherent, reinforcement-compatible neural modulations from the user. By integrating these two aspects, the joint learning framework establishes a principled method for aligning human neuroplasticity with machine adaptation, paving the way for more sustainable and generalizable BCI performance.

The neurophysiological analyses provide mechanistic support for the behavioral improvements observed under joint learning. Alpha- and beta-band ERD over contralateral sensorimotor areas emerged as robust signatures of motor imagery engagement, and these responses were significantly amplified when tactile stimulation was integrated within the adaptive training structure (Fig. 5a–d). Such desynchronization reflects the functional disinhibition of sensorimotor cortices, a hallmark of motor preparation and motor imagery55,56. Importantly, the joint learning group exhibited more pronounced and class-specific ERD than the tactile control group, indicating that tactile cues acted not merely as external sensory inputs but as reinforcement signals that selectively enhanced task-relevant neural patterns. This suggests that the framework actively shaped cortical excitability to increase the separability of MI-induced oscillations, thereby facilitating decoder discrimination.

Crucially, ERSP dynamics clarify why joint learning, and not tactile stimulation alone, produced durable gains. Across groups, the onset of tactile input elicited the strongest alpha-band activation (reflecting attention capture to the vibrotactile cue), but the trajectories diverged after MI began: in the joint-learning group, attentional resources shifted from the tactile sensation to the imagery task, allowing MI-specific ERD to dominate the post-cue window; in the tactile-only group, alpha ERSP remained dominated by the tactile drive, yielding less class-specific MI modulation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Early in training, “new” trials accompanied by tactile input evoked stronger alpha ERSP than their preceding non-stimulated trials, consistent with reinforcement of correct cortical activations (Fig. 5e). Topographical mapping further confirmed that tactile stimulation selectively enhanced contralateral alpha ERSP (Fig. 5f), aligning with the well-established lateralization of motor imagery rhythms20. Taken together, these temporal and spatial patterns indicate that joint learning uses tactile input as a scaffold—strong enough to drive attention and shape sensorimotor excitability when needed, yet structured so that attention is transferred to MI once the movement imagery is engaged. This scaffold-then-transfer process accelerates the transition from externally cued to internally stabilized representations, strengthening the physiological substrate for sustainable BCI control.

Taken together, these findings provide a coherent mechanistic explanation for the observed behavioral improvements. Tactile stimulation acted as a shared driver for both user and decoder adaptation: for participants, it amplified contralateral alpha band desynchronization, yielding neural patterns that were stronger and more separable than those without stimulation (Fig. 5e); for the decoder, the sample reweighting algorithm preferentially emphasized these tactile-evoked, informative signals while down-weighting less discriminable trials (Fig. 6a). In this way, both human and machine were guided by the same reinforcement-like cues, converging toward physiologically meaningful features that supported stable and generalizable control (Fig. 7b, c). This bidirectional convergence underscores the novelty of the sensory-guided joint learning framework—not simply improving short-term accuracy, but embedding biologically grounded reinforcement principles into adaptive decoding, thereby establishing a unified pathway for human–machine co-adaptation.

The benefits of sensory-guided human–machine joint learning can be understood within contemporary frameworks of sensorimotor integration, motor learning, and reinforcement-driven neuroplasticity. Modern neuroscience models emphasize that task-relevant sensory feedback is a core component of the motor control loop, shaping cortical dynamics by reinforcing appropriate neural activation patterns and suppressing competing activity. In particular, mechanosensory feedback has been shown to play a central role in refining motor commands, accelerating learning, and stabilizing sensorimotor representations through recurrent sensorimotor pathways57,58.

Within this context, tactile stimulation in the present framework functions as a structured reinforcement signal that increases the salience of successful motor imagery strategies. Such reinforcement facilitates stronger and more consistent modulation of sensorimotor rhythms, thereby promoting the emergence of separable neural patterns. Crucially, the joint learning algorithm preferentially amplifies these reinforced patterns through trial-level reweighting, aligning decoder adaptation with user-side neural plasticity. This coupling between sensory-driven learning and algorithmic adaptation is consistent with recent neural models of feedback-based motor control, in which adaptive behavior emerges from iterative interaction between sensory feedback and internal control policies59.

Importantly, the persistence of behavioral and neurophysiological improvements in the absence of ongoing tactile stimulation suggests that sensory guidance supports consolidation of learned sensorimotor representations rather than transient facilitation. This interpretation aligns with evidence from motor learning and neurorehabilitation research showing that repeated pairing of sensory input with voluntary motor intent can induce lasting cortical reorganization and functional stabilization. Together, these findings provide a neuroscience-based explanation for why sensory-guided human–machine joint learning yields robust and enduring performance gains.

While the present study demonstrates clear advantages of the sensory-guided joint learning framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our participant cohort consisted exclusively of healthy, BCI-naïve adults. Although this choice allowed us to probe fundamental learning mechanisms under controlled conditions, it remains to be determined whether similar benefits extend to clinical populations, such as stroke survivors or patients with spinal cord injury, who often exhibit functional reorganization of the sensorimotor cortex, impaired somatosensory pathways, and attentional deficits9,23. Translating the paradigm to these populations will therefore require targeted adjustments to specific components of the framework. Based on our findings, we anticipate that sensory guidance parameters—such as the location, modality, and intensity of tactile stimulation—will require the most substantial customization to ensure perceptibility and alignment with residual sensorimotor representations. In contrast, the core joint learning mechanism, implemented through progressive sample inclusion and trial-level reweighting, operates at an abstract learning level and is expected to generalize across populations as long as a consistent reinforcement signal is available. Attention-related impairments may further motivate slower learning rhythms or extended training schedules, which can be naturally accommodated within the existing curriculum-style framework. Together, these considerations suggest that clinical translation will primarily involve adapting sensory interfaces and learning pace, rather than redesigning the fundamental joint learning algorithm. Second, the tactile stimulation protocol, though effective, was limited to palmar vibrotactile input; future work may explore other modalities such as electrotactile or proprioceptive feedback to engage complementary somatosensory pathways and further strengthen cortical responses7. From an algorithmic perspective, the reweighting strategy successfully prioritized separable neural signals and aligned decoder adaptation with human learning trajectories. Nonetheless, our implementation relied on offline optimization of hyperparameters, which may limit scalability in fully online, real-world use. More advanced adaptive machine learning methods—such as meta-learning or reinforcement-driven adaptation—could allow the decoder to autonomously adjust to rapidly changing user strategies while minimizing computational overhead41. Third, consistent with prior MI-BCI studies, participants exhibited inter-individual variability in their responsiveness to haptic feedback. In the present study, such variability was not explicitly parameterized but was implicitly accommodated through the reweighting mechanism and progressive sample inclusion, which allowed the decoder to align with individual learning trajectories without subject-specific thresholds or manual tuning. While this design choice ensured protocol consistency and facilitated group-level comparisons, it does not fully exploit the potential benefits of personalization. Future extensions of the framework could incorporate adaptive feedback scheduling, individualized inclusion rates, or dynamic weighting functions based on each user’s neural responsiveness and learning speed, thereby further enhancing robustness and efficiency across heterogeneous user populations. An additional limitation concerns the imbalance in sample sizes across groups, with fewer participants in the tactile control (n = 8) and BCI2000 control (n = 8) groups compared to the joint learning group (n = 15). Given the inherently high inter-individual variability in BCI learning, smaller control group sizes may reduce statistical power and increase uncertainty in between-group comparisons. However, the primary conclusions are supported by consistent within-subject learning trends, repeated-measures analyses, and converging behavioral and neurophysiological evidence, which partially mitigate the impact of this imbalance. Future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts will be important to further validate these findings. Finally, beyond participant- and algorithm-related considerations, the present study also has a task-design–level limitation related to the physiological asymmetry of the 2D control paradigm. This design follows a commonly used convention in BCI2000-based MI-BCI systems, in which one control direction is encoded by a resting baseline rather than an active motor imagery state. While left/right and up movements were controlled via unilateral and bilateral motor imagery, respectively, the down direction was defined as rest. Discriminating between active motor imagery and a resting baseline engages a fundamentally different control mechanism than discriminating among multiple active motor imagery states and is known to be more sensitive to baseline fluctuations and inter-run variability. This active-versus-rest asymmetry may therefore constrain absolute performance levels in 2D control and could partially account for the reduction in accuracy observed when transitioning from 1D to 2D tasks. Importantly, despite this structural constraint, the joint learning framework consistently improved performance relative to control conditions across both paradigms, indicating robustness to increased task dimensionality and control asymmetry. Future work may explore more physiologically symmetric multi-class motor imagery designs or adaptive baseline modeling strategies to mitigate these effects.

Methods

Participants

Our study complies with all relevant ethical regulations regarding human research. 31 able-bodied, BCI-naive participants (18 female; mean age 23.8 ± 4.42) were recruited from the Pittsburgh area and provided written informed consent in accordance with the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki, and according to a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of Carnegie Mellon University. Participants were compensated for their time at a rate of $15 per hour. Participants were randomly assigned to three groups: joint learning (n = 15), BCI2000 control (n = 8), and tactile control (n = 8). All participants completed four sessions covering left/right and 2D motor imagery tasks. A subset (n = 21) continued for two additional sessions involving extended 2D control. In addition, an independent EEGNet control group (n = 8) was recruited for an additional control experiment, which followed the same six-session protocol as the other groups.

Tactile stimulation

Tactile feedback was delivered via sustained vibrotactile stimulation using linear resonant actuators (LRAs; 10 mm diameter, VG1040003D, Vybronics Inc.) affixed on the volar aspect of the wrist, near the base of participants’ palm (Fig. 7a). The actuators were driven by a 170 Hz carrier frequency modulated by a 30 Hz sinusoidal envelope, chosen to coincide with the actuator’s resonant peak for optimal efficiency. This waveform was designed to engage both Meissner corpuscles (responsive to 20–50 Hz flutter) and Pacinian corpuscles (responsive to >100 Hz vibration), ensuring broad somatosensory activation60,61. The palm, densely innervated by both mechanoreceptor types, provided an ideal site for delivering temporally precise tactile cues62.

Stimulation was lateralized to the same side as the imagined movement direction (e.g., right-hand stimulation during right-hand MI). In the 2D task, upward movement trials were paired with simultaneous stimulation to both hands, while no stimulation was delivered during downward movement trials. In the joint learning group, tactile stimulation was selectively delivered during “new” trials based on the decoding outcome of the previous trial. In contrast, the tactile control group received identical tactile patterns according to the task instruction, decoupled from user performance. This manipulation enabled the dissociation of sensory reinforcement effects from stimulation alone.

Joint learning framework and sample reweighting algorithm

The proposed sensory-guided joint learning framework facilitates bidirectional co-adaptation between the human user and the machine learning decoder by leveraging two complementary mechanisms: (1) a structured trial paradigm with behavior-contingent tactile stimulation, and (2) a sample-wise reweighting algorithm embedded in the decoder. Together, these mechanisms establish a closed-loop system in which both user and machine continuously adjust based on each other’s learning signals, effectively reducing learning inefficiency from both ends (Fig. 7b).

In motor imagery BCIs, human users often rely on reinforcement-based trial-and-error learning—gradually refining mental strategies based on feedback from system responses and perceived outcomes26,63. Unlike machines, humans are not gradient-following optimizers; instead, their learning benefits from clear, structured guidance and reward signals. To address this, our framework introduces a pairwise trial structure: in each pair, the first trial is always unstimulated. If this trial is with online accuracy above the threshold, the subsequent trial instructs the participant to reproduce the same motor imagery pattern with a “Copy” instruction, reinforcing internal consistency and promoting neural consolidation. Conversely, if the first trial yields low online accuracy, the second trial is with a “New” instruction—accompanied by tactile stimulation to actively reshape the user’s mental imagery and enhance motor-related signal generation. This structure mimics the logic of error-driven reinforcement learning in biological systems, where correct actions are reinforced, and incorrect ones are redirected via salient cues50.

For the decoder, the challenge lies in adapting to dynamically evolving user signals. As users learn and improve, their EEG responses begin to contain increasingly discriminative features—especially during trials reinforced by tactile cues. However, raw training data also contains substantial noise and suboptimal samples, particularly in early learning stages. To handle this, we introduce a sample-wise reweighting algorithm that adaptively adjusts the influence of each training trial based on its loss value. Here, loss refers to how far off the decoder’s prediction is from the correct label, with higher loss indicating poorer classification performance for that sample.

The decoder update was performed once after each training run. During each online run, decoder parameters were fixed, and no within-run model updating was performed. The updated decoder was then used in the subsequent run. No decoder fine-tuning was performed during online test runs. This update schedule synchronized user learning and decoder adaptation across two coupled timescales. At the trial-pair level, the participant’s online performance determined the subsequent copy/new instruction and whether tactile stimulation was delivered, thereby guiding the user’s exploration or consolidation of motor imagery strategies while the decoder remained fixed. At the run level, the EEG samples generated under this trial policy were then used for decoder updating through the sample reweighting algorithm. In this way, user-side learning and machine-side adaptation are coupled through the evolving separability of the EEG samples.

This mechanism assigns greater importance to trials that already contain reliable or behaviorally reinforced neural patterns—including those enhanced through tactile stimulation—while down-weighting noisy or misaligned samples. Because reinforced trials tend to produce lower losses, the windows belonging to these trials naturally receive higher weights and exert greater influence on subsequent parameter updates. As user learning progressively shapes the underlying neural representations (e.g., stronger or more consistent ERD/ERS patterns), the reweighting process amplifies the decoder’s sensitivity to these emerging structures. In this way, human learning directly guides machine adaptation, creating a synchronized co-learning process. The progressively increasing inclusion threshold implements a curriculum-style progression inspired by curriculum and self-paced learning paradigms64,65, in which optimization begins with highly reliable samples and gradually incorporates more challenging ones, improving convergence stability and robustness.

Crucially, the decoder’s output also serves as feedback to the user through the online cursor and the copy/new trial structure. The updated decoder, therefore, influences the feedback available in subsequent runs, which in turn shapes the user’s future motor imagery strategies. This establishes a closed-loop co-learning cycle in which users learn to generate more separable neural patterns and the decoder progressively prioritizes those patterns during optimization.

This synergistic alignment is illustrated in Fig. 7c. In traditional systems, user learning trajectories (orange arrow) often diverge from the global loss gradient due to unguided exploration. In contrast, the joint learning paradigm integrates tactile guidance and selective feedback (blue arrow) to better align the subject’s learning path with the optimal descent direction (dashed line), thereby achieving more efficient convergence toward the global optimum.

Experimental paradigm and BCI protocol

Each experimental session comprised a series of discrete motor imagery (MI) trials. Participants were seated in front of a computer monitor, wearing a 64-channel EEG cap (Neuroscan Quik-Cap) with their palms resting on tactile actuators positioned beneath each hand (Fig. 7a). EEG signals were recorded using SynAmps 2/RT amplifiers (Compumedics Neuroscan), with electrodes arranged in a modified international 10–20 layout, placing the reference electrode centrally between Cz and CPz. Impedances were reduced below 5 kΩ prior to each session. EEG data were sampled at 1000 Hz and notch-filtered at 60 Hz to eliminate power line interference. The digitized signals were streamed to the BCI2000 platform in 40 ms packets for real-time signal processing and BCI control.

All participants initially completed a BCI2000-based calibration run using a left/right MI task to establish baseline decoding accuracy. They then participated in 4 to 6 sessions. The first two sessions involved left/right MI tasks only, the third session introduced a combination of left/right and up/down targets, and the remaining sessions involved full 2D control. Each session comprised six runs: (1) a calibration run without feedback, (2) a test run with visual feedback but no tactile stimulation, (3–5) three training runs using the assigned paradigm, and (6) a post-training test run. A 5-min rest was provided prior to the final run. Test runs included half the number of trials as calibration and training runs (60/30 for L/R and 64/32 for 2D; see Supplementary Fig. 14a, b for trial details).

Each MI trial followed a fixed structure. A central fixation appeared for 2 s, followed by a directional cue indicating the MI target. Participants performed kinesthetic imagery of the corresponding limb with the cursor feedback for 5 s, followed by a 2 s rest (Supplementary Fig. 14c). Left/right cues instructed participants to imagine left or right hand movement to move the cursor horizontally. For upward movement, participants were instructed to imagine simultaneous bilateral hand movement. Downward movement corresponded to resting and not performing MI. Visual cursor feedback was provided during all runs except calibration.

In the joint learning group, trials were organized in pairwise structures, with each pair sharing the same directional cue. If the first trial of a pair achieved online accuracy above a pre-defined threshold, the second trial displayed the “copy” instruction along with the direction cue, prompting the participant to replicate the same mental strategy. If the first trial fell below the threshold, the second trial displayed the “new” instruction and delivered tactile stimulation to the corresponding hand.

In the tactile control group, trials were also presented in direction-matched pairs. However, no “copy/new” cues were provided. Instead, tactile stimulation was delivered in every trial, regardless of prior performance. This design isolated the effects of tactile enhancement without contingent reinforcement or strategic feedback.

The BCI2000 control group received no tactile stimulation or strategic cues across all runs. The number of trials and session structures were identical to those in the joint learning group, enabling direct comparison of learning outcomes under passive decoder conditions. For long-term experiments, the BCI2000 control group received feedback calculated by EEGNet, using the same EEGNet configuration as that used in the tactile control group and the EEGNet control group.

In the EEGNet control group, trials were presented in a non-pairwise, randomized order, with no tactile stimulation or “copy/new” instructional cues. Decoder outputs were generated using the same run-wise update schedule as in the joint learning and tactile control group. This additional control condition was designed to isolate the influence of decoder architecture and adaptation from the sensory-guided joint learning framework by removing tactile reinforcement and pairwise strategic structure while preserving the EEGNet decoder.

For training parameters of the EEGNet, model optimization was performed using mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with the Adam optimizer. The initial learning rate was set to 1 × 10−3, with default momentum parameters (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999). Binary cross-entropy loss was used as the base objective for classification. Training was conducted for a maximum of 300 epochs with early stopping based on training loss convergence to prevent overfitting. For subsequent sessions, weights were initialized from the model trained in the immediately preceding session, enabling gradual decoder adaptation over time. In contrast, for the long-term experiments, both the weighted EEGNet and the conventional EEGNet were reinitialized and trained from scratch in each session, without carrying over weights from previous sessions. No online fine-tuning was performed during test runs; all model updates occurred offline between runs.

A fixed accuracy threshold of 70% was used across all participants. This value was selected a priori based on established conventions in the BCI literature, where approximately 70% classification accuracy is commonly considered indicative of reliable and usable BCI control10,66. Applying a uniform threshold across participants ensured consistency of the training protocol and avoided subject-specific tuning that could confound group-level comparisons.

Performance metrics and analysis methods

To quantify both BCI control performance and the underlying learning processes, we employed a combination of behavioral, algorithmic, and neurophysiological metrics. Online accuracy, the primary behavioral metric, was computed for each trial as the mean classification accuracy across all 1000 ms sliding windows (stepped by 40 ms) within the 5 s motor imagery period, which is a common metric for continuous tasks29,66,67. At each window, the classifier output determined whether the cursor moved toward the cued target direction; the online accuracy was then calculated as the proportion of correct movements over the total number of movements in that trial. This method has two advantages: the chance level is analytically defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/{N}_{{classes}}$$\end{document}1/Nclasses,where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{{classes}}$$\end{document}Nclasses is the number of possible target directions; and it produces a continuous accuracy score for each trial, enabling fine-grained comparisons between trials.

To complement this continuous measure, we also employed two discrete, run-level performance metrics widely used in BCI2000-based experiments42. The percent valid correct (PVC) was defined as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${PVC}=\frac{{hits}}{{hits}+{misses}}$$\end{document}PVC=hitshits+misseswhere a hit denotes the cursor reaching the correct target at the end of the trial, and a miss denotes the cursor hitting an incorrect target. Like online accuracy, the chance level for PVC and fPTC is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/{N}_{{classes}}$$\end{document}1/Nclasses, but these discrete measures directly reflect the proportion of successfully completed trials, providing a more task-completion–oriented performance assessment.

The system performance was further assessed using an online test loss. Rather than reflecting training dynamics, this metric quantifies how well the decoder generalizes to unseen streaming data during online operation. Specifically, we computed the mean cross-entropy loss over all prediction windows within a run, using the continuously acquired EEG signals as test input (see Supplementary Methods 1). A lower online test loss indicates both improved decoders fitting to the participant’s evolving neural patterns and reduced ambiguity in the extracted features. Tracking this loss across runs, therefore, captures the combined learning effects of both the human user and the adaptive algorithm.

To quantify co-adaptation between the user and the decoder, we employed a vector-based alignment metric across consecutive training runs. For each subject and session, we computed the signal change vector as the difference in average neural signal vectors between two successive runs. In parallel, we obtained the averaged decoder gradient vector by feeding the neural signal from the earlier run into the trained network. The alignment between these two vectors was then characterized using projection length (magnitude of the dot product) and cosine similarity, reflecting not only the directional agreement but also the extent to which neural changes aligned with the model’s most informative dimensions (see Supplementary Methods 2). To visualize the adaptation process, neural features were reduced to two dimensions using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and plotted sequentially across runs, allowing the evolution of neural representations to be tracked over time.

For neurophysiological analysis, event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) and event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) were quantified in the alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) frequency bands from electrodes C3 and C4. To interpret the learned features of the neural network models, we employed activation and gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)72,73. Activation refers to the weighted sum of the input values and their corresponding network weights, representing how strongly a given feature contributes to a neuron’s response. While Grad-CAM highlights the spatial and spectral regions most influential for the decoder’s decision. In our implementation, Grad-CAM was computed from the gradients of class scores with respect to convolutional feature maps, and visualized on the EEG topography to reveal physiologically meaningful features. A full description of the equations and derivations of the procedure is provided in Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Methods 3.

All EEG preprocessing and decoder analyses were performed in Python, while behavioral metrics and ERD analyses were conducted in MATLAB and EEGLAB75.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, USA). Data are reported as mean ± s.e.m. or s.d., as specified in the figure legends. Given the relatively modest sample sizes, formal normality tests were not employed, as such tests have limited statistical power under small-sample conditions. Instead, non-parametric tests were adopted as a conservative default for single-factor and pairwise comparisons.

For comparisons involving one factor or pairwise contrasts, we used Wilcoxon-based non-parametric tests (signed-rank or rank-sum, as appropriate). Effect sizes for all non-parametric comparisons were quantified using the rank-based effect size r, computed as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{N}}\,,$$\end{document}r=ZN,where Z denotes the standardized test statistic and N the number of observations used in the corresponding test. This measure provides a distribution-free estimate of effect magnitude and is applicable to both paired and unpaired non-parametric comparisons.

For analyses involving multiple factors or repeated measurements across conditions or sessions, one-way or two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were employed, with primary interest placed on interaction effects (e.g., group × session). Partial eta squared (ηp²) was reported as the effect size for ANOVA results. Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the sphericity assumption was violated.

To assess sample size adequacy for the primary learning-related effects, we additionally conducted a simulation-based power analysis tailored to the non-parametric statistical framework used in this study. Statistical power was estimated via Monte Carlo simulations based on the empirical data distributions observed across training sessions, rather than relying on parametric assumptions.

This combined approach allowed conservative inference for simple comparisons while preserving statistical interpretability for multi-factor learning effects. Post hoc pairwise tests were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 unless otherwise noted.
