TBX5-AS1 induces ER stress and suppresses lung cancer growth and tumor stemness via the miR-494-3p/ATF6 axis
Authors: Guoqin Wang, Chaojiang Fu, Changling Tu, Zhuangqing Yang, Wei Shi, Lijuan Zhang, Shufen Tan, Youguang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor stemness maintenance and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response have been strongly correlated with the progression of lung cancer (LC). Nevertheless, the role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in these processes remains incompletely understood. We screened LC-associated lncRNAs from the GEO database and validated the expression of TBX5-AS1 in clinical samples. Functional experiments were conducted to assess the biological effects of TBX5-AS1, and western blot was used to detect ER stress marker proteins. The interaction mechanism of the TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis was elucidated through dual-luciferase reporter assays, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), and pull-down experiments. Rescue experiments and a nude mouse xenograft model were employed to validate the functional outcomes. TBX5-AS1 was significantly downregulated in LC tissues and cell lines, and its low expression was associated with advanced tumor stages and poor patient prognosis. Overexpression of TBX5-AS1 markedly suppressed LC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and self-renewal while promoting the activation of the ER stress pathway. Mechanistically, TBX5-AS1 competitively binds to miR-494–3p, thereby relieving its transcriptional repression of Activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). Rescue experiments demonstrated that miR-494–3p overexpression reversed the regulatory effects of TBX5-AS1 on tumor malignant phenotype and ER stress. In vivo experiments further confirmed that TBX5-AS1 overexpression significantly inhibited tumor growth, accompanied by upregulation of ATF6 and ER stress-related proteins. TBX5-AS1 functioned as a tumor-suppressive lncRNA by activating ER stress signaling through the miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis, thereby inhibiting LC growth and tumor stemness.Keywords: TBX5-AS1, ER stress, Tumor stemness, MiR-494–3p, ATF6

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer (LC) persists as the predominant etiology of malignancy-associated mortality, demonstrating a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate below 20%. Its high invasiveness and drug resistance are closely linked to cancer stemness.1 Although targeted therapies and immunotherapies have significantly improved the prognosis of some patients by inhibiting epidermal growth factor receptor, Anaplastic lymphoma kinase, or programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death ligand 1 pathways, the persistence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) continues to pose a major challenge in clinical management.2 Recently, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress has garnered attention due to its dual role in regulating tumor cell survival and apoptosis. Studies have shown that hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and chemotherapeutic agents in the tumor microenvironment can activate ER stress-unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling (eg, PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6 branches), driving CSC maintenance or apoptosis.3 Mild to moderate ER stress promotes cellular adaptation to stress through the UPR, while excessive or sustained ER stress triggers apoptotic signaling.4 However, the dynamic interplay between ER stress and tumor stemness, as well as the upstream regulatory networks, remains to be fully explored.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) serve as pivotal regulators of gene expression, modulating diverse biological pathways. The competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) theory posits that lncRNAs can sequester miRNAs to indirectly regulate mRNA targets, a mechanism extensively documented in oncogenesis.5 For instance, LINC00680 facilitates esophageal squamous cell carcinoma progression by sponging miR-423–5p to elevate PAK6 expression.6 Analogously, lnc-PLA2G4A-4 accelerates hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation and metastasis through the miR-23b-3p/VCAN signaling axis.7 Contemporary research further implicates lncRNAs in ER stress modulation across malignancies. For instance, GAS5 lncRNA activates the UPR via GRP78 binding, subsequently triggering caspase-9 and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) signaling to induce HepG2 cell apoptosis.8 SNHG1 lncRNA attenuates ER stress-mediated apoptosis through BIRC3 upregulation, thereby promoting glioma progression.9 Emerging evidence suggests lncRNAs may coordinate the equilibrium between ER stress and cancer stemness. For instance, LINC01534 suppresses ER stress to preserve cancer stemness in colorectal carcinoma.10 TBX5-AS1 is an antisense lncRNA located adjacent to the TBX5 gene locus. To date, its clinical relevance has been primarily reported in LC. Notably, several independent studies analyzing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset have consistently shown that low expression of TBX5-AS1 is significantly associated with poor prognosis in LC patients,11, 12, 13 suggesting that TBX5-AS1 may serve as a potential tumor suppressor and prognostic biomarker in this context. However, the functional role and underlying molecular mechanisms of TBX5-AS1 in LC remain largely uncharacterized.

Activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) is a transmembrane glycoprotein and transcription activator that plays a role in initiating the UPR signaling pathway during ER stress.14 Current studies reveal miRNA-mediated ATF6 regulation: miR-16–5p exacerbates ER stress and oxidative damage in cardiomyocytes via ATF6 targeting,15 whereas miR-340–5p modulates ER stress by dual regulation of PERK and ATF6 in oral squamous cell carcinoma.16 These findings collectively highlight the miRNA-ATF6 axis as an important regulatory node in tumor ER stress. However, how lncRNAs modulate this axis through ceRNA mechanisms to influence LC stemness and growth remains unclear. Furthermore, existing studies have predominantly focused on the pro-survival functions of ER stress, while its potential role in influencing tumor stemness remains underexplored. For instance, in breast cancer, ER stress-UPR plays an important role in stemness maintenance.3 However, whether ATF6 is involved in this process and whether this phenomenon exists in LC progression remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we identified TBX5-AS1 as significantly downregulated in LC tissues. Further experiments revealed that TBX5-AS1 overexpression competitively bound to miR-494–3p, relieving its repression of ATF6 and activating ER stress pathways, thereby significantly impairing tumor cell self-renewal. Our findings highlighted the biological role of TBX5-AS1 in LC and its molecular mechanism of regulating ER stress and tumor stemness through the miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis.

Results

TBX5-AS1 was downregulated in LC and correlated with tumor progression and poor prognosis

Through bioinformatics analysis of the GSE31210 dataset,12 several lncRNAs associated with ER stress were identified. Among them, TBX5-AS1 was remarkably downregulated in LC tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure 1a). Further validation using the UALCAN database confirmed that the level of TBX5-AS1 was notably lower in LC tissues than in normal lung tissues (Figure 1b). Moreover, its downregulation was correlated with tumor stage (Figure 1c). Survival analysis revealed that high TBX5-AS1 expression was associated with improved survival in LC patients (Figure 1d). To confirm these findings, we analyzed 47 surgically resected LC specimens and four carcinoma cell lines. Consistent with the bioinformatics data, TBX5-AS1 expression was noticeably reduced in LC tissues (Figure 1e) and cell lines (Figure 1f). Based on their characteristically low expression profiles, A549 and H1299 cell lines were subsequently selected for mechanistic exploration. Taken together, these results suggested that TBX5-AS1 was underexpressed in LC and that lower expression levels were associated with advanced tumor stage and poorer survival, supporting its potential function as a tumor suppressor during LC progression.

Overexpression of TBX5-AS1 inhibited LC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor stemness

To investigate the functional consequences of TBX5-AS1 dysregulation, we established TBX5-AS1 overexpression cell lines, with transfection efficiency confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure 2a). Functional assays demonstrated that TBX5-AS1 upregulation significantly attenuated cellular proliferation, as evidenced by CCK-8 viability (Figure 2b) and colony formation analysis (Figure 2c). The wound healing assay showed a significantly reduced wound closure rate at 24 h in the TBX5-AS1 overexpression group (Figure 2d), indicating inhibition of LC cell migration. Furthermore, Transwell invasion assay revealed a decreased number of cells penetrating through the Matrigel-coated membrane, demonstrating that TBX5-AS1 overexpression also suppresses LC cell invasion (Figure 2e). Notably, TBX5-AS1 expression was remarkably lower in tumor spheres compared to parental cells (Figure 2f), implying that its downregulation may be associated with the enrichment of CSCs. Subsequent evaluation of CSCs markers revealed that TBX5-AS1 overexpression significantly downregulated the expression of SOX2, CD44, and CD133 (Figure 2g). Moreover, it markedly reduced the number of tumor spheres formed in sphere-forming assays (Figure 2h). These results collectively suggested that TBX5-AS1 upregulation suppressed both the malignant phenotype and the stem-like properties of LC cells.

Overexpression of TBX5-AS1 enhanced ER stress to inhibit tumor progression

Given the potential link between TBX5-AS1 and ER stress suggested by our above findings, we further investigated the expression of key ER stress-related proteins in TBX5-AS1-overexpressing LC cells to validate this functional association. To induce endoplasmic ER stress in LC cells, we treated them with thapsigargin, a well-established pharmacological inducer of ER stress. Western blot analysis showed that thapsigargin significantly upregulated the expression of ER stress-related proteins (ATF4, CHOP, and GRP78), confirming its potent ER stress-inducing activity. Notably, overexpression of TBX5-AS1 similarly led to a marked increase in the levels of these stress markers, with an effect comparable to that of thapsigargin treatment. This indicates that TBX5-AS1 effectively activates the ER stress pathway in LC cells. Moreover, the upregulation of these proteins could be synergistically enhanced by Thapsigargin and TBX5-AS1 (Figure 3a and b), suggesting that TBX5-AS1 may mediate the enhancement of ER stress. To further validate whether the suppressive effects of TBX5-AS1 overexpression on the malignant behaviors of LC cells are dependent on the activation of ER stress, we treated the cells with 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA), a well-known chemical chaperone that alleviates ER stress by facilitating proper protein folding, reducing protein aggregation, and restoring ER function. Functional assays confirmed that under 4-PBA treatment, which suppresses ER stress, LC cells exhibited significantly enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor sphere formation. Notably, although TBX5-AS1 overexpression alone effectively suppressed these malignant phenotypes, this inhibitory effect was markedly attenuated in the presence of 4-PBA (Figure 3c-h). These results indicated that the tumor-suppressive function of TBX5-AS1 is largely dependent on its ability to activate ER stress. Therefore, TBX5-AS1 plays a critical role in suppressing LC progression by positively modulating the ER stress pathway.

TBX5-AS1 regulated ATF6 expression by competitively binding to miR-494-3p

Subcellular localization analysis revealed that TBX5-AS1 is predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm (Figure 4a), suggesting that it may function through cytoplasmic RNA regulatory mechanisms. Given that lncRNAs often act as ceRNAs in the cytoplasm by sequestering miRNAs, we hypothesized that TBX5-AS1 may regulate downstream genes through such a mechanism. Based on the TCGA database and literature,17 we obtained 6 miRNAs associated with lung adenocarcinoma overall survival (OS) and the ENCORI database was used to predict potential target miRNAs of TBX5-AS1, identifying two miRNAs (hsa-miR-4664–3p and hsa-miR-494–3p, Figure 4b). Bioinformatics analysis indicated that TBX5-AS1 and miR-494–3p had complementary binding sites at their 3′ ends, suggesting that TBX5-AS1 may regulate miR-494–3p through a ceRNA mechanism. We further hypothesized that its downstream pathway may involve ER stress-related factors. Target prediction analysis identified a complementary binding site for miR-494–3p within the 3′ untranslated region of ATF6. ATF6 is a key transcription factor in the ER stress response, whose mRNA is translated in the cytoplasm and subsequently activated to regulate the expression of multiple stress-responsive genes. Therefore, we speculated that TBX5-AS1, located in the cytoplasm, acts as a ceRNA by sponging miR-494–3p, thereby alleviating its inhibitory effect on ATF6 mRNA and promoting ATF6 expression, ultimately enhancing ER stress signaling.

To validate this regulatory axis, wild-type (wt) and mutant (mut) dual-luciferase reporter plasmids for TBX5-AS1 and ATF6 were constructed (Figure 4c). Co-transfection of the wt TBX5-AS1 reporter with miR-494–3p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity, whereas the mut showed no significant change, confirming specific binding between TBX5-AS1 and miR-494–3p (Figure 4d). Similar results were observed for ATF6 and miR-494–3p (Figure 4e). Notably, we detected relatively high basal expression of miR-494–3p in A549 and H1299 cells (Supplementary Figure S1A), and functional assays demonstrated that miR-494–3p overexpression promotes malignant behaviors, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness, supporting its oncogenic role in this context (Supplementary Figure S1C-H). RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays showed that TBX5-AS1, miR-494–3p, and ATF6 mRNA were more enriched in the RNA-induced silencing complex precipitated by anti-AGO2 antibody (Figure 4f). RNA pull-down assays further confirmed that TBX5-AS1 and ATF6 were specifically captured by biotin-labeled miR-494–3p (Figure 4g). Functionally, overexpression of TBX5-AS1 reduced miR-494–3p levels (Figure 4h), consistent with its role as a negative regulator. Moreover, miR-494–3p overexpression significantly suppressed ATF6 protein expression (Figure 4i), indicating that miR-494–3p directly targets ATF6. Together, these results support a regulatory axis in which TBX5-AS1 upregulates ATF6 by sequestering miR-494–3p as a molecular sponge, thereby enhancing the ER stress pathway.

TBX5-AS1 upregulation promoted ATF6 expression by sponging miR-494-3p, then affected the growth, metastasis, stemness, and ER stress in LC cells

To elucidate the functional role of the TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis in the progression of LC, we conducted co-transfection of miR-494–3p mimics in TBX5-AS1-overexpressing LC cells. Western blot analysis revealed that TBX5-AS1 overexpression significantly upregulated ATF6 protein expression, which was substantially attenuated by miR-494–3p mimic supplementation (Figure 5a). Rescue experiments demonstrated that miR-494–3p upregulation partially reversed the suppressive effects of oe-TBX5-AS1 on multiple oncogenic processes, including cellular proliferation (Figure 5b and c), migratory capacity (Figure 5d), invasive potential (Figure 5e), stemness marker expression (Figure 5f), and tumorosphere formation (Figure 5g). Additionally, significant downregulation of ER stress mediators (ATF4, CHOP, and GRP78) was observed in the oe-TBX5-AS1+miR-494–3p group compared to the oe-TBX5-AS1+miR-NC group (Figure 5h). These results further supported that TBX5-AS1 overexpression activated the ER stress pathway and suppressed tumor progression by sponging miR-494–3p.

Overexpressing TBX5-AS1 hindered tumor growth and induced ER stress in LC cells in vivo

To validate the in vivo function of TBX5-AS1, a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in nude mice was established. The results showed that both tumor volume and weight were measurably reduced in the LV-oe-TBX5-AS1 group compared to controls (Figure 6a and b), indicating that TBX5-AS1 overexpression inhibits tumor growth in vivo. Further analysis revealed markedly increased levels of TBX5-AS1 and ATF6 mRNA, as well as elevated ATF6 protein expression, in the LV-oe-TBX5-AS1 group. In contrast, miR-494–3p expression was significantly downregulated (Figure 6c and d), demonstrating that TBX5-AS1 suppresses miR-494–3p and promotes ATF6 expression in vivo. Furthermore, the protein levels of ER stress markers, including ATF4, CHOP, and GRP78, were prominently upregulated in the LV-oe-TBX5-AS1 group (Figure 6d), consistent with the in vitro findings. Taken together, these data indicated that TBX5-AS1 overexpression enhanced ER stress signaling through the miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis, thereby suppressing tumor growth in vivo.

Discussion

LncRNAs play critical regulatory roles in tumorigenesis and can function as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors in various cancers, including LC.18 TBX5-AS1, a novel cancer-associated lncRNA, is an antisense transcript of the TBX5 gene. Emerging evidence indicates that TBX5-AS1 is downregulated in LC tissues and is closely associated with poor patient prognosis. For instance, Xiong et al identified TBX5-AS1 as a key prognostic lncRNA in lung squamous cell carcinoma through RNA sequencing and survival analysis.19 Similarly, Cheng et al. integrated lung adenocarcinoma expression profiles from the TCGA dataset and performed Kaplan-Meier survival and Spearman correlation analyses, identifying TBX5-AS1 as a biomarker significantly associated with patient survival.13 Notably, however, experimental validation of TBX5-AS1 expression in LC remains limited-only one study has so far confirmed its low expression in both tumor tissues and cell lines.11 To substantiate these findings and provide additional experimental evidence, we systematically examined TBX5-AS1 expression in clinical LC samples and cell lines. Our results demonstrate that TBX5-AS1 is significantly downregulated in LC tissues, and its low expression is strongly correlated with adverse clinical outcomes, thus corroborating previous bioinformatics-based analyses.

Functional investigations revealed that overexpression of TBX5-AS1 noticeably attenuated malignant phenotypes, including cellular proliferation and migratory capacity in LC, aligning with existing reports documenting its oncosuppressive properties in non-small cell lung cancer.11 Notably, this study is the first to reveal that TBX5-AS1 may influence LC progression by regulating tumor stemness. CSCs represent a critical subpopulation driving tumor heterogeneity, recurrence, and therapy resistance,20 yet no previous reports have addressed whether TBX5-AS1 participates in the regulation of stemness. Our results showed that overexpression of TBX5-AS1 significantly reduces the expression of stem cell markers and impairs sphere-forming capacity, suggesting its important role in suppressing LC stemness. Bioinformatics analysis reveals that the tumor-suppressive effect of TBX5-AS1 is closely associated with activation of the ER stress pathway. Experimental data demonstrate that TBX5-AS1 overexpression upregulates ER stress markers (such as GRP78, ATF4, and CHOP), with effects similar to those induced by the ER stress agonist Thapsigargin. More importantly, inhibition of ER stress significantly enhances the proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness of LC cells, while overexpression of TBX5-AS1 partially reverses these effects, indicating that its tumor-suppressive function is dependent on ER stress activation. This finding expands the current understanding of TBX5-AS1's biological functions and suggests that it may suppress malignant phenotypes by inducing ER stress.

The ceRNA mechanism has been widely validated as an important way for lncRNAs to regulate tumor progression. For example, lncRNA-PNUTS modulates migratory and invasive capacities in A549 and NMuMG cell lines via specific miR-205 binding domain interactions.21 Similarly, lncARSR has been shown to enhance therapeutic resistance in renal malignancies through competitive combination of miR-34/miR-449, consequently activating oncogenic signaling through AXL and c-MET upregulation.22 At the molecular mechanism level, we focused on whether TBX5-AS1 regulates key miRNAs through the ceRNA mechanism. Bioinformatics prediction and experimental validation demonstrate that TBX5-AS1 acts as a molecular sponge to sequester miR-494–3p, thereby negatively regulating its expression. MiR-494–3p has been reported in multiple cancers as an oncogenic miRNA. For instance, in glioblastoma, miR-494–3p activates the PTEN/AKT signaling pathway to promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migratory capacity.23 In hepatocellular carcinoma, it enhances tumor growth and metastasis by downregulating BMAL1, thereby increasing GPAM-mediated lipid biosynthesis.24 Notably, miR-494–3p has also been demonstrated to exert oncogenic effects in LC. Du et al revealed that elevated expression of miR-494–3p in non-small cell lung cancer promotes cell migration and invasion by suppressing the SET/I2PP2A complex.25 Our findings further support these observations: overexpression of miR-494–3p significantly enhances the proliferative capacity, metastatic potential, and stemness properties of A549 and H1299 cells, thereby driving malignant progression.25, 26 Importantly, our results demonstrate that TBX5-AS1 negatively regulates miR-494–3p expression. Integrating bioinformatics prediction with experimental validation, we propose that TBX5-AS1 may function as a ceRNA by acting as a molecular sponge for miR-494–3p, thereby sequestering it and alleviating its repression of downstream target genes. This mechanism likely contributes to the tumor-suppressive role of TBX5-AS1. These findings uncover a novel TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p regulatory axis in LC progression, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying lung carcinogenesis.

Notably, among the potential downstream targets of miR-494–3p, ATF6 was predicted by bioinformatics and experimentally validated as a direct target gene. ATF6 is a key sensor of the unfolded protein response pathway and functions as a transcription factor during ER stress, regulating a series of genes involved in maintaining protein homeostasis. In recent years, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that ATF6 not only participates in tumor growth and invasion but also plays a critical role in modulating tumor cell subpopulations with stem-like properties and in reshaping the tumor microenvironment.3, 27 For instance, coordinated activation of ATF6 and XBP1 suppresses self-renewal capacity and malignant proliferation in colorectal carcinoma through PERK-mediated signaling cascades.28 Furthermore, the central role of ATF6 in regulating tumor-associated ER stress responses has been confirmed. A study by Amanda et al demonstrated that targeted inhibition of the ATF6-mediated ER stress pathway effectively blocks integrin-driven prostate cancer progression,29 highlighting ATF6 as a crucial node linking cellular stress responses to malignant tumor phenotypes. However, research on the functional roles and molecular mechanisms of ATF6 in LC remains relatively limited. In this study, we reveal for the first time that ATF6 plays a pivotal role in maintaining stem-like properties in LC. Mechanistically, TBX5-AS1 acts as a molecular sponge for miR-494–3p, thereby alleviating miR-494–3p-mediated repression of ATF6, enhancing ER stress responses, and ultimately suppressing tumor stemness. Based on these findings, we propose that the TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis constitutes a novel regulatory pathway that modulates LCr stemness and disease progression through the regulation of ER stress levels.

In summary, this study not only further validates the tumor-suppressive role of TBX5-AS1 in LC but also elucidates a previously uncharacterized molecular mechanism by which TBX5-AS1 regulates ER stress and tumor stemness via the TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis. This discovery functionally links the lncRNA-ceRNA regulatory network with the unfolded protein response signaling pathway, expanding the regulatory scope of non-coding RNAs in tumor stress responses, and provides potential new therapeutic targets and theoretical foundations for the precision treatment of LC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed a novel mechanism by which TBX5-AS1 enhanced the ER stress through the ceRNA-mediated miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis, thereby inhibiting LC growth and stemness. This discovery not only enriches the theoretical framework of non-coding RNA regulation in tumor stemness but also provides a theoretical basis for developing new therapeutic strategies targeting the ER stress pathway.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

This study was approved by the ethics committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Yunnan Cancer Hospital). LC patients who did not receive chemotherapy or radiotherapy were selected to be included in the scope of the trial. Adjacent non-tumor tissues and tumor tissues were collected from patients at the same time. All patients or their guardians signed informed consent. Experiments involving human tissue specimens complied with the declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture and transfection

HBE cells and four pulmonary carcinoma cell lines (A549, PC9, H23, and H1299) were procured from ATCC (USA). Cell cultures were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) under standard culture conditions (5% CO2, 37 ℃).

TBX5-AS1 was overexpressed using two complementary systems. For transient overexpression in vitro, a pcDNA3.1-based plasmid containing a 3655 bp insert corresponding to the reference transcript of TBX5-AS1 (RefSeq: NR_038440.1; oe-TBX5-AS1, OV288968, GenePharma, China) was used, with an empty vector (oe-NC) serving as the negative control. Although this plasmid was engineered to include a puromycin resistance gene, it was used exclusively for transient transfection in this study. For stable overexpression in vivo, lentiviral particles expressing TBX5-AS1 (LV-oe-TBX5-AS1, HL288968, GenePharma, China) were employed, with a control lentivirus (LV-oe-NC) as the matched negative control. miR-494–3p mimics (miR-494–3p) and their negative control (miR-NC) were also purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China).

For transient transfection experiments, 2×105 cells/well were plated in 6-well plates (Corning, USA) and transfected with 2 μg of oe-TBX5-AS1 or oe-vector using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent in vitro assays. For lentiviral infection, A549 cells were infected with LV-oe-TBX5-AS1 or LV-oe-NC in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). At 48 h post-infection, cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 2-3 weeks to establish stable overexpressing cell lines, which were used for in vivo experiments.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were prepared with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) and electrophoresed on 10% SDS-PAGE gels prior to electrotransfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following blocking with 5% non-fat milk, immunoblotting was performed with specific primary antibodies: ATF6 (ab203119, 1:500), CHOP (ab11419, 1:1000), GRP78 (ab230508, 1:100), ATF4 (ab31390, 1:1000), and GAPDH (ab9485, 1:2000; all from Abcam, UK). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech, China) were employed for chemiluminescent detection using ECL substrate (Millipore), with imaging conducted on a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from pulmonary tissues and cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by DNase I digestion (Takara, Japan) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. cDNA synthesis was performed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara), and quantitative PCR was carried out using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara). Gene expression quantification utilized the comparative Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method with primer sequences detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Subcellular localization assessment

Subcellular localization was determined using the PARISTM Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) for nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation. RNA from each fraction was purified with TRIzol (Invitrogen) and analyzed via RT-qPCR quantification.

CCK-8 assay

3×103 cells/well were cultured in 96-well plates (Corning). At indicated time points (0-72 h), cellular viability was assessed by supplementing 10 μL CCK-8 solution (Beyotime) followed by 2-hour incubation and absorbance measurement at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

To evaluate the proliferative potential of LC cells following genetic modulation, a clonogenic survival assay was performed. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well tissue culture plates at a density of 1000 cells per well 24 h after transfection and allowed to attach overnight under standard culture conditions. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh growth medium, and cells were maintained for an additional 14 days with periodic medium replenishment every 3-4 days to ensure nutrient availability. Upon completion of the incubation period, colonies were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 20 min. Excess dye was removed by thorough washing with distilled water. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were photographed using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus IX73) and manually counted.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (5×105 cells/well) and scratched with a 200 μL pipette tip. After washing with PBS, cells were cultured in medium containing 2% FBS. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus IX73), and wound width was measured using ImageJ.

Transwell invasion analysis

Matrigel-precoated Transwell chambers (Corning) were hydrated with 50 μL basal matrix for 1 h prior to use. Cellular suspensions (5×105 cells/mL) were introduced into the upper compartment, while the lower chamber received 600 μL chemotactic medium containing 20% FBS. Following 24 h incubation at 37 °C, non-migratory cells were removed from the upper membrane surface. Fixed specimens were processed with 4% paraformaldehyde and counterstained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich), with invasive cells quantified via light microscopy.

Tumorsphere formation assay

Dissociated single cells (1×103 cells/mL) were maintained in serum-free DMEM/F12 (Gibco) containing 20 ng/mL EGF, 10 ng/mL bFGF, and B27 (Invitrogen), using ultra-low adhesion culture plates (Corning). After 7 days of suspension culture, tumorspheres were counted using phase-contrast microscopy (Leica DMi8, Germany).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The 3' untranslated region sequences of TBX5-AS1/ATF6 (wt and mut variants) were cloned into pmirGLO dual-reporter vectors (Promega, USA). Transient co-transfection was performed in pulmonary carcinoma cells using 100 ng reporter plasmids combined with 50 nM miR-494–3p mimics (RiboBio) or control oligonucleotides. Relative luciferase activity was quantified 48 h post-transfection using a dual-detection kit (Beyotime), with Renilla luciferase serving as the internal normalization control.

RIP

RIP experiments were conducted using magnetic bead-conjugated anti-Ago2 (ab186733, Abcam) or isotype-matched IgG (ab172730, Abcam). Cellular lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation with antibody-coated beads (Thermo Fisher), followed by RNA isolation and subsequent quantification via RT-qPCR.

In vivo experiment

Female BALB/c nude mice (6-week-old, Vital River, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions and randomized into experimental cohorts (n = 5). Stable TBX5-AS1-overexpressing or control LC cells (5×106 cells/mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank. Tumor growth was monitored at 3-day intervals. Tumor specimens were harvested at day 21 for gravimetric analysis and molecular characterization of TBX5-AS1/miR-494–3p/ATF6 axis and ER stress markers.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup differences were analyzed by unpaired Student's t test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison correction (multi-group comparisons). For bioinformatics analysis of clinical data, gene expression profiles and their associations with clinicopathological features were retrieved from the UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu). Briefly, differences in TBX5-AS1 expression between LC and normal tissues were assessed using Student’s t test as implemented by UALCAN, while associations with tumor stage were evaluated by one-way ANOVA to determine significant trends across ordinal clinical stages. Survival distributions were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier methodology with log-rank testing. Experimental reproducibility was confirmed through triplicate independent experiments with three technical replicates per condition. Statistical significance threshold was established at P < 0.05 (GraphPad Prism 9.0, USA).
