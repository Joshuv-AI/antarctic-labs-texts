Angio planewave ultrasensitive imaging (Angio PLUS) as an innovative technique in depicting vascularity of median nerve: a prospective observational study
Authors: Yingying Li, Cheng Zhang, Yukun Luo, Mingbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsAssessment of median nerve (MN) microvascularity is critical for early diagnosis of neuropathy, yet conventional techniques like color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) lack sensitivity for small vessels, while contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is invasive. Angio Planewave Ultrasensitive imaging (AP), a novel non-invasive modality, offers high-resolution microvascular visualization, but its application in MN vascularity remains unexplored.ObjectivesTo determine the feasibility of Angio Planewave Ultrasensitive imaging (AP) in comparison with color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) for detection of median nerve (MN) vascularity in healthy volunteers and to explore the correlation between the blood flow distribution of MN on AP and the clinical parameters.Methods30 healthy volunteers were prospectively enrolled in our study. CDFI and AP were performed to evaluate the vascularity of MN in the wrist level and the mid-forearm level. MN vascularity using Adler’s grading on CDFI and AP were compared, and a new classification of vascular distribution was proposed. Two radiologists performed image review for evaluation of intra- and interobserver agreements. The new vascular types in the wrist level and the mid-forearm level were compared. The correlation of clinical parameters and the new vascular types were analyzed.ResultsAP was more sensitive in displaying the vascularity of MN compared with CDFI (p < 0.0001), presenting excellent intra- and inter-observer agreements (k > 0.90) based on the new vascular types. Type I and type II were most frequently detected in the wrist level and the mid-forearm level respectively. There was a weak correlation between the cross-sectional area and vascular distribution at the wrist level and the mid-forearm level (r = 0.290, r = 0.304 respectively, both p < 0.05).ConclusionsAP demonstrated superior sensitivity in visualizing MN vascularity compared to CDFI, with excellent intra- and inter-observer reproducibility using the proposed vascular classification. These findings provide a critical technical foundation for future studies exploring vascular changes in neuropathies and their correlation with clinical outcomes.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02326-y.

Introduction

Peripheral neuropathy is one of the most prevalent neurologic conditions encountered by physicians of all specialties and the prevalence of peripheral neuropathy in the general population is 2.4% [1]. With surging numbers of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) patients throughout the world, peripheral neuropathy concerns are becoming more apparent with an involvement up to 8.9% of COVID-19 patients [2]. Peripheral nerves are supplied by a rich anastomotic system of epineural and endoneural blood vessels [3, 4]. The microvascular system of peripheral nerves comprises two interconnected networks: extrinsic and intrinsic. The extrinsic system consists of arteries, arterioles, and veins within the epi-fascicular and inter-fascicular epineurium, forming a highly branched, anastomotic vascular plexus. In contrast, the intrinsic system comprises longitudinal endoneurial capillaries. These systems are interconnected via trans-perineurial vessels, which originate from the extrinsic plexus, traverse the perineurium, and supply the endoneurial compartment. Notably, the blood–nerve barrier is maintained by two specialized structures: tight junctions between perineurial cells (isolating fascicles from the inter-fascicular environment) and non-fenestrated endothelial cells in endoneurial capillaries (preventing plasma-to-nerve substance transfer). Disruption of this barrier or microvascular architecture is implicated in neuropathies, such as ischemic damage in diabetes or compression-induced venous congestion [5]. The changes of blood perfusion of the peripheral nervous system are closely relevant to nerve damage and lose of neurological function [6]. For example, the histopathology of diabetic neuropathy is due to ischemic nerve damage caused by a microvasculitis [7, 8], the pathophysiology of compression neuropathies is venous congestion with accompanying nerve edema in early stages and impairment of arterial supply in advance stages [9, 10]. Vascular disturbance of nerves may occur before nerve damage substantial enough to be detected by imaging modalities. Therefore, early detection of vascular disturbance of peripheral nerves is important especially in cases that may necessitate surgery. Imaging may help guide treatment decision in patients through diagnosis of site and changes of blood perfusion of peripheral nerve. Meanwhile, awareness and accurate interpretation of imaging features of normal peripheral nerves is crucial for radiologists to optimal contribute to patient diagnosis, medical decision making, rehabilitative care, and patient counseling.

Neuromuscular ultrasound (US), a highly sensitive technique, has become the first-line imaging modality for the evaluation of peripheral nerve diseases including median nerves (MN) diseases [11–14]. Routine high-frequency US enabled morphological assessment including the localization, continuity, and echogenicity of MN. Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) and superb microvascular imaging (SMI) could detect blood flows, however, CDFI has not reached a sufficient level of performance to image microvascularization in small vessels and intraneural blood flow has never been demonstrated by CDFI in healthy subjects [15, 16], and SMI has been shown to be sensitive to display the blood flow only in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) [17, 18]. Contrast enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is a feasible modality which can represent microvascular perfusion of MNs [19, 20]. However, it still has disadvantages, such as adverse response, high cost, and patients who are allergic to the components of the contrast agent cannot perform this examination. Therefore, a safe, feasible, non-invasive and reliable technique is highly desired for the demonstration of intraneural blood flow of MN in clinical practice.

Angio Planewave Ultrasensitive imaging (AP) is a non-invasive, contrast-free technique available on the Aixplorer ultrasound system (Supersonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) which could provide more detailed information for better detecting and imaging slow blood flow distribution with a minimum diameter of 50 μm for the microvessels [21]. AP relies on the unfocused or plane waves and 3-dimensional wall filtering techniques to enhance sensitivity and improve resolution which creates a new level of performance in microvascularization imaging [21, 22]. Recently, several studies have demonstrated the superior ability of this technique for the detection of micro-vascularization in breast masses and endoleaks after endovascular aneurysm repair [23, 24]. However, to our knowledge, there has been no published article on the application of AP in detecting the vascular distribution of MN.

Therefore, the purpose of our study was to determine the feasibility of AP in comparison with CDFI for the detection of MN vascularity and to explore the vascular distribution pattern of MN in healthy participants.

Materials and methods

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of our Hospital (No. S2020-504-01), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Study population

Participants were prospectively enrolled in this study from March 2019 to April 2019. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) healthy volunteers without neurological disease (including central and peripheral nerves); (ii) no clinical manifestations associated with neurological diseases. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients with median neuropathy or other neurological diseases; (ii) patients with medical history of upper limb fractures; (iii) patients with other diseases that may cause neuropathy (diabetes, chronic renal failure, etc.). A total of 30 healthy volunteers (15 men and 15 women; mean age, 32 years; range, 23–44 years) were included. Clinical and demographic features, including age, weight, height, body mass index, and sex were registered.

Data acquisition

The Aixplorer US system (AnigoPLUS, SuperSonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) equipped with a linear-array transducer SL10-2 (frequency range of 2 to 10) was applied in our study. Image acquisition was performed by one sonographer with over 10 years of experience in musculoskeletal US. Our sonographic protocol for median nerve assessment at the wrist and mid-forearm levels aligns with standardized guidelines for nerve imaging, ensuring consistent transducer positioning, anatomical landmarks, and measurement techniques [25].

All participants underwent B-mode US, CDFI, and AP. All participants were examined in the supine position on the examination couch with their arms fully exposed and completely relaxed. A gel standoff pad (Sien Science and techonology Co., FoShan, China) was placed on the skin surface to provide a better nerve image at a greater depth. The transducer was placed lightly on the gel pad perpendicular to the skin surface, and the participants were asked to hold their breath during image acquisition. The MN was observed at two levels in each arm, the wrist level and the mid-forearm level (Fig. 1). Images of both left and right MN were collected.

The MN was initially imaged in transverse and longitudinal planes using B-mode US. The skin-to-MN depth and the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the MN were determined in the transverse images at the wrist level and the mid-forearm level. On the axial view of the MN, the CSA was automatically calculated by tracing a continuous line of MN.

MN vascularity was assessed using CDFI and AP at the same level where the gray-scale images were taken. For each examination part, the region of interest (ROI) was drawn, containing the MN and the surrounding tissue. CDFI gain was optimized by increasing gain until noise appeared and then reduced just enough to suppress the noise. The AP scale was adjusted to 2.0 cm/s, medium wall filter, medium frame rate, and flash suppression mode. The blood flow gain was appropriate adjusted to maximize the vessels signals while minimizing artifacts.

Image interpretation and evaluation

Two radiologists with over 4 years’ experience of musculoskeletal US independently interpreted and evaluated the CDFI and AP images.

Adler’s grading [26] was used for the classification of the blood flow of MN on CDFI and AP. Grade 0: no blood flow; Grade I: 1 ~ 2 dotted or thin rod-shaped blood flow; Grade II: 3 ~ 4 dotted or 1 complete blood flow; Grade III: more than 5 dotted or 2 complete blood flow.

According to the vascular distribution characteristics of MN, we established a new blood flow classification of MN (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Type I: blood flow signal was detected only in the epineurium; Type II: blood flow signal was displayed in the epineurium and endoneurium; Type III: a major blood vessel was detected in epineurium of MN; Type IV: two or more large blood vessels or trans-perineurial vessels were detected in MN.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and range. The Mann-Whitney test was used to evaluate differences in continuous data. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used for the comparison of Adler’s grading on CDFI and AP. Cohen’s Weighted Kappa statistic was used to analyze the intra- and inter-observer agreement in terms of new blood flow types on AP. We used McHugh’s [25] cutoffs to determine the levels of k agreement: 0.60–0.79, moderate; 0.80–0.90, strong; >0.90, almost perfect. The Mann-Whitney test was applied in the comparison of the distribution of new blood flow types on AP between the wrist level and the mid-forearm level. Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for the comparison of clinical parameters (including age, sex, height, weight, BMI, skin-to-MN depth, and CSA of MN) among four blood flow types on AP. Correlations between clinical parameters and AP types were analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficients. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of new blood flow type distribution on AP in different levels

The frequency distribution of the new blood flow types on AP in the wrist level and the mid-forearm level are presented in Table 5. Type I was detected more frequently in the wrist level (85%) than in the mid-forearm level (1.6%), while type II, type III, and type IV were detected more frequently in the mid-forearm level than in the wrist level (76.7% vs. 11.7%, 16.7% vs. 3.3%, and 5% vs. 0%, respectively; p < 0.0001, Fig. 5).

Angio PLUS demonstrated distinct visualization of the MN microvascular architecture. Type I (epineurial vessels) primarily displayed longitudinally oriented vascular signals within the extrinsic epineurial plexus, consistent with larger extrinsic vessels. Type II (epineurial and endoneurial vessels) revealed finer, longitudinally aligned endoneurial capillaries within the intrinsic system, alongside epineurial signals. Type III (major epineurial vessel) highlighted prominent extrinsic vessels coursing along the nerve surface. Type IV (multiple large or trans-perineurial vessels) captured obliquely oriented trans-perineurial vessels bridging the extrinsic and intrinsic systems, as well as branching endoneurial microvessels. These patterns align with the anatomical hierarchy of MN vascularization, where extrinsic epineurial vessels anastomose with intrinsic endoneurial capillaries via trans-perineurial connections, as described in recent literature [5, 27].

Correlation between clinical parameters and new blood types on AP

No significant difference was observed in age, sex, height, weight, BMI, skin-to-MN depth, and CSA of MN among four blood flow types on AP. There was a weak correlation between the CSA of MN and new blood flow types on AP at the wrist level and the mid-forearm level (r = 0.290, r = 0.304 respectively, both p < 0.05). However, no correlation was found between the age, sex, height, weight, BMI, and skin-to-MN depth and blood flow types on AP.

Discussion

The present study was the first to evaluate the feasibility of AP in detection of vascular patterns of MN in healthy subjects, and we demonstrate several findings. First, the Adler’s grading of MN on AP was significantly higher than that on CDFI. Second, a new blood flow classification of MN was proposed and the intra- and inter-observer reliability of AP for the classification were all excellent. Third, type II, III, and IV were more frequently observed in the mid-forearm level than in the wrist level and the CSA of MN was correlated with new type although weakly. These results suggest that AP was more sensitive in detecting intraneural blood flow of MN compared with CDFI in healthy volunteers, and it could demonstrate the blood perfusion consistent with MNs’ anatomical characteristics with good reliability. Therefore, AP could be a useful imaging modality in providing information of vascularity disturbance for early diagnosis and, potentially, in monitoring the disease activity related with MNs.

It was evident that evaluation of perfusion of the peripheral nervous system is relevant to a wide array of human diseases. The blood supply to the peripheral nerve has a unique relationship to nerve anatomy and distinct physiology. Despite its potential importance, imaging examination techniques of nerve blood flow are not yet available. Ultrasound examination could easily provide a morphologic view and offer macroscopic estimates of flow based on identifying doppler signal associated with the nerve trunks. However, these recordings are largely limited to identifying hyperemia, given the low signals available from normal nerve. In this study, we explored the ability of AP for the detection of intraneural blood flow of MN in healthy subjects and compared with CDFI. Results showed high sensitivity of AP and superiority over CDFI. Most MNs showed no blood flow signals on CDFI (90.8%, 109/120) while all MNs displayed blood flow signals on AP. CDFI provides real-time data for evaluating vascular signals from vessels. Nevertheless, this method has some crucial limitation, which is its inability to image small vessel flow (below 100 micrometers) and detect the presence of microvascularization [21]. AP is based on Doppler technique and has been improved. It can detect and visualize slow blood flow in microvessels based on unfocused or plan waves and 3-dimensional wall filtering, enhancing sensitivity and resolution and therefore allows for a new level of performance in microvascularization imaging [21, 22]. We observed that AP offered better performance than CDFI for intraneural vascular of MN using Adler’s grading and further verified the superiority in imaging microvascularization in small vessels.

Several studies have reported that SMI is a recent advanced imaging method that demonstrates the diagnostic value for CTS [17, 18] and showed that SMI is more sensitive to evaluate the vascularity of the MN in CTS patients than CDFI. However, Chen et al. showed that in healthy volunteers, no statistical significance was observed in the blood flow display ratio of CDFI and SMI [17], which indicated that limited value of SMI in evaluating intraneural blood flow of MNs. Ali et al. reported that the vascular images obtained from SMI correlated substantially with the severity of CTS [18], which further illustrated that SMI is more valuable in hypervascularization diseases. CEUS has been used extensively in situations in which vascular circulation is important, and it could be considered as a valid modality for detection of hyperemic area. To the best of our knowledge, there has been only 1 clinical study of CEUS in detection of intraneural blood flow of MNs. Volz et al. represented that CEUS was a feasible method to image MN vascularity [19]. Nevertheless, CEUS has a more complicated study protocol than conventional US, which requires a longer examination time and additional skills. Before CEUS is performed, patients must be injected with contrast agents, which remains a disadvantage of invasiveness. Furthermore, lack of research on performance of CEUS for displaying MN vascularity makes it imperative that further studies in this area are still needed.

In this study, we proposed a new classification of intraneural blood flow distribution based on the anatomical characteristics of the MN vascular systems and AP demonstrated excellent intra- and inter-observer reliability. The MN has a complex vascular system including extrinsic and intrinsic microvascular systems. The extrinsic vessels stem from nearby arteries and connect to the epineurium, then divide, and become the intrinsic system including epineural, perineurial, and endoneurial layers [27]. The epineural vessels are largest and outermost layer of the vascular system. Inside the epineurium, the vessels of perineurial and endoneurial layers are gradually tapered. Therefore, four types of the intraneural vascular distribution according to the display of epineural and perineurial blood flow were built which is consistent with the blood supply features of MNs. Results showed that at both levels of the MNs including the wrist level and the mid-forearm level, the intra- and inter-observer reliability of blood flow classification on AP were excellent. It indicated that blood perfusion of MNs displayed by AP was reliable and reproducible, which could not be affected by different observers. It was very important because in the clinical practice, it was almost impossible to detect intraneural blood flow using CDFI or SMI in healthy subjects. Moreover, the propose of blood flow classification of normal MNs on AP laid the foundation and could facilitate revealing of the underlying pathological mechanism of MN diseases. Therefore, the comprehensive and accurate demonstration of blood perfusion in the nerves still needed to be carefully interpretation. The classification of blood flow distribution on AP should be standardized and clarified.

Our study showed that the blood flow type II, III, and IV were more frequently observed in the mid-forearm level than in the wrist level of MNs, indicating that MNs in the mid-forearm level could have more blood perfusion than those in the wrist level. The main reason may be the anatomic features of the complex vascular system of MNs as follows: (1) the extrinsic vessels stem connected with the epineurium of MNs is stemmed from nearby arteries in the surrounding connective tissues and muscles [28] which is more abundant at the mid-forearm level than that in the wrist level; (2) the caliber of vessels gradually tapers as the vessels go along from the proximal mid-forearm to the wrist and give rise to the microvessel branches in the perineurial and endoneurial layers [28] which may lead the blood flow signal more difficult to detect. Therefore, it is reasonable that the vascular perfusion is relatively less in the wrist level than in the mid-forearm level on AP. Furthermore, our results showed that weak linear correlation between MN intraneural blood flow with CSA. The possible reason could be the larger the MNs, the more intraneural microvessels. Based on these findings, AP is a feasible method for the detection of normal vascularity of MNs and it could be considered as a promising modality for diagnosis of MNs diseases providing functional information. With development, careful nerve blood flow analysis may present new insights into nerve disorders.

The results of this study highlight the potential of Angio PLUS as a transformative tool for evaluating median nerve (MN) vascularity in clinical practice. By demonstrating superior sensitivity over conventional CDFI in detecting intraneural microvascular signals, Angio PLUS enables non-invasive visualization of epineurial, perineurial, and trans-perineurial vascular patterns in healthy individuals. This capability is critical for early detection of vascular disturbances in conditions such as diabetic neuropathy, carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), or post-traumatic nerve injury, where microvascular changes often precede structural nerve damage. The excellent intra- and inter-observer reliability of our proposed vascular classification further supports its reproducibility in diverse clinical settings, facilitating standardized assessments across institutions. The observed differences in vascular distribution between wrist and mid-forearm levels may aid in localizing pathological changes, while the weak correlation between CSA and vascular types suggests that structural and functional alterations in nerves may coexist, warranting further investigation. Ultimately, Angio PLUS could enhance diagnostic accuracy, guide therapeutic decisions (e.g., timing of surgical intervention), and serve as a biomarker for monitoring treatment efficacy in neurovascular disorders. These advancements align with emerging efforts to integrate functional microvascular imaging into neuromuscular diagnostics, as highlighted in the literature on nerve vascularization [5].

There were several limitations to this study. First, the small sample size of healthy volunteers restricts the generalizability of the findings. Thus, further studies with large number of participants are needed to validate the final utility of AP for evaluating the blood perfusion of MNs. Second, while AP demonstrated high sensitivity in detecting vascularity in normal nerves, its diagnostic value in pathological conditions—such as metabolic (e.g., diabetic neuropathy), neoplastic, or compressive neuropathies—remains unvalidated. These pathologies may alter nerve microvasculature in distinct ways, necessitating targeted investigations. However, the results of this preliminary study do indicate the possibility of using AP to depict the blood flow distribution of MNs. Further studies using AP to evaluate the blood perfusion in abnormal MNs are needed to determine the value of AP for MNs. Third, the absence of a gold standard for direct comparison (e.g., contrast-enhanced MRI or CT angiography) limits our ability to validate AP’s accuracy quantitatively. Future studies should correlate AP findings with advanced imaging modalities and histopathological data to confirm its reliability in diverse clinical scenarios.

Conclusions

This preliminary study demonstrates that Angio PLUS detects intraneural vascularity in healthy median nerves with significantly higher sensitivity than CDFI, offering a reliable and reproducible method for visualizing normal microvascular patterns. The proposed vascular classification and observed differences in blood flow distribution between wrist and mid-forearm levels provide foundational insights into MN vascular anatomy. These findings establish a critical baseline for future investigations in pathological conditions where microvascular alterations may precede structural nerve damage. By enabling non-invasive, high-resolution mapping of nerve microvasculature, Angio PLUS holds promise as a research tool to explore angiogenesis and neovascularization in neuropathy, pending validation in clinical cohorts.
