Assessing quality of life in papillary thyroid cancer through a cross-sectional comparative study of SF-26 versus ThyPRO questionnaire
Authors: Cansu Çıkın, Adile Begüm Bahçecioğlu, Sevim Güllü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite excellent survival rates, papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) may negatively affect patients’ quality of life (QoL), especially due to fear of recurrence. This study aims to evaluate the applicability of the Thyroid-Related Patient-Reported Outcome Questionnaire (ThyPRO) in patients with PTC and to assess its correlation with the widely used SF-36 QoL instrument. A total of 121 PTC patients who underwent total thyroidectomy between July 2019 and June 2021 at Ankara University were enrolled. Patients those with comorbidities were excluded. QoL was assessed using the ThyPRO and SF-36 questionnaires. Thyroid function tests and ultrasound findings were also collected. Moderate negative correlations were identified between several corresponding subscales of ThyPRO and SF-36: tiredness and vitality (r = − 0.546, p < 0.001); anxiety (r = − 0.446), depressivity (r = − 0.403), and emotional susceptibility (r = − 0.545) with mental health (all p < 0.001); impaired social life with social functioning (r = − 0.378, p < 0.001); and impaired daily life with role-physical and role-emotional (r = − 0.335, p < 0.001). However, no significant correlation was found between the overall QoL scores of ThyPRO and the general health domain of SF-36 (r = − 0.157, p = 0.087). ThyPRO demonstrates acceptable validity when compared with SF-36 in relevant domains and may serve as a disease-specific instrument for QoL assessment in patients with PTC.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Papillary, Quality of life, Patient reported outcome measures, Anxiety, DepressionSubject terms: Endocrinology, Thyroid diseases, Thyroid cancer

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of all human tumors1. Among these cases, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) presents approximately 80% of diagnoses. With appropriate treatment, the prognosis for thyroid cancer is generally favorable2. Despite its excellent medical prognosis, with 10-year survival rates reaching 90–95%, PTC requires prolonged follow-up due to the risk of recurrence3,4.

Quality of life (QoL) assessment complements traditional health measures, evaluating physical, social, and psychological functioning5. In recent years, QoL evaluations have gained importance in assessing treatment effectiveness6. Disease-specific QoL scales are considered more sensitive than general instruments7. Cancer diagnoses can acutely impair QoL, and fear of recurrence may cause long-term distress8,9. Although PTC has a high survival rate, these concerns persist as prolonged follow-up and anxiety about recurrence significantly impact QoL10.

In 2009, the patient-reported outcome (PRO) measure for benign thyroid disorders (ThyPRO), a specific inventory validated for assessing of QoL in patients with thyroid disease, and available in several languages, was originally used only in patients with benign thyroid diseases, and thyroid cancer listed as an exclusion criterion11. The lack of disease-specific questionnaires for thyroid cancer patients has been considered a barrier to accurately assessing their perception of illness. In 2013, the Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life scale (THYCA-QoL) was developed specifically for thyroid cancer survivors to address cancer related concerns, including treatment side effects, fear of recurrence, and disease-burden. This scale focuses on issues unique to thyroid cancer survivors, such as scar-related distress, tingling or numbness, voice problems, and fatigue caused by cancer treatment12. Although ThyPRO was originally designed for benign thyroid disorders, several recent studies have assessed its use in patients with DTC and compared it with other QoL instruments13. In 2019, Giusti et al. examined QoL longitudinally using ThyPRO in DTC patients and in controls with benign thyroid conditions, finding that, many years after diagnosis, QoL in DTC patients was comparable to that of those with benign disease14. In a prospective five-year study, Monzani et al. suggested that TSH suppressive doses of levothyroxine more efectively improved ThyPRO QoL scores in 208 DTC patients following thyroidectomy15. ThyPRO assesses a broader range of physical, psychological, and social dimensions of QoL, including subscales for anxiety, depression, and emotional susceptibility, whereas THYCA-QoL, focuses mainly on cancer-specific issues. ThyPRO’s extensive validation across languages and populations enhance its cross-cultural reliability and applicability in both research and clinical practice. Although, the limited studies on ThyPRO in DTC patients have shown promising outcomes, there remains insufficient evidence regarding its consistency with more comprehensive QoL, such as the SF-36, and its routine applicability in evaluating QoL among patients with PTC.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the consistency of ThyPRO with SF-36 and to assess its potential for routine use in measuring quality of life in patients with PTC.

Patients and methods

Papillary thyroid carcinoma patients, aged 18 to 75, were consecutively recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Ankara University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism, between July 1, 2019 and June 30, 2021. Initial therapy consisted of total thyroidectomy followed by LT4 therapy. Patients with comorbidities (such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, postoperative hypoparathyroidism or other malignancies) that could affect the quality of life were excluded. ThyPRO questionnaire and SF-36 questionnaires were administered to participants through face-to-face interviews. Thyroid function tests were evaluated for all patients.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if their initial treatment for PTC had been completed at least one year prior to enrollment and if there either an excellent or indeterminate response according to dynamic risk stratification16. TSH concentrations were below 10 mU/l and no changes had been made to the LT4 dosage in the preceding four weeks ensuring that all patients were receiving a stable LT4 monotherapy.

All participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the School of Medicine at Ankara University (İ3-112-19). All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Thyroid related patient-reported outcome questionnaire: thypro

Developed by Watt et al., the ThyPRO, is a valid and reliable instrument, published in 2009 and consisting of 85-items across 13 subscales11. Physical symptoms (four scales), mental symptoms (two scales), function and well-being (three scales), participation and social function (four scales) and a single item evaluating overall QoL during the previous four weeks. The questionnaire requires approximately 15–20 min to complete. Each item is rated by the patient on a five-point Likert scale: 0 — not at all,1 — a little, 2 — some, 3 — quite a bit, and 4 — very much/completely. Scores for each item are transformed to a 0-100 point, scale by averaging and applying a linear transformation using the formula: “Transformed score = (raw sum score / highest possible raw sum score) × 100.” The ThyPRO does not yield a total score; instead, each subscale is interpreted individually, with higher scores indicating worse Qol, lower scores indicating better QoL.

After obtaining the developers’ approval, the questionnaire was translated into Turkish and its validity and reliability were evaluated by İlkaz et al. in 2018 (15). We adminestered the Turkish version of the ThyPRO questionnaire to all participants and evaluated the scores for each subscale separately.

Short form health survey (SF-36)

The SF-36, developed in 1992, is a general health-related QoL questionnaire that assesses eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations (physical and emotional), bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, and mental health. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better QoL17. The Turkish version, validated in 199918, was used in this study.

To evaluate the usability of ThyPRO in patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), both the ThyPRO and SF-36 were administered. Subscale comparisons included: ThyPRO Tiredness vs. SF-36 Vitality; ThyPRO Anxiety, Emotional Susceptibility, Depressivity vs. SF-36 Mental Health; and ThyPRO Impaired Social Life and Daily Life vs. SF-36 Social Functioning & Role-Physical/Emotional. Since higher ThyPRO scores indicate worse QoL and lower SF-36 scores reflect poorer health status, a negative correlation was expected.

Laboratory evaluation

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from all participants and centrifuged at 4100 rpm for 3 min to separate plasma and serum. Serum-free T4 (FT4, Beckman Coulter 33880, reference range: 7.00–15.96 pmol/L), serum-free T3 (FT3, Beckman Coulter A13422, reference range: 3.99–6.71 pmol/L), serum TSH (Beckman Coulter B63284– Access TSH 3rd IS, reference range: 0.38–5.33 µIU/mL), anti-thyroglobulin antibody (Tg Ab-Beckman Coulter A32898 –reference range: 0–4 IU/mL) were measured. Thyroglobulin concentrations were determined using an immunochemiluminometric DxI 800 assay (Beckman Coulter). The Tg and Tg Ab assays used in the analyses have functional sensitivities of 0.1 mg/L and 20 IU/L, respectively, with 95% confidence.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were presented as median, minimum, and maximum values, while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, and Anderson-Darling tests. For group comparisons, the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis H tests were applied to non-normally distributed data, with the Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner test used for post-hoc analysis. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and correlations were analyzed using Spearman’s coefficient (rS). Statistical significance was define as p < 0,05. All analyses were performed using Jamovi (v1.6.13.0) and JASP (v0.14.1.0).

Discussion

In the assessment of QoL in patients with thyroid cancer, the need for disease specific measurement tools has long been emphasized, particularly due to the limitations of general-purpose questionnaires. In this context, the ThyPRO questionnaire offers a structured and comprehensive approach to evaluating subjective symptoms such as fatigue, mood disturbances, and cognitive complaints issues commonly reported by patients with PTC but often overlooked in standard laboratory or imaging assessments. The use of ThyPRO in clinical practice allows for a more holistic evaluation of patient well-being, extending beyond biochemical euthyroidism. It enables clinicians to identify persistent QoL impairments even after the completion of oncologic treatment and to plan appropriate supportive care. In this regard, ThyPRO serves as a valuable complementary tool in the long-term follow-up of patients with PTC.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first cross-sectional study to evaluate the correlation between the disease-specific ThyPRO questionnaire and the widely used SF-36 instrument in patients with PTC, revealing moderate to high correlations between corresponding subscales. Significant negative correlations were observed between the ThyPRO subscales of tiredness, anxiety, depressivity, emotional susceptibility, impaired social life, and impaired daily life, and their respective SF-36 domains. Additionally, we found that anxiety subscale scores on the ThyPRO increased with decreasing age and higher education levels among PTC patients. These findings reveal that ThyPRO captures clinically meaningful aspects of quality of life (QoL) in PTC patients and may serve as a valid tool for QoL assessment in this population. Although the Turkish version of ThyPRO has been previously validated in benign thyroid disorders, evidence regarding its psychometric properties in thyroid cancer populations remains limited. Our study contributes to this growing area by evaluating the applicability and consistency of ThyPRO in a well-characterized cohort of PTC survivors, highlighting its potential utility in both research and routine clinical follow-up.

There are few studies in the literature that have utilized the ThyPRO questionnaire in patients with DTC. One of the earlier studies from the Netherlands, administered several instruments to patients, including the health-related quality of life questionnaire (RAND-36), the thyroid-specific quality of life questionnaire (ThyPRO), and the Multidimensional Fatigue İndex-20 (MFI-20). This study examined the relationship between thyroid function tests and the results of these questionnaires but did not compare or correlate the questionnaires13 themselves. In a five-year prospective study, involving 128 DTC patients, participants were categorized according to different stages of LT4 compensation, and their quality of life was assessed using the ThyPRO questionnaire. Significant differences were observed among the groups in anxiety, impaired social and daily functioning, and overall quality of life15. However, this study, did not include a comparison with another quality of life questionnaire.

Sex differences have been reported in perceptions of quality of life among patients. Carevenna et al. found that in a cohort of 150 DTC patients who completed the SF-36, male patients reported lower impact on pain, physical functioning, and emotional role subscale scores compared to female patients8. An Italian study involving 124 individuals with DTC demonstrated that somatic symptom levels decreased over time more in males than in females; however, somatization and hostility scores were significantly higher in males14. Similarly, in our study, scores for goiter, hypothyroidism, and eye symptoms, as well as fatigue, anxiety, emotional susceptibility, and cosmetic complaints significantly higher in women compared to men. Depressivity subscale score were also found to be approaching statistical significance in women. These findings suggest that hormonal factors, such as the influence of estrogen on mood regulation and immune function, may contribute to increased symptom burden in female patients. Although the proportion of female participants in our study was substantially higher than that of males—as observed in many previous studies—potentially introducing statistical bias, we believe the results still accurately reflect sex-based differences in quality of life among patients with PTC. Furthermore, societal expectations regarding appearance and greater concern over body image may heighten anxiety and cosmetic dissatisfaction in women. These gender-related factors should be considered during patient follow-up, emphasizing the importance of personalized support and psychological interventions when needed.

Thyroid cancer tends to occur at younger ages compared to many other types19. Evidence suggests that being diagnosed with cancer at a young age is a risk factor for reduced quality of life20. However, a recent Chinese study evaluating the quality of life in 286 thyroid cancer patients who underwent surgical treatment, found that age was not an independent risk factor for quality of life at the third postoperative month following thyroidectomy21. In this study, it was observed that in patients with DTC, ThyPRO anxiety subscale scores increased as age decreased and education level increased. While age did not significantly affect overall quality of life, the rise in anxiety scores among younger patients may be associated with the psychological stress of receiving a cancer diagnosis at an early age. Younger individuals may have less experience in coping with health-related stressors and fewer established coping mechanisms, which could contribute to heightened anxiety. Similarly, although higher education is often associated with improved health literacy, it may also raise expectations regarding health outcomes, thereby increasing anxiety levels. Variations in emotional resilience and problem-solving strategies across age groups may further influence anxiety responses. Moreover, as educational attainment increases, patients may have greater access to health information and awareness of their condition; however, easy access to unreliable or unfiltered information—particularly through the internet—may further exacerbate anxiety in this population.

Massolt et al. were the first to evaluate QoL in patients with DTC using the ThyPRO questionnaire, in a study involving 143 adult patients. In that study, only four ThyPRO scales—hyperthyroid symptoms, tiredness, cognitive complaints, and depression—were investigated in relation to thyroid functional status, and no associations were found between TSH, FT4, or FT3 levels and the evaluated ThyPRO scores13. Klaver et al. evaluated QoL in 9,491 healthy individuals in Germany and reported that SF-36 scores in participants with suppressed or markedly elevated TSH levels were generally not significantly different from those with normal or mildly elevated TSH values22. Another study evaluating QoL in DTC patients and a control group using the ThyPRO questionnaire similarly found no relationship between TSH levels and any ThyPRO subscales14. Hoftijzer et al. examined QoL in 153 cured DTC patients using four validated health-related questionnaires (SF-36, Multidimensional Fatigue Index-20, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, and Somatoform Disorder Questionnaire), and reported that neither TSH nor FT4 levels affected any of the questionnaire items23. Consistent with the existing literature, our study did not identify a relationship between TSH levels and any ThyPRO subscales. However, we did observe a positive correlation between higher FT4 levels and increased scores on the ThyPRO depressivity subscale, suggesting that prolonged TSH suppression may contribute to mood disturbances. While previous studies have not established a direct link, subtle thyroid hormone imbalances may influence neurotransmitter pathways, potentially increasing the risk of depression. Therefore, monitoring FT4 levels in patients undergoing long-term TSH suppression is essential. We believe that treatment duration should not be unnecessarily extended and should be adjusted based on treatment response and the patient’s overall condition to balance recurrence prevention with mental health considerations.

A longitudinal study reported that BMI levels were positively correlated with the ThyPRO subscales of goiter symptoms, hypothyroidism symptoms, hyperthyroidism symptoms, and cosmetic complaints. In our study, however, increased BMI levels were associated only with higher cosmetic complaint scores14. A separate study involving 38 couples, including thyroid cancer patients, found that some couples experienced reduced sexual activity following the cancer diagnosis. Nonetheless, it was noted that thyroid cancer had a relatively smaller impact on the relationship between patients and their partners compared to other cancer types24 In the present study, scores on the impaired sex life subscale were not influenced by any of the evaluated parameters.

To the best of our knowledge, there are few studies in the literature that examine the correlation between the ThyPRO and another quality of life questionnaire in patients with DTC. Teliti et al. evaluated sleep quality in 119 DTC patients and assessed sleep disturbances using the ThyPRO, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). They found that the ThyPRO general score was significantly correlated with the PSQI, and the ISI showed significant correlations with several ThyPRO subscales and the general score. The “anxiety” and “emotional susceptibility” subscales were the most strongly associated with both the PSQI and ISI25. Watt et al. examined the sensitivity of subscales between the ThyPRO and SF-36 in patients with benign thyroid disease. Their findings showed that the ThyPRO tiredness subscale correlated with the SF-36 vitality subscale; the ThyPRO emotional susceptibility, depressivity, and anxiety subscales correlated with the SF-36 mental health subscale; the ThyPRO impaired social life subscale correlated with the SF-36 social functioning subscale; and the ThyPRO impaired daily living subscale correlated with the SF-36 physical and emotional role subscales. The ThyPRO general quality of life item was also correlated with the SF-36 general health subscale. They noted that, except for the impaired social life subscale, the ThyPRO subscales demonstrated greater sensitivity compared to their SF-36 counterparts26. In this study, we similarly observed a correlation between the ThyPRO fatigue subscale and the SF-36 vitality subscale. Significant negative correlations were also found between the SF-36 mental health subscale and the ThyPRO depressivity, anxiety, and emotional susceptibility subscales. Furthermore, the ThyPRO impaired social life subscale was significantly negatively correlated with the SF-36 social functioning subscale, and the ThyPRO impaired daily living subscale was significantly correlated with the SF-36 physical and emotional role subscales. While strong correlations were observed across nearly all subscales, no statistically significant correlation was found between the ThyPRO general quality of life subscale and the SF-36 general health assessment. We believe that this lack of statistical significance may be addressed in future studies with larger patient samples, which could offer a clearer understanding of the relationship between these two instruments.

This study had several strengths: (a) the inclusion of patients without additional comorbidities allowed for the evaluation of quality of life changes solely attributable to thyroid malignancy; (b) the concurrent administration of the ThyPRO questionnaire alongside the SF-36 scale enabled the assessment of comparable subscales across both instruments, enhancing the robustness of the findings and supporting the potential applicability of the ThyPRO questionnaire in patients with DTC.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. As a cross-sectional investigation, it cannot establish causal relationships between the variables examined, and future longitudinal studies using the ThyPRO questionnaire in this patient population are warranted. The use of self-reported data may have introduced response bias, and the absence of preoperative quality of life assessments limits our ability to evaluate the direct impact of thyroidectomy. Additionally, the lack of a structured psychiatric interview may have constrained the interpretation of mood-related subscales. Although the overall sample size (n = 121) was sufficient for exploratory analysis, the significant imbalance in sex distribution (female/male: 103/18) may have limited the generalizability of certain findings. In this context, we employed Spearman’s rank correlation to explore associations between ThyPRO and SF-36 subscales. While multivariable regression modeling could have provided further adjustment for potential confounders, our primary objective was to assess the convergent validity of ThyPRO as an initial step. Nonetheless, future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts should incorporate multivariable approaches to better account for demographic and clinical variables.

Conclusion

We observed a moderate to high correlation between corresponding parameters of the ThyPRO questionnaire and the SF-36 scale, supporting the applicability of the ThyPRO questionnaire in patients with PTC. We believe that the ThyPRO questionnaire may serve as a valuable tool for identifying clinically relevant differences in quality of life in PTC, similar to its established use in benign thyroid conditions.
