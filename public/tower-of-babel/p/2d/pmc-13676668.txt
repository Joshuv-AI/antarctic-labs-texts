A qPCR identification scheme to differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma
Authors: Sahar Bakhiet, Emmanuel Siddig, Carole Pab Minlekib, Rihab Mohammed, Willemien Zandijk, Andrew K Watson, Mickey Konings, Ahmed Fahal, Maguette Faye, Fatehmeh Mazraati Tajabadi, Jeffrey Errington, Doudou Sow, Wendy W J van de Sande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycetoma is a Neglected Tropical Disease of the skin. It is characterized by large tumorous lesions in the subcutaneous tissue which hinder the patients in their daily activities. The disease can be caused by either bacteria (actinomycetoma) or fungi (eumycetoma), and treatment depends on the entity. Therefore, in 2022, a Target Product Profile was published by the World Health Organisation in which it was stated that there is an urgent need to develop a diagnostic tool that can differentiate the two reliably within a day.ObjectivesTo develop a qPCR that can not only differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma but can also identify the actinomycetoma causative agents to the genus level.MethodsWe developed singleplex, quadruplex and triplex qPCRs that can be used, depending on the endemic region and validated this against a large strain collection and grains.ResultsThe limit of detection (LOD) of each of the probes ranged from 0.000002 ng for the fungal probe to 0.002 ng for the Nocardia genus probe in a singleplex reaction. Higher LODs were obtained in the multiplexes. The sensitivity and specificity were both 100% for most combinations, although some cross-reactivity with the Actinomadura and Streptomyces genus probes was noted, mainly with non-mycetoma causative agents.ConclusionThe developed qPCR meets the requirements set in the Target Product Profile from the World Health Organisation.ImportanceIn this study a qPCR is developed which can differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma and identify actinomycetoma causative agents to the genus level. This qPCR adheres to the minimal requirements set in the Target Product Profile for a mycetoma diagnostic test by the World Health Organisation. When the qPCR is used on mycetoma grain it reduces the time to identification to only a few hours. With hand-held qPCRs this can also be performed in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05572-8.Keywords: Mycetoma, Eumycetoma, Actinomycetoma, qPCR, Target product profile, Diagnostics, Epidemiology

Introduction

Mycetoma is a Neglected Tropical Disease of the skin [1]. It is characterized by large tumorous lesions in the subcutaneous tissue, which hinder the patients in their daily activities [1]. Mycetoma can be caused by 90 different agents, either bacteria (actinomycetoma) or fungi (eumycetoma) [2]. Characteristic of the mycetoma causative agents is that they form granules in the tissue called grains [2]. Accurate and rapid differentiation between actinomycetoma and eumycetoma is crucial, as their treatments differ considerably. Actinomycetoma is treated in five-week antibiotic cycles and 90% of patients reach complete cure within three 5-week cycles [3, 4]. Eumycetoma is managed with a combination of antifungal treatment and surgery [5]. Under the conditions of a clinical trial this resulted in cure for 80% of patients [5]. In case series this resulted in a 56% corrected cure rate [6].

Due to the critical importance of prompt and accurate diagnosis to initiate the appropriate treatment, the World Health Organization (WHO) has established a Target Product Profile (TPP) emphasizing the need for an accessible in vitro point-of-care capable of identifying mycetoma causative agents to the species level [7]. Ideally, this test would operate under zero infrastructure conditions and identifies all causative agents to the species level. With 90 different causative agents, not an easy task to develop. A more achievable goal would be to develop a laboratory-based test which could differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma within a day, and which is performed in a peripheral health facility or national diagnostic testing laboratory would. Such a test would meet the minimal requirements as stipulated in this TPP [7].

Currently, diagnosis in endemic countries relies on clinical examination, histopathology, and culture [8], which results in an average time to identification of 8.5 days for histopathology and of 21.2 days for culture [9]. This exceeds the time set in the minimal requirements in the TPP. Furthermore, when the performance of these assays was validated in a cohort of 222 eumycetoma patients, it appeared that for histopathology a sensitivity of 93.8% and specificity of 42.8% was obtained and for culture a sensitivity of 84.5% and specificity of 85.7% [9]. The sensitivity of histopathology was in general lower for actinomycetoma patients caused by Streptomyces somaliensis (n = 142 cases; 93.7% sensitivity and 98.9% specificity), Actinomadura madurae (n = 71 cases; 74.4% sensitivity and 99.5% specificity) or Actinomadura pelletieri (n = 16 cases; 75.0% sensitivity and 100% specificity) [10]. For eumycetoma, molecular diagnostic assays such as PCR and qPCR were developed [11–14], which can identify the most common fungal causative agent Madurella mycetomatis in 2.5 h [9]. Using these PCRs a sensitivity of 98.9% and specificity of 100% was obtained in the same eumycetoma cohort of 222 patients [9]. For actinomycetoma, no species-specific PCRs have been developed for most of the causative agents and molecular identification to the species level could only be performed for Nocardia by using a genus specific PCR [15] or by using either a barcode PCR followed by restriction fragment length polymorphism analyses or sequencing or by whole genome sequencing directly from patient material [16–19]. These techniques are often not available in endemic countries and still require more than 1 day for identification.

During the SARS-COVID2 pandemic, many endemic countries obtained qPCR machines [20]. After the pandemic, these machines remained operational and could be used for other diagnostic purposes. We therefore aimed to develop a qPCR which could differentiate eumycetoma from actinomycetoma. Since bacterial co-infections are commonly found in mycetoma lesions, a pan-bacterial probe could not be used [21]. Since more than 95% of the actinomycetoma causative agents belong either to the bacterial general Actinomadura, Nocardia or Streptomyces, we therefore aimed to develop a multiplex qPCR which would differentiate eumycetoma from actinomycetoma caused by Actinomadura species, Nocardia species and Streptomyces species [2].

Materials and methods

Bacterial and fungal strains

For this study, we used a collection of 91 bacterial and 63 fungal isolates of mycetoma causative agents (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1) and Staphylococcus aureus and Mycobacterium tuberculosis as negative controls. The isolates were obtained from culture collections such as the germen collection of micro-organsims (DSMZ) or the Westerdijk fungal collection (CBS) or from patients seen at the Mycetoma Research Centre in Sudan or the University of Saint-Berger in Senegal. The details about the origin of each isolate are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Additionally, 10 grain samples (5 bacterial grains and 5 fungal grains) were obtained from 10 mycetoma patients from University Saint-Berger in Senegal. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the National Ethical Committee of Senegal (Comité National dÉthique pour la Recherce en Santé; CNERS) (No 0000063/MSAS/CNERS/SP) for the Senegalese isolates and from the Soba University Hospital Ethical committee (Khartoum, Sudan) for the Sudanese isolates. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

DNA isolation

DNA was isolated from a 0.25cm2 fungal colony, three bacterial colonies or a single fungal grain using the Fungal/Bacterial DNA MicroPrep kit (Zymo Research, USA). The DNA concentration was determined using the nanodrop and the Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA).

Identification of genus-specific target region, design and validation of the qPCR

For fungi the internally transcribed spacer (ITS) region serves as a widely used barcoding marker, capable of amplifying a broad range of fungal species. For actinomycetes either 16 S rRNA or heat shock protein 65 (HSP65) are used as a barcoding genes [22]. To design a genus-specific qPCR, the highly conserved HSP65 sequences of 69 different Actinomadura, Nocardia, Mycobacteria and Streptomyces species were retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary Table 2). The sequences were aligned in MEGA-X. Highly conserved regions within the Actinomadura genus, Nocardia genus and Streptomyces genus were used to design the primer regions. For the fungal species we compared the ITS regions and selected a highly conserved region.

The representative M. mycetomatis, A. madurae, N. brasiliensis and S. somaliensis sequences were loaded into Primer3 (version 0.4.0; https://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/). Optimum Tm was set at 60 °C (± 3 °C) for the primers and at 70 °C (± 3 °C) for the probes. A multiple primer dimer analysis was performed using the Multiple Prime Analyzer tool (https://www.thermofisher.com/nl/en/home/brands/thermo-scientific/molecular-biology/molecular-biology-learning-center/molecular-biology-resource-library/thermo-scientific-web-tools/multiple-primer-analyzer.html). The variable region between the primers was used to design genus-specific probes. Primers and probes are listed in Table 2.

Selected primers and probes

Primers were validated against a small subset of DNA samples with classical PCR. For this a master mix was prepared consisting of 0.25µL of forward primer (50µM), 0.25µL of reverse primer (50 µM), 10 µL of Roche FastStart™ PCR Master and 7.5 µl sterile deionized water. 18µL of the mix was transferred to a PCR tube (Bio-Rad, Lunteren, The Netherlands) containing 2µL of 2.5ng/µl bacterial or fungal DNA. The PCR was performed using a conventional PCR machine (Bio-Rad, Lunteren, The Netherlands), with a hot start at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C, melting for 30s, 60 °C annealing for 30s, and 72 °C elongation for 1 min. The amplicons were then visualized by gel electrophoresis on a 2.5% agarose (Sphaero Q, Gorinchem, The Netherlands ) stained with SYBR safe (Invitrogen, Eindhoven, The Netherlands). A 100 bp DNA ladder (Invitrogen, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) was used as a size reference.

Validation of singleplex qPCR

To assess the probe, a master mix was prepared consisting of 0.25 µL forward primer (50 µM), 0.25 µl reverse primer (50 µM), 0.1 µL probe (20 µM), 10 µL of LightCycler 480 Probes Master Mix and 7.4 µl DNA-free water. Then 18 µL of master mix was transferred to a 96-wells plate (LightCycler® 480 multiwell, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and mixed with 2 µL of DNA sample and run on the LightCycler® 480 (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with an amplification program consisting of a 5 min preheating step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles consisting each of a 5s DNA denaturation step at 95 °C, and a 30s annealing and extension step at 60 °C, and ending with a 1 min cooling step at 40 °C. The fluorescence output was measured at 465–510 nm, 533–580 nm, 595–615 nm and 618–660 nm and analyzed using the Abs Quant/Fit Points in the LightCycler 480SW 1.5.1 program (Roche, Basel), using 35 cycles as cut-off.

Validation of the multiplex qPCR

The multiplex qPCR was validated in four distinct steps. First, the probes were screened against a small subset of DNA samples in singleplex and multiplex to confirm the intended functionality of the probes. Second, the multiplex qPCR was screened against a larger DNA collection listed in Table 1. Third, the limit of detection (LOD) was determined based on a 10-fold dilution series ranging from 0.00001 ng to 10 ng of DNA isolated from A. madurae DSM43067, A. pelletieri DSM43383, S. somaliensis DSM40738 and S. sudanensis DSM41923. The LOD was determined for all three probes in triplicate, in singleplex, and multiplex. The data was analyzed as described above, and the Cq values of each sample were plotted against the respective DNA concentration and visualized for each of the respective probes. Finally the multiplex was validated against DNA isolated from 10 grain samples (5 bacterial grains and 5 fungal grains). The amount of each primer and probe used in the different multiplex qPCR set-ups are listed in Table 2. For each multiplex an amplification program consisting of a 5 min preheating step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles consisting each of a 5s DNA denaturation step at 95 °C, and a 30s annealing and extension step at 60 °C, and ending with a 1 min cooling step at 40 °C was used. The fluorescence output was measured at 465–510 nm, 533–580 nm, 595–615 nm and 618–660 nm and analyzed using the Abs Quant/Fit Points in the LightCycler 480SW 1.5.1 program (Roche, Basel), using 35 cycles as cut-off.

Statistical methods

To determine the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value of the singleplex and multiplex qPCRs, the number of true-positive (TP), false-positive (FP), true-negative (TN) and false-negative (FN) test results were analysed with the chi-squared test (Graphpad).

Results

No amplification was noted for the bacterial causative agents. A 103 bp amplicon was obtained when the DNA of the Actinomadura, Nocardia and Streptomyces species were amplified with the primers designed on the HSP65 gene with classical PCR (Fig. 1B). No amplification was obtained for S. aureus, M. tuberculosis and the fungal species. Due to the size differences between the fungal and bacterial amplicons, it was also possible to multiplex them in classical PCR and differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma based on the size of the amplicon (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Next, the fungal and bacterial probes were evaluated by qPCR. The number of cycles was set at 35; the time needed to complete these cycles was less than 1 h. As shown in Fig. 1C, only the fungal species had a positive signal with the fungal probe. As shown in Fig. 1D, E and F, the Actinomadura, Nocardia and Streptomyces probes were also genus-specific in this small subset of strains. Next, we tested the entire strain collection (Supplementary Table 1). The ITS probe gave a positive signal for all 63 fungal DNA samples, while none of the bacterial DNA samples showed amplification, resulting in a sensitivity and specificity of 100%. The mean Cq-value for the pan-fungal probe was 23.57 (range 13.74–32.73) when 5 ng of DNA was used. The Actinomadura probe gave a positive signal for all 41 Actinomadura DNA samples, the Nocardia probe gave a positive signal for all 14 Nocardia samples and the Streptomyces probe gave a positive signal for all 34 Streptomyces samples. The mean Cq-values for the Actinomadura and Nocardia and Streptomyces probes were 22.08 (range 19.85–25.33), 21.94 (range 20.11–23.99) and 21.72 (range 19.23–24.34), respectively (Fig. 2B; Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1). No cross-reactivity was noted with the fungal strains, however some cross reactivity was noted with the Actinomadura and Streptomyces probe. The Actinomadura probe gave a positive signal for all 41 Actinomadura samples, but also for the Sphaerosporangium sp (Cq-value of 20.6). This resulted in a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 99.12%. The Streptomyces probe gave a positive signal for all 34 Streptomyces samples, but also for 5 Actinomadura species and the Actinocorallia weidayensis isolate. A positive qPCR was noted with A. weidayensis (n = 1), A. cremea (n = 1), A. geliboluensis (n = 1), A. hallensis (n = 1) and A. welshii (n = 2) (Supplementary Table 1). Of these species, only A. welshii was a reported mycetoma causative agent (Table 1) [23]. The mean Cq-value for the Streptomyces probes was 21.72 (range 19.23–24.34). The five Actinomadura species had a mean Cq-value of 22.67 (range 22.11–23.60). Due to this cross-reactivity, a sensitivity of 100% was noted and a specificity of 95.04%.

The limits of detection of the qPCRs

Next, the limit of detection for each of the probes was determined. As shown in Fig. 2, in singleplex, 0.000002 ng of DNA was reliably detected using the pan-fungal probe. For the actinomycetoma probes, the LOD was 0.004 ng bacterial DNA for the Actinomadura genus probe, 0.002 ng for the Nocardia genus probe and 0.0004 ng for the Streptomyces genus probe (Fig. 2A; Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, it was noted that especially for the pan-fungal probe a large range of Cq-values was obtained. Therefore, the Cq-values were also plotted against the fungal species. As can be seen in Fig. 2C on average higher Cq-values were noted for the Madurella species and Medicopsis romeroi.

Multiplexing the probes

Instead of running four singleplex qPCRs, it is more efficient to run these qPCRs in multiplex. In the design, the dyes of the probes were selected in such a way that a quadruplex could be applied. As can be seen in Table 3, the limit of detection of the fungal probe increased 3500 times in the quadruplex compared to the singleplex. Furthermore, the DNA of 3 M. mycetomatis isolates could no longer be detected in the quadruplex. This resulted in a sensitivity of 95.24% and a specificity of 100.0%. For the Actinomadura genus, Nocardia genus and Streptomyces genus probes the reduction in LOD was less (Table 3) and all of the samples could still be detected with qPCR, resulting in a sensitivity and specificity of 100%.

To improve the performance of the multiplex assay, we narrowed down the quadruplexes to triplexes. In total, three triplexes were designed: one that included the causative agents most prevalent in Africa, one that included the causative agents most prevalent in Latin-America and one that only included the bacterial causative agents. As shown in Table 3, in all triplexes all DNA samples were again detected by their corresponding probes, resulting in a 100% sensitivity and specificity. The LOD for the pan-fungal probe remained lower than in the singleplex, but no fungal samples were missed. The LOD for most of the bacterial samples remained in the same range as in the singleplex. Despite the differences in the LOD and the Cq-values when the quadruplex and triplexes were compared to the singleplex, the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, remained the same (Table 3).

Validating the qPCR on mycetoma grains

To validate the African triplex on clinical material, the qPCR was performed on DNA isolated from grains obtained from 10 different patients (Table 4). As can be seen in this table, only for one patient infected with A. madurae a double signal was obtained. For all the other samples the qPCR identification was correct, resulting in a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 83.3% for the fungal probe and a sensitivity and specificity of 100% for the Actinomadura probe using clinical samples.

Discussion

The multiplex described here (Fig. 3) adheres to the minimal requirements set in the WHO TPP for a mycetoma diagnostic assay as it can differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma within a day, and can be performed in a peripheral health facility or national diagnostic testing laboratory [7]. Additionally, this qPCR also allows identification to the genus level for the Actinomycetoma causative agents. In the current design, the probes appeared quite specific, but some cross-reactivity was noted with the Actinomadura and Streptomyces genus probe with cultured isolates and with fungal probe in grain samples. The Actinomadura probe was also positive for the Sphaerosporangium sp. and the Streptomyces probe was also positive for A. cremea, A. hallensis, A. welshii and A. weidayensis. Of these only A. welshii was described as a causative agent of actinomycetoma [23]. At the time of the design of this qPCR, this was not known and no sequences for A. welshii were available.

qPCR scheme to differentiate eumycetoma from actinomycetoma

There was also a difference in Cq-values for the bacterial and fungal species. On average, the Cq-values for the actinomycetes were much lower than for the fungi. This difference is most likely due to the difference in genome size. The genomes of S. somaliensis, S. sudanensis, A. madurae and A. pelletieri range from 5.01 Mbp to 10.3 Mbp [24], while the genomes of M. mycetomatis and M. fahalii range from 35.7 Mb to 40 Mb [25–28]. This means that, on average, 4–7 times more copies of a single copy gene are present in 5 ng bacterial DNA compared to 5 ng fungal DNA. Furthermore, the ITS gene is a multi-copy region for which the copy number varies per species. Madurella species belong to the fungal order of the Sordariales, and fungi belonging to this order have, on average, 2.9 copies of the ITS region in their genome [29]. M. mycetomatis itself has only 1 [29]. Falciformispora species, T. grisea and M. romeroi belong to the fungal order of the Pleosporales which have on average 17.1 copies of the ITS region in their genome [29]. This difference in copy number also explains the large variation we noted in Cq-value with our pan-fungal probe.

The quadruplex qPCR designed with these primers and probes did result in a significant increase in Cq-values for the ITS probe only. This increase was not noted in the three triplexes designed. The increase could be the result of either oligonucleotide cross-reactivity, due to a high degree of similarity between one or more oligos, or by cross-talk between overlapping emission wavelengths, as was previously noted for other quadruplexes [30]. It could also be machine-dependent. For our settings, the problem was solved when the quadruplex was transformed into three different triplexes, each with its own specific use. Due to the variation in etiology worldwide, the quadruplex is not needed in most endemic regions [2]. In Africa, Nocardia species are not encountered and the triplex differentiating eumycetoma from actinomycetoma caused by Streptomyces and Actinomadura species would be able to detect > 95% of all causative agents [2]. In Latin-America, Streptomyces species are not encountered and therefore the triplex differentiating eumycetoma from actinomycetoma caused by Actinomadura and Nocardia will be enough. For most non-endemic countries, one could choose to run either a fungal singleplex and the bacterial triplex together or one of the region-based triplexes to identify the causative agent to the genus level. When the multiplex qPCR was validated against 10 grain samples, we were able to demonstrate the Actinomadura genus probe was able to identify all 5 Actinomadura grains. Only in a single grain Actinomadura grain sample a false positive fungal qPCR signal was obtained, resulting in a 100% sensitivity and 83.3% specificity for the fungal probe. Since dual mycetoma infections are rare, the Actinomadura probe is directed towards a genus, and the fungal probe is universal, the fungal probe most-likely detected a fungal superinfection. Indeed, due to the large taxonomic diversity of the fungal agents able to cause mycetoma, the pan-fungal probe was designed to detect all these fungal causative agents. The downside is that it will also detect dermatophytes which could be present on the skin. Therefore, in this specific case a dermatophyte or a fungal contaminant could be the cause of the signal. In cases where such dual signals are obtained, a second (q)PCR or sequencing of the ITS and 16 S barcoding genes can be used to identify the causative agent. For M. mycetomatis [13, 14], M. pseudomycetomatis [13], M. tropicana [13], M. fahalii [13], F. senegalensis [14], and F. tompkinsii [14] qPCRs are available. For actinomycetoma causative agents these qPCRs are currently being developed. Furthermore, beyond mycetoma, several other infectious and non-infectious conditions, such as cutaneous tuberculosis [31] and botryomycosis [32] can present with clinical features similar to mycetoma, posing diagnostic challenges. These can be effectively ruled out using this molecular platform and thereby preventing misdiagnosis and inappropriate therapy.

Since we were able to validate the performance of the qPCR only on 10 grain samples in Senegal, and fungal cross-contamination could occur, it is important to validate this qPCR on more patient samples and preferably in other endemic regions too with different etiologies too. In the current cohort, only fungal or Actinomadura grains could be validated. Therefore additional validation, especially in regions where Nocardia spp and Streptomyces spp are more common, is needed.

In conclusion, we developed a multiplex qPCR assay that can differentiate actinomycetoma from eumycetoma (Fig. 3). This is a significant advancement in the global effort to control and monitor mycetoma. Rapid and accurate differentiation between actinomycetoma and eumycetoma enables more precise mapping of disease distribution and prevalence, thereby informing targeted public health interventions. Moreover, the ability to identify causative agents swiftly allows for better understanding of regional variations in etiological profiles, facilitates timely treatment initiation, and helps reduce disease burden—particularly in resource-limited settings where mycetoma is endemic. As the assay becomes integrated into surveillance programs, it will also enhance data collection on pathogen distribution and resistance patterns, ultimately strengthening epidemiological research and guiding policy decisions aimed at improving disease management and progression toward eventual control or elimination of this neglected disease.
