Surrogacy from a reproductive rights and justice perspective: a narrative review
Authors: Margot Lherbet, Samir Hamamah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Since its emergence in its contemporary form in the late 1970s, surrogacy has been the subject of ongoing debate and controversy, shaped by developments in scientific research and social discourse. Introduction

Surrogacy is an assisted reproductive technology (ART) method where a woman carries a child for others and relinquishes it at birth. Although controversial from the start in the 1970s with stark opposition, evolving research has reshaped the debate, particularly as comprehensive approaches have emerged. They added complexity to the point of conceptualizing the social paradoxes of the practice as objects of study in their own right (Rozée et al. 2020). For instance, Stuvøy (2018) shows how varying conceptualizations of surrogacy – and of who it is perceived to affect – have given rise to different typologies of the ‘troubles’ associated with the practice. The international debate over surrogacy has intensified notably in 2024, marked by significant legislative and political developments. Italy has strengthened its anti-surrogacy stance by criminalizing the practice even when conducted abroad by Italian citizens, framing it as a defense of human dignity but drawing criticism from LGBTQ+ and family rights organizations (Piersanti et al. 2025). At the multilateral level, the UN, through Special Rapporteur Reem Alsalem, has characterized surrogacy as a form of systemic violence, exploitation, and even human trafficking, calling for its universal abolition (Alsalem 2024). However, several scholars and commentators have criticized the report for its lack of nuance and its reliance on a homogenizing portrayal of surrogacy as inherently exploitative (Scully 2025). Overall, the debate reflects a complex intersection of scientific scopes, national regulations, normative discourse, and legal and ethical considerations, revealing divergent perspectives on reproductive freedom, women’s rights, child protection, and dignity.

Without claiming exhaustiveness, we can outline several contexts in which oppositions and debates unfold. In Western philosophy, surrogacy is seen by some as violating Kantian human dignity, while others uphold bodily autonomy. Moving beyond this ethical dualism, scholars increasingly adopt care ethics, attributing ethical/legal issues not to surrogacy itself but to the absence of care and moral responsibility (Parks 2010). Economic views also diverge: some condemn surrogacy as commodifying the body (Fleurbaey 2011); others see it as revaluing reproductive labor (Ramskold & Posner 2013). Socially, it raises questions about the suffering of infertile individuals (Brunet & Fournier 2021), diverse family forms, including male couples and single men dealing with social sterilization (Smietana 2024), and the consequences of their procreative migrations when they cannot access surrogacy nationally. Transnational surrogacy often reflects North–South inequalities, echoing global care work patterns – a ‘global reproductive labor chain’ (Tain 2013) where Northern clients engage Southern women. Legally, surrogacy fragments motherhood (genetic, gestational, and social) and challenges filiation laws (Mahmoud & Romanis 2023). Medically, once risks are mitigated (Yau et al. 2021), attention turns to surrogate autonomy and justice in reproductive resource allocation. Autonomy may be compromised by socio-economic pressures or conflict with intended parents’ autonomy (Sifris 2015). Feminist debates highlight this dual vulnerability: for some, surrogacy is body exploitation or reproductive prostitution (Dworkin 1983); for others, it is a path to emancipation and gender norm resistance (Purdy 1989). Material feminism emphasizes surrogates’ autonomy and living conditions rather than moral judgments about the act itself (Stuvøy 2018). From different feminist perspectives, some authors advocate for the abolition of surrogacy, denouncing its potential for exploiting women, while others see it as an opportunity to challenge the heteropatriarchy by freeing women from subjection to traditional reproductive labor within the nuclear family (Lewis 2019).

These intersecting dimensions make surrogacy ethics and regulation complex. Research shows restrictive bans can have unintended consequences. In India, moving from a commercial market to a tightly regulated or banned system has been criticized as paternalistic and exclusionary, limiting access for single, LGBTQ+, and foreign-intended parents, while ignoring socio-economic realities (Kandoi & Verma 2025). Critics argue it undermines women’s autonomy by denying regulated compensated surrogacy and may increase informal, riskier arrangements (Pande 2021). Stringent bans often fail to reduce demand, instead fostering underground markets and cross-border services that expose surrogates to exploitation and displace intended parents toward less transparent alternatives (Lherbet & Hamamah 2024). Prohibition alone, thus, risks failing to protect women and children while complicating reproductive autonomy and promoting costly, risky transnational practices (Blyth & Farrand 2005).

The challenge, then, is to adopt a pragmatic approach that neither bypasses the paradoxes and complexities nor succumbs to moral categorization, but instead seeks to advance social justice. This narrative review asks the following question: which frameworks can provide analytically robust and politically meaningful tools for understanding and regulating surrogacy, while emphasizes the experiences and rights of women who provide gestational labor?

Drawing on feminist, postcolonial, and socio-legal scholarship, this article positions reproductive rights as a necessary ethical and legal baseline, grounded in bodily autonomy and non-discrimination. However, they often overlook structural conditions – economic precarity, racialization, gendered labor markets, and geopolitical asymmetries – that shape women’s entry into surrogacy. Reproductive justice, developed by women of color, shifts the focus from formal rights to lived conditions, emphasizing power, intersectionality (Ross & Loretta 2017), and epistemic inclusion (Bhakuni 2023).

Based on a narrative review of 86 peer-reviewed articles across medical, legal, sociological, and feminist literature, this review has three objectives. First, it clarifies the distinction between reproductive rights and reproductive justice, examining their contributions to surrogacy debates without reducing them to binary frameworks. Second, it uses these frameworks to assess how surrogates’ autonomy, health, and agency are unevenly protected across national and transnational contexts, highlighting geographic diversity in practices. Third, it critically evaluates institutional responses – medical, legal, and associational – considering their potential and limits in promoting reproductive justice for surrogates.

Rather than offering a prescriptive model, this review aims to contribute to ongoing debates on how surrogacy might be governed in ways that reduce harm, challenge reproductive stratification, and recognize women who undertake gestational labor as full rights-bearing subjects.

Methods

This study was conducted as a narrative review of the literature on surrogacy in relation to reproductive rights and reproductive justice. The review was carried out in several stages. First, a broad search was conducted across multiple academic databases (PubMed, Cairn.info, Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar, JSTOR, CAIRN International, and Sociological Abstracts) using the keywords ‘surrogacy’ combined with ‘reproductive rights’ and ‘reproductive justice’ in English, as well as ‘GPA’ and ‘gestation pour autrui’ in French.

In the second step, the selection was refined by focusing specifically on articles addressing dimensions directly related to reproductive rights within surrogacy (such as access to information, reproductive health, abortion, and contraception) and to reproductive justice (including socio-economic conditions and market-based analyses). Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text assessment to ensure relevance to these thematic criteria.

Finally, a cross-referencing approach was used to deepen the analysis. References cited within the initially selected articles were examined to identify additional relevant studies, particularly on themes that emerged prominently during the first stage of inclusion, such as surrogacy as a form of labor and country-specific case studies.

This search prioritized peer-reviewed original and review articles and resulted in the identification of 86 relevant publications. Data extraction focused on key themes, conceptual approaches, and empirical findings, and the synthesis followed an interpretative, thematic approach. This methodology enhances transparency while remaining consistent with the flexible and iterative nature of narrative reviews.

The aim of this review is to highlight both the strengths and the limitations of reproductive rights and reproductive justice frameworks for analyzing and informing the regulation of surrogacy.

What conceptual framework for surrogacy?

Surrogacy can be analyzed through multiple lenses: as a treatment for medical and/or social infertility, as an IVF procedure under the WHO definition, from demographic perspectives related to natality, or as a reproductive service. Yet, its complexity defies reduction to any single analytical framework, as each carries the risk of either oversimplification or fragmentation. The challenge, therefore, lies in adopting an approach that enables rigorous critical analysis while avoiding binary thinking and that integrates ethical, social, feminist, and medical dimensions. This section argues that a reproductive rights framework, grounded in reproductive justice, provides such a foundation for regulation while addressing its ethical, feminist, and political implications.

Ethical interest of reproductive rights

Reproductive rights constitute an ethical framework grounded in universal principles underpinning fundamental rights. This framework avoids choosing between deontological ethics centered on dignity and bodily autonomy, often portrayed as irreconcilable in Western surrogacy debates. In this perspective, fundamental rights cannot be reduced to formal legality alone but must be assessed in light of broader principles of justice and moral legitimacy. As fundamental rights, they derive their authority from their inherent universal foundation rather than from state recognition (King 1963), even though states remain responsible for their protection. These rights, therefore, apply without discrimination.

In this context, reproductive freedom can be understood as a key conceptual component of reproductive rights, specifying the scope of individual autonomy they are meant to protect. Robertson conceptualizes reproductive freedom as the ability to decide whether, with whom, and how to reproduce, framing it as central to identity, dignity, and the meaning of life. As a form of negative liberty, reproductive freedom limits state interference but does not guarantee access to services (Robertson 1994).

In part to address this limitation and safeguard this fundamental freedom from instrumentalization for national demographic or political agendas, reproductive rights were formally articulated in the 1994 Cairo Program of Action (CPA) (Undivided Rights 2004, Luna & Luker 2013, Nelson 2013), reflecting decades of feminist mobilization. In the 1980s, feminists from the Global South forged alliances culminating in a Women’s Declaration affirming reproductive autonomy, non-discrimination, and social justice. Reproductive rights are commonly defined as the ability to decide if and when to have children – central to women’s health and socio-economic outcomes – and are recognized in international instruments such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which affirms the right to decide ‘freely and responsibly’ on the number and spacing of children and to access the information and means to do so. Yet these rights are rarely invoked to counter global regressions in reproductive governance. Women’s autonomy is constrained by religious moral frameworks and neo-Malthusian approaches – prioritizing demographic control, resource scarcity, or social burden – distorting the emancipatory aims of reproductive rights (Gautier & Grenier-Torres 2014). Willie J. Parker argues these trends reflect capitulation to religious, patriarchal, or capitalist ideologies, while reproductive rights grounded in fundamental principles already provide the ethical basis for policies aligned with CEDAW and the International Women’s Rights Charter.

Although absent from the CPA, access to ART is now recognized by the WHO (2023) as a reproductive right, supporting the view that access to surrogacy for infertile couples may fall within this framework. While its scope varies across jurisdictions and remains contested (Cohen & Jackson 2021), it must not be conflated with a ‘right to a child’, a confusion frequently instrumentalized in surrogacy debates (Gunnarsson Payne & Handelsman-Nielsen 2022). From the surrogate’s perspective, recognizing surrogacy as an expression of reproductive rights raises questions when reproduction occurs for others. Initially, the focus is on rights surrounding (non)reproduction for oneself, encompassing the capacity to parent a child under fair conditions. Yet, in this context, reproduction does not entail parenthood for the surrogate. Some feminist scholars argue that this framing acknowledges women’s autonomy and reproductive agency, while others caution that it risks depoliticizing the gendered, racialized, and classed inequalities embedded in surrogacy markets (Essén & Johnsdotter 2015), or even the commodification of reproductive rights for marketing purposes (Rudrappa & Collins 2015).

Despite this, the reproductive rights framework remains fragile, particularly amid rising conservatism. No clear guidance exists on reconciling reproductive rights with ethical or religious pluralism (Gautier & Grenier-Torres 2014), nor on practices such as surrogacy, which were not explicitly addressed in 1994, despite the justice-oriented intent of the framework (Parker 2020). This gap is widely documented across regions, including Latin America (Torres et al. 2019), Africa (Adelakun 2018, Abiola et al. 2024), Europe (Merlet & Sénémaud 2010, Zervogianni 2019), the UK (Fenton-Glynn 2016, Hodson et al. 2019), Italy (Piersanti et al. 2021), and Germany (Kapfhamer & Van Voorhis 2016). Moral pluralism does not negate international responsibility, and the literature consistently identifies international regulation as the most ethical response (Frati et al. 2021). Shared regulation is needed to strengthen reproductive rights as an operational framework capable of mitigating harms from fragmented national ART policies (Parker 2020). In this article, reproductive rights are not mobilized to justify surrogacy or reproductive freedom per se, but as normative tools to strengthen protections for surrogates – understood neither as passive victims nor autonomous agents, but as rights holders – and to clarify the ethical, feminist, and social stakes.

Defending surrogates’ rights requires addressing documented abuses – such as routine cesarean sections (Hovav 2020), multiple embryo transfers (Attawet et al. 2022), and inadequate risk disclosure or abortions without the surrogate’s consultation (Tanderup et al. 2015) – observed notably in India (Saravanan 2013, Pande 2014, Rozée & Unisa 2014, Rudrappa 2015). Such violations underscore the need to strengthen reproductive rights-based policies. The CPA framework contributes by both exposing rights violations and supporting policy development. In the context of surrogacy, reproductive rights, thus, provide an essential normative foundation: they affirm that women acting as surrogates retain full bodily autonomy and reproductive health rights, irrespective of contractual arrangements. However, these rights must be assessed in light of structural barriers – class, gender, race, culture, and religion – that constrain access and implementation (Karlberg 2017). Without addressing these dimensions, reproductive rights risk becoming empty claims; hence, they require a reproductive justice lens to retain their substance and regulatory effectiveness (Ross & Solinger 2017).

Feminist interest in reproductive justice

The reproductive justice framework emerged in the 1990s from women of color activists, notably within the SisterSong Women of Color Reproductive Justice Collective, to foreground the interconnections between reproductive autonomy, structural inequality, and social justice (Ross 2006, Ross & Solinger 2017). It defines reproductive autonomy as the social, political, and economic power to make healthy decisions about one’s body, sexuality, and family, while centering intersecting oppressions of race, class, and gender. Unlike reproductive freedom – a philosophical principle – this is a concept born of activism, later reinforced by a theoretical framework. Initially focused on parenting in safe environments, preventing unwanted pregnancies, and affirming sexual rights, the initial framework did not explicitly address ART. Mamo (2023) proposes the concept of queer reproductive justice to account for the evolving practices of ART: its integration into a thriving global industry and the growing visibility of queer reproduction. Rudrappa (2021) nevertheless argues for extending this lens to surrogacy, linking it to broader dynamics of economic inequality and systemic discrimination.

Reproductive justice, thus, conceptualizes autonomy not only as non-interference but also as freedom from imposed reproductive norms and structural domination (Bhakuni 2023), including forms of ‘epistemological non-domination’ that allow marginalized women to shape the knowledge structures governing social and institutional norms (Bhakuni 2023). In the US context, founders of reproductive justice have long criticized mainstream reproductive rights discourse for prioritizing the concerns of white women – particularly abortion and contraception – over inequalities in access to care. Similarly, Pande (2021) critiques Western feminist dominance in surrogacy debates, which often marginalizes voices from the Global South. A broader global shift is underway, with individuals reclaiming reproductive narratives and resisting medical and political control (Parker et al. 2000), underscoring the need to center minority voices in ART, including surrogates and donors.

The growing literature on surrogates’ lived experiences shows that surrogacy is a heterogeneous practice, resisting binaries such as altruism versus commerce (Horsey 2024), exploitation versus emancipation, or framing surrogates as either victims or autonomous agents. Scholars stress integrating surrogate voices into analyses, situating them within multi-scalar power systems (Majumdar 2014, Horsey 2015). Twine (2012) analyzed the surrogacy industry in Egypt, Israel, India, and the US and disentangled the intersecting roles of race, religion, class inequality, religious law, and global capitalism. Bar-Am’s (2025) fieldwork in Israel reveals surrogacy as both alienating and emancipatory, with women navigating power through embodied, emotional, and narrative experiences. This invites a reconceptualization of surrogacy as a complex human phenomenon where agency and constraint coexist, extending analysis beyond pregnancy to broader reproductive biographies (Schurr & Perler 2021).

Empirical research reveals wide variation in the meaning they attribute to their act: some Californian surrogates describe it as a ‘labor of love’, some women interviewed in Karnataka view it as a moral act, and some Russian surrogates consider it as paid work (Smietana et al. 2021). In pronatalist states, such as Israel or Ukraine, surrogates may be celebrated as ‘national heroines’ (Teman 2010, Lance 2017). Despite structural inequalities, surrogates often resist legal, cultural, and heteronormative norms framed as protective but exclusionary (Corrêa 2020), with perceived vulnerability sometimes signaling autonomy and transgression (Rozée 2018). Studies also challenge assumptions of extreme poverty, as was the case for India: surrogates are not uniformly among the poorest (Rozée et al. 2019). In restrictive socio-economic contexts, surrogacy may be a temporary survival strategy for informal workers, with women negotiating choices and power domestically and professionally (Vora 2019, Jana & Hammer 2022). Yet the economic value of their labor is largely captured by agencies and clinics, making exploitation a function of constrained conditions rather than the practice itself (Vertommen & Barbagallo 2022).

Overall, this literature underscores the importance of empirical fieldwork capable of situating surrogacy along continuums of agency and constraint, grounded in surrogates’ lived experiences and ‘contexts of action’ (Courduriès et al. 2018), rather than in abstract ethical frameworks that treat surrogacy as an ethical exception and prioritize principles over people. Reproductive justice is, therefore, a particularly compelling feminist framework: intersectional, materialist, and pragmatic, it is less prone to ideological polarization than more abstract theoretical approaches. It also provides an analytical lens – developed further in the next section – to identify problematic situations in surrogacy by examining not only formal respect for reproductive rights but also the upstream, ongoing, and downstream conditions that generate and sustain inequalities.

While reproductive rights and reproductive justice are analytically distinct, this article treats them as complementary and interdependent. Reproductive rights provide a normative and legal baseline to identify violations and define obligations, whereas reproductive justice offers a critical lens to assess the structural conditions that shape the actual possibility of exercising these rights for a more comprehensive analysis of surrogacy practices across diverse socio-economic and geopolitical contexts.

Advantages and limitations of using a reproductive rights framework

Having defined justice and reproductive rights and clarified their ethical and feminist relevance to surrogacy, we now apply these concepts to assess their practical potential and limits in addressing surrogacy issues, focusing on autonomy, agency, and reproductive health.

Diagnostic interest of reproductive rights

The following section examines how these rights are exercised, constrained, or violated across diverse geographical contexts, as documented in the international literature.

From ‘third parties’ to rights holders

Surrogacy is a form of assisted reproduction involving at least one third-party contributor – the surrogate – and potentially additional parties, such as egg and/or sperm donors. From a reproductive rights perspective, a key issue is recognizing surrogates as rights holders rather than mere ‘third parties’ in ART. In surrogacy, identifying the patient is complex: treating a couple’s infertility entails prolonged medical intervention on another woman’s body. Recognition and visibility are, therefore, central. While the roles of gamete donors and surrogates differ significantly – particularly given the embodied, temporal, and relational dimensions of gestation – both have historically been subject to forms of institutional invisibilization. In Euro-American contexts, donor anonymity has upheld a heterosexual, coital model of filiation (Frith 2001, Delaisi de Parseval 2006, Macpherson 2019). This comparison is not intended to equate their contributions, but to highlight how reproductive arrangements have been structured to obscure certain actors, with concrete institutional effects that are aptly captured by the very terminology used to refer to these women: in Canada, the surrogate is the patient (Dar et al. 2015), whereas in Israel she is labeled a ‘collaborator’ (Shalev et al. 2016). Concepts such as ‘collaborative reproduction’ (Thompson 2005) and ‘reproductive relationships’ (Stuvøy 2018) better reflect these evolving roles and kinship forms in assisted reproduction. The central question is, thus, to determine for which actors (and their respective rights and interests) these pathways are primarily structured. In this sense, terminology, by virtue of its performative nature, is illuminating.

Labeling surrogates and donors as third parties also reflects asymmetric decision-making and limited bodily autonomy. Intended parents typically choose gamete donors and surrogates, whose capacity to choose recipients remains understudied. Clinics impose strict selection criteria and demand extensive disclosure from donors without reciprocal transparency, reproducing judgments of bodily ‘suitability’ shaped by health, class, and race (Deomampo 2016). In Mexico, egg donation is racialized: white Colombian donors are preferred, and Mexican surrogates carrying white babies may express pride rooted in postcolonial hierarchies (Schurr 2020).

Andal (2023) offers a donor-centered analysis of transnational surrogacy and egg donation, focusing on the bodily autonomy of women from the Global South. Poor, racialized, and marginalized donors are positioned at the intersection of market-driven reproductive industries and unequal power relations. Autonomy appears as a privileged condition constrained by economic need, weak legal protections, and structural exploitation. Using the ‘body as space’ lens, Andal shows how donors’ bodies become sites of commodification, surveillance, and control, highlighting ethical failures that exclude donors from decision-making. The study calls for recognizing reproductive contributors as rights-bearing subjects. In line with reproductive justice, the following section examines how these rights are challenged across diverse contexts. The analysis is structured around three dimensions of reproductive rights: i) the right to physical and psychological sexual and reproductive health, ii) including its social and relational dimensions with intended parents; iii) the right to accessible information and free and informed consent; and the ability to decide freely on the conditions, timing, and modalities of reproduction, without discrimination, coercion, or violence.

Access to reproductive health: physical and psychological aspects

A synthesis of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO), European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE), American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and American Society of Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) recommendations has been proposed to improve surrogate selection or eligibility and care (Kim 2020). Camel Shalev’s working group – an interdisciplinary group of ethicists, researchers, and practitioners convened in Israel to discuss the need for international governance of international ART use – emphasized the medical institution’s responsibility in ethically regulating international surrogacy and ensuring good clinical practices (Shalev et al. 2016). In fact, studies report practices such as abortions carried out without the surrogate’s consent, routine cesarean sections, and multiple embryo transfers (Guerra-Palmero 2017, Pande 2017). These practices raise serious concerns regarding violations of bodily autonomy and reproductive health standards, particularly given that multiple embryo transfers are associated with increased pregnancy risks (Kneebone et al. 2023).

Supporting surrogates and donors also requires psychological care, given the emotional, relational, and social dimensions involved. Multidisciplinary approaches are essential, and professionals need specific training. Surrogates often show desirability bias due to stigma (often downplaying negative experiences or challenges to practitioners), requiring multi-method tools to address it (Riddle 2020). While countries such as Canada, the US, Israel, and the UK generally offer more structured psychological support, evidence suggests that such support may be more limited or uneven in other contexts, including India (Yau et al. 2021). In Iran, practitioners developed ‘need-based reproductive health programs’ grounded in surrogate testimonies (Goli et al. 2021). These reveal needs related to social/familial stigma – e.g. hiding pregnancies, limiting interactions, partner tensions, and delays in personal reproductive plans after procedures such as C-sections. Surrogates are not typical patients; tailored programs are necessary. These needs recur across many studies (Patel et al. 2018, 2020). Ultimately, with a view to standardizing and regulating practices, it is worth noting that guidelines have been proposed to strengthen ethical, psychological, and relational care in surrogacy arrangements (Pizitz et al. 2013, Cabra et al. 2018, Kim 2020, Riddle 2020).

Relationship between surrogates and intended parents

In their review, Kneebone et al. (2022) show that surrogate–parent relationships, central to a positive surrogacy experience, vary by country. These findings, derived from thematically analyzed interviews, synthesize multiple perspectives, yet each participant’s experience remains distinct. In the US, Canada, and Greece, studies report close ties, with surrogates sometimes seen as family (Carone et al. 2017, Smietana 2017, Malmquist & Höjerström 2020). In international arrangements, contact occurs via Skype, SMS, email, or visits. In Mexico, Thailand, or India, it is minimal due to communication barriers (Ziv & Freund-Eschar 2015, Lustenberger 2016, Gezinski et al. 2018). In a study, parents using Indian surrogates express affection and recognize mutual benefit through compensation, but some feel a loss of control (Deomampo 2016). In India, intended mothers are seen as ‘big sisters’; in Israel and the US, more as friends with ‘special chemistry’ (Teman & Berend 2018). Surrogates often report deepening bonds during pregnancy (Kneebone et al. 2022). The review prompts reflection on how the organizational structure of surrogacy practices, communication methods, and cultural and ‘repro-regional moral landscapes’ (Smietana et al. 2021) all influence the relationships between intended parents and surrogates. Equally, each party’s individual commitment substantially shapes the dynamic.

Yet many clinics in India and Russia discourage such contact, despite evidence that it shapes the experience (Jadva et al. 2019). For surrogates’ well-being, care should allow relational space with parents when desired. Country disparities persist: in India, contact is minimal, while in Israel, teams foster a ‘fused body’ dynamic between surrogate and mother to legitimize the child’s birth – e.g. cutting the cord, coordinated lactation (Teman 2010). Reproductive rights exceed medical care; their aim is full well-being – physical, mental, and social – including the quality of these relationships, if wanted.

Information and consent: legal support and education

Significant disparities shape reproductive health education in surrogacy. For example, in examining the US context, Damelio & Sorensen (2008) propose a ‘soft law’ approach that recommends 6–8 h of education for first-time surrogates, including counseling and peer sessions with former surrogates. In India, by contrast, they may act as intermediaries in ‘surrogacy houses’, exercising authority over others (Deomampo 2016).

Reproductive rights include access to sexual and reproductive health information, yet in India, many surrogates cannot read or access contracts, often written in English (Rozée et al. 2020). They lack knowledge of their contractual rights, enforceability, negotiation capacity, and health risks. This reflects both a failure to uphold reproductive rights and their weak incorporation into national law (Shalev 1998), allowing parents’, clinics’, or brokers’ interests to prevail (Andal 2023).

As regards consent, surrogates in the Global North usually receive legal advice before signing contracts, while those in the Global South often do not (Ross 2021). This limits prior agreement on issues such as fetal reduction or malformations and raises the question of whose reproductive rights prevail (Tanderup et al. 2015) when a conflict of interest occurs. As shown so far, surrogacy is fundamentally relational – whether altruistic or commercial – requiring a relational understanding of reproductive rights and justice (Shah et al. 2022). Beyond ESHRE’s call for advance legal counseling, we argue that such counseling must address this relational challenge. In this regard, the role of professionals involved in the relationship – even as mediators – cannot be overlooked (we discuss this in the section titled ‘The role of the medical institution’). Mahmoud (2025) argues that surrogates’ nonnormative reproductive choices can reinforce the dominance of healthcare providers’ authority, leading to experiences of epistemic and hermeneutical injustice.

Self-determination also varies widely. In the UK, surrogates may keep the child as she is recognized as the mother at birth (Human Fertilisation & Embryology Act 2008, s. 33), with parentage later transferred to the intended parents via a parental order (s. 54); whereas in Ukraine, contracts are highly binding and expose surrogate women to severe consequences in cases of non-compliance (Horobets & Yakushchenko 2022). This ‘right to regret’, intended to protect surrogate autonomy (Lange 2014), is not endorsed by ESHRE (Shenfield et al. 2025).

Finally, tensions around information, consent, and self-determination reveal a core limit of individual rights-based approaches. Beyond negative rights (freedom from coercion), protecting surrogates’ specific reproductive rights is necessary to prevent ‘intercontractual injustices’, in which contractual arrangements override autonomy (Panitch 2013). Addressing these blind spots requires viewing surrogacy not only as an individual reproductive practice but also as market-based and highly medicalized work. Recent scholarship, therefore, explores regulatory frameworks drawn from labor law (Humbyrd 2009) and clinical trials, developed in the following sections after conceptualizing surrogacy as (re)productive and/or clinical labor (Armstrong 2022).

Moving beyond the limits: from reproductive bodies to labor

Transnational surrogacy involves multiple forms of labor and workers, including medical, legal, and intermediary actors. Within this broader labor ecology, surrogates’ activities can be conceptualized as reproductive labor, a perspective well established in feminist scholarship recognizing reproduction and childbirth as work. More contested is the recognition of gestational labor as paid, contractual work within a capitalist economy. Yet, in practice, surrogacy is fully embedded in such economic and contractual systems.

Surrogacy is among the most complex transnational markets in the reproductive economy. As part of the ‘reproductive bioeconomy’ (Waldby & Cooper 2008), it mobilizes clinics, agencies, donors, surrogates, and legal actors across global networks shaped by neoliberal, unequal, and colonial dynamics (Deomampo 2016). These reprowebs (König & Jacobson 2021), organized around reprohubs (Inhorn 2015), form a fragmented yet connected ‘reproductive archipelago’ (Rudrappa 2015): a global network of surrogacy practices, where legal, economic, and social conditions vary across regions – like islands in an archipelago – creating a decentralized but linked system of reproductive labor and governance that shifts reproductive labor to resource-rich or low-cost contexts.

The expansion of transnational surrogacy aligns with platform-based economic models, package deals, and cross-border legal services (Deonandan et al. 2012). Shaped by national laws, welfare regimes, and ethical norms, agencies exploit ‘fertility-friendly jurisdictions’ (Van Hoof & Pennings 2013) to bypass restrictions. Concepts such as reproductive tourism (Inhorn & Gürtin 2011) and reproscape (Inhorn 2011) capture these spatial dynamics. Growth in countries such as Georgia, Ukraine, and Mexico illustrates both market adaptability and vulnerability to legal and geopolitical change (Twine 2012).

Surrogacy models vary along three axes: commercial versus altruistic, paid versus compensated, and with or without intermediaries. The commercial model of some states in the US permits direct payment within a stable legal framework involving agencies and contracts (Jacobson 2016), but raises concerns about commodification and inequality. The UK altruistic model does not prohibit all payments to surrogates as such, but rather restricts commercial involvement by third parties. Under Section 2(3) of the Surrogacy Arrangements Act 1985, payments made ‘in respect of making, or negotiating or facilitating the making of’ surrogacy arrangements are prohibited on a commercial basis, while payments to surrogates themselves are permitted and subject to judicial scrutiny in the context of parental orders under the Human Fertilisation & Embryology Act 2008. Although often framed in practice in terms of ‘reasonable expenses’, this category remains legally flexible and open to interpretation (Horsey 2015). Although often framed as more ethical, it can still entail moral obligations and social pressure (Teman 2010, van den Akker 2007).

In light of these dynamics, some scholars advocate moving beyond this binary, even proposing a professional model (van Zyl & Walker 2013) for surrogacy and regulating surrogacy through labor law frameworks. Before assessing these proposals, however, it is necessary to clarify how surrogacy constitutes work in a political and economic sense, even when framed as altruistic.

Reproductive, intimate, and clinical work

Early Marxist feminist scholars emphasized the central role of reproduction in capitalist value creation, challenging the exclusion of reproductive labor from dominant economic theories (Federici 2012). By deconstructing binaries such as private/public, nature/society, and reproduction/production, they demonstrated how patriarchal capitalism defines work through male-coded productivity, relegating women’s reproductive activities to the realm of biology rather than labor. Maria Mies notably argued that gestation and child-rearing are conscious social practices that must be recognized as forms of labor (Mies 1987). Building on this tradition, later scholars have mobilized Marxian analytical concepts – rather than a normative Marxist framework – to examine surrogacy as a form of biological and reproductive labor. Although surrogates do not perform standardized or codified tasks, they engage in embodied processes that generate value within clinical and commercial systems. As Waldby & Cooper (2008) argue, surrogates ‘offer themselves as subjects, giving clinics access to the productivity of their biology in vivo – the biological labor of living tissues and reproductive processes’. In Marxian terms, this labor produces surplus value. However, a productivist approach does not erase the fact that it still involves agency and negotiation on the part of surrogates, as documented in empirical studies of Indian women’s experiences (Rudrappa 2018).

French sociologist Laurence Tain uses Marxist feminism to analyze reproductive labor at the intersection of gender, class, and race. Drawing on the ‘global care chain’ (Ehrenreich & Hochschild 2003), she proposes the ‘global reproductive work chain’ to map actors in transnational circuits and the shifting sexual division of labor, where domestic work becomes commodified (Tain 2013). In surrogacy, unlike care chains, parents are generally the ones who migrate. Even in a flexible and constantly evolving market, surrogates may be temporarily displaced from their country of origin.

Surrogacy is also ‘intimate labor’: gendered, bodily, emotional work within private spaces, based on ‘bodily and affective interactions in the service of social reproduction’ (Parrenas & Boris 2011). This labor is institutionalized in surrogacy under unequal power relations (Rudrappa 2018). ART has turned intimate labor into ‘clinical labor’, where the body and compliance with medical protocols are central (Waldby & Cooper 2008). Clinical labor includes not just biological capacity but also adherence to regimens, testing, appointments, and self-monitoring.

Surrogacy then involves diverse forms of labor that extend beyond reproduction and, thus, fall outside the scope of reproductive rights. It is, therefore, necessary to consider other legal frameworks.

Labor and clinical trial law

In Labour Is Labour, Armstrong (2022), drawing on the Sex Work Is Work movement and framing surrogacy as labor rather than as altruistic or exceptional, shows that labor law could provide concrete protections related to working conditions, health and safety, remuneration, and bargaining power. While acknowledging the ethical and political challenges of this shift, Armstrong argues that a labor-based approach offers a pragmatic, rights-oriented alternative capable of addressing exploitation without reinforcing binaries or exceptionalizing reproductive labor. This also situates surrogates in the Global South as informal workers facing limited alternatives and power asymmetries (Vora 2019). This aligns with labor rights discourses such as the International Labor Organization’s decent work agenda and the General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS), which seek to reduce global service inequalities (Brugger 2011), as well as with the World Fair Trade Organization’s advocacy for protections for marginalized workers (World Fair Trade Organization 2017).

Within this perspective, physician Casey Humbyrd proposes applying fair trade principles to surrogacy, including living-wage-based minimum compensation and more equitable fee redistribution (Humbyrd 2009). In the US, total surrogacy costs can reach $200,000, including $20–55k for surrogate compensation (Brandão & Garrido 2022). In India, clinics may earn up to 2 million INR while surrogates receive around 300,000 INR (Jana & Hammer 2022). Regulation could, therefore, require translated contracts, direct contact between surrogates and intended parents, and access to independent legal support through third parties, such as unions (Vincent & Aftandilian 2013). To ensure enforcement, Humbyrd further suggests linking compliance to immigration procedures governing the child’s entry into the intended parents’ country. Yet, the challenge lies in ensuring that these regulations do not override the best interests of the child.

Surrogates’ rights can also draw on clinical trial ethics and legal regulation, particularly the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring informed consent, confidentiality, withdrawal, and ethical oversight (World Medical Association 2013). This comparison does not imply that surrogates are research participants, but rather that both contexts involve power asymmetries, medical risk exposure, and the need for robust consent and oversight mechanisms. Another point of convergence is that these are healthy individuals who expose themselves to health risks for the benefit of others. Like research subjects, surrogates face ‘ethical variability’ where poverty, poor healthcare, and weak regulation are exploited (Petryna 2005). Similar risks exist in gamete donation in the Global South, making these frameworks vital to reinforce bodily autonomy (Andal 2023).

There is a strong case for framing surrogates’ reproductive rights alongside other legal frameworks to overcome the limits of a reproductive-only approach and address issues of protection and recognition surrounding surrogacy’s deeply embodied dimension, beyond gestation. However, the practice is also highly relational and medicalized, with intermediaries and institutions playing a pivotal role. The section titled ‘The role of the medical institution’ examines how these institutions can constrain or enable reproductive rights, considering both their power and potential pathways toward less institution-dependent forms of surrogacy.

The role of the medical institution

To move beyond the previous section, our literature review examines the role of medical institutions through key concepts proposed by Saravanan (2013) and Beier (2015): biopower, social contract, and trust theory. While these perspectives illuminate the concentration of authority within medical institutions, they also expose the limits of governing surrogacy solely through clinical or contractual frameworks. This opens space to consider the role of other actors’ involvement which may help rebalance power relations.

Critique of biopower, social contract, and Beier’s trust theory

Critique of biopower

Michel Foucault’s concept of biopower offers a lens to analyze the central role of medical institutions in surrogacy. Biopower governs life – bodies, reproduction, health, and populations – through institutional practices, expert knowledge, and regulation. Highly medicalized, surrogacy involves hormonal treatments, monitoring, embryo transfers, and prenatal surveillance, transforming gestation into a technical process governed by biomedical protocols (Foucault 2008). From a Foucauldian perspective, reproductive bodies are disciplined and normalized within clinical regimes of efficiency and risk management, often marginalizing relational and experiential aspects of pregnancy. In surrogacy, medical institutions render reproductive capacities visible, measurable, and governable, shaping the scope and limits of reproductive rights.

Biopower operates differently across contexts. In India, clinics often act as full-service enterprises – recruiting surrogates, mediating with intended parents, managing legal and medical processes – positioning them as central gatekeepers (Saravanan 2013, Dar et al. 2015). In Canada and the UK, institutional roles are more limited, confining medical involvement to clinical care and producing more fragmented governance. For instance, clinics are typically prohibited from matching surrogates with intended parents (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008), leaving this role to informal networks or nonprofit intermediaries.

This concentration raises ethical and political concerns. When medical professionals mediate both surrogate and parent interests, conflicts of interest emerge, privileging those financing the process and reinforcing inequalities (Saravanan 2013). Empirical studies show that in the Global South contexts, surrogates often lose autonomy during pregnancy: strict monitoring, restricted mobility, and limited decision-making, particularly in clinic-controlled housing (Rozée & Unisa 2014). Biopower, thus, transforms reproductive autonomy into conditional compliance over time.

From a reproductive justice perspective, these dynamics highlight the limits of relying on medical institutions as primary guarantors of rights. While essential for health and safety, their regulatory role can subordinate justice to clinical efficiency and market logic. Pluralized governance – confining clinics to clinical functions while distributing authority across legal protections, labor frameworks, and surrogate-led representation – can reconfigure biopower to enhance accountability, participation, and reproductive justice.

Social contract and trust theory

Beyond Foucauldian critiques, feminist political philosophy – especially Annette Baier on social contract and trust (Baier 1994) – provides tools to analyze surrogacy power relations. Surrogacy is often legitimized through contracts framed as voluntary agreements for mutual benefit, yet feminist critiques, notably Pateman (1988), show that social contracts are rarely neutral and are embedded in patriarchal structures assigning women to caregiving and reproductive roles.

Beier’s theory of trust (Beier 2015) highlights that contracts rely not only on consent but also on asymmetrical trust. Intended parents trust surrogates to gestate and care for the fetus, while surrogates trust parents and medical professionals to act ethically. Trust can not only enable cooperation but also heighten vulnerability when it substitutes for enforceable rights and oversight. This complicates simplified views of surrogacy as a dyadic power relation. Saravanan’s ethnography in India shows that power often resides with medical institutions controlling information, networks, contracts, and finances (Saravanan 2013). Medical institutions, thus, govern surrogacy arrangements, influence reproductive decisions, shaping knowledge, decision-making, and the mobilization – or breach – of trust. Physicians may face pressure to align practices with clients’ expectations. Intended parents can also face financial exploitation, reinforcing clinics as central power brokers. Scholars advocate limiting clinics to clinical roles via accreditation, enforceable standards, and sanctions against unregulated cross-border practices (Crozier & Martin 2012). Recent crises, including COVID-19 and the war in Ukraine, further underscore the need for transparency in reproductive mobilities and reprowebs (Whittaker et al. 2019, König & Jacobson 2021).

Giving more space to other actors

Literature offers several avenues to these critics. In this final subsection, we draw on the cases of Sweden, the UK, and New Zealand to examine alternative institutional mediators and limits, before turning to an example of an associative or community-based mediator in the US and Colombia.

Social services and ethic committees

In New Zealand, social workers are central to surrogacy, assessing intended parents’ readiness and ensuring surrogate consent, following a model similar to adoption, with parental rights transferred at birth via court order (Walker & van Zyl 2021). However, they report limited training, as surrogacy is less common than adoption. In Sweden, where surrogacy is banned, social workers assist with transnational surrogacy but may face ethical discomfort or bias, potentially stigmatizing parents (Walker & van Zyl 2021). In the UK, CAFCASS safeguards children’s welfare during parental order applications, assessing intended parents and advising courts (Surrogacy UK 2023). However, legal assessment is not standard in the UK. While these roles protect children and enforce legal agreements, they remain institution-centered, emphasizing procedural compliance over surrogate autonomy, relational experiences, or broader ethical concerns, potentially reproducing structural inequalities.

The same critique can be addressed to the ethics committees in New Zealand (ECART). ECART reviews all surrogacy applications, evaluating child welfare, surrogate-intended parent relationships, and living conditions, with clinics also requiring ethical approval. While this oversight manages risk and ensures procedural compliance, it prioritizes institutional interests over the surrogate’s autonomy and lived experiences, potentially reinforcing social hierarchies and neglecting structural inequalities in transnational or commercial surrogacy. Training for all mediators on normative conflicts and surrogate rights is recommended (Gautier & Grenier-Torres 2014). Taken together, these considerations point toward the need for a less procedural and institutionally centered approach, in favor of a more holistic framework that accounts for the social dimensions of surrogates’ lived experiences. Expanding support for surrogates – e.g. facilitating disclosure to their children, ensuring social support, and mitigating stigma – would strengthen a care-based approach (Arvidsson et al. 2015, Walker & van Zyl 2021).

Surrogate and intended parent associations

To assess this critique, Blazier and Janssens propose creating a third-party organization to protect surrogates’ interests, with mandatory membership to become a surrogate, akin to a professional union (Blazier & Janssens 2020). Membership costs would be covered by intended parents or clinics. However, the mandatory nature of this proposal and its effects on women’s autonomy must be questioned. They also envision an international treaty regulating commercial surrogacy, requiring part of payments to fund capacity-building for women and global inequality reduction projects through professional bodies. This would enhance women’s options and agency in choosing surrogacy.

In the papers we included, we did not find any studies on foundations or organizations of surrogates. We identified only a few through our own independent search. In Colombia, the Grandes Corazones foundation supports surrogates, guarantees rights, enforces contracts, provides material aid, training, health insurance, intervenes with clinics, and raises awareness against stigma. With over 100 volunteers, it has helped 1,200+ women. In the UK, SurrogacyUK, a nonprofit organization, manages a community of surrogates and egg donors, has an external ethics committee, and offers professional and peer support, plus events and training. Conversely, several intended parent associations exist, such as Men Having Babies (MHB), a globally recognized nonprofit organization founded in 2012 in New York. MHB started as a support network for gay biological and prospective fathers, offering workshops and an annual seminar. It has since expanded worldwide, developed recommendations for clinics and agencies, and collaborated with LGBTQIA+ family groups to create similar programs internationally. This organization exemplifies how surrogacy’s relational nature can shape not only policies benefiting surrogates but also its limitations.

The moral responsibility of intended parents: a lever for more respect for surrogates’ reproductive rights?

Surrogates participated in drafting MHB’s ethical charter, which is structured into three parts: general principles, guidelines for service providers, and ‘best practices’ for intended parents that can be grouped into three main categories: i) the relationship with the surrogate and her environment, ii) respect for her autonomy and dignity, and iii) the moral responsibility of intended parents throughout the journey. It is worth noting, however, that surrogates do not form a homogeneous group, and the selective inclusion of certain voices – frequently from Global North contexts – risks obscuring the diversity of experiences and cannot be assumed to reflect the perspectives of surrogates more broadly.

MHB emphasizes communication and the relationship as the foundation of the surrogacy journey. Intended parents and the surrogate are expected to discuss various topics: number of children, number of surrogacy journeys, the importance of the genetic link, pre- and post-journey relationships, birth narrative, and contact arrangements. While these recommendations aim to protect the surrogate, they also carry a risk of paternalism: the charter tightly regulates the relationship and ‘expected’ behaviors of the parents, potentially imposing implicit norms about what constitutes a ‘good relationship’ or ‘proper behavior’. Suggestions such as visiting the surrogate’s children or avoiding simultaneous surrogacies illustrate this normative potential. The charter also largely assumes a standard model of the relationship, showing limited sensitivity to specific cultural or social contexts and insufficiently incorporating the lived experiences of surrogates. The work of Teman (2010) in Israel and Lavoie et al. (2025) in Canada calls for particular caution regarding the tendency to construct a unified narrative of surrogacy as a ‘perfect journey’. They show that one of the main limitations – whether in organizations or agencies – lies in the homogenization of surrogacy experiences, which tends to obscure the diversity of practices, relational arrangements, and lived experiences of surrogates. This standardized storytelling may also lead to the uncritical adoption of categories and language drawn from public debates – such as ‘ethical’ or ‘altruistic’ surrogacy – without moving beyond the binary frameworks that structure these controversies.

Regarding autonomy and dignity, MHB stresses the non-commercial, altruistic nature of surrogacy, emphasizes that surrogates are not mere service providers, and recommends reasonable contractual restrictions (e.g. tobacco or alcohol use), avoiding the feminist and political interest of considering surrogacy as a labor. Reproductive autonomy, including non-medical abortion, is guaranteed, alongside access to independent legal counsel. These two points raise questions about the limits of contractualizing bodily autonomy, as when certain behaviors are prohibited under a free and informed agreement. This point demands rethinking it not in terms of the absence of constraints, but as a form of agency or capacity for equitable negotiation.

That said, there is a clear lack of organizations founded by and for surrogates, or at least such organizations are far less visible within the landscape of transnational surrogacy. In this respect, a parents’ organization – Surrogay – composed mainly of French and European intended parents pursuing surrogacy in Colombia, organizes webinars and training workshops for its members to raise awareness of the cultural issues that may arise when engaging in surrogacy in Colombia. This fieldwork, which forms part of the lead author’s doctoral research, also shows how the founders meet with surrogates outside the clinical setting to identify their needs. In this context, surrogates appear to be less interested in educational reintegration funds than in training aimed at learning how to manage their finances and the money received through the surrogacy process.

However, these organizations provide an instructive case study of how intended parent organizations attempt to respond to ethical concerns raised by surrogacy. Moreover, such organizations raise important questions about informal moral regulation: while they may mitigate certain abuses, they also risk recentering intended parents as ethical gatekeepers, thereby reproducing asymmetries of voice and power, recalling the need for ‘epistemic non-domination’ to advance reproductive justice.

Conclusion

This review has examined surrogacy through the combined lenses of reproductive rights and reproductive justice, not to propose a new normative model but to clarify how these frameworks can be productively articulated. In doing so, it builds on existing feminist, socio-legal, and bioethical debates by highlighting the limits of binary framings and the need to account for lived experiences, power relations, and epistemic inclusion.

While reproductive rights provide an essential normative baseline, they remain insufficient to capture the relational, institutional, and transnational dimensions of surrogacy. A reproductive justice perspective complements this by foregrounding the social and material conditions shaping surrogates’ experiences, including support structures, stigma, and inequalities.

This review does not resolve the challenges of global regulation. Rather, it shows that surrogacy governance requires context-sensitive approaches that go beyond formal legal frameworks to include clinical practices and social support. Its contribution lies in offering a more integrated analytical lens to inform ongoing debates, without advancing a single regulatory model.
