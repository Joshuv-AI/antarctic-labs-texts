Forest harvesting alters the biogeochemical character and protection of mineral associated carbon in a Podzolic soil
Authors: S. MacIntyre, L. Kellman, C. E. Gabriel, A. Diochon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forest harvesting can alter the biogeochemical processes that determine the quantity and chemical nature of stored soil organic carbon (SOC). This SOC is distributed amongst organic compounds in soils of varying biological and physical complexity, with a large fraction of this SOC associated with metals and minerals. The objective of this study was to investigate changes in fractions of SOC associated with metals and minerals and their chemical character through depth and time for Podzolic soils of a harvested temperate red spruce (Picea rubens Sarg.) forest chronosequence to understand biogeochemical changes in SOC and its sensitivity to forest harvesting disturbance. A standard analytical approach was used to sequentially extract C from mineral pools of increasing SOC retention strength through depth (0–5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, 20–35, 35–50 cm) and time (1, 15, 45, 80, and 125+-yr) following forest harvesting. The biogeochemical character of the largest pool was assessed using aromaticity (specific UV absorbance: SUVA254) and stable C-isotopes (δ13C). Results show that the organo-metal complexed fraction accounted for the largest pool examined (~ 59–85% of mineral associated SOC) and was most susceptible to harvesting losses, particularly in the deeper mineral soil. Biogeochemical patterns of SOC within this pool through depth showed distinct changes including: i) a lower aromaticity with lower C content across all sites within the deep mineral soil (20–50 cm), and ii), a correlation between decreases in aromaticity and increases in δ13C across all sites. This pattern of lower aromaticity and enriched δ13C can be interpreted as a shift towards more microbially processed and/or simpler compounds. Overall, the observed patterns support the conceptual hypothesis that forest harvesting alters the biogeochemical character and protection of the mineral associated fraction of SOC in soils of this region.

Introduction

Soils are the largest terrestrial reservoir of organic carbon (C), storing more than three times as much C as the atmosphere or terrestrial vegetation [1, 2]. Mineral soils typically store up to 50% of this C below 10–20 cm depth [3], and most of the C stabilized in these deeper soils is associated with soil minerals [4]. Soil organic C (SOC) comprises a substantial portion of the mass of the soil organic matter (SOM) pool and is found in a diversity of organic compounds [5] organized through interactions with particle surfaces and other molecules, and includes partially degraded litter, microorganisms, organic compounds attached to mineral surfaces, as well as organo-metal complexes and mineral precipitates [6]. Uncertainties remain regarding specific mechanisms that alter the stability of SOC (e.g., abiotic vs. biotic pathways, organo-mineral interactions) and their responses to disturbances [2, 7, 8].

Despite their global size, SOC stores may be sensitive to management activities [9]. While SOC held in the mineral soils of forest systems can have long residence times [7, 10], the stability of the bulk C and mineral associated organic matter (MAOM) pool under harvesting cycles has come under increased scrutiny [11–15] as mechanistic understandings of the factors controlling C stores improve [5, 16–19]. In forests underlain by acidic soils subjected to podzolization, pedogenic weathering processes result in the transport and accumulation of SOM and secondary minerals deeper in the soil profile [20–23]. Soil profiles experiencing podzolization represent large areas of temperate and boreal forest ecosystems [24], which are actively harvested for fibre. SOC in podzolic soils are significant from a carbon storage perspective (136 t C ha− 1 in 0–30 cm) [25], so disturbances such as forest harvesting that disrupt organic C bound to minerals in podzols are therefore important to consider [21, 23, 26–29].

Investigations of the effects of forest harvesting on SOC stores in podzols have generally focused on the first 15 y following disturbance (e.g. [27, 28, 30]). These studies have shown increases in SOC immediately (1–5 y) post-harvest consistent with inputs from slash that are transported into the B horizon [27, 28, 30]. However, these increases do not persist, and SOC returns to and may decline below pre-harvest estimates within a decade or two [28]. Comparing time since forest harvest across forests in the Northeastern United States, Petrenko and Friedland , [31] showed declines in SOC with time since harvest and deeper in the soil profile. Grand and Lavkulich, [28] and Petrenko and Friedland [31] both found positive relationships between the soil C: N ratios and concentrations of SOC, with lower SOC associated with narrower C: N ratios. This relationship was not evident in the control forests and was interpreted as evidence for higher rates of decomposition of SOC in response to disturbance. Declines in SOC were observed in the zone of illuviation suggesting a destabilization of mineral associated organic matter. These observations are consistent with the trends observed in our previous work [13, 32].

Methodological approaches used to distinguish different pools of SOC are generally conceptualized around turnover time [33] and include chemical [34, 35], as well as physical fractionation approaches (typically based on density or particle size) [32, 36–38]. In soils, operationally distinct and measurable SOM pools include particulate organic matter (POM) and MAOM. Laboratory-based water-extracted organic matter measurements can provide insight into the field mineral soil dissolved organic carbon (DOC) transport dynamics, arising from continuous exchanges between the soil solution and SOC associated with mineral surfaces [39]. Physical fractionation to separate SOC based on density (1.8 g cm− 3) has previously demonstrated losses in both the POM and MAOM fractions in response to forest harvesting in the podzolic soils included in this study [13, 20]. Isotopic evidence (δ13C) indicated accelerated microbial decomposition as a primary mechanism of SOC loss and that loss was greatest in the MAOM fraction [32].

Sequential chemical extractions can be used to characterize mechanisms of stabilization in the MAOM fraction [40, 41], which may be useful for identifying pools of SOC that are most affected by disturbance. Mineral associated organic carbon (MAOC), can be systematically separated into sub-fractions based on mineral classes of increasing binding strength, including water soluble minerals, organo-metal complexes formed by the precipitation of dissolved Fe and Al cations with organics [22], and poorly crystalline phases (such as ferrihydrite and imogolite) and crystalline mineral (including goethite and hematite) surfaces involved in sorption of SOC [21, 23, 36]. Organic C held within these extracted MAOC pools can be measured as solubilized/aqueous species.

In addition to the quantity of MAOM held in its associated fractions, the aromatic nature of SOC in specific fractions can provide additional clues about the origins of this material in soils. Aromatic compounds, derived mainly from plant matter, including lignin [42], are preferentially adsorbed to minerals [39], varying as a function of soil pH and the mineral surface chemistry [43] to form strong complexes with Al and Fe oxides in acid subsoils [44–46]. Aromatic compounds represent a SOC pool generally considered to be fairly resistant to decomposition [47]; however microbial communities in soils are able to decompose and alter a wide range of SOC substrates [48, 49], especially following changes in environmental conditions [2, 5] that may destabilize MAOC and make it available. Thus, comparisons of the aromatic nature of SOC through space and time in mineral soil fractions can provide an indicator of relative changes in the degree of SOC decomposition within the soil profile, with more microbially processed SOC having a lower aromatic content and a more enriched δ13C signature [39, 50–53].

This study investigates changes in SOC biogeochemical characteristics through depth (0–5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, 20–35, 35–50 cm) and time (forest ages 1-, 15-, 45-, 80-, and 125+ yr) for the Podzolic soils of the Abraham Lake, Nova Scotia, Canada, temperate forest harvest chronosequence, where previous analyses showed MAOC cycling on decadal scales drove significant mineral soil C losses [13, 20]. It quantifies SOC content across a range of standard mineral fractions using extractants (deionized water, sodium pyrophosphate, hydroxylamine, sodium dithionite) to sequentially release SOC associated with water soluble, organo-metal, poorly crystalline, crystalline minerals, respectively. This sequence extracts MAOC pools from the weakest to the strongest, as the strength of the organo-mineral associations varies with increasing crystallinity [22]. While this sequence is broadly interpreted to extract MAOC pools of increasing strength, it may not capture the full complexity of all MAOC associations. Furthermore, this study examines extracts from the largest fraction (organo-metal extracted with pyrophosphate) to determine whether patterns in aromaticity and MAOC δ13C demonstrate increased microbial decomposition since harvest within soil profiles. We expect, based on our conceptual model, that the more weakly bound MAOC will be most susceptible to forest harvesting induced C losses in these soils due to its large pool size and potential for reduced stability. If C losses from this mineral pool arise from increased microbial processing, we expect to observe decreased aromaticity coinciding with 13C enrichment.

Methods

Site description

This research was carried out in the Abraham Lake region of the Liscomb Game Sanctuary, Nova Scotia, Canada (45 10°N 62 38°W). Climatic conditions of the area include a mean annual air temperature of 5.8 °C, with January and July having mean temperatures of -5.8 °C and 16.9 °C, respectively. The area receives approximately 1300 mm of precipitation annually, and is located 185–200 m above sea level, with a rolling topography (< 10°) [13].

Soils in the study area are acidic, sandy loam textured (Table 1) Orthic Humo-Ferric Podzols [21, 32] from the Halifax Series, characterized by well-developed Ae eluvial horizon [54], a brown sandy loam illuvial B horizon high in organic matter, and an olive- to yellowish-brown sandy loam subsoil [55]. These soils would be classified as Haplic Podzols or Entic Podzols in the World Reference Base for Soil Resources [56]. The parent material for the Halifax Series is Beaver River Till, chiefly composed of quartzite [57]. Beaver River Till is a locally derived stony glacial till whose clast lithology reflects the relative uniformity of the underlying bedrock geology (i.e. Cambrian-Ordovician Meguma Terrane) [58]. This till is dominated by sandy greywacke facies derived from metasandstones of the underlying Goldenville Formation, with some contribution from clay-rich slates of the Halifax Formation [59, 60]. The clay mineralogy is predominantly illite with traces of chlorite, vermiculite and kaolinite [61].

The area contains one of the Acadian Forest Region’s last remaining old growth red spruce (Picea rubens Sarg.) forests [13]. A chronosequence consisting of 5 sites (1 year, 15 year, 45 year, 80 year, and 125 + yr) representing important stages of forest development (recent clearcut, stand initiation, stem exclusion, understory initiation, and intact, mature old growth, respectively) was established in 2005 [13, 62]. Sites were identified in collaboration with the Nova Scotia Department of Natural Resources based on their prior work at the sites [63]. The last date of intervention of the old growth stand is unknown, however trees within the stand are more than 200 years old [62]. The 125 + year old growth stand is used as a reference site to represent the undisturbed soil profile chemical state. To minimize between-site variability, sites are within 5 km of each other, with the same elevation, parent material, soil texture, as well as topography, so that the primary difference between sites is the time since harvesting.

Sample collection and preparation

Three soil pits measuring 71 cm x 71 cm x 50 cm deep were hand excavated in a 400 m2 area representative of the stand at each site. The organic horizons were removed prior to collecting mineral soil samples. The mineral soil was excavated by depth, using sampling depths of 0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, 20–35 cm, and 35–50 cm. The 0–5 cm and 5–10 cm depth intervals are coincident with the zone of elluviation, the 10–15 cm and 15–20 cm are a mixed layer of elluviation and illuviation with the underlying 20 + cm layers being distinct zones of illuviation, as indicated by their Fe concentrations. Representative samples were brought back to the laboratory, oven dried at 60 °C to constant weight, sieved to 2 mm and archived in dry storage. For the current study, a representative sample for each depth interval was created by compositing the three samples from each site [64] based on similar work done by Gabriel et al. [21] and Bekele [65]. Soil texture was determined on the composite sample using a LaMotte 1067 soil texture test kit (LaMotte, Maryland, USA). Soil texture was variable (Table 1), reflecting the highly heterogeneous, poorly sorted, and complex particle size distribution in a glacial till [66]. The presence of SOM has also been shown to lead to the overestimation of the LaMotte results for the silt and clay content [67]. The mineral fraction (Table 1), isolated on the basis of density (1.8 g cm− 3 NaI) following Sohi et al. [38], typically ranged between 960 and 990 g kg− 1 for mineral soil depths between 5 and 50 cm, with greater variability in the surface soil (0–5 cm) reflecting dynamic changes in organic matter concentrations at the surface [20].

Sequential soil extractions

Sequential chemical dissolutions were carried out on the archived composite soil samples to selectively extract C from mineral pools of increasing crystallinity following USGS Biogeochemistry Laboratory Soil Sample Processing Method SequExtr.2013.1. This USGS methodology was also adapted by Heckman et al. [22]. The extractions were carried out in triplicate for each composite sample. The first phase of the dissolution involved adding 30 mL deionized water to 1 g of sample to extract water soluble minerals. Deionized water extracts dissolved organic carbon and water-soluble amorphous materials [68]. Samples were shaken for 16 h and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant solution was decanted and then passed through a 0.45 μm filter and stored at 4 °C. Extracted soil samples were dried at 60 °C and reweighed prior to proceeding to the next extraction step. Organo-metal complexes were next targeted using 30 mL 0.1 M sodium pyrophosphate (at near-neutral pH), which extracts complexed iron, aluminum, organic carbon, and manganese associated with soil organic matter [35, 69]. Following shaking and centrifugation, the supernatant was decanted, and then for this, and all subsequent steps, the remaining solids were then rinsed with 20 mL deionized water, shaken further for 2 h and then centrifuged again at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The first supernatant and the rinse supernatant were combined and then filtered. Poorly crystalline minerals (e.g. ferrihydrite, imogolite) were extracted by adding 30 mL 0.5 M hydroxylamine-HCl to the pellet (prepared in 0.25 M HCl solution), which targets non-crystalline inorganic iron, aluminum, and organic carbon [69], followed by a rinse step with 20 mL deionized water. Hydroxylamine is also a carbon free extractant with a greater specificity for poorly crystalline minerals compared to oxalate [70], and solutions are more easily analyzed by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) than the traditionally used oxalate [71, 72]. The final stage of the dissolution focused on crystalline minerals and used 30 mL 0.05 M sodium dithionite which acts as a strong reducing agent, to extract crystalline iron oxyhydroxides such as goethite and hematite [73], followed by a rinse step with 20 mL 0.05 M HCl.

Operational pools extracted with deionized water, pyrophosphate, hydroxylamine and dithionite are considered as functional MAOC pools, referred to herein as water soluble, organo-metal, poorly crystalline and crystalline, respectively.

The organic carbon concentrations in the extracts were determined using a Shimadzu TOC-L Total Organic Carbon Analyzer. Iron (Fe) concentrations in the extracts was determined using flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer AAnalyte 300).

Chemical character of SOC pools

The aromatic chemical character of soil dissolved organic carbon extracts was determined using ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometry. Specific UV absorbance (SUVA), measured at certain wavelengths (e.g. 254 nm; SUVA254) is positively correlated with, and can be used as, a low-cost and simple indicator of, the aromatic compounds in DOC [51, 53]. In soils, MAOC can be mobilized (naturally or using chemical extractions), quantified, and characterized as DOC.

Absorbance spectra of soil extract samples were determined using a dual-beam Genesys 10 S UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and 1 cm path length quartz cuvettes. Spectroscopic analysis of DOC can be affected because of interference from several factors, including Fe and nitrate content and turbidity [53]. Absorbance values were corrected for absorption contributions of Fe in each selective dissolution soil extract [74, 75]. The extinction coefficient for 254 nm (254) was found in Poulin et al. [75]. SUVA254 values were calculated by dividing the Fe corrected absorbance at 254 nm by the DOC concentration and is reported in units of L mgC−1 m−1 as a measure of aromaticity [53]. All extracts were filtered to 0.45 μm so all had similar (negligible) values of turbidity, with low soil N mineralization rates and availability [76]. Values of δ13C for the organo-mineral fraction were from Diochon and Kellman [20]. Total mineral soil C storage in the fractions was calculated using bulk density values from Diochon et al., [13] (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Simple linear regressions were used to investigate relationships between DOC aromaticity (SUVA) of the organo-metal mineral fraction and bulk soil profile C storage in: i) shallow (0–20 cm) and deep (20–50 cm) soils for all sites combined and ii), through depth at individual sites. We would expect to observe decreases in SUVA correlated with reduced soil C storage if increased microbial processing of C was driving SOC losses from the mineral soil. Through soil depth, decreases in aromaticity (SUVA) of DOC extracted from the organo-metal fraction would be expected if this C pool experienced increased microbial processing relative to other depth increments. No relationship between aromaticity (SUVA) and C storage or depth increment within soils would suggest changes in microbial processing (decomposition) were not responsible for SOC losses or were obscured by other differences in surface plant-derived C inputs. We examined linear relationships between pyrophosphate extractable DOC and Fe in shallow (0–20 cm) and deep (20–50 cm) soils, as co-variation between these fractions would indicate that DOC is stabilized through organo-metal complexation with Fe.

Linear relationships between aromaticity (SUVA) of mineral-extracted DOC and δ13C for the organo-mineral fraction were examined for all sites and depths combined and for individual sites to determine whether enrichment in 13C of SOC in the organo-mineral pool correlated with decreased microbial processing of organo-metal extracted DOC. We would expect to observe a correlation between decreased aromaticity (SUVA) and increased organo-mineral fraction soil δ13C if increased microbial processing were responsible for C losses in this fraction in both the full dataset and individual sites.

Data analyses were completed using SigmaPlot 13.0 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Significance was assessed at α = 0.05. The extractions were carried out in triplicate for each composite sample and the mean values presented herein reflect the mean of the analytical replicates. There was no attempt to statistically compare the mean values using an analysis of variance because values are means of the analytical replicates. Linear regressions were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and constant variance (Spearman Rank Correlation).

Results

Soil carbon content

Carbon concentrations for each depth interval and mineral pool across the chronosequence (Table 2; Fig. 1) show the organo-metal complexed pool represents the largest pool at all sites and depth increments, ranging from approximately 3–23 mg C g soil− 1. Overall C concentrations in the water soluble, poorly crystalline, and crystalline pools were generally low in comparison (< 4.1, < 2.5, and < 5.4 mg C g soil− 1, respectively) (Table 2).

In the shallow mineral soil (0–20 cm depth), changes in the MAOC concentrations were variable across the chronosequence with no clear temporal trend evident (Table 2; Fig. 1). Carbon concentrations in the organo-metal fraction represented ~ 67–81% of the MAOC within the shallow mineral soil. Water soluble C represented less than 12% of the MAOC pool in shallow mineral soils, with the 15-year and 125+-year sites containing the lowest and highest concentrations, respectively. Carbon associated with poorly crystalline minerals held ~ 5–10% of total MAOC in the top 20 cm of mineral soil. Within the crystalline fraction representing ~ 4–15% of the total MAOC, the old growth site (125+-years) had the highest C concentrations, nearly three times as much as the recent clearcut (1-year).

In contrast to the shallow mineral soil, a clear temporal trend of C loss was observed in the deeper mineral soil across the chronosequence (Table 2; Fig. 1). In the organo-metal complexed pool (representing ~ 59–85% of total MAOC), significant differences between C concentrations were observed between the 125+-year site and the 15-, 45-, and 80-year sites for both depth intervals (20–35 and 35–50 cm). Water soluble C represented less than 6% of the MAOC fraction in deep mineral soils. Carbon associated with poorly crystalline minerals accounted for ~ 7–21% of total deep soil C. In the crystalline fraction representing ~ 4–16% of the total MAOC, C concentrations were highest in the 125+-year site and lowest in the 45-year site (Table 2).

Overall, C storage in the organo-metal fraction is highest at the old growth 125 + yr site (96 Mg C ha− 1) and lowest at the 15-year-old site (35 Mg C ha− 1) (Fig. 2). Changes in the poorly crystalline and crystalline pools also demonstrate losses, however these pools are small compared to the losses in the organo-metal complexed fraction. Because it represents the largest fraction the subsequent chemical character analyses for these soils are examined for the organo-metal fraction only.

Relationships between pyrophosphate extractable C and Fe for shallow and deep (Fig. 3) soils show a weak relationship for shallow (0–20 cm) mineral soils (Fig. 3a); a strong linear relationship for deep (20–50 cm) soil (Fig. 3b) is consistent with mobilization of C from organo-metal complexes.

Carbon chemical character (SUVA)

Aromaticity of SOC was examined in relation to MAOC quantity (Fig. 4). Relationships between Fe-corrected SUVA254 and C storage within the organo-metal complexed fraction show no clear patterns in the shallow mineral soil (Fig. 4a); within the deeper mineral soil (Fig. 4b), a positive relationship was found between SUVA and C storage. The variability is high but collectively represents a broad group of sites that represent different times since disturbance (1 year, 15 year, 45 year, 80 year), or no history of disturbance (125 + yr).

At each site a strong linear relationship between aromaticity (SUVA) and depth was evident (Fig. 5). Aromaticity (SUVA) decreased through depth for all sites in the organo-metal fraction (Fig. 5). The pattern of decreasing aromaticity with depth is enhanced at the 15- and 45-yr site relative to the reference site. While gradual recovery of the SUVA-depth relationship was documented after 15 yrs, a full recovery to that documented for the 125 + yr site was not observed at the 80 year site.

Relationships between SUVA and δ13C

Relationships between SUVA and δ13C for the organo-metal SOC fraction show that across all sites and depths, as aromaticity (SUVA) decreases, SOC becomes enriched in δ13C (Fig. 6) (R2 0.84; P < 0.001). To examine if this pattern holds across the chronosequence, this relationship is further broken down by site (Fig. 7). At individual sites strong relationships between aromaticity (SUVA) and δ13C were observed (Fig. 7). The linear relationship for the reference 125 + yr site reflected the narrow range of SUVA- δ13C values compared to the disturbed sites. Across the disturbed sites, this steady state trend (Fig. 7) of enrichment in δ13C with decreasing aromaticity (SUVA) changes significantly (P < 0.05) through time since harvest before returning to values similar to the reference site at 80 yrs.

Discussion

Clearcut forest harvesting is a routine practice in temperate North American forests, however the impacts of these activities on conceptually “stable” SOC in mineral soils remains a source of uncertainty in our understanding of forest soil C budgets [11]. Previous work at the chronosequence sites studied here document soil C storage estimates for these sites and soil C and nitrogen (N) storage losses over a period of decades associated with clearcut harvesting [13, 76]. A physical fractionation approach [20] identified MAOC (> 1.8 g cm− 3) as the largest soil C pool which was most susceptible to harvesting induced losses. Combined soil profile C content and isotopic patterns pointed to decomposition as a driving mechanism for the observed patterns [32]. Here, we focus on an analysis of the mineral and metal associated organic C using sequential extractions and consider whether observed chemical changes provide indicators that these conceptually stable pools may be vulnerable to harvesting disturbance. This analysis is restricted to the MAOC and does not attempt to draw conclusions about bulk C and POM.

Harvesting-induced changes in the dominant mineral-associated C pools identified using sequential chemical extractions

At all sites and sampled depth intervals within the chronosequence soils studied here, the organo-metal complexed pool represented > 60% (and as high as 85%) of all MAOC pools in these soils (Table 2; Fig. 1). This is consistent with other studies showing a connection between organo-metal complexation and long-term C storage [15, 22, 36], including another study in the same region [21]. All other MAOC pools in this study were similar in size and varied in their relative importance, depending on site and depth (Fig. 1). No clear changes in MAOC were observed in the upper profile (0–20 cm depth) over the decadal timescales investigated here (Table 2), but the organo-metal complexed C was the dominant MAOC fraction and drove temporal trends in the deep and combined mineral soil profile (20–50 cm) (Fig. 2). This, combined with the co-mobilization of Fe and DOC in pyrophosphate-extractable organo-metal associations (Fig. 3), suggests that forest harvesting drives destabilization of the mineral soil C reported in Diochon et al. [13] and Diochon and Kellman [20]. Profile patterns and harvesting age related patterns observed in this study are consistent with the findings of Gabriel et al. [21] from their analysis of sites representing a 35 year and 110 + yr reference stand in Podzolic soils in the same region. The increased mobilization of both Fe and Al in pyrophosphate extractable C from organo-metal pools in the Gabriel et al. [21] study also points to a pattern of destabilization of organo-metal associations through soil depth. If the persistence of SOC in mineral soils is largely a function of microbial access to SOM [2, 5], cyclical disturbances such as forest harvesting can result in changing soil conditions that have the potential to destabilize this large C pool, increasing microbial access to organic substrates [17, 77, 78]. Importantly, the results of this study demonstrate that organo-mineral associations, especially those containing redox sensitive minerals such as Fe, may not necessarily confer protection from microbial decomposition, which may be driven by other complex biogeochemical processes, including the cycling of Fe [79].

Chemical character of organo-mineral associated SOC

While measurement of soil C storage provides one tool for establishing disturbance driven changes in soil C [28, 80], confidence in data documenting SOC losses can be increased if there are observations that further support C concentration patterns. To date, stable isotopes have provided one option for supporting field observations (e.g. [81]).

A number of hypotheses have been proposed to explain the soil profile trends of 13C isotopic enrichment with depth, including mixing of above- and below-ground biomass sources, and kinetic fractionation [81, 82]. Previous investigation of the soils studied here determined that isotopic patterns could not be explained by mixing of isotopically different SOC within the soil profile and was therefore likely not the dominant process for observed isotopic enrichment trends: these patterns were instead explained by kinetic fractionation arising from increased decomposition of this C pool [32]. Furthermore, isotopic analyses of these separate MAOC pools by Gabriel et al. [21] revealed that the MAOC pools do have distinct isotopic signatures, with the organo-metal pool exhibiting δ13C signatures close to that of the bulk SOC.

In this study we also extend the use of a standard spectroscopic measurement (SUVA) to examine the chemical character of MAOC across a harvesting chronosequence, using the aromatic nature of mineral-bound SOC pools. While previous studies have used SUVA to demonstrate increased DOC exchange with MAOC in undisturbed soil profiles [39] and the nature of DOC transported in aquatic systems [51, 83], this study explores its use as an indicator of increased alteration following disturbance in mineral-bound C over forest harvest timescales.

The patterns of aromaticity in C extracted from the organo-metal complexed pool (as indicated by SUVA254) show that links between soil C storage and aromaticity vary between the shallow and deep soil profile (Fig. 4). While no significant relationship was found between aromaticity (SUVA) and C storage in the shallow soil (0–20 cm) at these sites, a significant relationship (P < 0.001) was found between SUVA and C storage in the deeper soil (20–50 cm) for all individual soil profiles (Fig. 5). These patterns suggest that as the SOC pool declines at depth following harvesting, the chemical character of SOC held within the dominant MAOC pool changes, becoming less aromatic. In surface soils, the net changes in the chemical character are either quantitatively unimportant or are obscured by shorter-term dynamics associated with changes in soil surface vegetation, changes to inputs post-harvest, and potential changes to podzolization processes [27, 28, 30]; deeper soils likely filter out some of the shorter-term variability in the C dynamics. Patterns of decreasing aromaticity are expected with depth in a stable soil profile as a function of increased processing of C [39]. But in a profile where disturbance may have destabilized MAOC pools, we would expect this pattern to be further enhanced if there is greater alteration of this C pool. The pattern observed across these sites (Fig. 5) is consistent with increased alteration of this MAOC pool in the decades following disturbance, with the greatest profile differences through soil depth observed at the 15 and 45 year sites.

Links between SUVA and δ13C

The δ13C patterns linked with SUVA measurements described here provide further support for the conceptual hypothesis that decomposition of destabilized SOC is likely the dominant mechanism driving C losses following forest harvesting at these sites. The combined relationship between δ13C previously reported at these sites for MAOC fractions [20], and SUVA measurements collected for all sites and depths in this study show that when δ13C increases, aromaticity decreases (Fig. 6). While the isotopic δ13C data used in this analysis represents all MAOC rather than just the organo-metal fractions as characterized in the SUVA analyses, the organo-metal fraction dominates this MAOC pool (Fig. 2) so differences in δ13C between this and the organo-metal pool isolated in this study are likely minor.

At the 125 + yr reference site (Fig. 7), the depth-dependent relationships between SUVA and δ13C are consistent with shifts in SOC properties with depth observed in stable soil profiles that have been attributed to a shift from fresh plant material (high SUVA, depleted in 13C) to highly altered OC (low SUVA, enriched in 13C) [39, 81]. Changes in SUVA became more pronounced with depth 15- and 45-years following disturbance (Fig. 7), with C in the deeper soil exhibiting a less aromatic character (lower SUVA) compared to the C at the old growth reference site at the same depth. This is consistent with greater processing of C in the decades following forest harvesting and suggests a pattern in δ13C and SUVA that are consistent with changes in the storage of plant-derived organic matter. While these patterns emerge in the Podzol soils of this study region, we cannot rule out the possibility that preferential degradation of organo-metal complexes containing more aromatic structures may also play a role through demonstrated strength of Fe bonds with some organic N compounds [84, 85]. Further investigations are needed to develop a more complete understanding of the complex relationships between the sequential MAOC extractions and functional SOC pools.

The aromatic chemical character analyses described though analysis of SUVA help verify that changes in the MAOC pool are not simply a function of natural variability in soil C storage between locations, but rather indicative of a shift in the dynamics that control C storage in mineral soils. SUVA analysis provides a cost-effective alternative to stable isotope analysis for tracking disturbance-induced changes in soil C chemistry.

Conclusions

Despite the importance of the global soil C pool, we have limited understanding of mineral soil C dynamics and its susceptibility to land use and climate change. The biogeochemical patterns of SOC examined in this study demonstrated changes in mineral associated C through time and with depth following forest harvesting. Within the dominant organo-metal mineral fraction, SOC illustrated distinct patterns in combined aromatic and δ13C content of SOC across all sites, supporting the conceptual hypothesis that forest harvesting alters the biogeochemical character and protection of SOC in soils of this region. While variations are expected in these processes globally, regional- and site-specific biogeochemical studies such as the one employed here provide insight into mechanisms that control SOC retention in soils, and biogeochemical approaches that may be helpful in documenting shifts in this important global C pool.
