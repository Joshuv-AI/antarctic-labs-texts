A 3D-Printed Transoral Guide Device to Aid Single-Person Fiberoptic Intubation: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Tong Wu, Mengjuan Dong, Hongyuan Sui, Wei Chen, Yulong Wang, Yongquan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided intubation (FOI) is a reliable method for managing difficult airways, typically requiring a 2-operator technique using jaw thrust. This study introduces a novel 3D-printed transoral guiding device (TGD) enabling single-operator FOI without jaw thrust. The aim was to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of TGD compared to a modified oropharyngeal airway (MOA) with assistant-applied jaw thrust.Material/MethodsA total of 218 patients undergoing elective orotracheal intubation were randomized into 2 groups: TGD and MOA. After anesthesia induction, Group TGD underwent FOI using the TGD without jaw thrust, while Group MOA used the MOA combined with jaw thrust. Outcome measures included time to successful intubation, first-attempt success rate, number of attempts or device adjustments, and airway visibility (epiglottis and bronchoscope view).ResultsSuccessful FOI was achieved in all patients in the TGD group versus 95.4% in the MOA group (P=0.024). The TGD group had significantly shorter times to visualize the vocal cords and tracheal carina (both P<0.001) and to complete intubation (P<0.001). First-attempt success was higher in the TGD group (99.1%) than in the MOA group (67.0%; P<0.001). Fewer attempts and device adjustments were needed in the TGD group.ConclusionsThe 3D-printed transoral guiding device (TGD) facilitates faster, more effective single-operator FOI without jaw thrust, offering a promising alternative to traditional 2-operator techniques.Trial registration https://www.chictr.org.cn/hvshowproject.html?id=87316&v=1.4,ChiCTR2100042607

Introduction

Airway management is a topic of great interest to anesthesiologists, but airway assessment in clinical work is often very subjective, and more than 90% of challenging airway cases are unexpected [1]. If airway management fails, serious complications can ensue, including cardiac arrest, severe hypoxia, and even death [2,3].

For patients with anticipated difficult airways, awake oral or nasal tracheal intubation remains the standard method. However, management of unanticipated difficult airways poses a significant clinical challenge to anesthesiologists, where rapid and secure airway establishment becomes critical to prevent hypoxic complications. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided tracheal intubation is an effective and reliable method for difficult airways in clinical practice. While FOI is a well-established and reliable technique for managing anticipated difficult airways, it is important to note that no robust evidence suggests it is superior to conventional direct laryngoscopy in patients without predicted airway challenges. Its utility in routine practice is therefore not universally advocated.

Fiberoptic intubation through the oral cavity can be questionable for patients with unpredictable difficult airway. In a state of deep sedation or anesthesia, without protective oral reflexes, the soft tissues of the oral cavity (eg, soft palate, tongue, uvula) and epiglottis move back to the posterior wall of the throat, resulting in decreased oropharyngeal space [4]. The fiberoptic bronchoscope is soft and cannot push past this tissue to the exposed glottis to guide endotracheal intubation. To solve this problem, a modified oropharyngeal airway (MOA) device has been reported for use in fiberoptic bronchoscope intubation [5–7], but it has some limitations. First, the front end of the device cannot lift the epiglottis, which often leads to difficulty in passing the fiberoptic bronchoscope through. Second, its use must be combined with jaw thrust [8]. The MOA device requires at least 2 people to complete fiberoptic bronchoscope intubation. Third, the MOA leaves the patient without an oxygen channel, which leads to serious risk for developing hypoxemia in patients with difficult airways. These issues severely limit its use, so we designed a transoral guide device (TGD) to aid fiberoptic intubation.

There have been few studies on how to quickly use fiberoptic bronchoscope to complete oral tracheal intubation under general anesthesia when an unpredictably difficult airway is encountered. Under anesthesia, fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided endotracheal intubation can be completed by a single person, which has not been reported before. Our TGD provides a new method for oral tracheal intubation under fibrobronchoscopy guidance. We assessed the effects of our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide and improved oropharyngeal airway on endotracheal intubation after induction of general anesthesia. This study aimed to evaluate whether the self-designed transoral guide device (TGD) facilitates single-operator fiberoptic intubation without requiring jaw thrust and to compare its performance with the modified oropharyngeal airway (MOA).

Material and Methods

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki after the ethics committee approved the registration of clinical trials on March 27, 2021 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry Hong Kong Center, Hong Kong Baptist University Road, Hong Kong SAR, China, Ethics Approval Number: ChiECRCT20210062) and it was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Register number: ChiCTR2100042607). All subjects provided written informed consent. Patients undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia between March 2021 and November 2023 in Yijishan Hospital Affiliated to Wannan Medical College and requiring tracheal intubation were included. Inclusion criteria for this study were patients aged 18 to 75 years with an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification of I, II, or III for transoral intubation. Exclusion criteria were (1) Patients requiring awake intubation; (2) Mouth opening <3 cm; (3) Thyromental distance <6 cm; (4) Neck circumference >40 cm; (5) Upper lip bite test class III; (6) BMI >40 kg/m2; (7) History of failed endotracheal intubation; (8) Poorly controlled circulatory or respiratory diseases that are potentially life-threatening; (9) Emergency surgery. The CONSORT flow diagram summarizes patient enrollment and allocation (Figure 1). Patient enrollment proceeded consecutively. All eligible patients presenting for elective surgery during the study period were approached for participation and enrolled upon providing written informed consent, without any selective criteria beyond the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

All basic patient information was recorded (Table 1). We monitored basic vital signs, including non-invasive blood pressure, heart rate, and pulse oximetry. The Mindray T6 monitor (Mindray Inc., Shenzhen, China) was used to measure these indices. The anesthesia machine used was an Aesprie 7900 and the fiberoptic bronchoscope was an Insighters iS3-C5 with an external diameter of 5.4 mm. The TGD was made by 3D printing technology using a model drawing. The TGD device we designed (application number or patent number: 202110457727.2, Figure 2) is accompanied with an oxygen channel at the front of the glottis to prevent hypoxemia and reduce carbon dioxide levels during FOI. There is scientific evidence that deep insufflation of oxygen in the laryngeal area and trachea during apneic episodes can avoid hypoxemia [9–11]. With a leaf-shaped design for the front end of the device, it needs little force to lift the epiglottis without using jaw thrust. Only 1 person is required to achieve a transoral fiberoptic intubation.

The patients were randomly assigned to one of 2 groups: the TGD group or the MOA group. The jaw thrust maneuver was only applied to the MOA group. Our intubation team consisted of 3 anesthesiologists: a senior anesthesiologist with over 15 years of FOI experience, an experimental data recorder (to avoid bias), and a junior anesthesiologist. In both groups, fiberoptic tracheal intubation was attempted by the junior anesthesiologist under the guidance of the senior anesthesiologist. Double blinding was not possible to achieve in this study because the junior anesthesiologist was the operator and to carry out the assessment of the airway and intubation, the airway had to be observed through the field-of-view of the fiberoptic scope. Patients were randomly allocated to either TGD group or MOA group using a computer-generated randomization sequence with variable block sizes. To ensure concealment, assignments were sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes, which were opened by the operator just before the procedure. While the operator was necessarily unblinded, a separate blinded assessor analyzed all video-recorded intubation outcomes to minimize assessment bias.

All patients were preoxygenated before induction of general anesthesia with an oxygen flow rate of 6–8 L/min. Total intravenous anesthesia was induced by intravenous administration of midazolam, sufentanil, etomidate, and cisatracurium, and fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided transtracheal intubation was carried out according to the results of randomized grouping after cisatracurium had taken effect.

Patients were positioned supine without a pillow, with the head in a neutral position. According to randomization, either the fiberoptic transoral guide device (TGD) or modified oropharyngeal airway (MOA) was inserted, followed by fiberoptic-guided transtracheal intubation. We used a standardized fiberoptic-guided orotracheal intubation protocol. The procedure consisted of 3 key steps: (1) The fiberoptic bronchoscope, preloaded with an appropriately sized endotracheal tube (7.5 mm internal diameter [ID] for males, 7.0 mm ID for females), was inserted through the guiding device to initiate intubation; (2) Removal of the guiding device after direct visual confirmation of tracheal placement (verified by carina identification) due to diameter limitations; and (3) Advancement of the endotracheal tube to 22±1 cm from the incisors with position confirmation by continuous capnography before secure fixation. Importantly, all TGD-assisted intubations were successfully performed by a single operator without jaw lift assistance, whereas MOA procedures consistently required 2 operators (one maintaining jaw thrust and another manipulating the bronchoscope). The position of the device tip was assessed via direct fiberoptic visualization and classified into 3 categories: A, B, or C (Figure 3). When the front end of the TGD is at position C, after slight backward adjustment, it can be placed in the epiglottis valley (position A) or under the epiglottis (position B), so that the epiglottis is away from the glottis. The position of the device tip was assessed and categorized intraoperatively based on direct fiberoptic visualization into one of 3 types: A (optimal), B (acceptable), or C (requiring adjustment). This assessment, while clinically relevant, is inherently subjective and based on the operator’s interpretation. When the anterior end is in the epiglottis valley or below the epiglottis, a clear glottis is directly exposed. By gently applying a downward force on the end of the transoral guide with the right hand, the epiglottis can be lifted to directly expose the vocal folds. At the same time, the left hand adjusts the direction of the fiberoptic scope to enter the trachea through the glottis. The number of airway adjustments and the position of the front end of the airway above or below the epiglottis were recorded (Table 2).

The operator of the fiberoptic scope (the junior anesthetist) assessed the intubation procedure based on an 11-point visual analogue scale (VAS, 0=very difficult, 10=very easy), while the resistance to advancement of the catheter by endotracheal intubation was recorded in both groups (Tables 2, 3). To assess the causes of obstruction to fiberoptic passage during fiberoptic intubation, we used a bronchoscopic field of view grading proposed by Greenland et al [12] (Table 1). We used the scoring system proposed by Jones et al to assess the ease of intubation [13] (Table 1). To assess the glottis levels, we used a scale that was also based on previous reports [14].

During tracheal intubation, if pulse oximetry (SpO2) was <90%, tracheal intubation was immediately terminated as a failed intubation, and ventilation with a 100% oxygen mask was started to improve oxygenation. Intubation was also defined as a failure if the tracheal intubation was not completed within 60 s [15]. If FOI was unsuccessful after 3 attempts, it was recorded as failed fiberoptic-guided tracheal intubation. In these failed cases, we used other alternative techniques for intubation, such as plain laryngoscope, visual laryngoscope, and light bar.

The primary outcomes of this study were: (1) Time to successful first-attempt intubation, defined as the time from the start of fiberoptic bronchoscope passage through the edge of the airway until confirmation successful tracheal intubation under direct vision, measured only in patients with successful intubation on the first attempt. (2) Overall intubation success rate, calculated as the proportion of patients who ultimately achieved successful fiberoptic tracheal intubation with the device among all patients in whom it was used. The secondary outcomes of this study were: Additional time metrics: Time to vocal cord visualization (from bronchoscope insertion past the device edge to full vocal cord exposure), Time to carina visualization (from bronchoscope advancement beyond the device edge to clear carina exposure); Visualization scores: grading of vocal fold exposure and grading of fiberoptic field of view; procedural characteristics comprising intubation resistance, number of device adjustments, and ease of intubation; safety endpoints covering oral mucosal injury (defined as visible blood on the device upon removal), postoperative complications, including sore throat and hoarseness on the day of surgery, and changes in hemodynamic parameters (blood pressure, heart rate) and oxygen saturation (SpO2) from pre- to post-intubation; and operator assessment reflected in the VAS score recorded at the time of intubation.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size calculation for this study was initially based on pilot data (10 patients per group). The primary outcome, first-attempt successful intubation time, was found to be non-normally distributed in the pilot data. Therefore, data are appropriately presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). The TGD group demonstrated a shorter median intubation time compared to the MOA group (30.0 s [IQR: 25.0–35.0 s] versus 34.0 s [IQR: 24.0–58.0 s]). An initial calculation for a Mann-Whitney U test (the non-parametric test chosen for the primary analysis), based on the observed effect size and variability, indicated that a sample size of 32 per group would provide 90% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. After accounting for an estimated 10% dropout rate, this yielded a total recruitment target of 72 patients. However, this sample size was expected to yield an unacceptably wide confidence interval around the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the median difference.

Therefore, a precision-based calculation was performed. The goal was to constrain the width of the 95% confidence interval for the median difference to a clinically acceptable margin. Based on the IQRs from the pilot data, this calculation indicated that a final analyzable sample of 218 patients (109 per group) would be required to achieve this precision. This sample size also comfortably exceeds the requirement for statistical power. Participants were randomly assigned via computerized randomization (SAS 9.4, 1:1 allocation) to evaluate the effectiveness of the device.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). For categorical variables, data are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons of normally distributed continuous variables were performed using the independent samples t-test. Non-parametric data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U rank sum test. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Data on hemodynamic changes were compared using ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant at P<0.05.

Results

A total of 305 patients undergoing tracheal intubation for elective surgery from March 2021 to November 2023 were included in the study; 218 patients met the inclusion criteria, with 109 patients in each group. There was no statistically significant difference in the baseline characteristics of the patients in both groups (Table 4).

All 109 patients in the TGD group achieved fiberoptic transoral intubation, and 104/109 patients in the MOA group completed fiberoptic intubation, with a significant difference in the success rates between the 2 groups, at 100% and 95.4%, respectively (P<0.05). The success rate of first-time intubation in the 2 groups was 99.1% and 67%, respectively, with a statistically significant difference of P<0.001. The number of intubation attempts in the TGD group was less than that of the MOA group (Table 5), and the result was statistically significant (P<0.001). The time to successful intubation was 12 seconds faster in the TGD group than in the MOA group (P<0.001). The time to expose the vocal cords was also shorter in the TGD group than in the MOA group (P<0.001). The time to expose the tracheal carina in the TGD group was also shorter than in the MOA group (P<0.001).

The TGD group was superior to the MOA group in both glottic exposure grading and fiberoptic field grading (P<0.001 for both). The VAS score for fiberoptic intubation was significantly higher in the TGD group compared to the MOA group (9 vs 8; P<0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of oral mucosal damage, sore throat, and hoarseness between the 2 groups (Table 6). However, there were statistically significant differences in the ease of intubation, intubation resistance and the number of device adjustments. After placing the TGD in the oral cavity, the anterior end of the device could be present in one of 3 positions – A, B and C (Figure 3) – with 22/109 (20.2%) in position A, 54/109 (49.5%) in position B, and 33/109 (30.3%) in position C. Therefore, 69.7% of the patients in the TGD group could be intubated without the need to adjust the position of the device (Table 2). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that hemodynamic changes during tracheal intubation were not significantly different between the 2 groups in terms of mean arterial pressure (MAP) and pulse oximetry (SpO2). The time factor of heart rate (HR) was statistically significant and the fluctuation of heart rate stimulation in the TGD group was smaller than that in the MOA group (Table 7).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that use of the TGD significantly reduced the time to successful tracheal intubation compared to the MOA method. The median intubation time was markedly shorter in the TGD group (23.0 s [IQR, 20.0–26.8]) than in the MOA group (35.0 s [IQR, 26.0–45.0]), a difference that was statistically significant (P<0.001, Mann-Whitney U test). The 12-s reduction in median intubation time (approximately a 34% decrease relative to the MOA approach) is clinically meaningful.

In our study, the exposure time of glottis was reduced by 50%, the successful intubation time was increased by 12 s, and the success rate of one-attempt intubation was reduced by 32.1%. This may be because the self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide can provide free operation space for auxiliary fibrobronchoscope to guide endotracheal intubation, and the shape of the fiberoptic transoral guide was based on the anatomy of the airway and its curvature fit the curvature of the mouth to the vocal folds. The fiberoptic bronchoscope is inserted into the oral cavity through an oral guide, usually located at or near the midline of the oral cavity, which can cause soft tissues such as the tongue to move forward and expose the glottis away from the posterior pharyngeal wall. In addition, when performing fiberoptic bronchoscope oral tracheal intubation, we apply a gentle backward and downward force at the end of the device, so that the laryngoscope pick at the front end of the device can lift the epiglottis and expose a clear glottic field of vision, thus greatly improving the power of the first intubation of tracheal intubation guided by the fiberoptic bronchoscope. Our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide performs epiglottis lifting function of the Macintosh laryngoscope. The difference is that when the Macintosh laryngoscope is intubated, the epiglottis is lifted with an upward force, while we only need the fiberoptic bronchoscope operator to apply a slight downward force at the end of the guide, supported by the upper gums of patients with upper teeth or who are edentulous. Using the lever principle, only a slight support is needed to lift the epiglottis and fully expose the glottis. Therefore, no other person is needed for assistance. Under anesthesia, a single person can complete tracheal intubation guided by the fiberoptic bronchoscope, which is completely different from previous studies that required others to assist in jaw lifting to complete tracheal intubation guided by fiberoptic bronchoscope under anesthesia [16,17].

In our study, we found that the average time of successful completion of tracheal intubation with fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide was 23.76 s and the median time was 23 s, both of which were shorter than previous studies. Cole et al reported that the average time used to complete fiberoptic bronchoscope tracheal intubation combined with jaw-lifting operation was 56 s, but the intubation time in our study using the TGD was considerably shorter [16]. The exposure time of glottis and tracheal carina using our fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide was 5.0 s and 11.0 s, respectively, which was also shorter than in previous reports. Greenland et al reported that the median time of exposing the glottis using Williams and Ovasapian tracheal intubators was 9 s and 12 s, respectively [12,17]. Smith et al used a similar inclusion criterion as ours, with a 60 s boundary [18]. Cases beyond the time limit were not included in the study. The median time for using the Berman airway was 34.9 s, which was much longer than the time required for our fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guidance device. In short, our fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide has significant advantages over previous studies in exposing the glottis and protrusions. Iqbal et al found 48 s was needed to see the protrusion during fiberoptic bronchoscopy intubation using Berman’s airway, while our TGD only took 11 s, which greatly shortened the required time [19]. They defined intubation failure as intubation failure when the intubation time exceeded 2 min, and based on this, the power of the first intubation was only 84%. Because it is very important to complete tracheal intubation quickly and effectively in clinical work, we defined the failure of intubation as a more stringent 60 s, and completing tracheal intubation within 1 min is more in line with clinical needs. There were 5 failed intubations in the MOA group after 3 attempts. all due to inability to intubate within 60 s. The tongue and enlarged epiglottis were near to the throat wall, making passage of the fiberoptic bronchoscope problematic. The success rate of our TGD group’s first intubation was 99.1%. Failed TGD group intubations occurred in 1 patient at first attempt, due to the front end of fiberoptic bronchoscope being difficult to move, making the intubation time exceed 60 s. Cao et al published a study using a tongue-base fixator, which observed the glottis for 6.48 s, which is also longer than our fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guide [20]. Our study shows that the time taken to observe tracheal carina is similar to what Cao’s found, but our first intubation success rate was higher, at 99.1% and provides better glottic field grading. Mendonca reported a 98% success rate for initial intubation with the i-gel intubation type laryngeal mask, which is also lower than the success rate of our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guidance device [15]. Han et al reported the success rate of tracheal intubation guided by fiberoptic bronchoscope without jaw-lifting manipulation was only 34.1%, which increased to 70.1% with assisted jaw-lifting, and the success rate was also very low [21]. Compared with previous clinical trials using different technologies to assist oral tracheal intubation with fiberoptic bronchoscopy, our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscope oral introducer has significantly improved the success rate of the first intubation and the time of successful intubation, and it does not require medical personnel to lift the jaw, so that a single person can operate the fiberoptic bronchoscope to guide tracheal intubation under anesthesia. This technology is innovative and completely different from previous research, and our device performs better than previous studies that require jaw-lifting assistance without the need for others to lift the jaw.

The fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide can significantly improve the glottis exposure classification and the fiberoptic bronchoscope visual field exposure classification. The use of the transoral guide device (TGD) resulted in a statistically significant improvement in both glottic exposure and fiberoptic field of view classifications (P<0.001). This is because our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide has a laryngoscopic blade design in its front end, which can be gently lifted to expose the glottis, providing a better visual field classification for fiberoptic bronchoscope tracheal intubation. In our study, we calculated the position of the TGD after placement into the oral cavity. When placed at position A or position B (with a total probability of 69.7%), it can directly raise the epiglottis and clearly expose the glottis. When it is at position C, the fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide gently retracts to place the front end at position A or position B, and then quickly exposes the glottis for complete tracheal intubation. With regard to fiberoptic bronchoscope oral tracheal intubation, there are relevant reports in the literature that can be applied to address the issue of constrained laryngopharyngeal or oropharyngeal space and increase the operating space in the oral cavity to better expose the fiberoptic bronchoscopy field of vision and glottis exposure field of vision. Standard approaches to fiberoptic orotracheal intubation, however, have relied on adjunctive maneuvers – often requiring an assistant – to achieve successful intubation. Examples include intubation type laryngeal mask [15,22–24], Williams airway [12,25], Ovasapian airway [17,26], and Lee intubation airway [7]. However, despite the use of these airways, the problem of partial or complete obstruction of the fiberoptic bronchoscope’s field of view is still common. In addition, these ventilation channels require assistance in lifting the jaw, making the operation cumbersome and increasing the workload of medical personnel. Some articles also reported on use of a tongue-root fixator to assist the fiberoptic bronchoscope in guiding oral tracheal intubation, but at least 3 people were required to complete the fiberoptic bronchoscope oral tracheal intubation. When difficult airways occur, this operation is very cumbersome and is not feasible in clinical work. More importantly, our self-designed TGD enabled a superior first-attempt success rate alongside significantly improved glottic view grades, all without requiring jaw-lift assistance. This performance exceeds that reported by Mendonca et al, where only 77% of cases achieved a grade 1 glottic view – a figure far lower than in the present study [15]. This suggests that our fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guide can provide better grading of glottic exposure and fiberoptic bronchoscopy field of view.

In terms of mucosal injury, there was no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups. The one-attempt success rate of intubation is high, the number of equipment adjustments is reduced, and the damage of oral mucosa caused by repeated adjustment of equipment for tracheal intubation is reduced. The fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide also has an oxygen delivery channel that directly reaches the glottis to slow the rate of decrease in oxygen partial pressure in the blood. The low number of intubation attempts required with our TGD indicates it is a highly efficient procedure. We hypothesize that this efficiency may lead to better patient oxygenation (a slower rate of oxygen partial pressure drop) and slower carbon dioxide rise by minimizing apnea time and airway trauma. This interpretation is consistent with findings in prior studies [27].

In summary, our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guide has the following advantages. First, it is very thin (1.5 cm), making it very suitable for patients with limited mouth opening. Second, there is no need to assist in jaw lifting during intubation, which is completely different from previous studies and it requires a minimum range of cervical motion, suitable for patients with cervical spine injuries and limited neck mobility. Third, there is no need to lift the jaw, and a single person can complete the intubation, making it more efficient and convenient. Fourth, the device is designed with an oxygen absorption channel, which can extend the patient’s oxygen and decrease the rate of descent, reducing the accumulation of carbon dioxide. In addition, the space supported by the fiberoptic bronchoscope oral guide can also be used as a sputum suction channel or as a regular oropharyngeal airway to open the airway and improve ventilation.

Our research also has some limitations. First, while our study employed a rigorous randomization process, it was intentionally limited to ASA I-III patients to prioritize safety during this initial device evaluation. This design limits the generalizability of our results to higher-risk patients (eg, BMI >40, severe cardiopulmonary compromise, need for awake intubation) and in more challenging clinical environments, such as the emergency department or intensive care unit. Future studies will include these populations to determine the device’s practicality for broader clinical use. We are considering including patients in future studies for a more comprehensive assessment of the practicality of the equipment. Second, the sample size was relatively small and the statistical analysis ability was limited. Third, further research is needed to investigate the role of oral guided oxygen channels through fiberoptic bronchoscopy in slowing the decrease in oxygen partial pressure in the blood. Fourth, we did not prospectively record or stratify patients based on dental status (eg, dentate, partially edentulous, completely edentulous, or with dentures). This is a recognized limitation, as the design of the TGD device inherently relies on engagement with the maxillary arch (teeth or gums) to elevate the epiglottis and facilitate glottic exposure. Consequently, factors such as tooth loss, alveolar ridge resorption, or the presence of dentures could theoretically influence the stability of the device and its effectiveness. While our cohort likely included a mix of dental conditions, reflecting a real-world clinical scenario, the lack of systematic assessment prevented us from performing a definitive subgroup analysis to ascertain this effect. Therefore, the performance metrics of the TGD reported here should be interpreted as an aggregate measure across varying dental statuses. We strongly recommend that future validation studies prospectively designate dental status as a key stratification variable to precisely quantify its impact on intubation success, time, and operator ease, thereby ensuring the device’s generalizability across diverse patient populations. Fifth, this study primarily enrolled patients with normal airway anatomy. The performance and safety of the TGD device in patients with specific oropharyngeal pathologies such as tumors, trauma, recent surgery, or congenital malformations remain unknown. These conditions could potentially contraindicate device use due to the risk of tissue injury, bleeding, or device dislodgement. Therefore, we recommend careful patient selection and avoiding use of the TGD in cases with known obstructive pathologies or friable tissues. Future studies should focus on establishing clear contraindications and evaluating the device’s safety profile in these high-risk populations. Finally, the classification of the device’s tip position (A, B, C) was based on intra-procedural visual assessment via the fiberoptic bronchoscope rather than objective radiographic confirmation. While this method is clinically relevant and practical, it is inherently subjective and operator-dependent. This subjective component is a study limitation, and future validation studies would benefit from using complementary objective imaging techniques, such as lateral radiography, to corroborate these visual classifications.

In addition, with the development of painless fiberoptic bronchoscopy diagnosis and treatment technology, the application of our self-designed fiberoptic bronchoscopy oral guide provides a potential feasibility for quickly exposing the glottis to complete airway examinations.

Clinical Implications and Scope of Application

The findings of this study naturally raise the question of the most appropriate clinical role for the TGD device. Based on our results, we propose that the TGD is not intended to replace conventional direct laryngoscopy for routine orotracheal intubation in patients with no predicted airway difficulties. Instead, its primary value lies in the context of fiberoptic intubation (FOI). Specifically, the TGD appears to be an ideal adjunct for patients who require FOI due to an anticipated difficult airway (eg, limited cervical spine mobility, restricted mouth opening, or pathological oropharyngeal conditions). In such patients, it is a true single-operator technique by obviating the need for a dedicated assistant to perform jaw thrust, thereby potentially increasing the efficiency and availability of FOI. Furthermore, its design, which provides a clear and stable conduit, may also make it a valuable rescue option when initial conventional or video laryngoscopy attempts have failed and an unplanned FOI is required. Ultimately, the TGD should be considered a specialized tool to simplify and improve the performance of FOI in selected patients for whom FOI is already indicated.

Conclusions

This study of the use of the self-designed transoral guide device (TGD) in ASA I-III patients undergoing elective surgery without anticipated difficult airways suggests that fiberoptic intubation (FOI) can be successfully completed by a single operator without the need for jaw-thrust maneuver. The findings indicate that the TGD may provide a shorter time to intubation, require fewer intubation attempts, and offer an improved glottic view compared to the MOA. Additionally, it appeared to reduce intubation resistance and ease the intubation process. However, the effectiveness and safety of the TGD in patients with known oropharyngeal pathology, higher ASA physical status (IV-V), or anticipated difficult airways remain to be fully established and require further investigation.

Based on these findings, we propose that the primary clinical role of the TGD is as an adjunct to facilitate single-operator FOI in patients who require fiberoptic intubation due to an anticipated difficult airway. It is not intended to replace conventional laryngoscopy for routine intubation in low-risk patients.
