Introduction

Fractures of the tibial plateau account for approximately 1 % of all fractures [1], and proximal tibial fractures (PTF) constitute the principal cause influencing the stability and flexibility of the knee joint [2]. Approximately 20 % of tibial plateau fractures involve the posterolateral quadrant [3], and no less than 7 % of tibial plateau fractures are located solely in the posterolateral region [4]. Research findings indicate that PLF is frequently associated with articular surface collapse and disruption of the posterolateral cortical bone within a 30 mm range [3]. It is evident that the implementation of appropriate interventions to achieve anatomical reduction in cases of PLF is of great importance. As an intra-articular partial fracture, the posterolateral fracture fragment (PLF) of the tibial plateau has long posed a significant challenge to orthopedic surgeons. This complexity arises from its unique anatomical characteristics, the risk of neurovascular injury, and the obstructive presence of the fibular head, which complicates the selection of appropriate surgical approaches and internal fixation strategies [5]. Open reduction often entails small bone fragments while the surgical approach brings about considerable trauma, which conversely exerts a certain influence on postoperative function.

For the treatment of intra-articular fractures, the crux of the surgery lies in reducing the fracture, ensuring stability and flatness of the intra-articular fracture, and facilitating the early restoration of normal movement and function of the joint. Conventionally, surgical incision is necessary to expose the fracture area. After reduction of the fracture fragments under direct vision, an internal fixation device, typically a support plate system, is placed to achieve anatomical reduction and prevent fixation loss, providing sufficient stability for early rehabilitation. Nevertheless, the fracture fragments in this region are typically covered by the fibular head and the ligamentous structures in the popliteal muscle area. When dealing with this special type of posterolateral fracture and performing open reduction and internal fixation, the posterior approach [6,7] or posterolateral approach [8,9] is frequently selected. Both these approaches heighten the potential risk of injury to the peroneal nerve and vessels [10]. A recently reported femoral osteotomy approach involves osteotomizing the femoral condyle to obtain a vision of the fracture area [[11], [12], [13]], and it requires discrimination of the lateral collateral ligament structure of the knee joint and entails the risk of nonunion.

Some scholars have also utilized the approach of combining the anterolateral incision with the posteromedial incision and employing rim plates to handle such fractures [14]. This report pertains to bicondylar posterior coronoid shear tibial plateau fractures, rather than isolated posterolateral fractures. In addition, the posterior medial incision is used to handle the lateral fracture, which causes significant trauma and cannot be extended laterally along the tibial shaft to avoid damaging the posterior tibial artery [15]. We have developed a minimally invasive reduction and fixation technique. The reason is that when using a single support reduction tool, bone fragments may tilt or rotate. Its innovation lies not only in providing multi-point support for fracture reduction, thereby maintaining the stability of reduction and facilitating the accurate placement of screws, but also in significantly reducing trauma and the risk of complications compared with previous reduction methods. The clinical and imaging results of this technique have been systematically evaluated. It may be a suitable option for treating such fractures. This case has been reported in line the SCARE criteria [16].

Presentation of case

History

A 35-year-old young female was struck by a car while walking one day prior to admission. Instantly, she experienced pain and restricted movement in her right knee joint, being unable to walk normally. In the emergency department of the local hospital, she was administered external fixation with a brace, as well as anti-swelling and analgesic treatment. The patient was brought to our hospital by her family members for further consultation. There is no previous history of medication use or allergies. During the physical examination, the posterolateral aspect of the right knee joint presented with mild swelling, without skin disruption. Localized tenderness was noted on the posterolateral side. No obvious dislocation or deformity was detectable. Passive flexion and extension of the knee joint were accompanied by pain and restricted movement. The peripheral blood supply and nerve sensation were normal.

Imaging

At a local hospital, X-ray and CT examinations were conducted, revealing a posterior lateral tibial plateau fracture. Preoperative imaging examinations are shown in Fig. 1. Based on the clinical manifestations, the preoperative diagnosis was a posterolateral fracture of the right tibial plateau. It is classified as postero-latero-central (PLC) and postero-latero-lateral (PLL) in accordance with the “Ten segment classification” [17].Fig. 1A and B represent plain radiographs of the knee joint. Surgical indications and procedure

Surgical intervention is considered imperative for the management of posterolateral fractures (PLF) with articular surface depressions exceeding 2 mm3. In this patient, the depression of the articular surface significantly exceeds 2 mm, fulfilling the surgical indications. Moreover, the patient has expressed a desire for surgical intervention.

The surgeon is a highly experienced doctor in the field of tibial plateau fractures. The entire surgical procedure does not employ tourniquets. After lumbar anesthesia, the patient assumes a supine position on the fluoroscopy-compatible operating table. Soft cushions are placed beneath the lower legs and knees to flex the knee joint at 15°, facilitating screw placement and fluoroscopy. Cefazolin sodium (2 g) was administered intravenously 30 min prior to surgery as a prophylactic anti-infective measure. The specific steps of the bamboo raft reduction are as follows: Based on the imaging data and “Ten-segment classification”, an approximately 1.5-cm incision is made on the lateral side of the tibial plateau, with a small amount of attached muscles dissected beneath. The tip of the first 2.5 Kirschner wire is obliquely drilled from the anterolateral to the posterolateral fracture site. When approaching the fracture fragment, the ends of the Kirschner wire are adjusted to prevent the tip from penetrating the fracture fragment. Subsequently, multiple Kirschner wires are drilled in parallel in the same manner, supporting the fracture fragment like a bamboo raft. Under fluoroscopy with the C-arm, it is observed that the fracture fragment is reduced and supported, with a smooth articular surface. Subsequently, two cortical bone screws (φ3.5 mm, Double Medical, China) of appropriate lengths are placed parallel to the articular surface as raft support screws, as shown in Fig. 2. The schematic diagram of the bamboo raft is shown in Fig. 3. Then, a screw (φ3.5 mm, Double Medical, China) is implanted obliquely from the posterolateral to the anteromedial direction close to the raft screws, namely the jail screw. The operation lasted 76 min and the blood loss was approximately 20 ml. Neither bone grafting nor the placement of a drainage tube is necessary.Fig. 2Schematic illustration of the intraoperative bamboo raft reduction approach: Kirschner wires successively support the fracture fragments, ensuring the stability of the reduced fracture fragments. Postoperative course

On the second day after the surgery, the patient was encouraged to perform flexion and extension activities. Two weeks after the operation, after the stitches were removed, the patient could attempt to bear weight on the ground and gradually transition to full weight-bearing by the fourth week. At one month and three months post - operation, follow - up examinations were conducted. The results indicated that the fracture fragments had neither been lost nor displaced, maintaining an optimal position. By the third month, the patient reported a good condition and was capable of walking unaided. Subsequently, the internal fixation device was removed.

During the six - month follow - up, the patient demonstrated unrestricted mobility. However, upon walking over long distances, the patient occasionally experienced mild discomfort. A follow - up CT scan revealed that the fracture had not undergone displacement, and there was no evidence of collapse. The functional score based on the Knee Society Clinical Rating System (KSS) [18] was 95 for the clinical aspect and 90 for the functional aspect. Discussion

PLF occurs when the knee joint is exposed to an axial compressive force on the posterior lateral plateau (buckling-valgus violence), and the posterior aspect of the wide convex surface of the lateral femoral condyle strikes the posterior lateral plateau of the tibia [19]. It is typically manifested as conical fragments, which are characterized by a relatively narrow articular surface area, a short length of cortical fracture, and a limited depth of joint depression [3].

For tibial plateau fractures, there exist multiple classification systems, such as the bicondylar four-part classification [20], Luo classification [21] and the OTA/AO classification [22] among others. However, they have relatively limited guiding significance for the surgical management of posterolateral fractures. The “Ten-segment classification” makes a detailed division of the morphology of posterolateral fractures and possesses a favorable reference significance for surgical guidance.

Frequently, the “L”-shaped anterior-lateral and “T”-shaped posterior-lateral internal fixation devices are employed to fulfill the biomechanical requirements for stabilizing fractures of the lateral and posterolateral columns [23]. Sun et al. [15] employed a prone posterior-medial L-shaped incision for open reduction and fixation with a supporting plate for this type of fracture. The incision needed to be 10–15 cm, and excessive dissection towards the lateral side of the tibial shaft was not allowed as it was necessary to avoid injuring the posterior tibial artery. A cadaver study [24] revealed that the posterior medial incision failed to completely expose the posterolateral area of the tibia, with certain blind areas in the posterolateral region remaining. Wang et al. [10] conducted a comparison of the biomechanical stability of a newly designed lateral tibia plateau hook plate system for posterolateral tibial plateau fractures with that of lateral variable-angle anterolateral locking compression plates and direct posterior buttress plates. The outcomes indicated that the novel plate system with posterolateral hardware demonstrated the same load-bearing effect as the direct posterior support plate theoretically. However, it was also more traumatic when used in actual situations, hypothetically.

In 2006, Petersen et al. [25] put forward the “Jail Screw Technique”, whose key point resides in guaranteeing direct contact between the upper and lower screws. The “jail screw” and the “conventional screw” exhibit similar biomechanical strength in fixation; however, the “jail Screw” can prevent the cutting action of the “conventional screw” on cancellous bone [26]. Jiang et al. [27] treated the collapse fractures of the anterolateral tibial plateau by open reduction and internal fixation with lateral locking plates combined with Jail screws. They believed that the Jail screw technique could reduce the shear force of raft-shaped screws on the cancellous bone beneath the plateau, enhance the fixation strength of the collapse fractures, and prevent the failure of internal fixation. This three-dimensional fixation pattern enables osteoporotic patients to perform partial weight-bearing activities without weight-bearing two weeks after surgery and allows patients to use crutches for early knee flexion and extension exercises. Huang et al. [28] reported that the TiRobot robot was used to assist in the closed fixation of tibial plateau fractures with the Jail technique. They precisely located the area and used a bone chisel to remove cancellous bone, which was then packed into the fracture site. However, no additional maintenance tools were used after fracture reduction, which inspired our reduction method. Additionally, although the presence of the robot makes fracture reduction and fixation more precise, some hospitals still do not have robot-assisted technology at present.

Somewhat similarly, Vauclair et al. [29] also employed the jail technique for the treatment of lateral plateau depression fractures. However, their treatment approach involved reducing the split component of the fracture using a Weber clamp. They utilized Kirschner wires to support the large bone fragments. Additionally, the fracture fragments in their cases presented as an integral unit, not being as severely comminuted as those in our cases.

In our cases, the fracture fragments belong to a special position within intra-articular fractures. The posterolateral bone fragment is relatively small. Conventional surgical incisions cause significant damage, slow postoperative recovery, and often result in difficulties during the removal of internal fixation in a second operation or the fixation being retained. Compared with the single thick-diameter prying tool for reduction, our minimally invasive reduction method causes less bone defect in the additional bone tunnel and has a larger reduction width, reducing the chance of poor reduction. Even for multiple fracture fragments and comminution, it can provide planar support. However, our minimally invasive internal fixation method is simple to operate, even without an assistant. It does not damage the soft tissues and blood supply around the fracture fragments. Theoretically, the fracture healing rate is high, and the removal of internal fixation after fracture healing is simple, even operable in an outpatient setting, with low hospitalization costs and a low risk of nerve and vascular bundle injury. Moreover, the multiple Kirschner wires implanted in a bamboo-like pattern are beneficial for the treatment and stabilization of fracture reduction. This is convenient in the absence of robot navigation. Adjusting a single Kirschner wire does not disrupt the good state of fracture reduction. When dealing with multi-column fractures of the tibial plateau, this method can be used to handle the posterolateral fracture fragment, avoiding additional surgical incision approaches. Adjusting the ends of the Kirschner wires is to prevent the tips from piercing through the fracture fragments and damaging the posterior structures. Sequentially withdrawing the Kirschner wires is because the retained Kirschner wires can maintain the fracture reduction state. When parallel support screws are implanted in the horizontal plane, the bamboo-like reduction method and the row of nails do not interfere with each other in the two planes, and there is no need to worry about the loss of fracture reduction during screw implantation. Theoretically, this technique should also be applicable to other injury patterns of the tibial plateau.

However, this study also has several limitations, including a small sample size (n = 1), the lack of long-term follow-up data, and the absence of a control group. During the initial surgery, the number of fluoroscopy images and the quality of reduction may vary depending on the surgeon's experience. Additionally, obese patients may take longer to get out of bed, and repeated fluoroscopy may increase the possibility of radiation exposure. In some cases, the widely used cannulated screws for minimally invasive fixation may be a more practical option. When the posterolateral fracture fragment is relatively large, the strength of the supporting plate may be the best compared to the jail screw, according to the biomechanical study by Jordan et al. [30].

Conclusion

Although the PLF of the tibial plateau poses significant clinical challenges due to its complex anatomy and the technical difficulties associated with open reduction, our “bamboo raft reduction technique” combined with “jail screw fixation” effectively addressed such fractures and offers the advantages of minimal trauma, stable reduction, and rapid recovery. This fixation approach demonstrates strong theoretical feasibility on weight-bearing joint surfaces. It is not only applicable to tibial plateau fractures but also shows potential for use in ankle fractures. With promising clinical application prospects, this technique may serve as a valuable reference for clinicians.

In future research, we will conduct further studies on a larger scale and a series of them, as well as biomechanical verification.
