Adolescents do not exist alone. Involving parents in care with creativity in a “Maison des adolescents”
Authors: Rahmeth Radjack, Maude Ludot Gregoire, Miriam Von Mücke Similon, Amalini Simon, Eglantine Mazeaud, Alice Titia Rizzi, Marie Rose Moro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe theme of the 21st International Congress of the European Society for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, “Beyond Nature and Nurture,” focused on issues surrounding the future of child and adolescent psychiatry. In particular, it focused on how the role of family involvement is evolving in child and adolescent psychiatry. Many mental health disorders affecting young people require a focus on the context and environment of the youth. The “Maisons des Adolescents” take into account the complexity of disorders that emerge during adolescence and their multifactorial causes. Psychological suffering can lead to major health risks, and these disorders often require family involvement. In our symposium, we described different forms of care involving parents in a research and a clinical setting. We looked at the management of suicidal crises in a day-hospital setting and the therapeutic education program for bulimia and binge eating disorder.Keywords: Maison des adolescents, Bulimic disorders, Suicidal crisis, Family and multifamily therapy, Transcultural therapy, Outpatient care

Introduction : context: what is a Maison des adolescents?

The first Maison des Adolescents (MDA) was founded in 1999 in Le Havre to address a growing recognition that adolescents experiencing psychological distress often did not access traditional child and adolescent psychiatric services and lacked dedicated, youth-centered spaces for care.

In 2004, a national five-year plan formally supported the development of the model, providing dedicated funding and setting the objective of establishing at least one Maison des Adolescents in each French administrative department (territorial unit). Although this target was not achieved immediately, the initiative led to substantial expansion between 2005 and 2010. During this period, the landscape of adolescent mental health care in France underwent a significant transformation, with the progressive implementation of these centers across the country [1, 2].

Since the late 1990s, the development of MDAs in France has reflected both political priorities and clinical concerns. In response to increasing evidence of adolescent psychological distress and rising help-seeking behaviors, policymakers and child and adolescent psychiatrists have highlighted the need for stronger governmental involvement in youth mental health care. This movement gained momentum from 2007 onward, supported by epidemiological and clinical data emphasizing the limitations of existing care systems in addressing adolescents’ specific needs. The maisons des Adolescents—literally “the home of teenagers”—were created from the beginning as accessible and comfortable spaces where young people and their families would feel welcomed [2].

Since 1999, more than 125 MDAs have been established across France, providing adolescents with access to integrated, youth-friendly services. These facilities are designed to offer low-threshold, non-stigmatizing entry points into care. National objectives currently aim to expand the network to approximately 250 MDAs by 2029.

Although the specific medical and psychosocial programs implemented within MDAs vary according to local team priorities and regional regulations, all MDAs share common core services. These include a daily open “Health and Prevention Space,” multidisciplinary consultations, and mobile outreach teams capable of meeting adolescents both at home and during hospital stays in non-psychiatric medical units.

MDA centers are characterized by their multidisciplinary care, which enables a comprehensive approach to adolescent health and well-being. Our MDA at the Cochin Hospital in particular has developed a dual psychiatric and pediatric approach. This integrated pediatric-psychiatric approach allows for the treatment of conditions such as severe eating disorders, distress that is often masked by somatic complaints (frequently seen in cases of anxiety-related school refusal and post-traumatic stress disorder), as well as chronic illnesses with psychiatric comorbidities.

In addition, MDAs often provide open centers for artistic and creative workshops, academic support through remedial or refresher courses, network meetings with institutional partners, and support groups for parents.

From their inception, MDAs have been structured around an age-based approach rather than a diagnosis-based model, targeting young people aged 11 to 21 years. This developmental perspective reflects the understanding that adolescents require community support for growth and well-being. Accordingly, care within MDAs systematically integrates the adolescent’s environment, including families and siblings, school partners, community resources, and cultural and linguistic contexts when relevant.

Our center, centrally located in Paris, is especially accessible to young people suffering from a wide range of mental health difficulties related to adolescence. It offers a particularly diverse set of services, grounded in a multidisciplinary approach. It is both a preventive and curative space, with strong links to local care networks, and serves as a bridge between the outside world, the health care system, and family life. The interventions offered include cultural and family-based activities. Particular attention is given to situations involving social vulnerability, migration, and cultural diversity.

Overall, MDAs are conceived as welcoming spaces not only for adolescents themselves but also for all those involved in supporting them, promoting a holistic and inclusive approach to adolescent health and well-being.

Objectives

We describe two treatment programs within a multidisciplinary MDA: a day hospital for adolescents in suicidal crisis and a therapeutic education program for bulimic disorders. Together, these programs illustrate the clinical role of MDAs within the current care landscape.

In France, adolescents with longstanding or chronic disorders are typically referred to secondary-level outpatient services, Centres Médico-Psychologiques (CMPs), which are geographically assigned. MDAs may serve as an entry point into this system, particularly for transient or emerging difficulties and for preventive care. Within this framework, behavioral and depressive symptoms, including severe and high-risk presentations, are conceptualized as dynamic and evolving rather than as fixed disorders.

Both treatment programs that we describe involve targeted interventions for clinical situations that might otherwise require hospitalization. Early engagement, attentive listening, and structured outpatient care support stabilization and reduce the need for inpatient admission. In contexts such as suicidal crises or bulimic disorders, adolescents may experience a subjective sense of impasse, often associated with unarticulated distress. Mobilizing the family system is therefore central to resolving crises and accessing underlying help-seeking needs.

Family involvement is a core component of both interventions. While the programs are structured, they remain flexible and creative, supporting active family participation in care. More broadly, MDAs implement accessible and proactive approaches that engage adolescents and help bring to light their sometimes implicit requests for support. Parental involvement also supports the management of symptoms in the home environment and may help prevent hospitalization, which can be difficult to access rapidly in the current system. In this article, we describe how these programs integrate parents either from the outset, based on existing evidence, or over time in response to emerging clinical needs.

Parental involvement helps improve the management of disorders at home and helps avoid hospitalization (which is difficult to implement quickly in the current care landscape). In this article, we describe how clinical programs have incorporated parents either from the outset (based on research findings), or during a program’s evolution in response to emerging needs.

A new day-hospital to take care of suicidal adolescents. An opportunity for intensive family commitment Maude Ludot-Grégoire

Suicide is the second leading cause of death among 14–24-year-olds, with a current re-attempt rate of 17–28% in France [3]. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, adolescent acute mental health crises have increased by 30% [4]. The relative scarcity of child and adolescent psychiatric beds makes hospitalizing suicidal teens more difficult than previously. Moreover, the protective effects of hospitalizing suicidal teens are being questioned, as are the potential iatrogenic effects of hospitalization. Ross et al. [5] and Ougrin et al. [6] highlight the iatrogenic risks of hospitalization, including social isolation, stigmatisation, difficulties in adapting to the home environment post-discharge, and the emergence of new learned risky behaviours. Outpatient intensive care, therefore, presents itself as an alternative solution and started becoming more popular during the COVID-19 pandemic. In late 2025, a new day-treatment program for suicidal adolescents, named “Weiji” (from the Mandarin word for crisis, symbolizing how crisis can represent an opportunity or turning point), was established at the Maison des Adolescents at Cochin Hospital in Paris. This day-treatment program was founded as an alternative to traditional inpatient hospitalization. It allows adolescents to remain in their home environment while actively involving families from the beginning of care. We believe that suicidal crises require greater integration of the family into treatment.

In our outpatient intensive care program, we provide programming for adolescents who have consulted an emergency department for intrusive suicidal ideation or after a suicide attempt. Participation in the program requires a minimum of 3.5 days per week for a total of one month. Patients benefit from both individual care (with continual reassessment of a patient’s safety plan) and group therapy. Each group includes six adolescents and operates on a semi-open basis, allowing for staggered admissions. Additionally, family involvement is a core component from the outset and includes family support groups, family assessment, and multi-family therapy (MFT) with sibling involvement.

Prior to the opening of the crisis day hospital, we conducted a pilot study among professionals at the Maison des Adolescents to determine how best to implement this new intensive care program, based on the perceptions, needs, and expectations of the professionals consulted. MDA professionals emphasized the importance of the relationship between parents and adolescents during a suicidal crisis. Parents can frequently interpret suicide attempts as a provocation. Though we would be remiss to dismiss entirely that possible dimension, our role is to emphasize the real danger of trivializing the attempt.

The day hospital for suicidal crises is embedded within a MDA, a multidisciplinary setting in which MFT is routinely implemented for other clinical indications, including eating disorders and anxiety-related school refusal. This context provides an institutional framework for the adaptation of MFT to adolescents presenting with suicidal crises. Referrals to the day hospital originate from both external and internal sources. External referrals are primarily made by emergency departments, while internal referrals arise within the MDA, which offers a walk-in, no-appointment intake system. Adolescents presenting in this setting who are identified as experiencing a suicidal crisis undergo psychiatric assessment. Based on this evaluation, they may be referred directly to the day hospital, thereby extending the continuum of care within the institution. Care within the day hospital is delivered through a multidisciplinary approach consistent with the broader institutional model. All patients receive a systematic pediatric assessment as part of their treatment.

Family involvement is an integral part of this program, and an adolescent’s acceptance in the program is dependent on whether the family can fully participate. This participation includes accompanying the adolescent to and from sessions every day, attending family meetings, participating in MFT, and being willing and able to help implement the adolescent’s safety plan for their suicidality. To help support parents in their participation of the treatment, clinicians are able to provide medical documentation to support temporary leave from work for one parent.

MFT combines the concepts of traditional family therapy with the therapeutic potential of group-based work. Traditionally, clinicians take responsibility for the process of therapeutic change, which often inadvertently displaces parental authority onto the clinicians. The displacement of this authority can contribute to unconscious and unintended rivalry between parents and clinicians. The systemic framework of multifamily intervention promotes a reorientation from adolescent-centered to family-centered care. Within this paradigm, parental responsibility remains central, and families are supported in recognizing and mobilizing their own therapeutic capacities, both within and across family units.

Within our program, MFT convenes six to eight families facing a shared clinical challenge: the management of suicidal crises. Siblings and other identified support persons may be included, and interpreters are available when required to ensure accessibility and full participation. The intervention consists of four structured three-hour sessions delivered within the day-hospital setting. Each session addresses a specific thematic focus : (1) understanding the suicidal crisis, (2) examining its relational and systemic impact, (3) mobilizing the broader support network, and (4) restoring collaborative and functional parent-adolescent relationships.

Experiential and playful activities are employed to highlight common everyday stressors and interactional patterns, fostering reflective dialogue among families. This format encourages the sharing of diverse coping strategies and the co-construction of adaptive solutions within the shared therapeutic space.

5 year development of a therapeutic education program for adolescents with boulimic symptoms Rahmeth Radjack

The MDA of the Cochin Hospital offers a therapeutic education program focused on the management of compulsive binge eating and bulimia. This structured, eight-session program provides a multidisciplinary framework of care designed to reduce treatment discontinuity, which is particularly common in this population [7].

Eligibility requires that adolescents demonstrate motivation to reduce the frequency of their binge-purge episodes. Motivation and perceived barriers to change are formally assessed using a 20-point rating scale. During an initial meeting with a designated nurse, a shared educational assessment is conducted to clarify expectations, perceived barriers, anticipated benefits, and potential facilitators of change. The structure of the program is explained, including the importance of parental involvement. Individualized therapeutic objectives are then collaboratively defined with the nurse. These goals often reflect themes such as improving the capacity to enjoy food and daily life, fostering better mutual understanding with the family (e.g., reducing parental criticism or irritability), strengthening self-acceptance, and developing greater tolerance of one’s binge eating episodes.

The program aims to deliver integrated care by addressing comorbid emotional, affective, and interpersonal difficulties, while monitoring and treating the medical sequelae of bulimia. Concurrently, caregivers are provided with psychoeducation to support symptom management and improve intra-family communication.

We consider therapeutic education particularly relevant within a multidisciplinary MDA setting. The program is delivered by a comprehensive team including a pediatrician, a general practitioner, a child and adolescent psychiatrist, two nurses, a dietician, and a nurse manager. Continuity of care is supported through structured follow-up, the designation of a referral nurse [8], and a containing therapeutic framework aimed at reducing treatment discontinuity [9]. This is especially important given that bulimia frequently emerges during transitional developmental periods, often toward the end of adolescence. International clinical guidelines recommend cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and family-based interventions as first-line treatments for eating disorders [10]. However, in France, access to CBT is often limited due to its expense and its limited availability within the public health sector.

The content of the program has evolved over time to better meet the clinical needs of this population. A range of assessment and therapeutic tools are employed, including Cottle’s Circle Test [11] to explore the perceived place of the disorder in the adolescent’s life, the bulimic Investigatory Test of Edinburgh (BITE), and individual motivational interviews incorporating structured crisis-management worksheets.

The development of MFT within our institution for anxiety-related school refusal and anorexia nervosa prompted its integration into this therapeutic education program (ETP). Early evaluations identified gaps in continuity of care, related to both patient factors and clinical practices, and highlighted the need to more clearly distinguish ETP from standard care.The introduction of a clinician trained in MFT enabled implementation of these approaches. The MDA setting supports this institutional flexibility. Incorporating diverse professional perspectives is viewed as a strength, fostering ongoing reflection within an evolving clinical framework. Continuous institutional reflection supports adaptation of care structures and interventions to meet the needs of the population, in alignment with both developmental and institutional timelines.

The symposium, “Adolescents Do Not Exist Alone : Involving Parents in Care” at the 21st International Congress, outlined the evolution of the program and its most relevant intervention methods. Alongside individual consultations, monthly parent groups and alternating monthly adolescent symptom-focused groups, co-facilitated by a child and adolescent psychiatrist and a nurse, are introduced as complementary components of care. Our clinical experience has highlighted several key elements in structuring care. In particular, the integration of techniques derived from MFT has appeared to be especially beneficial.

At the conclusion of this time-limited program, the various therapeutic modalities are individualized and better integrated into the adolescent’s clinical trajectory. The program facilitates the co-construction of a personalized and meaningful care plan. Both the youth and their parents are able to play an active role in shaping and sustaining the treatment process.

Following participation in the parent groups (each lasting two to three hours and held every two to three weeks over seven to eight sessions) several parents have described a sense of emptiness following the conclusion of the sessions, likely reflecting the intensity of the exchanges taking place within the groups. Several parents subsequently set up social media groups to sustain mutual support outside of the sessions. These observations have led us to favor group and collective modalities as they appear to enhance therapeutic engagement and efficiency while fostering peer support and shared reflection.

During the adolescent discussion groups, processes of intersubjective identification facilitated the recognition of shared experiences related to binge-eating episodes, including parental reactions, the temporal and situational context of episodes, the type of food consumed, and the associated emotional and cognitive states.

Within the parent discussion groups, the themes most frequently raised include enmeshed relational dynamics with their teen, symptom-related regression that paradoxically intensified dependency, and adolescents’ concurrent experiences of perceived intrusion. Parents also exchange practical strategies regarding food management (for example, purchasing individually packaged items) and frequently express feelings of guilt.

This innovative program, characterised by its multidisciplinary approach and substantial parental involvement, could be developed and formalized within a structured research protocol, given the limited empirical literature on effective treatment for bulimia and binge eating. In our clinical experience, the program frequently facilitates the collaborative development of an appropriate, personalized and meaningful care plan.

Discussion

The containing function of multidisciplinary care in the face of complexity or emergency

The multidisciplinary framework functions as a containing structure in the face of clinical complexity and crisis. Although requests for care in cases of bulimic disorders or suicidal behavior are often framed as urgent, therapeutic work may require a calibrated response that avoids mirroring the anxiety of the crisis itself. The care models described in this article provide a containing framework that can be adjusted to a patient’s capacity for reflection and psychological integration. While responding promptly to acute needs, the approach maintains a longer-term therapeutic objective: to establish effective intervention from the outset through the coordinated deployment of multidisciplinary expertise and creative clinical resources.

Early integration of the family into care helps to mitigate the risk of perceived rivalry between professionals and parents, and fosters the autonomy of young people and their families in managing symptoms over time. In some cases, temporary separation from parents may be necessary in order to prioritize safety and enable focused self-work away from the pressures of the everyday environment. Nevertheless, the guiding philosophy of our adolescent center remains to avoid experiences of separation whenever possible. Informing parents and assisting them is one of the MDA’s core missions. After the initial assessment stage, parents can be referred to a support group or to another institution for help. The MDAs have strong links with parent-specific institutions [12]. Parents do not always feel capable and often express their vulnerabilities and feelings of guilt. Parent support groups offered across programs help shift this position by fostering a sense of competence and agency in addressing the situation. Parents benefit from acknowledging the complexity of these situations, from not feeling alone in confronting severe disorders, and from the availability of flexible, individualized care rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.In cases involving child maltreatment, abandonment, or discontinuity of care—or when there is limited clinical progress—a dual approach combining educational support and clinical care is implemented, in collaboration with child protective services. This paper is situated within the French healthcare context, specifically in a MDA embedded in a university hospital and recognized as one of the largest child and adolescent psychiatry centers in the country. The primary strength of this center is that it utilizes an institutional model that combines psychodynamic and systemic approaches with multidisciplinary and transcultural perspectives. The MDA model has been widely disseminated within France and has influenced the establishment of comparable centers internationally, including in Mexico, suggesting both the model’s originality and its potential transferability.

This model is not fully transferable in all its aspects. There is considerable variability among MDAs in France, with care models differing across regions based on local needs, resources, and available staffing, particularly in settings with more limited medical and human resources. One current objective of the French National Association of Maisons des Adolescents (ANMDA) is to map and characterize practices across centers, including reasons for presentation, referral pathways, coordination between clinical, educational, and social services, and the organization of levels of care.

Achieving uniformity of practices is not a current objective. MDAs operate with a degree of flexibility that fosters institutional creativity. Emphasis is placed on early engagement, creating an open-access entry point for adolescents within an innovative model that integrates clinical and educational approaches. Core transferable elements include transdisciplinarity, diversity, comprehensive and paramedical care, creativity, flexibility, openness, and coordination with external partners (child welfare services [ASE], youth justice services [PJJ], schools, CMPs, primary care physicians, community organizations, and private practitioners).

The structured organization of multiprofessional and multidisciplinary outpatient care can provide a containing response to complex clinical situations. In many cases, this approach contributes to preventing hospitalization and interruption in continuity of care. At a time when public hospitals are unable to meet all urgent care demands, alternative solutions need to be sought. This evolution has led to greater reliance on intensive outpatient management characterized by high-frequency interventions (that were sometimes improvised) and heavily dependent on family models. When the indication is appropriate, such approaches can be effective. Today, these care models are deliberately structured, intensive, and designed to be delivered in the outpatient setting with the aim to prevent psychiatric decompensation [6].

Maisons des adolescents at the interface between psychiatry and societal issues

The international literature highlights the importance of involving families and broader support networks in the care of adolescents. One may question whether this principle requires further demonstration, given the apparent logic of minimizing fragmentation between inpatient and outpatient settings, and whenever possible, minimizing the unnecessary separation of adolescents from their families. These considerations invite broader reflection on the role of parents within therapeutic and educational contexts. In addition, they raise intracultural and transcultural questions regarding family authority, such as, to whom does the adolescent “belong” within systems of care?

Working with adolescents requires us to continually renew, readjust, and re-examine our practices. Their psychopathology is intertwined with broader societal issues. As Bernard Golse has provocatively suggested, the more agitated a society becomes, the less it tolerates hyperactivity in its children [13]. Similarly, Mazeaud, in a presentation at the same European Society for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry symposium, presented research examining the place and lived experience of assessments within families in situations involving identification of high intellectual potential—another socially salient topic [14]. Post-COVID lockdowns have also led to increased severity of eating disorders [15] and to forms of depression with more frequent eating-related symptoms [16]. Symptom expression, therefore, evolves in tandem with societal change.

Referrals to our adolescent center encompass a wide spectrum of psychiatric presentations arising during adolescence. The clinical approach integrates individual, societal, and systemic dimensions while considering diverse family structures. Family-based interventions, delivered in formats such as family therapy and transcultural consultations, constitute a central component of our adolescent center [17].

MDAs are situated in accessible settings and are conceptualized as providing comprehensive, developmentally oriented care for adolescents, rather than being associated with chronic or institutional (asylum) models of psychiatric illness. Social workers play a key complementary role alongside clinical care, providing structure and guidance in the daily support of hospitalized adolescents and supporting parents in reflecting on and adapting their responses [18]. Collaboration with child welfare services is frequent, particularly in cases involving the disclosure of physical or sexual abuse. Current institutional efforts focus on understanding how to maintain therapeutic alliances with parents despite mandated reporting or safeguarding procedures, and on facilitating continuity in educational and social support [19–21].

Socioeconomic disparities and adverse life experiences contribute to the emergence or exacerbation of psychiatric issues. In response, specialized services in our MDA have been developed in recent years, including dedicated consultations for unaccompanied minors, gender identity concerns, and international adoption, as well MFT addressing school refusal [22].

This openness to a broad range of clinical presentations allows concerns to evolve without prematurely fixing or crystallizing them into a diagnosis. In this respect, the model offers an alternative to highly specialized centers (“expertcentres”) while also differing from community mental health services (“centres médico psychologique”), not focused on a specific age group In contrast to disease-centered models, professionals in MDAs do not position themselves as subspecialists focused on a single diagnosis or presenting problem. Rather, this approach functions as an initial point of entry into care and may facilitate subsequent referral to more targeted interventions when indicated (e.g., addiction, gender identity). Child and adolescent psychiatrists conduct comprehensive, non-siloed assessments. Care is coordinated both within the institution and with external providers, including primary care physicians and community-based practitioners.

Although community centers remain the primary entry point for care, they are currently saturated throughout France, resulting in extended wait times for treatment [23]. Within these settings, child and adolescent psychiatrists often assume the roles of care coordinators and prescribers, with an emphasis on diagnostic clarification. This reliance on child psychiatrists may widen disparities in under-resourced regions with limited specialist availability, particularly between MDAs and community mental health centers.

Conclusion

This article explores transition as a core organizing theme in adolescent psychiatry. It examines several dimensions, including the transition from normal developmental processes to pathological states, the dialectic between neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities and environmental influences, from institutional care to family environments, and from a biomedical model to multidisciplinary care.

We situate ourselves within a new chapter in the history of psychiatry – one that seeks to move beyond parental blame or, conversely, by the exclusion of parents through a rigid medical-familial divide that framed the adolescent solely in terms of pathology. An intermediate and nuanced perspective involves recognizing parents as authentic partners in care, supported and guided by clinicians.

The MDA model departs from a strictly biomedical framework. It is characterized by the provision of a shared space in which adolescents, their families, and multidisciplinary professionals within a given territory can meet, collaborate, and exchange.
