Postoperative Day 1 Dangling Protocol with Tissue Oximetry Guidance Decreases Hospital Stay in Lower Extremity Fasciocutaneous Free Flaps: Early Experience
Authors: Rachel Danforth, Jordan E. Wiebe, Juan Socas, Mary E. Lester, Aladdin H. Hassanein
Published: 2024

Lower extremity reconstruction represents a complex problem for plastic surgeons with critical functional implications for patients. Free tissue transfer is frequently required for lower extremity defects. These flaps have a uniquely difficult physiology, with higher venous pressure and lower arterial pressure than other sites.1 Postoperative management protocols of lower extremity free flaps are inconsistent among microsurgeons.2 Various “early” or “late” dangling protocols have been described to condition the flap before ambulation. Recently, several studies have shown increasing support for early dangling.3,4 The purpose of this study was to assess a dangling protocol beginning on postoperative day (POD) 1, guided by continuous near-infrared transcutaneous tissue oximetry. This study was IRB exempt. We have instituted this preliminary protocol at our institution for select flaps.

All lower extremity fasciocutaneous free flaps over an 18-month period were retrospectively analyzed. Muscle flaps and flaps not monitored with transcutaneous tissue oximeter (Vioptix Inc. Fremont, Calif.) were excluded. Patients were grouped into (1) POD 1 early dangling protocol and (2) late dangling regimens beginning after POD 1 per surgeon preference. The early protocol begins with 5 minutes on POD 1 (Table 1). Dangling is aborted if the transcutaneous tissue oximetry drops by 20 points.5 Predictive variables were patient demographics, wound etiology, type and size of free flap, and tissue oximetry measurements. Outcome variables were length of stay following the flap, flap takeback, flap loss, and complications.

Thirteen patients were included in the study (five early dangling and eight late dangling, all anterolateral thigh flaps). The average age was 40.3 ± 16.1 years (P = 0.9). There were no differences between the early dangling group and late dangling group in age (41.2 and 39.8 years, respectively), wound size (99.2 ± 23.4 and 106.9 ± 56.9 cm2), number of venous anastomoses (1.2 and 1.5 veins), or venous coupler size (2.7 mm and 2.8 mm, respectively; P = 0.9). Dangling was initiated on an average of 7.6 ± 5.8 days postoperative in the late group. There was no flap loss in either group and no takeback for flap compromise in the early group, compared with one in the late group. Patients in the early group all tolerated POD 1 dangling and did not have a significant change in mean transcutaneous readings before (74.4 ± 10.7%) or after dangling (73.8 ± 12.6%) (P = 0.9) on POD 1. Hospital stay was significantly shorter in the early dangling group (8.8 ± 2.0 days) compared with the late group (15.6 ± 8.2 days; P = 0.01; Fig. 1).

The initiation of early dangling protocol beginning on POD 1 after lower extremity fasciocutaneous free flap reconstruction was safely implemented in our sample with continuous transcutaneous tissue oximetry guidance. Muscle flaps may become more easily congested and are not compatible with transcutaneous tissue oximetry monitoring unless a skin island is present. POD 1 dangling results in decreased length of hospitalization when compared with the “late,” more heterogenous group. This can facilitate earlier ambulation and decrease inpatient costs with earlier discharge.4 Use of transcutaneous oximetry as a monitoring adjunct to physical examination allows more objectivity to termination of dangling. Our initial results indicate that a larger series using this protocol is warranted.
