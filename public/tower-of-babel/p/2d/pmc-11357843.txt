Surface water quality database for five watersheds in the Arequipa region of Southern Peru
Authors: Molly Noel Rymes, Rebecca Rasmussen, Pablo A Garcia-Chevesich, Katie Burgert, Jean Long, Elsie McBride, Gisella Martínez, Kattia Martínez, Teresa Tejada, Kyle E Murray, Gary Vanzin, Jonathan O Sharp, John E McCray
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThough surface water quality has been monitored in southern Peru over the past and current century, it has been implemented by multiple organizations. The data lacks a centralized repository and access requires logistical and temporal hurdles associated with official requests. A substantial portion of the data has not been quality assured and is in difficult-to-access formats such as scanned PDF documents. These obstacles collectively make it challenging to maximize the impact of these monitoring efforts such as efficiently evaluating long-term water quality trends. To address this opportunity, we gathered available surface water quality information from five watersheds in the Arequipa Region of southern Peru: Camaná, Chili, Ocoña, Tambo, and Yauca. The effort required entry of more than 130,000 records of water quality properties across 274 monitoring stations with data including the concentration of select nutrients, metals, organic compounds, and biological taxa. The water quality records in the Chili watershed go back as far as 1905, while data for the other watersheds was largely confined to the years 2012–2021. This document describes how the surface water quality information was assimilated with quality control and provides a centralized Excel database so that the data can be efficiently used for research and decision making purposes.Keywords: Water quality, River pollution, Nutrients, Pesticides, Metals, Organics

Background

Water quality information in the Arequipa Region of southern Peru is currently spared among different sources, often only available upon official request in PDF format (scanned documents). This makes it nearly impossible to analyze and make further management decisions. The objective of this work was to gather all available information from different sources and formats regarding surface water quality in the Arequipa Region (specifically, the Camaná, Chili, Ocoña, Tambo, and Yauca watersheds), merge them into a standardized master searchable database, and publish the database so that data are available to scientists, decision makers, and the general public.

Data Description

This valuable database contains over 130,000 records (containing 29 general chemistry, nutrients, and parameters; 49 metals; 20 biological taxa; and 77 organic compounds). Data was analyzed from a total of 274 monitoring locations (stations) within the Camaná (45 stations), Chili (91 stations), Ocoña (41 stations), Tambo (84 stations), and Yauca (13 stations) watersheds. All parameters and contaminants included in the database are listed in Appendix A of the Repository (Tables 1.1–1.4), with their respective abbreviations and units of measurement. When needed, units were converted from originally reported units to standard units such as mg/L, for commonality. Original units were also retained for quality assurance purposes. Similarly, Appendix B of the Repository (Tables 2.1 through 2.5) lists the names of each monitoring station, what constituents were analyzed, and the geographical location (when available) of each station. The range of years sampled for each watershed are 2012 to 2014 for Camaná, 1905 to 2019 for Chili, 2012 to 2021 for Ocoña, 2013 to 2019 for Tambo, and 2014 to 2020 for Yauca. In the HydroShare database, there is an interactive spatial coverage map that shows the relative locations of these five watersheds in the Arequipa Region of southern Peru. This map can be changed to a satellite view to see the physical terrain of the area. There is also a map in the database outlining the five watersheds and stations available for download.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Surface water quality information for the five watersheds was obtained from Peru's Water Resources National Information System (https://snirh.ana.gob.pe/snirh/) in spreadsheet format or by official requests to Peru's National Water Authority (ANA), which normally provides information to the public in scanned PDF format.

A significant portion of the data were manually transcribed from water quality reports issued by multiple government and private organizations. The original data values (e.g., 3.5) or data values with qualifiers (e.g., “<0.10”) were retained in a (database) column for each water quality measure. An additional column was retained for the reported units (e.g., µg/L). A third column (i.e., standard column) was added to the database, for each water quality measure, representing the original data value converted into standard or consistent units (e.g., mg/L). When original data values were qualified because the concentration was outside the detection range (i.e., “<” or “>”) then the value in the standard column was the reporting limit (e.g., lower detection limit of the instrument and method) in the standard units. When data were not reported or given as NA (not available or not applicable) in the original data value column, then the standard column was empty or null in our database to allow easier processing. The goal of this method of organization was to allow the end user of the database to see the original value and qualifiers, and to selectively query detections only, include or exclude detection limits, and recalculate some other variation of the detection limit (e.g., one-half the detection limit) for the purpose of their project.

Because the raw data is originally from Peru, the numerical values use a comma for a decimal point. These were changed to periods, for example 0,025 would be changed to 0.025. All significant figures were retained. The only data manipulation has been to convert to a more appropriate/standard unit, such as the various nitrate and phosphate species, or converting from µg/L to mg/L. Because nitrate species were often reported as Nitrate–Nitrogen (NO3–N), we converted to straightforward NO3. To convert Nitrate-nitrogen (NO3–N) to Nitrate (NO3), we multiplied by 4.4268. For Nitrite-N, we multiplied by 3.2845. For Phosphate-P, we multiplied by 3.06619. All values were double checked to ensure accurate transcription and entered as given.

Additionally all analytes, methods of collection and storage, and location descriptions were translated into English, retaining only the Spanish location name. For example “Quebrada el Salto, agua abajo de la Laguna Chumille – Arcata” becomes “Quebrada El Salto, downstream of Laguna Chumille – Arcata.” By keeping the full proper names in Spanish, they align with the station ID codes. From the above example, the station code for Quebrada El Salto is QElsa1. Should a user find and use future data from ANA in Peru, the location names will be aligned with no ambiguity of which new data goes with which locations. Because translation is not always one to one, and there may be multiple English words for a given Spanish word, we also retained the Spanish version of the methods of collection and storage in a second Analytes tab, for those wishing to perform their own translation of these critical processes.

After data entry, a quality assurance quality control (QA/QC) check was performed by a second team member to ensure accuracy; that is, the values entered into the comprehensive database were compared to original sources and data were corrected when necessary, thus creating the new database in Excel format (Fig. 1).

Limitations

The data represents efforts by many parties and was conducted in different temporal windows. Hence protocols for sample collection and data analysis were not standardized by a centralized entity. While precautions were taken with QA/QC as described above, errors in manual transcription of data could have occurred by the authors or parties above. Also, though this is valuable information, many analytes were documented by different Peruvian organizations inconsistently within and among stations over time, which could result in differences in, for example, the way samples are collected on site. In some cases, precise coordinates for some stations were lacking. Some methods of testing are unknown. This includes the methods for detesting detergents or which detergents were tested. The sampling methods or consistency for the enumeration of bacteria and parasites is also unknown.
