The Double Burden: Food Insecurity and Depressive Symptoms in a Rural Community of Southern India
Authors: Kumar Guru Mishra, Nabnita Patnaik, Bobba Harshitha, Anu Mohandas, Adrija Roy, Snigdha Patnaik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Food insecurity has been linked to adverse mental health outcomes, particularly depression. However, there is a paucity of community-based studies investigating this association, especially in southern India. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of food insecurity and examine its association with depressive symptoms among adults in a rural village in Telangana, India.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study recruited 537 participants aged 18–60 years from Moinabad village using a multistage cluster sampling technique. Food insecurity was assessed using the Food Insecurity Experience Scale, and depressive symptoms were measured using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. Sociodemographic data, including age, gender, educational attainment, addiction history etc., were collected, and logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between food insecurity and depressive symptoms, adjusting for potential confounders.Results:The prevalence of food insecurity was high at 86.21%. A significant finding was the strong positive association between increasing food insecurity levels and presence of depressive symptoms. Compared to the food-secure group, those with moderate food insecurity had 10.55 times higher adjusted odds ratio (aOR) (aOR = 10.55, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.25–82.64) and those with severe food insecurity had 9.26 times higher aOR (aOR = 9.26, 95% CI: 1.16–71.78) of having depressive symptoms.Conclusion:This study reveals a high prevalence of food insecurity, disproportionately affecting vulnerable groups. It establishes a strong positive association between increasing food insecurity levels and depressive symptoms, highlighting the detrimental impact on mental health. Other risk factors for depression include sociodemographic characteristics such as older age, female gender, lower educational level, and addiction history.

INTRODUCTION

Food insecurity, defined as the limited or uncertain availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods or the inability to acquire such foods in socially acceptable ways,[1] has been recognized as a critical public health issue worldwide. It is a complex phenomenon driven by various socioeconomic and environmental factors, including poverty, unemployment, lack of access to affordable and nutritious food sources, and inadequate social support systems.[2] The global prevalence of food insecurity has been estimated to be around 9.9%, with significant variations across regions and countries.[3]

Numerous studies have established a strong association between food insecurity and adverse health outcomes, including mental health disorders such as depression.[45] Depression is a common mental health disorder that can be influenced by various factors, including food insecurity.[6] The bidirectional relationship between food insecurity and depression is complex and multifaceted. Food insecurity can contribute to the development or exacerbation of depression due to the stress and anxiety associated with uncertain access to food, as well as the potential nutritional deficiencies that may affect the brain function and mental well-being.[7] Conversely, individuals with depression may experience difficulties in maintaining employment, managing finances, and engaging in self-care activities, thereby increasing their vulnerability to food insecurity.[8]

Epidemiological studies have reported varying prevalence rates of food insecurity among adults, ranging from 12% to 54% depending on the population and geographic region.[910] Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated a higher risk of depression among food-insecure individuals compared to their food-secure counterparts.[1112] However, these associations may be influenced by various sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and access to health-care services.

Despite the growing body of evidence linking food insecurity and depression, there is a paucity of community-based studies that comprehensively examine these interrelated issues, particularly in the southern part of India. With this aim, we conducted this study to estimate the prevalence of food insecurity among adults and to determine the association of food insecurity with depression.

METHODS

This cross-sectional study was conducted over a 6-month period from July to December 2023 in Moinabad, a rural village located approximately 30 km from Hyderabad, Telangana, India. Participants aged 18–60 years and with no comorbidities or chronic diseases for a minimum of 1 year were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Exclusion criteria encompassed patients receiving antidepressant medication, pregnant women, lactating mothers, and hospitalized patients requiring immediate medical attention. Elderly individuals were excluded to avoid potential alternative disease pathways associated with the high prevalence of chronic conditions in this age group. A multistage cluster sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. First, clusters (e.g., neighborhoods or census enumeration blocks) within Moinabad were randomly selected. Then, households within each selected cluster were randomly chosen, and eligible participants were identified and invited to participate in the study.

Assuming a prevalence of 31% for food insecurity,[13] and considering the limited evidence in Indian scenarios, the sample size was calculated to be 539 participants. An appropriate design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for the cluster sampling approach and nonresponse rate of 10%.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ age, gender, educational level, employment status, household income, and marital status.

Food insecurity assessment: The Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES) was administered to assess the level of food insecurity among participants.[14] Developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations, this scale consists of eight questions evaluating access to adequate food at the household level. The questions are scored on a Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater food insecurity. FIES presents the behaviors and self-reported experiences related to food access owing to the absence of money and other resources for 12 months. The scale consists of eight yes/no questions ranging from minimal food insecurity (question 1) to severe food insecurity (question 8), with a yes response receiving a score of 1 and a no response receiving a score of 0. Overall scores ranging from 0 to 8 were calculated from the responses. FIES was divided into four groups, with scores ranging from 0 to 2 indicating food security, 3 to 4 indicating mild food insecurity, 4 to 6 indicating moderate food insecurity, and 7 to 8 indicating severe food insecurity.

Depression screening: The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) was used to assess the presence and severity of depressive symptoms.[15] This widely used and validated instrument consists of 17 items that measure various aspects of depression, including mood, sleep disturbances, and somatic symptoms. In a dichotomous variable. People with HDRS ≤7 were categorized as absence of depression, and people with HDRS ≥8 were categorized as presence of depression.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Bivariate analyses, including Chi-square tests or t-tests as applicable, were conducted to explore the association between food insecurity and depression, as well as other potential factors.

Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to ascertain the independent association between food insecurity and depression while adjusting for potential confounding variables.

In this analysis, HDRS was dichotomized into two categories, namely the presence and absence of depression, with the presence of depression encompassing mild, moderate, and severe categories on the scale. All explanatory variables were included in the regression model, and only those variables with a significance level of P < 0.20 were retained for the multivariate regression analysis. The final model was determined based on this criterion.

The statistical analysis was performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp. released 2023, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.2.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA].

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by institutional ethics committee of Apollo Institute of Medical Sciences and Research, Hyderabad, (EC/NEW/INST/1527/2022/11/44). Informed consent, in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration guidelines, was meticulously obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives, procedures, and confidentiality measures.

RESULTS

The study analyzed data from 537 participants to examine the prevalence of food insecurity, its association with sociodemographic factors, and its relationship with depressive symptoms.

Educational attainment was inversely related to food insecurity, with a higher proportion of individuals with lower educational levels (illiterate/can read and write, primary, and middle school) in the moderate and severe food insecurity groups. Unemployment rates were higher in the severe food insecurity group (56.67%) compared to the other groups. Monthly household income and socioeconomic status exhibited a clear gradient, with a higher proportion of individuals from lower-income households and lower socioeconomic strata experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity. Notably, 45% of those in the lower socioeconomic stratum reported severe food insecurity. The prevalence of addiction history was substantially higher among individuals with moderate (43.75%) and severe (72.50%) food insecurity compared to those with none (1.35%) or mild (3.02%) food insecurity.

In Table 2, a significant finding is the high prevalence of food insecurity in the study population, with 86.21% experiencing some level of food insecurity (mild 37.06%, moderate 26.81%, severe 22.34%). Severe food insecurity was more prevalent among older individuals (mean age 48.67 years), those who were unmarried/widowed/divorced (47.50%), illiterate or with minimal education (34.17%), unemployed (56.67%), from lower/lower-middle socioeconomic strata (80%), and with a history of addiction (72.50%). These findings highlight the vulnerable groups disproportionately affected by severe food insecurity.

Furthermore, the distribution of depression severity levels among the study individuals, as assessed by HDRS, is described. The majority of participants (91.98%) reported an absence of depression, indicating a relatively high level of mental well-being within the sample. A small percentage experienced mild depression (5.40%), suggesting some symptoms of depression but not to a debilitating extent. Other significant risk factors for depressive symptoms identified in the regression analysis include older age (aOR = 3.01, 95% CI: 1.23–6.56, P = 0.003), being unmarried/widowed/divorced (aOR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.25–0.79, P = 0.004), low education level less than primary (aOR = 20.97, 95% CI: 10.08–43.21, P < 0.001), unemployment (aOR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.02–0.12, P < 0.001), low monthly household income below INR 46,405 (aOR = 3.16, 95% CI: 1.52–6.29, P = 0.002), and lower socioeconomic status (aOR = 46.88, 95% CI: 10.21–203.67, P < 0.001). DISCUSSION

The present study reports several profound insights into the pervasive issue of food insecurity, its complex sociodemographic underpinnings, and its concerning association with depressive symptomatology.

Firstly, the staggeringly high prevalence of food insecurity observed in this study, with an overwhelming 86.21% of participants experiencing some level of food insecurity, is a stark reminder of the global crisis at hand. This finding aligns with numerous reports from authoritative organizations such as the FAO and the World Food Programme (WFP), which have consistently highlighted the widespread nature of food insecurity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).[1516] The reasons for such a pervasive lack of access to adequate and nutritious food in the current study context are likely multifactorial, rooted in the intricate interplay of poverty, income inequality, limited access to food resources, and deep-seated socioeconomic disparities within the study population.[21718]

Secondly, the sociodemographic factors identified as being associated with higher levels of food insecurity paint a sobering picture of the disproportionate burden borne by marginalized and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. The finding that older age, female gender, being unmarried/widowed/divorced, lower educational attainment, unemployment, lower household income, and lower socioeconomic status are all linked to increased vulnerability to food insecurity resonates with a vast body of literature from both LMICs and developed countries.[51920]

The higher prevalence among older individuals is a particularly concerning trend that could be attributed to a confluence of factors, including reduced earning potential, diminished social support systems, and the mounting financial strain of increased health-care costs associated with aging.[2122] The gender disparity observed in this study is a stark reflection of the enduring sociocultural barriers that perpetuate unequal access to resources, employment opportunities, and decision-making power within households for women.[2324] Furthermore, lower educational attainment and unemployment represent formidable barriers to securing stable income and accessing food resources, while lower household income and socioeconomic status exacerbate financial constraints, creating a vicious cycle of deprivation.[225]

Thirdly, the robust positive association between increasing levels of food insecurity and the presence of depressive symptoms, even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, is a finding of profound significance. This observation resonates strongly with an ever-growing body of literature documenting the adverse mental health consequences of food insecurity, particularly in the context of depression and anxiety disorders.[262728] The potential mechanisms underlying this association are multifaceted and interconnected, involving chronic stress, nutritional deficiencies, lack of coping resources, psychological distress associated with the inability to provide adequate food for oneself or one’s family, and the cumulative impact of adversities associated with socioeconomic deprivation.[2930]

Furthermore, the study identified several other sociodemographic factors as significant risk factors for depressive symptoms, including older age, being unmarried/widowed/divorced, low education level, unemployment, low household income, lower socioeconomic status, and the presence of addiction history. These findings are consistent with a vast body of research linking socioeconomic disadvantages and marginalization to an increased risk of mental health problems, including depression.[3132]

Taken together, these findings underscore the critical intersection between food insecurity, socioeconomic disparities, and mental health outcomes, particularly depressive disorders.

The intricate relationship between food insecurity and depressive symptoms in rural India is shaped by a complex interplay of cultural, economic, and social factors.[33] Traditional gender roles often exacerbate women’s vulnerability to food insecurity, potentially intensifying their susceptibility to depressive symptoms. This vulnerability is further compounded by the region’s heavy reliance on agriculture and limited alternative income sources, which increase rural households’ exposure to food scarcity.[34]

By adopting a holistic and integrated approach that recognizes the complex interplay of factors contributing to food insecurity and its associated mental health consequences, we can pave the way for more effective and sustainable solutions.

Study limitations: This study has several limitations. Firstly, the reliance on self-reported measures for both food insecurity and depressive symptoms may introduce reporting biases. Secondly, the exclusion of elderly individuals limits the generalizability of the findings, as older adults may experience different patterns of food insecurity and depression. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inferences.

The findings of this study have policy implications and underscore the urgent need for integrated policies addressing both food insecurity and mental health in rural India.

At the local level, community-based programs combining food assistance with mental health support could be effective. These initiatives should empower individuals through education, skill development, and economic opportunities, helping to break the cycle of poverty and food insecurity.

Nationally, policies should focus on strengthening social safety nets, improving access to culturally sensitive mental health services, and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. Educational campaigns aimed at reducing mental health stigma and increasing awareness of available resources are also crucial.

By recognizing the multidimensional nature of these challenges and treating food security, economic opportunities, and mental health support as fundamental rights, we can work toward a more equitable and sustainable future for rural Indian communities.

CONCLUSION

This study findings reinforce the disproportionate impact of food insecurity on vulnerable groups, such as older individuals, unmarried/widowed/divorced individuals, those with lower education levels, unemployed individuals, and individuals from lower socioeconomic strata. Notably, the study establishes a strong positive association between increasing levels of food insecurity and the presence of depressive symptoms, even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors. This finding underscores the detrimental impact of food insecurity on mental health, particularly the increased prevalence of depressive disorders among those experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity. The study also identifies other risk factors for depressive symptoms, including older age, marital status, low education level, unemployment, low household income, lower socioeconomic status, and the presence of addiction history.
