Hemorrhagic posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome with pontine infarction in early-onset eclampsia: An atypical MRI spectrum
Authors: Srikanth Nalla, Pravin Sudhakar Patil, Pawar Swathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome is a clinicoradiological condition characterized by reversible vasogenic edema predominantly involving the posterior cerebral regions. It is commonly associated with hypertensive emergencies and pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders such as eclampsia. Although the typical imaging pattern involves the parieto-occipital white matter, atypical manifestations including brainstem involvement, infarction, and hemorrhage have been increasingly recognized. A 26-year-old primigravida at 25 + 2 weeks of gestation presented with multiple generalized tonic-clonic seizures and severe hypertension (190/110 mmHg). Laboratory investigations revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, and mild transaminase elevation, consistent with severe preeclampsia. MRI performed using a comprehensive MRI protocol demonstrated bilateral parieto-occipital vasogenic edema with additional frontal and capsuloganglionic involvement. Diffusion-weighted imaging revealed pontine and occipital infarcts, while susceptibility-weighted imaging demonstrated petechial hemorrhagic foci. MR venography excluded cerebral venous thrombosis. The patient required intensive care admission, mechanical ventilation, and subsequent termination of pregnancy following confirmation of intrauterine fetal death. She demonstrated gradual neurological recovery and was discharged in stable condition.Keywords: Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, Eclampsia, Brainstem infarction, Hemorrhagic PRES, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a clinicoradiological syndrome characterized by acute neurological symptoms including seizures, headache, altered mental status, and visual disturbances accompanied by characteristic neuroimaging findings. The syndrome was first described by Hinchey et al. and has since been recognized in association with several clinical conditions that result in endothelial dysfunction and impaired cerebral autoregulation [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

PRES is increasingly recognized in pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders [[1], [2], [3]] and has been reported in up to 90%-98% of patients with eclampsia undergoing neuroimaging evaluation. Recent studies have also highlighted atypical imaging manifestations including diffusion restriction, hemorrhage, and brainstem involvement, expanding the traditional concept of PRES as a purely reversible vasogenic edema syndrome [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Hypertensive emergencies represent one of the most common precipitating factors. Pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders, particularly eclampsia, are well-known causes of PRES and account for a significant proportion of cases encountered in clinical practice.

The classic imaging pattern consists of symmetrical vasogenic edema involving the parieto-occipital regions. However, atypical manifestations involving the frontal lobes, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem have increasingly been described. In severe cases, diffusion restriction and hemorrhagic components may also occur, reflecting a more aggressive disease spectrum.

We report a case of early-onset eclampsia presenting with atypical PRES demonstrating pontine infarction and hemorrhagic components.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old primigravida at 25 + 2 weeks of gestation was brought to the emergency department in an unconscious state following 3 consecutive episodes of generalized tonic-clonic seizures at home. According to her attendants, she had no prior history of seizures. Shortly after arrival at the emergency department, she experienced an additional generalized seizure episode. Review of antenatal records revealed that her first antenatal visit was at approximately 20 weeks of gestation, during which her blood pressure was recorded as 160/100 mmHg. She had been prescribed oral labetalol; however, compliance was irregular, and she had not taken antihypertensive medication for 3 days preceding presentation.

On admission, the patient was in a postictal state and was not responding appropriately to verbal commands. Blood pressure was 190/110 mmHg, pulse rate was 92 beats/min, respiratory rate was 20 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation was 100% on room air. Pedal edema was present. Neurological examination demonstrated reactive pupils bilaterally; however, detailed motor assessment could not be performed because of altered sensorium.

Laboratory investigations revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels (626 U/L; reference range 0-248 U/L), elevated aspartate aminotransferase (60 U/L; reference range 10-40 U/L), hypoalbuminemia (3.0 g/dL; reference range 3.5-5.0 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (1.15 × 10⁵/µL; reference range 1.5-4.5 × 10⁵/µL), and significant proteinuria (urine albumin 3+), consistent with severe pre-eclampsia.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was performed. MRI demonstrated symmetrical T2-weighted and FLAIR hyperintensities involving the bilateral parieto-occipital cortical and subcortical regions, consistent with vasogenic edema typical of PRES (Fig. 1). Additional FLAIR hyperintensities were observed within the bilateral capsuloganglionic regions and bilateral frontal subcortical white matter, indicating atypical distribution (Fig. 2). FLAIR images also demonstrated hyperintense signal within the bilateral frontal sulcal spaces.

Diffusion-weighted imaging with corresponding ADC maps revealed focal restricted diffusion within the pons and left occipital lobe, compatible with acute infarction (Fig. 3). The diffusion-restricted lesions demonstrated corresponding low signal intensity on ADC maps, supporting true cytotoxic edema rather than T2 shine-through artifact. Brainstem involvement predominantly affected the pontine parenchyma without significant mass effect. Susceptibility-weighted imaging demonstrated focal blooming within the pons and left occipital lobe, indicating hemorrhagic components (Fig. 4).

MR venography demonstrated normal flow signal within the superior sagittal sinus, straight sinus, torcular Herophili, right transverse sinus, and bilateral sigmoid sinuses without evidence of thrombosis. A hypoplastic left transverse sinus was noted as an anatomical variant (Fig. 5).

Management

The patient was managed in the medical intensive care unit. Magnesium sulfate therapy was initiated according to the eclampsia protocol, together with intravenous antihypertensive treatment using labetalol infusion. Owing to worsening neurological status, low Glasgow Coma Scale score, and gasping respirations, endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were performed. Multidisciplinary management involving obstetrics, neurology, intensive care, and internal medicine teams was undertaken.

Fetal outcome

During hospitalization, fetal heart activity became absent and intrauterine fetal death was confirmed on ultrasonography. Following counseling of family members, medical termination of pregnancy was performed using mifepristone and misoprostol, resulting in expulsion of a stillborn female fetus.

Clinical outcome

Neurological recovery was gradual, with improvement in Glasgow Coma Scale from the third hospital day. The patient was successfully extubated on day 4 and transferred out of the intensive care unit on day 5. At discharge, she was neurologically coherent, tolerating oral feeds, and hemodynamically stable on oral antihypertensive and antiepileptic therapy. At outpatient follow-up approximately 2 weeks later, no recurrent seizures or new neurological symptoms were reported.

Discussion

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome is believed to result from disruption of cerebral autoregulation and endothelial dysfunction leading to breakdown of the blood–brain barrier and development of vasogenic edema. Acute elevations in systemic blood pressure may overwhelm autoregulatory mechanisms, particularly within the posterior circulation where sympathetic innervation is relatively sparse.

Although the parieto-occipital regions are most commonly involved, PRES demonstrates a wider spectrum of lesion distribution than originally described. In a large imaging study evaluating patients with PRES, vasogenic edema was observed in the parietal or occipital regions in approximately 98% of patients, while involvement of the frontal lobes (68%), temporal lobes (40%), and cerebellum (30%) was also frequently identified. Less common but recognized sites of involvement include the basal ganglia, brainstem, deep white matter, and splenium of the corpus callosum.

PRES occurs in approximately 90%-98% of patients [1,[2], [8]] with eclampsia undergoing neuroimaging evaluation, whereas atypical brainstem involvement has been reported in approximately 10%-20% of cases. Brainstem infarction accompanied by hemorrhagic changes remains uncommon and is described mainly in severe forms of the disease. This makes recognition of atypical imaging manifestations particularly important in pregnant patients presenting with seizures and severe hypertension.

Three major imaging patterns of PRES have been described: the dominant parieto-occipital pattern, the holohemispheric watershed pattern, and the superior frontal sulcus pattern. The dominant parieto-occipital pattern represents the classic imaging presentation with posterior cerebral hemisphere involvement. The holohemispheric watershed pattern demonstrates linear vasogenic edema extending across the frontal, parietal, and occipital lobes along watershed zones between the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral artery territories. The superior frontal sulcus pattern is characterized by linear involvement along the superior frontal sulcus with variable extension into parietal and occipital regions. Recognition of these imaging patterns helps identify PRES even when the distribution is atypical.

In the present case, brainstem involvement predominantly affected the pons and was associated with diffusion restriction and hemorrhagic changes, indicating a severe disease spectrum.

Another important imaging feature is the presence of partial or asymmetric expression of these patterns, which may occur in a substantial proportion of patients. In such cases, lesions may involve only a portion of the expected distribution or demonstrate unilateral involvement. Awareness of these variations is important because absence of classic parieto-occipital involvement does not exclude PRES.

Role of MRI sequences in PRES

Magnetic resonance imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and characterization of PRES. FLAIR sequences are highly sensitive for demonstrating cortical and subcortical vasogenic edema. Diffusion restriction in PRES is relatively uncommon [9] and is believed to represent progression from reversible vasogenic edema to cytotoxic edema resulting from severe endothelial dysfunction, prolonged autoregulatory failure, and ischemic injury. Such lesions may subsequently evolve into permanent infarction and have been associated with less favorable radiological outcomes. Susceptibility-weighted imaging improves detection of hemorrhagic complications such as petechial hemorrhage or focal hematoma, which may not be evident on conventional sequences [10]. MR venography and MR angiography may also be helpful in excluding important differential diagnoses such as cerebral venous thrombosis or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome [11].

Imaging pattern correlation with this case

The imaging findings in the present patient correspond primarily to the dominant parieto-occipital pattern, with symmetrical vasogenic edema involving the posterior cerebral hemispheres. However, the additional involvement of the frontal white matter and capsulo ganglionic regions represents an atypical extension of the disease spectrum.

A notable feature in this patient was the presence of pontine restricted diffusion, indicating associated infarction. Brainstem involvement has been reported in approximately 10%-13% of PRES cases and most commonly affects the pons or midbrain.

Susceptibility-weighted imaging demonstrated hemorrhagic foci within the pons and occipital lobe, reflecting a more severe disease manifestation. The coexistence of infarction and petechial hemorrhage has important prognostic implications because these findings suggest a more severe form of PRES and may reduce the likelihood of complete radiological reversibility. Nevertheless, favorable neurological outcomes may still be achieved when prompt blood pressure control and definitive treatment of the underlying cause are instituted [12].

Acute ischemic stroke was considered because of the presence of diffusion restriction; however, the extensive bilateral vasogenic edema pattern extending beyond a vascular territory favored PRES. Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome may demonstrate overlapping clinical features including headache, seizures, and hypertension, but characteristic angiographic vasoconstriction was not identified. Cerebral venous thrombosis was excluded by MR venography, which demonstrated patent dural venous sinuses and deep cerebral veins.

Follow-up MRI was not available in the present case. However, the patient demonstrated substantial clinical recovery, including improvement in Glasgow Coma Scale, successful extubation, absence of recurrent seizures, and stable outpatient follow-up. These findings support the diagnosis and suggest favorable neurological recovery despite severe initial imaging abnormalities.

Conclusion

This case illustrates an atypical and severe imaging spectrum of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome associated with early-onset eclampsia, demonstrating brainstem infarction and hemorrhage. Recognition of such atypical manifestations is essential to differentiate PRES from other cerebrovascular conditions and to guide timely management of hypertensive emergencies in pregnancy. Furthermore, this case highlights that favorable neurological recovery remains possible despite severe imaging abnormalities when prompt multidisciplinary treatment is instituted.
