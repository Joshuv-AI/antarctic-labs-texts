Trapezius fascia reveals mechanosensory capacity and predominance of nociceptive axons in occipital neuralgia
Authors: Vlad Tereshenko, Merel H. J. Hazewinkel, Madison R. Hussey, Katya Remy, Charles D Hwang, Giulia L. E. Mönnink, Michael C. McCormack, Lisa Gfrerer, William G Austen Jr.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Occipital neuralgia (ON) is a complex and painful condition that presents significant clinical challenges, often resistant to conventional treatments. This study focuses on the trapezius fascia’s role in ON, exploring its neural and mechanosensitive properties. We conducted a detailed analysis of the trapezius fascia in 18 patients with ON and ten control patients using specific molecular markers (PGP9.5, S100, CGRP, TH and Piezo2). Our findings reveal a rich neural composition within the fascia, characterized by heterogenous tissues that include abundant collagen, vasculature, and diverse neural components. Notably, both myelinated and non-myelinated axonal populations were identified, with a significant presence of sympathetic nerve fibers (tyrosine hydroxylase-positive) in ON patients but absent in controls. Mechanosensitive entities resembling cutaneous mechanoreceptors such as Pacinian- and Ruffini-like corpuscles were also observed, highlighting the fascia’s capacity for mechanosensation, which could contribute to pain perception in ON. Crucially, we observed a 71% increase in the nociceptive axonal population in ON patients compared to controls, suggesting a profound alteration in nociceptive signaling pathways and peripheral sensitization. This alteration was underscored by the marked upregulation of Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) specifically within neural components of the fascia, indicating a potential mechanism for the observed pain sensitization. These alterations were not significantly influenced by the presence of neck injuries, indicating other underlying pathophysiological mechanisms at play. The study underscores the trapezius fascia’s critical role not only in biomechanical support but also in neuromuscular communication and pathological pain processing in occipital neuralgia. These insights provide a deeper understanding of the neurobiology of ON and may guide future therapeutic strategies targeting these underlying mechanisms for more effective management of the condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42746-y.

Introduction:

Occipital neuralgia (ON) significantly impacts a vast number of individuals worldwide, leading to severe and debilitating symptoms1. Although surgical interventions, like neurolysis of the occipital nerves, offer symptom relief, challenges persist, including recurrence and unclear etiological origins2–4. Despite its profound impact, occipital neuralgia remains one of the lesser-studied conditions within the spectrum of painful neuropathic syndromes and headache disorders5–7.

Advancements in patient selection criteria and patient-reported outcome measures offer promising avenues for enhancing the management of this condition8,9. Yet, the underlying neurobiology of occipital neuralgia remains poorly understood. There is growing evidence linking the neuropathophysiology of occipital neuralgia with other peripheral neuropathic disorders, suggesting shared mechanisms that contribute to neuropathic pain and nerve compression syndromes10.

Studies on the molecular architecture of nerves implicated in peripheral neuropathies illuminate potential origins of nociceptive perception5. Patients with occipital neuralgia exhibit signs of compression of the greater and lesser occipital nerves11,12. These nerves are encased within the trapezius fascia, which often shows thickening observed during surgical interventions and has been confirmed radiologically13. This suggests that neurolysis might be necessary to alleviate nerve entrapment symptoms, similar as in patients with nerve entrapment syndromes in other areas of the body (i.e., carpal tunnel syndrome, tarsal tunnel)14,15.

The mechanisms underlying fascia thickening and subsequent nerve compression are not fully understood but are thought to involve inflammatory responses from traumatic injuries in the head and neck area13,16,17. Interestingly, many patients without a history of neck trauma also present with similar symptoms, indicating other contributing factors.

In our study, we analyzed tissue samples from the trapezius fascia surrounding the compressed greater and lesser occipital nerves in patients undergoing neurolysis and compared them to control samples from individuals without occipital neuralgia (Figure 1). We focused on identifying nociceptive markers and mechanosensitive endorgans. Materials and methods

Patients’ enrolment

The trapezius fascia samples were harvested from two groups of patients: (1) occipital neuralgia patients undergoing greater and/or lesser occipital nerves neurolysis at the Massachusetts General Hospital between 2023-2024 and (2) patients without the history of headaches undergoing spinal closure at the Weill Cornell Medicine Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery. Baseline characteristics.

The tissue samples were harvested during neurolysis procedure of the GON and LON nerves (1). After midline incision the nerves were exposed through meticulous preparation. If the compression site was identified, the GON and LON nerves were released from the surrounding tissues and a piece of the adjacent trapezius fascia was resected for further analysis. The trapezius fascia from the patients undergoing spinal closure (2) was harvested between the level of the nuchal ridge and second thoracic vertebraT2.

Exclusion criteria for (1) and (2) patient groups were: neurodegenerative disorders, history for lower back pain. For group 2, patients with a prior cervical surgery, a diagnosis of migraine or occipital nerve injury or a history of head and neck trauma including whiplash were excluded. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Massachusetts General Hospital (Protocol 2013P002428) and Weill Cornell Medicine Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery (Protocol 2023P002001). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the approved guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to participation.

Tissue harvesting and processing

Fascia samples were fixated immediately after harvesting by immersion into the 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) diluted in 0.1M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4, at +4°C, for 12 hours. Afterwards, the samples were extensively washed by PBS, pH 7.4, followed by dehydration in ascending sucrose/PBS solutions (10%, 25%, 40%) for 24 hours in each at +4°C. After preparations, the samples were embedded using Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound (Fisher HealthCare, Houston, TX, USA) and stored at -80°C. Trapezius fascia samples were cut into 50 µm thick cross sections using a cryostat (Thermo Scientific Microm HM 550 MVPD, Cryostat 956544D, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Each trapezius fascia sample was divided in 3 equal pieces from cranial to caudal. Ten sections were performed for each piece, thus, 30 sections per sample were analyzed.

Immunofluorescent staining

The double immunofluorescent staining was used as previously established18,19. Primary antibodies were obtained from Merck/Millipore (Temecula, CA, USA), Cell Signaling Technologies (Danvers, MA, USA), Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA), and Agilent Technologies (Lexington, MA, USA). Antibodies from Merck/Millipore included chicken anti-neurofilament (anti-NF, catalogue number AB5539; lot numbers: 4095043), rabbit anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (anti-TH, AB152, lot number: 4003833) and anti-Piezo2 (catalogue number HPA031975; lot number: 000015850). From Cell Signaling (Danvers, MA, USA), rabbit anti-calcitonin gene related peptide (anti-CGRP, catalogue number D5R8F; lot number: 2798662) was obtained. From Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA, USA), mouse anti-Protein Gene Product 9.5 (PGP9.5, catalogue number 7863-1004; lot number: 158905) was obtained. Antibodies from Agilent Technologies (Lexington, MA, USA) included Polyclonal Rabbit Anti-S100 (S100, catalogue number GA504; lot number: 41514833) and was used to identify Schwann cells and their relationship to axons, thereby distinguishing myelinated fibers (enclosed in myelin sheaths derived from Schwann cell plasma membranes) from unmyelinated fibers (multiple axons embedded within individual Schwann cell cytoplasm). Anti-neurofilament was used at concentration of 1:2000, anti-S100 at 1:200, anti-PGP9.5 at 1:500, anti-TH at 1:250, anti-CGRP at 1:500 and anti-Piezo2 at 1:200. Secondary antibodies conjugated with alexa fluor 488 or 568 were obtained from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA). All secondary antibodies were used at concentration of 1:500. Anti-neurofilament is specific for myelinated axons and was used to visualize myelinated nerve fibers. Anti-PGP9.5 marker labels a broader range of axons, including myelinated and unmyelinated fibers, as well as nerve endings. It provides a more comprehensive view of the nerve structure, including both sensory and autonomic fibers. Nociceptive afferent fibers were visualized by anti-CGRP and sympathetic nerve fibers by anti-TH. The mechanosensitive entities within trapezius fascia were visualized using a combination of PGP9.5 and S100 to show both the ultrastructural morphology and molecular expression characteristics of the mechanoreceptors. Piezo2 is a mechanosensitive transmembrane protein responsible sensing mechanical stimuli. All primary antibodies were validated for use in human tissue20–22.

Sections of the fascia samples were stained with (1) anti-PGP9.5 and anti-CGRP, (2) anti-PGP9.5 and anti-S100, (3) anti-neurofilament and anti-TH, and (4) anti-PGP9.5 and anti-Piezo2. Before labelling, frozen sections were air dried followed by incubation in 10% normal goat serum (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in PBS containing 0.1% Triton23. Thereafter, sections were incubated for 48 h with the primary antibodies, washed with PBS, and incubated for two hours with the secondary antibodies. Finally, the tissue was rinsed again in PBS and mounted in fluorescence mounting medium (Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA).

Confocal imaging and image analysis

Fluorescently labelled fascia samples were analyzed with a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 with DarkPanel Cage Incubator from Oko Labs). A series of virtual CLSM sections of 0.5-1 µm thickness were cut through the region of interest. The images of each section were acquired with a 1024×1024 pixel resolution, and 3D projections were rendered using the confocal software. Double-colored images were generated using lasers with excitation wavelengths of 488 and 568 nm. 3D projections were recreated into the videos using the confocal software. Image assessment was performed in a single-blinded manner, whereby the evaluator was kept unaware of whether samples originated from patients with occipital neuralgia or from healthy controls. All images were coded prior to analysis to prevent identification bias, and the coded designations were only decoded following completion of the quantitative assessment.

The intensities and cross-sectional areas of the identified signals were evaluated for quantification analysis using the same parameters under x20 magnification. Cross-sections were stained using anti-PGP9.5 and anti-CGRP antibodies, and QuPath version 0.5.1 was used for image analysis24. PGP9.5-positive axons were detected using the pixel classification module because of the heterogeneous and inconsistent morphological features of axonal profiles. The pixel classification module employed supervised machine learning algorithms trained on manually annotated regions of interest to distinguish PGP9.5-positive axonal pixels from background and non-specific staining, using color intensity thresholds and texture features specific to the immunohistochemical signal pattern. Subsequently, the cell detection module was applied to identify and segment individual CGRP-positive signals within the tissue sections. The cross-sectional areas of PGP9.5-positive and CGRP-positive signals were measured, and colocalization quotients were calculated to quantify the degree of overlap between the two markers. Signal intensity of CGRP-positive staining was documented both within colocalized areas with PGP9.5-positive axons and in non-colocalized regions to determine nerve fiber phenotypes. Following automated classification and detection, each analyzed image was manually verified to ensure accurate recognition of PGP9.5 and CGRP immunoreactive signals and to correct any misclassifications by the algorithm.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Non-parametric values are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). To check normal distribution of the variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. All measured data were determined to be non-normally distributed and were therefore analyzed using non-parametric statistical tests. The types of data subjected to analysis included: (1) cross-sectional areas of immunofluorescent signals measured in μm2 (PGP9.5-positive axonal area, CGRP-positive signal area, and colocalized areas), (2) signal intensity measurements in arbitrary units (AU) for CGRP fluorescence intensity, and (3) density and ratio measurements expressed as percentages (density of CGRP-positive signal within neural networks, ratio of nociceptive axons to overall axonal populations, and ratio of CGRP expression within neural components to overall tissue CGRP expression). Non-parametric data were compared between occipital neuralgia patients and control groups using the Mann–Whitney U test (unpaired). The density of CGRP-positive signal within the neural network was calculated as the percentage of CGRP-positive pixels within manually defined neural regions divided by the total number of pixels in those regions, as quantified using QuPath image analysis software. Results with a p-value of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Neural components are inherent in the trapezius fascia in both health and disease

Trapezius fascia presents a heterogenous tissue composed of abundant collagen, vasculature and neural components in healthy (n=10) and ON patients (n=18). Trapezius fascia is a mechanosensitive organ

As an integral biomechanical support of the neuromuscular system, the trapezius fascia demonstrates mechanosensitive capacity as well. Beside the presence of neural components within the trapezius fascia, diverse mechanosensitive entities were identified across the fascia samples. The fascia samples of healthy individuals as well as in patients with multiple mechanosensitive receptors were observed using antibodies against PGP9.5 and S100 (Video 1). The identification of mechanosensitive entities in the trapezius fascia were distinguished using double immunofluorescence staining using antibodies against PGP9.5 (expressed preferentially in nerve endings) and S100 (expressed in Schwann cells). The nerve ending winding with myelin sheaths encircling them were identified and selected based on the following criteria as described before: independence of the nerves trajectory, morphologically differentiated aspect25. Moreover, to be regarded as mechanoreceptors S100-positive signals need to encircle the PGP9.5 axons26. In agreement with these premises, we identified capsular corpuscle-like structures of variable size and shape containing numerous axon profiles complexly arranged27. 3D reconstruction of the acquired images demonstrated the winding axonal endings embedded into S100-positive signal, resembling various mechanoreceptors found regularly in the skin27. Interestingly, none of the identified mechanoreceptors were positive for Piezo2 expression (Figure 4G-I), which suggests that they might resemble Pacinian or Ruffini corpuscles20.

Expression of CGRP is upregulated in trapezius fascia of occipital neuralgia patients

The fascia from the ON patients demonstrates a higher density of nociceptive nerve fibers compared to the control patients (Video 2). The most striking finding of the study was the higher number of CGRP-positive axons within the pathological fascia (Figure 5). Only two out of six patients without history of ON showed presence of CGRP-positive signal within the neural components of the trapezius fascia (Figure 5A-C). While the overall area of CGRP-positive signal did not statistically differ between ON and control patients (376.3 [IQR 141.6-612.9] vs. 148.2 [IQR 100.3-288.3] μm2, p=0.15), the area of CGRP-positive signal colocalized with PGP9.5 signal differed significantly between ON and control patients (77.5 [IQR 26.94-330.1] vs. 0 [IQR 0-1.1] μm2, p<0.0001) (Figure 5D-F, G, H). Moreover, the ratio of nociceptive axons (% of PGP9.5-positive area colocalized with CGRP-positive area of overall PGP9.5-positive area) compared to the overall axonal populations was higher in ON patients: 71.0 [IQR 51.1-83.4] vs. 0 [0-2.25]%, p<0.0001) (Figure 5I). All measurements were performed as described previously19 (Figure 6A-C). The ratio of CGRP expression level within the axonal population compared to the overall CGRP expression within the fascia tissue was higher in ON patients 29.9 [IQR 16.8-50.1]% vs. 0 [IQR 0-4.1]%, (p<0.0001), suggesting a neural origin of peripheral sensitization via CGPR upregulation within the fascia of ON patients (Figure 6E). The density of the CGRP-positive signal within the neural system was also higher in ON patients 22.6 [IQR 14.3-34.2] vs. 0 [IQR 0-12.5] %, (p=0.002) (Figure 6D). Interestingly, the CGRP-positive signal intensity within axonal populations was higher in the ON patients compared to the healthy patients as well 689.3 [IQR 433.1-1255] vs. 0 [IQR 0-297.4] AU, (p=0.0002) (Figure 6F). Discussion

The field of nerve decompression surgery in the head and neck is rapidly advancing particularly for patients with headaches that are unresponsive to conservative treatments2. Over recent decades, the causality between pathological processes in cranial nerves and the occurrence of headaches has been established, prompting the development of innovative surgical approaches targeting the potential peripheral origins of these conditions28,29. Although nerve decompression surgery has improved patient outcomes, the etiology underlying these headache conditions has not been fully elucidated30,31. In individuals with ON, a notable thickening of the fascia is consistently observed; however, the specific neuropathological or molecular mechanisms behind this phenomenon are still not well understood13,16. The most striking finding of our study is the ultra-morphological evidence supporting the trapezius fascia’s mechanosensory capacity. Additionally, we observed significant upregulation of nociceptive neurotransmitters within the axonal populations in ON patients.

The fascia, predominantly composed of connective tissue, serves as a biomechanical modulator within the neuromuscular system. Traditionally, its role was thought to be limited to providing biomechanical support and facilitating force transmission across multiple muscle groups, thereby contributing to coordinated movement. This view has been supported by numerous studies over the years that highlighted its structural importance32,33. However, recent advances in medical research challenge the notion of fascia’s role as purely biomechanical. Anecdotal evidence and emerging studies now suggest its multimodal capacity in modulating muscle homeostasis, pain, and contractile properties34–36. This shift in perception recognizes the fascia as a comprehensive system that extends throughout the neuromuscular framework of the human body, playing a critical role not just in supporting, but actively modulating highly coordinated neuromuscular responses to various external and internal stimuli. While no significant sex-related differences in the expression patterns of PGP9.5, Piezo2, S100, TH, or CGRP were observed between male and female subjects in either the control or occipital neuralgia groups, our research contributes to this evolving understanding by demonstrating a rich innervation of the trapezius fascia, suggesting a significant role for the fascia as a communicator between the neuromuscular system and the brain. This neural involvement indicates that the functions of fascia extend beyond traditional biomechanical support, influencing both neuromuscular communication and brain interactions. Further, confirming the complexity of fascia’s role, recent findings from human and animal studies have identified neural entities within the thoracolumbar fascia37, fascia of the upper limb muscles38, visceral fascia39, and other body sites40. Given the extensive coverage of skeletal muscles by the fascial system, it is plausible to assume its crucial role in detecting diverse external and internal stimuli, thereby acting as a critical feedback mechanism for muscle contraction and homeostasis. These studies, along with our findings, highlight the unique and often understated neural component of the fascia’s architecture, shedding light on previously undiscovered functional aspects of this vital organ system.

Beyond the presence of axonal populations, our findings highlight the mechanosensitive entities within the trapezius fascia, suggesting its capacity for autonomous mechanosensation. This mechanosensory ability indicates a significant role for the fascia as a mediator between the neuromuscular system and the brain, potentially influencing body posture and coordinated movement through adaptive neuromuscular responses.

While the ubiquitous presence of these mechanosensitive entities is confirmed across patients, their specific physiological properties remain undefined, making their exact role in neuromuscular coordination unclear. Further elucidation of these entities’ nature may require advanced electrophysiological testing and three-dimensional ultra-morphological reconstruction. Interestingly, the absence of Piezo2 expression suggests that the mechanoreceptors might resemble Ruffini or Pacinian-like corpuscles20. To date, only a few studies, such as those on the plantar fascia of horses41 and thoracolumbar fascia of mice42, have provided morphological evidence of mechanoreceptors. The mechanosensitive capacity of fascia represents an essential evolutionary adaptation that may facilitate peripheral nociceptive sensitization, potentially leading to the chronicity of pain syndromes43. Our insights into the neural contributions and mechanosensitive capabilities of the trapezius fascia may illuminate its role in pathological conditions like fibromyalgia and other pain syndromes44,45. These findings could further inform the effectiveness of manual therapy and mechanical stimulation in managing painful conditions46.

Occipital neuralgia is a debilitating and painful syndrome and an increasingly recognized condition that presents substantial clinical challenges47. Although nerve decompression surgery effectively alleviates symptoms by addressing the greater and lesser occipital nerves, symptom recurrence remains an issue in some cases48. Recent reports have observed a thickening of the trapezius fascia in patients suffering from ON, which may explain nerve compression13. However, the recurrent nature of symptoms and unclear etiology of the fascia thickening underscore an incomplete understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Our findings illuminate the molecular alterations in the fascia of patients with occipital neuralgia. Specifically, we observed upregulation of Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) within the neural components of the trapezius fascia (Figure 5). Although CGRP is not an exclusive marker of nociceptors, multiple experimental studies have demonstrated that CGRPα‑expressing small‑diameter primary afferents largely correspond to peptidergic nociceptors and are required for behavioral responses to noxious heat, capsaicin, and itch. Accordingly, the marked increase of CGRP within PGP9.5‑positive axons in ON patients most likely reflects an enhanced nociceptive drive within the trapezius fascia rather than a nonspecific change in overall sensory innervation. This increase in CGRP expression suggests changes in the neural populations towards a nociceptive phenotype, potentially explaining the peripheral sensitization observed in pathological fascia. While increased CGRP expression has been noted in other studies, suggesting its role in pain mitigation, the causality of this upregulation in ON patients remains elusive42. It is plausible that damaged nerves could undergo axonal collateral sprouting into the surrounding tissues, including fascia, which might account for the nociceptive nerve fibers observed in patients with a history of neck injury or inflammatory responses. However, in our ON patient cohort, the prevalence of neck injury was 44%, with no significant differences in CGRP expression levels identified between patients with or without neck injuries. This suggests other mechanisms might be responsible for the higher CGRP levels in ON patients without a history of neck injury. One hypothesis, supported by an experimental study, showed an increase in nociceptive nerve fibers following artificially induced inflammation of the thoracolumbar fascia in rats49, suggesting that systemic inflammatory events may trigger a nociceptive phenotype switch in the neural network. The etiology of nerve compression may be multifaceted, as indicated by potential commonalities in various nerve compression syndromes10. The abundant presence of CGRP within the thickened fascia might be explained by a predisposition in these patients to compression syndromes, where CGRP expression marks the pathological process. Interestingly, CRGP plays an important role in the development of migraine as well. Moreover, anti-CGRP antibodies have proven effective in preventing and treating migraine symptoms50. Previous research shows that patients with ON often experience concomitant migraine51. The results of this study suggest a potential shared pathomechanism between ON and migraines. Moreover, this may indicate a possible peripheral involvement in migraine onset, challenging the long-standing theory that migraines originate primarily from central mechanisms.

Another serendipitous finding of our study is the abundance of sympathetic nerve fibers (TH-positive) in patients with occipital neuralgia (ON). This suggests a potential role of the sympathetic nervous system in the dysregulation of vascular homeostasis52 or in the increased fascial tone observed in ON patients22. The presence of TH-positive fibers, which may be modulated by stress, trauma, or sudden changes in temperature, could lead to increased sympathetic activity. This, in turn, may result in alterations in thermoregulation, lymphatic flow, and venous circulation53, potentially explaining the chronic changes in fascial homeostasis, vascularization, and subsequent tissue remodelling observed in ON patients23.

A notable limitation of our study is the small sample size, which necessitates further validation of our findings across a broader cohort. Additionally, while our methods were effective in identifying specific nociceptive markers and mechanoreceptors within the trapezius fascia samples obtained during surgery, they did not allow for examination of the fascia in its entirety. Future research should incorporate tissue clearing and advanced microscopy techniques to enable a more comprehensive analysis of the fascia’s neural architecture and mechanosensitive properties.

The insights from our study enhance the understanding of the neurobiology of occipital neuralgia. By elucidating the nociceptive and mechanosensitive properties of the trapezius fascia, which may help pave the way for novel therapeutic approaches. These findings hold the potential to offer more sustained relief for patients suffering from this debilitating condition, and they underscore the importance of targeting these underlying mechanisms in future treatments.
