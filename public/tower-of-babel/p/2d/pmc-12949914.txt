The Effect of CYP2D6 Gene Polymorphism on Tramadol Efficacy in Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: ERDİ GÜRBÜZ, ABDO A. ELFİKY, NUSRET ÖK, AYLİN KÖSELER
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimTramadol, a centrally acting analgesic, is widely used in postoperative pain management. This study investigates the relationship between CYP2D6 gene polymorphisms and the efficacy of tramadol in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty.Patients and MethodsA total of 210 participants, including 110 TKA patients and 100 healthy controls, were enrolled. CYP2D6 genotyping was performed using PCR amplification and DNA sequencing. TKA patients received standard postoperative intravenous tramadol (100 mg), and pain intensity was assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS) at 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes. Genotype-phenotype distributions and VAS time-course changes were statistically compared.ResultsThe extensive metabolizer (EM) phenotype was the most common in the patient group (78.2%). Significant correlations were observed between VAS score changes and CYP2D6*3 and *4 alleles. EM patients demonstrated superior analgesic response to tramadol compared to intermediate metabolizers. Docking results revealed strong binding affinities for tramadol and its metabolites to CYP2D6, ranging from –6.0 to –8.0 kcal/mol.ConclusionThe CYP2D6 genotype influences tramadol efficacy and side-effect profiles, suggesting that pharmacogenetic testing should guide postoperative pain therapy.

Introduction

Tramadol is well known and widely used as an effective painkiller for patients recovering from operations, particularly in cases of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) (1). The operative procedure in TKA, commonly known to cause the induction of serious pain, therefore requires an optimal intravenous administration in order to encourage early recovery, to ameliorate the results of other functions, and to condense the hospitalization period or stay (2-5).

This drug has both central action and can be used for pain relief treatment in several ways; it is a weak μ-opioid receptor agonist, and it hampers noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake. Consequently, the presence of these therapeutic options, tramadol seems like a more favorable alternative for that particular group of patients, mainly because it has relatively fewer downsides when compared with its counterparts, which are the traditional opioids.

As medically necessary as it may be, the therapeutic efficiency of tramadol seems to be very different from one patient to another, which is likely to lead to problems with postoperative pain treatment. One of the key genetic factors that play a significant role in determining this variability is the polymorphisms in the CYP2D6 gene, which is the required coding for the enzyme cytochrome P450 responsible for tramadol metabolism into its active metabolite, O-demethyltramadol (1, 6). CYP2D6 is known to show a quite polymorphic profile due to a variety of alleles, which could be the ground for classifying people as slow, moderate, rapid, or ultrarapid metabolizers. This distinction of classes detects mainly the drug’s pharmacokinetics and, therefore, the drug’s analgesic potential.

It is a well-documented fact that people with poor metabolization phenotypes can experience a lack of the required drug’s effective conversion into its active metabolite, which can potentially result in the lack of proper control over post-operative pain (2). In contrast, ultra-rapid metabolizers may suffer increased severe side effects, including sedation, respiratory depression, and others, which are common among opioid users and are related to the drug. These result differences give a reason for the physician to examine tramadol prescription through a genetic lens because the differences may lead to the result not being optimal, and magnify the chance of side effects (7-9).

Poor metabolizers are usually the ones that are defective, and this leads to a decrease in the action of the enzyme, thereby reducing the ability to convert tramadol into its active metabolite. This may ultimately lead to sub-therapeutic plasma concentrations of the metabolite and hence taint the effectiveness of the postoperative anesthetic. It is worth noting that the opposite, the presence of ultra-fast metabolizers who have the genes of different CYP2D6, would lead to such a situation where the levels of metabolism would be higher than the normal range. It can occur that hemorrhoids are a result of the increase in enzymes in such organisms as the capacity for forming the compound O-desmethyltramadol, thus may go beyond the normal, and thus result in a higher analgesic response. However, it also boosts the propensity of experiencing adverse effects and opioid toxicity (10).

An individual’s genetic profile understanding respective CYP2D6 activity is a critical part of getting the correct tramadol dose by the doctor. To illustrate, the genotyping that is done before the total knee replacement surgery helps in the identification of the patients who are poor metabolizers and might benefit from the selection of alternative pain relievers or the adjustment of tramadol doses in order to ensure adequate control of the pain. There are efficiency and safety issues, both of which come from this genetic diversity. Studies have pointed out that patients with the extensive metabolizer (EM) phenotype get good pain relief with standard tramadol doses, while poor metabolizer (PM) individuals might feel the pain is not being managed effectively, even with an increase in doses (11).

Hence, there is a variability in the activity of CYP2D6 transcription and thus in the reaction of tramadol to drugs; therefore, a thorough investigation of genetic predictors outlining post-operative pain profiling will be needed. Data from clinical practice is considerably changing, which in turn is the basis for a personalized pain management programme after the surgery, the effectiveness of the individualized treatment is such that it allows patients attain desired health outcomes because of genetic variations that are a relative CYP2D6 is a significant human hepatic P450 that converts various drug compounds, including tramadol, and some subjects have dysfunctional alleles (11, 12).

In the perioperative care of patients with TKA, the effect of the CYP2D6 gene polymorphism on the effectiveness of tramadol is a field of contemporary interest. Tramadol, an opioid pain reliever, uses a unique dual action mechanism, as it is converted to an active form, O-demethyltramadol, mainly by the CYP2D6. The CYP2D6 gene differences can group people into poor metabolizers, intermediate metabolizers (IM), normal metabolizers, and super-rapid metabolizers. This is an important factor in the drug metabolizability and the painkiller effectiveness as well (13).

The study’s aim was to investigate the association between CYP2D6*2, *3, *4, and *10 alleles and the efficacy of tramadol administered for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing TKA is grounded in the pharmacogenetic basis of tramadol metabolism.

Patients and Methods

Study design.The present study was a prospective case-control study, and the required approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Pamukkale University prior to the study (No. E-60116787-020-155464).

Study population. The study was conducted with a specific group of patients who had recently undergone TKA at Pamukkale University Hospital, Department of Orthopedics. These patients, understanding the nature and purpose of our study, willingly agreed to participate. However, not all patients were eligible for inclusion in the study. We established a set of exclusion criteria to ensure the safety and validity of our research. Patients who had taken any form of analgesics within the last 6 hours were excluded to avoid any potential interference with the study drugs. Additionally, patients with chronic conditions such as renal failure and liver cirrhosis were also excluded due to the potential health risks. Pregnant women were not included in the study due to the potential risks to both the mother and the unborn child. We also excluded patients with a known hypersensitivity to tramadol or any of its ingredients to prevent allergic reactions. Finally, patients who were taking an MAO inhibitor or had taken one in the last 14 days were not included in the study due to the potential for serious drug interactions. One hundred and ten patients with total knee anthropoplasty who presented for post-operative pain treatment and 100 healthy controls (210 subjects in total) were enrolled in the study. The patients in the study group were recruited in accordance with specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. A 100 mm visual analog scale (VAS) was utilized to measure the severity of postoperative pain in patients with TKA. The patients were asked to score their pain severity along the VAS between 0 and 100, with zero indicating the absence of pain and 100 mm indicating the most severe pain.

Tramadol (contramal; 100 mg) was administered after randomization. The drug was diluted in 150 ml of physiological saline and given as an intravenous infusion within 20 min. Pain severity was assessed along the VAS at 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min and followed up in terms of vital signs and possible side effects. The study was terminated at 60 min.

Genotyping. Extraction of genomic DNA from whole blood was performed using a commercial kit (High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany®). DNA samples were quantified and stored at -20˚C until further analysis. Genomic DNA was amplified by PCR using primers for the specific variants studied. The genotype analyses for the CYP2D6 *2, *3, *4, and *10 alleles were performed with PCR by sequencing on an ABI PRISM 7700 Sequence System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Statistical analysis. IBM SPSS v.25 statistical package program was used for all the analyses. Student’s t-test was performed for continuous variables (age, VAS score) in the patient and control cohorts, while Pearson’s Chi-square test was employed to evaluate categorical variables (sex, CYP2D6 genotype). The effect size of the factors was analyzed by Logistic Regression (Backward Wald). The statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for all the analyses.

Computational analysis. We intended to study the binding energies of tramadol and two of its derivatives, namely, desmetramadol and O-demethyltramadol, to the active site of CYP2D6. In order to attain this goal, we downloaded the structure data files (SDF) for the three ligands from the PubChem database of the National Library of Medicine, National Center for Biotechnology Information (15). The CIDs of tramadol, Desmetramadol, and O-demethyltramadol are 33741, 9838803, and 130829, respectively. Additionally, we downloaded all the 3D structures of the wildtype CYP2D6 from the protein data bank (PDB) (16). All the structures were subjected to preparation steps before the docking, which include the following steps. Firstly, water and all ligands (except the heme group) were removed. Secondly, we added the missing H-atoms. Finally, if the structure contains more than one polypeptide chain, it was separated into different PDB files. We use the UCLA Chimera software to prepare both the ligands and the solved structures of CYP2D6 (17). The charges and any missing H-atoms are added, and PDBQT files are generated. The default docking parameters were then used with an exhaustive value of 8. The search box was set to cover the entire protein. After docking, selected structures were further examined for formed interactions utilizing the protein-ligand interaction profiler (PLIP) webserver (18). Some complexes were depicted with PyMOL software (19).

Discussion

The most recent trial conducted by Hamilton et al. showed how crucial it was to present the CYP2D6 genotype information in the tramadol treatment guidelines (11). The authors noted that if patients are sorted for CYP2D6 genes, it will consist of gaining a better understanding of the process and the outcomes. They classified patients based on their CYP2D6 genotype as EM, IM, and PM, which were the three groups. They demonstrated that EM patients get a better effect from the drug, and IM and PM patients who are less affected by the drug-provoking pain are found to be more likely to consume opioids, respectively. It is concluded that pain relief and opioid consumption between the various groups varied significantly. That is to say, the efficiency of tramadol, which is metabolically activated by CYP2D6 to bring about the analgesic effect, is quite dissimilar due to different patients’ genetic profiles.

Despite constituting only 2-4% of hepatic CYP enzymes, CYP2D6 stands out as one of the most extensively studied (21, 22). Notably, when dealing with patients using multiple drugs in a single dose, an examination of the association between metabolites of the CYP2D6 gene polymorphism and tramadol use reveals significant differences in pain management (21, 23). Recognizing CYP2D6 gene polymorphisms could prove immensely beneficial in post-surgery pain management.

When we analyzed the healthy controls and the effect of the CYP2D6 gene on the study population, their mean age was 57.05 years, which was considerably lower than the patients. However, since the CYP2D6 genome is stable in-patient genetics and it is not an enzyme that changes with age, we did not include the difference between age and groups in the statistics.

Having followed up the patients for two years, another study provided a comparative evaluation of 214 male and 1,040 female patients undergoing total knee replacement surgery (24). In that study, tramadol was co-administered along with similar opioids for post-operative pain control, and the patients were reported to be in the geriatric population, with the mean age of 67.9 in men and 67.3 in women. In our study, our sex-wise analysis revealed that 86.4% of the patients undergoing a prosthesis intervention due to knee osteoarthritis were women. Previous investigations clearly reveal that women tend to present to healthcare centers with knee osteoarthritis complaints more frequently than men, and the higher number of female patients in our study confirmed earlier studies.

In an investigation into the frequency of the *4 allele in the Turkish population, Köseler et al. identified its frequency to be 21% among the population residing in the Bursa region, which turned out to be higher than the previous reports exploring the Gaziantep and Istanbul regions (11% and 15%, respectively) (25). Furthermore, the activation metabolism of the major active metabolite of tramadol is predominantly mediated through CYP2D6 (26). A clinical study on the pharmacokinetics of tramadol identified the drug half-life time to be 49.3% higher in *2/*10 carriers than their *1/*1 counterparts, also revealing substantial decreases in catalytic activity for tramadol among those harboring *2/*10 and *10/*10 compared to *1/*1 carriers (27). This study also did not reveal any marked difference in the pharmacokinetic properties of tramadol between *1/*1 and *2/*2 carriers, adding that its pharmacokinetics were unaffected by the CYP2D6*2 allele.

Another study on the liability of the interaction of CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 genomes with tramadol reported that a lower dose was maintained in heterozygous and wild-type alleles of *3 and *4 in the CYP2D6 gene, while a higher dose was required in homozygous *3 and *4 alleles (21). Eight out of 10 genotyped individuals were CYP2D6 allele (PM or IM) carriers with a differential haplotype and were taking an additional drug known as a CYP2D6 inhibitor (21).

It is already well-established that VAS proves to be an effective and sufficient scale in rating pain severity to assess the effects of post-operative pain treatment; thus, the VAS measurements in our study can be considered a valid scoring to evaluate tramadol and analgesic efficiency of pain severity (28, 29). A published report analyzing tramadol efficacy through VAS and comparing its effect with CYP2D6 genomes documented that 52.6% of the subjects were harboring the *10 allele (30)the patients were divided into three groups (CYP2D6*1/*1, n = 33; CYP2D6*1/*10, n = 28; CYP2D6*10/*10, n = 50. Another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial showed that wild-type *1/*1 alleles and heterozygous *3 and *4 alleles were more prevalent in the carriers. When compared to its effect on pain scores, two phenotypes (+)-and (–)-tramadol and (+)- and (–)-M1 concentrations *10 μm, *3 μm, and *4 to 5 *pm triggers are reported as haplotypes for CYP2D6 variances (31).

As far as genotyping is concerned, two different views exist in relation to matching in the *2, *3, *4, and *10 genomes. Some previous research argued that the alleles carrying two different polymorphisms were combined and considered as one different genome in combined heterozygote carriers (14, 32, 33). Therefore, the combined heterozygote in the CYP2D6 genome of a patient harboring *1/*3 and *1/*4 polymorphisms could be displayed as *3/*4, and accordingly, *3/*4 was phenotyped as EM, *2/*10 as IM, and *3/*4 as UM. Though homozygous polymorphisms remained undetected in our study, we additionally investigated these variances in compound heterozygotes.

In the present study, no homozygous *2/*2, *3/*3, *4/*4, or *10/*10 haplotypes were detected among the CYP2D6 genotypes. Furthermore, although the VAS scores, indicative of pain threshold, decreased below 50 in the 60th minute in all patients who received tramadol following TKA, patients in the EM group reported significantly lower pain intensity.

Study limitations. The primary limitation highlighted in the study is the restricted sample size, which could potentially impede a thorough examination of the relationship between tramadol efficacy and the CYP2D6 gene polymorphism. Therefore, further research is needed to increase the statistical power of these results. TKA, which necessitates a high pain score, is a surgery where tramadol, a powerful analgesic agent, is used. A single genetic screening was performed on the blood samples provided. A second, confirmatory test was not performed on the same patient. Finally, as a candidate-gene study, there is a chance that other genes or polymorphisms that were not included in the study could have a functional impact. However, tramadol treatment can be used as a pharmacokinetic indication of CYP2D6 enzymes, which are thought to be outside the limitations of our study.
