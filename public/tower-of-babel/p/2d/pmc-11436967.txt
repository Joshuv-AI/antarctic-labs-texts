Patients’ intention to make an up-front payment at private outpatient clinics in Malaysia as a no-show reduction method
Authors: Aqil M Daher, Nitiyanantam Perremal, Adlina Suleiman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulating patients’ no-show behavior is critical from the standpoints of sustainable medical services and operational efficiencies. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the patients’ intention to make partial up-front payments at outpatient clinics. This was a cross-sectional study design introducing a self-administered questionnaire to 221 outpatients at a private health facility. The questionnaire measured the patient’s demographic characteristics, perceived usefulness (PU), trust in the health facility, and intention to make upfront partial payments. Out of the total respondents, 57.4% were female. There were 34.8% Malays, 40.6% Chinese and 24.6% Indians. The majority (66.5%) of the respondents attained tertiary education. Nearly a third of the respondents (30.5%) reported an income between 3000 and 5000 Malaysian Ringgit (RM). Regarding payment mode, just more than half (51.1%) made self-payment, and 21.8% by guaranteed letter. A quarter (24.9%) waited more than 3 h for consultation and 59.6% visited the health facility more than 2 times in a year. Initial analysis showed that PU, trust, age, education, number of visits, and hours of waiting were significantly associated with the intention to make a partial payment. Multiple linear regression showed that perceived usefulness (B = 0.517, p < 0.001); trust in hospital management (B = 0.288, p < 0.001) and number of visits (B = 0.216, p < 0.001) were associated with the intention to make partial payment. Intention to make partial up-front payments is associated with higher perceived usefulness in making such payments and hospital trust. Visiting the health facility frequently was associated with a higher intention to make upfront partial payment. Introduction

Healthcare administrators are directly accountable for the financial success and growth of their facilities. As a result, they are continuously looking for methods to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of their operations1.

A no-show was described as “failing to call to cancel a scheduled appointment“2,3. No-show behaviour has negative consequences for both patients and hospital officials. No-shows translate to lost revenue and scheduling difficulties during a period when a practice’s overhead meter is still running in terms of labour, rent, and electricity expenditures4,5. One of the primary areas impacted by no-shows is waiting time6,7. When no-show takes place, the health facility must reschedule the appointment to an already packed appointment list which impacts the waiting time of patients. Moreover, the healthcare facility usually resorts to overbooking to compensate for anticipated no-show. Accumulation of patients more than expected can elongate patients’ waiting time8–10.

Literature has shown that no-show is a widely prevalent issue. No-show rates at general medicine clinics and urban community centres have been estimated to be between 15% and 30%11. It has been suggested that no-show rates in primary care can reach 50%12. Among 1296 outpatient clinic sessions at a Swiss university, 15.8% reported no-show events13. An 18.8% no-show rate was reported from major clinics in the UK with subspecialist clinics having the highest percentage14. Locally, reports showed that no-show rates in 2019 hit 28% in Hospital Kuala Lumpur15 and around 35% in the dietetic clinic of a tertiary hospital in southern Malaysia in 2017. The figure from Singapore reached 28.6%16. In contrast, South Korea reported a rate as low as 6.12% in 201817.

Prior research in health information systems and healthcare management have widely agreed that patients’ no-show behaviour is not random, and has examined the elements that influence it, such as gender, age, referrals, appointment time, transportation services, and weather18.

Several strategies have been developed, without success, to reduce no-shows. These include automated reminder phone calls, staff reminder phone calls, postcard reminders, direct cell phone calls, SMS text messaging, e-mail reminder messages, double booking no-show patients, booking on different days of the week, releasing no-show patients, and administrative charges for patient no-shows19–21.

Partial or full up-front payments have been practiced in tourism and commerce, nonetheless, there is no evidence of its use in the healthcare system. To investigate patient behavioural intention, TAM theoretical framework has been utilized in this study. Up to the conduct of this study, TAM model has been widely applied to predict user intention to adopt specific technologies. In healthcare realm it has been applied to predict telehealth22, remote health technologies23, ), smart hospital service24, wearable device25 and acceptance of technologies designed for hypertension management26. Nonetheless, investigating patient intention to make partial upfront payment is rather novel. No previous research has been attempted.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to assess the patients’ intention to make a partial up-front payment to lower outpatient clinic no-show rates among a sample of patients attending a private health facility. Specifically, we tested a theoretical model that predicts a patient’s intention and assessed factors associated with a patient’s intention including sociodemographic characteristics and healthcare-related factors.

Methods

Participants

This was a cross-sectional study design conducted among outpatients attending a private healthcare facility in the state of Selangor, Malaysia. Inclusion criteria included Malaysian adult patients who are 18 years and above and able to read and comprehend the English language of the questionnaire. Patients who were attending for procedural purposes or having a mental illness were excluded.

Sample size

The initial sample size was determined to be 193 using the formula for linear regression analysis by Sokal and Rohlf27 with the assumption of a 0.2 correlation between variables at a significance level of 0.05. Applying the variance inflation formula to accommodate for multiple comparisons, the calculated sample size became 201. To accommodate for 10% non-response rate, an additional 20 respondents are required, and this brought the final sample size to 221.

Sampling methods

Participants were selected using a systematic random sampling method. A list of patients scheduled for specialist appointments was obtained from the front desk. Based on the logic of systematic random sampling, participants were randomly selected at predetermined intervals. The interval was calculated by dividing the total expected number of patients per day of operation by the required sample in a day. We decided to recruit 15 participants every day. With an average of 300 outpatients, the sampling interval was 20. The first patient was selected with a simple random sampling method from the first twenty registered patients by a simple lottery method. Subsequently, every twentieth respondent was chosen. This method suits outpatient clinics as patients are arranged in a sequence of registration. If the selected patient was missed or didn’t turn up, the next immediate patient was recruited.

The researcher distributed the questionnaires to patients who were waiting to be seen at specialist outpatient clinics. The researcher explained the objective of the study, risks, and benefits of participation. Patients’ confidentiality and voluntary participation were ensured. Informed consent was obtained before handing the questionnaire to the selected participants. The investigator remained seated near the participant if any clarification was sought. The data collection was conducted from August 2021 to September 2021.

Data collection tool

For this study, we adopted the most widely used theoretical framework to measure behavioural intention proposed by Davis (1998). The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is an extension of the reasoned action theory which is used to explain an individual’s behavioral intention to take a specific action. TAM was utilized in this study with modification by replacing ease of use with trust in hospital management as a potential factor that may predict patients’ intentions. We then tested the relationship between perceived usefulness, trust, and behavioural intention to make a partial up-front payment.

The questionnaire (S1) was developed in the English language and consisted of four parts:

Part I

Respondents’ sociodemographic data consisted of age, gender, ethnicity, educational level, estimated monthly household income, frequency of attending specialist OPD in a year, mode of payment at the OPD, average waiting time at OPD, the incidence of skipping OPD appointment, and waiting long time due to others’ no-show.

Part 2

Respondents’ Trust (T) toward the hospital. Four items assess patients’ trust in hospital management and are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree.

Part 3

Respondents’ Perceived Usefulness (PU). Four items assess the patient’s perception of the usefulness of up-front payment as outpatient clinic booking. The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree.

Part 4

Respondents’ Intention (I). Three items assess the patient’s intention to make up-front payment. The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree.

Pilot study

A preliminary pilot study was conducted on a smaller subsample of the population that met the demographic profile and inclusion requirements for the actual study. During the pilot testing, a sample of 30 individuals (10 Malay, 10 Chinese, and 10 Indian respondents) responded to the questionnaire. Internal consistency testing guided the investigator on the choice of final items that represent each construct. The final version survey was then pre-tested for acceptability and comprehension.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered and analysed using the IBM SPSS v. 26 and SmartPLS v.3 Software. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Categorical variables were described with frequency and percentage, and numerical variables were described with mean and standard deviation. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire were ascertained through construct validity statistics (Factor loadings), discriminant validity statistics, and reliability for the three constructs; Intention, Trust, and PU.

After ascertaining the validity, and reliability of the questionnaire, the multiple linear regression model was used to identify factors associated with patients’ intentions. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

Of the 221 distributed questionnaires, 193 returned the completed form yielding a response rate of 87.3%. Table 1 shows that out of the total respondents, 57.4% were female. There were 34.8% Malays and 40.6% Chinese. The majority (66.5%) of the respondents had attained tertiary education (66.5%), and 38.9% had visited the healthcare facility 3–4 times per year. Regarding payment mode, just more than half (51.1%) made self-payment. Pertaining to waiting time, about 42.5% experienced a waiting time of 2–3 h while 24.9% waited more than 3 h. Discussion

The hospital appointment system improves the efficiency and effectiveness of hospital operations, for example, by reducing patient waiting time, reducing medical staff workloads, and improving hospital revenue. Patients’ no-show behaviour is a widespread problem that complicates the operation of hospital appointment systems. To the best of our knowledge, we are reporting the results of the first study addressing patients’ intention to make partial up-front payment; a new approach to reduce no-shows among a sample of outpatients at a private health facility. This study supports that perceived usefulness is an important determinant of patients’ intention to make partial up-front payments.

The model that included the main three latent variables was found to be sufficiently fit, resulting in a good measurement model with discriminant validity. The model also has a high level of internal consistency and composite reliabilities, both of which are higher than the specified criterion28.

According to Davis, Perceived usefulness reflects the extent to which a person believes that a system may contribute to improving performance29. In the context of this study, performance translates to shorter waiting times for consultation. It is pertinent to mention that perceived usefulness is a subjective evaluation of performance, even if the system practically improves the outcome, user perception of its benefits is crucial for using the said system30. Our results indicate that the respondents may perceive the benefits of making a partial up-front payment that was associated with a higher intention to make the said payment.

Studies which ascertained the relationship between perceived usefulness and behavioural intention are abundant. It is well recognized that the perceived usefulness of computers has been considered to have a positive association with system usage31–33. Albeit not closely related, perceived usefulness along with other factors predicted consumer intention to make digital payments34–36.

Among the other factors associated with the intention to make an up-front payment is hospital trust which refers to the confidence, faith, or belief that individuals have in a particular hospital or healthcare institution. Hospital trust could be considered as a cumulative construct that is influenced by various factors such as the hospital’s reputation, past experiences, quality of care, and the perceived competence of the healthcare professionals37. Results of this study showed that the respondents have a high level of trust reflecting better satisfaction and positive experience with the health facility. As such respondents with higher trust expressed higher intention to make an up-front partial payment. Given that trust was also found as a significant predictor, further studies are required to ascertain the causal pathway between trust and perceived usefulness.

It was expected that education level would be favourably associated with the intention to pay. Previous research indicated that a higher level of education was associated with a greater intention to make payments38–40. The findings that a higher education level is associated with a lower intention could be surprising. It might be explained by the fact that higher education is surrogate to better financial stability41, in which the respondent may assume the ability to pay in full rather than making a partial payment.

Moreover, higher education may reflect financial maturity and planning which may deter the respondent to sacrifice the up-front payment in case of an emergency no-show. The other factor that may have contributed to the above relationship is the tendency to avoid financial risk among respondents who attained higher education.

This research demonstrated that the frequency of past visits has a substantial effect on the intention to make a partial up-front payment. This finding may be attributed to two factors. First, recurrent visits may be associated with longer waiting times thus it affects the patient’s intention to make an up-front payment. The simple linear regression analysis showed that patients who waited more than 3 h had a higher intention to make an up-front payment. Secondly, frequent visits to the hospital may reflect loyalty and trust in the hospital. Trust in the hospital, as mentioned earlier, has a positive influence on patients’ intention to make an up-front payment42.

We expected that the payment method would impact patients’ intention to make an up-front payment. It is worth noting that the presence of an insurance plan may affect the above relationship, participants whose medical expenses are covered by an insurance company may feel more secure regarding financial matters and thus accept an up-front payment.

It is pertinent to mention that none of the sociodemographic characteristics was associated with the patient’s intention. This comes along with our expectations that gender, race, educational level, and age would not be important predictors of intention to make up front payment. Being the first study of its kind, we find it difficult to compare our results to other studies. Nonetheless, studies have shown an association between sociodemographic characteristics and intention to engage in health care43, to receive SMS for health information44, and screening intention45. We expected that the payment method would affect patient intention, but it was not evident in our study. Patients who are covered with medical insurance or copayment would have a higher intention to make payments.

Nonetheless, studies concerning the impact of payment methods on healthcare utilization have shown that hospitalization was increased with increased patient cost-sharing for the elderly due to delays in seeking medical attention which results in increased hospitalization46, losing health insurance was associated with lower emergency department visits47.

A few limitations have been recognized that might affect the interpretation. First, the data comes from a single-centre study which limits the generality of the results. Secondly, patients’ intention is a hypothetical model and hasn’t been experienced in local settings; the results might be overestimated as no commitment is made with the responses.

Conclusion

Intention to make a partial up-front payment is associated with higher perceived usefulness in making such payment and hospital trust. Visiting the health facility frequently is associated with a higher intention to make upfront partial payment. It is hoped that the results contribute to a better understanding of an innovative business model to recognize the adoption of a partial up-front payment for outpatient departments to lower the overall no-show rate.
