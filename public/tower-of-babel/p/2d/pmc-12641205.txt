Organ‐Specific Epidermal Bladder Cell Contribution to Quinoa's Performance
Authors: Jon Miranda‐Apodaca, Aitor Agirresarobe, Alberto Muñoz‐Rueda, Usue Pérez‐López
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQuinoa is a facultative halophyte capable of thriving in harsh environmental conditions. Its epidermal bladder cells (EBCs) have been suggested to play a key role in salinity tolerance. To clarify their importance, several experiments have been conducted to assess the effects of EBC removal. However, existing studies have yielded conflicting evidence, both supporting and rejecting their significance. Notably, most of these investigations have focused on leaf EBCs, despite the fact that quinoa accumulates more ions in the stem than in the leaves. To address this gap, we designed a manipulative experiment to remove EBCs from the leaves and stems. Our results demonstrate that stem EBCs is crucial under both saline and non‐saline conditions. Their removal led to reduced growth and transpiration in non‐saline environments and decreased shoot biomass and Na+ accumulation in the shoot under saline conditions, while the removal of leaf EBCs did not alter the growth under either non‐saline or saline conditions. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that stem EBCs play a role in ion homeostasis and water movement.Keywords: epidermal bladder cells, halophyte, quinoa, salinity tolerance, stem

Introduction

At present, nearly 20% of the cultivated and half of the irrigated lands are affected by salinity, being a major constraint in crop production worldwide (Tuteja et al. 2012). It has been estimated that soil salinization will increase due to climate change and anthropic interventions like the irrigation with brackish water or the overuse of fertilizers (Hassani et al. 2021). In these conditions world food security will be threatened (FAO 2024). Consequently, two important aspects to safeguard it have been identified: on one hand, crop diversification by introducing naturally salt‐tolerant crops and, on the other hand, the identification of key mechanisms in these tolerant plant species to improve the salt tolerance of non‐adapted crops (Jacobsen 2003; Flowers 2004; Afzal et al. 2022; Slatni et al. 2024).

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is a facultative halophyte plant native to the Andean region of South America which produces highly nutritious grain (Hinojosa et al. 2018). It is known for its tolerance to harsh growing conditions, including high salinity (Miranda‐Apodaca, Yoldi‐Achalandabaso, et al. 2018). Different tolerance mechanisms have been described in quinoa, including efficient control of xylem loading and sequestration of Na+ in leaf cell vacuoles and apoplast, K+ retention and control over stomatal conductance and density (Adolf et al. 2013; Rasouli et al. 2021). Epidermal bladder cells (EBCs) are probably the most remarkable anatomical feature of halophytes (Flowers and Colmer 2008) allowing them to excrete Na+ and Cl− (Munns and Tester 2008). They are found in about 50% of halophytic plants, including quinoa, and cover the aerial surface of the plant, being particularly pronounced on the surfaces of young leaves (Zou et al. 2017; Moog et al. 2022). Salt bladders are modified balloon‐like trichomes that in quinoa are composed of an epidermal cell, one to two stalk cells, and an epidermal bladder cell—enlarged vacuolated cell—that appeared even in non‐saline conditions (Barkla et al. 2018; Otterbach et al. 2021). These spherical leaf‐external structures are 200 to 1000 times larger in volume than common leaf cells, representing about 25% of the total aerial volume, and have been shown to play a key role in helping plants tolerate salinity stress by providing a higher Na+ sequestration capacity (Agarie et al. 2007; Kiani‐Pouya et al. 2017; Böhm et al. 2018). In fact, Kiani‐Pouya et al. (2017) designed an EBC removal approach experiment where the removal of EBCs from quinoa plants resulted in a salt‐sensitive phenotype. Other functions postulated for EBCs include storage of water and various other metabolic compounds (Shabala and Mackay 2011; Wang et al. 2025).

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the number of studies carried out in quinoa to gain more insights on the role of the EBCs. In fact, these studies have questioned the postulated role of EBCs in the salinity tolerance of quinoa (Jaramillo Roman et al. 2020; Otterbach et al. 2021; Moog et al. 2022, 2023; Palacios et al. 2024). The results put in doubt that EBCs have a notable contribution to salinity tolerance, as low amounts of Na+ were detected in the EBCs relative to the total amount. Besides, Moog et al. (2022) observed that mutant plants of quinoa lacking EBCs did not lose salinity tolerance and Jaramillo Roman et al. (2020) calculated that EBCs represent about 1.3% of the fresh weight of leaves, which may not provide a great capacity to store large amounts of Na+. Furthermore, although cation transporter genes were highly expressed in EBCs of quinoa, the expression was not affected by salinity (Böhm et al. 2018). A recent work (Moog et al. 2023) focused on proposing EBCs as a deterrent to a broad range of generalist arthropod herbivores through their combined function of forming both a chemical and physical barrier.

Until now, there is evidence both supporting and rejecting the contribution of EBCs to salinity tolerance; however, we would like to highlight that almost all the studies have been done in leaf EBCs. Nevertheless, we know that in quinoa Na+ accumulation is bigger in the stems than in the leaves (Estrada et al. 2021; Agirresarobe et al. 2022; Palacios et al. 2024); despite this, there is no information about the specific characterization of the EBCs from the stem and their contribution to the postulated role in salinity tolerance of quinoa (Isobe et al. 2024).

In short, since the role of EBCs in the salinity tolerance of quinoa is being discussed, we performed a mechanical EBC‐removing approach treatment under high salt stress conditions to provide more information about the role of EBCs as a mechanism supporting salinity tolerance in quinoa. Besides, given that EBCs are also present in the stems and, taking into consideration that more Na+ is accumulated in this organ than in the leaves, we wanted to characterize the morphological and functional characteristics of the EBCs from the stems and investigate their specific contribution to quinoa salinity tolerance. In addition, since the EBCs are present even in non‐saline conditions in the leaves and stems, we wanted to explore the role of EBCs in quinoa in the absence of salinity stress.

Material and Methods

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

A Spanish cultivar of Chenopodium quinoa Willd. (cv. Marisma) was used in this study. Seeds of this cultivar were sown in a 3:1 mixture of perlite/vermiculite in 3‐L pots with one seed per pot. Plants were grown from sowing until the end of the experiment in a Conviron PGR15 controlled‐environment growth chamber under a daily 14 h light regimen, an average day/night temperature of 24/17°C, and a relative day/night humidity of 60/80%. During the light period, the photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was 400 μmol m−2 s−1. Light was provided by a combination of incandescent bulbs (Osram 70 W Halogen Eco) and warm‐white fluorescent lamps (Philips Master TL‐D 70 W 848). To minimize the effects of intrachamber environmental gradients, pots were carefully and randomly repositioned within the chamber three times per week.

Plants were watered with Hoagland's solution three times per week until they were 21 days old. From this day on, plants were divided into three groups: (1) in the first group, EBCs from the leaves were brushed with gloved fingers (Leaf‐Br plants), (2) in the second group, EBCs from stems and petioles were brushed with a cosmetic brush (Stem‐Br plants), and (3) in the third group, EBCs were maintained intact (Non‐Br plants). Removing the EBCs using this method allowed us to ensure that there was no physical damage to the organs brushed (Wang et al. 2025). Besides, each group was then subdivided into two. In a subgroup, the plants were grown under zero‐saline conditions (plants irrigated with Hoagland's solution), and in the other subgroup high‐saline conditions were imposed (plants irrigated with Hoagland's solution supplemented with 500 mM NaCl). These treatments lasted for 3 weeks (21 days). In the first week, salinity was gradually imposed in three rounds of irrigation: 125 mM NaCl on the first day, 250 mM NaCl on the third day and 500 mM NaCl on the fifth day. During the treatments, as the plants grew, EBCs from new leaves and elongated stem parts were brushed three times per week. We did not remove EBCs from the shoot apices and inflorescences in order to avoid abnormal growth. With the combination of EBC‐removing treatment and salt treatment, we have plants growing in six different conditions. Each experimental condition had five different pots, each representing one biological replicate (n = 5).

EBC Patterning Analysis

Images of EBCs from leaf and stem surfaces were obtained. Two young fully developed leaves from each pot were used. As EBCs can be easily removed when plants are manipulated, the leaves were cut carefully from the plant and were hold with a tweezer. In order to obtain EBC images from both adaxial and abaxial surfaces, a cut along the main nerve was made, obtaining two leaf pieces, one for the analysis of each leaf side. From the central part of each leaf side, carefully and maintaining the EBC intact, two leaf discs from each leaf side of each leaf were cut in order to have a flat surface and, therefore, an image focused on the EBC. At the same time, from the main stem of each plant at the height of the youngest fully developed leaf, the stem was carefully cut perpendicularly with a scalpel and held with tweezers and one slide was taken. The slide was then placed onto a glass microscope slide. Leaf and stem samples were observed using a Nikon SM645 Stereo Microscope 0.8X−5X with a zoom range of 2X. Images were captured with an Optikam Microscopy Digital USB Camera 4083.B3 (OPTIKA Microscopes) and Optika View 7.1 program (OPTIKA Microscopes). The images were analysed using the graphical tool LabelGlandula (Casado‐García et al. 2022). This tool defines EBC density and EBC surface area considering the EBCs as spheres. The percentage of EBC on a given surface was calculated as the EBC surface area multiplied by the EBC density (EBC coverage).

Mineral Analysis

For mineral analysis, finely grounded material samples from leaves, stems and roots were used. For K+, Na+ and Ca2+ analysis, grounded plant material (0.5 g DW) was dissolved in 10 mL nitric acid (1%). These cations were determined with inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‐AES, Horiba Jobin Yvon Activa). Ion concentrations were expressed as mg of the respective ion divided by the weighed dry grounded plant material used for the quantification. The total content was calculated by multiplying the mineral concentration by each organ biomass and the partitioning was expressed as a percentage of each organ with respect to the total content. Na+, K+ and Ca2+ uptake rates were measured as Pérez‐López et al. (2014).

Measurements of Plants Water Parameters

To measure leaf relative water content, a leaf disc was weighed to calculate its fresh weight, then placed in a petri dish containing distilled water and stored at 4°C for 24 h. The leaf disc was weighed again to calculate the turgid weight and dried in the oven at 80°C for 48 h to determine the dry weight. Relative water content (RWC) was determined using the equation RWC = 100·[(FW−DW)/(TW−DW)], where FW, DW, and TW are fresh, dry, and turgid weights of the sampled leaf, respectively. Midday leaf water potential was measured using the Scholander pressure‐equilibration technique (Scholander et al. 1965). This leaf was also used to measure leaf osmotic potential, once the leaf segments placed in Eppendorf vials were frozen in liquid nitrogen. The stem samples to measure the osmotic potential were collected from the main stem at the height of the leaves collected for the aforementioned measurements. Samples were thawed and sap was extracted by microcentrifugation for 5 min. The leaf and stem osmotic potential were measured through the freezing point of the cellular sap by an osmometer (Osmomat 030, Gonotec). To obtain the full turgor osmotic potential, 3 leaf discs were placed in darkness for 24 h in petri dishes that contained water to allow complete rehydration. Dehydration was calculated as the difference between leaf full turgor osmotic potential and leaf osmotic potential for each treatment. Osmotic adjustment was calculated as the difference in the full turgor osmotic potential between salt‐stressed and non‐saline plants within each removal treatment (Pérez‐López et al. 2009).

Growth Parameters

Plants were separated into leaves, stems, and roots and were weighed to determine FW. Leaf area was determined using Fiji from ImageJ2 (Schindelin et al. 2012) with a specific plugin to detect the area of the leaves. The plant samples were then dried at 80°C for 48 h and then the leaf, stem and root DWs, and total plant DW were determined. The succulence of leaves was measured as Agirresarobe et al. (2022) by dividing the fresh weight of all the leaves by the total leaf area.

Stomatal Characteristics

Following the same procedure as for EBC patterning, we calculate stomatal characteristics such as area and density. Leaf imprints were obtained from both adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces as described by Casado‐García et al. (2020). Immediately after the application of cyanoacrylate glue (Loctite Superglue‐3, Loctite Corp.) on the leaf, it was gently pushed onto a glass microscope slide for a few seconds. After that, the leaf was removed, obtaining the final impression on the glass slide. The prints were stored in darkness to prevent degradation. Epidermal prints were observed and images acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE 50i fluorescence microscope with a Leica DFC 420C camera. Four images were taken at ×10 magnifications from each epidermal print, using the LAS V3.7 program (Leica Microsystems). For that, each epidermal print was divided into four squares. The objective of taking a photo for each square was to analyse the variability in the stomatal traits that could be in the sample itself and saline treatment. Stomatal density was measured from the images using LabelStoma (Casado‐García et al. 2020).

Water and Gas Exchange Related Parameters

The cumulative plant water transpiration (water use) during the salt stress period was calculated by the gravimetric method. Each pot was weighed every 2 days at the same time, before and after watering (Miranda‐Apodaca, Pérez‐López, et al. 2018). The whole plant water use efficiency (WUE) was calculated as the estimated dry weight accumulation per plant during the salt stress period divided by the cumulative plant water transpiration (Martínez‐Goñi et al. 2023). Stomatal conductance was measured using a SC‐1 Leaf Porometer (Decagon Devices Inc.) and was expressed as mmol H2O m−2 s−1.

Statistical Analysis

Results are reported as the mean ± standard error of five biological replicates. Data analysis was performed using R (R Core Team 2019). Two‐way analysis of variance was used to evaluate the salt and EBC‐removing interaction. Means were compared by Duncan's multiple range test. P‐values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Before mean comparisons, homogeneity of variances by Levene's test and normally distributed residuals by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were tested.

Results

EBC Patterning

EBCs were observed in stems and leaves of quinoa plants under non‐saline and saline growing conditions. The density, volume and coverage of the EBCs on leaves and stems were analysed separately in order to find differences related to their organ of origin (Figure 1). For the characterization of EBC traits, we pooled the data of Non‐Br and Stem‐Br (for leaf adaxial and abaxial surface) and the data of Non‐Br and Leaf‐Br (for stem surface) treatments, of non‐saline or saline plants. There were two reasons for doing so. First, in the case of Leaf‐Br, there were no data for leaf EBC traits and in the case of Stem‐Br, there were no data for stem EBC traits, so we pooled them to avoid missing bars and for better visualization. Second, the statistical analysis showed that EBC traits of stems were not affected when EBCs were removed from leaves and that EBC traits of leaves were not affected when EBCs were removed from stems. The EBC density tended to be different depending on the plant surface (Figure 1A). Thus, the abaxial leaf surface showed a higher EBC density than the stem surface and the adaxial leaf surface, which, in turn, showed a similar EBC density. Besides, the EBC density increased with salinity by 64% in the adaxial surface of the leaves and by 42% on the abaxial leaf surface, but not in the stem. The greatest EBC volume under non‐saline and saline conditions was obtained in stems (Figure 1B). Besides, salinity only significantly affected the EBCs volume of the stem (+44%); no change in EBCs volume was observed in both leaf surfaces upon salinity.

The EBC coverage varied among plant surfaces, occupying the greatest portion of surface in the cases of stem and abaxial surface, around 20% (Figure 1C). However, this similar EBC coverage was due to a higher number of EBC of smaller size in the abaxial surface, but a lower number of EBC of higher size in the stem surface. This difference was increased under salinity stress, where the coverage was increased in these two surfaces, in the case of the leaf abaxial surface due to an increase in EBC density, and in the case of the stem surface due to an increase in EBC size.

Ion Homeostasis

Under non‐saline conditions, differences among organs were detected in the concentration pattern of the mineral analysed (Table 1). Thus, [Ca2+] in stems was 31% lower than in leaves, while [K+] in the stem was 69% higher than in the leaf. Higher [Na+] was registered in the roots with respect to leaves and stems, while lower [K+] and [Ca2+] were recorded. The Na+ content was distributed mainly to stems (39.7%) and then to the leaves and roots around 30% (Table 3). In the case of K+ and Ca2+, the distribution pattern was also uneven, being higher in the aerial part than in the roots.

The salinity stress increased the sodium uptake rate and the potassium uptake rate, while it decreased the calcium uptake rate by 60%. As expected, an increase of [Na+] was observed, especially in the aerial parts. The total Na+ content per plant was 216.82 mg, nearly 20 times that under non‐saline conditions, allocated to leaf, stem and root at 41.0%, 50.7% and 8.3%, respectively. The [K+] increased in the leaves when the salt stress was imposed, while the concentration in the stems and roots diminished. Likewise, salinity diminished the K+ content per plant by 16% and a change in the distribution pattern was detected; the allocation to leaves increased from 38% to 58% at the expense of the stems, where it was reduced from 55% to 39% (Table 3). The [Ca2+] decreased in the leaves and stems by 60% and 70%, respectively. This decrease in the concentration was accompanied by a diminution of the Ca2+ content.

Under these saline conditions, the only EBC treatment that caused an alteration in the mineral homeostasis was the removal of stem EBCs, where the increase detected in Na uptake rate by the salinity was lower in Stem‐Br plants than in the Non‐Br and Leaf‐Br. Consequently, the [Na+] in leaves and stem was lower than in the other EBC treatments, while [K+] and [Ca2+] suffered no changes.

Water Status Parameters

Neither RWC nor water potential was affected by the EBC removing treatment (Figure 2). On average, RWC decreased by 25% under saline conditions (Figure 2A). Water potential also decreased with salinity from −0.56 MPa to −2.8 MPa on average (Figure 2B). Salinity reduced leaf and stem osmotic potentials significantly (Figure 2C,D), and these parameters were not modified by the removal of leaf EBC, while leaf and stem osmotic potentials were less negative in plants deprived of stem EBC. Dehydration was significantly lower in Stem‐Br plants than in intact and Leaf‐Br plants (Figure 2E), while no differences were detected in the osmotic adjustment among EBC treatments (Figure 2F).

Growth

EBC treatment and salinity affected plant growth (Figure 3). Under non‐saline conditions and compared to intact plants, the Leaf‐Br treatment did not alter plant biomass (average value was 12.20 g DW plant−1); however, Stem‐Br caused a decrease in the plant biomass of each organ by ~15%. Salt stress decreased the biomass of each organ, by around 40% for roots and 20% for stems and leaves. Under saline conditions, the EBC treatment altered the plant biomass. Leaf‐Br only changed leaf biomass, where it increased by 10%, although this increase was not significant. Stem‐Br decreased the leaf and stem biomass by 15% compared to Leaf‐Br. Salinity affected both leaf area and succulence (leaf fresh weight divided by leaf area), although these parameters were not affected by EBC treatment (Figure 3E,F). Leaf area decreased on average by 67% under salinity and succulence increased on average by 19%.

Water and Gas Exchange‐Related Parameters

Under non‐saline conditions, lower cumulative transpiration rates were obtained when stem EBCs were removed (Figure 4A), although the EBC removal did not affect total leaf area (Figure 3E). Salinity decreased significantly the cumulative transpiration, but it was not affected by EBC treatment (Figure 4A). WUE increased significantly with salinity by 65% on average and it was not affected by EBC treatment (Figure 4B).

Stomatal density was similar on both leaf sides (Figure 4C). Taking together the stomatal densities from both leaf sides, the stomatal density was not affected significantly by the EBC‐removing treatment or salinity. However, salinity stress did result in a significant reduction (80%) of the stomatal conductance but no significant effect of the EBC‐removing treatment was observed for this parameter (Figure 4D).

Discussion

Until recently, EBCs were supposed to mainly be a salt tolerance protection mechanism (Adolf et al. 2013; Kiani‐Pouya et al. 2017). Lately, new pieces of evidence have arisen that indicate new roles for the EBCs against abiotic or biotic stresses in quinoa. Adolf et al. (2013) also mentioned that EBCs could reduce transpiration by forming a secondary epidermis and by functioning as water reservoirs. Oh et al. (2015) suggested that EBCs could provide a UV protection mechanism and Moog et al. (2023) postulated an herbivore defense mechanism through a physical barrier limiting insect movement through the plant material and a chemical barrier through the accumulation of oxalic acid and saponins. However, none of the previous works addressed the implication of EBC under non‐stress conditions, nor the comparative response of the contribution of EBC between leaves and stems.

EBCs, Possible Regulators of Transpiration and Hydraulic Conductance Under Non‐Saline Conditions?

Regarding non‐saline conditions, the removal of leaf EBCs did not affect the biomass or any other variable studied related to ion homeostasis and water relations parameters. However, the removal of stem EBCs generated some changes. The removal of the stem EBCs caused a decrease in transpiration (Figure 4A), so the potential role as secondary epidermis to avoid water loss (Wang et al. 2025) could be discarded, as seen by Kobayashi and Fujita (2024) in detached leaves of quinoa. Moreover, the decrease in transpiration happened with no changes in leaf area (Figure 3E) or in stomatal conductance (Figure 4D). This could indicate that the reduction in transpiration could be related to a lower hydraulic conductance in the stem, pointing to a function of EBCs related to water movement, maybe through ionic exchange and signalling. The metabolic and lipid profile of EBCs has been characterised and more than 300 known metabolites have been detected in EBCs (Otterbach et al. 2021), together with different ions such as Na+ and K+ (Moog et al. 2022; Palacios et al. 2024). Thus, it is clear that EBCs are metabolically active structures; therefore, they could be functioning in the regulation of water movement and its signalling. Yoldi‐Achalandabaso et al. (2024) proposed a strong correlation between aquaporin activities and hydraulic conductance and it has been postulated that aquaporin gating is regulated by ion signalling (Törnroth‐Horsefield et al. 2006; Chaumont and Tyerman 2014). Jou et al. (2007) proposed that the raphide crystals of EBCs are Ca2+ and K+ reservoirs regulating the Ca2+ and K+ homeostasis, and this role would be suppressed in brushed plants. Therefore, the lower capacity of retaining and compartmentalizing ions like K+ and Ca2+ in EBCs could have caused the lower hydraulic conductance. Nevertheless, a specific study should be made to disentangle the causes of the reduction of hydraulic conductance when EBCs are removed from the stem.

This reduction in transpiration could be the reason behind the plant biomass decrease observed in Stem‐Br. This highlights the importance of stem EBC over leaf EBC in quinoa in non‐saline conditions. This higher importance of stem EBC than leaf EBC could lie in the fact that the EBC's volume is higher in stems than in leaves, so the capacity of acting as a reservoir of either solutes or water and contributing to hydraulic conductance would be higher.

Confirming Quinoa's Salt‐Tolerant Mechanisms

Quinoa is a facultative halophyte plant that is able to grow and thrive in either salty or non‐salty environments (Shabala et al. 2012). As expected, the sodium contents in the plants were low under zero‐saline conditions, whereas under saline conditions, plants followed a Na+ inclusion mechanism. This strategy is typical of halophytes (Estrada et al. 2021). Furthermore, Na+ accumulated less in the root than in the shoot (Table 1). This result reinforces quinoa as a species that translocates almost all these toxic ions to the shoots; in fact, more than 90% of Na+ taken up is translocated to the shoot (Table 3), hence decreasing the osmotic potential in shoot cells. This also contributed to reducing the plant water potential below that of the soil, favouring water intake and thus reducing the osmotic impact of salt in the rhizosphere.

If we analyse the effect of removing the EBCs from the leaves, there were no significant differences compared to Non‐Br plants in all the parameters measured. Consequently, our results suggest a weak contribution of the EBCs from leaves in salinity tolerance of quinoa, as their removal did not modify their response to salinity. Orsini et al. (2011) observed that excretion through sequestration into EBCs was modest in quinoa compared with other halophytes. In agreement with our results, different studies on varieties other than studied here have recently determined that EBCs contain low concentrations of Na+, indicating that EBCs contribute moderately to the salinity tolerance of quinoa (Jaramillo Roman et al. 2020; Otterbach et al. 2021; Moog et al. 2022). However, Kiani‐Pouya et al. (2019), in a large‐scale screening with different varieties, found that high leaf EBC density confers salt tolerance and that the removal of leaf EBC caused a decrease in biomass, so the leaf EBC role in salinity tolerance could be variety specific.

Do Stem EBCs Matter in Quinoa?

Analysing the shoot, we detected that quinoa plants accumulate more Na+ in the stems than in the leaves (Table 2), as other studies have also seen (Agirresarobe et al. 2022; Palacios et al. 2024), and also present EBCs in stems (Ruiz et al. 2016). This result could suggest an important contribution of the stem to quinoa salinity tolerance. In addition, the sequestration of Na+ and Cl− in stems, far away from photosynthetically active tissues, should be an advantage to diminish the negative effects of salinity. Therefore, we wanted to examine whether there was a differential role between EBCs in the leaves and in the stems. As far as we know, this comparative study between organ EBCs has not been done in any halophyte species before. EBCs from both organs increased the coverage under salt stress; however, the reasons behind these increases were opposite between the two organs. In the leaf EBC, the increase was through a higher EBC density, keeping the volume of each EBC constant. However, the increase in stem EBC coverage was achieved by an increase in EBC volume, while the EBC density was the same as under non‐saline conditions. This leads us to think that stem EBCs are of even higher importance than those of the leaf in quinoa, because they give more volume to accumulate Na+ and other ions than leaf EBCs. Kiani‐Pouya et al. (2019) observed a positive correlation between leaf EBC diameter and salinity tolerance, underlying the importance of the EBC patterning parameters, like EBC size, to understand salt tolerance mechanisms. This correlation tested in leaves should also be tested in stems in future research, given the higher volume accumulating Na+ and other ions in stem EBCs.

To deepen the understanding of the contribution of stem EBCs to salt tolerance in quinoa, we removed the EBCs from the stem (Stem‐Br plants). Then, we detected a reduction in shoot biomass. Analyzing the possible causes of this decrease, we also spotted a diminution of Na+ contents in the shoot, both in leaves and stem. Plants that face salt stress carry out osmotic adjustment to deal with the effects of salinity. In this respect, it is known that, in quinoa, osmotic adjustment is achieved mainly via the accumulation of inorganic ions like Na+, K+ and Cl− resulting in an energy‐saving strategy (Adolf et al. 2013). Therefore, a lower ion concentration could hamper the ability of the plant to perform osmotic adjustment, as we saw a less negative osmotic potential in the Stem‐Br treatment, and ultimately affect growth.

Regarding the reasons behind the reduction of Na+ concentration in Stem‐Br plants compared to Leaf‐Br plants, (1) we discard contact cleaning as the cause of this decrease, as EBCs were removed continuously as soon as they appeared so there was no time for accumulating significant Na+ contents; furthermore, the other ions analysed (like K+ and Ca2+) did not decrease their concentration. Besides, EBCs were not regenerated on already brushed surfaces. (2) We also discard a lower cumulative transpiration since in both EBC removing treatments (Leaf‐Br and Stem‐Br) the cumulative transpiration was similar. In the case of non‐saline conditions, we observed a reduction in cumulative transpiration for Stem‐Br due to a lower hydraulic conductance; however, under salt stress, the massive decrease in stomatal conductance (Figure 4D) could have masked the effect of stem EBC in cumulative transpiration. Therefore, to understand the lower Na+ concentration in Stem‐Br plants compared to Leaf‐Br plants, we need to unravel the mechanisms involved.

Various authors postulated that EBC could act as a reservoir of water and K+ and other ions and release them to other tissues on demand (Agarie et al. 2007; Moog et al. 2022). In quinoa, the activities of abundant ion transporters have been reported. (Jaramillo Roman et al. 2020) and Bazihizina et al. (2022) found strong evidence that the stalk cell of the EBCs is metabolically very active. Therefore, a role of EBCs in ion homeostasis regulation should be considered. Furthermore, Palacios et al. (2024) demonstrated that the medullary parenchyma of the stem accumulates excess sodium. This, together with our observed decrease in Na+ in stems of Stem‐Br, could evidence a role of EBC in the regulation of Na+ uptake. Therefore, the removal of EBCs could explain the reduction of Na+ uptake rate through an imbalance in ion homeostasis. This ion imbalance could be the cause of the reduction of the osmotic potentials, resulting in a small decrease in the shoot biomass in the Stem‐Br compared to Leaf‐Br or Non‐Br. In addition, Kiani‐Pouya et al. (2022) revealed a critical role of EBCs in coordinating salt stress response of quinoa through hormone signalling and gene expression, so these implications should not be discarded. However, this postulated role of stem EBCs in ion homeostasis should be deepened to establish the mechanism.

Finally, the differences in plant biomass among EBC treatments were not enormous and, given that the effects observed in the literature showed high variability, the experimental design could play a role in detecting the importance of EBCs. In the majority of the studies performed to disentangle the role of EBCs, the salt concentrations used were around 400 and 500 mM, and the experiments lasted from 1 to 5 weeks (Kiani‐Pouya et al. 2017; Moog et al. 2022, 2023; Palacios et al. 2024). Taking this into account, we propose that in order to determine without doubts the role in salinity tolerance of the EBCs, both from leaves and stems, it would be necessary to, on one hand, increase the duration of the stress until grain production to allow quinoa plants to develop their full set of tools to adapt to salinity; and, on the other hand, carry out these experiments also in lower salt concentrations to avoid the EBC role being masked by the intense response to high salinity.

Conclusions

These results highlight the higher importance of stem EBC in non‐saline conditions and in salt conditions than leaf EBC. Stem EBCs in quinoa have a role in hydraulic conductance; when removed, a reduced growth was observed due to a decline in transpiration in non‐saline conditions. Stem EBCs also seem more important than leaf EBCs regarding ion accumulation capacity under salt stress as their removal caused a decrease in growth compared to non‐brushed plants or plants that have their leaf EBCs removed. Given the need to preserve food security under climatic future conditions where salinity is expected to impact the food production, Shabala et al. (2014) stated that once the molecular mechanisms of Na+ and Cl− loading in EBCs are elucidated, this information should be used in breeding programs to increase the salinity tolerance of traditional crops. With these results that highlight the higher importance of stem EBCs than the leaf EBCs, a focus on how stem EBCs function and their role should also be considered.
