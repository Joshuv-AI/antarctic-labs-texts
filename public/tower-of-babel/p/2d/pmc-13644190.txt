Evolocumab in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of efficacy and safety
Authors: Sha Li, Cuigai Zhang, Yanping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvolocumab, a proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitor, potently reduces low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. However, its effects on hard clinical endpoints and safety in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) require updated synthesis, particularly incorporating data from recent long-term extension studies and diverse subpopulations.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of evolocumab in patients with ASCVD.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, OVID, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (WANFANG MED ONLINE and CNKI), and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched from January 2014 to March 2026. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing evolocumab with placebo in patients with ASCVD were included. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). A random-effects model was used for meta-analysis, with subgroup analyses, meta-regression, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessment.ResultsEight RCTs comprising 40,658 patients were included. Regarding lipid parameters, evolocumab significantly reduced levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C; standardized mean difference [SMD]: -2.546, 95% confidence interval [CI]: -3.024 to -2.067, P < 0.001), total cholesterol (TC; SMD: –1.543, 95% CI: –1.947 to –1.139, P < 0.001), apolipoprotein B (Apo B; SMD: –1.598, 95% CI: –2.169 to –1.027, P < 0.001), and lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a); SMD: –0.713, 95% CI: –0.938 to –0.489, P < 0.001) compared with placebo, but showed no significant effect on high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; SMD: 0.233, 95% CI: –0.058 to 0.524, P = 0.117) or triglycerides (TG; SMD: –0.315, 95% CI: –0.765 to 0.136, P = 0.171). For clinical endpoints, evolocumab significantly reduced the odds of MACE compared with placebo (OR: 0.416, 95% CI: 0.248–0.698, P < 0.001; I2 = 86.7%); however, the 95% prediction interval crossed unity (0.13–1.36), indicating uncertainty about the true effect in future studies. Myocardial infarction (OR: 0.711, 95% CI: 0.661–0.765, P < 0.001), stroke (OR: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.693–0.864, P < 0.001), and coronary revascularization (OR: 0.777, 95% CI: 0.734–0.821, P < 0.001) all showed significant odds reductions. Evolocumab significantly reduced odds of cardiovascular death (OR: 0.771, 95% CI: 0.657–0.904, P = 0.002) and all-cause mortality (OR: 0.852, 95% CI: 0.762–0.953, P = 0.004). Safety analysis indicated that injection site reactions were more common with evolocumab (OR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.12–1.59, P < 0.01), with no significant differences in serious adverse events, neurocognitive events, muscle-related events, or liver function abnormalities.ConclusionsIn the context of optimized background therapy, evolocumab significantly reduces the odds of MACE, myocardial infarction, and stroke, and improves multiple atherogenic lipid parameters in patients with ASCVD. Notably, significant reductions in both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality were also observed in this updated analysis. Introduction

Atheroscleroticcardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide [1], imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a key causal factor in atherosclerosis, and its reduction is linearly associated with decreased cardiovascular event risk [2]. Although statin-based lipid-lowering therapy has substantially improved outcomes in patients with ASCVD, a considerable residual cardiovascular risk persists [3].

The advent of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors has provided a new option for intensive lipid lowering [4]. Evolocumab, a fully human IgG2 monoclonal antibody, inhibits PCSK9 binding to the LDL receptor, thereby increasing hepatic LDL receptor availability and potently reducing circulating LDL-C levels [4, 5]. The landmark FOURIER trial demonstrated that evolocumab added to statin therapy further reduces the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [6]. Subsequent and subgroup analyses of the FOURIER trial have further elucidated its benefits in specific populations, including patients with diabetes [7], metabolic syndrome [8], recent myocardial infarction (MI) [9], polyvascular disease [10], and its effects on peripheral arterial events [11].

Several subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed these findings. For instance, the Cochrane systematic review by Schmidt et al. provided high-quality evidence that both evolocumab and alirocumab reduce cardiovascular event risk when added to existing lipid-lowering therapy [12]. A more recent meta-analysis focusing on stroke and cardiovascular events confirmed this preventive effect without an increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke [13]. Furthermore, a dedicated analysis of statin-treated patients further confirmed the potent lipid-lowering efficacy and favorable safety profile of evolocumab [14].

However, with the publication of long-term extension studies such as FOURIER-OLE [15]and additional randomized controlled trials (RCTs) targeting diverse populations and regions particularly Chinese populations (e.g.Hao [16]Zhang C [17]Wang C [18]Chen BZ [19], Yang F [20]), an updated synthesis of the available evidence is warranted. Key unresolved questions include: (1) whether the effect of evolocumab on hard endpoints, particularly cardiovascular death, evolves with extended follow-up; (2) whether its comprehensive impact on the atherogenic lipid profile aligns with clinical benefits; and (3) whether its safety profile remains favorable in broader populations. Therefore, we conducted this updated systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of evolocumab in patients with ASCVD, providing the most precise evidence for clinical practice.

Methods

Registration and reporting guidelines

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [21]. The completed PRISMA checklist has been submitted as a Supplementary materials S1. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261337509). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, OVID, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (WANFANG MED ONLINE and CNKI), and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched from January 2014 to March 2026. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms was used, including "Evolocumab," "Repatha," "AMG 145," "PCSK9 inhibitor," "Proprotein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin 9," "atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease," "ASCVD," and "randomized controlled trial." An example PubMed search strategy was as follows: (evolocumab OR Repatha OR "AMG 145") AND ("cardiovascular disease" OR "myocardial infarction" OR stroke OR atherosclerosis OR MACE OR "cardiovascular death") AND (randomized OR randomised OR trial OR randomly). Additionally, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually searched.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Two investigators (Sha Li and Cuigai Zhang) independently performed literature screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. Data were extracted using a predefined form, including study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, sample size, follow-up duration), patient baseline characteristics (age, sex, background therapy), numbers of outcome events, and changes in lipid parameters. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool [22]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third party.

Statistical analysis

For dichotomous variables (clinical endpoints and adverse events), odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous variables (lipid parameters), standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Given anticipated heterogeneity across studies in populations, intervention details, and follow-up durations, all pooled analyses were performed using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model [23].

Heterogeneity was quantified using the Q test (P < 0.10 considered significant) and the I2 statistic. I2values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [24]. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plot asymmetry and statistically using Egger’s linear regression test [25]. If publication bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied for correction [26].

Sensitivity analyses were performed using the leave-one-out method, and diagnostic tools such as Galbraith and L’Abbé plots were used to assess heterogeneity and robustness. For the primary outcome, predefined subgroup analyses (large-sample vs. small-sample studies) were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression was used to explore the relationship between follow-up duration and effect size. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) with the meta and metafor packages. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search results

The initial search yielded 4,487 records (PubMed: 548; Embase: 1,910; Cochrane Library: 437; OVID: 781; Wanfang Data: 188; CNKI: 42; ClinicalTrials.gov: 33). After deduplication, 2,779 records remained. Following screening of titles and abstracts, 2,116 records were excluded (including guidelines, meta-analyses, animal studies, reviews, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, and case reports). Of the remaining 663 records undergoing full-text assessment, 621 were excluded because they were not RCTs or data could not be retrieved. Subsequently, 42 records were assessed in detail, and 32 were excluded (8 for non-ASCVD populations, 5 for reporting only lipid parameters, 6 for follow-up < 2 months, 7 for not reporting cardiovascular outcomes, and 6 for comparing only different doses). Ultimately, 8 RCTs [6, 16–20, 27, 28] were included in the meta-analysis (Fig.1).Bonaca [11]was excluded as it represents a predefined subgroup analysis of the FOURIER trial population andMcClintick [15] was excluded due to its open-label extension design without a concurrent placebo control.

Baseline characteristics of included studies

The 8 included studies were published between 2017 and 2025, comprising three international multicenter RCTs [6, 27, 28]and five single-center RCTs conducted in China [16–20]. The total sample size was 40,658 patients, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 46 to 13,784. Follow-up duration ranged from 2 to 54 months. All patients were randomized to receive evolocumab or placebo on a background of statin therapy.

MACE definitions varied across the included studies (Table 1). Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool for the 8 included RCTs (Fig. 2). According to the domain-level judgments three studies (Sabatine 2017 ; Koskinas 2019 andBohula 2025 ) were rated as having low risk of bias across all five domains. The remaining five studies (Hao 2022 ; Zhang C 2023 ; Wang C 2024 ; Chen BZ 2025 and Yang F 2025) were judged as having high risk of bias overall. Specifically, these five studies showed high risk in the domain of 'deviations from intended interventions' due to lack of blinding, and four also showed high risk in the domain of 'measurement of the outcome.' Detailed domain-level judgments for each study are presented in Fig. 2.

Efficacy outcomes

Changes in lipid parameters

Compared with placebo, evolocumab was associated with significant improvements in multiple atherogenic lipid parameters (Fig. 3). Pooled analysis showed that evolocumab significantly reduced LDL-C levels (in mmol/L; SMD: −2.546, 95% CI: −3.024 to −2.067, P < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 92.4%). Evolocumab also significantly reduced TC (in mmol/L; SMD: −1.543, 95% CI: −1.947 to −1.139, P < 0.001), Apo B (in mg/dL; SMD: −1.598, 95% CI: −2.169 to −1.027, P < 0.001), and Lp(a) (in mg/dL; SMD: −0.713, 95% CI: −0.938 to −0.489, P < 0.001). The pooled result for Lp(a) showed very low heterogeneity (I2 = 6.1%). However, the effects of evolocumab on HDL-C (in mmol/L; SMD: 0.233, 95% CI: −0.058 to 0.524, P = 0.117) and TG (in mmol/L; SMD: −0.315, 95% CI: −0.765 to 0.136, P = 0.171) did not reach statistical significance.

Clinical efficacy endpoints

Major adverse cardiovascular events

The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which was reported by all 8 included studies. Random-effects meta-analysis showed that evolocumab significantly reduced the odds of MACE compared with placebo (OR: 0.416, 95% CI: 0.248–0.698, P < 0.001), albeit with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86.7%) (Fig. 4A). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.13 to 1.36, indicating that the true effect may vary across different settings.

Forest plot of MACE: (A) Overall analysis; (B) Subgroup analysis by study type

MACE definitions varied across the included studies. Despite this definitional heterogeneity, the direction of the treatment effect was consistent across all individual studies. We extracted events as reported by each original study and acknowledged this variation as a potential source of the observed statistical heterogeneity. To further explore this, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding two studies with markedly divergent definitions—Wang C 2024 (MACE defined as heart failure, myocardial infarction, or cardiovascular death, omitting stroke) and Yang F 2025 (MACE defined as angina, heart failure, or myocardial infarction, omitting both stroke and cardiovascular death)—which yielded similar results (OR: 0.457, 95% CI: 0.251–0.831, P = 0.010) with substantially reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 0.7%). This finding confirms that the observed statistical heterogeneity was largely driven by these two small single-center trials and supports the robustness of our primary findings.

Subgroup analysis by study type further revealed a marked difference in effect sizes: Chinese single-center RCTs (5 studies; OR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.19–0.38, I2 = 0.0%) reported substantially larger effects than international multicenter RCTs (3 studies; OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.29–0.74, I2 = 88.6%), with a significant subgroup difference (P for interaction < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). However, given the small sample sizes (n = 92–129), short follow-up durations (2–12 months), and lack of blinding in these single-center studies, this subgroup finding should be interpreted with caution. The observed effect size may be influenced by publication bias, selective reporting, or other methodological limitations inherent to small, single-center trials.

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analysis showed that all pooled effect estimates remained statistically significant (OR range: 0.327–0.432, all P < 0.05), indicating that no single study dominated the pooled result. The trim-and-fill method identified no missing studies, and the adjusted effect estimate (OR: 0.547, 95% CI: 0.372–0.805) was consistent with the original result, further supporting the robustness of our findings.

Myocardial infarction: sensitivity analyses and diagnostic plots

To further assess the robustness of the MI outcome, we performed a series of diagnostic analyses, including Galbraith plots, meta-regression bubble plots, influence (leave-one-out) plots, and L’Abbé plots (Fig. 5).

Diagnostic plots for myocardial infarction: (A) Galbraith plot; (B) Meta-regression bubble plot; (C) Influence (leave-one-out) analysis; (D) L’Abbé plot

The Galbraith plot (Fig. 5A) showed that all study points were distributed around the regression line, with no obvious outliers, indicating that the observed heterogeneity was primarily due to random variation rather than extreme outliers.

The meta-regression bubble plot (Fig. 5B) revealed a significant positive correlation between follow-up duration and log OR (slope = 0.0135, 95% CI: 0.002–0.025, P = 0.024), suggesting that the protective effect of evolocumab on MI may increase with longer follow-up. Bubble size represented study weight, with large-sample studies (e.g., FOURIER) dominating. This model explained 32.1% of the variance in effect sizes (R2 = 32.1%).The influence plot (Fig. 5C) showed the pooled effect estimates after sequentially omitting each study. All estimated ORs were below 1.0, and the 95% CIs did not cross 1.0 (dashed line), indicating that the pooled result was robust and not dominated by any single study. The solid line represents the original pooled OR, and the dashed line represents the OR = 1 reference line.

The L’Abbé plot (Fig. 5D) showed the relationship between event rates in the intervention and control groups across studies. Most study points fell below the diagonal line, indicating that event rates in the evolocumab group were generally lower than those in the control group, consistent with the pooled analysis.

Collectively, these diagnostic analyses demonstrated that the meta-analysis results for MI were robust, with no evidence of significant small-study effects or dominance by a single study. The association between follow-up duration and effect size further supports the potential for greater benefit with longer-term treatment.

Secondary hard endpoints

Evolocumab also significantly reduced the risk of MI (OR: 0.711, 95% CI: 0.661–0.765, P < 0.001; I2 = 0%), stroke (OR: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.693–0.864, P < 0.001; I2 = 0%), and coronary revascularization (OR: 0.777, 95% CI: 0.734–0.821, P < 0.001; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 6). Based on stroke events, the number needed to treat was 126 (based on a control event rate of 1.2%).

Mortality endpoints and unstable angina

Notably, evolocumab significantly reduced the risk of cardiovascular death (OR: 0.771, 95% CI: 0.657–0.904, P = 0.002; I2 = 0%) and all-cause death (OR: 0.852, 95% CI: 0.762–0.953, P = 0.004; I2 = 0%). For hospitalization for unstable angina, the pooled result showed a significant reduction (OR: 0.470, 95% CI: 0.222–0.996, P = 0.049), with no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 7).

Safety outcomes

In terms of safety (Table 3), injection site reactions were significantly more common in the evolocumab group (OR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.12–1.59, P < 0.01). However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in any serious adverse events (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.95–1.06), neurocognitive events (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.88–1.29), muscle-related events (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 0.94–1.17), or liver function abnormalities (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.83–1.17).

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for MACE did not reveal obvious asymmetry (Fig. 9A). However, Egger's linear regression test suggested the presence of small-study effects (t = −7.53, df = 6, P = 0.0003), indicating that smaller studies tended to report larger treatment effects. After trim-and-fill adjustment, the pooled effect estimate for MACE remained essentially unchanged (adjusted OR: 0.547, 95% CI: 0.372–0.805), further supporting the robustness of the results despite the presence of small-study effects (Fig. 9B). The observed small-study effects were consistent with our subgroup analysis, which showed substantially larger effect sizes in small Chinese single-center trials (OR: 0.27) compared with large international multicenter trials (OR: 0.47).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents an up-to-date and comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of evolocumab in patients with ASCVD, encompassing 8 RCTs with 40,658 patients. Our findings clearly delineate the benefit and risk profile of evolocumab. The main findings are consistent with large-scale meta-analyses such as the Cochrane systematic review by Schmidt et al., which reported that PCSK9 inhibitors reduce cardiovascular event risk by approximately 16% compared with placebo [12]. A recent meta-analysis focusing on stroke prevention also aligns with our results, showing that evolocumab and alirocumab significantly reduce stroke risk without increasing the risk of hemorrhagic stroke [13].

Efficacy and mechanistic insights

Our study confirms the potent lipid-lowering effects of evolocumab at the mechanistic level. The SMD for LDL-C reduction was –2.546, a magnitude far exceeding that of conventional statins and consistent with the potent PCSK9 inhibitory effect of the drug [5]. We also quantified its impact on other atherogenic lipids, including significant reductions in Apo B and Lp(a). As an emerging independent risk factor, Lp(a) is not affected by statin therapy [29]; therefore, this additional benefit of evolocumab has important clinical implications. Notably, the pooled result for Lp(a) showed very low heterogeneity (I2= 6.1%), indicating high consistency of this effect across studies. These pleiotropic lipid effects beyond LDL-C are important components of the overall cardiovascular benefit, as emphasized in recent reviews on PCSK9-targeted therapies [4, 5].

Regarding hard clinical endpoints, our study demonstrated that evolocumab was associated with a 58.4% relative reduction in the odds of MACE (OR: 0.416), a more pronounced benefit than reported in previous meta-analyses [12, 30], possibly due to the inclusion of more high-risk populations and longer follow-up studies. However, this estimate was associated with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86.7%) and a prediction interval that crossed unity (0.13–1.36), indicating uncertainty about the true effect size in future studies or different populations. The effect estimate appeared to be influenced by study type, with five small Chinese single-center trials reporting substantially larger effects (OR: 0.27) than the three large international multicenter trials (OR: 0.47). For individual endpoints, the protective effects were particularly robust: MI (OR: 0.711, 95% CI: 0.661–0.765), stroke (OR: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.693–0.864), and coronary revascularization (OR: 0.777, 95% CI: 0.734–0.821), corresponding to relative odds reductions of 28.9%, 22.6%, and 22.3%, respectively, all with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2= 0%). These results are consistent with the magnitude of LDL-C reduction and the theoretical expectation of plaque stabilization and regression, as demonstrated by imaging studies using intravascular ultrasound and optical coherence tomography [31–33].

Potential role of vascular biomarkers

Beyond these traditional lipid and clinical outcomes, recent studies have extended our understanding by examining the relationship between PCSK9 inhibitor therapy and vascular health. Previous studies have demonstrated that, after 6 months of add-on PCSK9 inhibitor therapy, changes in pulse wave velocity (PWV) (a well-established, non-invasive marker of arterial stiffness and vascular health) were significantly associated with reductions in LDL-C levels [34, 35]. Furthermore, a recent study showed that the dual reduction of LDL-C and lipoprotein(a) achieved with PCSK9 inhibitors was associated with significant improvements in PWV [36].

These findings suggest that PWV may serve as a simple, non-invasive instrumental biomarker for monitoring the vascular response to PCSK9 inhibitor therapy. Assessment of PWV could potentially help identify patients who may derive greater benefit from earlier initiation of evolocumab, providing additional clinical context to the cardiovascular benefits observed in our meta-analysis. However, further prospective studies are needed to validate the utility of PWV-guided treatment strategies in clinical practice.

Mortality benefit: evidence from the updated analysis

In contrast to some previous meta-analyses that did not detect a mortality benefit, our updated analysis revealed significant reductions in both cardiovascular death (OR: 0.771, 95% CI: 0.657–0.904, P = 0.002) and all-cause death (OR: 0.852, 95% CI: 0.762–0.953, P = 0.004). Previous meta-analyses may have been underpowered to detect mortality differences. Our updated analysis, which included longer follow-up data from the FOURIER trial (up to 26 months) and excluded open-label extensions to maintain methodological rigor, provides evidence for a mortality benefit of evolocumab in ASCVD patients.

Several factors may explain why our analysis detected a mortality benefit while others did not:

Follow-up duration: Mortality events typically require longer follow-up to manifest. Although individual studies such as FOURIER-OLE [15]provided longer-term follow-up data, this open-label extension was excluded from our primary analysis to maintain methodological rigor. The original FOURIER trial [6] provided 26 months of follow-up, which may have been sufficient to detect mortality differences, particularly given the large sample size (27,564 patients). Meta-regression analysis of our data showed that longer follow-up was associated with greater effect sizes (Fig. 5B), supporting the importance of adequate follow-up duration.

Exclusion of overlapping populations: By excluding subgroup analyses (Bonaca MP 2018 ) and open-label extensions (McClintick DJ 2024 ) that could introduce bias or dilute treatment effects, our analysis provides a cleaner estimate of the true intention-to-treat effect of evolocumab on mortality.

Event accumulation: In our pooled analysis, the control group had a cardiovascular mortality rate of approximately 2.1% and all-cause mortality rate of approximately 3.8%. The absolute risk reductions achieved with evolocumab (0.48% for cardiovascular death and 0.57% for all-cause death) translated to numbers needed to treat of 208 and 175, respectively. These modest absolute benefits may explain why earlier meta-analyses with smaller sample sizes or shorter follow-up failed to achieve statistical significance.

Consistency with the LDL-C hypothesis: The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' Collaboration meta-analysis demonstrated that each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C is associated with a 21% reduction in major vascular events and a 12% reduction in all-cause mortality [37]. The approximately 1.98 mmol/L (62%) LDL-C reduction achieved with evolocumab in our analysis is consistent with the mortality benefit observed.

Subgroup benefits

Post-hoc and subgroup analyses of the FOURIER trial provide a more granular clinical perspective. These results indicate that the relative benefit of evolocumab is consistent across subgroups, but the magnitude of absolute benefit varies according to baseline patient risk.

Diabetes and metabolic syndrome: Patients with diabetes or metabolic syndrome have significantly higher baseline cardiovascular event risk and therefore derive greater absolute risk reduction from evolocumab [7, 8]. Importantly, evolocumab did not increase the risk of new-onset diabetes or worsen glycemic control [7].

Recent myocardial infarction and polyvascular disease: In patients with recent MI (within 1 year), the absolute risk reduction with evolocumab was more pronounced (number needed to treat [NNT] = 27 for evolocumab) [9]. Similarly, patients with polyvascular disease (e.g., concomitant peripheral artery disease) have extremely high risk of cardiovascular and limb events and derive particularly substantial absolute benefit from evolocumab [10, 11].

Elderly and Asian populations: Analysis of elderly patients (≥ 75 years) showed that the relative benefit was comparable to that of younger patients, with greater absolute benefit (NNT = 19), and no new safety concerns were identified [38]. In Asian patients, evolocumab was similarly effective and safe, with even greater LDL-C reduction observed at equivalent doses [39].

Inflammatory status: The relative benefit of evolocumab was consistent regardless of baseline high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels, but patients with higher baseline hs-CRP showed more pronounced absolute risk reduction, suggesting that patients with higher inflammatory burden derive greater benefit from intensive lipid lowering [40].

These subgroup analyses provide important evidence for identifying patients most likely to benefit from evolocumab in clinical practice and support guideline recommendations for more aggressive lipid management in very-high-risk patients [41]. However, the observed difference in effect sizes between study types should be interpreted with caution, as discussed in the Limitations ("Limitations" section).

Safety profile

This study comprehensively confirmed the favorable safety profile of evolocumab. Aside from an increased incidence of injection site reactions (mostly mild and transient), no increased risks were observed for serious adverse events, neurocognitive events, muscle-related events, or liver function abnormalities. In particular, the analysis of neurocognitive events (OR: 1.07, 0.88–1.29), incorporating data from large studies such as FOURIER [6]and FOURIER-OLE [15], provides robust evidence to alleviate clinical concerns about this potential adverse effect. Our pooled estimates are consistent with the EBBINGHAUS study, which concluded that evolocumab has no clinically significant impact on cognitive function [42]. Further analysis of patient-reported cognitive function in the FOURIER study also supports this conclusion [43].

Comparison with other lipid-lowering agents

Although this analysis focuses on evolocumab, it is important to consider its role in the context of other non-statin agents. Ezetimibe modestly reduces LDL-C (by approximately 24%) and has been shown to provide cardiovascular benefit [44]; as an oral agent with low cost, it is a first-line option for combination therapy. Bempedoic acid, an oral ATP citrate lyase inhibitor, provides an additional 21% LDL-C reduction and has recently been shown to reduce cardiovascular events in statin-intolerant patients [45]. For patients with hypertriglyceridemia, icosapent ethyl has been shown to reduce cardiovascular risk in the REDUCE-IT trial [46]. Network meta-analyses suggest that although evolocumab and alirocumab provide the greatest LDL-C reduction, their efficacy is comparable to twice-yearly inclisiran [47]; however, long-term cardiovascular outcome data for inclisiran are awaited from ongoing trials [48].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the definition of MACE varied substantially across the eight included studies, ranging from 5-component to 3-component composite endpoints with variation in specific components (Table 1). This definitional heterogeneity likely contributed to the substantial statistical heterogeneity observed (I2 = 86.7%) and represents a major limitation of the pooled MACE estimate.

Second, despite extensive efforts to explore heterogeneity through subgroup analyses and meta-regression, substantial heterogeneity persisted for the primary outcome (I2 = 86.7%), with a 95% prediction interval crossing unity (0.13–1.36) ("Clinical efficacy endpoints" section). This heterogeneity was disproportionately driven by small single-center trials, which reported substantially larger effect sizes than the large international multicenter trials ("Clinical efficacy endpoints" section). These smaller studies had limited sample sizes, short follow-up, and lacked blinding—factors that may have introduced bias and inflated treatment effects. Consequently, the pooled MACE estimate warrants caution, particularly when extrapolating to broader populations.

Third, some secondary outcomes (e.g., unstable angina) and safety outcomes (e.g., neurocognitive events) were reported in only a limited number of studies, reducing statistical power for these endpoints.

Fourth, this is a study-level meta-analysis; individual patient data were unavailable, precluding deeper exploration of treatment effects in specific subgroups, such as by baseline LDL-C levels or individual comorbidities.

Fifth, we excluded open-label extension studies (e.g., FOURIER-OLE [15]) from the primary analysis to maintain methodological rigor, as these studies lack concurrent placebo control and blinding. While this decision enhances internal validity, it also means that efficacy data beyond the original trial follow-up periods were not incorporated—a trade-off between rigor and long-term data completeness.

Sixth, although trim-and-fill adjustment did not materially change the pooled estimate, Egger's linear regression test suggested the presence of small-study effects (t = −7.53, df = 6, P = 0.0003). This statistical finding aligns with the larger effect sizes observed in the small, lower-quality single-center trials (Second) and reinforces the need for caution when generalizing the MACE estimate.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that, when added to optimal background therapy, evolocumab significantly improves the atherogenic lipid profile and reduces the risk of MACE, MI, stroke, and coronary revascularization in patients with ASCVD. Significant reductions in cardiovascular and all-cause mortality were also observed in this updated analysis. The overall safety profile remains favorable. In high-risk subgroups, including patients with diabetes, recent MI, or polyvascular disease, the absolute benefits are more pronounced. However, the substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86.7%) and the prediction interval crossing unity (0.13–1.36) warrant caution in interpreting the pooled MACE estimate. The particularly large effect sizes observed in the small Chinese single-center trials highlight the need for larger, more rigorous studies to confirm these findings in diverse populations. Nonetheless, the consistent direction of effect across all studies and the robust sensitivity analyses support the overall benefit of evolocumab as an effective option for intensive lipid lowering in patients with ASCVD.
