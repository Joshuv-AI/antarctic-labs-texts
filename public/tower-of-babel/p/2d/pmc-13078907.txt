Do Not Anchor on Vasculitis: Pancreatic Cancer Presenting With Isolated Celiac/Superior Mesenteric Artery Thickening
Authors: Maryam Own, Matthew J Koster, Kenneth J Warrington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMedium‐vessel vasculitis involving the visceral vasculature in adults is rare. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has been implicated in medium artery vascular thickening secondary to extravascular migratory metastasis with involvement of the celiac and superior mesenteric vessels.MethodsPatients presenting to the rheumatology vasculitis subspecialty clinic were retrospectively identified using an electronic health record data exploration tool evaluating for diagnosis of pancreatic cancer, periaortitis, and vasculitis between January 2015 and December 2024.ResultsWe retrospectively identified four patients with PDAC initially presenting with periarterial thickening of the celiac and superior celiac and superior mesenteric vessels (100% White, 50% male, median age 70.5 years). All four patients had abdominal pain with significant unintentional weight loss, with a median weight loss of 25 pounds (range 25–50 pounds). The median time to correct diagnosis was 221 days (range 162–340 days). All four patients had an elevated carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 level (range from 114 to 10,702, with a median of 341.5 U/mL). All four patients were diagnosed with endoscopic ultrasound–guided fine‐needle aspirations. The median number of biopsies required to make the diagnosis was three.ConclusionIsolated celiac and superior mesenteric periarterial thickening or “cuffing” is a vasculitis mimic that should prompt a comprehensive malignancy workup. Expedited referral to a tertiary center is essential given difficulty in tissue sampling with conventional methods and to avoid further delays in diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Medium‐vessel vasculitis involving the visceral vasculature in adults is rare. Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) is considered to be the classic medium‐vessel vasculitis. PAN has a reported annual incidence of two to nine cases per million and prevalence ranging from 2.6 to 31 cases per million; the frequency of PAN has decreased in recent years because of widespread hepatitis B vaccination. Furthermore, isolated gastrointestinal vasculitis is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature.

In patients with abdominal symptoms and radiographic evidence of thickening of the mesenteric vessels, vasculitis is generally at the top of the differential diagnosis. However, given that mesenteric vasculitis is rare and typically is not amenable to confirmation by biopsy, one should also consider alternate causes of arterial wall thickening. Specifically, mimics of vasculitis include intramural hematoma (due to non‐inflammatory vasculopathies such as heritable collagen vascular disorders, segmental arterial mediolysis, and fibromuscular dysplasia) as well as more serious conditions such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). PDAC has been implicated in medium artery vascular thickening secondary to extravascular migratory metastasis (EVMM) with involvement of the celiac and superior mesenteric vessels. Radiographically, this presents with perivascular cuffing that can be observed in PDAC even when overt pancreatic pathology is absent. Pancreatic cancer is the third leading cause of cancer deaths in the United States; therefore, early diagnosis of occult malignancy is critical given a reported overall five‐year survival of 9.3%.

To date, there has been only one case series describing perivascular cuffing as a presentation of PDAC. Drucker Iarovich et al described an Israeli cohort of 14 patients with PDAC, of which 3 patients presented with isolated celiac or superior mesenteric artery perivascular cuffing as the only diagnostic finding for PDAC. Interestingly, none of the patients showed pancreatic pathology on imaging. The remainder of the cases consisted of seven patients with abdominal vasculitis and four with retroperitoneal fibrosis. All of the three patients with PDAC died due to their underlying malignancy. 1 Here, we describe a case series of four patients with PDAC presenting as isolated superior mesenteric artery or celiac thickening.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study population

Patients were observed at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, at the vasculitis subspeciality clinic in rheumatology. Patients were identified using an electronic health record data exploration tool evaluating for diagnosis of pancreatic cancer, periaortitis, and vasculitis between January 2015 and December 2024.

Clinical data

Clinical data were collected by retrospective chart review. Data collected included patient demographics (age, sex, and race), clinical presentation, laboratory findings, imaging description, positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, biopsy method and number of attempts, initial diagnosis at presentation, final diagnosis, and time elapsed to correct diagnoses (Table 1). Given that this is a retrospective case series, our study is exempt from the institutional review board review at the Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota.

RESULTS

A total of four patients were identified with periarterial thickening surrounding the celiac artery (100% White, 50% male; Table 1). All four patients were initially referred due to concern for vasculitis later found to have PDAC (100% White, 50% male, median age 70.5 years). All four patients had abdominal pain with significant unintentional weight loss with a median weight loss of 25 pounds (range 25–50 pounds). The median time to correct diagnosis was 221 days (range 162–340 days). The median number of biopsies required to make the diagnosis was three. All four patients with a diagnosis of PDAC had an elevated carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19‐9 level (range from 114 to 10,702, with a median of 341.5 U/mL). Markers of inflammation were normal in all but one patient. All four patients had imaging showing isolated thickening of the celiac or superior mesenteric vasculature (Figure 1A–C). PET imaging was only mildly fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avid (maximum standardized uptake value range 2–3.6), with one patient not having any FDG avidity (Figure 1D). All four patients were diagnosed with endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (FNA) biopsies showing abnormal cytology (Figure 2A).

DISCUSSION

Due to delayed diagnosis, approximately half of the patients with PDAC present with metastatic disease, contributing to the disease's high mortality rate. 2 Therefore, early recognition of isolated perivascular cuffing of the celiac or superior mesenteric artery representing EVMM as a manifestation of PDAC is key because evidence of metastatic PDAC has a poor five‐year survival rate of only 2.9%. Our four patients with PDAC were similar to the case series presented by Drucker Iarovich et al with isolated involvement of the celiac and superior mesenteric artery without involvement of the aorta. Similar to the three patients with PDAC in the case series presented by Drucker Iavoich et al, our patients with PDAC also presented with abdominal pain accompanied with weight loss. Unlike our patients, their patients with PDAC had normal levels of CA 19‐9. Therefore, the use of CA 19‐9 for testing would be helpful in supporting the diagnosis; however, it should not be used in the exclusion of PDAC. None of our patients demonstrated pancreatic pathology on computed tomography imaging similar to Drucker Iarovich et al. 1

The celiac axis is often inaccessible by conventional biopsy methods and poses risk for complications given proximity to the aorta and difficulty in obtaining adequate tissue to provide a definitive diagnosis. A referral to the tertiary centers for EUS‐guided FNA is often needed. In 2020, Malikowski et al reported a total of 142 patients who underwent EUS‐guided FNA of the celiac structures as part of evaluation for malignancy or staging for PDAC at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, during which a close five‐day follow‐up showed no reported adverse events. 3

In 2019, Rustagi et al performed a retrospective analysis at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, of 253 patients with suspected metastases along the blood vessels (EVMM) who underwent EUS‐guided FNA. The majority of the patients (223 of 253) were found to have malignancies, with 132 patients being newly diagnosed with PDAC and 3.8% of patients having absent pancreatic pathology similar to our patients and the patients in the study by Drucker Iarovich et al. Similar to our case series, Rustagi et al noted a median of four FNAs (range 1–20) required to make an accurate diagnosis. Furthermore, the EUS‐guided FNA was able to make the diagnosis in 28% of patients when imaging was inconclusive. 4 , 5

Although EUS‐guided FNA mitigates the risks associated with the conventional methods, it is still prone to the sampling error. Given the susceptibility of sampling error, especially after multiple attempts that can cause delays in diagnosis, we suggest using an intraoperative pathology review evaluating the tissue acquired at the time of the procedure until pathologic tissue is obtained before procedure completion to optimize diagnostic accuracy and decrease the time to diagnosis. In our case series, all the patients underwent EUS‐guided FNA biopsies, which established the diagnosis.

Our case series adds to the existing literature regarding isolated superior mesenteric or celiac artery thickening presenting as the only finding of PDAC. We found in our case series a utility in using CA 19‐9 in evaluating suspected cases; however, we acknowledge the limitations of this test because other series have shown PDAC among patients with normal CA 19‐9. As such, an elevated CA 19‐9 should prompt aggressive consideration of PDAC, but a negative study does not exclude the possibility, and thus, further testing should be based on clinical suspicion. Although our study is limited by its retrospective and descriptive nature, the findings are assistive in alerting clinicians regarding this sinister mimic of medium‐vessel vasculitis. Notable strengths of the current study include a complete comprehensive workup of four patients, with none being lost to follow‐up throughout their workup.

In conclusion, isolated perivascular thickening or “cuffing” of the superior mesenteric artery or celiac artery is a vasculitis mimic that should prompt a comprehensive malignancy workup. Expedited referral to a tertiary center is essential given difficulty in tissue sampling with conventional methods and to avoid further delays in diagnosis given the time‐sensitive nature of correctly diagnosing PDAC in those presenting with EVMM. This case series uniquely describes PDAC presenting as isolated perivascular thickening of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries, closely mimicking medium‐vessel vasculitis. All patients lacked identifiable pancreatic masses on cross‐sectional imaging and demonstrated only mild or absent FDG avidity on PET, contributing to the substantial diagnostic delay. Building on the limited existing literature, this study highlights EVMM as the sole manifestation of PDAC and underscores the need for heightened malignancy suspicion in patients with unexplained mesenteric arterial “cuffing.”
