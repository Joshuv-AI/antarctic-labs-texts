O2L-001, an innovative thrombolytic to evacuate intracerebral haematoma
Authors: Audrey M Thiebaut, Estelle R Louet, Mélina Ianszen, Marie-Julie Guichard, Daniel F Hanley, Christophe Gaudin, Jérôme Parcq
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracerebral haemorrhage is an unmet medical need affecting more than 3 million people worldwide every year and leading to the formation of an intracerebral haematoma. Updated guidelines (2022) for the management of intracerebral haemorrhage patients recognize that minimally invasive approaches for the evacuation of supratentorial intracerebral haemorrhage have demonstrated reductions in mortality compared with medical management alone. However, improvement of functional outcome with a procedure involving thrombolytic therapy was neutral in the last large phase 3 clinical trial and requires a more effective and safer thrombolytic agent than those currently available. Here, we demonstrate that O2L-001 allows for the extended release of W253R/R275S recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (rtPA). A new rtPA variant, called optimized tPA (OptPA), offers improved efficacy for haematoma evacuation as well as improved safety.OptPA was produced in a Chinese hamster ovary cell line before purification, nanoprecipitation using the NANOp2Lysis® technological platform followed by suspension in a solution of 17% poloxamer 407 to obtain O2L-001. Plasmin generation assays were performed to demonstrate O2L-001 safety. Ex vivo haematoma models using human blood were used to demonstrate O2L-001 thrombolysis properties and efficacy. For the best translational significance, a clinical sized haematoma was used to ensure catheter placement and to allow administration of the thrombolytic agent into the core of the haematoma via a minimally invasive procedure.The capacity of OptPA to convert plasminogen into plasmin is strongly decreased compared to rtPA, thereby reducing potential bleeding events. However, a clot lysis assay showed that OptPA had the same fibrinolytic activity as rtPA. We demonstrated that long-term exposure to a thrombolytic agent was essential to achieve high thrombolysis efficacy. Indeed, 24 h continuous exposure to 0.1 µg/ml rtPA had similar efficacy than repeated short exposure to 30 µg/ml rtPA.This finding led to the development of O2L-001, allowing the extended release of OptPA in the first 6 h following injection. An ex vivo model using human blood was used to demonstrate O2L-001 efficacy. Interestingly, unlike rtPA, O2L-001 was able to induce the complete lysis of the 5 ml haematoma. In clinical sized haematomas (obtained from 30 ml of human blood), a single injection of O2L-001 at 1 mg/ml into the core of the haematoma led to a 44% increase in thrombolysis compared to rtPA.Taken together, these results demonstrate that O2L-001 provides new hope for haematoma evacuation and the treatment of patients with intracerebral haemorrhage.Keywords: thrombolysis, intracerebral haematoma, haemorrhagic stroke, plasminogen activator, haematoma model

Introduction

Worldwide, more than 3 million spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhages occur annually.1 Non-traumatic intracerebral haemorrhage results from a weakened blood vessel rupture leading to the formation of an intracerebral haematoma, which is defined as a solid swelling of clotted blood responsible for the increase of intracerebral pressure and subsequent lesions. While intracerebral haemorrhage represents <27% of all strokes, it has the greater morbidity and mortality.1 According to the healthcare economic burden study of ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage represented a €27 billion cost across Europe in 2017, nevertheless it is still an unmet medical need.2 The minimally invasive surgery plus alteplase for intracerebral haemorrhage evacuation phase III (MISTIE-III) clinical trial tried to remove haematoma by injecting, through an external catheter, recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (rtPA) into the clot to liquefy it and allow its drainage.3 The MISTIE-III trial did not show an improvement of functional outcome 365 days after intracerebral haemorrhage. As rtPA is short acting effect in haematoma, with a thrombolytic activity limited to the first 15 min following injection,4 up to nine injections were planned in the protocol of the MISTIE trial to overcome this limitation, in vain. Interestingly, a clear association between clot removal and a good functional outcome was highlighted in this trial. Robust exploratory analyses suggested that a reduction in clot size to 20 ml or less was associated with good functional outcome.3 While the therapeutic strategy seemed compelling and the procedure was demonstrated as safe, the thrombolytic agent used in this trial has limitations. Only 58% of patients treated with rtPA achieved a reduction of the haematoma below 15 ml. Furthermore, rtPA has deleterious side effects in the brain parenchyma, such as brain–blood barrier permeability and subsequent bleeding risks5-9 and pro-neurotoxic properties mediated by N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor.10,11

In 2018, we demonstrated the efficacy for haematoma evacuation of optimized tPA (OptPA), an engineered rtPA variant with two mutations, a substitution of tryptophan-253 in the Kringle 2 domain by arginine to prevent its interaction with the NMDA receptor, and a substitution of arginine-275 by serine to prevent its capacity to promote plasmin generation off target (beyond the clot), and highlighted that OptPA had a safer profile than rtPA in a rat model of intracerebral haemorrhage.12 In the present publication, we hypothesized that O2L-001, whose active compound is OptPA embedded in a solution of 17% (w:v) poloxamer 407, may offer better safety as well as improved efficacy for haematoma evacuation.

Material and methods

Materials

Human blood samples, red blood-cell bags, plasma bags and buffy coat bags were obtained from the Etablissement français du sang (EFS). Studies were declared and approved by the French Ministry of Research (Declaration DC-2018-3154). Sodium chloride, Tris-Imidazole, HEPES and CaCl2 were from Sigma-Aldrich. Poloxamer 407 was obtained from BTC Europe. Dulbecco's PBS (dPBS, Gibco™) was from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Recombinant tissue factor supplemented with synthetic phospholipids (Dade® Innovin®) was provided by Siemens Healthcare. Plasminogen was purchased from Enzyme Research Laboratories Ltd. The pNAPEP 1751 was from Cryopep. EXTEM-S substrates for thromboelastography and the ROTEM delta instrument were from Werfen.

Drugs

The rtPA (alteplase) was from Boehringer-Ingelheim and was resuspended as described by the manufacturer. OptPA was produced using the GPEx® technology, proprietary technology of Catalent Biologics. O2L-001 was prepared by including OptPA in a solution of 17% (w:v) poloxamer 407. Briefly, an OptPA solution was precipitated by the addition of tetraglycol and gently mixed at 4°C. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and precipitates were resuspended in the poloxamer solution.

In vitro thrombolytic activity

Fresh blood (300 µl per assay, used within 6 h) was harvested in a pre-citrated tube of 2.7 ml (BD Vacutainer®) and was studied using rotational thromboelastography (ROTEM delta, TEM®, Werfen) in the presence of rtPA and OptPA (0.6 and 0.9 µg/ml) and EXTEM-S substrate. Plasminogen activators were diluted in HEPES buffer (HEPES 10 mM pH 7.4, NaCl 150 mM). Lysis onset time (LOT; defined as the time from clotting to a decrease of 15% of clot firmness), clot lysis rate (CLR), and the area under the curve (AUC) were measured to characterize and quantify thrombolysis.

In vitro plasminogen activation

Plasminogen activators were incubated with Glu-plasminogen (0.25 µM) and pNAPEP 1751 (0.6 mM). The reaction was carried out at 37°C in a buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-Imidazole pH 8.0 in a final volume of 50 µl. Enzymatic activity of the plasmin (APln), converted by the action of plasminogen activator on plasminogen (AtPA), was determined by measuring an increase in absorbance of the free chromophore (pNA) generated per time unit at a wavelength of 405 nm over 18 h, in a microplate reader (BIOTEK ELx 808). APln is the first derivate of AtPA determined by the calculation of the slope as a function of the time: APln = (dAtPA) / dt, where dAtPA ≠ 0. Analyses were made with six concentrations of rtPA (0.25 nM to 7.5 nM) and with three concentrations of OptPA (2.5 nM, 5 nM and 7.5 nM).

Ex vivo model

Whole blood samples or red blood cells, platelets and plasma fractions were purchased from EFS. Red blood cells, plasma and platelets were mixed in a 4:4:1 ratio to mimic ratios present in whole blood. Blood analysis and blood components dosage were performed on reconstituted human blood before coagulation at the Centre-Hospitalo Universitaire de Caen-Normandie. The coagulation was initiated by adding 2.5% Innovin® and 0.020 M CaCl2 to human citrated whole blood or reconstituted human blood. Clots were kept overnight at 37°C.

Ethics and declaration of use of human blood

Consent for the use of blood for research purposes during a blood donation is collected by the EFS before the donation. The absence of opposition is verified by the EFS and the samples are anonymized. Only the age and gender are communicated by the EFS. No information is provided to identify the donor. An agreement opening the right to purchase blood derivatives was signed with the EFS (agreement PLER-UPR/2018/041). In addition, a prior declaration was made to the Ministry of Research (Declaration DC-2018-3154) before the initiation of the present studies.

Rheological measurements

The thermosensitive gelation process of O2L-001 was investigated during temperature sweeping from 4 to 40°C using a rheometer MCR102 (Anton-Paar) with the following parameters: geometry plate-plate (plates space 40 mm); strain 0.1%; frequency 1%; heating rate 1°C/min. Storage (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded over the temperature shift. The transition from liquid to gel is achieved when tan δ = G″ / G′ = 1. Gelation time was calculated as the time between the transition phase (tan δ = 1) and the plateau of the tan function (plateau following one-phase decay model).

O2L-001 dissolution method in a flow-through cell

O2L-001 dissolution kinetics were investigated by SPS Pharma in a dissolution system (SOTAX CE7 Smart, flow-through cell method) configured as a closed-loop system allowing the use of a fixed O2L-001 volume. Experiments were done using a sample size of 500 mg of O2L-001 (1 mg of protein/g gel) in 50 ml of dissolution medium (0.9% NaCl in PBS pH 7.4). This corresponds to the dissolution medium’s final protein concentration (OptPA) of 10 µg/ml. OptPA was quantified in the USP4 system using an HPLC method. Briefly, the parameters were the following: C4 column Jupiter (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, 300 Å); mobile phase A: 0.1% TFA in deionized water v/v; mobile phase B: 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile v/v and a flow rate of 1.2 ml/min. Protein was detected at 220 nm and the expected retention time was ∼8.8–9.5 min.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality tests were performed on all samples (D’Agostino-Pearson omnibus test and Shapiro–Wilk test). If the normality of the residuals passed, the differences between multiple groups were analysed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When normality could not be assumed, we used non-parametric tests: one-way ANOVA followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. When two independent variables were studied on multiple groups, two-way ANOVA was used followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons tests. To explore possible linear relationships between two variables, simple linear regression was used. Statistics were computed using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.2 (226) for Windows, GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA, www.Graphpad.com. Differences were considered statistically significant if probability value P < 0.05.

Results

Optimized tPA has the same thrombolytic activity than rtPA but promotes less plasmin generation in absence of fibrin

We previously reported that OptPA, produced and released by human embryonic cells (HEK)-293 cells, was a non-proneurotoxic agent.12 To move toward the clinical phases, we manufactured a ‘clinical’-grade OptPA produced by CHO (Chinese hamster ovary) cells. We first characterized its thrombolytic activity (versus rtPA), as well as its capacity to convert plasminogen into plasmin in absence of fibrin. In a thrombo-elastogram assay, three parameters were recorded to analyse effects of rtPA and OptPA on thrombolysis: (i) the lysis onset time (LOT; Fig. 1A) defined by the time span from clotting time to the start of significant lysis (15% breakdown of clot firmness); (ii) the clot lysis rate (CLR; Fig. 1B); and (iii) the area under the curve (AUC; Fig. 1C) that relates to the clot formation and the lysis induced by the thrombolytic agent. There was an impact of the concentration of thrombolytic agents on the LOT parameter (Fig. 1A): both OptPA and rtPA had lower LOT at 0.9 µg/ml than at 0.6 µg/ml. For rtPA at 0.6 µg/ml the LOT was 24.4 min versus 15.8 min for rtPA at 0.9 µg/ml (P = 0.0031). For OptPA at 0.6 µg/ml the LOT was 29.8 min versus 19.9 min for OptPA at 0.9 µg/ml (P = 0.0029). However, the concentration of rtPA and OptPA did not influence the CLR or the AUC. Interestingly, at the two concentrations tested (0.6 µg/ml and 0.9 µg/ml), there was no difference between rtPA and OptPA on the LOT (Fig. 1A), the CLR (Fig. 1B) or the AUC (Fig. 1A), showing that OptPA and rtPA had similar thrombolytic activity.

As tPA plays direct and indirect multiple roles in the CNS13 with plasmin-dependent pathway, we measured the ability of OptPA to convert plasminogen into plasmin in absence of fibrin (i.e. out of the blood clot). The enzymatic activity of rtPA and OptPA was evaluated on their natural substrate plasminogen and measured in a kinetic assay using the chromogenic plasmin substrate pNAPEP 1751 (Fig. 1D–F). In the range of concentrations tested, OptPA showed less capacity than rtPA to activate plasminogen in the absence of fibrin and thus convert the pNAPEP 1751 substrate (Fig. 1D and E). Linear regression of the maximum velocity (Vmax) as a function of plasminogen activators concentration has confirmed that OptPA has a lower enzymatic activity than rtPA in absence of fibrin (linear regression slope: 1.9 × 10−3 OD/min/nM for rtPA versus 0.3 × 10−3 OD/min/nM for OptPA; P = 0.0003; Fig. 1F).

Extended exposure to low rtPA concentrations allows high thrombolytic activity

OptPA and rtPA had similar thrombolytic efficacy. The next step was to overcome this limit and increased thrombolytic efficacy to better treat haemorrhagic stroke patients. We, thus, tested the effect of long-term exposure (24 h) to plasminogen activator by immerging 1 ml blood clots (n = 7 clot per condition) in baths containing PBS (negative control) or concentrations of rtPA ranging from 0.001 µg/ml to 1 µg/ml (Fig. 2A). We compared at each time point (1 h, 3 h, 5 h, 7 h, 24 h) the rtPA effect versus negative control (PBS). There was no difference of thrombolysis between PBS and rtPA at 0.001 µg/ml and 0.01 µg/ml, at all the time points studied (Fig. 2B). Conversely, at 7 h, rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml induced a significative clot lysis compared to PBS (P = 0.0147; Fig. 2B). Interestingly, rtPA at 1 µg/ml induced a significative clot lysis at all time points except 1 h when compared to PBS (at the time points 1, 3, 5, 7 and 24 h, the P-values were P = 0.30931, P = 0.0013, P = 0.0180, P = 0.0185, P = 0.0285, respectively) (Fig. 2B). The AUC, which relates the total thrombolysis induced by rtPA during the 24 h of treatment, showed that rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml induced a significant clot lysis compared to PBS (P = 0.0022 and P = 0.0001, respectively). Conversely rtPA at 0.001 µg/ml and 0.01 µg/ml did not induce a significant clot lysis (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, there was no difference of thrombolysis between rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml, meaning that the highest effect was obtained with 0.1 µg/ml (Fig. 2C).

We then compared long-term exposure to rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml to a setting that mimics rtPA administration in MISTIE and CLEAR clinical trials (Fig. 2A). We immersed 1 ml clots three times for 15 min, every 4 h, in baths containing a high concentration of rtPA, 30-fold the top dose used for long-term exposure (30 µg for 1 ml clot size). The thrombolytic rate was not statistically different between 24 h baths containing rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml and the three 15 min baths containing rtPA at 30 µg/ml (Fig. 2D and E).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that a higher thrombolysis effect cannot be achieved by increasing the concentration of rtPA in this model. Conversely, similar thrombolytic efficacy can be reached with continuous exposure by using concentrations 30 or 300 times lower than those used in short and spaced exposure.

The impact of tPA treatment duration

The first 15 min of exposure to rtPA at 30 µg/ml were very effective at 1 h with a clot lysis of 37.3% (P = 0.0207) and similar clot lysis rate (36.9%) was observed over the next 23 h (therefore, a total clot lysis of 74.1% after 24 h), meaning that 50.3% of the global thrombolysis effect of rtPA at 30 µg/ml was observed after 1 h of treatment (i.e. 15 min of rtPA at 30 µg/ml bath + 45 min of PBS bath) (Fig. 2A and D). Conversely, a long-term exposure to rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml led to a more progressive thrombolysis with only 13.4% (P = 0.1803, non-significant) and 12.4% (P = 0.0977, non-significant) of clot lysis after 1 h of treatment, respectively but 54.4% (P = 0.002) and 64.1% (P = 0.0007) of clot lysis was observed over the next 23 h (Fig. 2D).

Interestingly, a 24 h immersion in a bath containing rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml induced 52.4% of clot lysis in the first 7 h of treatment (P = 0.0021), and only 15.4% of clot lysis between 7 and 24 h (P = 0.37, non-significant). In the same way, a 24 h immersion in a bath containing rtPA at 1 µg/ml induced a clot lysis of 58.1% in the first 7 h of treatment, and only 18.4% of clot lysis between 7 and 24 h (P = 0.0223). These results highlighted that >75% of the global effect of rtPA was observed in the first 7 h of treatment (77.3% and 75.9% of the global effect of rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml) for extended exposure to plasminogen activator (Fig. 2D).

O2L-001 allows an extended release of OptPA

Based on the previous results, we considered a new formulation allowing an extended release of OptPA, to increase the period of thrombolytic activity and to decrease the concentration of OptPA in the clot by unit of time. To achieve that, OptPA was nanoprecipitated and suspended in a poloxamer solution to obtain O2L-001. As described by Liu and collaborators,14 18% poloxamer 407 allows 8 to 10 h release of protein encapsulated in the polymer. As decreasing the concentration of poloxamer 407 reduces the release time, we selected a 17% poloxamer 407 carriers solution to suspend OptPA nanoprecipitates to allow a release time in the range of 7 h with acceptable thermosensitive features (Fig. 2).

We then aimed to characterize the release profile of OptPA (Fig. 3A). Once the gel was formed, the kinetic release of OptPA from O2L-001 at 1.0 mg/ml was evaluated with a flow-through cell method (USP4). After incubation at 37°C to allow jellification, the dissolution medium was pumped into the cell in a closed loop. A series of 1 ml harvests proceeded at different time points and the quantity of OptPA released was evaluated by HPLC. Two steps were identified in the release of the protein of interest: the first phase was a burst release during the first 30 min (71.9% of total released material) and the second phase was an extended release of the remaining 28.1%, over the next 4.5 h, enough to ensure effective thrombolysis. The plateau was reached at 5.2 h (Fig. 3A).

Rheological measurements were performed (Fig. 3B and C) to calculate the sol-to-gel transition temperature (Fig. 3D and E) and the time to get to a stable gel state after reaching the transition temperature (Fig. 3F and G). Two different concentrations of OptPA (0.2 and 1 mg/ml) in 17% poloxamer 407 were analysed to evaluate if the OptPA concentration impacted the sol-to-gel transition temperature and the gelling time. As expected, O2L-001 was liquid at temperatures below 20°C (G″ > G′) and it became a gel (G″ < G′) above the sol-to-gel transition temperature (loss modulus value equals the elastic modulus value, G″ = G′) (Fig. 3B and C). Computed sol-to-gel transition temperature (Tan δ = G′ / G″ = 1) was 25.7°C ± 0.16 at 0.2 mg/ml of O2L-001 (Fig. 3D) and 26.3°C ± 0.16 at 1.0 mg/ml of O2L-001 (Fig. 3E), which are the optimal temperatures to ensure stable gel formation at body temperature. Decreasing the OptPA concentration by a factor of five did not impact the sol-to-gel transition temperature of the P407 (Fig. 3D and E). Here, the gelling time was determined as the time between the gel transition state (Tan δ = 1) and the stable gel state (plateau observed following the one phase decay). Gelation times of 1.2 min for the 1.0 mg/ml concentration and 1.5 min for the 0.2 mg/ml concentration were calculated (Fig. 3F and G).

In this series of experiments, the O2L-001 drug product allowed an extended release of a small concentration of OptPA per unit of time. The product jellified at a sol-to-gel transition temperature of ∼26°C and the gelation time was fast, <2 min, at the concentrations tested. The characteristics of the product should allow fast in situ gelling at 37°C in the core of the haematoma following local injection.

O2L-001 is a more effective thrombolytic than regular rtPA for haematoma lysis

We then tested the thrombolytic efficacy of O2L-001 on small human blood haematomas (n = 6 haematomas per conditions). Ex vivo haematomas were produced with 5 ml of human blood incubated overnight at 37°C with tissue factor to induce blood coagulation. Before treatment administration, haematoma weights were not different (Fig. 4B). Treatment involved injection with a syringe into the core of the haematoma. As the objective was to improve the thrombolysis that was observed in the MISTIE programme (i.e. three injections per day for up to 3 days), this setting was mimicked here and the haematomas were treated with three injections of rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml spaced from 4 h (3 × 133 µl to have a 400 µl final volume of treatment) (Fig. 4A). O2L-001 was injected in a single shot of 400 µl at 0.03 mg/ml, 0.1 mg/ml, 0.3 and 1.0 mg/ml and a single injection of PBS was used as a negative control (Fig. 4A). Haematomas were weighed after 9 h of treatment and lysis of the haematomas was calculated. At 1.0 mg/ml rtPA induced a significant haematoma lysis of 72.67% when compared to PBS (72.67% versus 15.17% of haematoma lysis, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C and D). Haematoma lysis was of 60.33%, 77.17%, 85.83% and 93.67% for O2L-001 at 0.03 mg/ml, 0.1.0 mg/ml, 0.3 and 1.0 mg/ml, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). At every concentration tested, O2L-001 induced a significant haematoma lysis compared to PBS (P < 0.0001 for the four tested concentrations; Fig. 4C). Interestingly, five of six haematomas treated with O2L-001 at 1.0 mg/ml were completely lysed or lysed at >90% (Fig. 4C). Haematoma lysis induced by O2L-001 at 0.03 and 0.1 mg/ml was not significantly different from haematoma lysis induced by rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml (Fig. 4C). However, O2L-001 at 0.3 mg/ml induced a haematoma lysis significantly higher than rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml (85.83% versus 72.67%, P = 0.0373; Fig. 4C). Furthermore, O2L-001 at 1.0 mg/ml had a better efficacy than rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml; haematoma lysis was increased by 28.9% with O2L-001 at 1.0 mg/ml (93.67% versus 72.67%, P = 0.0004; Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the increased efficacy of O2L-001 at 0.3 and 0.1 mg/ml compared to rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml was independent of the initial weight of the haematomas, as this parameter is known to influence haematoma lysis (Fig. 4D).

Thus, the formulation of OptPA in O2L-001 induced a higher thrombolytic activity than rtPA at the same concentration, over the same period of observation (9 h).

Ex vivo model of clinical size haematoma

The proof-of-concept of the higher thrombolytic efficacy of O2L-001 compared to rtPA was established using the 5 ml haematomas. The clinical set-up involved parameters such as the catheters used for injection, a period of catheter closure and a period of passive drainage to allow gravity-based evacuation of the liquefied blood. We decided to develop a new model of haematoma similar to those found in the clinic. To ensure the best clinical translation, we decided to use human blood and to perform our experiment in an ex vivo set-up to control the placement of a catheter at the core of the haematoma (Fig. 5A). To access such quantities of human blood, we used red blood cells, plasma and buffy coat fractions to reconstitute human blood. The fractions were mixed in a 4:4:1 ratio, as described by Ågren and collaborators15 (Fig. 5A). In our model, platelets and white blood cells including neutrophils and lymphocytes counts and fibrinogen and factor V levels were similar to those found in healthy subjects (Fig. 5B), confirming the translation and predictivity of the model. The mean of red blood cell count was lower than normal range and can be considered as moderate erythrocytopenia, which is often correlated with iron deficiency anaemia, as observed in a large part of the population that could suffer from haemorrhagic stroke. Indeed, anaemia affects 1.62 billion people, which corresponds to 24.8% of the population.16 Interestingly, the level of partial thromboplastin time (PTT), which is a measurement of the efficacy of the intrinsic coagulation pathway, was in the normal range.

(B) Red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (NEUT), lymphocyte (LYMP) and platelet (PLT) counts, fibrinogen (Fbg) and factor V (FV) levels and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) were investigated in reconstituted human blood before coagulation. Black dotted lines represent normal ranges and red dotted lines represent levels associated to severe pathological condition.

A retrospective analysis performed on 744 supratentorial primary intracerebral haemorrhage patients showed that median haematomas volumes were 13 ml (IQR, 5–40 ml) and 39 ml (IQR, 16–75 ml) for deep and lobar intracerebral haemorrhages, respectively.17 Consequently we decided to perform our experiment on clinical size haematomas using 30 ml reconstituted human blood (Fig. 5A).

O2L-001 induces higher thrombolysis than rtPA

To reproduce the clinical use of rtPA in MISTIE, 1 ml of rtPA at 1.0 mg/ml was injected into the core of the haematoma, through an external ventricular drainage catheter. The haemorrhage was closed 1 h after injection and then opened for 8 h to drain the blood (Fig. 6A). The closure/opening period are not compatible with best benefit of our product due to the extended-release profile detailed above. Thus, to cover the complete extended-release period, 1 ml of O2L-001 at 1.0 mg/ml was injected at the core of the haematoma (n = 9 haematomas per condition) followed by a catheter closure period of 8 h to ensure complete OptPA release, and a catheter opening period of 1 h to drain the lysed blood (Fig. 6A). Before administration of treatment, there was no difference between haematoma weights (Fig. 6B). After 9 h treatment (catheter closure period + catheter opening period), haematomas were weighed and haematoma lysis was calculated. In this model, rtPA induced a significative haematoma lysis of 39.4% when compared to PBS control (39.4% versus 11.22% of haematoma lysis, P = 0.0002). Our thrombolytic, O2L-001 triggered a significant haematoma lysis of 56.9% when compared to the PBS control (56.9% versus 11.22%, P < 0.0001). Our procedure with O2L-001 induced an increase of thrombolysis of 44.24% when compared to rtPA (56.89% versus 39.44%, P = 0.0201) meaning that our new thrombolytic had a better efficacy than rtPA for the thrombolysis of human clinical size haematomas in the most translational model of efficacy (Fig. 6C–E). Again, the increased efficacy of O2L-001 compared to rtPA was independent of the initial weight, as this parameter in known to influence haematoma lysis (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

O2L-001 is a new thrombolytic agent composed of a tPA variant (W253R/R275S rtPA) named OptPA. OptPA has been nanoprecipitated and included in poloxamer 407 to allow its extended release. Here, we break the lock of the limited activity of thrombolytic agent to evacuate brain haematomas, by demonstrating that our innovative product, O2L-001, allows the extended release of OptPA and increases the thrombolytic efficacy on human blood haematomas compared to the gold standard thrombolytic agent. O2L-001 induced a lysis of 56.9% on 30 ml human blood clinical size haematomas, which represents an increase of haematoma lysis of 44% compared to rtPA.

The MISTIE III trial attempted to remove haematomas thanks by injection of rtPA at 1 mg/ml into the haematoma through an external catheter.3 The catheter was closed for 1 h to allow rtPA-haematoma interaction and then opened for 7 h to drain the lysed blood. The rtPA was injected every 8 h for up to nine doses. Unfortunately, the MISTIE III trial did not demonstrate an improvement of the clinical outcome 365 days after intracerebral haemorrhage. This result can be explained by two main factors: the insufficient efficacy and the deleterious side effect of tPA. Only 58% of patients treated with rtPA have achieved the surgical aim (haematoma size < 15 ml) and the proportion of patients with asymptomatic bleeding was higher in the MISTIE group (+24% within 72 h after the last dose). Moreover, reports from the bench showed that rtPA injection to decrease haematoma volume in pigs was responsible for the extension of the peri-haematoma oedema,18 an effect dependant on the NMDA receptors.19 A secondary analysis of the MISTIE III clinical trial has demonstrated a robust association between a good functional outcome and a reduction in haematoma size to 20 ml or less, which provides the basis for a surrogate marker of efficacy when using haematoma volume reduction.3 Consequently, our goal was to develop a new thrombolytic with improved efficacy and safety.

rtPA is known to interact with different proteins/receptors in the brain parenchyma leading to several effects, including neurotoxicity, brain–blood barrier leakage and asymptomatic bleeding.6,7,10,13 In order to decrease tPA deleterious side effects, we developed OptPA. This variant of rtPA has two mutations: a first mutation in the Kringle 2 domain to prevent its interaction with the NMDA receptor responsible for neuronal death; and a second mutation to decrease its capacity to convert plasminogen into plasmin outside the blood haematoma.12,20 In a previous publication, we demonstrated that OptPA has a better safety profile than rtPA and can be used to treat intracerebral haemorrhage without increasing brain oedema and secondary haemorrhage.12

In the present study, we focused on the thrombolytic efficacy. We demonstrated that increasing the concentration of the thrombolytic agent does not induce higher thrombolysis. We showed that long-term exposure to rtPA at 0.1 µg/ml and 1 µg/ml induced the same thrombolysis levels. Even short and spaced exposure to concentrations 30 to 300 times higher did not improve thrombolysis. This result supports the observation made by Bonnard and collaborators21 and others showing a maximum lysis rate at concentrations of plasminogen activator >1.4 nM (0.1 µg/ml). We can therefore conclude than increasing the thrombolytic concentration is not the answer to increase the thrombolytic efficacy. Interestingly, the half-life activity of rtPA injected into a haematoma was estimated between 15–30 min.4 We decided therefore to investigate the impact of exposure time on thrombolysis. We demonstrated that thrombolysis of large haematomas requires hours, as a difference with small haematomas obstructing vessels. In our model, >75% of the global effect of rtPA was observed over the first 7 h of treatment. Based on these observations, we developed a more effective thrombolytic, O2L-001, by nanoprecipitating and including a thrombolytic agent in an extended release polymer solution. The thrombolytic agent chosen was OptPA because it has a better safety profile than rtPA. At body temperature, O2L-001 allowed a release of OptPA over the first 5.5 h of treatment leading to more progressive and efficient thrombolysis. Administration of 1 mg/ml O2L-001 induced the complete lysis of two of six 5 ml haematomas, while none of the haematomas treated with rtPA showed more than 90% lysis. This result suggests that it will not be necessary to inject up to nine doses of O2L-001 as it was the case with rtPA in the MISTIE trial, to achieve the clinical aim in patients, which is another crucial element to limit asymptomatic bleeding.22

To achieve the best clinical translation and to find the best procedure to treat patients, we developed an ex vivo model with clinical size human blood haematomas. The ex vivo model was preferred to the in vivo model for several reasons: (i) the species specificity of haemostasis; (ii) the poor resistance of pigs to anaesthesia limiting the catheter closure period at 3 h, which is not adapted to the kinetic profile of O2L-001; and (iii) the poor predictivity of the catheter placement in 1–2 ml haematomas that can be achieved in pigs,4 while the clinical practice demonstrated the importance of this parameter to achieve high efficacy.23 This model is based on reconstituted human blood; red blood cells, plasma and buffy coat bags were mixed in a 4:4:1 ratio. Blood counts demonstrated that reconstituted blood is similar to healthy ‘old’ subjects. Moreover, platelet count and PTT were in the same range as those found in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage enrolled in the CLEAR study,24 confirming that this model is well adapted for preclinical assessment. Coagulation through the extrinsic pathway using tissue factor was preferred to mimic fast haemostatic response due to tissue factor release from endothelial cells facture the rupture of the vessel. When 30 ml of reconstituted blood is coagulated, haematomas weighing on average 12.13 g ± 0.923 were formed, with a range of heterogeneity of 10.34 g to 13.87 g, highlighting different levels of contraction of the haematoma, another important parameter to consider in a translational and predictive model. Interestingly in this ex vivo model, a single injection of rtPA was more effective than what was observed in the MISTIE clinical trial, which can be explained by the fact that we have perfect catheter placement in the haematoma leading to improved reproducibility and efficacy.23

To treat a 30 ml clinical size haematoma with O2L-001, we proposed the following protocol: injection of O2L-001 at 1 mg/ml followed by >6 h of catheter closure to allow an optimal extended release of OptPA (here we tested 8 h catheter closure period versus 1 h of MISTIE) and 1 h of catheter opening for drain the blood (versus 7 h for MISTIE). In this experiment, we did not add a group with rtPA exposed for 8 h plus 1 h drainage as it was not a set-up applicable in clinic due to the high risk of rebleeding. Our investigation showed that O2L-001 was a more effective thrombolytic agent than rtPA, increasing haematoma lysis by 44% on clinical sized haematomas. In the two models tested here (5 ml haematoma and 30 ml haematoma), an average +20% absolute thrombolytic increase was observed compared to rtPA.

Overall, we have shown that O2L-001, an innovative thrombolytic with a new mechanism of action, allowing for an extended release of optimized tPA, is a safer and more effective therapeutic to treat intracerebral haemorrhagic stroke, an indication without any treatment so far. This drug addresses all of the limitations observed in the MISTIE clinical trial with rtPA.
