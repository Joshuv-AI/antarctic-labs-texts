Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a multisystem disease of increasing prevalence and thus of great public health importance.[1] Pruritus is a common dermatologic manifestation of CKD, and it has been associated with increased occurrences of mood and sleep disorders, increased rates of infection and hospitalization, reduced overall quality of life and increased mortality rates.[2,3,4] Chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus (CKD-aP) has been reported at varying severities in all stages of CKD; however, the highest prevalence has been among those with end-stage kidney disease on renal replacement therapy.[5,6] CKD-aP, a term that replaced uremic pruritus, is a chronic debilitating condition that is under-recognized and underreported despite its huge burden among sufferers.[7]

In CKD, the exact pathophysiology of pruritus is unknown. However, various mechanisms have been postulated, which include abnormalities of calcium, phosphate and vitamin D metabolism, iron deficiency anaemia, uraemic toxins, hyperparathyroidism, immune system dysregulation and altered endogenous opioid levels.[8-10] Similarly, dialysis, stress, cold and bathing are known precipitants of pruritus in CKD.[11] Skin lesions in CKD-aP are non-primary, bilaterally symmetrical lesions, worse at night and mostly generalized or located in the back, trunk and head.[12] Various validated unidimensional and multidimensional scales have been used in assessing the severity of pruritus in CKD.[13,14] The visual analogue scale (VAS), numerical rating scales (NRS) and verbal rating scales (VRS) are the most commonly used tools to assess CKD-aP and correlate significantly with various quality of life tools.[15]

Management of pruritus in CKD include- optimizing dialysis frequency and adequacy, reducing serum calcium- phosphate product, correction of iron deficiency anaemia, as well as symptomatic relief using gabapentinoids, mast cell stabilizers, antidepressants and ultraviolet B phototherapy.[16,17]

Knowledge of the prevalence and severity of pruritus in CKD will help in its early diagnosis and treatment, ultimately improving care and quality of life in CKD patients with pruritus.

Methodology

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study carried out in a tertiary hospital in Southern Nigeria between June 2024 and August 2024.

Sample size was calculated using the Taro Yamane finite population correction formula: [18] n = N/(1+N(e)2); where N = 255 (yearly CKD population), e = 0.05. n = 255/(1+255(0.05)2) = 255/(1+255(0.0025)) = 255/(1+0.638) = 155.8. Therefore, the calculated minimum sample size (including 10% attrition), n = 156+15 = 171, was gotten. All stages 3-5 pre-dialysis CKD adult patients who gave informed consent were recruited for this study. Patients with primary skin disorders were excluded. Information on socio-demographic characteristics, stage and aetiology of CKD was extracted from the clinic notes, skin examination was done, and all findings were entered into a questionnaire. The Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) was used to categorize the severity of pruritus in the preceding 24 hours into: None (0), mild (1-3), moderate (4-6) and severe (7-9).[19]

CKD was defined according to KDIGO guidelines as abnormalities of kidney structure and function persisting more than 3 months, including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 60ml/min/1.73m2 and/or markers of kidney damage such as albuminuria. [20] Stages 3,4 and 5 CKD were defined as eGFR of 30-59, 15-29 and <15ml/min/1.73m2 respectively.[20]

Data were analysed using SPSS version 23 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Descriptive statistics for baseline data were performed on continuous variables using mean, standard deviation, range and median where appropriate. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Data were presented as tables and charts. Chi-square test and Fisher's exact test were used to test for association between variables. p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the University of Benin Teaching Hospital with protocol number ADM/E/22/A/VOL. VII/48311673 dated 5th June 2024.

Results

A total of 171 participants were involved in this study. The mean age in this study was 59.9 ± 16.9 years, with an age range of 18 to 90 years. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study population. There were 96(56.1%) males and 75(43.9%) females. Most (77%) of the study participants were married, and 46.8% of them had a tertiary level of education. There were 40 (23.4%) participants with stage 3 CKD, 51(29.8%) and 80(46.8%) with stage 4 and 5 diseases, respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence and pattern of chronic kidney disease- associated pruritus in pre-dialysis patients. The prevalence of pruritus in this study was 26.9%. CKD-aP and its severity were not significantly associated with gender or CKD etiology.

The mean age of this study was 59.9 years. This finding differs from several Nigerian studies where a majority of CKD patients are in their economically productive years, usually less than 45years, as opposed to what is observed in developed countries where the disease commonly affects the elderly.[21,22] Our finding was, however, similar to the mean age of 52.8years in a study by Berenice et al in the Benin Republic.[23]

The leading CKD aetiology was diabetes mellitus. This finding is similar to reports by Edeki et al in Benin City, Nigeria, but differs from some studies in other parts of Nigeria where chronic glomerulonephritis and hypertension are the undisputed leaders of CKD.[24,21,22] The finding depicts a shifting trend that mirrors what is obtainable in many other parts of the world, with diabetes mellitus as the primary driver of CKD.[25,26]

The prevalence of CKD-aP in this study was 26.9%. This finding is notably similar to the prevalence of 26.7% reported by Falodun et al. [27] but lower than the prevalence of 40% and 42% reported by Berenice et al in the Benin Republic and Adejumo et al in Ondo, Nigeria, respectively.[23,28] This variance may be explained by the differences in study populations and methodologies. While our study was carried out in pre-dialysis CKD, those by Berenice et al and Adejumo et al were in CKD patients on maintenance haemodialysis and a mixed population (of both pre-dialysis CKD and those on maintenance dialysis), respectively.

Thirty-nine (39%) of participants had generalized pruritus, and pruritus was of moderate and mild severity in 73.9% and 15.2% of our study population, respectively. This is similar to reports by Falodun et al who reported mild to moderate pruritus severity in 97% of end-stage kidney disease patients managed conservatively and on maintenance dialysis.[28] Pruritus is believed to be more severe among those on dialysis. Interleukins are thought to be released during dialysis treatments following interaction of blood and dialyzer membranes and other parts of the extracorporeal system, contributing to and worsening pruritus severity.[29]

Xerosis was the most common skin change observed in those with pruritus, also similar to findings by Szepietowski et al in Poland and Falodun et al in Ibadan, Nigeria.[6,27] Xerosis is the most common dermatological manifestation observed in CKD and is caused by reduced sweat and sebaceous glands and medications (especially diuretics) used in the course of treatment. This may act as the precipitant for the itch-scratch cycle.[30] Xerosis in CKD is worsened by warmer climatic conditions resulting from reduced skin hydration and low humidity. [31]

There was no significant association between pruritus, gender, CKD aetiology and CKD stage, as well as between pruritus severity, gender, CKD aetiology and CKD stage. This finding is similar to reports by Solak et al, Adejumo et al and Towfik et al done in various CKD populations.[10,28,32] These results suggest that CKD-aP is a complex systemic complication that is likely driven by metabolic imbalances rather than demographic or disease-specific variables. It should therefore be evaluated and managed in all patients with CKD.

Recall bias, the subjective nature of the NRS among respondents, as well as exclusion of laboratory predictors such as serum calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone and haemoglobin were the limitations of this study. However, the strength of this study is that it is the only study that assessed CKD-aP exclusively among the pre-dialysis CKD population in Nigeria, to the best of our knowledge.

In conclusion, CKD-aP was relatively common among pre-dialysis CKD, and there was no association observed between pruritus, gender, CKD aetiology and CKD stage. It is therefore recommended that CKD-aP should be assessed periodically and managed accordingly in all patients with CKD, as this may improve their quality of life and reduce morbidity and mortality.
