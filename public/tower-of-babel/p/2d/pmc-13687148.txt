Integrated care pathway for the management of diabetic foot disease: a position statement from the Italian Diabetic Foot Study Group
Authors: Laura Giurato, Marco Meloni, Alessia Scatena, Roberto De Giglio, Giuseppe Murdolo, Marco Mastinu, Maurizio Gicchino, Desiree Gabriele, Roberto Anichini, Cesare Miranda, Luigi Uccioli, SID-AMD Diabetic Foot Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic foot is one of the most serious chronic complications of diabetes mellitus and reflects the systemic burden of the disease. Preventive strategies and structured management pathways are therefore essential elements of diabetes care. Lifetime occurrence of foot ulceration has been reported in nearly one-third of individuals with diabetes, and these lesions account for most non-traumatic lower-extremity amputations. Furthermore, the estimated five-year mortality following ulceration is 30%. This document aims to ensure an appropriate, multidisciplinary care pathway for patients with diabetic foot, tailored to clinical complexity and aligned with the different levels of care of Italian diabetes centers. The integrated model of diabetic foot care is based on the level of care that diabetes centers (local and hospital) can provide, including available personnel, expertise, and diagnostic and therapeutic resources to manage varying degrees of clinical complexity. Accordingly, three levels of care complexity, low, medium, and high, have been defined, with corresponding key diagnostic and therapeutic activities and healthcare professionals involved.Keywords: Diabetic foot, Care pathways, Diabetology setting, Multidisciplinary care

Introduction

Diabetic foot disease is among the most challenging complications of diabetes, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life, survival, and the use of healthcare resources. Over a lifetime, up to one-third of individuals with diabetes will develop foot complications, which contribute substantially to hospital admissions related to diabetes. Globally, foot ulceration is estimated to affect approximately 18 million people [1–3].

Ulceration represents a critical stage in the progression of diabetic foot, frequently preceding lower-limb amputation [4]. Despite advances in diagnostics and treatment, prognosis remains poor: the five-year survival rate is around 30% in patients with active diabetic foot ulcers, and following a major lower-limb amputation, mortality may reach 70% [5]. Complete healing occurs within 12 weeks in approximately one-third of ulcers [6]. While recurrence is common, affecting about 42% of patients within the first year and nearly 65% within three years [7, 8].

The development of foot ulcers is a result of multiple interacting factors. Peripheral neuropathy, structural foot deformities, and peripheral arterial disease are the primary causes of ulceration. Chronic kidney disease, especially dialysis, further raises the risk of ulcers [9, 10].

Optimal outcomes require early identification of at-risk patients, proactive preventive strategies, and a structured, multidisciplinary approach integrated into a coordinated care pathway [11].

Within Italy, the National Diabetes Plan (PND, 2012) identifies the diabetic foot as a key priority for improving diabetes care. The plan advocates for systematic foot risk assessment, timely intervention, enhancement of professional skills in foot care, and the development of coordinated care models to enable quick recognition and management of complications. Prevention of amputation involves regular foot and footwear assessments, early management of existing lesions, multidisciplinary treatment strategies, prompt detection and intervention for peripheral arterial disease, including revascularization when necessary, and ongoing follow-up for individuals with a history of ulceration [12].

Methods

The Italian diabetology network currently encompasses various types of facilities, including community outpatient services, hospital-based diabetes clinics, and diabetic foot reference centers.

Management of diabetic foot disease shows considerable variation across regions and healthcare settings, reflecting differences in socioeconomic context, local resources, and care standards. National surveys have identified persistent challenges, such as delayed referral to specialized foot units and limited access to urgent surgical procedures [13].

To evaluate current practice, we analyzed data from the Annals of the Associazione Medici Diabetologi (AMD), a nationwide quality assessment program active since 2006. In 2024, the records indicated that structured foot evaluation was documented in only 15.8% of patients [14].

To fill these gaps, a structured diagnostic and therapeutic pathway for the care of the diabetic foot should be integrated into the routine management of diabetes. Accurate and timely staging is essential to assign patients to the most appropriate level of care, ranging from routine foot risk assessment and early detection of ulcers to more complex interventions and specialist management.

Objective of the document

The current document aims to offer practical guidance for diabetologists in the management of diabetic foot disease. The focus is on three key aspects: early identification of patient-specific risk factors to prevent ulcer formation (primary prevention), coordinated multidisciplinary management of active lesions according to their severity (acute phase), and structured long-term follow-up for patients with prior ulcers or amputations (secondary prevention).

Implementation of systematic foot screening across all services within the national diabetes care network is essential. Effective management relies on close coordination among centers with different levels of expertise, with the intensity of interventions tailored to each center’s capacity to manage different clinical scenarios, including the presence and severity of ulcers, ischemia, and infection. Time allocation

When a dedicated outpatient setting is not available, screening activities may be organized within predefined appointment slots. This approach avoids prolonging routine diabetes visits and promotes structured follow-up.

Ulcer risk stratification

Patients with diabetes are at varying risk for foot ulceration, and the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) suggests stratifying this risk based on loss of protective sensation (LOPS), the presence of peripheral arterial disease, foot deformities, previous ulceration or amputation, and advanced renal disease treated with dialysis [15].

Risk stratification guides the definition of appropriate follow-up intervals and enables regular surveillance aimed at the timely identification and management of associated risk factors.

Management of patients with active lesions

The diabetologist, in cooperation with wound care nurses and trained podologists, is the main professional responsible for the integrated management of foot lesions, supported by specialist consultants in a multidisciplinary care pathway.

Defining an ulcer accurately is crucial, taking into account the depth of tissue involvement, the presence of ischemia, and the risk of infection. Frequently applied classification methods include the Wound, Ischemia, and foot Infection (WIfI) system and the IWGDF/IDSA criteria [16, 17].

At the end of each treatment, the diabetologist ensures that patients or caregivers receive specific education aimed at preventing ulcer recurrence, verifies adherence to protective measures, such as the regular use of therapeutic footwear, and schedules appropriate follow-up (see Table 3).

Priority access to high-complexity centers

Patients with complex clinical conditions, such as ischemic lesions requiring surgical or endovascular revascularization or infected lesions requiring surgical interventions (e.g., abscess drainage, debridement, ulcerectomy, sequestrectomy, or minor amputations), should have priority access to high-complexity service, as these procedures cannot be performed in medium-complexity care settings.

Additionally, if no improvement is observed after 4–6 weeks of standard care (defined as the absence of reparative signs and less than 50% reduction in ulcer diameter), the patient should be referred to a high-complexity diabetic foot center.

Management of complex infections

Integrated and timely management of soft tissue and bone infections is essential to reduce the risk of amputation. The diabetologist collaborates with the infectious disease specialist to manage targeted antibiotic therapy, with the vascular specialist or interventional radiologist to select the appropriate revascularization procedure, and with the radiologist to coordinate diagnostic investigations. A diabetologist with surgical expertise may independently perform wound debridement and surgical interventions when indicated.

Regenerative medicine in diabetic foot pathology: High-complexity centers have access to advanced therapeutic options, including regenerative therapies such as growth factors, stem cell and cellular therapies, as well as dermal and bone substitutes, biomaterials, and bioactive scaffolds for tissue regeneration [19, 20] (Table 4).

The diabetologist should ensure specific expertise in the clinical management of complex diabetic foot. To effectively coordinate the multidisciplinary team, the diabetologist should know different clinical fields (vascular surgery, wound care, antibiotic therapy, etc.), necessary to achieve shared therapeutic decisions and ensure the appropriate care pathway for each patient [21].

Furthermore, the diabetologist should remain up-to-date on the various therapeutic options and take responsibility for the education and training of any members included in the multidisciplinary team.

Orthotic therapy in patients with diabetes

Indications for shoes and insoles in primary prevention

In patients with diabetes, the use of inappropriate footwear is a major contributor to foot ulceration. While unsuitable shoes may trigger ulcer development, appropriate footwear can prevent it. Therapeutic footwear and custom-made insoles must meet functional and protective standards to ensure plantar load redistribution and protection of high-risk areas.

Therapeutic shoes and custom insoles should satisfy specific functional and protective requirements to ensure even distribution of plantar load, prevent trauma by accommodating foot deformities, and promote safe ambulation [22–24].

Footwear is an essential therapeutic device in the management of the diabetic foot, as important as glycemic control and ulcer care. As a true therapeutic intervention, it must be tailored to the individual patient and their clinical profile. The selection of shoes and insoles should be guided by ulcer risk level, according to the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) classification, while also considering physical activity, lifestyle, and individual patient needs [3].

For this risk class, which defines secondary prevention, it is essential to prescribe footwear and insoles with the specified features to prevent recurrent ulceration. Patients on dialysis also fall into this category, as dialysis is an independent risk factor for ulceration. Footwear and insoles should therefore be prescribed based on the presence or absence of deformities.

For at-risk patients, therapeutic footwear must be worn in all settings, both outdoors and at home, to ensure continuous foot protection. Home-use models are available, designed to accommodate insoles and featuring a closed heel to provide protection and pressure offloading.

Recommended digital tools

Diabetologists are advised to use an electronic medical record system (such as MetaClinic) to document and characterize patients during both preventive and acute phases. This structured, standardized, and integrable digital tool supports primary and secondary prevention as well as acute diabetic foot management, improving clinical effectiveness, traceability, and quality of care.

Training of healthcare professionals according to level of care

Healthcare professionals involved in diabetic foot management should receive training tailored to their specific role and the stage of disease.

The diabetologist must be trained in the assessment and management of the diabetic foot according to disease progression and complexity, through university-level Master’s programs and specialized courses, with periodic refresher training. Additionally, at medium- and high-complexity care levels, diabetologists should acquire basic and emergency surgical skills through dedicated specialized training programs.

Nurses and podiatrists should receive specialized training in diabetic foot management through university-level Master’s programs and targeted courses. Scientific societies play a key role in professional education and training.

Diabetologists involved in diabetic foot care, in addition to their clinical expertise, should actively contribute to the training of other healthcare professionals involved in patient management.

Discussion

The organization of diabetic foot care should be structured according to the level of clinical complexity, with clearly differentiated responsibilities across care settings.

At the low-complexity center, the primary objective is risk containment. This requires systematic foot risk stratification integrated into routine diabetes visits, alongside structured patient education focused on daily inspection, hygiene, and early recognition of warning signs. In this setting, prevention is not an adjunct activity but the principal clinical mandate, aimed at reducing both first-time ulceration and recurrence.

As clinical demands increase, medium-complexity services must expand their scope beyond prevention to include the management of non-complicated acute lesions. Here, the diabetologist assumes direct responsibility for early therapeutic interventions while maintaining vigilance for signs that require escalation of care. The ability to distinguish cases suitable for local management from those requiring referral becomes a critical competency.

High-complexity or specialized centers, equipped with dedicated Diabetic Foot Units, provide the structural and professional framework necessary for multidisciplinary management of advanced disease. In this context, the diabetologist functions as clinical coordinator, ensuring diagnostic coherence, therapeutic prioritization, and continuity across different specialist contributions. Leadership in this setting does not imply exclusivity of action but responsibility for integrating expertise within a multidisciplinary care plan [25].

Delays in accessing specialist care are largely due to organizational issues, including a lack of standardized referral criteria, a lack of community-based risk stratification, failure to recognize the lesion early, and the absence of clearly structured care pathways [26].

Across all levels of care, the diabetologist maintains a pivotal role; however, the intensity and nature of clinical responsibility should adapt to the patient’s degree of complexity. This dynamic allocation of responsibilities prevents both under-treatment in high-risk patients and unnecessary centralization of low-risk cases.

This document aims to outline a model of diabetic foot care as an example of a patient management pathway based on clinical risk stratification, in terms of lesion prevention and its timely management according to the level of clinical care, resources, timely referral, and proportional intensity of intervention.

Conclusion

Stratifying patients based on clinical complexity allows for more efficient distribution of care between community services and hospitals.

Each level of care plays a pivotal role in the management of diabetic foot disease. Preventive strategies, ranging from individual risk stratification for every person with diabetes to the treatment of pre-ulcerative conditions and the prescription of appropriate footwear, represent the most effective measures to reduce the risk of ulcer development.

Early identification of ulceration and its prompt management contribute to lowering the associated morbidity and mortality.

Patients with high clinical complexity benefit from management in specialized multidisciplinary centers, where access to advanced therapies can be guaranteed.

Finally, implementing standardized care pathways and structured training for healthcare professionals supports continuity of care and improves clinical efficacy at all levels of diabetes care (see Table 6).
