Analysis of the correlation between sleep spindles and cognitive impairment in patients with ischemic stroke
Authors: Yixi Zheng, Tianyu Jing, Liwen Xu, Shutong Sun, Wenyi Yu, Ruonan Liu, Gang Xu, Cheng Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep spindle (SS) are characteristic electroencephalographic hallmarks of nonrapid eye movement sleep (NREM), typically defined as oscillatory activity in the sigma frequency range (11–16 Hz) lasting 0.5–3.0 s. Research indicates that SS are involved in memory consolidation, promote memory and learning, and are significantly associated with physiological aging and cognitive decline. To explore the changes in the number, amplitude, and duration of SS in patients with acute ischemic stroke, to analyze the impact of SS on cognitive function in stroke patients, and to understand whether SS can serve as biomarkers for assessing cognitive function in stroke patients. This retrospective study included a total of 314 patients who underwent polysomnography (PSG) at the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University from March 2022 to February 2024 were selected and divided into a stroke group (229 patients) and a control group (85 patients). Baseline data, questionnaire scores, PSG parameters, and SS parameters were collected. Differences between the two groups were compared. Multiple linear regression analyse was conducted to evaluate the influencing factors of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score in patients with ischemic stroke. Compared with the control group, the stroke group had lower MoCA score, fewer SS in nonrapid-eye-movement sleep stage 2 (NREM2) and nonrapid-eye-movement sleep stage 3 (NREM3), lower SS indices, a shorter average duration of SS in N2 sleep, and a lower maximum amplitude of SS in the N3 stage. The multiple linear regression models showed that the MoCA score of stroke patients was significantly correlated with age, sleep efficiency and N2 sleep spindle (N2-SS) index. In stroke patients, there is a reduction in the number, a shorter duration, and a decrease in amplitude of SS. The decrease of spindle activity is one of the potential factors affecting the cognitive level of stroke patients. The change of N2-SS can be related to the degree of cognitive impairment in ischemic stroke patients. SS is expected to be a new objective physiological indicator to evaluate the cognitive function after stroke. However, this study represents an initial exploration of associations rather than causal relationships. Future research should focus on longitudinal designs to clarify whether interventions targeting SS activity could improve cognitive outcomes.Keywords: Stroke, Sleep spindles, Cognitive function, Poststroke cognitive impairment, Electroencephalography, Montreal cognitive assessmentSubject terms: Medical research, Neurology

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is caused by the blockage of cerebral blood vessels, which leads to interrupted cerebral blood flow. The resulting changes in blood flow, ischemia, hypoxia, and the toxic effects of excitatory amino acids can interfere with sleep-wake mechanisms1, resulting in alterations in sleep structure and a decline in sleep quality. A study on the topographic sleep electroencephalograph of stroke patients revealed that the frequencies of slow waves, theta waves, and SS in the infarcted area are lower than those on the unaffected side2, indicating a potential association between sleep electroencephalography (EEG) parameters and stroke.

SS are transient EEG rhythms, typically defined as oscillatory activity in the sigma frequency range (11–16 Hz) lasting 0.5–3.0 s3. SS are a hallmark of the second stage of NREM sleep and represent a unique EEG oscillatory event that occurs during NREM stage. SS originate from the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN), and GABAergic neurons of the TRN are believed to be involved in the generation of SS. The SS produced by the thalamus are propagated to the cortex via thalamocortical axons and are synchronized by glutamatergic thalamocortical projections4. Research indicates that the coordinated discharge patterns of thalamocortical neurons during SS activity are related to various brain functions5, including memory consolidation, maintenance of sleep quality and stability, and sensory processing.

An experiment in rodents revealed that stroke can alter the processing of sleep. Electrophysiological monitoring of rat models revealed that the precise coupling of SS and slow oscillations (SOs) can promote memory consolidation6. However, stroke disrupts this coupling, impairing sleep processing and consequently affecting memory and motor performance. Although animal studies have preliminarily confirmed the associations between stroke-induced cognitive impairments, such as memory loss and SS clinical research is still limited. We believe that SS are affected by ischemic stroke and that spindles may be among the factors influencing cognitive impairment after stroke. In this study, we further explored the changes in SSs in patients with ischemic stroke and their impact on cognitive function.

Methods

Subjects

This retrospective study included patients admitted to the Department of Neurology of the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University who underwent PSG as part of clinical care from March 2022 to February 2024. Our research team has previously conducted clinical studies involving stroke patients, and all participants have signed informed consent forms. Following renewed ethical approval (Ethics 2023-YKL09) from the Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University, we conducted a secondary analysis of the available data. Subjects selected for a diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke (within 2 weeks of symptom onset) were defined as the stroke group, and the diagnosis was based on the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Edition (ICD-11) (cited, available: https://icd.who.int/en accessed November 2021) and the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke7 were used as the criteria. The incidence time, head Magnetic Resonance imaging (MRI) and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) were used as the criteria. The control group comprised non-stroke patients admitted during the same period, primarily consisting of neurology department patients hospitalized for benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). This population demonstrated strong self-health awareness, with most participants recognizing the critical impact of sleep quality on overall wellness. Consequently, these individuals voluntarily underwent PSG monitoring as part of their comprehensive health evaluation. Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and patients with severe motor/sensory deficits (NIHSS > 15) were excluded.

Finally, we included 314 subjects, including 229 in the stroke group and 85 in the control group. Baseline data

We collected demographic information (including age and sex), body mass index (BMI), smoking and drinking history, and comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease from all patients.

Questionnaire assessment

All participants completed relevant questionnaires on the day of PSG. The Chinese version of the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) was used to assess subjective daytime sleepiness. The ESS consists of 8 items that assess the tendency to fall asleep in different situations in daily life8, with a score above 10 considered to have excessive daytime sleepiness. The REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ) was used to obtain information on patients’ nighttime sleep behavior disorders. The questionnaire included 13 items and the information was derived from the patients’ self-assessment. The Chinese version of the RBDSQ had high sensitivity, specificity and reliability and could be used as a tool for screening RBD patients in China9. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) in its psychometrically established Chinese version was used to evaluate anxiety and depression levels. The HADS contains two subscales (HADS-A and HADS-D), both with 7 items, higher scores indicate more severe symptoms10. The culturally adapted Chinese edition of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to collect patients’ self-assessment of subjective sleep quality. PSQI includes a self-assessment scale consisting of 7 components, including Subjective sleep quality, Sleep latency, Sleep duration, Habitual sleep efficiency, Sleep disturbances, Use of sleeping medication, and Daytime dysfunction. Previous research has shown that the Chinese version of the PSQI is a reliable measure of patients’ sleep quality and sleep disturbances11. The scores of the patients was performed by the professional sleep doctors.

Cognitive scales

The cognitive assessment scales used included the MoCA and the subjective cognitive decline questionnaire-9 (SCD-Q9). The MoCA is an effective tool for screening mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and covers areas such as visuospatial abilities and executive function, memory, attention, language, abstract thinking, calculation, and orientation, with a total score of 30 points. A higher score indicates better overall cognitive function, with a score of ≥ 26 considered normal12. To facilitate a more convenient and effective assessment of subjects, this study used the Beijing version of the MoCA tailored to the local context and language habits. The Beijing version of the MoCA has been validated in Chinese populations, it demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance, exhibiting a sensitivity of 95.35% and specificity of 55.56% in detecting cognitive impairment13.

The SCD-Q9 is used to assess the degree of cognitive function decline and consists of 2 dimensions with a total of 9 items, including overall memory function and temporal comparison (OTC, 4 items) and daily activity ability (DAA, 5 items). The total score is 9 points, with higher scores indicating more severe subjective cognitive impairment14,15. The SCD-Q9 Chinese version showed Cronbach’s α = 0.81 in prior studies16.

PSG and EEG

PSG was performed within one week of each patient’s admission to the hospital. The subjects underwent continuous overnight monitoring in a dark, interference-free, independent sleep monitoring room. On the day of monitoring, the consumption of alcohol, coffee, and caffeinated beverages, as well as the use of sedatives or stimulants, was prohibited. A PSG device produced by SOMNOmedics GmbH in Germany was used, with sleep variables obtained from EEG, electrooculography (EOG), and electromyography (EMG). The EEG monitoring utilized frontal, central, and occipital leads17, referencing the mastoid (C3M2, F3M2, O1M2, C4M1, F4M1, and O2M1) derived. Sleep respiratory variables were obtained from flow (nasal pressure sensors, oral‒nasal thermistors), chest and abdominal respiratory effort, and pulse oximetry. In order to ensure the smooth shape of brain waves, filter pre-processing was performed using 0.5–30 Hz before sleep data analysis. The macro structure of sleep and the marking of sleep events are interpreted manually by qualified professional PSG technicians. The interpretation standards and the calculation of related indexes are all based on the standards of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM)18.

Nocturnal sleep-related parameters, including total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency, wake-time after sleep onset (WASO), sleep onset latency, N1/N2/N3/REM period percentage, the respiratory effort-related arousal index (RERAI), apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), mean pulse oxygen saturation (Mean SpO2), minimum pulse oxygen saturation (Min-SpO2), and periodic limb movements index (PLMS), were continuously collected from the subjects.

The judgment criteria and collection of SS refer to relevant literature19, and the spindles are extracted as independent signals. Certified sleep technologists performed visual inspection and verification of all sleep EEG data, following standard preprocessing, all parameters were automatically derived using the analytical software of the PSG system (SOMNOmedics GmbH, Germany) sleep recording system. SS primarily originate from the central lobe (C4:M1)20. C represents the central area, and the electrode position is located 1 cm inward from the central groove.The EEG data was transformed into the frequency domain using the fast fourier transform (FFT) and then filtered by 11–16 Hz, and then the amplitude threshold of the filtered EEG signal was monitored. The parameters were all obtained via the automatic analysis software of the SOMNO sleep recorder. The spindle index is calculated based on the number of SS and the duration of sleep in each sleep stage. Finally, we collected the number of spindles, the spindle index, average spindle duration (in seconds) and the overall maximum spindle amplitude (in µV) of N2 and N3 stages. The main parameters to describe the characteristics of spindle incidence in clinical practice are spindle density or spindle index. Spindle density is usually defined as the ratio of the number of spindles to the number of minutes per stage21,22. There seems to be no uniform standard for the definition of the spindle index. Some studies define the spindle index as the number of spindles per frame (30 s) in the sleep stage23, while others define it as the average number of spindles per hour24. This study adopted the latter definition. The spindle index of N2 and N3 stages is calculated as the ratio of the number of spindles to the number of hours in each stage. Given the multidimensional nature of sleep-cognition interactions in stroke, we collected diverse metrics spanning demographic, polysomnographic, neurophysiological, and cognitive domains. Data analysis

The data were analyzed via the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). First, we conducted descriptive analyses between the two groups. Continuous variable data that met the criteria for normality and homogeneity of variance are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent samples tests. Data that did not meet the criteria for normal distribution or homogeneity of variance were expressed as the median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)] and comparisons between groups were made via a nonparametric test (Mann‒Whitney test). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov (K-S) test was used to analyse the normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the χ2 test. The influencing factors of MoCA score in patients with ischemic stroke were investigated via multiple linear regression model. The multiple linear regression models were adjusted for baseline data (age, sex, BMI, comorbidities) and sleep breathing parameters (AHI). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the relevant indicators of ischemic stroke patients with non-stroke patients, and studied the relationship between MoCA score and SS activity in 229 stroke patients by regression analysis. We found that cognitive decline in stroke patients was associated with a reduction in overnight SS activity.

Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is a series of cognitive disorders that occur following a stroke. It refers to a decline in cognitive abilities that can manifest as various cognitive disorders, and it is considered a component of vascular cognitive impairment. Patients typically exhibit impairments in functions such as visuospatial abilities, executive function, attention, memory, and language skills25. Research shows that more than one-third of stroke survivors have a significant degree of cognitive impairment within the first month after stroke26. The prevalence of PSCI in the first year after the event is 38%27. This study assessed the cognitive function of subjects using the MoCA scale and the SCD-Q9. The results indicated that the MoCA scores of stroke patients, as well as the sub-items related to spatial and executive abilities, attention, and memory, decreased. However, there was no significant difference in SCD-Q9. The absence of significant differences in SCD-Q9 scores may reflect reduced self-awareness of cognitive deficits in stroke patients. In contrast, the MoCA objectively captured impairments in executive function, attention, and memory. Cognitive impairment in stroke patients is related to multiple factors. Reports indicate that in addition to demographic factors such as age and race, hypertension, blood sugar levels, atrial fibrillation, smoking, alcohol consumption, and chronic kidney disease are associated with cognitive impairment after a stroke28.

Sleep, as an independent factor, is closely related to stroke, and there is a bidirectional relationship between sleep and stroke29. On one hand, sleep disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) induce oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction through intermittent hypoxia, promoting the instability of atherosclerotic plaques, while sympathetic overactivation causes disruption of blood pressure circadian rhythm, both mechanisms collectively elevate stroke risk. On the other hand, post-stroke thalamocortical circuit damage directly impairs sleep spindle generation, and neuroinflammation exacerbates sleep fragmentation by inhibiting GABAergic neurotransmission. Subsequently, post-stroke thalamic injury directly suppresses SS activity and triggers neuroinflammation30, leading to reduced slow-wave sleep and consequently impairing hippocampal-dependent memory consolidation. Simultaneously, declining prefrontal executive function triggers circadian rhythm disturbances that further intensify sleep fragmentation. Our research found that ischemic stroke disrupted patients’ sleep structure and reduced the activity of SS, and patients with ischemic stroke performed worse on cognitive assessments. Previous studies consistently report poorer nighttime sleep quality in stroke patients31, characterized by reduced total sleep time, increased awakenings after sleep onset, and diminished slow-wave sleep proportion. Our study revealed that stroke patients had shorter TST and longer WASO than non-stroke patients did, which is consistent with the previous result in terms of shorter TST. We also found that the stroke group had a lower proportion of patients in the N3 stage than did the control group, although there were no statistically significant differences in sleep structure between the two groups. Finally, we further observed that stroke patients had higher AHI, RERAI, and PLMS index. The mechanisms behind sleep disturbances and severely disrupted sleep following a stroke are complex. The current understanding suggests that stroke-associated sleep apnea, hypopnea and sleep-related hypoxemia are linked to damage in respiratory control centers within the brainstem, as well as in areas such as the frontal lobe, internal capsule, and basal ganglia32. This damage can lead to paralysis of respiratory muscles, including the intercostal and diaphragmatic muscles. Intermittent respiratory disturbances during the night, along with micro arousals, can cause sleep fragmentation and a decrease in deep sleep. The disruption of sleep‒wake cycles is believed to be associated with imbalances in neurotransmitter regulation and alterations in inflammatory markers33. In summary, these data indicate that stroke patients have poor sleep continuity and that the interruption of sleep continuity is the main reason for their poor quality nighttime sleep. It is worth mentioning that in terms of subjective questionnaire measurement, including patients’ subjective overall sleep quality, daytime sleepiness degree and sleep behavior, this study only found that the stroke group had lower PSQI-daytime dysfunction score, and the other questionnaire scores showed no significant difference compared with the control group. Previous studies have suggested that objective sleep parameters lack consistency with subjective scores, and age-related changes in sleep quality may affect elderly patients’ perception of sleep quality34, while subjective scores may not fully reflect changes in objective sleep quality.

This study revealed that, compared with non-stroke patients, stroke patients presented changes in the number, density, duration, and amplitude of sleep spindles during the N2 and N3 sleep stages, characterized by a decrease in quantity and frequency, as well as a reduced duration and amplitude. Clearly, stroke significantly disrupts the generation of SS. Previous studies have shown that the intensity and consistency of spindle activity in stroke patients are significantly reduced2,35 and that brain damage can affect the spontaneous generation of SS activity. Our results are consistent with previous conclusions, and we also found that the impact of stroke on SS in the N2 and N3 stages differed, the duration of N2-SS in the stroke group was shorter than that in the control group, whereas the amplitude of N3-SS decreased. N3 is characterized primarily by high-amplitude slow waves, which may be more susceptible to changes, and the specific reasons for this require further research in the future. SS, as one of the electrophysiological characteristics of sleep, are related to sleep quality and homeostasis, promoting sleep continuity and reducing wake time after sleep onset36. A reduction in sleep spindles typically leads to the occurrence of sleep apnea, excessive daytime sleepiness, and insomnia37,38. The impairment of SS is one of the main factors contributing to the interruption of sleep continuity. Based on the findings, we speculate that cortical lesions caused by stroke affect the process of spindle generation, ultimately leading to adverse effects on the sleep quality of patients.

There is an increasing amount of research indicating a relationship between sleep and cognition39–41. A cross-sectional study of patients with first-time strokes revealed that excessively long or short TST, daytime sleepiness, and poor sleep quality are risk factors for cognitive dysfunction42. Another study on patients with mild ischemic stroke indicated that years of education, TST, and minimum SpO2 are independent risk factors for PSCI43. In our research, sleep efficiency was significantly correlated with the MoCA score, indicating that this factor affects cognitive function in stroke patients beyond previously identified variables. Given that OSA is an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment44, we included the AHI in the regression model. Although AHI was higher in stroke patients compared to controls, we did not find an association with cognition in regression. We speculate that because the cognitive measurement tool only uses the MoCA scale, it may have insufficient sensitivity or specific defects, and a more comprehensive cognitive assessment is needed. In addition, studies have shown that the negative effects of pathologic AHI on cognitive function in older adults were less pronounced than in younger adults45. The average age of stroke patients in this study is older so the effect of sleep disordered breathing on cognitive function in these subjects may be limited, and the specific mechanism remains to be further explored.

Regarding spindles and cognition, we found that in the linear regression model, a decrease in cognition represented by the MOCA score was significantly associated with the N2-SS index, suggesting that a lower N2-SS index in stroke patients appears to be associated with worse cognition. It can be seen that the reduction of SS in stroke patients affects the consolidation and stability of memory, resulting in impairment of cognitive functions such as memory. In addition to parameters such as quantity, density, index, and other SS characteristics, the amplitude of SS can also facilitate learning. The amplitude of individual spindle waves is believed to promote the activation of neurons in the hippocampus and the visual cortex region involved in scene selection46. SS reflect rhythmic brain network activity47, and good brain function and an optimal external sleep environment are essential.

Furthermore, the temporal coupling between spindle waves is an important factor in memory consolidation during sleep48. Animal experiments have revealed the underlying pathological mechanisms involved. Research has revealed a significant positive correlation between the recovery of SO-nested spindles in rats after stroke and improvements in task performance5. It is evident that the number of SO-nested spindles decreases after a stroke and increases during later recovery. The disruption of SO-nested spindles coupling can impair the memory consolidation of wakeful experiences related to sleep48. A subsequent clinical study also confirmed a relative decrease in SO-nested spindles in stroke patients by analyzing their EEG data49. Our research suggests that the N2-SS index can serve as a new biomarker for patients’ cognitive function. Specifically, a drop in the N2-SS index predicts lower MoCA score. This finding supports the link between SS and cognitive function. The Stroke Roundtable Consortium proposed to designate the first 24 h as the hyperacute phase, the first 7 days as the acute phase, the first 3 months as the early sub-acute phase, the months 4–6 as the late sub-acute phase, and from 6 months on as the chronic phase50. Each stage of stroke interacts bidirectionally with sleep and sleep microstructure. During the acute phase, slow-wave sleep facilitates the clearance of neurotoxic metabolites51, while suppression of REM sleep is associated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines that may impede early neural repair52. In the subacute phase, SS density serves as a predictive biomarker for motor recovery through thalamocortical circuit remodeling5, and comorbid OSA exacerbates white matter hyperintensity progression. Most critically, sleep fragmentation during the chronic phase (> 3 months post-onset) independently predicts 2-year stroke recurrence53. These findings underscore sleep microstructure as a novel and actionable therapeutic target to optimize functional outcomes in stroke survivors.

The limitations of this study is that it is a small, single-center and retrospective study, which limits the recording of some other variables that may affect cognition, such as participants’ socioeconomic and lifestyle54. In addition, the record of lesions in stroke patients was missing in this study. The study pointed out that ischemia in multiple lesions and temporoparietaloccipital lobes are factors of cognitive impairment55, and differences in the location, number and size of infarcts may affect the observed results. Future studies should prioritize lesion-symptom mapping to clarify differential effects of infarct topography on sleep parameters, particularly contrasting thalamic and brainstem localizations. Integrating advanced neuroimaging with PSG would enable identification of neural substrates underlying location-specific sleep impairments such as SS deficits or apnea severity. For the sake of accuracy and completeness of the MoCA scale score, stroke patients with combined language comprehension and expression disorders, such as aphasia, were not included in this study, and further assessment of these patients was lacking. Differences in the location, number, and size of infarcts may affect the observed results. Also, we mainly studied the spindle activity in the central region without examining the EEG of the frontal and occipital regions and different hemispheres, which may make the research results insufficient. Due to the laterality of sleep oscillation49, there may be differences in SS variations across different regions. In addition, we only used the MoCA scale and SCD-Q9 for overall cognitive assessment, missing the assessment of cognitive subdomains, such as pattern recognition memory (PRM), Spatial Recognition Memory (SRM), Spatial Working Memory (SRM), verbal working memory, etc. Related research has indicated that spindle waves in the prefrontal cortex of OSA patients are associated with verbal working memory, whereas spindle waves in the occipital region are related to visual memory56, which requires further exploration in stroke patients. Furthermore, SS can be categorized by frequency into slow spindles (11–13 Hz) and fast spindles (13–16 Hz), with fast spindles being more beneficial for learning and memory consolidation than slow spindles57. Future studies could further differentiate these categories. Finally, although we excluded patients with chronic sleep disorders and included sleep parameters in regression analysis to reach preliminary conclusions, the complex association between sleep disorders and SS after stroke the complex association between sleep disorders and spindles after stroke has not been fully understood. Broader factor consideration and more thorough evaluation and experimental design are critical.

This study focuses only on sleep characteristics during the acute phase and does not conduct a longitudinal study on specific changes in sleep patterns throughout stroke recovery. Therefore, our findings may not fully reflect the evolving relationship between sleep and cognition during recovery. Given that neuroplasticity during the subacute phase may profoundly affect the interaction between sleep and cognition, future research should track brain wave patterns from the hyperacute phase through to the chronic phase. Additionally, using hospitalized patients with BPPV as a control group, rather than healthy individuals living in the community, has limitations. Vestibular disorders can affect sleep structure, and factors related to the hospital environment, such as noise and lighting, can independently cause sleep disturbances. Therefore, this control group might not accurately represent healthy individuals in the community. Notably, post-stroke aphasia predominantly occurs in patients with left-hemisphere lesions58 due to typically left-lateralized language networks. Consequently, excluding aphasic patients may lead to an overrepresentation of right-hemisphere strokes, this introduces selection bias that could limit the generalizability of our findings to all stroke patients.

In summary, this study discussed the changes of SS and their relationship with cognitive function after stroke and provided a certain reference for clinical practice. Traditional cognitive function assessment usually relies on cognitive scales, which have limitations such as complexity and subjectivity, especially for patients with speech disorders. As a more objective physiological indicator, SS has potential diagnostic and therapeutic value. The identification and analysis of SS is susceptible to many factors such as individual differences, resulting in their consistency and reliability in different patient populations that have not been fully validated and are still in the exploratory stage. Future studies still need to make further progress in standardized measurement methods, data interpretation and clinical applicability. SS hold promise as a new objective physiological indicator for evaluating cognitive function after a stroke. Should future trials validate SS as a biomarker, sleep-targeted interventions could improve post-stroke cognitive outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study found that SS activity in stroke patients may be impaired in terms of number, duration, and magnitude, which in turn affects the continuity of sleep. The decrease of SS activity is one of the potential influencing factors of cognitive decline in stroke patients. The change of sleep spindle index in N2 stage is related to the degree of cognitive impairment in ischemic stroke patients. This study provides preliminary evidence for the relationship between SS and cognitive function in stroke patients, but further research is needed to verify its validity and explore spindle-targeted interventions to improve cognitive impairment and overall prognosis after stroke.
