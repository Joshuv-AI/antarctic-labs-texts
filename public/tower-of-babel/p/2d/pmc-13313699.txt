Exploratory 6-year prediction of LTCI certification using KCL data: A decision tree analysis in a retrospective observational study
Authors: Yuto Owa, Noriki Yamaya, Tomomi Furukawa, Kazuki Kitazawa, Kazuki Hirao, Takaaki Fujita, Fusae Tozato, Yayoi Kitamura, Naoyuki Oi, Tsutomu Iwaya, Kenji Tsuchiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amid the progression of an aging society, it is essential to develop a long-term predictive model capable of distinguishing between older adults with high and low risks of requiring Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) services. However, previous models were limited in that they could not simultaneously examine multiple and heterogeneous relationships, despite the fact that risk factors interact in complex and multifaceted ways. In addition, the existing model was restricted to a short-term prediction span. This study aimed to develop a predictive model for LTCI certification using Kihon Checklist data and a 6-year follow-up data with a decision tree analysis. This study was designed as a retrospective cohort, longitudinal, and observational study. Data were obtained from 3263 individuals aged 65 and older in a community-dweller. The dependent variable was LTCI certification status after 6 years. Independent variables included 6 Kihon Checklist domains, age, and gender. Decision tree analysis and a confusion matrix were used to make and evaluate the model. The decision tree model identified age, gender, and depression risk as key predictive factors. The model demonstrated moderate specificity (79.4%) and high negative predictive value (97.1%) but limited sensitivity (52.2%) and positive predictive value (11.1%). These findings indicate that the model performs better in identifying individuals unlikely to require LTCI certification than in detecting future LTCI users. Although it may provide preliminary population-level risk stratification over a 6-year horizon, its predictive performance is insufficient for standalone clinical or policy decision-making, and further refinement and validation are required.

1. Introduction

The enhancement of life expectancy on a global scale has given rise to an increase in the proportion of older adults within the population.[1,2] Japan is characterized by a faster rate of population aging than other countries worldwide,[3] being in a position to serve as a model for other nations facing similar demographic challenges. These nations might be able to develop and implement strategies that promote healthy aging by observing the Japanese example. In 2023, 29.1% of Japan’s population was aged 65 and older.[4] To alleviate the burden of healthcare costs in an aging society, Japan implemented the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) system. The LTCI certification process consists of a two-step evaluation. The first step is a computer-based assessment, incorporating information from a home-based survey conducted by municipal staff and a physician’s clinical evaluation. In the second step, the Long-Term Care Certification Board classifies individuals into 3 categories: “Condition of Need for Long-Term Care,” “Needed Support Condition,” or “Nonqualification.”

Since the LTCI system is publicly funded, an increase in certified individuals places a growing financial burden on the system.[5] Addressing these challenges requires reducing the number of people requiring LTCI certification while promoting healthy aging. It is important to distinguish between older adults at high and low risk of requiring LTCI certification, and reallocating social resources from those at low risk to those at high risk as part of early preventive interventions represents an effective strategy.

One tool used for this purpose is the Kihon Checklist (KCL), a self-administered questionnaire developed by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare to identify frail older adults.[6] The KCL was translated into English,[7] Chinese,[8] and Spanish,[9] and has become used worldwide for comprehensive screening of frailty. The KCL has been used for screening for LTCI certification along with computer-based evaluation.[5] The KCL consists of 25 Yes/No questions, where higher scores indicate greater functional decline.[10] It assesses 7 domains: activities of daily living (questions 1–20: excluding the mood domain), physical strength, nutritional status, oral function, houseboundness, cognitive function, and mood.[7,10]

Previous studies have used the KCL to predict LTCI certification through logistic regression analysis. Ito et al[11] found that KCL items could screen for LTCI risk 3 months later, while Kamegaya et al[12] demonstrated that KCL scores reflected long-term care risk 3 years later. However, these studies had limitations: multiple regression and logistic regression assume independence and linearity among variables, which may not fully capture complex relationships.[13] Furthermore, traditional statistical methods struggle to handle heterogeneous relationships within datasets, making it difficult to address individual differences.[13] Consequently, existing KCL-based LTCI prediction models have been limited in that they could not simultaneously examine multiple and heterogeneous relationships, despite the fact that risk factors interact in complex and multifaceted ways.

To overcome these challenges, decision tree analysis using the Exhaustive Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detector (E-CHAID) offers a more flexible approach.[14] Unlike traditional statistical methods, E-CHAID does not rely on assumptions of independence, linearity, or homogeneity.[15,16] It can dynamically model data relationships, making it particularly effective in older individuals’ care research.[13] To date, only one study has applied E-CHAID to predict LTCL certification 2 years later using the KCL.[13] However, this study had 2 key limitations:

Short follow-up period: A 2-year prediction window may not sufficiently capture long-term risks.

Lack of model evaluation: The study did not assess the practical utility of the decision tree model.

Long-term follow-up studies are crucial for identifying older adults at risk of LTCI certification and determining key predictive factors for early intervention. Previous research on aging-related outcomes commonly employed a 6-year timeframe, including studies on disability incidence (i.e., a new LTCI service requirement certification) and mortality[17] and the relationship between frailty and long-term care risk.[18] Additionally, individuals aged 65 to 70 often experience major life transitions such as retirement and reemployment, further underscoring the importance of long-term follow-up.[19] Additionally, the decision model derived from Sato study[13] has yet to be subjected to evaluation, thereby leaving its practical utility uncertain.

The purpose of this study was to explore whether a simple and implementable model based solely on routinely available municipal screening data (KCL and basic demographic characteristics) could provide medium-term population-level stratification in a 6-year follow-up survey. Decision tree analysis with E-CHAID was employed to address the limitations of traditional statistical methods. The resulting predictive model may aid in screening LTCI certification risk, enabling municipalities to stratify older adults according to their relative probability of LTCI certification.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population and participants

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort, longitudinal, and observational study. This study used anonymized data provided by Iiyama City, Nagano Prefecture, Japan. The city has conducted an annual municipal government survey of older adults in Iiyama City, targeting older adults who were not certified for LTCI. Iiyama City is the most aged rural city in Nagano Prefecture, with a significant elderly population. Therefore, it serves as a suitable data source for this study, providing a sufficient sample size for developing a model to predict LTCI certification. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagano University of Health and Medicine (No. 2020-4). The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting statement[20] and the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis-Artificial Intelligence statement[21] for prediction model studies.

We included older individuals aged 65 years or older as of November 1, 2014, who were not certified for LTCI. The follow-up period spanned from November 1, 2014, to October 31, 2021 (Fig. 1). The municipal government distributed the KCL to 6035 eligible older individuals on November 1, 2014, and subsequently collected responses. A total of 5524 individuals responded, yielding a collection rate of 91.5%. The data were anonymized and provided to the corresponding author’s affiliation as unlinked data.

Flow diagram illustrating the selection process for the analyzed sample.

Of the 5524 respondents, 900 individuals with 1 or more missing values were excluded, resulting in a sample size of 4624. Subsequently, 1000 individuals who were certified for LTCI between November 1, 2014, and October 31, 2020, were excluded, leaving a sample size of 3624. Furthermore, 323 individuals who were not certified for LTCI but either died, moved out, or were otherwise excluded between November 1, 2014, and October 31, 2020, and an additional 38 individuals who were excluded for similar reasons between November 1, 2020, and October 31, 2021, were removed. As the outcome was defined as LTCI certification status at 6 years among surviving residents, participants who died or moved away during the follow-up period were excluded from the final analysis. Thus, the final number of valid samples analyzed was 3263.

In a sensitivity analysis, of the 5524 respondents, 1315 individuals who were certified for LTCI between November 1, 2014, and October 31, 2020, were excluded, leaving a sample size of 4209. Furthermore, 395 individuals who were not certified for LTCI but either died, moved out, or were otherwise excluded between November 1, 2014, and October 31, 2020, and an additional 47 individuals excluded for similar reasons between November 1, 2020, and October 31, 2021, were removed. Thus, the final number of valid samples analyzed was 3767.

2.2. Variables used in the analysis

The independent variables included KCL scores, age, and gender as of November 1, 2014. This selection was intentional and reflects the data routinely available at the municipal level. The objective of this study was not to construct a clinically comprehensive predictive model incorporating comorbidities or medical history, but rather to evaluate whether a parsimonious model based on publicly administered screening data could provide medium-term stratification. The dependent variable was the presence or absence of LTCI certification during the year from November 1, 2020, to October 31, 2021. LTCI certification data included both individuals classified as being in a “Condition of Need for Long-Term Care” and those in a “Needed Support Condition” as determined by the Long-Term Care Certification Board. Six of the 7 KCL domains were utilized as independent variables: physical strength, nutritional status, oral function, houseboundness, cognitive function, and mood. The activities of daily living domain was excluded due to the absence of an established calculation method.[22] The activities of daily living domain of the KCL was not included as a predictor in the decision tree analysis. The activities of daily living items primarily reflect existing functional limitations that are closely related to the criteria used for LTCI certification. Including these items in the model could therefore capture current functional dependency rather than early risk factors for future LTCI certification. The KCL was originally designed as a screening tool for identifying frailty and risk of functional decline among community-dwelling older adults before the onset of severe disability. Each domain was assessed using a predefined calculation method to determine low physical strength, low nutritional status, low oral function, houseboundness, low cognitive function, and depression risk (see Online Resource for details on the calculation methods).[22,23] The results were then converted into dummy variables, with 0 indicating the absence of a low domain and 1 indicating the presence of a low domain, for use in the analysis.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The 3263 participants were randomly assigned to 2 datasets: 70% (2284) for the training dataset and 30% (979) for the test dataset, following previous research methodology.[24,25] The training dataset was used to develop the predictive model, while the test dataset was used to evaluate its performance. Among the study samples, 4.6% were certified for LTCI, while 95.4% were not. This class imbalance posed a potential risk of reducing the predictive accuracy of the classifier. It is well-documented that class imbalances in target variables can significantly impact the performance of machine learning algorithms.[26,27] To address this challenge, 70% of the training data underwent resampling using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique for Nominal Continuous (SMOTE-NC) to balance the minority classes.[28] However, no further optimization for maximizing sensitivity was performed, as the primary objective was interpretability and practical applicability.

Following resampling, decision tree analysis was performed using E-CHAID to identify risk factors for LTCI certification in a hierarchical and composite manner. E-CHAID is increasingly used in medical outcome prediction and overcomes the limitations associated with the basic Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detector (CHAID).[14,29] The CHAID framework has been widely used as an exploratory data analysis technique for identifying complex interaction structures in large datasets. This method was considered suitable for the present study for several reasons. First, previous studies have applied CHAID-based approaches to examine the relationship between LTCI certification and KCL domains, suggesting its relevance for this research context. Second, the incidence of severe disability has been shown to increase substantially with age, approximately doubling with every 5-year increase,[30] indicating the potential presence of hierarchical and interacting risk factors. Unlike binary decision tree methods, E-CHAID allows multi-branch splits, which enables more flexible modeling of such complex relationships. The maximum tree depth was set to automatic, with a minimum parent node size of 500 and a minimum child node size of 250. The minimum parent and child node sizes were set at 500 and 250, respectively. These values were intentionally selected to produce a parsimonious and interpretable tree structure rather than a highly complex model. Larger node size thresholds reduce tree depth and help prevent overfitting, particularly in epidemiological datasets with imbalanced outcomes. In the context of municipal-level policy implementation, a simpler and more stable decision structure was considered preferable to a deep tree that may capture complex interactions but lacks interpretability and reproducibility. Previous studies using CHAID and E-CHAID have also employed relatively large minimum node sizes. For example, Koç et al[31] reported that increasing minimum case thresholds (500/250) resulted in shallower trees without substantial deterioration in goodness-of-fit indices, and stable performance was observed within a wide range of node size parameters. Similarly, Zubieta et al[32] applied large node size thresholds (600/300) in a hospital-based decision tree analysis. The significance level for split nodes was set at P < .05.

Subsequently, the decision tree-derived models were validated using a confusion matrix on the 30% test dataset to assess classification performance. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets to perform internal validation. The decision tree model was developed using the training dataset and subsequently evaluated using the independent testing dataset. This hold-out validation strategy was selected to approximate the performance of the model when applied to unseen data. In addition, this approach enabled us to present a single interpretable decision tree structure derived from the training data and evaluated in an independent dataset. In decision tree models, repeated resampling approaches such as k-fold cross-validation, particularly when combined with oversampling methods such as SMOTE-NC to address class imbalance, can produce substantially different tree structures across iterations. Because the primary aim of this study was to derive a transparent and interpretable rule-based model that could potentially be applied in municipal health planning, we prioritized the stability and interpretability of a single final decision tree. Nevertheless, more robust validation procedures, including cross-validation, temporal validation, and external validation using independent datasets, should be conducted in future studies to further assess the generalizability and robustness of the model.

In the primary analysis, participants with missing values were excluded (complete-case analysis). To assess potential selection bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which missing values were imputed using median substitution. The decision tree analysis was then conducted using the same modeling procedures. We compared the resulting tree structure and classification performance with those of the primary analysis to evaluate robustness. Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (ver. 3.11.7) for SMOTE-NC implementation and IBM SPSS Statistics 27 for decision tree analysis.

3. Result

3.1. Demographic characteristics of the dataset

The study included 3263 participants with a mean age of 72.0 ± 6.0 years, comprising 1545 men and 1718 women. Among them, 152 were certified for LTCI, while 3111 were noncertified during the observation period (November 1, 2020 to October 31, 2021). In 2014, the distribution of participants across different domains was as follows: low physical strength (17.2%), low nutritional status (1.0%), low oral function (12.4%), houseboundness (6.5%), low cognitive function (26.5%), and depression risk (14.7%).

3.2. LTCI-certified predictive model

A decision tree analysis using E-CHAID was conducted on 4356 training datasets resampled with SMOTE-NC, identifying 10 classification patterns (Fig. 2). Age emerged as the most significant primary predictor, effectively dividing participants into 3 major risk tiers: Group A (≤70 years), Group B (71–78 years), and Group C (≥79 years) (Table 1).

In the sensitivity analysis using median imputation for missing values, minor differences in node composition were observed compared with the primary complete-case analysis. However, the overall hierarchical structure of the decision tree remained consistent. Age continued to be the primary splitting variable. Among individuals in their late 70s, depression risk was identified as a key branching factor, whereas among those in their 80s, low physical strength remained an important determinant (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). The classification performance metrics were comparable to those of the primary analysis: an accuracy of 78.9%, a sensitivity of 67.2%, a positive predictive value of 14.3%, a specificity of 79.4%, and a negative predictive value of 97.9%. This indicates that the predictive characteristics of the model were largely unchanged after imputation.

4. Discussion

This study should be interpreted as an exploratory risk stratification analysis using municipal health survey data rather than a finalized prediction model intended for universal implementation. In this study, we developed a decision tree model to classify LTCI certification status 6 years later. Although the model achieved moderate specificity (79.4%) and a high negative predictive value (97.1%), its sensitivity (52.2%) and positive predictive value (11.1%) were low. The primary analysis was based on a complete-case approach, which may introduce selection bias if excluded participants differed systematically from those included. To address this concern, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using median and mode imputation for missing data. Although minor differences in node composition were observed, the overall hierarchical structure and performance metrics remained largely consistent (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). Although more advanced approaches, such as multiple imputation, could enhance the methodological rigor of future research, these findings suggest that the main conclusions are relatively robust regardless of the method used to handle missing data.

The prevalence of LTCI certification in this cohort was 4.6%, resulting in a highly imbalanced dataset. Although SMOTE-NC was applied to the training data, the model demonstrated limited sensitivity (52.2%) and positive predictive value (11.1%). These results indicate that the model has a limited ability to detect individuals who will require LTCI certification in the future and that its utility is substantially constrained, particularly due to its low sensitivity and positive predictive value. Regarding specificity and negative predictive value, the model showed a specificity of 79.4% and a negative predictive value of 97.1%. However, the high negative predictive value should be interpreted with caution because LTCI certification occurred in only 4.6% of the study population. In populations with a low event rate, negative predictive values tend to be high regardless of model performance. Therefore, the high negative predictive value observed in this study partly reflects the low prevalence of LTCI certification in the cohort rather than solely the predictive ability of the model. On the other hand, the model demonstrated moderate specificity (79.4%), indicating some ability to identify individuals who would remain noncertified. Accordingly, this model should not be interpreted as a reliable tool for identifying high-risk individuals who will require LTCI certification. Rather, it may serve as an exploratory population-level stratification tool for identifying groups of older adults with a relatively low probability of LTCI certification over a medium-term period. From a public health planning perspective, using routinely available municipal data (KCL and basic demographic variables), identifying individuals with a relatively low probability of certification within a 6-year horizon may still provide practical value, as it can support more efficient allocation of preventive resources.

In the model developed in this study, age emerged as the most significant primary predictor for LTCI certification after 6 years, effectively categorizing participants into 3 major risk tiers: Group A (≤70 years), Group B (71–78 years), and Group C (≥79 years). Specifically, Group C exhibited the highest overall certification rates. In contrast, Group A demonstrated a consistently high proportion of non-certification regardless of gender, suggesting that individuals aged 70 years or younger at baseline are highly likely to maintain their independence over the next 6 years. For Group B (aged 71–78), the risk was more nuanced, with depression risk and gender serving as key differentiators. This suggests that those 79 and older are more likely to require services provided by the LTCI system 6 years later. Individuals aged 79 at the time of the questionnaire would be 85 or older in 6 years, which is classified as extreme old age.[33] Previous studies have shown that frailty increases with age[34] and that the likelihood of LTCI certification rises with advancing age.[35] Moreover, those in extreme old age often experience chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and dementia,[33] which contribute to frailty and impair independence in daily living.[36–38] Sato et al[13] also identified age as a primary risk factor. Therefore, there is broad consensus that chronological age is a significant predictor of long-term care certification. However, the present study offers a novel and valuable contribution to the field by demonstrating how age interacts with other factors in a hierarchical and nonlinear manner using a decision-tree approach (E-CHAID).[39–41] This approach, which cannot be readily captured by traditional linear models such as logistic regression, provides a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of age as a predictor.[39,40,42] Consequently, when assessing long-term predictions over 6 years, the key evaluation criterion may be the likelihood of an individual reaching extreme old age within this timeframe.

Depression risk and gender were also important predictors. Among individuals aged 71 to 78, 3 patterns emerged: “71 to 78 years of age, without depression risk, and women” (Pattern 5), “71 to 78 years of age, without depression risk, and men” (Pattern 6), and “71 to 78 years of age, with depression risk” (Pattern 7). After 6 years, Pattern 5 exhibited the highest percentage of non-certification, while Patterns 6 and 7 showed the highest certification rates. In other words, the risk of certification increased when depression risk was present, regardless of gender. Previous studies have found that depressive symptoms and depression contribute to the risk of disability and LTCI certification,[43,44] which supports our findings. In this age group, men were more likely to receive certification, regardless of their depression risk status, indicating gender differences in certification risk. However, Sato et al[13] found that gender was not a significant factor for individuals aged 78 or younger. While this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the independent variables examined, the difference could also stem from the varied duration of the prediction period. Therefore, gender differences should be considered when evaluating the need for future LTCI services, particularly if depression risk is not present for individuals aged 71 to 78.

In contrast to traditional statistical and regression models, decision tree models have 3 strengths. First, decision tree models like CHAID differ conceptually from regression by using recursive partitioning to automatically identify complex, nonlinear interactions without the need for a priori model specification.[45,46] While logistic regression assumes linear relationships and requires predefined interaction terms, decision tree models uncover hierarchical risk structures that often remain hidden in traditional models.[39,42] Second, these methods are uniquely suited for public health because they provide intuitive subgroup structures that are more interpretable for clinical decision-making than population average effects.[40,42] In contexts such as long-term care prevention, this hierarchical segmentation helps identify specific risk profiles to prioritize preventive interventions.[39,40] Third, while traditional methods focus on limited markers or average outcomes, the E-CHAID model reveals the complex, multifactorial interplay of behavioral and health factors.[40,41] This allows for the identification of primary partitioning variables, such as age, within a data-driven hierarchical structure.[39,40] These findings suggest that long-term prediction using multiple factors may help identify individuals at lower risk of LTCI certification, thereby enabling the reallocation of social resources toward those at higher risk as part of early preventive interventions.

The derived decision tree model demonstrated moderate specificity (79.4%) and negative predictive value (97.1%), indicating that it can classify a large proportion of older adults as unlikely to require LTCI certification 6 years later. However, its sensitivity (52.2%) and positive predictive value (11.1%) were limited, suggesting a substantial proportion of future LTCI users would not be correctly identified. Compared with previous studies predicting frailty or LTCI risk,[6,13,47] the present model offers an alternative hierarchical representation of risk patterns; however, its predictive performance remains insufficient for clinical or policy-level decision-making without further refinement. To improve its usefulness, additional measures are required. First, the relatively large minimum parent and child node sizes (500/250) may have limited tree depth. In addition, sensitivity analyses for alternative hyperparameter settings of the decision tree (e.g., different node size thresholds) were not conducted. We acknowledge that smaller thresholds could yield more complex trees capable of modeling nonlinear interactions. However, the primary objective of this study was not to maximize predictive complexity, but to derive an interpretable and implementable stratification model suitable for municipal-level planning. In applied public health settings, overly deep trees may reduce transparency, increase instability, and limit practical applicability. By setting higher node size thresholds, we aimed to enhance model stability and prevent overfitting. Future studies may explore alternative parameter settings or ensemble methods to optimize predictive performance. Second, to improve detection of the minority class, SMOTE-NC was applied to the training dataset to address class imbalance. However, no further optimization strategies (e.g., cost-sensitive learning, threshold adjustment, or ensemble-based classifiers) were implemented. This decision was made to preserve interpretability and maintain a transparent model structure suitable for municipal-level application. We acknowledge that additional methodological approaches may enhance sensitivity and minority class detection. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory. Future studies should investigate alternative modeling strategies to improve detection performance while balancing interpretability and practical feasibility. Third, another limitation is the lack of external, temporal, and robust internal validation. The model was developed using data from a single rural municipality in Japan and internally validated using a random hold-out sample from the same dataset, without external validation. Therefore, the generalizability of the model to other municipalities, urban populations, or different healthcare systems remains uncertain. Furthermore, temporal validation using data collected at different time periods was not performed in this study. Temporal validation would provide a more realistic assessment of the model’s predictive performance when applied to future populations. Although cross-validation could provide more robust internal validation, this study prioritized the development of a single interpretable decision tree structure that could potentially be applied in municipal health planning. Repeated resampling procedures combined with oversampling methods may produce varying tree structures, which can reduce interpretability and practical usability. Future studies should therefore evaluate the robustness of the model using cross-validation, temporal validation, and external datasets. Fourth, another methodological limitation is that alternative predictive models (e.g., logistic regression, random forest, or gradient boosting) were not compared with the E-CHAID model. The present study prioritized model interpretability and the exploration of transparent rule-based structures rather than maximizing predictive performance. Future studies should therefore examine the robustness of these findings by comparing E-CHAID with alternative predictive modeling approaches. Fifth, this study included only KCL domains, age, and gender as predictors. Other potentially important factors, such as quality of life, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, medication history, and prior hospitalizations, were not incorporated into the model. The absence of these variables may introduce omitted variable bias and limit predictive accuracy and generalizability. However, this restricted variable selection was intentional, as the aim of this study was to evaluate a pragmatic model based solely on routinely available municipal screening data. In many local government settings, detailed clinical and socioeconomic information is not accessible for population-level planning. Therefore, this study should be interpreted as an exploratory, population-level analysis conducted under realistic data constraints rather than as a comprehensive clinical prediction model. Future research incorporating richer clinical and social determinants of health may improve predictive performance. Moreover, when implementing preventive interventions, individual assessment of health status, comorbidities, and medication use remains essential to tailor appropriate support strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected from older individuals in Japan’s snowy regions, so the findings may not be generalizable to other populations. Future research should consider using data from older individuals in urban and rural areas to enhance the generalizability of the model. Second, the KCL questionnaire is self-administered and subjective, which may lead to inaccuracies due to cognitive decline. Further studies should combine this model with an objective assessment of each domain to improve reliability and validity. Third, death is generally considered a competing risk for LTCI certification, as individuals who die during follow-up are no longer at risk of certification. In this study, individuals who died or moved away during the 6-year follow-up were excluded from the final analysis. We acknowledge that this approach may introduce survivor bias, particularly if individuals at higher risk of LTCI certification were also more likely to die during follow-up. In such cases, the estimated risk structure among surviving participants may differ from that of the original cohort. However, the primary objective of this study was not to model time-to-event outcomes or cumulative incidence accounting for competing risks, but rather to explore predictors of LTCI certification status among community-dwelling older adults who remained residents at the 6-year follow-up. From a municipal screening perspective, preventive interventions are implemented among living residents; therefore, restricting the analysis to surviving individuals is consistent with the practical application context. Nonetheless, future studies using survival analysis frameworks that explicitly account for competing risks may provide a more comprehensive understanding of long-term care trajectories. Fourth, the activities of daily living domain of the KCL was not included as a predictor because these items mainly capture existing functional dependency, which is closely related to LTCI certification criteria. However, excluding this domain may have reduced the overall predictive performance of the model, as activities of daily living limitations are known to be strong predictors of future disability. Future studies could examine models that incorporate activities of daily living measures while carefully addressing potential overlap with LTCI assessment criteria.

5. Conclusion

This study developed a composite predictive model for forecasting LTCI certification 6 years later in older individuals 65 and older using decision tree analysis. The findings revealed that age, presence of depressive risk, and gender were significant predictors for future LTCI certification. Specifically, individuals aged 79 and older, those aged 71 to 78 with depressive risk, and men aged 71 to 78 without depressive risk were more likely to be certified for LTCI 6 years later. The model demonstrated moderate specificity (79.4%) and high negative predictive value (97.1%), whereas it showed limited sensitivity (52.2%) and positive predictive value (11.1%). These findings indicate that the model performs better in identifying individuals unlikely to require LTCI certification than in detecting future LTCI users. Although it may provide preliminary population-level risk stratification over a 6-year horizon, its predictive performance is insufficient for standalone clinical or policy decision-making, and further refinement and validation are required. The model should be interpreted as an exploratory analytical approach rather than a definitive predictive tool.
