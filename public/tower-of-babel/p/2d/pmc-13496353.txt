Family Visits and Symptom Burden in Nursing Home Residents With Cognitive Impairment
Authors: Gretchen G Tucker, John G Cagle, Timothy E Stump, Wanzhu Tu, Todd D Becker, Alex Floyd, Peiyuan Zhang, Kathleen T Unroe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFamily visits with nursing home residents have been shown to improve residents' quality of life. However, little is known about the association between in‐person family visits and physical (e.g., pain) and behavioral symptoms (e.g., agitation) among residents with cognitive impairment. Using data from a large clinical trial of a palliative care program in Maryland and Indiana, we examined the association between family visits and staff‐reported resident symptom burden.MethodsUsing cross‐sectional baseline data from an ongoing multi‐state clinical trial involving 194 nursing home residents with cognitive impairment, we conducted bivariate analyses to examine the association between in‐person family visits (family involvement) as reported by family members and staff‐reported resident symptom burden. Linear regression models assessed the association between symptom burden and family visits in an adjusted framework. The model controlled for other relevant covariates, including resident race, gender, and age, and the care partner's relationship to the resident. Staff‐reported symptom burden was measured using a modified version of the Comfort Assessment in Dying End of Life in Dementia (CAD‐EOLD) scale. Family involvement was measured by the average weekly frequency of in‐person visits reported by the family over the past month.ResultsOn average, spouses visited 5 days per week, children 2 days per week, and other family members 1.5 days a week. In bivariate analyses, there was a weak but statistically significant and positive correlation between family involvement and symptom burden (r = 0.16, p = 0.04). However, in the adjusted model, the association was not statistically significant (b = 0.16, p = 0.09).ConclusionBivariate analyses suggest a significant and positive correlation between family visitation and symptom burden. However, this finding was not in the expected direction. Family visitation was associated with residents experiencing greater symptom burden. The association was not statistically significant in the adjusted model.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04520698Keywords: family, long‐term care, staff assessment, visitation

Introduction

It is estimated that, in the United States, there are 1.2 million persons age 65 years and older living in nursing homes. Among these residents, 65% have moderate‐to‐severe cognitive impairment, such as dementia [1]. Residents living with cognitive impairment require increasing assistance over the illness course. They require assistance with activities of daily living (e.g., bathing and dressing), managing behavioral and psychological symptoms (e.g., wandering and agitation), and symptoms such as pain and depression [2, 3, 4, 5]. Residents with cognitive impairment may struggle to communicate their concerns or changes to their health and well‐being, which could have an adverse effect on their health‐related quality of life [6]. For residents living with cognitive impairment, assessing and addressing their symptoms and needs can be challenging for staff. Family members play an important role in supporting nursing home residents, particularly by communicating residents' needs to staff [7, 8]. For example, family members serve as key informants when a resident is experiencing pain, particularly among residents with advanced cognitive impairment who may not be able to articulate what they are experiencing [8].

As cognitive impairment progresses, individuals experience a decline in speech and language and often develop behavioral and psychological symptoms such as anxiety and aggression [3, 4, 5]. This combination makes it difficult for residents to communicate their needs and symptoms to staff, hindering staff's ability to reduce or alleviate them [9]. For example, although residents with mild cognitive impairment may be able to self‐report experiencing pain to staff for symptom relief, those with impairment may not be unable to [10, 11, 12]. In advanced stages, staff most commonly rely on observing changes in resident behavior, by looking for facial expressions (e.g., grimacing or frowning), vocalization (e.g., moaning and calling out), and body language (e.g., restless, or rocking). Additionally, they may review medical histories and talk with family members [10, 11, 12].

Previous research indicates that family members often continue to be involved in the day‐to‐day lives of residents living with cognitive impairment following nursing home admission [13, 14, 15, 16]. This involvement may include visiting, decision‐making, providing emotional support, and care [9, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Family involvement is generally considered beneficial for residents, as family members can help identify residents' care needs and alert staff to address such concerns. Additionally, family members may share relevant information with staff, such as the resident's social and health history, and provide explanations for certain behaviors [22, 23]. For example, family members may serve as key informants when a resident experiences changes in pain or behaviors.

Visitation by family members has been associated with positive effects on the well‐being and health‐related quality of life of nursing home residents [8, 24, 25]. Findings from Arai et al. [24] suggest that more frequent family visits were associated with a lower likelihood of symptoms worsening or even a decline in symptoms among residents with behavioral and psychological symptoms related to cognitive impairment, such as wandering and aggression. However, Roberts and Ishler's [8] findings contrast with those reported by Arai et al. [24], suggesting that more frequent family visits (at least weekly) are associated with perceptions of poorer quality of life for the resident's. However, Roberts and Ishler's findings were attenuated when communication between family and staff was good, and family members perceived an increase in residents' quality of life [8].

Additionally, prior studies indicated that more frequent family visits are associated with increased rates of family members observing and reporting to staff resident health‐related changes such as respiratory issues and pain [7, 20]. Families who know the resident's health history and behaviors were able to observe changes. This includes recognizing non‐verbal signs of pain, such as facial expressions [20]. Family members often communicated changes in health or symptoms to staff and shared their observations so that staff could recognize the signs that a resident's experiencing symptoms, which they may have previously missed [20]. Thus, emphasizing that communicating with staff and advocating for residents is an important part of the caregiving role that family members engage in to ensure residents are adequately cared for [17].

Study Objectives

The objective of the current cross‐sectional study using baseline data was to examine the association between the frequency of family involvement and staff‐reported accounts of resident symptom burden among nursing home residents living with cognitive impairment. The research question was: Is family involvement significantly associated with staff proxy reports of symptom burden? Based on clinical experience that family visits generally improve resident well‐being, we hypothesized that family involvement would be significantly and inversely associated with residents' staff‐reported symptom burden.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The present analysis on family involvement and symptom burden is a cross‐sectional, secondary analysis using baseline data from a large stepped‐wedge clinical trial funded by the National Institute on Aging (grant number AG066922‐01; NCT04520698). Utilizing Palliative Leaders in Facilities to Transform Care (UPLIFT) was designed to evaluate the impact of palliative care consultations on the well‐being of nursing home residents with cognitive impairment. UPLIFT was conducted between September 2021 and July 2025 across 16 nursing homes, with 8 each in both Indiana and Maryland. Data were collected at baseline and at three additional time points [26].

For UPLIFT, residents with moderate‐to‐advanced cognitive impairment were enrolled using a proxy‐informed opt‐out approach. Resident eligibility requirements included: having resided in the nursing home for > 30 days, not currently enrolled in hospice, receiving long‐term care (i.e., not receiving skilled nursing care through Medicare Part A), and demonstrating moderate‐to‐advanced cognitive impairment [27]. Staff who confirmed both familiarity with enrolled residents and the ability to provide information on their symptoms within the past 30 days were recruited, consented, and enrolled. Family members of enrolled residents were also invited to participate, and, if willing, consented and enrolled to provide their perspectives about their related residents. In a minority of cases, the “family member” was an unrelated representative medical decision maker for the resident (e.g., a legal guardian).

A total of 655 residents were enrolled in UPLIFT. Among these residents, 194 family members provided information about the frequency of visiting with their respective residents. Many family members were unable to be reached or declined to participate in data collection. Data on these 194 residents were collected by research team members from both staff and family members (via in‐person survey administration and telephone survey administration, respectively). Both family members and nursing home staff provided their perception of resident symptoms; in this analysis, staff symptom reports are used. Several staff members provided data on their observations of multiple residents. Thus, the total number of unique staff who provided data for this study was 109. The Institutional Review Board approved this study (#2005967061).

Dependent Variable

Symptom Burden

Data on perceived resident symptom burden were collected from nursing home staff using a combined modified version of the Comfort Assessment in Dying End of Life in Dementia (CAD‐EOLD) and Symptom Management at the End of Life in Dementia (SM‐EOLD) scales [28]. This modified CAD‐EOLD was developed using information from the original CAD‐EOLD and SM‐EOLD scales. The original CAD‐EOLD assessed the intensity of 14 symptoms. The SM‐EOLD assessed the frequency of nine items. We combined the two scales to create a composite measure that assesses both frequency and intensity. Due to overlap in symptoms listed in the original CAD‐EOLD and SM‐EOLD, our modified instrument contains a total of 18 symptoms [28] (see Table 1).

The modified CAD‐EOLD consists of 18 symptoms, including pain, discomfort, choking, gurgling, anxiety, depression, and quality‐of‐life items that capture states of well‐being, such as calmness and peace. For the frequency of symptoms, staff were asked, “Over the past 30 days, how often did [Resident] have [symptom]?” Response categories included: “never,” “once a month,” “2–3 days a month,” “once a week,” “several days a week,” or “every day.” For each of the 18 symptoms present, the staff member was then asked to rate the level of intensity over the past 30 days. Response options included: “not at all intense,” “somewhat intense,” and “very intense.” If the symptom was not present, the intensity item was not asked and inferred to be “not at all intense.”

The total symptom burden score is calculated as a mean across all items, with higher scores indicating greater burden. Each composite item was calculated by multiplying the frequency item by the intensity item, resulting in a possible range of 0–15. Higher scores on the scale indicated worse outcomes, more frequent and more intense for the first 15 items, and less frequent and less intense for the well‐being items (calm, serenity, and peace). At least 9 of the 18 items (50%) had to be answered for a total mean score to be calculated. The three well‐being items (calm, serenity, and peace) were reverse coded to be consistent with scale scoring.

Independent Variable

Family Involvement

Family involvement was assessed using the following question: “Acknowledging all the responsibilities you have in your life, during the past 30 days, on average, how many days a week (0–7) did you visit [Resident] in‐person?”

Covariates

Covariates obtained from the Minimum Data Set included resident age (years), race (White or Non‐White), gender (female or male), Brief Interview for Mental Status (BIMS) score, and activities of daily living (ADLs). BIMS scores range from 0 to 15, with lower scores indicating more significant cognitive impairment (0–7 = severe cognitive impairment, 8–12 = moderate cognitive impairment, and 13–15 = intact cognition) [27]. ADLs were assessed using MDS self‐performance items (e.g., transfers, eating, toileting, and bed mobility) to measure functional status. Other covariates included family members' relationship with the resident (e.g., spouse/partner, sibling, child, or other), and the state location of nursing homes in the study (Indiana or Maryland).

Statistical Analysis

Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical data (race, gender, BIMS score [moderate vs. severe cognitive impairment], family member relationship to the resident, staff job role, and length of employment at the nursing homes). Means and standard deviations were calculated for age and BIMS score.

Assumptions were evaluated, including visual inspection of a histogram and Q‐Q plots and assessment of skewness and kurtosis. Visual inspection suggested normality, and skewness (0.81) and kurtosis (0.36) fell within acceptable limits (Figures S1 and S2). Pearson correlation was used to examine the association between in‐person family visits and symptom burden. Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between in‐person family visits and symptoms, respectively, because the distribution of some of the individual symptom scores was non‐normal. These analyses were conducted using SPSS.

The primary analysis consisted of multivariable mixed‐effects linear regression to examine the association between the frequency of family involvement and the modified CAD‐EOLD total score, adjusting for resident demographics, BIMS score, relationship to the resident, and facility state location. Random intercept terms for both facility and staff member were also included in the model to account for the within‐group correlations from the hierarchical structure of the data. The model was analyzed using R (Version 4.4.3) and the lmer function from the lme4 package [29, 30]. An a priori alpha level of 0.05 was established for statistical significance and a two‐tailed assumption was used for all analyses. A supplementary analysis, Spearman correlation, was conducted to assess the association between family reported symptom burden and in‐person visit frequency and staff reported symptom burden; results are presented in Table S1.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Tables 2 and 3 present descriptive statistics for participating residents, family members, and staff. Of the 194 residents enrolled in the study who had both family and staff‐reported data at baseline, 61% of the residents resided in nursing homes in Indiana compared to 39% in Maryland nursing homes. The average age of residents was 76.6 years (SD = 13.4 years). A majority of residents were female (61%) and White (71%). Residents had a mean BIMS score of 6.5 (SD = 3.9), indicating severe cognitive impairment. Visiting family members had an average age of 59.2 years (SD = 12.6 years). These family members were mostly females (69%), White (70%), and adult children of residents (57%). The average age of staff was 41.2 years (SD = 13.4 years). Most staff were female (94%), Non‐White (67%), and certified nursing assistants (36%).

Univariate

Symptom burden was captured by staff members using the modified CAD‐EOLD total score. Staff members reported an average total symptom burden score of 3.8 (SD = 2.3).

Bivariate

Pearson correlation results indicated a weak and positive statistically significant association between in‐person family visits and symptom burden (r = 0.16; p = 0.03). Spearman rank correlations revealed that four of the 18 symptoms were significantly associated with increased symptom burden at the 0.01 level: discomfort (ρ = 0.18) and at the 0.05 level: pain (ρ = 0.24), Fage, serenity (ρ = 0.16), and peace (ρ = 0.14; see Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between family involvement (family‐reported in‐person visits) among nursing home residents living with cognitive impairment, and staff‐reported severity of residents' symptom burden. In the fully adjusted regression model, there was no association between family visits and symptom burden. Specifically, only state of nursing home location was significantly associated with differences in symptom burden. Residents of Indiana nursing homes experienced greater symptom burden than those in Maryland nursing homes. The result may reflect differences at the facility level between the nursing homes in Indiana and Maryland. These facility‐level differences may include ownership status (e.g., for‐profit vs. not‐for‐profit), location, facility size, and staffing levels [31, 32].

For the bivariate analyses, an association was found between family visits and symptom burden (r = 0.16; p = 0.03). Based on clinical experience and interactions with staff during data collection, we expected family involvement would generally improve resident well‐being and thus be associated with lower staff‐reported symptom burden. The direction of the association in the bivariate analysis was somewhat unexpected. Instead, with family involvement, residents experienced greater symptom burden, as indicated by staff‐reported data using the modified CAD‐EOLD.

In the bivariate analysis, four of the 18 symptoms were significantly associated with increased symptom burden (pain, discomfort, serenity, and peace). Both serenity and peace were reverse coded. Thus, for these symptoms, family involvement was associated with staff reports of greater peace and serenity among residents. One potential explanation for residents experiencing greater symptom burden with family involvement is that when family members visit, they alert staff when the resident is experiencing pain or appears more agitated, sensitizing the staff to the frequency and intensity of these symptoms. These findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating that family members who visit, observe, and often inform staff of changes in residents' symptoms and behaviors [7, 20, 33]. As cognitive impairment progresses, residents are unable to self‐report symptoms, and staff rely on other methods, such as visual cues or information from family members to recognize changes in health [10, 11, 12].

Another potential explanation for the association between residents' greater symptom burden and family involvement is that staff may ask family members to visit the facility more frequently to support residents with active symptoms, thus more actively engaging them in care. Family members often know the resident well. They understand their health histories, behaviors, preferences, and needs, which are valuable, particularly for residents with cognitive impairment. The association between greater symptom burden and family involvement may be confounded by factors such as the family‐resident relationship, the types of activities family members engage in during visits, and the family‐staff relationship. As previously discussed, family involvement may include monitoring the resident's care and health, providing personal care, socialization, and decision‐making [13, 14, 17, 18, 20]. However, previous studies caution that family involvement is heterogeneous. The types and extent of engagement in activities may vary, and communication with staff may range from minimal or extensive [33, 34]. Families may monitor the residents' care and be actively involved in care decisions. Whereas other families may take a more “hands‐off” approach unless they are concerned about the residents' care and well‐being [35].

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

Strengths of this study include a relatively large, diverse sample of residents drawn from 16 nursing homes across two states. Additionally, the focus on family involvement, which is an important source of support for residents, and examining factors that influence nursing home resident symptoms among residents with cognitive impairment addressed a significant gap in existing literature. Combining elements from the CAD‐EOLD and SM‐EOLD assessments, which included 18 symptoms and both the frequency and intensity of these symptoms, provided a more detailed representation of the residents' symptoms and impact on their health‐related quality of life.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the use of cross‐sectional baseline data. Such data do not allow for causal inference or an analysis of change over time. Selection bias is also a potentially significant limitation; many family members were unable to be reached or declined to participate in surveys. Family members who are less involved and visit infrequently may be less likely to participate in research. The 194 family members who did participate may be more accessible and involved in the resident's care. Additionally, data on visits as reported by family members asked only about how frequently they visit. That is, there were no questions about the types of activities they performed during the visits (e.g., personal care). Future research could examine the changes in family involvement and symptom burden over time.

Conclusion

Prior work has shown the importance of family involvement in the care of NH residents. This study analyzed baseline data from the UPLIFT‐AD clinical trial in 16 nursing homes in Indiana and Maryland to discern whether there is a correlation between in‐person visitation frequency of residents living with cognitive impairment, as reported by family, and resident symptom burden, as reported in terms of frequency and intensity by nursing home staff.

The findings of this analysis suggest that nursing home staff may be more likely to report higher symptom burden among residents visited by family members. This may reflect increased awareness of symptoms due to family advocacy or increased involvement of family members due to difficult to manage behaviors or symptoms. Symptom burden also appears to vary at the state level, perhaps due to facility‐level differences (e.g., ownership, quality). Further analyses may examine the longitudinal relationship between family involvement and symptom burden, assessing how changes in visitation frequency are associated with changes in symptom burden over time at multiple levels: resident, facility, and geographical regions.
