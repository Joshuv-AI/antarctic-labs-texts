Poor quality of sleep and associated factors among pregnant women attending antenatal clinic in Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, 2023
Authors: Esuyawkal Mislu, Abraraw Terefe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep is an essential physiological function in humans and is a key indicator for mental health. During pregnancy, poor sleep quality is a common issue that can affect maternal and fetal outcome. This study aims to assess the prevalence of poor sleep quality and its associated factors among pregnant women attending the antenatal care clinic at Woldia Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, in 2023.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January to February 2023. Participants were selected using a systematic sampling method, and data were collected through a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Version 25. Both bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with poor sleep quality. Statistical significance was determined at p-value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 415 participants were included in the study, making a response rate of 97.4%. The prevalence of poor quality of sleep (PSQI > 5) was identified among 51.33% (213) of pregnant women. Urban residency (AOR: 4.01; 95% CI: 2.29–7.03), primiparous women (AOR: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.02–4.34), and multiparous women (AOR: 2.7; 95% CI: 1.37–5.55), compared to nulliparous women; second trimester pregnancy (AOR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.34–0.92) compared to third trimester; unplanned pregnancy (AOR: 3.10; 95% CI: 2.15–5.81), low hemoglobin levels (AOR: 2.17; 95% CI: 1.24–4.42); alcohol consumption in the past month (AOR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.13–3.85); and caffeine intake after dinner (AOR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.47–7.62) were significantly associated with poor sleep quality.ConclusionMore than half of pregnant women in the study area were identified as having poor sleep quality. Residency, parity, type of pregnancy, gestational age, low hemoglobin level, alcohol use in the past one month, and caffeine intake after dinner were significantly associated with poor quality of sleep among pregnant women. Therefore, it is advisable to take actions such as providing behavioral change counseling on sleep hygiene practices and addressing the factors associated with poor sleep quality during antenatal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08023-y.

Introduction

Sleep is an essential physiological function in humans, affecting wellness in both physical and psychological domains [1, 2]. It keeps the body physiological processes at the highest possible level of performance by conserving energy, repairing cells, modulating immune functions, optimizing brain circuits, and consolidating memory and learning [3]. Quality sleep is a predictor of vitality as well as physical, and mental health of both the mother and fetus [4–6]. It is recommended that during pregnancy women should get at least eight hours of sleep at night [4, 6].

Quality of sleep is a widely recognized indicator of mental health, maternal sleep disturbances may contribute to mental impairments in the mother, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including an increased risk of preterm birth, cesarean delivery, prolonged labor, and preeclampsia [7–9]. It is also associated with negative neonatal outcomes such as lower Apgar scores, greater likelihood of NICU admission, and reduced birth weight and length, all of which point to potential developmental challenges [8, 10, 11].

Sleep related disorders are very prevalent in the general population and are associated with significant medical, psychological, and social disturbances [12, 13]. Reports from nationwide surveys in Canada and the United States showed that more than 20% of the general adult population reported difficulty either of initiating or maintaining sleep [14], and also 16.6% in Africa and Asia [15]. However, it seems to be disrupted substantially during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Nearly, 78% of pregnant women’s had any forms of sleep disorder during their pregnancy time [4], with the most common problems were insomnia, sleep apnea, nocturnal gastro esophageal reflux, and Sleep-related breathing problems [16]. Prevalence rates for altered sleep during pregnancy range from 15 to 80%, depending on the population studied and the time of assessment, with higher rates observed in the third trimester [17]. Other studies reported poor sleep quality among 50% of pregnant women in Taiwan [18], 96.2% in Korea [19], and 53.3% in Pakistan [20].

The good news is that the highly prevalent problem of poor sleep quality during pregnancy is a manageable condition, through behavioral strategies and pharmacological interventions [16]. Moreover, addressing modifiable factors and improving sleep quality are believed to play a critical role in preventing adverse pregnancy outcomes including maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality, as well as anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairments [21].

However, there is limited information on the prevalence and factors affecting sleep quality among pregnant women in developing countries like Ethiopia, particularly in the local context. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of poor quality of sleep and its associated factors among pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in 2023.

Methods and materials

Study setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, from January to February 2023. The hospital is located about 521 km far from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, and serves for more than 2 million populations. It has different service units including: maternal and child health, gynecology, psychiatry, adult health, pediatric, emergency care, ophthalmic service, surgical, neonatal intensive care, outpatient department, dentist, laboratory, and pharmacy unit. On average, around 726 women visit the hospital each month for antenatal care.

Study population

All pregnant women who visited to Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital for ANC visit during the data collection period were the study population.

Eligibility criteria

All pregnant women who came to the hospital for ANC visit were included in the study, regardless of their gestational age or gravidity. However, pregnant women who were not willing to participate, had established psychiatric disorder, use of any kind of psychotropic medication, or had worked night shifts in the last month were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using Epi-info (Version 7.2.5.0), based on both the estimated prevalence and associated factors. The larger sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula, considering the prevalence of poor sleep quality. The assumptions included a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, 80% power, an assumed prevalence of poor sleep quality of 50% [22], and 10% non-response rate. Hence, the final sample size was 423.

Sampling technique

Study participants were selected using systematic sampling method, after identifying the total number of pregnant women expected who will visit the hospital for ANC service (N) and calculating for sampling interval (K). According to hospital records, a total of 1,452 pregnant women attended ANC services during the two months preceding the actual data collection period. K = N/n = 1452/423 = 3.43. Therefore, samples were selected at every third interval.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a pre-tested, interviewer-administered questionnaire. The interviews were conducted by BSc Midwifery internship students who received one day of training on the study objectives, ethical considerations, and proper interviewing techniques before the actual data collection began. Data collection also included a review of participants’ medical records including laboratory findings, diagnosis, medications, procedures, and documented complications to supplement obstetric history. The data collection process was supervised by the principal investigator to ensure data quality and completeness.

Measurements

The questionnaire included items on socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric history, substance use, and sleep quality. Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a validated and standardized tool [23]. The PSQI is a 19-item self-report instrument that assesses sleep quality over the past month. It consists of seven component scores: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction. The component scores are summed to yield a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. A global PSQI score greater than 5 was considered indicative of poor sleep quality [23, 24].

The seven components include: subjective sleep quality, which evaluates how individuals perceive the overall quality of their sleep; sleep latency, which measures how long it takes to fall asleep; and sleep duration, which reflects the total number of hours of actual sleep per night. Habitual sleep efficiency assesses the percentage of time spent in bed that is actually spent sleeping. Sleep disturbances capture how frequently sleep is disrupted by factors such as nighttime awakenings, bathroom needs, or physical discomfort. Use of sleeping medications reflects the frequency of using medication to aid sleep. Lastly, daytime dysfunction assesses the effect on staying awake or maintaining the motive to carry out daily activities [23, 24].

Regarding substance use, a woman was considered as “ever user” for any substance when she identified as she had used any of the substances at least once in her life time [25], and “current user” if she had used any amount of the substance at least once within the last 30 days [25, 26].

Data analysis procedures

Data were collected using Kobo Collect, then exported to SPSS version 25. Descriptive Analysis results were presented as frequencies, means, proportions and percentages using tables and text. Bi-variable and multivariable logistic regressions were conducted to identify variables that had independent association with quality of sleep. The final regression model was constructed using the forced entry method, which was chosen to ensure that all theoretically relevant variables were controlled for in the adjusted analysis. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. In the multi-variable analysis variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered as significantly associated with poor sleep quality, and adjusted odd ratios (AORs) with their 95% confidence interval (CI) were utilized to describe the strength of associations.

Data quality management

To assure the quality of the data, technical training was given for data collectors and supervisors before execution of data collection. The training focused on the objectives of the research, introduction to the data collection tool, and reporting procedures to the supervisor. Data collectors were closely supervised by the supervisors and principal investigator during the whole data collection period. The questionnaire was translated to Amharic (local language) and then retranslated back to English for its consistency by experts. Necessary modifications were made based on the pretest result. Data accuracy, consistency and completeness were checked daily by the supervisor (Annex 1). Additionally, informed written consent was obtained from each study participants prior to data collection and confidentiality was assured throughout the study.

Results

Out of 423 eligible participants expected to be included, 415 participants provided complete data, resulting in a response rate of 97.4%. The majority (54.5%) were in the age range of 20–30 years with a mean age (± SD) of 25.83 ± 5.6. One hundred eighty-five (44.6%) of them didn’t proceed beyond primary school, 395 (95.18%) were married, and 37 (8.9%) of them were government employees (Table 1).

Prevalence of poor sleep quality

Among all 415 participants included in the study, poor quality of sleep was identified in 213 pregnant women, resulting in a prevalence of poor sleep quality as 51.33% (95% CI: 44.66–58.7). The mean time taken to fall asleep (sleep latency) was 61.6 min (SD ± 13.3 min). The average bedtime and wake-up time among participants were 9:40 PM and 7:15 AM, respectively. Actual sleep duration ranged from 4 up to 10 h, with a mean of 6.7 h (SD ± 0.35). Additionally, 106 women (25.54%) rated their sleep quality as very poor (Table 3).

Factors associated with sleep quality

Bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with poor sleep quality among pregnant women. In the bi-variable analysis, variables such as the woman’s age, level of education, occupation, residence, parity, type of pregnancy, gestational age, caffeine use after dinner, low hemoglobin level, and alcohol use were identified as candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression, as they had a p-value of < 0.25.

In the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, residency, parity, type of pregnancy, gestational age, low hemoglobin level, alcohol use in the past month, and caffeine use after dinner were identified to be statistically significant association with poor sleep quality at a p-value less than 0.05.

The odds of experiencing poor sleep quality were four times higher (AOR: 4.01; 95% CI: 2.29–7.03) among pregnant women residing in urban areas compared to rural areas. Primiparous women had twice the odds (AOR: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.02–4.34), and multiparous women had 2.7 times the odds (AOR: 2.7; 95% CI: 1.37–5.55), of experiencing poor sleep quality during pregnancy compared to nulliparous women in the study area. Women in their second trimester had a 44% lower odds (AOR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.34–0.92) of poor sleep quality compared to those in the third trimester.

Women with unplanned pregnancies had 3.10 times the odds (AOR: 3.10; 95% CI: 2.15–5.81) of poor sleep quality compared to those with planned pregnancy. Low hemoglobin levels were associated with 2.17 times the odds (AOR: 2.17; 95% CI: 1.24–4.42) of poor sleep quality compared to normal hemoglobin levels. Alcohol consumption in the past month was associated with twice the odds (AOR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.13–3.85) of poor sleep quality compared to those who did not consume alcohol. Additionally, caffeine consumption after dinner was associated with 2.27 times the odds (AOR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.47–7.62) of poor sleep quality compared to those who did not consume caffeine after dinner (Table 5).

Discussion

The prevalence of poor sleep quality among pregnant women attended ANC in Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period was 51.33%. This finding is in line with a study conducted in Foshan Women’s and Children’s Hospital, China (51.8%) [27], Pakistan (53.3%) [20], referral hospitals in Nekemte (59.1%) [28], and a multicenter study in Referral Hospitals in the Oromia Region, Ethiopia (54.6%) [29], as well as at the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia (42.2%) [22]. However, the result is higher than studies conducted in China (15.2%) [30], and a study done among HIV-positive pregnant women in Northwest, Ethiopia (39.4%) [31], at Maternity Clinic of Turgut Ozal University in Ankara, Turkey (43.5%) [32], and at Jimma Medical center, Southwest Ethiopia (30.8%) [4].

On the other hand the prevalence is lower than those reported in studies conducted in two teaching hospitals in central China (87.0%) [33], Rawalpindi, Pakistan (73.7%) [34], Family Health Center in Adana, southern Turkey (88%) [35], Alzahra and Taleghani hospitals in Tabriz, Iran (96.4%) [36], an online survey from a pregnancy website conducted by Mindell et al. (76%) [37], and Wadila Primary Hospital, North East Ethiopia (68.4%) [9]. In general a significant number of pregnant women were identified for having poor sleep quality during pregnancy. The difference in the prevalence of poor sleep quality among studies might be as a result of difference in sample size, study population, study period, study area related traditions, sampling technique. This implies that the quality of sleep during pregnancy is a big issue than needs attention for pregnant women to ensure positive experiences and outcome.

Regarding factors significantly associated with poor quality of sleep, pregnant women who reside in urban areas were more likely to experience poor quality of sleep as compared to rural dwellers. This finding aligns with a study conducted in China, where a higher proportion of urban dwellers reported poor sleep quality [31]. This might be attributed to difference in housing conditions, neighborhood environments [38, 39], and other factors related to urbanization [39, 40].

Both multiparous and primiparous women were also more likely to experience poor sleep quality compared to nulliparous. This might be due to due to challenges of parenting, such as greater responsibilities, changes in lifestyle, and difficulties in balancing work and family life, all of which can impact their quality of sleep negatively. This result is in line with studies done in Saudi [41], Ohio, USA [42], Northern Ethiopia [9], and referral hospitals in Oromia Region, Ethiopia [29].

When we see pregnancy related factors, women in their second trimester were identified for having lower odd of poor quality of sleep as compared to those in the third trimester. This finding is in line with studies that showed an increase in poor sleep quality as gestational age advances [9, 28, 29, 34, 42–44]. This may be due to increasing physical discomfort such as leg cramp, increase abdominal size, pelvic pain, back pain; urinary frequency; hormonal change including fluctuation in progesterone levels that could cause awakening in the night time. Other contribution factors include heart burn, indigestion, and stress and anxiety.

The odd of poor sleep quality were higher among participants with unplanned pregnancies compared to those with planned pregnancies. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted in Nekemte [28] and in hospitals across five provinces in China [31]. This may be because women with unplanned pregnancies tend to experience higher levels of stress and anxiety, and get lower social support from her family or friends. This highlights the importance of addressing psychological well-being during pregnancy.

Low hemoglobin level was another pregnancy-related variable significantly associated with poor sleep quality. Supporting evidence for this association has been reported in various studies including Benz et al., Pai et al., Semiz et al., Fitiria et al., and Ghahremanfard et al., [45–49]. This association might be for a reason that anemia has association with restless legs syndrome, sleep apnea, fatigue, and a large stressors for our brain, leading to shorter sleep duration and more disturbed sleep [50–54].

Additionally, two behavioral factors, alcohol drinking and caffeine intake after dinner, were significant association with higher odd of poor sleep quality among participants. This association is supported by findings from other studies [22, 55, 56]. The likely reason might be the physiological, emotional, or cognitive influence which interferes with sleep quality [22, 57].

While interpreting findings from this study, it is better to consider the following limitations. This study was conducted in a single institute setup, and a self-reporting questionnaire was used for assessing the quality of sleep. Additionally, this study focuses on sleep quality as measured by PSQI scoring instead of other sleep related disorders.

Conclusions

Poor sleep quality is highly prevalent among pregnant women in the study area, with a prevalence of 51.33%. Significant associations were identified between poor sleep quality and factors such as residency, parity, type of pregnancy, gestational age, low hemoglobin levels, alcohol use in the past month, and caffeine consumption after dinner. These factors were found to be key determinants of poor sleep quality during pregnancy.

Therefore, it is recommended that health service organizations, researchers, and other stakeholders take targeted actions to address sleep quality among pregnant women. This can include providing behavioral change counseling on sleep hygiene and educating about the risk factors associated with poor sleep. Healthcare providers are advised to counsel pregnant women on practical lifestyle modification that include increasing the intake of iron-rich foods to address anemia, avoiding alcohol consumption, and limiting caffeine intake, particularly after lunch. Educating women on the impact of these behaviors is essential for improving sleep and pregnancy outcomes.

Additionally, it is better to recognize and manage sleep-related issues during antenatal care. Fostering multidisciplinary collaboration can also help ensure a more holistic and effective approach to maternal well-being.
