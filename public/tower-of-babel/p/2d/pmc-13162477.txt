Endogenous corticosteroid-induced glaucoma secondary to an adrenal adenoma in a postpartum woman: a case report

Background

Corticosteroid-induced ocular hypertension is a well-recognized cause of secondary open-angle glaucoma, typically linked to exogenous steroid use [1]. In contrast, glaucoma secondary to endogenous hypercortisolism, such as Cushing’s syndrome (CS), is rare and often underdiagnosed. Previous studies indicate that intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation does occur in patients with CS, although the degree is typically milder and clinically significant glaucomatous damage is reported only in a minority of cases [1, 2].

In this report, we present a rare case of bilateral acute ocular hypertension in a postpartum woman, ultimately diagnosed as corticosteroid-induced glaucoma secondary to an adrenal adenoma. This case underscores the importance of recognizing systemic endocrine disorders in atypical glaucoma presentations and highlights the need for timely interdisciplinary collaboration.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old woman, one year postpartum, presented with progressive blurred vision in her left eye for one month. She had a history of gestational diabetes treated with metformin, with no other pregnancy-related complications. At presentation, she was taking only metformin and no other ocular or systemic medications. No additional past medical history was reported. She had no history of ocular trauma, infection, or family eye disease, specifically no family history of glaucoma.

On examination, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1.0 in the right eye and 0.2 in the left eye. IOP was 50 mmHg in the left eye limited by corneal edema and 14 mmHg in the right eye. The anterior segment examination showed no signs of inflammation, with no anterior chamber flare or keratic precipitates (KP), and no Krukenberg spindle, iris atrophy, iris transillumination defects, or corectopia observed. Gonioscopy revealed open angles bilaterally with normal pigmentation and no evidence of a Sampolesi line, posterior embryotoxon, or peripheral anterior synechiae. Fundus examination revealed glaucomatous changes in the left eye with a cup-to-disc ratio (C/D) of 0.8 compared with 0.3 in the right eye (Fig. 1). Optical coherence tomography showed retinal nerve fiber layer thinning (Fig. 2A) and visual field testing confirmed moderate defects in the left eye (Fig. 2B). Despite maximal medical therapy and anterior chamber paracentesis, IOP remained uncontrolled, and trabeculectomy with mitomycin C (0.4 mg/mL) was performed 14 days after the initial diagnosis. Within one month, the right eye developed similarly elevated IOP and corneal edema, refractory to medication, requiring trabeculectomy, with C/D increasing to 0.4 prior to surgery. At the 2-month postoperative visit after trabeculectomies, BCVA stabilized at 0.8 in the right eye and 0.25 in the left eye, with IOP maintained at approximately 10 mmHg and no evidence of postoperative hypotony.

Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), including juvenile forms, was considered unlikely given the acute bilateral onset of markedly elevated IOP with associated corneal edema, which is atypical for the chronic progressive course of POAG. Other causes of glaucoma in young patients, including pigment dispersion syndrome and anterior segment dysgenesis such as Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome, were also considered but were not supported by the clinical findings, including normal gonioscopic features and the absence of characteristic anterior segment abnormalities. Therefore, the initial diagnosis was bilateral secondary glaucoma, with uveitis-related secondary glaucoma considered in the differential diagnosis.

At the 11-month postoperative follow-up visit in the ophthalmology clinic after trabeculectomy, the patient reported that she had experienced progressive postpartum weight gain of approximately 20 kg since delivery. Further history-taking and physical examination revealed facial acne, centripetal obesity, and prominent abdominal and thigh striae, raising suspicion for Cushing’s syndrome (CS). Given the atypical ocular course, these findings suggested the possibility of endogenous corticosteroid-induced glaucoma, and the patient was referred to the internal medicine department for further evaluation. On systemic examination, the internist additionally noted hair loss and elevated blood pressure (151/95 mmHg), further supporting the suspicion of hypercortisolism. A 24-hour serum cortisol assessment showed elevated levels (667.22nmol/L, 690.27nmol/L, and 612.92nmol/L at 08:00, 16:00, and 23:59, respectively) with loss of diurnal rhythm. Total 24-hour urinary free cortisol excretion was 1197.3 nmol/24 h. Low-dose dexamethasone suppression testing revealed insufficient cortisol suppression, confirming “non-ACTH dependent Cushing’s syndrome”. Further abdominal computed tomography identified a left adrenal mass (Fig. 3), which was surgically removed. Pathology confirmed an adrenal cortical adenoma, and serum cortisol levels normalized the day after surgery. Although ocular hypertension had been surgically controlled before the endocrine diagnosis, the elevated IOP was likely caused by corticosteroid-induced glaucoma secondary to benign adrenal adenoma.

Following adrenal adenoma resection, BCVA improved to 0.63 in both eyes, and IOP remained stable at approximately 10 mmHg throughout 30 months of follow-up without additional glaucoma treatment. Additionally, the patient’s body weight returned to pre-pregnancy levels and the features of hypercortisolism gradually resolved, including central obesity, facial changes and hypertension. These outcomes underscore the effective control of both the ocular and systemic aspects of the patient’s condition.

Discussion and conclusions

This case illustrates a rare form of secondary open-angle glaucoma caused by endogenous cortisol excess from an adrenal adenoma. Recent nationwide cohort data also indicate a higher risk of glaucoma in patients with endogenous CS3. In our patient, the rapid bilateral rise in IOP approximately one year after delivery initially obscured the underlying endocrine disorder, as the systemic signs of hypercortisolism were subtle and attributed to postpartum changes. In addition, compared with previously reported systemic corticosteroid-induced glaucoma cases, this case is notable for the young age at presentation, markedly elevated IOP with established damage, and the need for surgical intervention despite relatively low corticosteroid exposure. Asymmetric presentation with delayed fellow-eye involvement may highlight the variability of steroid responsiveness or optic nerve susceptibility. This case emphasizes the need to consider systemic endocrine causes when glaucoma presents atypically or progresses rapidly.

While corticosteroid-induced glaucoma is typically associated with exogenous steroid use, chronic endogenous cortisol excess, as seen in CS, can also elevate IOP, representing a less common but pathophysiologically similar mechanism [3–7]. Active cortisol binds to glucocorticoid receptors on trabecular meshwork cells, leading to structural changes in these cells and the deposition of extracellular matrix, thereby increasing aqueous outflow resistance [3, 8, 9]. Additionally, cortisol stimulates aqueous humor production by acting on the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium [9]. In the present case, IOP was refractory to medical therapy, and the rapid progression posed a substantial risk of irreversible optic nerve damage. Surgical intervention with bilateral trabeculectomies was therefore necessary and effective in achieving pressure control and visual preservation.

Existing evidence indicates that patients with endogenous CS have a substantially higher risk of developing ocular hypertension or glaucoma, and the onset tends to occur at a younger age than in the general population [3, 10]. The degree of IOP elevation has been shown to correlate with the level of cortisol exposure, with higher urine-free cortisol values associated with a greater likelihood of ocular hypertension [10]. Notably, in some patients, IOP decreases after endocrine surgery and normalization of cortisol levels, without the need for additional ophthalmic intervention [5, 10]. This suggests that, in certain cases, glaucoma may represent an ocular manifestation of an underlying endocrine disorder rather than a primary ophthalmic disease.

The main limitation of this case is that endocrine evaluation was initiated only after bilateral trabeculectomies, preventing assessment of whether earlier recognition and treatment of hypercortisolism could have avoided the need for filtering surgery. Nevertheless, this sequence reflects a common real-world scenario in which ocular signs precede systemic diagnosis, underscoring the need for heightened clinical suspicion.

In our case, characteristic systemic features were present but initially unrecognized, as they were attributed to postpartum physiological changes. This case also demonstrates that when definitive endocrine treatment such as adrenalectomy is delayed, filtering surgery can provide effective IOP control and prevent further optic nerve damage [11]. While filtering surgery may be required in rapidly progressive or medically refractory cases such as ours, less invasive options may be reasonable when short-term pressure control is needed but the systemic diagnosis is still evolving. In such situations, minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) may serve as a temporizing approach to lower IOP while further evaluation is pursued [12, 13]. What’s more, regular IOP monitoring is essential in patients with hypercortisolism to prevent irreversible glaucomatous damage [10].
