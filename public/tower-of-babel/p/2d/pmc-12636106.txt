Screening Tools for Malnutrition in Hospitalized Children with Respiratory Tract Infections: A Single Center Experience in a Low/Middle Income Country
Authors: Shaimaa Sayed, Enas AA Abdallah, Nehal El Koofy, Salma M Yosry, Mortada H F El-Shabrawi, Hala H Mansour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDetecting the risk of malnutrition in pediatric patients using simple, rapid, and validated screening tools is important. The most frequently used screening tools are the Screening Tool for the Assessment of Malnutrition in Pediatrics (STAMP), Pediatric Yorkhill Malnutrition Score (PYMS), and Screening Tool for Risk on Nutritional Status and Growth (STRONG-kids). We aimed to assess the value of STAMP as a nutritional screening tool for the evaluation of nutritional status compared to other tools in children with respiratory tract infections (RTIs) admitted to a single tertiary referral hospital in a low/middle-income country.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 80 children aged 1–12 years who were admitted for RTIs. Each participant was evaluated anthropometrically, and three screening tools (STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids) were used.ResultsAnthropometric measurements showed that 65% of the patients were stunted, 77.5% were underweight, and 60% were wasted. The STAMP score showed the most sensitivity (91.9%) and specificity (77.8%) in predicting malnutrition compared to weight-for-age, while STRONG-kids showed a sensitivity of 85.5% and specificity of 50%. Similarly, the STAMP score showed the most sensitivity (88.7%) and specificity (77.8%) in predicting malnutrition compared to height-for-age, while STRONG-kids showed a sensitivity of 81.8% and specificity of 29.6%.ConclusionThe STAMP is a reliable screening tool for malnutrition in children admitted for RTIs. In addition, STRONG-kids is a simple and quick nutritional screening tool for hospitalized children, and it predicts nutritional status on admission.Keywords: Pediatrics, Malnutrition, Nutritional assessment, Screening Tool for the Assessment of Malnutrition in Pediatrics, Pediatric Yorkhill Malnutrition Score, Screening Tool for Risk on Nutritional Status and Growth

INTRODUCTION

Detecting the risk of malnutrition in pediatric patients using simple, rapid, and validated screening tools is important. Malnutrition is associated with the risk of infection, increased length of hospital stay, morbidity, mortality, and increased hospital costs [1]. The risk of malnutrition is higher in patients with respiratory or cardiac diseases [2]. Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) are very common important health problems [3], with a higher incidence of lower RTIs among children than among other age groups [4]. Severe acute lower RTIs in children under 5 years lead to hospitalization and mortality in 15% of cases [5]. In a study conducted in Egypt, approximately one-third of the infections in children under 5 years were lower RTIs. Malnutrition is a significant risk factor for lower RTIs in children under 2 years [6].

One responsibility of pediatricians is to detect malnutrition. However, anthropometry is time consuming and can be interpreted incorrectly. Therefore, international organizations have recommended using screening tools, such as the Screening Tool for the Assessment of Malnutrition in Pediatrics (STAMP), the Pediatric Yorkhill Malnutrition Score (PYMS), and the Screening Tool for Risk on Nutritional Status and Growth (STRONG-kids), to identify malnutrition risk [7]. However, the usefulness of these tools is conditioned by their predictive validity in detecting nutritional risks, their reliability, and their ease of application in clinical practice [8].

Evidence is lacking on the predictive accuracy to justify the choice of one nutritional screening tool over another, particularly in the setting of limited resources in low/middle-income countries (LMICs) [9]. We aimed to assess the value of STAMP as a nutritional screening tool for the evaluation of nutritional status compared to other tools in children with RTIs admitted to a single tertiary referral hospital in Egypt, an LMIC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional study enrolled 80 children of both sexes with RTIs admitted to Cairo University Children’s Hospital, the largest tertiary referral hospital in the country, between January 2023 and June 2023. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Cairo University (approval code: MS-52-2021).

Patients of both sexes, aged 1-12 years, who presented with RTIs and were hospitalized in a general ward, regardless of the presence of an underlying chronic illness, were eligible for inclusion. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of patients. The nutritional status of each patient was assessed as follows.

Anthropometry

Body weight was measured in kilograms. Length (cm) was measured for infants below 2 years of age, while height (cm) was measured for patients above 2 years. Weight-for-height measurements were recorded for patients younger than 2 years of age, while body mass index (BMI) was calculated for patients older than 2 years. The measurements were interpreted according to the World Health Organization (WHO) growth chart Z-scores [10].

Pediatric yorkhill malnutrition score

This comprises four steps: 1) low BMI (-2 standard deviation [SD]), 2) history of recent weight loss, 3) recent change in nutritional intake for at least the past week, and 4) the likely effect of the current medical condition on the patient’s nutritional status for at least the next week. Each step has a score of 0 to 2, and the total score represents the degree of malnutrition risk. A score of 0 indicates a low risk of malnutrition; a score of 1, moderate risk; and a score of ≥2, high risk [11].

Screening tool for the assessment of malnutrition in pediatrics

This comprises three questions evaluating the medical condition, nutritional intake, and anthropometric measurements of a child and the sum used to calculate the risk of malnutrition: 0–1 represents low risk; 2–3, medium risk; and ≥4, high risk [12].

Screening tool for risk on nutritional status and growth

This comprises four items: 1) High-risk disease, 2) subjective clinical assessment, 3) nutritional intake and losses, and 4) poor weight gain or loss. Each item was given a score of 1 or 2 points, with a maximum total score of 5 points. Therefore, a score of 0 was classified as low risk; 1–3, medium risk; and 4–5, high risk [13].

The results of each screening tool and anthropometry were compared to assess the value of STAMP as a nutritional screening tool for the evaluation of nutritional status compared to other tools in children with RTIs. Hospital stay was affected by many factors, and in our study, long hospital stay was defined as more than 10 days in the hospital.

Sample size

This was calculated using the PASS 11 program at a setting power of 80 408× error at 5%. A previous study showed that the prevalence of malnutrition in children with RTIs was 25%; therefore, assuming a sensitivity and specificity of nutrition screening questionnaires of approximately 80%, a sample size of at least 80 patients is needed.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically described in terms of mean±SD, median and range, or frequencies (number of cases) and percentages when appropriate. Numerical data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparison of numerical variables between the study groups was performed using Student t-test for independent samples to compare two groups of normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U-test for independent samples to compare non-normally distributed data. Normally distributed numerical variables between more than two groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance. Non-normal numerical variables were compared between more than two groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. To compare categorical data, the chi-square test was performed. Exact test was used when the expected frequency was <5. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis models were used to test for the preferential effect of the independent variable(s) on the dependent variable(s). Accuracy was represented using the terms sensitivity and specificity. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to determine the optimum cutoff values for the studied diagnostic markers. Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Co.). was used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

This cross-sectional study included 80 children with RTIs admitted to the Cairo University Children’s Hospital. Thirty-six (45.0%) were girls and 44 (55.0%) were boys. No significant relationship was observed between sex and score interpretations (STAMP, p=0.342; PYMS, p=0.703; STRONG-kids, p=0.110).

The median age of patients was 5.03±3.334 years, with a range of 1 to 12 years. No significant relationship was observed between age and STAMP score (p=0.275), PYMS (p=0.345), or STRONG-kids score (p=0.841). Similarly, no significant relationship was observed between long hospital stay and malnutrition detected by the STAMP score (p=0.527), PYMS (p=0.473), or STRONG-kids score (p=0.294).

Forty participants (50.0%) in our study had no related chronic illnesses. The mean duration of hospital stay was 17.65±7.96 days, with concomitant comorbidities present in 40 patients (50.0%). Fifty-eight patients (72.6%) had long hospital stay. Eleven patients (13.8%) had chronic cardiac disease (cardiomyopathy [1 patient] and congenital heart disease [10 patients]). Twenty-five patients (31.2%) had chronic respiratory diseases (cystic fibrosis [seven patients], bronchiectasis [five patients], pulmonary fibrosis [four patients], pulmonary hypertension [three patients], interstitial lung disease [three patients], bronchial asthma [two patients], and bronchopulmonary dysplasia [one patient]). Other comorbidities included down syndrome (two patients), cerebral palsy (one patient), and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (one patient). A statistically significant difference was observed between the STRONG-kids interpretation and associated comorbidities (p=0.025).

The anthropometry of the study population is shown in Table 1. An assessment of the anthropometric measurements showed that 77.5% of the patients were underweight, 65% were stunted, and 60% were wasted. According to the WHO, malnutrition (wasting) classification for weight-for-height revealed that 18 (22.5%) patients were mildly malnourished, 16 (20.0%) were moderately malnourished, and 14 (17.5%) were severely malnourished.

In the current study, anthropometry was used as the gold standard for malnutrition assessment. Consequently, the results of STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids, divided into low and medium/high risk groups to determine sensitivity and specificity, were compared with anthropometry to determine the most reliable screening tool for predicting the risk of malnutrition in hospitalized patients with RTIs.

ROC was calculated for the risk of malnutrition in STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids using weight-for-age (an indicator of acute malnutrition/underweight), as shown in Table 5 and Fig. 1, and height-for-age (an indicator of chronic malnutrition/stunting), as shown in Table 6 and Fig. 2. When compared with weight-for-age, STAMP showed a sensitivity of 91.9%, specificity of 77.8%, and accuracy of 88.8% in underweight prediction. When compared with height-for-age, STAMP showed a sensitivity of 88.7%, specificity of 48.1%, and accuracy of 75% in predicting stunting. STRONG-kids showed a sensitivity of 85.5%, specificity of 50%, and accuracy of 77.5% in underweight prediction. When compared with height-for-age, STRONG-kids showed a sensitivity of 81.8%, specificity of 29.6%, and accuracy of 51.3% in predicting stunting.

DISCUSSION

Malnutrition screening is recommended because malnourished children admitted to hospitals experience prolonged hospital stays and adverse outcomes [14]. Anthropometric measurements are the gold standard for assessing the health of a child [15].

In the current study, the prevalence of malnutrition was high; 77.5% of the patients were underweight, 65% were stunted, and 60% were wasted according to anthropometric measurements. In a study conducted in India, the prevalence of protein energy malnutrition among children aged 1–5 years was 56.4% [13]. A dietary survey conducted by Vinod and Savadatti [14] in 2013 found that 52.2% of children were suffering from various grades of malnutrition. This may be explained by poor socioeconomic conditions, poor maternal health and nutritional status, inappropriate feeding and frequent hospital admissions in early life, poor access to appropriate healthcare, lack of food security, inappropriate feeding practices, a monotonous diet with low nutrient density, and lack of water sanitation and hygiene services [15].

In the current study, a significant proportion of the study participants were malnourished. Fifty percent of them did not have chronic diseases, whereas 35% had chronic respiratory diseases. White et al. [15] observed a high prevalence of malnutrition and nutritional risk in Australian pediatric patients with chronic lung diseases [16]. This can be explained by the effect of systemic inflammation caused by inflammatory cytokines and an increase in the basal metabolic rate, which leads to a negative nutritional balance and eventually weight loss. In addition, anorexia and associated gastrointestinal disorders markedly reduce caloric and nutritional input [17].

The patients were classified into risk groups (low, medium, and high) using all three tools: STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids. We observed that STAMP classified 11.3% of children as being at low risk, 42.5% at medium risk, and 46.3% at high risk of malnutrition. PYMS identified 11.3% of the children as being at low risk, 46.3% at medium risk, and 42.5% at high risk of malnutrition. Lastly, STRONG-kids showed that 10% had low risk, 55% had medium risk, and 35% had high risk of malnutrition.

This was similar to the results of the study carried out by Lee and Yang [17], which demonstrated that STAMP classified 11.4% of children as being at low risk, 39.7% at medium risk, and 48.8% at high risk of malnutrition. According to the PYMS, the study found that 26.5% of children were at low risk, 25.4% were at medium risk, and 48.1% were at high risk of malnutrition. Regarding STRONG-kids, 35.6% of children were at low risk, 58.9% were at medium risk, and 5.5% were at high risk of malnutrition [17].

When comparing the results of two studies conducted by Moeeni et al. [18,19], who used STAMP and STRONG-kids, with our study results, we concluded that almost three-quarters of our study participants were classified as high and medium risk, with a small proportion classified as being at low risk of malnutrition. In contrast, the two studies mentioned above concluded that a higher percentage of their participants was classified as low risk, and a lower percentage was classified as being at high risk of malnutrition. The difference between these results and ours may be explained by the fact that these studies included both hospitalized and healthy children, whereas our study comprised 80 hospitalized children with RTIs, including patients with associated chronic diseases who have a higher risk of malnutrition.

In the current study, no significant relationship was observed between malnutrition by STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids and long hospital stay; however, the length of hospitalization was longer for those at high risk than for low-risk patients, which partially agrees with the results of a study that found a longer hospital stay in high-risk patients than in low-risk patients. This may be due to failure to meet the increased energy requirements of hospitalized children owing to both underlying disease and malnutrition [20]. Malnutrition and its risk factors are responsible for one-third of child deaths before the age of 5 years, and most of the deaths were reported in developing countries [21].

Gambra-Arzoz et al. [22] used STRONG-kids to test for malnutrition in their study. Children who scored high for malnutrition were admitted to the hospital for noticeably longer periods and at higher costs.

In our study, a significant relationship was observed between malnutrition by weight-for-age and malnutrition by STAMP score; however, no significant relationship was observed between malnutrition by PYMS and malnutrition by STRONG-kids, which partially disagrees with the results in the study by Pars et al. [23], who found that weight-for-age and height-for-age were significantly correlated with STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids. Additional information in their study, such as food intake, clinical status, and physical examination contributed to this difference [23].

In our study, we used ROC curves to determine the sensitivity and specificity of each screening tool in the prediction of malnutrition compared with anthropometric measurements, the gold standard. The STAMP screening tool showed the best accuracy and the most sensitivity with the highest negative predictive value in comparison with anthropometric measurements, the gold standard. This may be due to the presence of items related to anthropometry in the main principal items of the STAMP score. For this reason, STAMP may be closely associated with wasting and stunting upon hospital admission. Therefore, STAMP levels may be good indicators of wasting and stunting, suggesting malnutrition in pediatric patients [20].

In accordance with our results, a study carried out by McCarthy et al. [24] recommended the STAMP score as the most reliable tool, with a sensitivity 70% and a specificity of 90%. Another study conducted by Wonoputri et al. [25], comparing STAMP, PYMS, and STRONG-kids with full nutritional assessment, reported that STRONG-kids had the lowest specificity, whereas STAMP had the highest positive predictive value. However, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value vary according to prevalence [25], which explains the differences between the results of our study and those of other studies. A study carried out by Carey et al. [26] suggested that STAMP may be a valid nutritional screening tool for identifying malnutrition risk in a general inpatient population.

A comparison between four variables (sex, age, associated comorbidity, and long hospital stay) and the three scores revealed a statistical significance between associated comorbidity and two scores (STAMP and STRONG-kids).

While STAMP and PYMS interpretations showed that 11/36 and 15/36, respectively, had medium risk of malnutrition by weight/age (acute malnutrition), the STRONG-kids interpretation showed that 20/36 had a medium risk of malnutrition by weight/age. According to the STRONG-kids and PYMS interpretations, 21/36 had a medium risk of malnutrition by height/age (chronic malnutrition); however, 14/36 had a medium risk of malnutrition by height/age, according to the STAMP interpretation. This is consistent with the findings of Omar et al. [27], who found that STRONG-kids had the highest accuracy in identifying both acute and chronic malnutrition.

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of STRONG-kids to predict the presence of acute malnutrition were 100%, 13.75%, 19.03%, and 100%, respectively. For chronic malnutrition, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were 91.6%, 11.92%, 14.19%, and 90%, respectively.

In conclusion, we considered the STAMP score as a reliable screening tool for detecting malnutrition risk in hospitalized children with RTIs. In addition, the STRONG-kids is a simple, sensitive, and specific questionnaire.
