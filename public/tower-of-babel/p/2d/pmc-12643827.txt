Repetition and order of exposure to salinization and nutrient enrichment drive freshwater microbial community responses
Authors: Rose Fuggle, Miguel G Matias, Mariana Mayer‐Pinto, Ezequiel M Marzinelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological systems face multiple stressors that impact biodiversity and ecosystem functions, with complex interactions that vary spatially and temporally leading to unpredictable outcomes. In freshwater ecosystems, benthic microbial communities underpin vital functions like decomposition and primary productivity, but are threatened by stressors such as salinization and nutrient enrichment, which are intensifying and increasingly co‐occurring due to climate change. We experimentally tested how stressor repetition and different orders of stressor exposure affect freshwater benthic microbial community responses over time. Communities established on tiles in 1000 L open freshwater ponds established more than 10 years ago in the field were exposed to elevated salinity and nutrient enrichment, either once or repeatedly, independently or in combination, and under different orders. Repeated exposure to nutrient enrichment led to stronger functional changes than the single exposure, while repeated exposure to elevated salinity resulted in weaker changes compared to a single exposure. Critically, we found that the sequence in which stressors occurred was a major determinant of microbial responses, driving interaction outcomes in opposing directions. When exposure to nutrient enrichment preceded elevated salinity, gross primary productivity was halved and carbon metabolic rates increased by 50% compared to communities treated in the reverse order. This study is among the first in a complex, outdoor freshwater system to demonstrate that stressor sequence can strongly shape multiple stressor effects, highlighting the order of stressor exposure as a key but often overlooked dimension of global change ecology. These findings suggest that microbial functions, including productivity and carbon cycling, will fluctuate more dramatically as stressors increasingly occur in different sequences under global change.Keywords: benthic microbial communities, biofilm, carbon metabolism, climate change, freshwater ecosystems, multiple stressors, nutrient enrichment, primary productivity, salinization, stressor interactions, stressor order, stressor repetition

INTRODUCTION

Ecological systems are experiencing diverse stressors that can interact to drive complex, non‐additive effects on biodiversity and ecosystem functioning (Côté et al., 2016; Folt et al., 1999; Orr et al., 2020; Vinebrooke et al., 2004). The prevalence of nonadditive interactions across terrestrial (Rillig et al., 2021), marine (Crain et al., 2008; Griffen et al., 2016), and freshwater systems (Birk et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2016; Orr et al., 2024) presents significant challenges for research and policy as the effects of single stressors might not be informative in multiple stressor scenarios (Piggott et al., 2015).

The effects of multiple stressors depend on their nature (i.e., persistence in the environment), their magnitude (i.e., intensity of their application; e.g., concentration), and temporal dynamics (e.g., frequency, duration, and order of occurrence) (Courcoul et al., 2022; Dal Bello et al., 2017; Jackson et al., 2021). Temporal (or spatial, e.g., within a landscape) dynamics of multiple stressors have been largely overlooked (Jackson et al., 2021; Orr et al., 2024), although emerging research is revealing how such dynamics can influence multiple stressor effects (Calderón et al., 2018; MacLennan & Vinebrooke, 2021; Penk et al., 2024; Romero et al., 2019; Sun & Arnott, 2022, 2024). For instance, stressor order strongly affected nitrogen cycling in soils, with one sequence producing effects twice as large as the reverse (Calderón et al., 2018). Order effects may be especially strong when stressors differ in temporal persistence: long‐lasting “press” stressors (Bender et al., 1984) (e.g., sustained warming) can co‐occur with subsequent stressors, while short‐lived “pulse” stressors may only leave indirect legacy effects (Cuddington, 2011). Exposure to a press stressor followed by a pulse stressor may result in direct interactions between stressors, while the reverse order (pulse stressor followed by press stressor) may result in only indirect interactions, possibly driving different functional and structural effects for individuals and communities.

Freshwater lakes and ponds are critical habitats that support biodiversity, primary productivity, carbon cycling, and water supply, and are increasingly recognized as highly threatened systems globally (Birk et al., 2020; Reid et al., 2019). Two key interacting stressors impacting freshwater communities are salinization and nutrient enrichment (Berger et al., 2019; Carrier‐Belleau et al., 2023; Cunillera‐Montcusí et al., 2022; Moyano Salcedo et al., 2024). Freshwater salinization is occurring in diverse environmental contexts due to climate change, mining, agricultural runoff, freshwater extraction, drought, and other human activities (Cunillera‐Montcusí et al., 2022). Salinization and nutrient enrichment can occur in combination (Lind et al., 2018; Moyano Salcedo et al., 2024) or can occur sequentially. For example, during heavy rainfall, coastal freshwater systems can simultaneously experience saltwater intrusion and inorganic nutrient pulses carried by runoff. In contrast, inland freshwater systems experience fluctuating cycles of salinization during periods of extended drought followed by nutrient pulses during periods of heavy rain (Reid et al., 2019). The timing and order of such events may strongly shape their combined effects, yet this remains poorly understood in freshwater systems.

The effects of multiple stressors, including salinization and nutrient enrichment, on the functioning of freshwater ecosystems are likely to be mediated by changes to the structure and function of benthic microbial communities. These communities play important roles in many ecosystem functions, for example, biogeochemical cycling and primary productivity (Battin et al., 2016; Sentenac et al., 2022). Individually, increased salinity causes osmotic stress, which typically reduces microbial growth and decreases abundance (Berga et al., 2012, 2017; Sivakumar et al., 2019), alters community structure in favor of euryhaline organisms (Berga et al., 2017; Vendrell‐Puigmitja et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2014), and decreases rates of organic carbon decomposition (Berger et al., 2019; Canhoto et al., 2022; Martínez et al., 2020). In contrast, increased nutrient concentrations typically increase microbial biomass and bacterial growth rates (Koch et al., 2018; Rindi & Benedetti‐Cecchi, 2023; Van Horn et al., 2011) and shift community structure in favor of fast‐growing copiotrophic bacterial taxa driving homogenization (Geng et al., 2022; Leff et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2022) and increase the production of carbon‐degrading extracellular enzymes, organic carbon decomposition, and community respiration (CR, Ferreira et al., 2015; Manning et al., 2018; Van Horn et al., 2011). When experienced simultaneously, nutrient enrichment and elevated salinity can cause stronger decreases in rates of carbon metabolism and changes to community‐level physiological profiles (CLPP) of benthic freshwater microbial communities than when experienced individually (Fuggle, Matias, et al., 2025). However, their interactions when experienced sequentially are unknown.

This study assessed the influence of stressor order and repetition on the effects of elevated salinity and nutrient enrichment on the taxonomic structure and metabolic and productivity‐related functions of benthic microbial communities. Our study was conducted in 1000‐L semi‐natural freshwater ponds established over 10 years ago in the field (Nava et al., 2022; Pereira et al., 2021), allowing stressors to be manipulated under a field environmental setting. We manipulated concentrations of salts and inorganic nutrients in the ponds such that ponds were treated with one individual stressor applied only once or repeatedly, or with both stressors in different orders. We aimed to determine whether changes to microbial community structure and function differed between single and repeated exposure to the same stressor; whether the order in which different stressors were experienced influenced the nature of the stressor interaction; and whether these effects changed over time. Given that salinity and nutrients can act in opposite directions, we hypothesized that stressor order would strongly influence outcomes. We expected nutrient enrichment to initially stimulate microbial activity and biofilm thickening, buffering later salinity, and resulting in weaker combined effects compared to the opposite sequence.

We hypothesized that (1) the effects of repeated exposure to the same stressors would be stronger than the effects of single stressors, (2) the combined effects of elevated salinity and nutrient enrichment would be stronger where communities were exposed to elevated salinity first followed by nutrient enrichment compared to the reverse sequence, and (3) treatment effects would weaken through time, with the effects of salinity lasting longer than the effects of nutrients. We quantified benthic microbial community structure, via 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, and function, through physiological profiling on the metabolic rates of 31 different carbon sources, rates of CR, net primary productivity (NPP) and gross primary productivity (GPP), photosynthetic efficiency, and total chlorophyll a concentrations. These functional metrics directly underpin freshwater primary productivity and carbon cycling, key ecosystem functions threatened by global change.

METHODS

Field ponds

The experiment was performed between June and September of 2023 in an outdoor field in Toledo, Spain, which contained 32 open freshwater experimental ponds (hereafter “ponds”). Each pond was a 1000‐L polyethylene cattle tank, 0.70 m deep and 1.85 m in diameter. The ponds were set up in 2014 by adding 100 kg of locally sourced topsoil and filling them with locally collected well water. The ponds were subsequently colonized by a variety of organisms, from bacteria to small vertebrates, resulting in naturally established freshwater communities that resemble natural small freshwater ponds (Nava et al., 2022). The water column is dominated by alternating cycles of phytoplankton and zooplankton, while the benthos contains diverse assemblages of aquatic macrophytes and invertebrates (e.g., beetles, dragonflies, and mosquito larvae) (Pereira et al., 2021).

Experimental design

We used 21 ponds (hereafter “treatment ponds”) to test the effects of salinization and nutrient enrichment, independently, repeatedly, and in combination through time, on benthic microbial communities. Ponds were randomly selected and allocated to each of seven treatments (three ponds per treatment, spatially interspersed): elevated salinity (S), repeated elevated salinity (SS), elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN), nutrient enrichment (N), repeated nutrient enrichment (NN), nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity (NS), and undisturbed (i.e., ambient; A) (Figure 1). Ponds were treated with their first stressor: elevated salinity or nutrient enrichment. Elevated salinity was achieved by dissolving 15 g NaCl/L using commercially available rock salt (sodium chloride, NaCl), while nutrient enrichment consisted of adding 10 mg N/L (as sodium nitrate; NaNO3; GPR RECTAPUR) and 1 mg of P/L (as sodium dihydrogen phosphate; NaH2PO4; GPR RECTAPUR). Salt and nutrient treatments of these magnitudes have strong combined effects on freshwater benthic microbial community metabolic functions while maintaining ecological relevance (Fuggle, Matias, et al., 2025). A quantity of 15 g NaCl/L is representative of peak salt concentrations in freshwater ponds experiencing significant salinization during snowmelt following road de‐icing, for example, in the North American spring (Hintz & Relyea, 2019; Novotny et al., 2008). These salinity levels can also be observed in coastal freshwater systems following significant seawater intrusion events, for example, as occurred in the Changjiang estuary following a series of extreme weather events (Zhu et al., 2020). Nitrate concentrations of 10 mg/L represent maximum safety thresholds in freshwater (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2012) and nutrient manipulations of this magnitude have been shown to lead to eutrophic conditions in similarly sized pond mesocosms while maintaining 10:1 N:P ratios (Chase, 2010).

Experimental design. Biofilms were established on ceramic tiles in ponds for 30 days before being transplanted into treatment ponds. After 7 days, ponds received their second stressor treatment, generating the treatments: ambient (A; green), nutrient enrichment (N; red), repeated nutrient enrichment (NN; orange), nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity (NS; pink), elevated salinity (S; light blue), repeated elevated salinity (SS; dark blue), and elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN; purple). Samples were collected on days 1, 21, and 81.

Treatments were applied by dissolving salts or nutrients in a 20‐L bucket with water from each pond by stirring and then adding the dissolved salts or nutrients back into the pond. A second application of the stressors was performed after one week for ponds receiving repeated stressors (repeated elevated salinity; SS and repeated nutrient enrichment, NN) or combined stressors (elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment, SN; nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity NS). In this experiment, ponds were nitrogen limited (Appendix S1: Table S1), so nutrients were readily consumed from the water column and assimilated into biotic components of the mesocosm (e.g., biomass or the benthos) after being applied (Fuggle, Matias, et al., 2025). In contrast, the increase in salinity levels in the treated ponds was relatively sustained throughout the experimental period (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1). Twenty‐one days after the second stressor treatment, ponds that received two salinity treatments had close to double the conductivity, a measure of salinity, as single salinity treated ponds (37.8 and 60.26 ms/cm, respectively). The difference in conductivity between these two salinity treatments was not significant after 81 days (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1), most likely because salt in the repeated salinity ponds began to precipitate out of the solution and crystallize on the sides of the tanks as water evaporated through time.

This experiment was designed to test for the effects of repeated exposure and the order of stressor exposure. For salinity, repeated exposure necessarily increased both the frequency of stress events (one vs. two additions) and the total magnitude (i.e., double the cumulative salt concentration after the second addition). Thus, repetition in this case is operationally defined as a combined change in frequency and magnitude, which our experiment was not designed to disentangle. For nutrients, repeated exposure also increased the frequency of stress events (one vs. two additions); however, because nutrients were rapidly incorporated into biomass, concentrations of nutrients in the water column were depleted before the second pulse and therefore total magnitude was not increased after the second addition (i.e., similar increase in nutrient concentration after the first and second addition). This temporal asymmetry in stressor persistence reflects natural dynamics in closed, nutrient‐limited freshwater systems, where salt persists but nutrients are quickly assimilated. Consequently, differences between repeated salinity and nutrient treatments may partly reflect differences in temporal persistence, rather than inherent mechanistic differences between stressors. Therefore, our analyses focused on the ecological outcomes of single versus repeated exposures for the two different stressor types without making inferences as to whether such effects were driven by frequency and/or magnitude.

Biofilm establishment and transplant

A total of 336 ceramic tiles (2 × 2 cm) were randomly deployed in four additional ponds under ambient conditions (hereafter “development ponds”). Tiles were placed ~2–5 cm apart on plastic trays sitting on the sediment at the bottom of each pond. The 30‐day development reflects timeframes for benthic prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms to embed in an adherent extracellular matrix and form a relatively taxonomically stable community (Brislawn et al., 2019). After 30 days of biofilm development, tiles were moved into treatment ponds (i.e., A, S, SS, SN, N, NN, or NS) with each pond receiving 12 tiles, three tiles from each of the four development ponds. After tiles had been in the treatment ponds for seven days, repeated stressor ponds were then treated with their second stressor (for the treatments SS, SN, NN, and NS). Salt or nutrients were added as described above.

Pond conditions

To assess abiotic and biotic conditions among the four ambient biofilm development ponds, water oxygen concentration, conductivity, pH, chlorophyll a concentration, nitrite, nitrate, and phosphate concentrations in water were measured as in Fuggle, Matias, et al. (2025). Briefly, dissolved oxygen concentration, conductivity, and pH were measured using a HQ40d Multi‐Meter (Hach, Australia), chlorophyll a concentration was quantified using an AquaFluor (Turner Designs) portable fluorometer, and the concentration of nitrite, nitrate, and phosphate was determined using UV–VIS spectrophotometry. Water conditions were basic (pH 9.7–10.3), oxygen depleted (0.0–0.78 mg O2/L), and nitrogen limited (NO2 < 0.025 mg/L, NO3 < 0.01 mg/L). Other abiotic and biotic conditions varied, with phosphate concentrations ranging from 0.08 to 6.00 mg/L, chlorophyll a concentration ranging from 86.81 to 3024 μg/mL, and conductivity ranging from 2.820 to 5.920 mS/cm (Appendix S1: Table S1). Each treatment pond received tiles from each development pond to ensure that potential variation among development conditions affected all treatments equally. To test hypotheses about whether the effects of treatments on the abiotic and biotic characteristics of water in the treatment ponds varied through time, on days 21 and 81, dissolved oxygen concentration, pH, conductivity, and chlorophyll a concentration were also measured directly in each treatment pond as described above.

Biofilm sampling

After 1 day, 21 days, and 81 days after the second stressor application, three tiles were randomly selected from each pond and destructively sampled. Tiles were shaken underwater for 30 s to remove sediment and any loosely attached organisms. One tile was used for CLPP and analyses of metabolic rates, one tile was used for analyses of CR, NPP, GPP, concentration of functional groups, and photosynthetic yield, and the third tile was used to quantify bacterial community structure. After 81 days, we quantified the concentration of groups of phototrophic organisms, photosynthetic yield, and bacterial community structure to determine long‐term stressor effects. CLPPs, CR, NPP, and GPP were not quantified at 81 days because pond water had turned anoxic in treatment ponds. Given the anoxic conditions, it was not possible to detect changes in oxygen concentrations during in situ incubations to quantify CR and NPP, nor was it possible to incubate EcoPlates in conditions similar enough to the field (i.e., anaerobically). For each of these analyses, measurements were performed using the whole tile community assemblage that remained attached to the tile after 30 s of shaking in the pond water (i.e., an adherent mixed species biofilm, sensu Flemming & Wuertz, 2019). All measurements were done as in Fuggle, Matias, et al. (2025) and are described in brief below.

Community‐level physiological profiling

To assess the CLPPs of microbial communities after exposure to different stressors and their combinations, tiles (n = 4 per treatment and time point, each from a different pond) were placed in individual watertight jars filled with water from their source pond and transported to the MNCN‐CSIC laboratory facilities in Madrid. Upon arrival, tiles were gently rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline solution (PBS). Biofilms were removed with a cell scraper and suspended in 5 mL of PBS. Absorbance at an optical density of 590 nm (OD590) was measured and diluted to ~0.05 to minimize effects of initial inoculum cell density on metabolic rates (Garland, 1997; Garland et al., 2001). Next, 150 μL of cell suspension was added to each well of a 96‐well Biolog EcoPlate (Biolog Inc. Hayward, CA, USA; hereafter EcoPlate), with each plate corresponding to one tile. Each EcoPlate had three replicates of 31 sole carbon sources, along with three replicate water blanks serving as negative controls (i.e., 96 wells per plate) together with the redox dye tetrazolium violet. Formazan is produced as carbon sources are oxidized leading to a color change over time that can be quantified spectrophotometrically. The baseline OD590 of each well was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer and plates were incubated in the dark at 25°C, the day temperature of ponds during the experimental period. OD590 readings were repeated every 24 h for 96 h.

CLPP data processing

OD590 readings for each well in each plate for each reading (0, 24, 48, 72, 96 h) were negative‐control corrected by subtracting the mean OD590 of water negative‐control wells. Mean OD590 values of the three technical replicates for each carbon source in each plate were then calculated and used to plot OD590 curves through the 96‐h incubation period. Mean and maximum slope values were calculated for each curve. These kinetic parameters capture the rate of carbon metabolism and are generally more sensitive indicators of metabolic function compared to single‐point measures like average well color development (AWCD) or substrate richness, and are less sensitive to biases introduced by the timing of incubations and differences in initial inoculum densities (Preston‐Mafham et al., 2002). Therefore, mean and maximum slope were used in subsequent univariate and multivariate analyses (Lin et al., 2021). For univariate analyses, the mean and maximum slope values for each carbon source were considered as replicates (i.e., 31 values per plate/replicate tile). We also calculated mean and maximum slope values for each of the six carbon groups by pooling the data by carbon type. All univariate metrics were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models (details on models fitted below). To test hypotheses about whether the effects of treatments on multivariate carbon metabolic profiles varied through time, permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) models were run on Euclidean distances calculated on mean metabolic rates of each of the 31 carbon sources at two time points (details on models fitted below). Principal components analysis (PCA) was used to visualize multivariate patterns of metabolic carbon profiles.

Productivity, chlorophyll a, and photosynthesis

To test hypotheses about the effects of treatments and time on CR, NPP and GPP of microbial communities, oxygen production and consumption were quantified. Pond water was filtered to 20 μm to minimize the contribution of larger water‐borne organisms to oxygen production and consumption. Smaller organisms (e.g., waterborne bacteria) may have contributed to these oxygen fluxes and therefore these data represent the oxygen production and consumption from both benthic and planktonic bacteria, although the contribution of the latter is assumed to have been much lower than the benthic component given the density of planktonic organisms in the water column is generally much lower than the density of organisms in biofilms (Flemming & Wuertz, 2019). Dissolved oxygen concentration was measured in each jar containing each tile before and after each 2‐h incubation in the dark, and then before and after each 2‐h incubation in the light, using a HQ40d Multi‐Meter (Hach, Australia) equipped with an IntelliCAL LDO101 luminescent/optical dissolved oxygen probe. CR rates were calculated by subtracting the oxygen concentration at the end of the dark incubation period from the oxygen concentration at the beginning of the dark incubation period. NPP rates were calculated by subtracting the oxygen concentration at the end of the light incubation period from the oxygen concentration at the beginning of the light incubation period. The changes in oxygen concentrations (in milligrams per liter) were converted to micromoles per liter (μM/L) using the molecular weight of O₂ (32 g/mol). The CR and NPP values were adjusted to account for the surface area of the tiles (0.0016 m2) and incubation time (2 h). GPP rates were calculated by subtracting CR from NPP (Martinez et al., 2022; Mayer‐Pinto et al., 2023).

After oxygen incubation, tiles were placed under black plastic covers for 5 min to allow for dark adaptation and maximum photosynthetic quantum yields were measured using a pulse amplitude modulated (PAM) fluorometer (WALZ, Germany) (Moyano Salcedo et al., 2024). Total chlorophyll a concentrations were calculated as the sum of the concentrations for green algae, diatoms, and cyanobacteria (Kahlert & McKie, 2014; Pérez‐Calpe et al., 2021).

Community structure

To determine the bacterial structure of biofilms after exposure to stressors, at each sampling time (1, 21, and 81 days), one randomly selected tile was removed from each pond of each treatment, shaken underwater for 30 s (as above), and the surface of the tile was swabbed for 30 s using a sterile cotton swab. Swabs were placed in sterile cryotubes and frozen at −20°C in a portable freezer for 24 h until reaching the laboratory, where samples were stored at −80°C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and bioinformatics

DNA was extracted from swabs using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro DNA kit (QIAGEN) as per manufacturer's instructions. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed on DNA to amplify the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene using the 341–forward primer (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and the 805–reverse primer (GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) (Klindworth et al., 2013). PCR conditions were adapted based on the protocol in the Illumina library preparation workflow: 95°C for 3 min followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 55°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 30 s. Quality control and library preparation using two‐step barcoding and paired‐end amplicon sequencing were performed on an Illumina MiSeq (v3) sequencer at the Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics with a 300‐bp sequence length using a single flow cell.

Primers and barcodes were removed from the raw sequences which were then processed through the DADA2 package (v 1.30.0) within the Bioconductor software environment in R (v 4.3.2) (Callahan et al., 2016). Briefly, reads were quality filtered and base pairs with a mean Q score < 20 were removed, resulting in a truncation at 290 bp for forward reads and 250 bp for reverse reads (Prodan et al., 2020). Maximum error rates were calculated and rare amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and chimeric sequences were detected using the DADA2 denoising algorithm. Paired reads were then merged and an ASV abundance table was constructed. Taxonomic assignment was initially performed using the SILVA reference database (v 138.1) (Quast et al., 2012). A second round of taxonomic assignment was performed using the Genome Taxonomy Database (v 202) (Parks et al., 2020).

Total abundance of the 16S rRNA gene was quantified for each sample by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the primers 341F/805R using the QuantStudio 3 thermocycler (Thermo Fisher with PrimeTime Gene Expression Master Mix, Integrated DNA Technologies) and associated software. The reaction conditions were 56°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, and 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Relative ASV abundances were corrected to absolute abundances using 16S gene copies obtained from the qPCR (Nappi et al., 2023). ASVs assigned to the kingdom “Archaea” (<1% total ASVs), unassigned ASVs at the kingdom level (<0.01% total ASVs) and chloroplasts (<1% total ASVs) were then removed. Singletons and ASVs with abundances <0.001% of the total ASV count, which were selected as a cut‐off point by inspecting ASV abundance plots, were also removed, reducing ASV count from ~11,000 to ~2000. Species accumulation (rarefaction) curves were generated using the “specaccum” function in the vegan package in R with the “random” method (Oksanen et al., 2024) (Appendix S1: Figure S2). Shannon's diversity index was calculated in the R vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2024). Beta‐diversity analyses were done on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities among sample pairs, calculated on square‐root transformed, absolute ASV abundances, as well as Jaccard dissimilarities based on presence–absence (i.e., composition only). Dissimilarities were analyzed using PERMANOVA fitting a model described below. Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (nMDS) ordination was used to visualize multivariate patterns of community structure.

Concentration of groups of phototrophic organisms

To determine the concentrations of groups of phototrophic organisms in the benthic communities after exposure to treatments, one tile was removed per pond and the concentrations of diatoms, cyanobacteria and green algae were quantified based on pigment fluorescence using a BenthoTorch fluorometer (BBE, Moldaenke) (Harris & Graham, 2015). The BenthoTorch fluorometer distinguishes phytoplankton groups, including green algae, diatoms, and cyanobacteria, by sequentially exciting microalgal accessory pigments which have unique excitation profiles, using multiple light‐emitting diodes and measuring fluorescence at 680 nm (Echenique‐Subiabre et al., 2016; Harris & Graham, 2015).

Statistical analyses

To test hypotheses about the effects of treatment (fixed, seven levels: A, S, SS, SN, N, NN, NS) and time (fixed, crossed, 2 or 3 levels: 1, 21, 81 days) on benthic microbial community structure and function, Shannon's diversity index, 16S rRNA gene copies (a proxy for total bacterial abundance), concentration of groups of phototrophic organisms (cyanobacteria, diatoms and green algae), maximum metabolic rates, mean metabolic rates, photosynthetic yield, CR, NPP, GPP, and total chlorophyll a were analyzed by fitting univariate linear mixed‐effects models using the lmer function from the “lme4” package in R (Bates et al., 2015). “Pond” was included as a random factor nested in treatment, but crossed with time, to account for pond‐to‐pond variation and repeatedly sampling ponds.

For univariate models, p values were calculated using the F‐statistic with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom in the “Anova” function (type 2) of the “car” package in R and, where appropriate (significant treatment × time interactions or, where interactions were absent, significant main effects), post hoc tests were conducted using R “emmeans.” Plots of residuals versus fitted values were inspected to check model assumptions and model fit. For multivariate analyses, models were fitted using PERMANOVA in PRIMER v6 (PRIMER‐E, UK). Multivariate dispersions were assessed for homogeneity by analyzing distances from centroid data using Permdisp in PRIMER.

Finally, to test for associations between the Euclidean distance matrix calculated on CLPPs and the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix calculated on square‐root transformed ASV abundances, we used Mantel tests (9999 permutations) based on Pearson's (linear relationship) and Spearman's (monotonic relationship) coefficients, using the R package “ape” (Paradis & Schliep, 2019).

RESULTS

Pond conditions

Conductivity was significantly higher in ponds treated with elevated salinity (S, SS, NS, and SN) compared to ambient ponds (A), and compared to ponds treated only with nutrient enrichment (N and NN) 21 and 81 days after the second stressor treatment (F 6,17 = 5.25, p < 0.001) (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1). After 21 days, ponds treated with repeated elevated salinity (SS) also had higher conductivity than ponds treated with single elevated salinity (S), nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity (NS) and elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN) (F 6,17 = 5.25, p < 0.001) (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1), although SS no longer significantly differed from S, SN, or NS after 81 days (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1). After 21 days, oxygen concentration in pond water was significantly lower in single and repeated nutrient‐enriched ponds (N and NN) and repeated elevated salinity (SS), compared to ambient (A), elevated salinity (S), and elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN) (F 6,17 = 5.33, p = 0.003), but there were no significant differences among treatments after 81 days (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1). Chlorophyll a concentration in water was significantly higher in ambient ponds (A), nutrient‐enriched (N), and repeated nutrient‐enriched (NN) ponds compared to other treatments independent of time (F 6,17 = 3.87, p = 0.013) (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1). pH did not significantly differ among treatments but was lower on day 81 compared to day 21 (F 1,17 = 21.18, p < 0.001) (Appendix S1: Table S2, Figure S1).

Effects of stressor repetition

Stressor repetition significantly affected the function of microbial communities, and these effects were stressor specific and changed through time. Single treatment with elevated salinity resulted in a different CLPP, significantly lower total community mean and maximum metabolic rates and significantly lower mean carbohydrate metabolic rates, compared to ambient communities on day one (CLPP: pseudo‐F 6,16 = 1.54, p = 0.046; maximum total metabolic rate: F 7,1442 = 3.96, p < 0.001; mean total metabolic rate: F 7,1442 = 7.97, p < 0.001; mean carbohydrate metabolic rate: F 6,384 = 4.51, p < 0.001) (Figures 2, 3, 4; Appendix S1: Figure S3, Tables S3–S5). The CLPPs and metabolic rates of single salinity treated communities became similar to ambient communities by day 21. In contrast, the CLPP and metabolic rates of communities treated with repeated elevated salinity did not differ from ambient at any time point (Figures 2, 3, 4; Appendix S1: Figure S3, Tables S3–S5). Neither single nor repeated elevated salinity treatments affected NPP, GPP, CR, photosynthetic yield, or total chlorophyll a concentration at any time point (Figure 5; Appendix S1: Figure S4, Tables S6 and S7).

Effects of stressor order

Stressor order significantly affected the structure and function of benthic microbial communities, and these effects changed through time. On day 1, the combined effects of stressors in either order (elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment, SN; or the reverse order, NS) on metabolic rates and CLPPs did not differ from ambient or from each other (Figures 2, 3, 4; Appendix S1: Figure S3, Tables S3 and S4). However, by day 21, the metabolic rates of nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity (NS) treated communities were over 50% higher than those of elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN) treated communities (maximum metabolic rates: F 7,1442 = 3.96, p < 0.001; mean metabolic rates: F 7,1442 = 7.97, p < 0.001) (Figures 2, 3, 4; Appendix S1: Table S4). The CLPPs of these communities (i.e., NS vs. SN) also differed significantly from each other (pseudo‐F 6,16 = 1.54, p = 0.046) (Figures 2 and 4; Appendix S1: Table S3). When analyzing treatment effects within carbon types, stressor order significantly affected the mean and maximum metabolic rates for carbohydrates (F 6,384 = 4.51, p < 0.001) and carboxylic acids (F 6,338 = 2.67, p = 0.015), but not amino acids, polymers, phenols, or amines (Figure 2; Appendix S1: Figure S3, Table S5). Rates of NPP and GPP were also two times higher in the elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment (SN) treated communities compared to those treated in the reverse order (NS) after 1 day (NPP: F 6,17 = 2.77, p = 0.046; GPP: F 6,17 = 3.21, p = 0.027), but were similar by 21 days (Figure 5; Appendix S1: Table S6). There were no significant effects of stressor order on CR, photosynthetic yield, or total chlorophyll a concentration (Figure 5; Appendix S1: Figure S4, Tables S6 and S7).

There was also an effect of stressor order on the multivariate dispersion of bacterial community taxonomic structures, which were significantly smaller in the nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity treatment (NS) than in the reverse order (salinity followed by nutrients; SN), independent of time (F 6,61 = 2.80, p = 0.035) (Figure 6; Appendix S1: Table S8). There were no other significant effects of stressor order on community compositional metrics, including bacterial diversity (Shannon's index), bacterial community structure, bacterial abundance, and the concentration of groups of phototrophic organisms (Figure 6; Appendix S1: Figures S5 and S6, Tables S8–S12). There was no significant association between bacterial community structure (Bray–Curtis) and EcoPlate functional profiles (Euclidean) (Mantel Pearson's Z = 30.81, p = 0.87; Mantel Spearman's Z = 30.81, p = 0.86; Appendix S1: Table S13).

DISCUSSION

We found that repetition and order of stressor exposure strongly influenced the effects of nutrient enrichment and salinization on freshwater benthic microbial community function and these effects changed significantly over time. The effects of stressor repetition differed between stressor types. Repeated exposure intensified stressor effects compared to a single exposure for nutrient enrichment but showed the opposite trend under elevated salinity. Additionally, our findings strongly support the prediction that the functional consequences of the stressors would be greater when salinity preceded nutrient enrichment compared to the reverse sequence.

Under nutrient enrichment, both individual and repeated treatment caused similar decreases in community metabolic rates, but repeated nutrient enrichment also led to a strong increase in community productivity that was not observed under a single nutrient enrichment. The stronger effect of repeated nutrient enrichment on rates of net and GPP compared to single nutrient enrichment was expected and aligns with previous research that nutrient enrichment causes strong stimulatory effects on aquatic microbial community functions which typically recover over time (Koch et al., 2018; Rindi & Benedetti‐Cecchi, 2023; Salgar‐Chaparro et al., 2020). In contrast, after a single exposure to salinity, community metabolic rates significantly decreased and CLPPs shifted compared to ambient communities after one day, whereas the effects of repeated exposure to elevated salinity were weaker than those of a single exposure and did not differ from ambient levels. The weaker metabolic effects of repeated salinity elevation compared to a single salinity elevation observed here were unexpected and do not align with the general finding that the effects of salinization on freshwater microbial communities increase with increasing salt concentration (Berga et al., 2012, 2017; Canhoto et al., 2022; Martínez et al., 2020). One possible explanation for this unexpected result is potential nonlinear changes in the physiology of organisms in response to varying salinity; however, in this study, we did not measure underlying physiological mechanisms directly, which is necessary to test this hypothesis. Indeed, a recent experiment in a synthetic aquatic community with 12 bacterial taxa illustrated that microbial growth rates had a nonlinear relationship with salinity, increasing with salinity up to mid‐range salinity levels (15 g NaCl/L) before decreasing again as salinity levels increased stepwise to 35 g NaCl/L (Carmichael et al., 2025). This highlights the importance of quantifying the effects of repeated stressor exposure directly, as increases in stressor magnitude and/or frequency may drive nonlinear changes in community responses. Since our experiment aimed to examine effects of repeated salinity stressors as they often occur in the field, where frequency and magnitude typically co‐vary (i.e., increases in frequency of salinization events typically result in increases in total magnitude as salt persists in the system), our experimental design did not allow disentangling the effects of each of frequency and magnitude for this stressor. Future research explicitly designed to test the relative role of frequency versus magnitude increases could enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underpinning microbial resistance to repeated salinity exposure. In addition, to better resolve the timing of these dynamics, future experiments should incorporate an additional sampling point between the first and second stressor applications.

Aligning with our predictions, we found strong effects of stressor order on the function of benthic microbial communities and these effects changed through time. Initially, NPP and GPP were significantly higher in communities treated under the elevated salinity followed by nutrient enrichment stressor order, but these effects were no longer significant after 21 days. In contrast, the combined effects of both stressors in either order of application (elevated salinity then nutrient enrichment, or the reverse) on community metabolic rates initially did not differ from ambient levels or from each other but intensified in different directions by 21 days. Communities exposed to nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity had metabolic rates 50% higher than the reverse order, and the CLPPs of these communities also differed significantly.

The strong influence of stressor order likely results from differences in how the stressors interact across the two sequences. Where elevated salinity was applied first followed by nutrients, salinity levels remained higher than the ambient ponds throughout the experiment so both stressors were present simultaneously, allowing for direct interaction. In this case, community metabolic rates decreased, consistent with the strong decreases in benthic bacterial community metabolic rates observed where elevated salinity and nutrients were applied simultaneously (Fuggle, Matias, et al., 2025). Similar effects have been observed in animal systems, for example, where exposure to a long‐acting toxin followed by a short‐acting toxin drives significantly higher toxicity on freshwater crustaceans compared to the reverse order of exposure (Ashauer et al., 2017). We speculate that the simultaneous presence of two different selective pressures may have forced organisms in the benthic bacterial community to redirect energy away from resource acquisition (e.g., production of extracellular enzymes for carbon metabolism) toward survival mechanisms (e.g., expression of genes related to salt tolerance and low oxygen conditions) as has been observed in other microbial communities in response to stress (Schimel et al., 2007). While energy redirection may plausibly underpin the decreased metabolic rates observed here, changes in gene expression rates were not measured in this experiment and should be followed up in future work to test this hypothesis.

Conversely, in the reverse order—nutrient enrichment followed by elevated salinity—nutrients in the water column had likely returned to baseline levels by the time salinity was added, so the stressor interaction was mediated only through indirect legacy effects. One potential mechanism that may have underpinned this legacy effect is an increase in EPS production and thickening of the biofilm when nutrient enrichment was briefly lifted (Flemming & Wingender, 2010; Salgar‐Chaparro et al., 2020; Van Horn et al., 2011). Such biofilm thickening could have buffered the subsequent salinity stress, maintaining metabolic function, although this needs to be tested, as we did not measure EPS or biofilm structure directly. This is further supported by smaller multivariate dispersions observed under this treatment which can indicate greater community resilience in aquatic bacterial communities (Jurburg et al., 2024). However, this ameliorative effect of a nutrient pulse we observed is likely to be context‐dependent, as N and P additions might only promote biofilm thickening where they had previously been limiting (i.e., oligotrophic environments). The influence of order on the combined effects of nutrient enrichment and elevated salinity should be experimentally tested in mesotrophic and eutrophic conditions to determine the role of context in this stressor interaction.

Finally, in contrast to our predictions, we did not observe strong effects of stressors, individually, repeatedly, or in any combination, on bacterial diversity, abundance, composition, or on the concentration of different groups of phototrophic organisms. One possible explanation for the lack of treatment effects on these metrics is the high compositional diversity that likely developed among communities across four different ambient ponds during establishment. In this experimental system, each pond has had over 10 years of independent succession since it was initially established, resulting in large variation in the biotic conditions across replicates (Nava et al., 2022). This diversity may have been greater than the size of treatment effects, potentially masking effects on community taxonomic structure. Additionally, we found no association between bacterial community structure and CLPP, indicating that the observed functional shifts were largely decoupled from composition. This weak link between composition and function suggests that functional changes under salinity and nutrient stressors in this system may be driven by physiological plasticity rather than by taxonomic turnover. Thus, our findings highlight the utility of functional measures including microbial metabolic profiling as sensitive indicators of stress responses, particularly in communities where taxonomic structure is relatively stable, such as mature biofilms.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the influence of stressor repetition and order of exposure in shaping the structure and function of benthic microbial communities under nutrient enrichment and elevated salinity. Repeated exposure to the same stressor drove stronger functional changes compared to single exposure under nutrient enrichment, but weaker functional changes under elevated salinity. When communities were sequentially exposed to both nutrient enrichment and elevated salinity, their combined effects on microbial community structure and function were strongly mediated by the order in which the stressors are applied. Stressor order not only affected the magnitude of community metabolic responses but also changed the direction of these effects, with strong metabolic decreases occurring when salinity preceded nutrient enrichment, and increases in the reverse sequence. These findings highlight the complexity of freshwater ecosystem dynamics under multiple interacting stressors and emphasize the importance of considering temporal dynamics in ecological research and stressor mitigation efforts. As global change continues to alter the frequency and intensity of environmental stressors, including nutrient enrichment and salinization, the rates of key microbial carbon metabolism may fluctuate more drastically, raising concerns for the stability of the freshwater carbon cycle. Given that the combined effects of multiple stressors can differ significantly depending on frequency, magnitude, and order of stressor exposure, future research should incorporate realistic temporal sequences when investigating stressor interactions. Doing so will enhance our ability to predict how microbial communities—and, by extension, ecosystem functions—will respond to ongoing global change.
