A rare case of native Thrombotic aortic valve thrombosis with acute coronary syndrome-like presentation
Authors: Hamidreza Biranvand, Akbar Shafiee, Dorsa Ghorban Sarvi, Shervin Mossavarali, Mohammad Sahebjam, Mehdi Dehghani Firoozabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThrombosis of the native aortic valve is a rare, potentially embolic condition that has been increasingly identified with modern imaging and can present variably from myocardial infarction to stroke, limb ischemia, or be clinically silent. We present an idiopathic native aortic valve thrombus manifesting as suspected acute coronary syndrome.Case presentationAn 84-year-old man with multiple cardiovascular risk factors and a history of opium use presented with chest pain, sweating, and vertigo. Initial evaluation showed severe three-vessel coronary artery disease and chronic cerebral ischemic changes. Echocardiography revealed impaired left ventricular function, an apical thrombus, and a suspicious thickening on the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve. He underwent coronary artery bypass grafting, during which a firm valvular mass was identified and excised. Histopathology confirmed organized thrombus without malignancy or infection. The postoperative course was uneventful.ConclusionThis case emphasizes that valve-attached thrombi can present atypically in elderly patients with vascular risk factors; targeted valve imaging and combined surgical removal plus anticoagulation may be required.

Introduction

Thrombosis of native aortic valve is a very unusual but challenging condition due to its potential for embolic events with poor outcomes. Less than 80 cases have been reported in the past 50 years, but its incidence has increased from just 2 cases in the 1980s to 37 in the last decade likely as a result of advances in imaging techniques, particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). It could have different presentations, such as myocardial infarction (MI), cerebrovascular accident (CVA), limb ischemia, or it may even be asymptomatic [1]. The most recognized etiology of this rare condition is hypercoagulable diseases, although it can occur idiopathically [2]. Here, we describe a case of idiopathic native aortic valve thrombosis presenting like acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

Case presentation

An 84-year-old man with opium addiction a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CVA presented to the emergency department of his town with a central chest pain accompanied by sweating and vertigo. On initial assessment, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Laboratory investigations revealed a white blood cell count of 9,090/µL (neutrophils 59.2%, lymphocytes 30%), hemoglobin of 14.8 g/dL, and a platelet count of 146,000/µL. Serum troponin I levels were normal. Mild renal impairment was noted (creatinine 1.33 mg/dL), with an INR of 1.2; other electrolyte and metabolic parameters were unremarkable.

Due to his chest pain and non-specific changes in electrocardiogram (Fig. 1), a diagnostic coronary angiography was performed in that center a week before he came to our center and it revealed a right-dominant system with severe three-vessel disease involving the left main coronary artery, with slow coronary flow. The left main was 90% stenosed (Fig. 2A), the ostial left anterior descending artery was completely occluded, and there were high-grade stenoses at the origins of the diagonal, ramus, left circumflex, and obtuse marginal branches. The right coronary artery showed moderate proximal and severe distal stenosis (Fig. 2B). The patient was referred to our center for more assessments and surgical revascularization.

Given the associated vertigo, neuroimaging was pursued in our center. A non-contrast CT scan of the head showed periventricular ischemic changes and cortical atrophy. Carotid Doppler ultrasound revealed atherosclerotic plaques in the right common carotid artery and bulb without significant luminal narrowing and preserved flow in both carotid and vertebral arteries. Brain MRI confirmed diffuse cerebral atrophy and multiple small-vessel ischemic changes, along with a small chronic lacunar infarct in the left frontal region.

Due to his chest pain, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was done and demonstrated moderate global left ventricular systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 35%, grade I diastolic dysfunction, and a 13 × 5 mm immobile thrombus adherent to the apex (Fig. 3). The left atrium was mildly enlarged; right-sided chambers and function were preserved. The aortic valve was thickened with doming and demonstrated mild stenosis and insufficiency. A focal 7 × 4 mm thickening was noted on the non-coronary cusp, raising suspicion for a valvular mass. A chest CT-scan was also performed which showed calcified coronary arteries (Fig. 4).

After all of these assessments, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was done for him. Intraoperatively, four coronary artery bypass grafts were placed to the obtuse marginal, ramus, LAD, and PDA. Aortotomy revealed a firm, non-friable 2 × 2 × 1 cm mass attached to the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve. The lesion was excised by shaving, and the native valve leaflets were preserved. Histopathological examination confirmed the mass to be an organized thrombus with no evidence of neoplasm or infective endocarditis.

The postoperative course was uneventful. He was discharged on the following medications: warfarin (target INR 2–3) for the left ventricular thrombus, aspirin 80 mg daily, atorvastatin 40 mg nightly, carvedilol 6.25 mg twice daily, losartan 25 mg daily, and furosemide 20 mg daily.

Discussion

Virchow’s triad basically describes the process of thrombogenesis, which includes hypercoagulability of blood, alteration in blood flow, and endothelial damage [3]. Virchow’s triad can explain the pathogenesis of thrombus formation not only in blood vessels but also on heart valves, particularly prosthetic ones [4]. Thrombosis of native aortic valve is a very rare condition, which could be also described by this phenomenon. One contributing factor is hypercoagulability, observed in individuals with disorders such as protein S or C deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome, or polycythemia [5–8]. Furthermore, valvular and aortic root abnormalities (e.g., bicuspid aortic valve, congenital heart diseases, or ruptured aortic root atheroma) may induce turbulent flow or cause local endothelial disruption, further promoting thrombus formation [2]. In our patient, however, extensive laboratory and imaging work-up revealed no evidence of the classic hypercoagulable disorders or structural valve/root lesions described above. Accordingly, we have classified this case as idiopathic native aortic valve thrombosis.

Potential contributing factors have nevertheless been reported. Common risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can promote thrombosis through mechanisms related to Virchow’s triad, such as platelet hyper-reactivity, coagulative activation, and endothelial dysfunction —though their role in aortic-valve thrombus formation remains unstudied [9–11]. Opium consumption may be another under-recognized contributor in which a report found that opioid use elevates levels of coagulation factors I, II, VII, and IX [12]. While these factors are not established causes of native aortic valve thrombosis, they may represent underlying conditions that contributed to a prothrombotic state in our patient.

Advances in imaging, particularly TEE, have improved detection of aortic valve thrombi. TEE provides superior resolution for assessing leaflet morphology, and valvular masses, and can help differentiate thrombus. However, its utility in aortic valve assessment is limited by the valve’s anterior position and restricted visualization of the left ventricular outflow tract [13]. Current guidelines recommend TTE as the initial modality, with TEE reserved for inconclusive cases. In our patient, TTE findings combined with a clear surgical indication allowed intraoperative diagnosis and excision, making preoperative TEE unnecessary.

Previously reported cases of idiopathic native aortic valve thrombosis have mainly presented with acute thromboembolic events, most commonly MI, followed by limb ischemia, CVA, sudden cardiac arrest, or incidental findings. Management has largely consisted of surgical thrombectomy with vitamin K antagonist therapy, with generally favorable outcomes, although mortality has been reported, particularly in MI or cardiac arrest presentations. However, our patient had no acute embolic complication; the lesion was detected incidentally during CABG work-up and excised intraoperatively with preservation of the native valve. The coexistence of severe multivessel coronary artery disease, left ventricular thrombus, and an organized aortic valve thrombus, together with the patient’s advanced age, makes this case particularly unusual and, to our knowledge, the oldest reported case of idiopathic native aortic valve thrombosis.
