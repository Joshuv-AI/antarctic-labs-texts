Tyrosine kinase Cps2C regulates the synthesis of capsular polysaccharides and plays a role in the virulence of Streptococcus suis serotype 2
Authors: Feng Zheng, Miao Lin, He Fang, Yuhe Wang, Qiaoqiao Wang, Xumiao Liu, Chengliang Tang, Yutao Wang, Zhuqing Shao, Hua Ni, Xiuzhen Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus suis is a gram-positive zoonotic pathogen that causes several serious diseases in humans and various animals. Among its virulence factors, capsular polysaccharides (CPS) play a particularly pivotal role in its pathogenicity. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing CPS synthesis remain elusive. In this study, we identified a gene located at the 5’ end of the cps locus, cps2C, that encoded a tyrosine kinase. Deletion of cps2C resulted in significant changes in bacterial morphology that were characterized by a reduced colony size, an abbreviated chain length, and decreased CPS levels. Through tandem mass-labeled quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis, we discovered significant downregulation in the phosphorylation levels of numerous proteins in the Δcps2C mutant, including key CPS synthesis-associated glycosyltransferases (Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, and NeuB), cell division-related proteins (FtsZ, FtsE, SepF, and DivIVA), and components of the phosphotransferase system (EI, HPr, EIIABMan, and EIICGlu). Both Cps2F and Cps2H were confirmed to be the key substrates of Cps2C via in vitro kinase assays. In addition, deletion of cps2C in S. suis serotype 2 enhanced bacterial adherence to HEp-2 cells, reduced anti-phagocytosis properties, and attenuated pathogenicity in mice. Taken together, our data suggest that Cps2C regulates the capsular synthesis process of S. suis serotype 2, thereby affecting bacterial virulence. These results suggest that Cps2C may be a promising target for controlling S. suis serotype 2 infection.

Introduction

Streptococcus suis is a facultative anaerobic, gram-positive bacterium, as well as one of the most important zoonotic pathogens in the world [1]. It can infect piglets to induce a wide range of diseases, including septicemia, endocarditis, meningitis, arthritis, pneumonia, and even acute death, causing severe economic losses to the pig industry [2]. Moreover, cross-species transmission enables S. suis to infect humans, resulting in endocarditis, meningitis, permanent deafness, and streptococcal toxic shock-like syndrome (STSLS) [3]. In recent years, new serotypes of S. suis and drug-resistant strains have continued to emerge, increasing the difficulty of preventing and controlling this disease [4]. S. suis is therefore considered to be a major burden on public health worldwide.

S. suis can be classified into 29 serotypes according to the antigenic differences of their capsular polysaccharides (CPSs) [5]. Among them, S. suis serotype 2 (S. suis 2) is the most virulent, and it is commonly isolated from diseased pigs or humans [6]. It has been suggested that the CPSs of S. suis 2 play a key role in protecting the organism from phagocytosis by innate immune cells and in masking bacterial surface proteins involved in host cell activation [7–9]. Isogenic unencapsulated S. suis 2 mutants are nonvirulent in mouse and porcine models [10,11]. CPSs consist of a unique arrangement of a variety of sugars, and the CPSs of S. suis are formed by the repetition of specific oligosaccharide units, including glucose, galactose, N-acetylglucosamine, rhamnose, and sialic acid [12]. The capsular structures and components of many S. suis serotypes, including serotypes 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 14, 18, and 1/2, have been determined [13–17]. However, the mechanism of CPS biosynthesis in S. suis 2 is not well understood.

The genome sequencing of S. suis 2 reveals that it has 25 open reading frames encoding proteins related to glycosyltransferases, capsular polysaccharide polymerase, sialic acid synthetases, and regulators, namely ORF2Z, ORF2Y, ORF2X, and Cps2A – Cps2V [12]. Among these, seven enzymes are likely to play a key role in the CPS synthesis process [16]. Zhang et al. constructed various capsular mutants, including Δcps2E, Δcps2G, Δcps2J, and Δcps2L, and found that the CPS content was significantly reduced in these mutants [11]. Another study showed that an unencapsulated strain of S. suis 2 isolated from porcine endocarditis reestablished its capsule, which restored its virulence, after in vivo passage [18]. Furthermore, unencapsulated isolates from porcine endocarditis showed a high mutation frequency of two glycosyltransferases, Cps2E and Cps2F [19]. Additional studies have shown that HP0197 regulates CPS synthesis by enhancing catabolite control protein A (CcpA) activity [20]. These studies indicated that CPSs, as a key virulence factor, is subjected to intricate and complex regulation, including gene mutation and the transcriptional regulation of genes related to CPS synthesis. However, it is not clear whether there are other regulatory mechanisms involved in the process.

Herein, we note that almost all serotypes of S. suis have cps gene clusters located between orfZ-aroA, as well as a conserved gene, cps2C, located on the 5’ side of the clusters [12]. Notably, our previous research found that Cps2C is a homologous protein of the tyrosine kinase CpsD from Streptococcus pneumoniae. Therefore, we theorized that Cps2C was involved in the regulation of capsular synthesis. In this study, we constructed a cps2C mutant strain of S. suis 2, named Δcps2C. Deletion of Cps2C resulted in a significant decrease in CPS levels and morphological changes in the bacterial cells. The results of in vitro kinase assays and phosphoproteomics showed that Cps2C was a protein tyrosine kinase that regulated CPS formation through the direct phosphorylation of glycosyltransferases (Cps2F and Cps2H). Based on the phosphoproteomics results, we hypothesized that Cps2C may also be involved in regulating cell division processes and nutrient uptake. In addition, deletion of cps2C enhanced S. suis 2 adhesion to HEp-2 cells and decreased S. suis 2 resistance to Raw264.7 phagocytosis. The Δcps2C strain exhibited significantly reduced virulence in a mouse infection model and decreased viability in mouse whole blood. Taken together, these findings shed light on new regulatory mechanism of CPS synthesis, enrich the biological functions of Cps2C, and provide new insights into the biology of this clinically important bacterium.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, cells, and growth conditions

The cells, strains, and plasmids associated with this study are listed in Table S1. The highly virulent S. suis 2 wild-type (WT) strain 05ZYH33 was isolated from an infected patient during the 2005 outbreak in Sichuan, China [21,22]. The 05ZYH33 and mutant strains were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, DF0370–17–3) or grown on Columbia blood plates at 37°C. The E. coli DH5α (TransGen Biotech, CD201–01) and BL21 strains (TransGen Biotech, CD601–02) were cultured in Luria – Bertani (LB) broth or grown on LB agar plates at 37°C. E. coli DH5α served as the host strain for cloning, whereas BL21 was used for target protein expression. The following antibiotics were added to the media at the indicated concentrations: for the isogenic mutant strains, spectinomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, S0692) was added at 100 μg/mL; for E. coli, ampicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, A9518) or kanamycin (Sigma-Aldrich, K1377) was added at 100 or 50 μg/mL.

HEp-2 cells were grown in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2 mM glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich 1,294,808), 100 µg/mL penicillin and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, G6784), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, A5256701). RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS without penicillin or streptomycin. For these experiments, HEp-2 cells and Raw264.7 macrophages were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 hours or until the cultures were confluent in 24-well cell culture plates.

Construction of the cps2C knockout S. suis mutant

The recombinant mutant strain Δcps2C was engineered using homologous recombination. First, the spectinomycin resistance (SpcR) gene and the left arm (LA) and right arm (RA) fragments flanking the cps2C gene were amplified by PCR using the 05ZYH33 strain genomic DNA as a template. The DNA fragments were then inserted into the pUC19 vector (Takara, Cat. # 3219) to construct the recombinant plasmid pUC19:LA-Spc-RA. The recombinant plasmid was subsequently used for the efficient transformation of competent 05ZYH33 cells. The success of the transformation was verified by multiplex PCR with specific primers, followed by RT-PCR analysis. Finally, Δcps2C was confirmed by western blot with a Cps2C-specific antibody from our laboratory. The relevant primers are listed in Table S2.

Determination of bacterial growth curves

Single colonies of the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were picked from Columbia blood plates, inoculated in TSB liquid medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and cultured in a shaking incubator for 12 hours at 37°C and 200 rpm. Then, the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were inoculated separately into flasks containing 100 mL of TSB medium at a 1:50 ratio and incubated at 37°C. The absorbance (600 nm) of the cultures was monitored every hour using a spectrophotometer. The cell cultures were diluted 1,000 times using sterile TSB, and 100 μL of the diluted solution was coated and cultured on TSB solid plates at 37°C for 48 hours. The viable bacteria on the plates were then counted.

Bacterial capsule staining

The 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were grown in TSB liquid broth, harvested during the mid-log phase, and then washed three times with PBS. Gram staining was conducted according to the instructions provided with a Gram staining kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, D008–1–2). Malachite green phosphate detection was performed according to the instructions of a malachite green phosphate staining kit (Beyotime, S0196M), and Hiss staining was carried out according to instructions provided by a Hiss staining kit (Bioss, S0223). CFSE labeling was carried out as follows: 5 (6)-carboxyfluorescein diacetate, succinimidyl ester (CFDA-SE 21,888) was added to a bacterial suspension at a concentration of 10 μM, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes. The cells were pelletized by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 5 minutes and washed three times with PBS. The labeled bacterial pellets were air-dried on glass slides and then sealed with an antifade solution containing DAPI (Solarbio, C0060). Images were obtained via a light microscope or an LSM 710 Meta (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

The 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were cultured in 24-well cell culture plates on glass coverslips at 37°C for 24 hours, and then the bacteria were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4) for 2 hours. Following fixation, the bacteria were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes. The bacteria were washed three times with PBS and then incubated in 4% BSA (Sigma, 9048–46–8) in TBST at 25°C for 1 hour. The bacteria were then stained for 2 hours with a rabbit pAb against S. suis 2 CPSs at a 1:100 dilution in 4% bovine serum albumin in TBST. The bacteria were rinsed three times for 5 minutes each time in PBS and incubated with a donkey anti-rabbit FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A11057) at a 1:2000 dilution. Then, the bacteria were washed three times and mounted with antifade solution containing DAPI. Confocal images were obtained via an LSM 710 Meta (Carl Zeiss, Germany) or ultrahigh-resolution microscopy imaging system (DeltaVision OMX SR, GE, USA).

Ultramicroscopic evaluation of the cell morphology

For transmission electron microscopy, the bacterial strains (05ZYH33 and Δcps2C) were harvested during the mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.8), fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 hours, and then washed with PBS. The fixed cells were incubated in 1% osmium tetroxide carboxylate buffer at room temperature for 1 hour in the dark. The precipitated cells were dehydrated using an ethanol gradient for 20 minutes and then embedded in Epon-812 epoxy resin. The samples were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate in ultrathin sections and observed under a JEM-1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

For scanning electron microscopy, all samples were grown in TSB broth and harvested during the mid-log phase. The cells were spotted on poly-lysine coverslips and washed with PBS. The cells were fixed in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4°C for 8 hours. The glutaraldehyde solution was then discarded, and the cells were washed three times with ddH2O, 7 minutes each time. The cells were subsequently dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (70%, 85%, 95%, and 100%) for 15 minutes at each concentration. The cells were dried using carbon dioxide critical point drying and covered with a 10-nm-thick gold/platinum layer. The samples were then observed with a Quanta200 (FEI Co. Ltd., Oregon, USA) scanning electron microscope.

Capsular polysaccharide extraction and content determination

Single colonies were picked from Columbia blood plates containing the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains, inoculated in TSB liquid media (10% FBS), and incubated at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) until the mid-log phase. The suspension was collected through centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C. The pellets were subsequently washed three times with PBS, resuspended in 0.1 M glycine buffer containing lysozyme, and then lysed with stirring at 37°C for 6 hours. The supernatant of the lysate was obtained by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C, after which protease inhibitors (100 μg/mL) were added to the supernatants. Then, DNase and RNase were added to the supernatants at 37°C for 1 hour, after which CaCl2 was added to continue the reaction for 1 hour. Next, one third volume of absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 2 hours at 4°C. The supernatant was obtained through centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C. Then, a triploid volume of absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 16 hours at 4°C. Crude capsular polysaccharide was obtained through centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C. The samples were stored at − 80°C until use.

Protein expression and purification

The primers used in the amplification of the target DNA fragments are listed in Table S1. The gene sequences of cps2C, cps2F and divIVA were amplified from the 05ZYH33 genome using the appropriate primers. The PCR products of cps2C, cps2F and divIVA were digested with BamHI/XhoI and then inserted into a digested pET-32a (Sigma-Aldrich 69,076) or pET-28a vector (Sigma-Aldrich 70,777) to generate the recombinant plasmids pET32a-cps2C, pET32a-cps2F, and pET28a-divIVA, which were subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. The target genes of cps2H, cps2J, and sepF were synthesized using gene synthesis technology. These genes were then inserted into the pET30a vector (Sigma-Aldrich 69,077) through seamless cloning, resulting in the recombinant plasmids pET30a-cps2H, pET30a-cps2J, and pET30a-sepF, which were subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells.

When the OD600 of the suspension reached approximately 0.8, protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Sigma-Aldrich, I6758), and the cells continued to incubate at 20°C for 15 hours. After centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 minutes, the bacteria were collected and lysed using a lysis solution followed by ultrasonic disruption (600 W, 10 minutes). The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was carefully collected. The protein was eluted using pre-equilibrated Ni-NTA agarose (Genscript, L00250) and different concentrations of imidazole. The elution peak around 150 mM imidazole was collected to obtain high-purity protein. The purified protein was identified via a western blot analysis using a peroxidase-conjugated anti-polyhistidine monoclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A7058).

Western blot analysis

Aliquots of total protein lysates (10 μg) from the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Sigma-Aldrich, P2938). The membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% fat-free milk for 2 hours at 25°C and then incubated with primary antibodies specific to p-tyrosine (PTM Bio, PTM-702). Then, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Solarbio, SE12). Specific proteins were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate kit (Millipore, WBKLS0500).

Cps2C kinase enzyme activity assay

Enzyme activity was determined according to the instructions provided with an ADP-Glo™ kinase assay kit (Promega, V6930). Briefly, rCps2C (1 μg) was mixed with 100 mM of the ATP solution and allowed to react for 40 minutes at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding the same volume of termination solution at room temperature for 40 minutes. Then, the same volume of detection solution was added and mixed evenly, and the reaction was performed at room temperature for 30 minutes. The luminescence was subsequently measured with a microplate reader (time setting: 0.25–1s).

Phosphorylation quantitative mass analysis

WT 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C pellets were obtained by centrifuging mid-log phase cultures. The pellets were then homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and lysis buffer was added to the powdered pellets. A small amount of trypsin was added to each protein sample at a 50:1 mass ratio, and the mixture was digested overnight. Subsequently, a final concentration of 5 mM dithiothreitol was added to the samples and incubated at 56°C for 30 minutes. Then, 11 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 25°C for 15 minutes in the dark. Peptides digested by trypsin were desalted with Strata X C18 (Phenomenex) and vacuum freeze-dried. Peptides were dissolved in 0.5 M tetraethylammonium bromide and labeled according to the operating instructions for the TMT kit. The simple procedure was as follows: the labeling reagent was thawed and dissolved in acetonitrile, mixed with peptides, and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Then, the labeled peptides were mixed, desalted, and lyophilized. Peptides were fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The peptides were dissolved in enrichment buffer solution (50% acetonitrile, 6% trifluoroacetic acid), and the supernatant was then transferred to the prewashed IMAC material, which was placed on a rotary shaker and incubated with gentle shaking. Peptides were separated via ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS), then injected into an NSI ion source for ionization and analyzed via Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometry.

The phosphoproteomic differences between the whole-cell proteins of the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strains were analyzed. The experiment used three replicate samples. The fold change of each phosphosite was calculated as the ratio of the mean of three biological replicate values of the sample pairs. A t test was performed on the relative quantified values of each phosphosite in the sample pairs, and the corresponding p-value was calculated. A p-value < 0.05 and a fold change > 1.3 was considered as significantly upregulated, while a fold change < 1/1.3 was considered as significantly downregulated, with a phosphosite localization probability > 0.75. To ensure high-quality analysis results, the false discovery rate for determining the accuracy of identification at the protein spectral, peptide, and protein levels was set to 1%. Proteins were considered identified only if they contained at least one unique peptide.

Adhesion and anti-phagocytosis assays

Bacterial cells in mid-log phase were collected by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 10 minutes, washed 3 times with PBS (pH 7.4), and then adjusted to a density of 5 × 108 CFU/mL. CFDA-SE was labeled as previously described in the Materials and Methods, in the section entitled “Bacterial capsular staining.” Adhesion and antiphagocytosis assays were performed using HEp-2 cells (ATCC CCL23) and Raw264.7 (TIB-71) murine macrophages according to a previous report [23]. Cell-based assays were conducted using 1 × 106 HEp-2 and Raw264.7 cells pretreated with 1 × 108 CFU of CFSE-labeled bacterial cells in a 1:100 ratio at 37°C for 2 hours. For anti-phagocytosis testing, cells were exposed to penicillin (5 μg/mL) and gentamycin (100 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, G3632) for 1 hour in the dark to eliminate extracellular bacteria. After incubation, the pelletized cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for cell lysis. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACSCalibur (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Adhesion and phagocytosis efficiency were quantified based on the normalized mean fluorescence intensity (NMFI).

We also used immunofluorescence staining to observe adhesion and phagocytosis. Briefly, HEp-2 or Raw264.7 cells growing in 24-well cell culture plates were incubated at 37°C for 0, 1, 2, and 4 hours with CFSE-labeled bacterial cells (1 × 107 CFUs) at a ratio of 100:1. The cells were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4) for 2 hours. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes and stained for 30 minutes with TRITC-conjugated phalloidin (Sigma, P1951) at a 1:2000 dilution in TBST. The cells were washed three times for 5 minutes each in PBS and mounted with antifade solution containing DAPI. Confocal images were obtained using an LSM 710 Meta (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Experimental infection of mice

To assess the pathogenic potential of the Δcps2C and 05ZYH33 strains, a bacterial challenge assay was conducted in a previously established mouse model. A total of 30 healthy 4-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade female BALB/c mice (Sipeifu Co., Beijing, China) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10 per group): 1) negative control (sham-infected mice), 2) mice infected with 1 × 108 CFU of the 05ZYH33 strain via intraperitoneal injection, and 3) mice infected with 1 × 108 CFU of the Δcps2C strain via intraperitoneal injection. Negative controls were treated with equal volumes of aseptic TSB medium. The mice were monitored for clinical symptoms for 1 week, and the number of dead mice in each group was recorded. After the completion of the experiment, mice were euthanized using cervical dislocation. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Center for Disease Prevention and Control of the Eastern Theater in Nanjing, China (Animal Ethics Committee Approval #: 2019011). Mice were housed and operated in accordance with relevant regulations.

Detection of serum cytokine levels

Fifteen 4-week-old female SPF BALB/c mice (Sipeifu Co., Beijing, China) were divided into three groups. One group (5 mice) served as the blank control (Mock), while the other groups received intraperitoneal injections of 1 × 108 CFU of the 05ZYH33 and Δcps2C strain. Six hours after the injection, three mice from each group were randomly selected for blood collection via orbital puncture. The blood samples were set aside at 4°C for 3–4 hours, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant was collected and stored at − 20°C.

Inflammatory levels were measured using ELISA. First, the reference standard and samples were added to a 96-well plate and incubated at 25°C for 2 hours. The plate was washed with PBST four times and dried with filter paper. Next, a working solution was added, and the samples were incubated at 25°C for 1 hour, followed by 4 washes with PBST and drying with filter paper. An enzyme-labeled antibody solution was added, and the plate was incubated at 25°C for 30 minutes. After another round of washing with PBST and drying, a substrate working solution was added, and the plate was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. The reaction was then stopped by adding a termination solution to the samples and gently mixing, and the samples were allowed to react for 15 minutes. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme spectrometer, and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard errors, and the normality and homoscedasticity of the data were verified. The statistical significance of differences between the means of three or more independent groups was compared via one-way analysis of variance, with comparisons performed between the experimental and control groups via Student’s t-test. A Kaplan – Meier survival curve analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 8.0 to test for significant differences among the different groups. The following significance levels were adopted: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001; ns indicates no significant difference. All protocols and analyses were performed by blinded and calibrated examiners. This study was conducted from 1 September 2022, to 28 August 2025.

Results

Deletion of cps2C retarded the growth of S. suis 2

To explore the biological function of Cps2C, an isogenic mutant strain, Δcps2C, was constructed through homologous recombination. The mutant was verified through PCR, RT-PCR, and western blot (Figures 1A–H). We found that the average colony diameter of the Δcps2C strain was notably smaller, whiter, and much drier than that of 05ZYH33 (Figure 1I). The average diameter of Δcps2C colonies was 1.46 ± 0.17 mm, which was approximately 0.6-fold smaller than that of the 05ZYH33 (2.39 ± 0.23 mm) (Figure 1J). However, there was no significant difference in the diameter of the hemolytic ring on the blood plate between Δcps2C (2.97 ± 0.23 mm) and 05ZYH33 (3.06 ± 0.22 mm). As a result, the ratio of the hemolytic ring diameter to the colony diameter was significantly increased for the Δcps2C (2.00 ± 0.27) as compared to 05ZYH33 (1.29 ± 0.14) (Figure 1K). To test whether the smaller colony diameter was due to the slower growth rate of the mutant strain, we counted the cell number of the mutant and 05ZYH33 strains at different growth periods. The results showed that the number of viable Δcps2C cells was significantly reduced compared to that of 05ZYH33 at the same growth period (Figure 1L). Deletion of cps2C resulted in changes in bacterial morphology

As cell growth retardation was associated with abnormal cell division, we inspected the morphological cellular changes of the Δcps2C strain. Gram staining revealed that the Δcps2C strain was gram-positive; however, the chain length formed by Δcps2C cells was shorter than the 05ZYH33 strain (Figure 2A). To rule out the possibility that shorter chain lengths were caused by stirring or rotating the culture during the staining process, we inspected the bacterial morphology using the viable cell dye CFSE to label the bacteria resting in 24-well plates. Evidently, the results confirmed that the chain length of the Δcps2C was shorter than that of 05ZYH33 (Figures 2B,C). Additionally, a comparison of the chain length distributions revealed that the proportion of short chains (one to three cells) in the Δcps2C strain was significantly greater than that in the 05ZYH33 (Figure 2D), while the proportion of long chains (over three cells per chain) was significantly lower (Figure 2E). We further observed via scanning electron microscopy that septum formation during cell division in the Δcps2C was abnormal (Figure 2F). Deletion of cps2C resulted in decreased capsule production

Since the cps2C gene was located in the same operon as genes responsible for capsular polysaccharide synthesis, we then inspected changes in the capsular structure of the mutant strain. The results of malachite green phosphate detection and HISS staining revealed that the capsular structure of Δcps2C was significantly decreased (Figures 3A,B). Furthermore, the CPS content of the Δcps2C was significantly lower (14.0 ± 3.00 μg/mL) than that of the 05ZYH33 strain (52.51 ± 10.76 μg/mL, p < 0.0001) as determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method (Figure 3C). Subsequently, we detected the capsular structure with a structural illumination microscope and found that the capsule was reduced in Δcps2C cells compared to 05ZYH33 (Figure 3D). In addition, transmission electron microscopy revealed that the capsule that wraps around the outer side of the cell wall in the parental S. suis 2 cell was clearly decreased in Δcps2C cells (Figure 3E). By measuring the thickness of the capsule, we found that Δcps2C cells displayed a significantly thinner capsule (9.1 ± 1.1 nm) than 05ZYH33 cells (23 ± 2.5 nm, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3F). Furthermore, S. suis 2 usually had two cell morphologies, as shown in Figure 3G: spherical and ellipsoidal. The long axis of the Δcps2C cells was longer (0.60 ± 0.12 μm) than that of the 05ZYH33 cells (0.54 ± 0.11 μm), while the short axis was shorter (0.39 ± 0.028 μm) than that of the 05ZYH33 cells (0.64 ± 0.72 μm) (Figures 3H,I). Owing to this increased length/width ratio (Figure 3J), the shape of the Δcps2C cells was thinner and longer than that of the 05ZYH33 cells, which typically had an ellipsoidal shape. Taken together, the deletion of Cps2C resulted in a significant reduction in CPS content in S. suis 2. Quantitative phosphoproteome screening of putative Cps2C substrates

Since our previous study found that Cps2C is a homologous protein of tyrosine kinase, and also exhibits serine/threonine kinase activity, we first examined the phosphorylation level of total protein of the Δcps2C cells. Clearly, cps2C deletion led to a significant reduction in total protein phosphorylation (Figure 4A). To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying Cps2C regulation of capsular synthesis, we used quantitative phosphoproteomics to screen possible target proteins of Cps2C. We found that the phosphorylation levels were significantly altered at 190 unique phosphorylated sites of 133 proteins in the Δcps2C strain (fold change > 1.5, p < 0.05), of which 141 phosphosites were downregulated (Table S3) and 49 phosphosites were upregulated (Table S4) compared with the 05ZYH33 strain (Figures 4B,C). The downregulated phosphoproteins were analyzed by clusters of orthologous groups (COGs), which revealed that the 94 phosphoproteins were primarily related to translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis [G], carbohydrate transport and metabolism (PTS system and ABC transporter) [J], cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, NeuB, and GlmM) [M], and cell cycle control (FtsE, FtsZ, SepF, DivIVA, and STK) [D] (Figure 4D). Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis found that these proteins were mainly enriched in carbohydrate transport, cell division pathways, and membrane proteins (Figures 4E,F). The differentially expressed phosphoproteins were compared using the STRING (v.11.0) protein network interaction database (confidence score > 0.7), and a protein interaction network diagram was generated (Figure 4G). The results indicate that Cps2C may interact, either directly or indirectly, with capsular synthesis-related proteins, such as Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, NeuB and Cps2H, and cell division-related proteins, including FtsE, FtsZ, SepF, DivIVA, and STK. Among capsular synthesis-associated glycosyltransferases, we identified seven enzymes that likely played a key role in the CPS synthesis process (Figure 5C). We expressed and purified five candidate substrate proteins of Cps2C, including three of the glycosyltransferases (Cps2F, Cps2H, and Cps2J) and two cell division-related proteins (DivIVA and SepF) (Figures 5D,E). Interestingly, Cps2C was found to not only phosphorylate the tyrosine residue of Cps2H, but also the threonine residue (Figure 5F). Furthermore, the 177th tyrosine residue of Cps2F may be a key phosphorylation site of Cps2C (Figure 5G). Deletion of cps2C resulted in enhanced adhesion to HEp-2 cells of S. suis 2

Adhesion and colonization are key steps for pathogenic bacteria to invade the host. It has been reported that the absence of a capsule on S. suis 2 can enhance its adhesion to platelets and promote the progression of infective endocarditis [24]. Similarly, our results demonstrated that the adhesion of Δcps2C strain to HEp-2 cells was significantly enhanced (Figure 6A). With an increasing incubation time from 1 to 4 hours, the number of Δcps2C adhering to the surface of HEp-2 cells increased dramatically from 1.06 ± 0.31 to 32.03 ± 3.15; however, the adhesion rate of 05ZYH33 did not change (0.077 ± 0.035 to 0.079 ± 0.048) (Figure 6B). Furthermore, flow cytometry results showed that the Δcps2C strain had significantly enhanced adhesion to HEp-2 cells (Figures 6C,D). The adhesion ability of Δcps2C (15.57 ± 0.25) was increased nearly 5-fold compared with the 05ZYH33 strain (4.20 ± 0.27) (p < 0.0001, Figure 6E). Next, we analyzed the major organs in the infected mice for damage. Pathological examination of the lung tissue revealed marked bleeding and a small amount of inflammatory cell infiltration in the peribronchial and alveolar spaces, as well as alveolar wall thickening at 6 hours post-infection. These symptoms were further aggravated at 24 hours post-infection (Figure 8N). In contrast, the number of infiltrating inflammatory cells and the histopathological score revealed that the extent of lung damage was much lower in the Δcps2C group (Figures 8O,P). The spleen of 05ZYH33-infected mice was significantly atrophied, including the germinal center, and the margin zone between the red pulp and white pulp was not noticeable. Moreover, the interstitial hemorrhage in the spleen was severe, and the white pulp zone was reduced in 05ZYH33-infected mice (Figure 8Q). The mice infected with the Δcps2C strain had alleviated splenic injury and lower histological scores (Figure 8R,S), such as a visible germinal center, a boundary between the red pulp and white pulp of the spleen, and a reduction in extravasated blood. These results suggest that disruption of cps2C attenuates the pathogenicity of S. suis 2.

Discussion

S. suis is a major pathogen that causes a variety of severe diseases in pigs and humans worldwide with high morbidity and mortality [21]. Because unencapsulated bacteria are almost completely harmless, CPSs are considered one of the most important virulence factors of S. suis [3]. The proper regulation of CPS biosynthesis in S. suis is therefore essential for its survival in the host. In this study, we constructed a cps2C isogenic mutant strain via homologous recombination. Morphological observation revealed that the capsule was significantly reduced or partially absent in the Δcps2C strain. In addition, both in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that the Δcps2C strain exhibited reduced pathogenicity. By performing a comparative phosphoproteomic analysis, we found that the downregulated phosphoproteins were associated with the following biological processes: 1) carbohydrate transport and metabolism; 2) cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis; 3) cell cycle control; and 4) translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis. These findings suggest that Cps2C in S. suis 2 is equipped with a complex regulatory mechanism.

Capsular polysaccharides are essential components of the bacterial cell surface; they have been found to play an important role in the interaction between cells and the environment and are important virulence factors for many pathogens [25]. Although these glycosyltransferases are essential for CPS biosynthesis, their regulation is poorly studied. A previous study revealed that CpsD was an autophosphorylating protein-tyrosine kinase, and mutation of CpsD resulted in the loss of encapsulation or a lower amount of capsular polysaccharide than that in a WT strain of S. pneumoniae [26,27]. Moreover, the phosphorylation of CpsD may be increased by SpAdK (adenylate kinase in S. pneumoniae), leading to increased CPS production [28]. These findings suggest a positive correlation between tyrosine phosphorylation of CpsD and CPS production in S. pneumoniae. CpsD-mediated phosphorylation of endogenous substrates may regulate capsule biosynthesis and export. For example, the CpsE homolog, EpsE, is phosphorylated and activated by the CpsD homolog, EpsD, in Streptococcus thermophilus [29]. A recent study revealed that the CpsC/CpsD complex was able to capture the polysaccharide polymerase CpsH at the division site, regulating the capsule polymerization process in S. pneumoniae [30]. An acceptable hypothesis is that the same event could occur in S. suis 2. Interestingly, in this study, the abundance of phosphoproteins, including Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, NeuB, and a glycosyltransferase located outside the cps locus, was significantly lower in the Δcps2C strain than in the 05ZYH33 strain (Table S3). On the basis of the above studies and our experimental results, we speculate that Cps2C, a homolog of CpsD, participates in the capsule synthesis of S. suis 2 by phosphorylating its downstream enzymes, such as Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, and NeuB. Disruption of Cps2C leads to a decrease in enzyme activity and the termination of capsule synthesis. In this study, we demonstrated through in vitro phosphorylation experiments that Cps2C phosphorylated Cps2F and Cps2H. However, when we used homologous recombination to transform the E. coli-S. suis shuttle plasmid pVA-838-cps2F177Y/E or pVA-838-cps2H158S/E into Δcps2C competent cells, we were unable to select for phosphorylation mutant strains of Cps2F or Cps2H. Therefore, elucidating the effects on capsule synthesis regulation of phosphorylation at key sites of the glycosyltransferases Cps2F and Cps2H still requires further experimental verification. In future studies, focusing on the phosphorylation modifications of glycosyltransferases involved in capsule synthesis could be a meaningful direction for exploring their regulation of capsule formation.

Peptidoglycan (PG) is a major component of the bacterial cell wall and protects cells from mechanical stress. Peptidoglycan biosynthesis is found to be similar in all bacteria and is essential for cell morphology and survival. S. suis, a member of the phylum Firmicutes, is a gram-positive bacterium that possesses a thick cell wall. In this study, we found that the phosphorylation of several proteins involved in peptidoglycan synthesis, including GlmS, GlmM, and STK, was significantly downregulated in Δcps2C (Table S3). GlmS is a glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase involved in the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate (Fru-6P) to glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcN-6P), which is the initial substrate for peptidoglycan synthesis [31]. In Streptococcus mutans, glmS deletion mutants cannot be cultured in TSB but can survive after the addition of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), indicating the importance and necessity of the gene in cell growth and division [32]. Additionally, GlmM, a phosphoglucosamine mutase, catalyzes the conversion of glucosamine 6-phosphate to glucosamine 1-phosphate, an early step in the formation of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, which is an essential common precursor for cell envelope components, such as peptidoglycan, muramic acid, and lipopolysaccharide [33]. GlmM has been characterized in various bacteria, and its mutation appears to influence bacterial cell growth and morphology, biofilm formation, and sensitivity to penicillins due to a decrease in the key precursor of peptidoglycan, UDP-N-acetylglucosamine [34–37]. Recent studies have suggested that GlmM is phosphorylated by the eukaryotic serine-threonine kinase STK at the Ser-101 site and that the mutation strain exhibited a similar phenotype to Δstk, such as increased cell volume, an absent capsule, decreased resistance, and decreased survival caused by unusual peptidoglycan synthesis in S. suis 2 [38]. As a global regulator, STK is associated with multiple cellular biological processes, among which the synthesis or activity of several key enzymes involved in PG metabolism is also regulated by STK. Taken together, a deficiency in the tyrosine/threonine/serine phosphorylation in these proteins may disrupt their function, and therefore, affect PG synthesis and metabolism, which, in turn, may contribute to delayed growth in cps2C mutant strains.

It is important to maintain a balance between cell growth and division, which requires complex spatiotemporal regulation to maintain precise proliferation and proper morphology [39,40]. The processes of cell division and capsule synthesis are precisely coordinated, and the tyrosine kinase CpsD is important in this process. In S. pneumoniae, dysfunction of CpsD was reported to cause elongated cells and delay cell constriction. To date, only the chromosome- partitioning protein ParB has been shown to interact with CpsD, but whether ParB is phosphorylated is unclear [30]. Our results revealed that Δcps2C cells had a relatively long morphology, which was consistent with previous studies showing impaired cell division. Comparative phosphoproteomic data from this study revealed that among the numerous substrates of Cps2C, cell division-related proteins were significantly enriched in the COG classification. For example, FtsZ, FtsE, DivIVA, SepF, actin-like ATPase, GlmM, and poorly characterized membrane proteins associated with cell division showed lower phosphoprotein abundances in Δcps2C than in 05ZYH33. Notably, protein phosphorylation mediated by STKs has been confirmed to be a key regulatory mechanism for cell division and morphogenesis [41]. Several substrates of STK are involved in the regulation of cell division site localization, nascent peptidoglycan synthesis, and cell elongation, including FtsZ [42], MapZ [43], DivIVA [23], GlmM [38], and MurC [44]. Our quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis revealed that five STK phosphorylation sites (48Y/292T/305T/313S/319T) were significantly downregulated in the Δcps2C mutant. This raises an interesting question of whether there is a mechanism of phosphorylation cross-talk between STK and Cps2C. The function of Cps2C in cell division needs to be further studied, and confirming the interaction between these candidate substrates and Cps2C can provide insights into its biological functions.

The phosphotransferase system (PTS) is a complex enzyme system that includes enzyme I (EI), histidine phosphate carrier protein (HPr), and the enzyme II complex, which mainly phosphorylates various sugars and their derivatives and then transports them into the cell. PTS not only participates in the metabolism of carbohydrates, but also regulates the virulence of some pathogens. According to the quantitative phosphoproteomic results, the levels of phosphorylation of EI, HPr, EIIABMan, and EIICGlu were significantly decreased at the 183T, 12T/20T/46S, 277S, and 144S/209S/272T/274S/269S residues, respectively (Table S3). A previous study revealed that EIIABMan and EIICGlu are involved in carbon catabolite repression (CCR) and regulate the expression of known virulence genes in streptococci [45]. For example, in S. mutans, EIIABM is involved in exopolysaccharide synthesis and biofilm formation [46]. In addition, a study on S. suis 2 revealed that the mannose-specific phosphotransferase system component IID negatively regulated the expression of suilysin, an important virulence factor [47]. The PTS may affect the synthesis of capsular polysaccharides by regulating the uptake and metabolism of various sugars. Cps2C-dependent regulation the phosphorylation of PTS components may be another aspect of how Cps2C regulates capsule synthesis.

In conclusion, this work demonstrated that the inactivation of cps2C disrupted diverse cellular activities of S. suis 2, including capsular biosynthesis (Cps2F, Cps2H, Cps2J, NeuB, and a glycosyltransferase), cell division (FtsZ, FtsE, DivIVA, SepF, actin-like ATPase, and GlmM), sugar transport (PTS system and ABC transporter), and virulence (e.g. enolase, Adk). Based on the results of phosphoproteomics and in vitro kinase assays, Cps2C directly phosphorylates the glycosyltransferases CpsF and Cps2H involved in capsule synthesis. We hypothesized that Cps2C may regulate capsule synthesis through the phosphorylation of key enzymes in the process. The capsule loss of the Δcps2C strain is not only related to the abolished phosphorylation of key glycosyltransferases, but that the phosphoproteomic changes in the PTS system and cell division-related proteins may also have complex influences. Overall, Cps2C may play a key role in regulating capsule synthesis and also influence bacterial virulence and pathogenicity through multiple mechanisms.
