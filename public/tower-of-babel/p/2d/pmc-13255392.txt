Introduction

Pincer-type cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is a distinct and severe clinical subtype of CSM, characterized by simultaneous spinal cord compression from both the ventral and dorsal aspects of the spinal canal [1]. Given the high risk of rapid neurological deterioration, current consensus dictates that surgical intervention should be performed promptly once the diagnosis is established [2, 3]. Standard surgical options primarily include isolated anterior approaches, isolated posterior approaches, or combined anterior-posterior (A-P) procedures. Driven by a deeper understanding of cervical pathology and advancements in surgical instrumentation, single-stage A-P surgery has gained increasing clinical traction for its ability to provide comprehensive decompression [4].

However, traditional open A-P surgery is frequently associated with substantial surgical trauma, including prolonged operative times, significant intraoperative blood loss, and a high incidence of postoperative complications such as axial symptoms [5]. The recent evolution of spinal endoscopy has offered a promising alternative to mitigate the invasiveness of combined procedures. Evidence suggests that anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) combined with endoscopic techniques can achieve safe and effective results in this patient population [6]. Specifically, unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) has matured as a robust technique for both cervical and lumbar disorders, offering the advantages of a dual-portal design, a spacious working environment, and a stable, high-definition visual field [7, 8].

Despite these technical advances, a significant gap remains in clinical practice: there is a lack of simplified, quantifiable imaging indicators to determine precisely when the dorsal component of a “pincer” compression necessitates concurrent surgical management. Furthermore, a standardized, minimally invasive clinical pathway for circumferential decompression has yet to be fully established. In this report, we present the case of an elderly patient with pincer-type CSM where the “posterior occupancy ratio (POR)” was employed as a quantitative metric to evaluate dorsal stenosis and guide a single-stage ACDF combined with posterior UBE decompression. We describe the favorable early outcomes and provide a comprehensive review of the relevant literature to discuss the feasibility of this objective, minimally invasive strategy.

Case presentation

Clinical data

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second People’s Hospital Affiliated to Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the anonymous publication of clinical data and associated images.

A 75-year-old man had a long-standing history of neck pain for 10 years, and developed progressive numbness and weakness of the left upper extremity over the preceding 10 days. Physical examination upon admission revealed restricted cervical range of motion and localized tenderness over the C4/5 spinous processes. On neurological examination, pinprick and light-touch sensation were decreased over the left C6/7 dermatomes. Manual muscle testing showed grade 4/5 weakness in the left wrist extensors (C6) and triceps (C7), with preserved strength (5/5) in deltoid (C5), finger flexors (C8), and intrinsic hand muscles (T1). Deep tendon reflexes were brisk in the upper extremities, with a positive Hoffmann sign on the left. Tandem gait was unstable, and fine motor tasks (e.g., buttoning) were impaired.

Radiographic evaluation, including static and dynamic cervical X-rays, showed degenerative changes and C4 spondylolisthesis (Fig. 1A–C). Cervical MRI identified ventral disc herniations at C3/4 and C4/5. Notably, the C3/4 level exhibited concomitant dorsal ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (LFH), resulting in a “pincer-like” cord compression. To quantify the dorsal contribution to stenosis, the posterior occupancy ratio (POR) was measured at the most stenotic axial T2-weighted MRI slice (C3/4). The maximal protrusion distance of the hypertrophied ligamentum flavum was a = 4.22 mm, and the bony sagittal canal diameter was b = 8.75 mm, yielding a POR of 48%(Fig. 1D–E). As this value exceeded the proposed 40% threshold, isolated ACDF was considered unlikely to achieve sufficient posterior decompression; therefore, a single-stage hybrid strategy (ACDF + posterior UBE) was planned. An intramedullary T2-weighted hyperintensity was observed at this level, indicating myelomalacia. Based on the integration of clinical symptoms, physical signs, and multi-modal imaging, a diagnosis of pincer-type cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) was established. Preoperative assessment concluded that ventral (C3/4, C4/5) and dorsal (C3/4) compressive factors necessitated a single-stage circumferential decompression, as isolated anterior indirect decompression was deemed insufficient.

Surgical procedure and technical nuances

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position with the neck slightly extended in a neutral, fixed position. A standard Smith-Robinson anterior approach was utilized to expose the C3/4 and C4/5 levels. Following discectomy, the posterior longitudinal ligament was resected to expose the dura and ensure thorough decompression. Interbody fusion cages were implanted at C3/4 and C4/5, followed by anterior plate fixation to restore cervical stability and sagittal alignment.

The patient was then carefully repositioned to a prone position, avoiding hyperextension, with intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) employed as needed. A posterior UBE approach was initiated at the C3/4 level. After establishing the viewing and working portals, the laminae and interlaminar spaces were visualized under continuous saline irrigation. The irrigation pressure was maintained at the minimum level required for clear visualization and dynamically adjusted based on dural tension and hemostasis to prevent secondary cord compression from high hydrostatic pressure. A laminotomy was performed centered on the C3/4 interlaminar space, with partial resection of the laminae and medial facets. The LFH was detached and removed bilaterally until the dural sac was fully decompressed and spontaneous pulsations were restored. A drainage tube was placed before layered wound closure. This hybrid approach achieved 360° circumferential decompression while minimizing posterior soft-tissue dissection.

Perioperative outcomes and follow-up

The procedure was completed successfully with a total operative time of 185 min and an estimated blood loss of 50 mL. Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable without complications such as neurological deterioration, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, surgical site infection, or C5 nerve root palsy. Ambulation was initiated on postoperative day 1, and the patient was discharged on day 5.

During the follow-up period, the patient reported significant relief from neck pain and radicular symptoms. Physical examination showed a conversion to a negative Hoffmann’s sign and normalized tendon reflexes. At the 3-month follow-up, the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score had improved from 11 preoperatively to 16. Follow-up MRI confirmed the complete resolution of circumferential compression at C3/4, restoration of spinal cord morphology, and a reduction in the extent of the T2-weighted intramedullary hyperintensity(Fig. 2E–F). At 6 months, radiographs demonstrated stable hardware positioning and satisfactory fusion at C3/4 and C4/5, with no evidence of implant loosening or instability(Fig. 2A–B). No significant axial symptoms or recurrent neurological deficits were observed.

Supplementary institutional case

To illustrate the potential pitfall of underestimating posterior compression, we additionally present an institutional illustrative case (Fig. 3). A 60-year-old man underwent single-level ACDF for ventral pathology but showed limited neurological improvement due to persistent posterior compression from hypertrophied ligamentum flavum on postoperative MRI (Fig. 3C–D). He subsequently received posterior decompression (posterior single-door laminoplasty with spinal canal enlargement), achieving adequate canal expansion and symptomatic relief (Fig. 3E–F). Written informed consent was obtained, and the case was included under the same institutional ethics approval. This case is included solely to illustrate the risk of neglecting posterior compression and shall not be incorporated into the statistical conclusions.

2. Discussion

The “pincer-type” cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) presents a unique clinical challenge due to the simultaneous “clamping” effect from both ventral and dorsal spinal cord compression. Given the high risk of irreversible neurological damage, surgical decompression is the primary intervention to halt disease progression and facilitate functional recovery [9]. Current surgical strategies—comprising isolated anterior, isolated posterior, or combined anterior-posterior approaches—each possess specific indications and inherent limitations.

Anterior approaches, such as ACDF and anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF), are highly effective for directly removing ventral pathology (e.g., disc herniations, osteophytes) and restoring disc height and sagittal alignment. However, they provide limited direct decompression for dorsal pathologies like ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (LFH) or facet joint hyperplasia. In cases where posterior compression is significant, relying solely on indirect decompression via anterior column lengthening may prove insufficient [10, 11]. Conversely, posterior approaches (laminectomy or laminoplasty) offer extensive dorsal decompression but are less effective for ventral disc herniations. Furthermore, traditional open posterior surgery requires extensive stripping of the paraspinal musculature, often leading to debilitating complications such as chronic axial neck pain and muscle denervation [12, 13]. While traditional single-stage open A-P surgery achieves circumferential decompression, its widespread adoption is hindered by substantial surgical trauma, prolonged operative times, and high blood loss, particularly in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities [14]. Therefore, achieving thorough 360° decompression while minimizing soft-tissue trauma remains a critical objective in the management of pincer-type CSM.

As spinal surgery transitions toward precision medicine, clinical decision-making is shifting from subjective experience to objective, quantifiable imaging metrics. In pincer-type CSM, surgical failure often stems from underestimating the “static” or “dynamic” dorsal compression when focusing primarily on ventral pathology [15, 16]. In particular, neck hyperextension may aggravate dorsal compression through buckling of the hypertrophied ligamentum flavum, thereby intensifying the ‘pincer’ effect on the spinal cord (Fig. 4).

Schematic illustration of the dynamic compression mechanism. Hyperextension of the neck leads to the buckling or folding of the ligamentum flavum, which acutely exacerbates spinal cord compression in patients with pre-existing stenosis

This dynamic component helps explain clinical fluctuations and underscores the rationale for directly addressing the posterior element when dorsal stenosis is substantial. The success of the clinical decision in this case was predicated on the preoperative application of the Posterior Occupancy Ratio (POR). We defined POR as the ratio of the maximal protrusion distance of the hypertrophied or buckled ligamentum flavum (a) to the bony sagittal diameter of the spinal canal (b) on axial T2-weighted MRI: POR = a/b×100%. To improve reproducibility, POR should be measured on the axial T2-weighted slice showing the maximal posterior indentation at the index level, using a standardized window setting and clear anatomical landmarks (posterior margin of the ligamentum flavum for a, and the bony canal boundary for b). Because POR is inherently a two-dimensional surrogate and can be affected by slice selection, patient positioning, and reader-dependent measurements, we consider the 40% value a pragmatic reference point derived from prior reports rather than a definitive decision boundary. In clinical practice, POR is best interpreted together with neurological severity, dynamic factors, sagittal alignment, and the distribution of ventral versus dorsal pathology. POR was measured independently by two experienced spine surgeons and the averaged value was used for planning; formal reliability testing was not performed. Previous studies suggest that when this ratio exceeds 40%, the dorsal ligamentous structures exert a significant space-occupying effect that is unlikely to be adequately resolved by indirect anterior decompression alone [10, 17]. In our patient, the POR at C3/4 reached 48%, significantly exceeding the threshold and providing a robust objective rationale for a combined A-P approach over isolated ACDF. Our clinical experience (as illustrated in our supplementary case, Fig. 3) reinforces that ignoring a high POR often leads to incomplete decompression and suboptimal recovery, necessitating revision surgery. Therefore, a POR around 40% may serve as a useful adjunctive indicator to flag substantial posterior element contribution; however, the optimal cut-off and its predictive performance should be confirmed in larger cohorts.

Guided by this quantitative metric, we propose a systemic, stepwise treatment framework for pincer-type CSM (Fig. 5). The core of this framework is the alignment of surgical selection with the specific occupancy characteristics of the compression.

The “ACDF + Posterior UBE” protocol utilized in this case represents an advanced minimally invasive solution within this framework. By combining direct ventral clearance and height restoration via ACDF with the high-definition, targeted dorsal decompression provided by UBE, we achieved 360° circumferential decompression while markedly reducing soft-tissue dissection [20]. This approach epitomizes the modern surgical philosophy of balancing thorough decompression with functional preservation.

Despite the favorable outcome, this study has several limitations:

As a single case report with a relatively short follow-up, the long-term rates of fusion, adjacent segment degeneration, and axial symptoms remain to be evaluated.

Conclusion

This case suggests that a single-stage hybrid strategy combining ACDF and posterior UBE can be a feasible minimally invasive option for selected patients with pincer-type CSM presenting with substantial dual-directional compression. POR may serve as an objective adjunct to preoperative imaging assessment to help characterize the posterior component; however, the proposed threshold and the associated framework should be interpreted cautiously and validated through larger, prospective studies with reliability testing and longer follow-up.
