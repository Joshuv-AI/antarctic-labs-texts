Feasibility and reproducibility of a novel introital exo‐anal ultrasound approach for assessment of the external anal sphincter
Authors: Aly Youssef, Vito Bernardi, Maddalena Paoli, Irene Fioravanti, Elisa Fortuna, Aurora Del Magno, Elena Brunelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObstetric anal sphincter injuries are a major cause of anal incontinence and are frequently underdiagnosed at delivery. Imaging techniques that are feasible, reproducible, and applicable in obstetric practice may be useful to support assessment in selected clinical situations. This study evaluated a three‐dimensional exo‐anal introital ultrasound technique combining linear freehand reconstruction (Polyline OmniView) and contrast enhancement using Volume Contrast Imaging (VCI) to enable coronal reconstruction and measurement of the anterior external anal sphincter (EAS).Material and MethodsThis was a prospective feasibility and reproducibility study conducted at Sant'Orsola University Hospital, Bologna, Italy. Nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies were recruited between September 2023 and September 2024. 3D introital exo‐anal ultrasound volumes were acquired using a high‐frequency transvaginal probe. Volumes were reconstructed using Polyline OmniView combined with VCI to obtain a coronal view of the anterior EAS. The primary outcome was the feasibility of measuring the coronal length of the anterior portion of the EAS. The secondary outcome was intra‐ and interobserver reproducibility of this measurement, obtained after standardized offline volume analysis.ResultsSeventy women were included. Measurement of the anterior EAS length was feasible in all cases (100%). Reproducibility was excellent, with minimal mean differences, intraclass correlation coefficient values of 0.96 (95% CI, 0.94–0.98) for intraobserver agreement and 0.97 (95% CI, 0.96–0.99) for interobserver agreement, and narrow limits of agreement on Bland–Altman analysis. The mean length of the anterior EAS was 14.4 ± 1.5 mm for the first measurement by Operator 1, 14.4 ± 1.6 mm for the second measurement by Operator 1, and 14.6 ± 1.6 mm for measurements by Operator 2.ConclusionsThree‐dimensional exo‐anal introital ultrasound incorporating linear freehand reconstruction and contrast enhancement allows feasible and highly reproducible measurement of the anterior external anal sphincter. This approach may represent a feasible imaging option for anal sphincter assessment and warrants further evaluation not only in women with suspected obstetric anal sphincter injuries but also in the longitudinal follow‐up of women with OASIS and in the assessment of patients presenting with anal incontinence.Keywords: anal sphincter [MeSH], fecal incontinence [MeSH], obstetric anal sphincter injuries, obstetric labor complications [MeSH], Transperineal ultrasound, ultrasonography [MeSH]

Key Message

Three‐dimensional introital exo‐anal ultrasound with a high‐frequency transvaginal probe allows reproducible visualization and measurement of the anterior external anal sphincter in pregnancy, supporting the feasibility of this approach and warranting evaluation in postpartum women with suspected obstetric anal sphincter injury.

INTRODUCTION

Obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS) are a major cause of anal incontinence in women. 1 , 2 Their true incidence is likely underestimated, as ultrasound studies suggest that a substantial proportion of sphincter injuries remain undetected at delivery. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Careful clinical examination, appropriate training, and improvement in immediate diagnosis and repair remain the cornerstone of management. Ultrasound imaging, however, plays an important role in the subsequent assessment of sphincter integrity, including detection of previously unrecognized sphincter injuries, evaluation of functional symptoms, long‐term follow‐up, and quality assessment of diagnosis and repair. In this context, imaging techniques that allow accurate visualization and quantitative assessment of anal sphincter morphology may support anatomical and functional evaluation in selected clinical situations.

While three‐dimensional endoanal ultrasound is considered the gold standard for sphincter assessment, 3 , 8 , 9 its use in obstetric settings is limited by availability, invasiveness, and the need for dedicated expertise. Alternative ultrasound approaches that are more accessible and less invasive may therefore be of interest, particularly if they allow reliable visualization of sphincter anatomy. 10

Magnetic resonance imaging has also been explored for evaluation of the anal sphincter complex; however, its high cost, limited accessibility, and lower practical applicability restrict its clinical utility in the obstetric setting. 11

Transperineal ultrasound is a well‐established, objective, and reproducible imaging technique for assessment of the pelvic floor. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Four‐dimensional transperineal ultrasound (4D‐TPUS) has also been proposed as a less invasive and more accessible alternative for assessing anal sphincter integrity, with good reproducibility and agreement with endoanal ultrasound. 13 , 19 , 20 However, image acquisition may be suboptimal in certain clinical scenarios, particularly in the presence of significant perineal trauma. 21

Previous studies have described the use of a transvaginal transducer for assessment of the external anal sphincter (introital exo‐anal ultrasound), exploiting the close proximity of the probe to the anal canal and the possibility of using higher ultrasound frequencies. 22 , 23

Emerging evidence suggests that quantitative assessment of anal sphincter morphology may be clinically relevant. In particular, studies using three‐dimensional endoanal ultrasound have demonstrated that, among women who sustain obstetric anal sphincter injuries, external anal sphincter thickness correlates with the severity of anal incontinence. 24 However, such detailed morphometric assessment is not currently reliably achievable with standard transperineal ultrasound, which remains the most widely available imaging modality in obstetric practice.

Building on these considerations, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility and reproducibility of a three‐dimensional reconstruction approach for quantitative assessment of the anterior external anal sphincter, based on introital exo‐anal ultrasound using a high‐frequency transvaginal probe combined with linear freehand reconstruction (Polyline OmniView) and contrast enhancement (Volume Contrast Imaging; VCI). The primary objective was to assess the feasibility of quantitative measurement of the anterior external anal sphincter, and the secondary objective was to evaluate the reproducibility of this measurement using standardized offline analysis of stored 3D volumes.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

A non‐consecutive series of nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies, at various gestational ages and prior to labor onset, were enrolled between September 2023 and September 2024, either during outpatient visits or upon admission to the Obstetrics and Prenatal Medicine Unit of the IRCCS Sant'Orsola University Hospital, Bologna, Italy. Women were recruited when one of the investigators was present for the aim of the study. Pregnant nulliparous women were selected both for practical reasons, as the study was conducted in an obstetric unit where pregnant women represent the most readily available population, and to ensure evaluation of the anal sphincter in a population unlikely to have pre‐existing obstetric sphincter injury.

3D ultrasound volume acquisition

All participants underwent introital exo‐anal ultrasound using a 3D high‐frequency transvaginal probe (5–9 MHz, Voluson E8 or E10, GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria). Examinations were performed with the women in the dorsal lithotomy position, with the hips flexed and slightly abducted. The probe was positioned in the axial plane, vertically at the vaginal introitus at the level of the posterior fourchette, with its tip oriented perpendicularly to the anal canal while avoiding excessive pressure on the anal sphincter (Figure 1A). To ensure that the acquisition plane was appropriately low, the operator first moved the probe caudally until the anal canal disappeared—resulting in a black screen due to contact with air—and then advanced cranially by a few millimeters until both the external and internal anal sphincters were clearly visualized (Figure 1B). The patient was instructed to remain still for a few seconds during acquisition, and a minimum acquisition angle of 120° together with maximum 3D quality settings was used.

Each volume was anonymized and stored for subsequent offline analysis. All volumes were acquired by seven operators (Aly Youssef, Vito Bernardi, Maddalena Paoli, Irene Fioravanti, Elisa Fortuna, Aurora Del Magno, and Elena Brunelli). The first author (Aly Youssef), an expert in 3D pelvic floor and anal sphincter ultrasound who developed the technique, trained the other six investigators and supervised ten volume acquisitions performed by each operator prior to study commencement. At the time of the study, Elena Brunelli had more than 5 years of experience in 3D transperineal anal sphincter ultrasound, whereas Vito Bernardi, Maddalena Paoli, Irene Fioravanti, Elisa Fortuna, and Aurora Del Magno had less than 1 year of experience with 3D transperineal anal sphincter ultrasound.

3D ultrasound volume analysis

All analyses were performed offline on stored 3D volumes using a standardized reconstruction protocol. Analysis of each volume began with multiplanar reconstruction to achieve optimal alignment of the data set (Figure 2). In particular, correct alignment of the anal canal was ensured by positioning it horizontally in the sagittal plane (Figure 2B) and vertically in the coronal plane (Figure 2C), with adjustments performed as needed.

Illustrates the multiplanar display of the anal sphincter complex across the three orthogonal planes. In the axial plane (A), the internal anal sphincter (IAS) appears as an anechoic circular structure surrounding the anal canal, while the external anal sphincter (EAS) forms a hyperechoic outer layer. In both the axial (A) and sagittal (B) planes, arrows indicate the IAS and EAS. The central area (asterisk) corresponds to the anal mucosa and is visible in all three planes. In the coronal plane (C), the levator ani muscle (LAM) is visible bilaterally. The sagittal and coronal planes are used to achieve correct spatial alignment prior to reconstruction. In particular, the anal canal is aligned horizontally in the sagittal plane and vertically in the coronal plane to ensure accurate orientation of the data set before application of the curvilinear reconstruction technique (Polyline OmniView; GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria).

After plane alignment, curvilinear reconstruction using the OmniView function with freehand Polyline tracing was performed. 25 A curved line was manually traced across the thickness of the anterior portion of the external anal sphincter (EAS) in the axial (transverse) plane. The curved line was traced along the anterior semicircular portion of the external anal sphincter, extending symmetrically from approximately the 3 o'clock to the 9 o'clock position, using the midline (12 o'clock position) as the reference point. This procedure generated a three‐dimensional coronal rendering of the selected structure on the right side of the screen, corresponding to the anterior portion of the EAS (Figure 3).

The figure demonstrates the use of the linear reconstruction technique (OmniView; GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria) with Polyline reconstruction applied in the axial plane (A). Freehand tracing of the anterior semicircular portion of the external anal sphincter is performed, generating a reconstructed image displayed in the right panel (B), which is used for quantitative measurement of sphincter length. The measurement of the central portion of the external anal sphincter is indicated by a double‐headed arrow.

Image quality was further optimized by applying thick‐slice contrast enhancement using Volume Contrast Imaging (VCI; GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria). The VCI thickness was set at 2–3 mm, corresponding approximately to the reported thickness of the anterior portion of the EAS in the literature 26 , 27 (Figure 3, Video S1). Once reconstruction was completed, the length of the anterior portion of the EAS was measured at the midline (central component) on the coronal view (Figure 3).

To assess intra‐ and interobserver reproducibility of the EAS length measurement, three independent assessments were performed for each acquired volume: two by Operator 1 (Aly Youssef) and one by Operator 2 (Vito Bernardi). For each assessment, the operator reopened the volume and repeated multiplanar alignment, Polyline tracing, VCI optimization, and the final length measurement on the coronal reconstruction. The first assessment performed by Operator 1 was used for interobserver analysis. A minimum interval of 1 week was maintained between the two assessments performed by Operator 1 to reduce recall bias. Operators were blinded to each other's measurements.

Statistical analysis

Mean (SD) and n (%) were used as descriptive analysis. Intraobserver reproducibility was assessed by comparing the two measurements obtained by Operator 1 on the same stored volume. Interobserver reproducibility was assessed by comparing the mean of the two measurements obtained by Operator 1 with the measurement obtained by Operator 2.

Agreement between measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For intraobserver reproducibility, ICC was calculated using a two‐way mixed‐effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements. For interobserver reproducibility, ICC was calculated using a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements.

Agreement was further evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis. The differences between measurements were plotted against their mean, and limits of agreement were calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations of the differences. Confidence intervals for the limits of agreement were calculated according to the method described by Bland and Altman. 28

To investigate potential factors influencing measurement variability, the absolute differences between measurements were calculated. Separate linear regression models were fitted for intraobserver and interobserver measurement variability using the absolute difference between measurements as the dependent variable and maternal age and body mass index (BMI) as continuous predictors.

As this was a pilot study, no sample size calculation was performed. All data were analyzed using SPSS software version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Stata 15 (StataCorp., College Station, TX, USA). The significance level was set at 0.05.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that measurement of the anterior external anal sphincter using a novel three‐dimensional introital ultrasound reconstruction technique is feasible and highly reproducible. Both intraobserver and interobserver agreement was excellent, with narrow limits of agreement on Bland–Altman analysis.

Obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS) remain a major cause of anal incontinence and long‐term pelvic floor morbidity in women. Despite their clinical relevance, OASIS are frequently underdiagnosed at the time of delivery, highlighting the importance of adequate clinical training and meticulous perineal and rectal examination. 29 , 30 At the same time, imaging techniques that are accurate, reproducible, and applicable in obstetric practice may play a supportive role in improving detection, particularly in complex or equivocal cases. In this study, we evaluated a 3D transperineal introital exo‐anal ultrasound approach using a transvaginal probe combined with linear freehand reconstruction (Polyline OmniView) and contrast enhancement using Volume Contrast Imaging (VCI) to visualize and measure the anterior portion of the external anal sphincter (EAS) in the coronal plane.

Our results demonstrate that this combined reconstruction approach is highly feasible and reproducible. Measurement of the anterior EAS length was possible in all examined cases, with excellent intra‐ and interobserver agreement and narrow limits of agreement. These findings indicate that the technique provides consistent and reliable measurements. Importantly, the aim of this study was not to assess diagnostic accuracy for sphincter injury, but to establish a reproducible method for quantitative evaluation of sphincter morphology.

An additional practical advantage of this approach is that, once appropriate 3D volumes have been acquired and aligned, the reconstruction process is relatively straightforward. Although the technique requires specific reconstruction tools such as Polyline OmniView and Volume Contrast Imaging, these software applications are often available on modern ultrasound equipment and are already used in obstetric and gynecological imaging. Furthermore, measurement reproducibility was not influenced by maternal age or body mass index, supporting the robustness of the method across different patient characteristics.

Direct tracing of the coronal course of the EAS, following proper spatial alignment, allows confirmation that the reconstructed image represents the full anterior semicircular extent of the sphincter. The mean EAS length obtained with this approach (approximately 14–15 mm) is consistent with values previously reported using established imaging modalities, including 3D endoanal ultrasound and MRI, supporting the anatomical validity of the measurements derived from this technique. 31 , 32 , 33 Another potential advantage of this approach relates to the use of a high‐frequency transvaginal probe positioned at the vaginal introitus, which provides high spatial resolution of structures located close to the probe surface. When combined with three‐dimensional reconstruction, this allows clearer visualization of the anal sphincter complex and facilitates differentiation between adjacent muscular structures. In particular, the external anal sphincter can be distinguished from the transverse perineal muscle anteriorly and from the levator ani muscle posterolaterally, as illustrated in the reconstructed axial view shown in Figure S1.

An important strength of the present study is the use of an exo‐anal introital approach with a high‐frequency probe, which differs from conventional transperineal ultrasound using the curvilinear transducers. Despite good reproducibility and agreement with three‐dimensional endoanal ultrasound, image acquisition with transperineal approaches may become suboptimal in the immediate postpartum setting, when near‐field resolution is particularly critical. This limitation is most evident in the presence of second‐degree perineal tears, episiotomy, or higher neonatal birthweight—clinical conditions associated with an increased risk of anal sphincter injury and representing scenarios in which additional diagnostic support is most needed. 34 In this context, the exo‐anal introital approach used in the present study, based on high‐frequency transvaginal imaging, may facilitate visualization of structures located close to the probe surface in early postpartum assessment of the anal sphincter.

The proposed reconstruction approach also differs from the conventional tomographic ultrasound imaging (TUI) technique commonly used in transperineal ultrasound assessment of the anal sphincter. 35 TUI generates a series of axial slices of the anal canal obtained at predefined intervals starting from a midsagittal reference plane. This allows systematic evaluation of the sphincter at different levels. In contrast, the present technique enables direct visualization of the anterior external anal sphincter along its coronal course through curvilinear reconstruction. This may facilitate evaluation of the longitudinal extent of the sphincter, a parameter that is relevant in commonly used classifications of obstetric anal sphincter injuries.

This study has several limitations. First, the proposed technique was not directly compared with the gold standard, three‐dimensional endoanal ultrasound, nor with MRI. However, identification and tracing of the EAS in the axial plane ensured that the subsequent coronal reconstruction referred to the same anatomical structure, and the resulting dimensional measurements were consistent with those reported using established imaging modalities. Direct head‐to‐head comparisons with reference imaging techniques, in both normal conditions and in women with OASIS, therefore remain an important objective for future research. Second, the study was designed as a pilot feasibility and reproducibility investigation and did not include women with confirmed OASIS; consequently, diagnostic accuracy and clinical impact could not be assessed. Third, analysis and measurements were performed on already acquired volumes, and acquisition‐related variability was not formally evaluated; however, volumes were acquired by multiple operators with heterogeneous levels of experience, with 100% feasibility of measurements, suggesting that this limitation may be of limited practical relevance. Finally, measurements were restricted to the central (midline) portion of the anterior external anal sphincter. While this choice ensured anatomical consistency and reproducibility, the central portion of the sphincter may not represent the most clinically relevant parameter in women with established sphincter injury. In the presence of OASIS, alternative measurements—such as mean anterior sphincter thickness or minimum residual thickness—may prove more informative. The relative clinical relevance of these parameters cannot be determined from the present study, which included only nulliparous women without sphincter injury, and should be explored in future investigations involving women with confirmed OASIS.

In the future, the proposed technique may be explored in a range of clinical contexts. In addition to enabling reproducible quantitative assessment, the ability to reconstruct the coronal plane of the external anal sphincter may facilitate a more comprehensive anatomical evaluation, including visualization of the continuity and integrity of the sphincter complex. This may be relevant not only for the postpartum assessment of suspected obstetric anal sphincter injuries but also for the evaluation of the appropriateness and quality of primary repair, as well as for follow‐up in women with previously diagnosed injury or in those presenting with anal incontinence.

Furthermore, previous studies using three‐dimensional endoanal ultrasound have demonstrated associations between morphometric parameters of the external anal sphincter and the severity of anal incontinence. 24 In this context, future studies comparing the present approach with established techniques such as endoanal ultrasound are warranted to determine its potential role in clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

Three‐dimensional transperineal ultrasound using a transvaginal probe at the vaginal introitus (exo‐anal introital ultrasound) combined with linear freehand reconstruction (Polyline OmniView) and contrast enhancement (VCI) is a feasible and highly reproducible method for assessing the anterior external anal sphincter. Further studies are warranted to define its clinical utility in patient care, including evaluation in women with suspected or previous obstetric anal sphincter injuries.
