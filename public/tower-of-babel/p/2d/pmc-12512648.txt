Translation and psychometric evaluation of the Persian version of the nurse team resilience scale in the context of cardiopulmonary resuscitation
Authors: Mobina Rajaee, Mehrsa Basiri Moghaddam, Alireza Jafari, Mahdi Basiri Moghaddam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is one of the most stressful situations in emergency medicine, where nurses play a central role and are exposed to significant levels of stress. Among the factors that can maintain team cohesion and performance, enabling effective adaptation to changes, risk factors, and stressors, is nursing team resilience. The present study aimed to conduct a Persian translation and psychometric evaluation of the Nursing Team Resilience Scale in the context of cardiopulmonary resuscitation.MethodsThis cross-sectional methodological study was conducted between June 2024 and March 2025 in Gonabad and Bajestan in Iran, on 360 nurses using convenience sampling method. For translation process, the Forward-Backward method was used. Face validity was performed on 10 nurses, and the opinions of 10 experts in the field of nursing and psychometrics of the tool were used for content validity, and CVR and CVI values ​​were calculated. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (sample: 160) and confirmatory factor analysis (sample: 200).To assess reliability, two indices were calculated: internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 22 and AMOS version 24.ResultsNo items were removed during the face and content validity assessments; only minor revisions were applied. the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index was found to be 0.76, indicating adequate sampling adequacy. The result of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 298.29, df = 28, p < 0.001), confirming the suitability of the data for factor analysis. In Exploratory factor analysis, 2 factors were extracted that explained 41.29% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) demonstrated good fit for the two-factor model (AGFI = 0.911, CFI = 0.938, PCFI = 0.637, RMSEA = 0.071, PNFI = 0.602, GFI = 0.953, IFI = 0.940, and RMR = 0.040). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the entire questionnaire was 0.75 and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.95.ConclusionThe results of the present study demonstrated that the Persian version of Nursing Team Resilience Scale in the context of cardiopulmonary resuscitation has satisfactory validity and reliability. This instrument demonstrates strong potential for use as a standardized tool in assessing nursing team resilience among Iranian nurses, particularly in high-stress settings like CPR.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Nurses are recognized as the largest group of healthcare providers across all countries and play a vital role in the healthcare system [1]. As the complexity of the healthcare system increases, teamwork has become an essential activity for improving the quality of care and is strongly emphasized [2]. Furthermore, group processes have become a critical component of the roles and responsibilities of nurses within the care and treatment system [3]. A study by Hessarak et al. demonstrated that effective teamwork among nurses is a key factor in ensuring quality care [4].

One of the most important procedures that requires collaboration among multiple professions is cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) [5]. While resuscitation is performed by different members of the resuscitation team with various specializations, the significant role and position of nurses within the team are undeniable. Nurses play a central role in identifying critical conditions, and preventing cardiac arrest. Additionally, their effective performance in resuscitation and post-resuscitation care make substantial contributions to improving treatment outcomes for hospitalized patients [6]. CPR is one of the most stressful situations in emergency medicine. As a result, nurses who participate in both the basic and advanced stages of resuscitation are exposed to significant stress [7]. Studies have shown that performing basic CPR is a stressful and anxiety-inducing experience for nurses working in hospital wards [8]. Intensive care nurses, in particular, experience high levels of stress, emotional exhaustion, guilt, burden, and anxiety during the CPR process. These findings underscore the importance of addressing and managing the psychological well-being of nurses in resuscitation environments to mitigate the negative effects of stress and enhance their resilience and overall well-being [9].

One of the most important human abilities that facilitates effective adaptation to challenges, risks, and stressful conditions—and serves as a factor of resistance against problems and crises—is resilience [10]. It enables nurses to adapt to the demands of their workplace [11]. Resilience is associated with increased patient satisfaction, better quality of care, and improved attitudes toward patients. It can buffer the negative impacts of workplace stress and positively influence nurses’ performance [12].

Research defines resilience on individual, team, and organizational levels [13]. Team resilience refers to a team’s ability to recover or bounce back from potentially stressful situations [14]. It is an adaptive process in response to adverse events that enables the team to better manage stress and thereby maintain cohesion and performance [15]. Studies have shown that team resilience affects overall team performance, and teams with higher levels of resilience are more likely to achieve positive outcomes [14]. However, the concept of team resilience has received less scholarly attention compared to the extensive literature on individual resilience [16].

Based on these definitions, researchers have developed tools to measure team resilience in industrial companies and sports teams [17]. Among these, few tools have been specifically studied among nurses. One such tool is the Crisis Team Resilience Questionnaire, which was developed for emergency response workers and has been studied among nursing teams in the context of COVID-19 [17, 18]. Another tool is the Analyzing and Developing Adaptability and Performance in Teams to Enhance Resilience (ADAPTER) Scale, which contains 51 items and was tested among corporate employees in the Netherlands [19]. Additionally, there is the Hierarchical and Multidimensional Team Resilience Scale, which consists of 50 items and includes 10 sub-dimensions [20]. Although this scale was used in 2021 to assess the resilience of cancer care teams in France, its complex structure and lengthy completion time make it less practical for nurses [17, 21]. Another existing tool is the Work Team Resilience Scale, which includes 42 items [22]. Like the previous tools, it has a complex structure and requires a significant amount of time to complete [17].

As previously mentioned, studies indicate a lack of concise assessment tools for measuring team resilience, while most existing scales contain more than 30 items and are rarely developed for use among nursing staff. In 2023, Su et al. designed and validated the Nursing Team Resilience Scale (NTRS) in the context of public health emergencies. This scale includes 8 items and is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” [1] to “strongly agree” [5]. The total score ranges from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating a greater level of nursing team resilience. According to the findings of that study, the 8-item NTRS demonstrates good validity and reliability and is considered suitable for evaluating nursing team resilience [17].

Psychometric evaluation of instruments in nursing research is important to ensure the validity and reliability of the information obtained and is a fundamental step in producing scientific evidenc [23]. if measures are to be used across cultures, the items must not only be translated well linguistically, but also must be adapted culturally to maintain the content validity of the instrument at a conceptual level across different culture [24].

Considering the importance role of nurses in CPR process and the importance of team resilience, there is a clear need for a valid and reliable instrument to assess nursing team resilience. Such tools can contribute to improved quality of care, help identify team strengths and weaknesses, and serve as valuable guides for nursing managers in decision-making. In light of the limited studies in this area and the absence of a validated instrument for measuring nursing team resilience in Iran, the present study was conducted to translate and psychometrically evaluate the Persian version of the Nursing Team Resilience Scale in the context of cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional methodological design and was conducted between June 2024 and March 2025 in Gonabad and Bajestan, two cities in northeastern Iran. The primary aim was to translate and psychometrically validate a tool for measuring nursing team resilience, tailored to the cultural and demographic characteristics of Iranian nurses.

Study participants

Participants were recruited from Allameh Bohlol Gonabadi Hospital and Shahid Madani Bajestan Hospital using a convenience sampling method. Based on methodological recommendations, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) typically requires 2 to 20 participants per item [25], while confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) requires a sample size between 150 and 500, with a minimum of 200 participants recommended [26, 27]. Based on these recommendation and a total of 360 nurses participated, the sample were randomly assigned into two groups: 160 for EFA (20 participants per item) and 200 for CFA (In accordance with the minimum required sample size). The simplest way for splitting a sample into two halves is to divide it randomly [28, 29]. Inclusion criteria were: willingness to participate, holding a bachelor’s degree in nursing or higher, involvement in at least one cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and a minimum of one year of clinical experience. Participants who left more than 20% of the questionnaire unanswered were excluded.

Data collection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Gonabad University of Medical Sciences. Additionally, permission was secured from the original developer of the questionnaire, and coordination was carried out with the hospital administrators. The researcher visited the participating hospitals during various shifts to facilitate data collection. Eligible participants were informed about the study objectives, and written informed consent was obtained. Paper-based questionnaires were then distributed and collected.

Study instruments

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire collected information on participants’ age, gender, marital status, education level, work experience, work shift, workplace, work units, employment status, income level, and frequency of involvement in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).

Nurse team resilience scale (NTRS)

The Nurse Team Resilience Scale (NTRS) was developed by Su et al. in China to assess team resilience among nurses during public health emergencies. It is based on Morgan et al.‘s comprehensive theoretical framework, which includes four dimensions: team structure, mastery approaches, social capital, and team effectiveness. The final version of the scale consists of 8 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater team resilience. A score above 24 reflects a high level of resilience [17].

The study conducted by Su et al. showed that the 8-item NTRS has good validity and reliability and is suitable for assessing nurse team resilience [17]. Content validity of the 16 items produced was assessed by 9 experts in two stages. In the first round, one item out of 16 (6.25%) received a score lower than 0.78 on the item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) (ranging from 0.67 to 1). The average Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was 0.92. Based on these results, 5 items were removed, 3 items were merged, and 2 items were revised, resulting in a final 8-item scale. In the second round, none of the items had an I-CVI lower than 0.78, and the average S-CVI was 0.99, indicating very good scale validity. After two rounds of consultation, the final 8-item questionnaire was developed. The KMO value was 0.94, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity showed (χ² = 2818.254, p < 0.0001) that factor analysis was logical and appropriate. In the Exploratory factor analysis confirmed a unidimensional structure explaining 72.33% of the variance, and confirmatory factor analysis supported the model fit. Internal consistency, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.93 to 0.94. The internal reliability of the scale, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.93 to 0.94 for the individual items and 0.94 for the entire questionnaire, which is considered excellent.

Translation process of the scale

The translation process followed Brislin’s forward-backward method [27]. After obtaining formal permission from the original developer, two bilingual experts independently translated the English version of the scale into Persian. The two translations were reviewed by the research team to produce a consensus version. This Persian version was then backtranslated into English by two different bilingual individuals, who had no prior involvement in the study. The final English back-translation was submitted to the original tool developer for review and received formal approval.

Psychometric assessment of the scale

The process of validating the translated scale was carried out using content and face validity, construct validity (through Confirmatory and Exploratory Factor Analysis), internal consistency reliability, and stability reliability.

Face validity

Face validity examines whether the developed tool appropriately reflects with the research goal. In this study, both quantitative and qualitative methods were used to evaluate clarity, relevance and structure of each item from participants’ perspectives [30].

Qualitative face validity

Translated Persian version of the questionnaire was provided to 10 hospital nurses. They were asked to review the items in terms of difficulty, relevance, and clarity of each item and provide their feedback.

Quantitative face validity

For quantitative assessment of face validity, 10 nurses were asked to review the items of the questionnaire and rate the importance of each item on a five-point Likert scale (from 1: Not important to 5: Very important).The impact score for each item was calculated by multiplying the percentage of respondents who gave a rating of 4 or 5 to the item (frequency) by the average of all the ratings given to that item (importance). Items with an impact score greater than 1.5 were considered suitable and were used in the subsequent stages of analysis [31].

Content validity

Content validity refers to how well a measurement tool’s content represents the construct it aims to measure. It examines whether the content of the tool accurately represents the concept or set of characteristics intended for measurement [30]. In this study, content validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively.

Qualitative content validity

Translated Persian version of the questionnaire was provided to 10 experts in the fields of nursing and psychometrics. They were asked to provide their views on the grammatical accuracy, sentence structure, clarity, comprehensibility, relevance and cultural suitability for the Iranian context.

Quantitative content validity

For the quantitative evaluation, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were calculated based on the feedback from 10 specialists in nursing and psychometrics. For CVR, the necessity of each item was assessed by the experts. The final evaluation for retaining an item is based on CVR [32, 33]. CVR of 0.62 or above is considered acceptable according to the Lawshe table for 10 experts [34]. For Content Validity Index, experts assessed the relevance of each. A CVI value greater than 0.80 is considered acceptable [29, 35].

Construct validity

Construct validity refers to the degree to which the scores from a tool align with the hypotheses based on which it is expected to accurately measure the intended construct. This alignment can include internal relationships between items, the correlation of scores with other similar tools, or the differentiation between related groups [36]. In the current study, construct validity was assessed using both Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA is widely used in medical education research. The researcher examines the factor loading of the items and refines and optimizes the tool [37]. A recommended sample size is 2 to 20 participants per it [25]. In this study, EFA was performed on 160 participants. Sample adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. The KMO value should be greater than 0.7, and Bartlett’s Test should be significant with a p-value less than 0.05 (p < 0.05). Items with factor loadings below 0.4 were removed [35, 38]. To assess the adequacy and naming of the extracted factors, either Varimax or Promax rotation is used. In this study, Varimax rotation was applied [39].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA is used to to verify the factor structure [40]. In the present study, CFA was performed on 200 nurses using AMOS version 24 software. Before conducting CFA, data checks were performed, including the examination of outliers and the normality of the data. Outliers were identified using the Mahalanobis distance statistic. To confirm the final tool, standardized factor loadings of each item and model fit indices were evaluated, including: Chi-Square (χ²) and Degrees of Freedom (df); Chi-Square to Degrees of Freedom Ratio (χ²/df) (should be less than 5); Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (RMSEA < 0.08); Root Mean Square Residual (RMR) (RMR < 0.08); Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI) (AGFI >0.90); Comparative Fit Index (CFI) (CFI >0.90); Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) (GFI >0.90); Parsimonious Normed Fit Index (PNFI) (PNFI >0.50); Parsimony Comparative Fit Index (PCFI) (PCFI >0.50); Incremental Fit Index (IFI) (IFI >0.90). These indices were evaluated to determine the fit of the model and to confirm the final structure of the tool [41–44].

Reliability

To evaluate the reliability of the instrument, two methods were used: internal consistency reliability (measured using Cronbach’s alpha) and test-retest reliability. A Cronbach’s alpha coefficient greater than 0.7 was considered acceptable [45]. Test-retest reliability was employed to assess the temporal stability of the instrument, examining how consistently it produced results over time when administered to the same individuals. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated based on data collected from 30 participants, with a time interval of 1 to 2 weeks between the initial and follow-up administrations of the questionnaire. The sampling method in this phase was convenience, based on inclusion criteria and efforts were made to ensure that the participants were different from those in the construct validity.

The ICC was interpreted using the following classification: less than 0.20 indicates poor reliability, 0.20–0.39 acceptable, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 good, and an ICC of 0.80 or higher indicates near-perfect repeatability [46, 47]. In the present study, the ICC was calculated using data collected from 30 nurses, two weeks apart, to determine the test-retest reliability of the instrument.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22), while confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using AMOS version 24. Descriptive statistics included frequency/percentage and mean ± standard deviation. Analytical statistics comprised factor rotation analysis, correlation, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A significance level of 0.05 was considered for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Gonabad University of Medical Sciences (IR.GMU.REC.1403.035). Informed consent was obtained from all participants both verbally and in writing. Additionally, the translation and psychometric evaluation of the instrument were carried out after obtaining permission from the original developer of the tool.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 360 nurses from Gonabad and Bajestan participated in the study. Of these, 218 (60.6%) were female and 142 (39.4%) were male. The participants’ mean age was 31.43 ± 6.77 years (range: 22–55). Most nurses were married (72.5%) and employed in Gonabad (86.1%). The majority held a bachelor’s degree (84.2%) and had an average work experience of 7.88 ± 6.57 years (range: 1–33). Additionally, 88.3% worked rotating shifts, and 56.4% had participated in more than 10 CPR procedures. Emergency (23.6%) and internal medicine (17.5%) were the most common work units. Face validity

A total of 10 nurses participated in the face validity assessment of the instrument. All eight items were evaluated in terms of difficulty, relevance, and clarity. The feedback provided by the participants was reviewed by the research team, and necessary modifications were applied to the items accordingly. Impact scores were calculated using the formula: Impact score = frequency (%) × importance, That “frequency” indicates the percentage of participants who rated the item as 4 or 5, and “importance” is the mean importance rating across all participants [29, 48, 49]. The impact scores for the items ranged from 4.14 to 5. Since all scores were above the acceptable threshold of 1.5, all items were retained at this stage.

Content validity

Content validity was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. In qualitative phase of content validity, 10 experts in nursing and psychometrics reviewed the questionnaire items for grammatical accuracy, sentence structure, clarity, comprehensibility, relevance and cultural suitability for iranian nurses. Their feedback was evaluated and incorporated into the items by the research team. For the quantitative assessment, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated based on experts’ ratings of item necessity. As previously mentioned, according to Lawshe’s table, a CVR of 0.62 or higher is considered acceptable [34]. In this study, the CVR values for all items ranged from 0.8 to 1, indicating that none of the items needed to be removed. Additionally, the Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated for each item based on relevance, and found to be 1 for all, confirming strong content validity.

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed on responses from 160 nurses. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.76, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 298.29, df = 28, p < 0.001), indicating suitability for factor analysis. All items had factor loadings >0.40 and were retained. Promax rotation identified two distinct factors, which together explained 41.29% of the total variance. These factors were labeled: [1] Team Stability in Critical Situations and [2] Team Adaptability and Growth, based on the conceptual definition of resilience and team resilience, as well as the primary objective of developing this questionnaire [50, 51]. Discussion

This study aimed to translate and psychometrically validate the Persian version of the Nurse Team Resilience Scale (NTRS) within the context of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). The findings supported the scale’s face, content, and construct validity, as well as its reliability, confirming its suitability as a robust tool for assessing team resilience among Iranian nurses in high-stress clinical settings.

The NTRS has several noteworthy advantages. While numerous instruments have been developed to assess team resilience, few specifically target nursing teams or have been applied within the nursing profession. This tool represents the first dedicated scale developed to evaluate resilience specifically within nursing teams. Furthermore, studies have highlighted the scarcity of concise instruments for measuring team resilience. Many existing tools include more than 30 items, posing challenges for practical use in clinical environments, especially among nurses with limited time availability [17].

The original version of the NTRS was developed by Su et al. in China and initially consisted of 16 items. Following face and content validity assessments, the final version was refined to 8 items [17]. In the current study, all 8 items were retained during the face validity phase. In terms of content validity, the CVR values for all items ranged from 0.80 to 1, and the CVI for each item was 1, indicating excellent face and content validity for the Persian version of the scale.

In this study, despite retaining the items from the original version of the questionnaire, the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) resulted in the extraction of two distinct factors that explained 41.29% of the total variance. In contrast, the original version of the questionnaire had one factor that explained 72.33% of the total variance. This difference may be attributed to cultural, linguistic, and conceptual factors. Research has shown that the understanding, interpretation, and response to instruments can be influenced by individuals’ cultural beliefs and values, as well as linguistic barriers. Furthermore, individuals may respond to questions based on their personal viewpoints [52]. In fact, cultural and linguistic differences can impact the factor structure of measurement tools [53]. Resilience has global, cultural, and contextual aspects that influence its understanding and expression [54]. It is a complex construct that may be defined differently in different contexts, so this could be a reason for different factor structure compare to original version [55]. Another reason is the difference in the work environment and professional expectations. Moreover, hospital cultures and the work environment of nurses affect the performance of clinical teams, especially in high pressure or critical situations [56].

After examining and analyzing the items of the questionnaire, the two factors were named based on the definition of resilience, team resilience, and the objective of designing this questionnaire [50, 51]. Su et al. developed their scale based on the framework proposed by Morgan et al. [57], which is considered one of the most comprehensive theories for the development of team resilience scales. This theory comprises four key dimensions [58, 59]. One of the dimensions of team resilience in this framework is team structure and stability, which leads to cohesive team performance.Based on this, the first factor was named “Team Stability in Critical Situations”. Another dimension of resilience in this framework involves approaches to dominance that include flexibility. Based on this, the second factor was named “Team Adaptability and Growth”, emphasizing flexibility, learning, and team improvement, which reflecting the multifaceted nature of resilience in CPR contexts.

Confirmatory factor analysis provided strong support for the two-factor model and eight items, with all fit indices falling within acceptable ranges. Additionally, the Persian NTRS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.753) and excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.957), comparable to or exceeding the psychometric thresholds reported in the original study by Su et al., despite cultural and contextual differences. In the original version of the questionnaire, only Cronbach’s alpha was used, with a reported value of 0.94.

This comparison demonstrates the cross-cultural adaptability of the scale while confirming the robustness of the Persian version for use in Iranian nursing teams. Future research could explore the implications of cultural factors on team resilience in different healthcare settings and the potential modifications to improve measurement tools based on these factors.

Unlike the original NTRS, which was validated during public health emergencies such as COVID-19, this study evaluated team resilience in the high-pressure context of CPR—a situation characterized by acute stress, rapid decision-making, and interprofessional coordination. Previous studies have highlighted that nurse performance during CPR can be adversely affected by stress and burnout [60, 61]. In this regard, a validated team resilience tool can serve as an important asset for identifying team dynamics and guiding interventions aimed at improving performance and psychological support in resuscitation environments [58, 62].

Team resilience refers to a team’s capacity to resist and overcome stressors in a way that enables consistent performance. It helps teams manage challenges that might threaten their cohesion and performance and enables them to recover from them [58]. Recent studies have shown that managing resuscitation teams, as well as the individual and professional characteristics of team members, in addition to patient characteristics, are the main factors contributing to low success rates in CPR in Iran [62]. Given the importance of CPR in Iran’s healthcare system, the central role of nurses in performing CPR, and the contribution of team resilience to improving cohesion and managing stress, using this tool can be a positive step toward enhancing the quality of nursing care in critical conditions and supporting nursing teams.

Having a standardized tool to better understand the performance and resilience of nursing teams in difficult situations can guide researchers, managers, and policymakers in designing, implementing intervention and then evaluating their effectivenes in this area. Clinically, Persian version of the NTRS can be used to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the resuscitation team, which providing a basis for improving team performance. It can be a valuable resource for developing educational content for nurses. This study offers valuable insights into the importance of measuring and fostering team resilience in the high-stress environment of CPR, which can contribute significantly to improving patient outcomes and team efficiency.

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations. First, participation were drawn from two cities, Gonabad and Bajestan, in northeastern iran, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the entire population of Iranian nurses. Additionally, the original NTRS was developed and validated in China, and its psychometric properties were only evaluated within that country. Therefore, the researchers were only able to compare their findings with those of Su et al.‘s study. self-reported data and potential response bias can be mentioned as other limitations of this study. Future research should explore the scale’s validity in other regions of Iran and among different nursing specialties to further establish its external validity.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate the Persian version of the Nursing Team Resilience Scale in the context of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) is a valid and reliable instrument. It can be considered a standardized tool for use within the Iranian nursing community to assess the resilience of nursing teams in CPR situations. This tool can serve as a valuable resource for researchers, clinical educators, and healthcare managers to evaluate and improve team performance under high-stress conditions. Moreover, health policymaker can use this tool for educational plannig in resilince. It can also help identify weaknesses in nursing teams and evaluate the effectiveness of related interventions.
