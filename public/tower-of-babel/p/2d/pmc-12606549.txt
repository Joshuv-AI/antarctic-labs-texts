More Than Childbirth: Unveiling the Risks of Marriage on Women's Mortality in Tang Dynasty China
Authors: Yan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study investigates how marital and maternal statuses influenced female mortality in the Tang Dynasty (618–907 ce), China. It challenges the focus on reproductive risks by exploring both biological and social factors affecting female mortality in reproductive and post‐reproductive years.Materials and MethodsEpitaph data were analyzed from four female groups: bureaucrats' wives (married, reproductive), eunuchs' wives (married, nonreproductive due to husband's castration), never‐married religious women (never married, nonreproductive), and widowed religious women (reproductive, later left marriage). Ages at death were illustrated using summary statistics and kernel density plots, analyzed using bootstrapped polynomial regression, pairwise comparisons with 9999 replicates, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Male bureaucrats and eunuchs were also included for contextual comparison.ResultsBureaucrats' wives had a significantly lower adjusted mean age at death than nonreproductive groups, eunuchs' wives (−7.43 years; p = 0.006), and never‐married religious women (−7.07 years; p = 0.006). Survival curves support that reproductive risks shaped mortality. Among post‐reproductive women, those who remained in marital roles had a significantly lower mean age at death than those who joined a religious order (−3.9 years; p = 0.003). The negative effects of remaining in marriage in later life were supported.DiscussionFemale mortality resulted from a complex interplay of biological and social factors. Reproductive risks primarily affected females during younger ages. During post‐reproductive years, remaining in marriage and widowhood negatively affected survival, while entering religious orders was protective.

Introduction

Human females experience elevated risks of mortality during their reproductive years (Kassebaum et al. 2016; Say et al. 2014; WHO et al. 2023). This is particularly evident when women lack the ability to avoid pregnancy (Ross and Blanc 2012; Utomo et al. 2021). Several hypotheses have been proposed to account for risks associated with female reproduction (Stone 2016). Evolutionary explanations such as the obstetric dilemma (Haeusler et al. 2021) and the energetics of gestation and growth hypothesis (Dunsworth et al. 2012) highlight that high maternal risks are deeply embedded in human biology, which have posed significant threats to women's survival throughout history.

The studies of female remains from bioarcheological contexts are consistent with the predicted impact of reproduction on their health and mortality (Penezić et al. 2020; L. Guo et al. 2023; Willis and Oxenham 2013; Zhou et al. 2020). While most bioarcheological studies infer elevated maternal mortality indirectly from comparisons of males and females of reproductive age (Stone 2016), a few studies more directly estimate maternal mortality risks through the direct identification of women who died during childbirth, often with fetal remains or newborn infants (e.g., Arriaza et al. 1988; Owsley and Bradtmiller 1983; Riccomi et al. 2021). Elevated risks of female mortality have also been documented in studies of historical records (Dobbie 1982; Grauer 2002). The most common interpretation for these higher rates involves reproductive risks. However, several authors (Dong et al. 2017; Grauer 2002; Wells et al. 2012) emphasize the importance of considering gender biases influencing women's living conditions and social roles as well.

In contrast, women who formally join religious orders, such as Christian nuns, tend to have a longer life expectancy with a later peak in mortality (Morin Doody et al. 2000; Zuijderduijn 2013). While avoiding childbirth is considered a significant factor in their longevity, other aspects of their social roles and lifestyles may also play a role. Living in isolated communities and being free from the social expectations of a wife or widow may also contribute to their extended lifespans.

The challenge with previous research is that it is difficult to control biological and social factors, and typically impossible to observe their influences separately. Prior to effective birth control measures, most married women faced risks associated with reproduction, though the extent of these risks may be debated and is likely context‐specific (Stone 2016). While women who joined religious orders experienced childbirth much less often, they also lived different lifestyles with social roles that may have reduced risks of mortality as well. Consequently, it is difficult to determine the extent to which each factor influences female lifespans.

To address this challenge, I take advantage of the circumstances and information to compare four Chinese female groups from the Tang Dynasty (618–907 ce), each group defined by marital status and the likelihood of reproduction: bureaucrats' wives (married, assumed to face risks of marriage and reproduction), eunuchs' wives (married, but nonreproductive due to husband's castration), never‐married women who entered religious orders at a young age (never married and unlikely to reproduce), and widowed religious women (married, but who left marriage after husband's death to join an order).

Life Inside and Outside Marriage During the Tang Dynasty

Though eunuch marriages were occasionally recorded throughout Chinese history, they became widely accepted during the Tang Dynasty. Particularly from the mid‐Tang Dynasty onwards, eunuchs gained more political power. Their duties were not limited to serving the royal family in the capital, but also included crucial positions as civil and military officials. At the same time, they obtained rights similar to those of bureaucrats, including the right to legally marry. Marriage was an important way for them to build connections with other elites and pass on their political power through adopted children (Du 2000; Hoeckelmann 2017; Y. Liu and Yao 2017). The widespread practice of eunuch marriage during this period created records of a unique group of women who were married but nonreproductive due to their husbands' castration, allowing us to explore the risks associated with marriage beyond reproduction. I acknowledge that using information from epitaphs biases the study toward individuals from high‐ranking families who are likely to have experienced relatively advantaged circumstances (Yao 2011). This is unlikely, however, to invalidate comparisons of differences in mortality risk as I explain below.

For women in the Tang Dynasty, marriage was one of the most significant milestones in their lives and was, therefore, frequently recorded on their epitaphs. Legally, the minimum age for women to marry was 13 years. Published data from epitaphs show that marriage ages ranged from the early teens to the late 20s, with nearly 70% marrying between the ages of 15 and 20 years (Yao 2020). However, some women chose a different path by entering religious life. One of the two key points when females chose to enter religious life was before marriage. The reasons for ordination at a young age varied (H. Guo and Liu 2021; Jia 2018; Yao 2020). These females who entered religious life early on never assumed the social role of a wife and, in most cases, did not face reproductive risks.

Most women still entered marriage. Married lives among the wives of bureaucrats and eunuchs are assumed to be similar in terms of social expectations and household responsibilities, including caring for parents and siblings‐in‐law, educating biological or adopted children, managing family rituals, and supporting their husbands (Du 2000; Yao 2020). The primary difference between these two groups is associated with reproduction. This makes them particularly valuable for assessing how reproduction, apart from shared marital roles, contributes to women's mortality risks.

Differences in mortality risks not linked to reproduction are more likely to be identifiable among women who have passed their reproductive peaks. Estimates of when this occurs vary. I selected an age of 46 years, as explained in the next section. During this post‐reproductive period, some women were widowed. While remarriage was socially acceptable, most elite wives adopted less socially active lives as widows (G. Zhang 2007). A minority of widows chose to leave their social roles as wives and enter religious orders, either as a means of coping with their loss, or as an opportunity to seek their own interests (H. Guo and Liu 2021; Jia 2018; Yao 2020; G. Zhang 2007).

Health research conducted in modern contexts suggests that in post‐reproductive years, widowhood tends to modestly increase female mortality risk (Kalediene et al. 2007; Moon et al. 2014; Shor et al. 2012), but turning to religion can help reduce the emotional challenges faced by the elderly after the death of a spouse (Carr 2020). Additionally, interacting with peers who share similar experiences has been shown to improve the quality of life for widows. Examples of this are living in communities with a great number of widows (Subramanian et al. 2008), or maintaining female friendships (Panchadhyayi 2021). Therefore, I explore whether women entering religious orders were associated with greater longevity as compared to other groups of women who survived to at least 46 years of age. Mean ages at death among male bureaucrats and eunuchs are also considered briefly to partially evaluate the potential that socioeconomic differences associated with these two social roles contributed to differing mortality patterns. This comparison is limited because the males are not necessarily from the same families that contributed information about females. Modern demographic research indicates that the mortality effects of a shared socioeconomic environment are similar for wives and husbands (Mineau et al. 2002; Smith and Zick 1994). Therefore, if the distributions of age at death differ by sex, I judge this to probably reflect differences in either sex‐associated biology and/or gendered lived experiences.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Data for this study were primarily drawn from Tang Dynasty epitaphs. These inscriptions provide essential biographical information, often including family background, husbands' official positions for married females, important life events such as ordination for religious people, marriage and widowhood for females, official careers for males, personal achievements or virtues, as well as the year, age at death, and funeral arrangements. But they rarely specify the cause of death, and information on children must be treated with caution as daughters, as well as children who died young, were often under‐reported (Yao 2020), and biological vs. adopted children were not always distinguished (Du 2024). It should also be noted that epitaphs primarily document individuals from high‐ranking families (Yao 2011). However, because all groups in this study were drawn from broadly similar elite contexts, comparisons of mortality risks among them remain valid.

Given these limitations, and considering the social context of the Tang dynasty—where the patrilineal line was strictly regulated, expectations of female chastity were growing (Y. Guo 2021; Zhan 1996), and effective contraception was absent—social roles recorded in epitaphs were used as the primary proxy for reproductive activity and as the basis for defining the four female groups compared in this study. This does not imply that reproduction accounted for all deaths among bureaucrats' wives, but rather that, as married and reproductively active women, they were generally exposed to such risks during their reproductive years, whereas eunuchs' wives and never‐married religious women were not.

The individuals relevant to this study were identified from the following sources: epitaph anthologies, epitaph excavation reports, research articles containing full texts of epitaphs, the database of epitaph rubbings curated in Zhejiang University Library, and the Prosopography and Social Network Database of the Tang and Five Dynasties, version 1.5, created by Nicolas Tackett (www.ntackett.com), as well as a biography in official Tang history. Full details of all identified individuals are available in Supporting Information S2: Datasets.

I first identified 41 females whose epitaphs indicated their husbands were eunuchs. Based on their age at death and the year of death, they were born between 662 and 831 ce. The epitaphs reveal that these women came from bureaucratic, eunuch, or non‐office‐holding families. Widowed ages of nine of these individuals are recorded in their epitaphs or can be calculated based on their husbands' age at death. These 41 females were included in the test of Hypothesis 1. Of these women, 36 survived to at least 46 years of age and were included in the test of Hypothesis 2.

Second, I identified 764 females whose husbands were bureaucrats, excluding those from imperial families. This was done because none of the eunuchs' wives were from imperial or royal backgrounds. Among these, the widowed ages of 129 individuals could be calculated based on their husbands' year of death. From the initial 764 wives, I selected 650 women who had the same range of birth years as the eunuchs' wives to test Hypothesis 1. Among these women, 386 survived until at least 46 years of age and were included in the test of Hypothesis 2.

Additionally, I identified 88 women whose epitaphs recorded official registration in Buddhist or Daoist institutions. They were born between 620 and 844 ce. Among them, 62 entered religious orders without entering marriage. Of these, 47 women were selected with a similar range of birth years as eunuchs' wives to test Hypothesis 1. Additionally, among 26 women who were formerly married, 22 were recorded as joining religious orders after their husbands' death. For the remaining four, while widowhood was not explicitly stated, their ordination occurred in their mid‐30s to 50s, and they entered Buddhist institutions that forbade marriage. Therefore, it is likely they were also widowed. Among the 26 widowed religious women, 17 recorded either the age at which they officially entered a religious order or the duration of their religious life until death. Additionally, one had been married to a eunuch; therefore, she is included in both groups. Three of the 26 were excluded because their birth years were earlier than 662 ce (625–641 ce). Of the remaining, 21 widows survived until at least 46 years of age and were included in the test of Hypothesis 2.

In addition to the females, a total of 133 eunuchs born during the Tang Dynasty between 627 and 858 ce were identified. Among these eunuchs, 103 were noted as having been married at least once, suggesting that marriage among eunuchs was common. I selected 115 for comparison, whose range of birth years (663–829 ce) nearly matched those of eunuchs' wives. Along with the eunuchs, a total of 1666 bureaucrats from non‐royal backgrounds who held government offices were identified. These individuals were born from 619 to 898 ce. Of these, 1410 men were selected who had a range of birth years from 662 to 831 ce.

I tested Hypothesis 1 using the boot package in R to implement a bootstrapped polynomial regression analysis (R Core Team 2025). This approach was adopted to accommodate the differences in sample size and variance, and the non‐normal distribution of the ages at death among the bureaucrats' wives (Kabacoff 2015). Age at death was the criterion variable with social roles (three levels) as the predictor and birth year and birth year squared as covariates. A quadratic function for birth year captured the curvilinear trends in age at death seen from approximately 750 ce onward. Model fit was summarized by R 2 (bootstrap 95% confidence interval [CI] = [0.104–0.193]).

I then conducted bootstrapped pairwise comparisons of mean age at death across groups, reporting bias‐corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% CIs based on 9999 resamples per contrast. Freedman–Lane residual permutation provided raw p values. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) across multiple comparisons; FDR‐adjusted p values were reported.

I added family name and a birth year by social role interaction in preliminary models to evaluate whether an imprecise measure of shared kinship within lineages, or differences in mortality trends among subgroups over time, helped account for age at death. Both were excluded from the model reported because they showed no improvement in model fit (nested‐model F‐tests and whole‐term F‐tests within augmented models, all p > 0.20), and the 95% CIs for R 2 showed no meaningful change.

Hypothesis 2 involved only women who had survived to a minimum of 46 years (Table 1). This age was chosen based upon estimates of average age at widowhood from a minority of women in the study datasets (see Table 2) and published work estimating reproductive histories. R. Zhang (2008) reported last‐childbirth ages for eight women, with the oldest being approximately 42 years. Demographic archives show a similar pattern: in Turfan families, over 90% of births occurred before age 39, while Dunhuang records show a longer tail, with around 20% of births occurring between ages 40 and 49 (Ren 2009). This pattern corresponds to social perceptions recorded in the literature from the Tang Dynasty (Ouyang and Song 1975). Taken together, I use 46 years as a reasonable point to begin comparisons of post‐reproductive influences on mortality risk. To assess robustness to this choice, the full Hypothesis 2 analysis was repeated using later cutoff ages of 50 and 55 years; details are provided in the Supporting Information S1.

I tested Hypothesis 2 in two ways. First, I compared all older women who remained in a marital role, including both bureaucrats' and eunuchs' wives (married, n = 422), with older women who either entered a religious order before marriage or after widowhood (religious, n = 60). Second, I re‐estimated the model using the four previously defined social roles. The analytic approach followed the same procedure as in Hypothesis 1: polynomial regression with birth year (and its quadratic term) as covariates; bootstrapped pairwise comparisons (BCa 95% CIs); raw p values from Freedman–Lane residual permutation with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR adjustment. Bootstrap 95% CIs for R 2 were approximately 0.05–0.15 for both tests—still informative but lower than in Hypothesis 1. Given the smaller cell sizes in the four‐group analysis, pairwise comparison CIs are expected to be wider. Accordingly, marginal findings are interpreted with caution. The R scripts implementing the boostrapped analyses for Hypotheses 1 and 2 are provided in Supporting Information S3.

Along with the bootstrapped regression analyses, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were employed to illustrate the survival patterns of the female groups. In these survival curves, the time axis is divided into discrete intervals, with each interval defined by the occurrence of an event. For each interval, the probability of death is calculated by dividing the number of individuals who died by the number of individuals still alive at the start of the interval. The survival probability is then obtained by subtracting the probability of death from one (Tolley et al. 2016). This study used age at death recorded in epitaphs as the time interval, and the event of interest was defined as “death.” Because all individuals died, the curves drop from 1 to 0. The extent of differences in mean age at death suggested by the regression analyses can be more fully described using these survival curves.

Results

As shown in Table 1, the widowed religious women (n = 23) have the highest mean age at death (66.9 years). This is probably because they represent a longevity‐biased subset of women. They survived the reproductive years, outlived their husbands, and then turned to religious orders. Never‐married religious women (n = 47) have the second‐highest age at death (59.15 years), followed by eunuchs' wives (n = 41; 55.46 years). Bureaucrats' wives (n = 650) show the lowest age at death (49.59 years) but have a longer tail than the eunuchs' wives. Figure 1 illustrates the distributional differences among these four female groups. These differences will be further explored in the following sections.

I briefly consider differences between bureaucrats and eunuchs to assess whether mean age at death, survival to age 46, and the distribution of ages at death convey similar information to that observed for the women's groups. As noted earlier, men in these social categories were not necessarily the husbands of the women, so interpretations must be circumspect. As shown in Table 1, eunuchs died, on average, 2.3 years later than bureaucrats. This difference partially reflects the potential for bureaucrats to inherit official positions from their families as children. Figure 2 shows that, at younger ages, the bureaucrat curve rises slightly above that of eunuchs: 57 bureaucrats died before age 35, the youngest age at death among the eunuchs. Overall, being eunuchs or bureaucrats does not result in a meaningful difference in males' age at death, whereas factors influencing female mortality vary, leading to distinct patterns among the female groups.

Shadows of Marriage: Reproductive Risks

Hypothesis 1, that reproductive risks significantly contribute to female mortality, and their very probable absence provided a survival advantage during the typical reproductive years, is supported (Table 3). Bootstrapped pairwise contrasts of mean age at death indicate that as compared to eunuchs' wives and never‐married religious women, bureaucrats' wives had significantly lower ages at death, −7.43 and −7.07 years, respectively (adjusted p = 0.006). Survival curves (Figure 3) support the expectation that bureaucrats' wives faced relatively more severe mortality risks from their twenties, which became less pronounced in their late 30s but remained disadvantageous.

Test 2 across the four female groups (Table 4) reinforces this pattern. Bureaucrats' wives died 6.16 years earlier than widowed nuns (adjusted p = 0.002) and 2.52 years earlier than never‐married nuns (not significant, adjusted p = 0.205). Given the model's relatively modest explanatory power and the small group sizes, this marginal result is worth considering, although wide confidence intervals prevented statistical significance.

In repeated analyses using later cutoff ages (50 and 55 years), effect directions remained concordant: in Test 1, religious women maintained an advantage over married women. In Test 2, the widowed religious women consistently outperformed the two wife groups, and contrasts involving the never‐married religious group strengthened at older cutoff ages, reaching statistical significance against the wife groups (Tables S1 and S2). These checks indicate that the main conclusion, that religious life is associated with a protective advantage in later life, is robust.

Discussion

Results here strongly suggest that some factors only affected females, leading to varied mortality patterns among female groups with different marital and maternal statuses in two life stages. During the typical reproductive years, the reproductive risks appear to be crucial in shaping female mortality, distinguishing the bureaucrats' wives from the eunuchs' wives and the never‐married religious women. It results in higher mortality risks from the age of marriage until their late 30s (see Figure 3), which is consistent with previous demographic research using epitaph data from the Tang dynasty and earlier and later periods (Lee 1993; L. Zheng 2010; Lu 2014; Ma 2020; Yao 2020).

Maternal death occurring during pregnancy, childbirth, or following pregnancy termination, remained a serious concern for women of reproductive age in China into the 20th century (Li 2023; Yan 1989), indicating maternal mortality was a persistent, sex‐specific cost of reproduction, shaping women's survival across historical periods and human populations. Although only a few epitaphs from the Tang Dynasty directly mention such tragic deaths occurring among females (Men et al. 2006; Yao 2020), many medical and religious texts document specific practices aimed at supporting successful reproduction, which reflect such concerns (Lee 1997; Tian 2023; Xie 2024). Meanwhile, Buddhist scriptures and bureaucratic ethics highlighted the severe pain and fatal risks associated with reproduction as a foundational reason for filial piety and the moral duty children owe to their mothers (Cole 1998; Liao 2009; Lin 2017). Altogether, this evidence suggests that the high risks of reproduction were widely recognized and accepted as part of social understanding. These likely contributed most to the significantly lower survivorship of bureaucrats' wives compared to the two nonreproductive female groups.

Apart from the deaths directly caused by reproduction, as the primary caregivers, mothers are often involved in intense childcare demands in modern contexts, and sometimes sacrifice their own well‐being (Brand et al. 2014; Gaur et al. 2024; Woloski et al. 2014). The extent to which elite wives were personally involved in childcare is rarely documented. In epitaphs, “good mothers” are typically praised for imparting female morals to their daughters, educating sons for official success, and managing the household (Yao 2020). Care is generally described in broad terms, such as “kindness” or “feeding,” without further details. Again, medical texts from the period document specific childcare practices, including cleaning and feeding newborns, swaddling, and treating common ailments according to season and age (Tian 2023; Y. Zheng 2014). These tasks required mothers' participation. However, wet nurses were also common during the Tang Dynasty, taking on much of the daily caregiving, including breastfeeding, companionship, and general medical practices. In elite families, wet nurses—often family slaves—could accompany the children throughout their lives (Pan 2020). These would greatly reduce the caregiving pressures on elite mothers, but not reduce the risks of childbirth.

In comparison, eunuchs' wives may have even fewer childcare responsibilities than bureaucrat wives, as their adopted children were often older than infants and therefore required less physical care. A law established in 791 ce made adoption legal for eunuchs and restricted the number, age, and lineage of adopted children. Only eunuchs of the fifth rank or higher were permitted to adopt a child who had the same family name and was under 10 years old (Q. Zheng 1995). In practice, however, these regulations were often ignored. Among the 133 eunuchs in this study, 129 recorded their children in epitaphs; 89 of them mentioned having two or more children. 90% of the children recorded in fathers' epitaphs are sons. These epitaphs did not specify the ages at adoption, but historical records indicate that many high‐status eunuchs adopted sons who were beyond their early childhood, such as young eunuchs or military officials (Lei and Huang 1977b; Sun 2007). For example, Gao Lishi, the most powerful eunuch during Emperor Xuanzong's reign (712–756 ce), was adopted after joining the court, likely at an age over 15 years (Lei and Huang 1977a). Although his father's epitaph still described him as a child receiving parental care, children at this age are much more independent. Therefore, while eunuchs' wives did have children through adoption, their childcare responsibilities were likely less demanding than those of bureaucrat wives, which may also contribute to their higher survival possibility during the typical reproductive years. But the reduction in mortality associated with lower childcare demands would have been minor compared to the substantial impact of not undergoing childbirth.

As anticipated, the absence of risks associated with reproduction, as well as the potential “benefits” of not having biological children, are not seen among the eunuchs' wives, do not persist throughout the entire life. From their 50s onward, eunuchs' wives' survival probabilities sharply drop and overlap with those of bureaucrats' wives (Figure 3). Meanwhile, both groups of religious women exhibit survival advantages during the post‐reproductive years (Figure 4). This disparity is likely because elite wives, whether married to eunuchs or bureaucrats, face similar household responsibilities and age‐related risks, including a similar form of widowhood, later in life.

Widowhood may be particularly common among these elite wives due to the delayed marriage age of elite males and the larger age differences between spouses. Mao (1995) analyzes the epitaphs of 370 couples and uses age differences between spouses and wives' marriage ages to estimate the husbands' marriage ages. He finds that the average marriage age for males increased over time, from 23 years in the early Tang Dynasty to 27 years in the late Tang Dynasty, while the marriage age for females remained stable at just over 15 years. Weng (2001) attributes this trend to the expansion of the imperial examination system, which led more males to dedicate time to studying, taking exams, and securing official positions before marriage. Particularly for bureaucratic elite families, a husband's status and official position were far more important than his age or whether he had previous wives (Luo 2011; G. Zhang 2007). This was likely even more true for marriages with eunuchs, who needed more time to attain significant political power to attract marriages from noble families. However, the females' marriage ages still fell in the same average range, which probably resulted in more frequent widowhood among these families.

The implications of being widowed help explain the results described here if they meant reduced social contacts for the women involved. Widows were expected to follow strict social norms: to express extreme sadness for their loss, live in isolation and simplicity, avoid cosmetics and entertainment, and dedicate themselves entirely to raising their children and caring for their in‐laws (Mao 1995; G. Zhang 2007).

For eunuchs' wives, widowhood may have been even more isolating, which could help explain their sharp decline and modestly lower survival probabilities compared to bureaucrats' wives. While their epitaphs always emphasized the grief of sons, daughters, and in‐laws, the extent to which these sentiments reflected reality is uncertain. Emotional bonds likely varied depending on the nature of adoption, though the records regarding their family relationships are limited.

Close mother–daughter relationships within eunuch families were possible, similar to those in bureaucrat families. Because daughters could not inherit their patrilineal ancestral lineages, the laws governing their adoption were less strict than those for adopted sons (Sun 2007). Therefore, mothers may have had more freedom to choose the daughters they preferred or to adopt them at a younger age, fostering closer relationships. One example from this study illustrates the mother–daughter bond: the eldest daughter, who had a different family name from both parents, was still recorded in her mother's epitaph, with an emphasis on her role in arranging the funeral, the same as the oldest son (Hu and Rong 2012). However, her name was absent from her father, Song Guoliang's, epitaph (Du 2019). The exact reason is unknown, but this may reflect her importance to her mother despite the lack of clan connection.

Conversely, adopted sons, selected for political potential at older ages—especially those holding military positions before adoption—may have been more distant. Historical records from the late Tang Dynasty indicate that such sons frequently engaged in political conflicts with their eunuch fathers and siblings (Lei and Huang 1977b). It is likely that their relationships with their adopted mothers were also less emotionally supportive. Among those I evaluated, only 13 out of 41 eunuchs' wives recorded daughters in their epitaphs, while 39 of them recorded sons. This imbalance likely reflects the strong political considerations underlying eunuch adoption echoing the prevalent strong gender bias, which may have prioritized political utility over the emotional needs of these wives.

Despite these potential differences in emotional support, the physical care and financial support provided to eunuchs' widows may have been similar to that of bureaucrats' wives. Filial piety required biological and adopted children to care for their mothers. Failing to do so was seen as a failure to meet the moral requirements expected of government officials (Liao 2009; Sun 2007; Wang 2011). This expectation is reflected in the epitaphs of widowed eunuchs' wives in this study, all of whom were recorded as having passed away in family homes, with their funerals arranged by their children. Some records also note the care they received or that their sons were granted official leave to tend to them during illness before their death. However, for those with only adopted sons chosen for political reasons, their well‐being might still have been compromised due to the lack of emotional support during widowhood, even though they did not lack physical care.

In contrast, becoming a nun often not only fulfilled their religious faith, but also gave them more freedom and diverse ways of self‐expression. In addition to studying scriptures and engaging in religious practices with peers, nuns also educated believers, preached doctrines, and hosted large public expounding events in temples (Jiao 2013, 2014). Their social networks also extended beyond religious institutions. Because of the widespread influence of Buddhism and Daoism, nuns were often invited to royal and elite households for religious services, with some influencing political and military decisions through divination. In low‐status communities in Dunhuang, Buddhist nuns led women's associations that not only conducted religious practices, but also fostered sisterhood, daily mutual support, and financial assistance (Gao 2011; S. Liu 2019). Daoist nuns, renowned for their poetry and cultural salons, widely socialized with male and female writers and readers through poetic exchanges, and were even summoned by emperors due to their fame (Gao 2011; Jia 2018).

According to the epidemiological research in modern contexts, a significant increase in female mortality is often observed after the loss of a spouse (Moon et al. 2014; Shor et al. 2012). Additionally, social isolation and reduced social networks are associated with higher risks of cardiovascular diseases (Golaszewski et al. 2022; Hakulinen et al. 2018), functional disabilities (L. Guo et al. 2021), and mortality among older women (Ellwardt et al. 2015; Steptoe et al. 2013). The various activities of religious women may have benefitted their health and life quality during the post‐reproductive years and resulted in greater longevity in the two groups of religious women.

Conclusion

As noted in Section 2, epitaph data have inherent limitations: they disproportionately represent elite families, provide no direct cause‐of‐death information, and vary in sample size across groups. While these issues cannot be eliminated, the use of role‐based definitions of reproductive activity and bootstrapped analyses helps mitigate their impact.

Additional challenges remain. First, background heterogeneity in women's early life experiences cannot be fully assessed, though I consider it unlikely to explain the observed group differences due to the high‐ranking background of most epitaph samples. Second, historical events, such as the An Lushan Rebellion (755 ce) (Tackett 2014), may have disproportionately affected bureaucrats' families and could have intensified female mortality risks in ways not directly related to reproduction. However, as noted in the Methods, I modeled birth year flexibly (including a quadratic term) and tested a birth year × social role interaction, which was not significant and did not alter the results. Finally, broader gendered inequalities in Tang society may also have interacted with biological risks, shaping the survival patterns observed.

Taken together, these caveats caution against over‐interpreting any single mechanism. Nonetheless, they do not undermine the central conclusion: maternal and marital roles significantly shaped female mortality in Tang Dynasty China.

In conclusion, this study indicates that marriage can be particularly harmful to females in the Tang Dynasty, not only through direct biological effects but also via social impacts that, in turn, influenced biological health. Avoiding marriage entirely or leaving marriage later in life to join a religious order appears to benefit females' survival in this social context.

This partially challenges previous research, which primarily emphasizes reproductive risks as the dominant factor influencing female mortality. While reproductive risks are significant, they mainly affect females at younger ages. This study suggests a more complex interplay of biological and social factors shaping female mortality, particularly clear during the post‐reproductive years. It highlights the negative impact of widowhood and the potential protective effects of religious life and social activities, which may help mitigate the risks of sadness and social isolation.

The discovery and publication of more epitaphs from this and later dynasties could provide valuable data to further explore these issues. As Huang (2013) notes, epitaph practices in later imperial China extended to families from a broader range of social backgrounds, offering opportunities to study women's experiences beyond the elite. Incorporating such epitaphs alongside other sources would enrich the understanding of how women's lives were shaped by the intersection of reproductive risks, social roles, and wider political and cultural changes through history.
