Craticula vanensis sp. nov., a new species of diatom with a complex mitogenome, inhabiting soda lakes in central Europe and Anatolia
Authors: Elif Yılmaz, Romain Gastineau, Ewa Górecka, Cüneyt Nadir Solak, Rosa Trobajo, Claude Lemieux, Monique Turmel, Christian Otis, Brian Boyle, Matt Ashworth, Edward C Theriot, Éva Ács, Horst Lange-Bertalot, David G Mann, Andrzej Witkowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article formally describes Craticula vanensis sp. nov., a new species of diatom from Lake Van, the largest soda lake in the world, and the fifth new species identified in the lake following an 81-years gap in investigations. A genomic approach based on short reads allowed the complete cluster of nuclear rRNA genes to be retrieved alongside the complete plastome, both later used in several single or multigene phylogenies, which surprisingly tended to associate C. vanensis to species of the genus Dorofeyukea. The complex structure of the mitogenome could only be resolved by long-read sequencing. It is characterized by its large size (116,698 bp), the presence of several introns in the cox1 gene, the use of an alternative genetic code with the putative reassignment of the UGA codon to arginine and the unexpected presence of six copies of the rRNA genes and nine copies of truncated nad2 sequences. A survey of published metabarcoding datasets suggested the presence of C. vanensis in a soda lake from Central Europe (Kakasszéki, Hungary), which was later confirmed by microscopy and suggests an extended distribution of this taxon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62093-2.Keywords: Alkaline lakes, Extreme environment, Organellar genomes, Lake Van, Metabarcoding, TürkiyeSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Craticula Grunow1 is a genus of naviculoid raphid pennate diatoms (Bacillariophyta). Species belonging to this genus can be found in various habitats, from acidic oligotrophic waters to alkaline, saline, eutrophic and heavily polluted waters2. Algaebase3 lists 60 ‘accepted’ species for Craticula, 10 accepted varieties and one forma (checked on October 2025). The history of this genus is complex and spans almost two centuries. It was erected by Grunow in 18684 to accommodate a species originally described as Surirella craticula by Ehrenberg5. When Ehrenberg introduced this species, which he found to be cosmopolitan, he characterized it by the presence of a ‘craticula’, which he considered to be a grid-like, skeletal structure on the internal face of the valve. However, when Grunow described the genus, he showed that the ‘craticula’ is in fact a valve, and that the frustule therefore comprises two different types of valves. One of them, the ‘craticula’, is internal to the other (“valvis duplicibus, exterioribus (?) nodulis centralibus et terminalibus instructis, striato-punctatis, interioribus (?) surirellaeformis costatis”; translated, “with double valve, the exterior ones (?), with punctate striae and central and terminal nodules, the inner ones (?) ribbed as in Surirella”) (question marks from the original text).

Following this initial description, the validity of the genus Craticula was strongly debated, especially after Pfitzer6 showed that the craticular valves were formed within the cells of two well-known species, Navicula cuspidata and N. ambigua. Several authors considered the grid-like structures to be teratological; for example, Cleve7 called them “monstrosities”. However, the genus was reinstated by Round et al. in 19908 and in the following year, Mann & Stickle9 described the characteristics of Craticula in detail, in order to distinguish it from Navicula sensu stricto. Their work included light (LM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), ultrastructural studies, and observations of gametogenesis and auxospore formation. Based on all these elements, the validity of the genus was reassessed and it is now widely accepted among the diatomist community. Meanwhile, studies by Schmid10 clarified that the craticular valves and a further morphologically divergent internal valve, the “heribaudii” form, are parts of a resting spore that develops in relation to osmotic stress.

Lake Van is located in the highlands of Eastern Anatolia, Türkiye. In terms of volume, this is the world’s largest soda lake and the fourth largest lake with a closed hydrological basin. This 3,570 km2 lake is 1,648 m above the sea level, with a maximum depth of 460 m, pH ranging between 9.5 and 9.9, salinities between 21 and 24‰, and an alkalinity of c. 155 m eq/l11–13. The water level and chemistry have been fluctuating drastically throughout the Quaternary period. Lake Van has been suggested to be a model for the proposed highly alkaline chemistry of the early ocean14–18 and its past characteristics and sediment records have been extensively studied11,19–24, providing an important climate record proxy for an area which is both biologically and archaeologically important. Lake Van contains very large cyanobacteria-built microbialites (microbial mats) that can reach 40 m in height25. It also hosts two endemic species of fishes, Alburnus tarichi26 and the recently discovered Oxynoemacheilus ercisianus27–29. Lake Van was also at the center of the Iron age kingdom of Urartu, whose ruins can be found around and inside the lake30.

Studies of the current biodiversity of the microorganisms inhabiting the lake are rather scarce. Most of them are dedicated to prokaryotes, with an emphasis on those involved in the formation of the microbialites31–35. The zooplankton communities were also investigated36, while the biodiversity of the phytoplankton, including diatoms, remains an almost open field for further investigations.

The first study of Lake Van diatoms was published in the ‘Beihefte zum Botanischen Centralblatt’ by Legler & Krasske37. In 1936, the authors obtained two samples of water from Lake Van, brought back to Germany by a colleague following a trip to Anatolia. In the samples, they observed cyanobacteria and diatoms, and listed 24 diatom taxa. One specimen was too poorly preserved to be formally identified to species level and could only be ascribed to the genus Surirella. Two taxa were considered to be new varieties (Rhopalodia musculus var. suprasemicirculatus and Amphiprora paludosa var. densestriata) and two new species were introduced: Nitzschia incognita and Surirella invicta. The remaining 19 taxa were identified by Legler & Krasske as belonging to species occurring in the Central European diatom flora38, which led the authors to question the ecological preferences known for these taxa (mostly seen as mesohalophilic or oligohalophilic). A reinvestigation of Krasske’s collections, including the Lake Van material, was the topic of the third volume of Iconographia Diatomologica by Lange-Bertalot et al.39.

For 81 years after Legler & Krasske37, there were no works published on the present-day diatoms from Lake Van until the description of Nitzschia anatoliensis40, a small-celled, cryptic species from the genus Nitzschia, which was impossible to differentiate from other small, oval Nitzschia spp. without the help of SEM and molecular phylogeny. Subsequently, we have begun a new research program on the diatom diversity of Lake Van to better understand the communities of this extreme environment. New sampling campaigns were conducted in Lake Van between 2019 and 2024 and, applying a similar approach as for N. anatoliensis, three additional new species have already been described, namely Navicula vanseea41, Halamphora vantushpaensis42 and Halamphora witkowskii43.

In some of our newly acquired samples, we found specimens of a Craticula species that we could not identify; it corresponds to none of the species for lake Van listed by Legler and Krasske (who would have classified Craticula species within Navicula). So far, out of the 60 described species of Craticula spp3., 11 have been recorded in Türkiye44. Among them, six are often reported (namely Craticula accomoda, C. ambigua, C. cuspidata, C.halophila, C. simplex, C.buderi), while the other five have scarcely ever been observed, namely Craticula minusculoides, C. molestiformis, C. subminuscula and C. anatoliana. As suggested by its name, the last species was discovered in Anatolia, more precisely in Lake Nar, a brackish lake in the central part of Türkiye45. Preliminary observations suggested that although displaying similarities with C. anatoliana, the newly observed Craticula specimens from Lake Van could represent a distinct new species.

The aim of the current article is to document the characteristics of the Craticula from Lake Van and formally describe it as Craticula vanensis sp. nov. We give a detailed morphological description based on LM and SEM, followed by comparisons with similar species. We take advantage of the increasing number of metabarcoding studies of continental waters to investigate the distribution of C. vanensis outside Lake Van. The plastid (cpDNA) and mitochondrial genomes (mtDNA) were sequenced and the results used, in part, to construct a phylogeny of Craticula and related taxa.

Etymology

The specific epithet “vanensis” refers to Lake Van, the highly alkaline soda lake in eastern Türkiye where the species was first discovered.

Distribution and ecology: The species was observed at various sampling sites along the lake’s shores, specifically in Ahlat, Adilcevaz, and Erciş. The populations seem to be perennial, with specimens observed in 2021 and 2024. Physicochemical parameters were measured at sampling sites: the salinity was 11.2‰, pH 9.5 and conductivity 17.667 µS/cm. The species was also later found in different soda ponds in Hungary, extending its known distribution range from eastern Anatolia to Central Europe.

Comparisons with other Craticula species and Craticula systematics

When Round et al.8 and Mann & Stickle9 restored Craticula as an independent genus, Grunow’s original criterion – the capacity to form skeletal internal valves – was only one of several characteristics they used to separate Craticula from Navicula. Others were raphe structure (simpler and more symmetrical internally in Craticula), areola structure (the poroids of Craticula are not elongate ‘lineolae’ as in Navicula), the absence of any central area in Craticula (though it is sometimes very small in Navicula), the presence of long bar-like pyrenoids in Navicula but not Craticula, and chloroplast division occurring before chloroplast rotation in Navicula9. More than 30 years later, there is almost no extra information on the chloroplasts of any of the c. 60 species now assigned to the genus, except for C. pseudocitrus, in which there seems to be a single H-shaped chloroplast51, as in early interphase in C. halophila9. Craticula vanensis has two chloroplasts per cell for at least part of interphase but apart from this we were unable to determine chloroplast dynamics or pyrenoid shape.

With respect to valve structure, C. vanensis agrees well with the pattern established by Mann & Stickle9 for C. cuspidata, C. ambigua and C. halophila and confirmed since in many other Craticula species: the raphe sternum is more or less symmetrical internally (lacking the accessory ribs present in Navicula and the allied genera Trachyneis, Pseudogomphonema, Seminavis and Rhoikoneis), there is no central area, and the central (proximal) raphe endings are not enclosed in a thickened ‘nodule’. However, unlike the three other species mentioned, most of the areolae of C. vanensis are apically elongate, internally as well as externally (Fig. 2). In contrast, in C. halophila, although the areolae are elongate externally, internally they are more or less circular9.

Craticula vanensis was not observed to form craticular valves in our cultures, nor in the natural populations we studied. We surveyed the literature and found reports of craticular valves in C. perrotettii1,52, C. cuspidata and C. ambigua6,9, C. pampeana53,54, C. halophila55, C. halopannonica2, C. acidoclinata2,56, C. riparia2,57, C. sardiniana58, C. obaesa59, C. fumantii, C. germainii and C. aff. acidoclinata60, and C. coloradensis. It is noticeable that the species known to form craticular valves are large-celled, whereas those in which no craticulae have been seen are generally smaller (e.g. with valves < 10 μm wide, such as C. buderi, C. molesta, C. molestiformis and others) than those in which craticulae have been reported, though some large-celled species (e.g. C. orientalis, C. subpampeana, C. gadorensis) have also not (yet) been seen with craticular valves. This correlation was noted also by Kusber & Cocquyt46. It will be interesting to see whether craticula formation is restricted to a clade of large-celled species within the genus (the rbcL and 18 S rRNA gene trees given by Tuji et al.51 are consistent with this idea but include too few large-celled species to be supportive).

Craticula vanensis is a small-celled species and displays similarities to Craticula buderi, C. elkab, C. anatoliana and C. vixvisibilis (Table 1). Craticula elkab has similar dimensions but is generally narrower at the centre and has differently shaped apices, which taper to protracted, subcapitate apices. It is typically associated with electrolyte-rich and likely hyperalkaline waters2. As described by Lange-Bertalot2, Craticula buderi is variable in shape, ranging from broadly elliptic to elliptic-lanceolate, lanceolate, or linear-lanceolate and the apices can be broadly rounded to protracted, rostrate, or subcapitate. Lange-Bertalot also indicates that the cell size is rather variable, with lengths between 10.0 and 40.0 μm and widths of 5–8 μm, and that its stria density can vary from 19 (exceptionally 17) to 24 in 10 μm. However, in the original description of the species, Hustedt47 and also Beauger et al.48 indicated a much narrower range of shape, size and dimensions (Table 1). Craticula buderi sensu Hustedt47 is apparently a much shorter and relatively wider diatom than C. vanensis. Moreover, our measurements from the type specimens illustrated by Lange-Bertalot2 give striae densities of 15–18 per 10 μm, which is lower than given by Hustedt47 and much less than in C. vanensis. In another population identified by Lange-Bertalot2 as belonging to C. buderi, from Lake Tegel near Berlin, the stria density is again lower than in C. vanensis (15–20 in 10 μm)2. Ecologically, C. buderi is said to be cosmopolitan, inhabiting fresh- and brackish waters with average to high electrolyte content. Other specimens referred to as C. buderi in Lange-Bertalot2, collected from a freshwater spring in marly soil and limestone near Frankfurt am Main, Germany, likely represent an undescribed independent species. Overall, we agree with Beauger et al.48 that “most likely different taxa including undescribed species, have been included in C. buderi” by Lange-Bertalot2 and other authors and it is possible that C. vanensis has been seen previously but identified as C. buderi.

Craticula vanensis and Craticula anatoliana have similar valve outlines and dimensions but C. vanensis has more closely spaced areolae (50 areolae in 10 μm compared to 40–45) and almost parallel striae towards the poles. In C. anatoliana they are convergent45.

Finally, taxa such as C. vixvisibilis or vixvisibilis var. distinctior have a similar stria density to C. vanensis but differ in their attenuated apices and the positions of the polar nodules (i.e. where the raphe fissures end internally), which are distant from the apex61. Other Craticula species are wider than C. vanensis, or have coarser striation, or are lanceolate-elliptical, and are unlikely to be confused with it.

Genomics

The rRNA operon—The complete rRNA operon has been obtained and deposited in GenBank (PQ892177). Its complete length is 6,068 bp, consisting of 1,784 bp for 18 S rRNA, 413 bp for the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1), 155 bp for 5.8 S rRNA, 468 bp for the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) and 3,248 bp for 28 S rRNA.

Plastome—The complete plastome (115,466 bp, GenBank: PQ891886) (Fig. 3) could be directly retrieved from the assembly of short-read sequences. The large single-copy region (LSC) is 61,650 bp long and encodes for 75 proteins and 17 tRNAs. It also contains the ssrA gene coding for a putative transfer-messenger RNA. The small single-copy region (SSC) is 40,238 bp long and encodes 51 proteins, seven tRNAs and one non-conserved open reading frame (ORF), namely orf133. The inverted-repeat region (IR) is 6,789 bp long and encodes two proteins, three rRNAs and three tRNAs.

Map of the plastome of Craticula vanensis SZCZEY 2171.

The plastome contains several of the protein-coding genes that have been reported as occasionally missing among diatoms62,63. Among them, bas1/ycf42, thiS, thiG and acpP are worth being mentioned, considering that they are not found in species of Fistulifera, the closest genus from Craticula with available plastid genomes. The plastome contains tsf and syfB, which are also often lost.

Mitogenome—It proved impossible to retrieve a complete, circular mitogenome from the assembly of short-read sequences. BLASTn queries on this assembly returned several fragments, some of them at much higher coverages than other contigs. Most of the protein-coding genes were contained in a 56,958 bp contig with 170× coverage. This contig lacks cob, nad5 and nad6, which were found in another 10,770 bp contig with 167.0× coverage. The rRNA genes were found in two high-coverage contigs: rnl in a 2,559 bp contig at 1158.4× coverage and rns in a 1,385 bp contig at 917.0× coverage. These contigs are available as fasta sequences in the Supplementary file S1.

The mitogenome was retrieved as a complete circular molecule in the assembly of Oxford Nanopore reads (Fig. 4). Its size stabilized at 116,698 bp (GenBank: PV031684) after three iterations of Pilon polishing with short-reads. The mitogenome encodes 34 conserved proteins and 29 tRNAs. Nine group II introns were found in protein-coding genes, eight of them in cox1, the remaining one in nad7. No candidate for atp8 could be identified, a gene that was also reported missing in Eunotia naegelii64. The genome also contains 16 ORFs. One of them (orf151) corresponds to the conserved ORF generally found inside a cluster associating rps11 and mttB/tatC65,66. For the remaining ORFs, six are putative group II intron-encoded reverse transcriptases in the cox1 gene. The remaining nine ORFs (orf313_1 to orf313_3 and orf314_1 to orf314_6) are located in the complex region described below. They show similarities to partial NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (Nad2), especially to the N-terminal part. All genes are encoded on the same strand of the mitogenome, except mttB, rps11, tRNA-Tyr, tRNA-Pro, tRNA-Arg and tRNA-Ala. As already observed among other species of diatoms65, nad11 is split into two neighbouring subunits, here labelled nad11a and nad11b.

Map of the mitogenome of Craticula vanensis SZCZEY 2171.

While annotating the mitochondrial genome, we noticed that numerous protein-coding genes contain multiple in-frame UGA codons (Table 2) that would lead to heavily truncated proteins if translated using code 1. Functional proteins could be obtained by translating by genetic code 4 instead, in which both UGA and UGG code for tryptophan, and the distribution of UGA and UGG codons is indicated in Table 2, showing comparable levels. However, subsequent investigations of the meaning of the UGA codon by Codetta suggested that it could instead be reassigned to arginine (Supplementary File S2). In such case, out of seven potential codons to translate arginine (Table 2), almost a third of the residues would be coded by UGA. In the absence of confirmation by protein sequencing and taking into account that GenBank does not currently provide a corresponding translation table for this reassignment, code 4 was retained for annotation. Because of this, the current protein translations that appear on GenBank might be incorrect.

Although rare, genetic code 4 is not unknown among Stramenopiles66,67. It has been especially documented in the mitogenomes of species from the diatom genus Thalassiosira68–70, in which a dedicated mitochondrial tRNA-Trp(UCA) was also found. Deviations to genetic code code 4 also evolved independently among certain groups of Chlorophyceae71–73.

A reassignment of UGA to arginine has never been documented for diatoms, but it has been suspected in the plastid genomes of macroalgae from the order Trentepohliales74 and the mitogenome of some tunicates of the family Oikopleuridae75. The validation of this putative reassignment of the UGA codon and the distribution of this alternative code among diatoms remain to be studied. As confirmed by recent molecular phylogenies76, the genus Craticula belongs to the family Stauroneidaceae. Four complete mitogenomes for this family are currently available, namely those of Fistulifera solaris77,78, Fistulifera saprophila79, Proschkinia staurospeciosa80 and Schizostauron trachyderma81. All are decoded using the standard genetic code. Sequencing of additional mitogenomes from Craticula spp. are needed to determine if the change of genetic code is a widespread feature of this genus, or is in contrary limited if not restricted to C. vanensis.

Multiple copies of several genes were identified in the large mitogenome region that could be resolved only in the Oxford Nanopore assembly. This region, which extends from rns_1 to rnl_6 (Fig. 3), contains six complete copies of rns and rnl that are highly similar in their sequences (99.74% to 99.93% sequence identity for rnl; 97.79 to 99.87% sequence identity for rns). In addition to the nine aforementioned ORFs, four copies of tRNA-Glu were identified in this region. To our knowledge, C. vanensis represents the first species of diatom with multiple copies of mitochondrial rRNA genes. Duplications of other genes have been recorded in diatoms. For example, duplications of cox1 were found in the mitogenomes of Navicula tsukamotoi and Nitzschia traheaformis82. In the case of N. traheaformis, three copies of cox1 gene with variable intron contents were identified. Two copies of the cob genes were also found in Haslea pseudostrearia (GenBank: OK729583). Finally, a pseudo-gene originating from a duplication of the atp6 gene was found to be conserved in two geographically and genetically distinct populations of Haslea silbo83. Only few large tandem repeats with a number of copies above three were found. One is located between rps4 and tRNA-Asn, is 153 bp long with five copies. The second is located between two tRNA (tRNA-Val and tRNA-Met), is 119 bp long with three copies.

Phylogeny.

Out of the four different trees obtained, only the one inferred from a concatenated alignment of the 18 S rRNA, rbcL and psbC genes presented a balance between sampling of species and robustness. For this reason, it is the only tree embedded in the main text of the article. On the subtree presented (Fig. 5), C. vanensis is sister to a clade of Dorofeyukea spp., although this relationship displayed unequal support depending on the software used for phylogeny (bs = 65% by RAxML, but bs = 95% by IQTREE and pp = 1.0 by Bayesian Inference). The complete trees are available as Supplementary File S3 (RaxML tree), Supplementary File S4 (IQTREE) and Supplementary File S5 (ExaBayes). The C. vanensis + Dorofeyukea clade was sister to a clade containing other Craticula spp. included in the dataset (poorly supported in all 3 analyses). To test this relationship, we inferred an additional phylogenetic analysis in which we forced all Craticula spp. into monophyly. The resulting tree was not significantly different from the tree illustrated in Fig. 5, and for this reason, is not shown here.

Maximum phylogenetic tree inferred from RAxML analysis of the concatenated alignment of the 18 S rRNA gene, rbcL and psbC genes from 547 taxa. Support values for each node are reported in the order of bootstrap support from RAxML and IQTREE, followed by posterior probability from ExaBayes. Support values reported as “--” when the clade was not observed in that analysis. The subtree shows the clade that contains Craticula spp. and Dorofeyukea spp.

Though the surprising position of C. vanensis in the phylogenetic tree presented above appears to be fairly robust and well-supported by most analyses, we cannot rule out the effects of incomplete data among the three genes forming the dataset. Indeed, of all the Craticula and Dorofeyukea spp. included in the tree, only three feature a complete three-genes dataset. Also, the sampling of taxa for Craticula only partially represents the known diversity of the genus.

The possibilities of running single-gene trees for verification were impeded by the composition of the dataset. In relation specifically to the cluster containing C. vanensis, no psbC gene was available for any of the Dorofeyukea species and all 18 S rRNA sequences available for this genus were shorter than 500 bp long. As a consequence, no psbC or 18 S rRNA tree would have been informative in the present case, so only a rbcL-inferred tree was obtained (Supplementary File S6). As for the 3-gene tree, the rbcL tree separates C. vanensis + Dorofeyukea spp. from the rest of Craticula spp. but with very low support (only 4%). Also, there was only a 57% support at the node associating C. vanensis with Dorofeyukea spp.

The two remaining trees were inferred from 7 genes (18 S rRNA, atpB, psaB, psaA, psbA, psbC, rbcL) and the nucleotide sequences of 124 plastid genes (list of genes available as Supplementary File S7) datasets, with consequently decreasing sampling of taxa (210 and 166, respectively). In the 7-gene tree (Supplementary Files S8), C. vanensis appears sister to C. cuspidata strain HK061, the only Craticula species for which a complete 7-gene dataset was available. The tree inferred from the 124 plastid genes (Supplementary Files S9) associates C. vanensis with Fistulifera spp., with maximum support.

From a taxonomical point of view, assignment of C. vanensis to Dorofeyukea is clearly not supported by morphological characters. The main criteria to distinguish a diatom from the genus Dorofeyukea, in the context of separation from Craticula, are the presence of a pseudoseptum at each apical ending and the radiating striae84, both features absent from C. vanensis (but present in a wide variety of other raphid pennate diatoms, suggesting multiple instances of convergent evolution). From a practical point of view, therefore, placing C. vanensis in Dorofeyukea would not be ideal (they share no unique morphological characteristics) while establishing a separate genus for C. vanensis can be justified only if the relationship indicated by the 3-gene tree is real (which remains doubtful given current gene and taxon sampling).

In conclusion, we consider the association of C. vanensis with Dorofeyukea in the 3-gene tree to be an interesting phenomenon that requires further investigation when (a) there has been further genetic sampling of Craticula species and (b) longer sequences and/or (b) genomic data are available for Dorofeyukea, when new strains can be obtained. Meanwhile, as a practical expedient, C. vanensis is placed in Craticula, even though this genus may subsequently prove to be paraphyletic, as the current 3-gene tree suggests.

Metabarcoding insights and microscopical confirmation

Using BLASTn searches of ASV datasets, we detected C. vanensis in the outputs from eight metabarcoded samples collected in 2019 or 2020 during studies of Hungarian soda pans and lakes85,86. The sites represented were a bog area of Velencei-tó, Kisteleki-Müller-szék, Madarász-tó, Ősze-szék, Kakasszéki-tó and Nagy-Széksóstó. All have high Na+, CO32− and HCO3− (values of ca. 440–780, 28.5–240 and 960–1940 mg/L, respectively), conductivity (c. 2200–3600 µS/cm) and pH (8.5–9.5).

The presence of C. vanensis was later confirmed by direct SEM observations of cleaned wild material, as presented in Fig. 6. The frustules observed were 20.3–20.4 μm in length and 4.7–4.8 μm in width, with stria density of 22 in 10 μm.

In contrast to the Hungarian soda pans and lakes, there were abundant C. cuspidata ASVs and sometimes also ASVs related to C. halophila in samples of microbial mats from high-sulphur environments with conductivities between 2015 and 5480 µS/cm87, t, but no match with C. vanensis. Searches in the 12 rbcL datasets assembled by Pérez-Burillo et al.88 also failed to recover any close matches to C. vanensis. This set comprised metabarcoding data from freshwater lakes, rivers and streams in North America (California, Ohio and Ontario), Europe (France, Spain, UK, Fennoscandia), an Indian Ocean island (Mayotte) and Tibet. Likewise, the very large US river and stream dataset (> 1700 samples) analysed by Smucker et al.89 contained no ASVs matching C. vanensis.

The most abundant soda lake ASV (ASV_12) that mapped to C. vanensis was an exact match to the homologous 263-bp region of the reference sequence (GenBank: PQ891886). Despite this, ASV_12 was not assigned to Craticula by the Wang classifier; indeed it remained unclassified at genus, family, and even at order level. This is understandable given the results of a BLASTn search in GenBank, using ASV_12 as the query, which returned no hits with better than 93.9% identity, even among other Stauroneidaceae (Craticula, Stauroneis, Dorofeyukea), while there was a smattering of hits of between 92 and 93.9% identity drawn from a variety of genera and families (e.g. Amphora, Gomphonema, Luticola, Sellaphora and even the eupodiscalean genus Pseudictyota).

Eight further ASVs from the soda lakes mapped to C. vanensis, seven of them differing from the reference C. vanensis sequence by 1 bp and one differing by 2 bp. None of these ASVs occurred in more than one sample: one contained a stop codon and most exhibited amino-acid substitutions that involved a change in structural type (e.g. Gly→Val, Gly→Arg, Ala→Asp); only one involved no change in the amino-acid coded. The next most similar ASVs to the C. vanensis reference sequence were well separated from C. vanensis but seemed to be part of a clade also containing isolates identified as C. halophila or C. buderi, though this relationship was without statistical support.

A PebbleScout search in the NCBI SRA archive, using the C. vanensis 18 S rDNA sequence as the query, revealed very good matches with several Roche 454 pyrosequencing reads from run SRR618381, including one of 100% identity over 487 aligned bases (Sequence ID: SRA: SRR618381.68366.2). The sample from which the reads were obtained was taken from the surface of Lake Arenguadi, a soda lake in Ethiopia (8.69483°N, 38.97733°E) on 27 March 2011; the pH was 9.85 and salinity 0.28%. The reads were from shotgun sequencing and hence contained sequences from both eukaryotes and prokaryotes90.

Our study began with a survey of samples and isolates from Lake Van and the discovery of a Craticula that did not have any morphological match among described species, nor any molecular match among GenBank rRNA or rbcL sequences. By definition, therefore, the known geographical range of C. vanensis was initially only Lake Van and its niche requirements could be estimated only from the ranges of environmental variables measured in the Lake Van biotopes where it was found. However, metabarcoding data show that C. vanensis (or more precisely, short Craticula rbcL sequences exactly matching C. vanensis) also occur in soda pans and lakes in Hungary, c. 2000 km away, and 18 S rRNA sequences suggests that a closely related species, if not the same, is present in a soda lake in Ethiopia, more than 2800 km away, suggesting that C. vanensis is not a Lake Van endemic. At the same time the data suggest that the species extends into less saline waters than are characteristic of Lake Van, though apparently not into the more dilute freshwaters represented in the other datasets included in our analysis. This use of metabarcoding datasets to determine geographical and ecological ranges is still unusual for freshwater diatoms88 but has been employed successfully for a while for marine planktonic diatoms, taking advantage of the massive global sampling program of the TARA Oceans and other projects91,92. The rapid expansion of metabarcoding data clearly has great potential for accelerating the search for newly described or redefined species distant from the type localities, providing of course that suitable DNA reference sequences are available. However, with such short barcodes (263 bp in most of the datasets we used) and the use of rather conservative genes (rbcL, 18 S rRNA), there is always the possibility that two or more morphospecies may share the same barcode (as shown for some surirelloid species by Pérez-Burillo et al.93. Hence, metabarcoding records should always be checked by reference to physical specimens in parallel samples and this is something we plan to do for all the Craticula species present in the soda pans.

We interpret the Hungarian ASVs differing from C. vanensis by just one or two base-pairs as likely artifacts, judging by the amino-acid changes they imply. None of them occurred in any sample where the exact C. vanensis ASV was not also present. However, as further metabarcoding datasets and Sanger sequences accumulate, it will be worthwhile to scan them to see if any of the deviant ASVs do in fact represent real lineages.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Epilithic samples were collected from Lake Van, located in Eastern Anatolia, Türkiye, during two separate periods: July 2021 and September 2024. Sampling sites included the southern side of Adilcevaz (38°79’83’’N, 42°72’16’’E), Erciş (38°56’5755"N, 43°10’5544"E) and Ahlat (38°45’2737"N, 42°30’5697"E) in the province of Van, Türkiye (Fig. 7). Physicochemical parameters (including salinity, pH and conductivity) were measured in situ using a LangeHach 40 d multi-parameter meter (Hach Lange CmbH Headquarter, Düsseldorf, Germany).

Map of the sampling location (A) location of Lake Van in Türkiye. The red frame indicates the position of Lake Van (B) general view of the lake. The pins indicate the position of the sampling area (Esri. (2023). The map was created by the authors using ArcGIS Pro version 3.1.0 software (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA; https://www.esri.com).

Isolation and cultivation

Wild samples were inoculated into a sterile plastic Petri dish containing f/2 culture medium94, modified to match the salinity of Lake Van as measured in the field. Single cells were isolated from Petri dishes by means of glass micropipettes and using an inverted light microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS100; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a ×10 and ×20 lens. The culture was considered monospecific after three subsequent reinoculations into new Petri dish at intervals of at least two weeks and thorough inspections of the cultures using the inverted microscope. For active growth, the cultures were kept at room temperature of ca. 16–18 °C, with a light intensity of 60 µmol photons m−2s− 1 and a photoperiod of 14 h of light/10 h of darkness.

Microscopy

For both wild and cultivated material, frustules were cleaned using first 10% hydrochloric acid (HCl), followed by a several rinses with deionized water, then boiled at 170 °C for 6 h in a 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), then rinsed four times with deionized water. Cleaned diatom frustules suspended in dH2O were pipetted onto microscope cover slips and left to air dry to be later mounted on microscope glass slide with Naphrax (Brunel Microscopes, Chippenham, UK) for LM investigations.

LM microphotographs were taken at the University of Szczecin using a Zeiss Axio Scope A1 (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) with an oil immersion Zeiss Plan Apochromat ×100 lens using a Canon EOS 500D camera with the Canon EOS Utility software (Canon, Tokyo, Japan).

For SEM, the cleaned material was pipetted onto a Whatman Nuclepore polycarbonate membrane filter with 2–5 μm pores and then filtered. The filter was mounted onto aluminum stubs. The stubs were sputter coated with 20 nm of gold. SEM observations were performed using a Hitachi SU8020 ultra-high-resolution microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

SEM samples of the Hungarian material were prepared as described above, but pictures were taken at the Eskişehir Technical University (Türkiye) on a ZEISS Ultra microscope (Oberkochen, Germany).

Sequencing, assembly and annotation of the organellar genomes

For short reads sequencing, a 150 mL culture in exponential growth phase was maintained at the University of Szczecin under active growth and harvested by gentle centrifugation. DNA was extracted following Doyle and Doyle95 and sent to the Beijing Genomics Institute (Shenzhen, China) where it was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform for a total of ca. 40 M 150-bp paired-end reads. Assembly was conducted using SPAdes 3.15.596 with a k-mer of 125. Sequences of interest were datamined from the contig file using standalone BLASTn queries97. Plastome contigs were merged with the help of Consed98. As explained in the results section, the mitogenome could not be completely resolved by short reads sequencing.

For long reads sequencing, all the steps (DNA extraction, preparation of the library and sequencing on MinION cell) were conducted at the Genomic Analysis Platform of the Laval University (Québec, Canada) following an established protocol99. The reads were assembled with Flye 2.9.2, using the --meta mode and an overlap parameter of 2000100. The mitogenome retrieved from this assembly was polished by three iterations of Pilon v1.24101.

Protein-coding genes were annotated with the help of the findORF suite of tools developed at the Laval University, as described in previous publications77,102–105. The boundaries of the tRNA genes were found with tRNAscan-SE106, and those of rRNA genes by alignments with the corresponding sequences from Haslea nusantara107. The presence of repetitions was investigated Tandem repeats finder108. Codon usage of mitochondrial protein-coding genes was assessed using the Sequence Manipulation Suite109. The coding region of all annotated proteins, including the putative reverse transcriptase, were also analyzed using Codetta v2.0110. Maps of the organellar genomes were obtained using OGDRAW111.

Phylogenetic analyses

Four different phylogenies were conducted, based on four different datasets. The first dataset was based on 3 genes widely used by the diatomist community74,112–116, namely the nuclear 18 S rRNA, plastid rbcL and psbC from 546 taxa. 18 S rRNA gene sequences were aligned using SSU align117 and a covariance model based on secondary structure models of 33 diatoms118 as outlined in Li et al.119. Protein encoding plastid genes (psbC and rbcL) were aligned using MUSCLE implemented in Seaview 4.0120. Data were then partitioned by stem and loop (paired and unpaired sites), and protein encoding data were partitioned by codon position and gene, for a total of eight starting partitions (alignment available as Supplementary File S10, and partition as Supplementary File S11). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analyses were run on the 3-gene dataset using the RAxML and IQTREE platforms, in addition to a Bayesian Inference run using ExaBayes. Analyses were conducted following the procedures outlined in Lobban et al.121. Two species of the genus Asterionellopsis (A. socialis and A. glacialis) were used as an outgroup. To test the monophyly of Craticula, all Craticula data in the 3-gene dataset were constrained to monophyly and reanalyzed. Site likelihood values were calculated independently for each constraint tree in RAxML and then read into CONSEL, where a battery of tree tests were performed122–124.

Two other phylogenies were later tested, using RAxML only. The first one was based on the rbcL dataset previously used, with the same options and same outgroup. The second was based on previously published datasets125,126 consisting in the concatenated alignment of seven genes (nuclear 18 S rRNA gene, plastidic atpB, psaB, psaA, psbA, psbC and rbcL), representing 210 species and rooted with Bolidomonas pacifica (Bolidophyceae). The last phylogeny was conducted on a concatenated nucleotide alignment of 124 plastid genes (Supplementary File S6) from 166 species127 and rooted with seven species of Triparmales128. Genes were aligned separarely with MAFFT 7 and the -auto option129, then alignments were trimmed with trimAl and the -automated1 option130. Trimmed alignents were concatenated with Phyutility 2.7.1131 and the best model of evolution (GTR + I+G4) was chosen using by ModelTest-NG132 on the concatenated alignment. Phylogeny was conducted using IQTREE 3.1.1133 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. Total lengths of the alignments were 3,915 bp, 1,350 bp, 9,349 bp and 81,060 bp for the 3-gene, rbcL only, 7-gene and 124-gene datasets respectively.

Metabarcoding

In order to explore the wider geographical distribution of C. vanensis, we looked for matches to the C. vanensis rbcL sequence in several available metabarcoding datasets that used the 263-bp or 331-bp barcode regions developed by Vasselon et al.134 and Kelly et al.135 respectively (for a comparison of these regions with respect to species differentiation, see Pérez-Burillo et al.93. The datasets comprised the 12 datasets analysed by Pérez-Burillo et al.88; a large survey of river and stream periphyton in the USA (GenBank BioProject PRJNA1086463)89; a study of high-sulphur, low-oxygen environments specifically highlighting Craticula species (BioProject PRJNA1005976)87; and studies of soda lakes by Duleba et al.85 and Àcs et al.86. In each case (for both 263- and 331-bp marker datasets), we generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) de novo from the raw fastq files using cutadapt136 and DADA2137, as described by Pérez-Burillo et al.138. Preliminary taxonomic assignments were made using the naïve Bayesian classifier139 with the Diat.barcode reference database140, version 12 (https://entrepot.recherche.data.gouv.fr/dataset.xhtml? persistentId=doi:https://doi.org/10.15454/TOMBYZ). However, since version 12 does not contain C. vanensis and because the C. vanensis rbcL sequence (GenBank PQ891886) is quite divergent from other Craticula species, we used local BLASTn95 to search for matches in each of the target datasets, rather than relying only on Wang–Diat.barcode assignments to Craticula.

To explore the relationships of the Craticula ASVs in the Hungarian soda lakes, we downloaded and aligned all available rbcL Sanger sequences of Craticula isolates (and single cells) from GenBank and Diat.barcode and eliminated any for the same species that were exactly alike over their whole length. We then added the ASVs to the alignment and checked them for stop codons and implausible amino-acid substitutions (changes in amino-acids that would likely disrupt folding or function). We trimmed the alignment using trimAl130, using either the ‘gappy’ option or, much more conservatively, a ‘custom’ option with gap threshold 0.5, similarity threshold 0.9, and minimum conservation 80%. Some terminal taxa became exact synonyms as a result of trimming. The resulting alignments were then used, partitioned by codon position, for maximum likelihood analyses with the GTR+gamma model141, with 1000 or 500 bootstrap replicates (thorough bootstrap option). Trees were visualized using iTOL142.

In addition, we used PebbleScout143, implemented at https://pebblescout.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to search for other possible records of C. vanensis in metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets, using the C. vanensis 18 S rRNA gene sequence as the query.
