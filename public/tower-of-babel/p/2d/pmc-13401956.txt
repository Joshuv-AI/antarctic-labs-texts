Azurins With Tunable Reduction Potentials as Redox Mediators for Extracellular Electron Transfer
Authors: Alexis J Holwerda, Jing‐Xiang Wang, Yang Gao, Yi Lu, Benjamin K Keitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectroactive microbes, such as Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1, have been harnessed for a variety of bioelectrochemical and catalytic applications. Efforts to improve the electrochemical performance of these bacteria have included the use or biological synthesis of small‐molecule redox mediators, such as flavins. However, limited redox range and complex biosynthetic pathways have restricted their broad applications. To overcome these limitations, we report the use of azurin, a cupredoxin commonly used as electron transfer proteins, as a redox mediator for S. oneidensis extracellular electron transfer (EET). We showed that S. oneidensis reduces four different azurin variants, spanning a 440 mV range, and that the reduction rate is dependent on azurin concentration (K M = 28 ± 9 µM) and redox potential. We then applied these findings to several different bio‐ and electrosynthetic applications. We utilized azurin as a mediator in multiple EET‐driven biocatalytic reactions, including atom transfer radical polymerization and azo dye decolorization. Finally, we showed that the inclusion of exogenous azurin improves current generation in microbial fuel cells. These results demonstrate the potential advantages of engineered redox proteins possess over small molecules for use in EET‐driven applications.Keywords: azurin, biocatalysis, extracellular electron transfer, metalloenzymes, Shewanella oneidensis

Introduction

Respiratory electron flux from electroactive organisms can be harnessed for a variety of applications, such as power generation via microbial fuel cells (MFCs), microbial electrosynthesis, and catalysis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. For many of these applications, the model electroactive bacterium Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1 is an attractive species due to its genetic tractability and respiratory plasticity [6, 7]. Under anaerobic conditions, S. oneidensis MR‐1 leverages extracellular electron transfer (EET) and the exchange of electrons across the cell membrane for respiration, movement, and sensing via the metal‐reducing pathway MtrCAB [8, 9]. In its native environment, the MtrCAB complex has a redox potential between −450 and +100 mV [10] (all E°′s in this article are vs. NHE) and MtrC specifically has a redox potential of −312 mV [11]. Natively, S. oneidensis utilizes the MtrCAB complex to transfer electrons to flavins (E°′ = 197 mV [12]), as well as inorganic electron acceptors like iron citrate. Using the Mtr pathway, S. oneidensis can carry out either direct electron transfer (DET) to exogenous acceptors or mediated electron transfer (MET) via soluble redox shuttles [13, 14, 15, 16]. MET relies on flavins, which are natively synthesized by S. oneidensis [14, 15], and the addition of exogenous flavins generally increases electron flow and current output in bioelectrochemical systems [17, 18]. Similarly, heterologous expression of flavins and flavoprotein biosynthesis pathways both enhance EET [19]. However, flavins are formed via relatively complex biosynthetic pathways, have fixed reduction potentials (E°′s), and relatively narrow redox ranges with modification [14, 20, 21, 22], making them challenging targets for further biological engineering and optimization.

In addition to flavins and other small‐molecule mediators, many bacteria utilize redox proteins for electron transfer [23]. For example, periplasmic cytochromes, such as small tetraheme cytochrome (cctA or STC), are necessary for the reduction of a variety of organic compounds in S. oneidensis [24]. Similarly, Geobacter sulfurreducens utilizes the periplasmic redox protein PpcA as a part of its more intricate redox network [25]. While these redox proteins offer more opportunities for biological engineering, they each have a fixed E°′. Further, since these proteins have coevolved with the specific microorganism's electron transport chain, they are difficult to tune without influencing metabolic fitness. To take advantage of the full potential of electroactive organisms for broad applications, we sought to develop a redox mediator that could be tuned over a broad redox range.

We investigated cupredoxins, which are a family of type 1 copper (T1Cu)‐containing proteins that facilitate electron transfer in several vital biological processes, including photosynthesis, respiration, and catalysis, as potential EET acceptors [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Of particular interest is azurin from Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is natively involved in electron transfer to cytochrome c 551 oxidase [31]. Recently, we tuned azurin's E°′s to span the entire physiological E°′ range from ≈ −1 to ≈ +1 V without affecting the overall structure or conformation of the protein [32, 33]. In general, E°′ can be tuned by introducing mutations around the T1Cu site and varying either the hydrophobicity or the hydrogen‐bonding network close to the conserved Cys that coordinates to the T1Cu. However, some azurin variants are difficult to purify and are not stable in ambient conditions [32]. Nevertheless, the azurin family is an ideal protein engineering candidate for enhancing EET in S. oneidensis for several reasons. First, azurin natively interacts with other c‐type cytochromes, similar to those in the Mtr pathway [6, 31, 34]. Additionally, S. oneidensis may have its own distant azurin ortholog [35], and azurin and its variants fall within the E°′ range for electron transfer via the Mtr pathway [36]. Finally, S. oneidensis MR‐1 has been shown to independently reduce small‐molecule Cu(II) species under a variety of conditions [37, 38, 39]. Thus, we hypothesized that S. oneidensis could utilize a non‐native electron transfer protein like azurin as a redox mediator for EET (Figure 1).

Simplified electron transfer between inner membrane (IM) to outer membrane (OM) of S. oneidensis and MtrCAB complex to proposed final electron acceptor, azurin.

Here, we show that S. oneidensis reduces azurin via EET from the Mtr pathway. We correlate reduction rate to azurin redox potential using several azurin variants. Next, we harness azurin as a mediator to enhance EET‐driven dye decolorization. We also show that azurin can be utilized in place of synthetic copper catalysts for atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP), and that polymerization rates depend on the azurin variant. Finally, we demonstrate azurins utility as a mediator in MFCs. Overall, our work highlights azurin as a useful biological redox mediator to enhance S. oneidensis electrosynthesis while bypassing constraints of typical chemical redox mediators.

Results and Discussion

Extracellular Electron Transfer from S. oneidensis MR‐1 Reduces Recombinant Wild‐Type Azurin

Wild‐type azurin (WT‐Az) has a redox potential of +310 mV [32]. Thus, we first quantified EET between S. oneidensis MR‐1 and azurin. Utilizing UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy, we anaerobically monitored azurin reduction after the addition of 30 µM oxidized WT‐Az (λ max = 625 nm [40]) to an anaerobic culture of S. oneidensis MR‐1 (Figures 2A and S1). The reduction rate of azurin was calculated from a pseudo‐first‐order kinetics model ((3.6 ± 0.4) × 10− 3 s−1). Assuming an OD600 = 0.2, ca. 108 CFU mL−1, the reduction rate of azurin per each S. oneidensis cell was ≈(3.6 ± 0.4) × 10−11 cell− 1 s−1. Given that S. oneidensis can reduce Fe(III) at a rate of (9.7 ± 0.5) × 10−8 cell− 1 s−1, based on previous data, the limiting step is likely due to protein–protein interactions [41].

(A,B) S. oneidensis MR‐1 reduces WT‐Az with dependence on Mtr pathway. Data represent mean ± SD of N = 4. Data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA with multiple comparison correction at α = 0.05. ****p < 0.0001, ns p > 0.05. (C) Concentration‐dependent fitting using a Michaelis–Menten model (K M = 28 ± 9 µM, V max = 0.008 ± 0.005 s−1) representing mean ± SD of N = 3.

We found that S. oneidensis was unable to reduce oxidized azurin as efficiently as the native redox partner for azurin, reduced cytochrome c 551, even when normalized to donor protein concentration. Assuming there are ≈2520 MtrC proteins per S. oneidensis cell [42], then the concentration of MtrC is ≈0.05 µM at an OD = 0.2. The fastest S. oneidensis‐reduced WT‐Az reduction rate, normalized for MtrC, measured under these conditions was (7.6 ± 0.3) × 10− 2 µMMtrC −1s−1. For comparison, the previously reported rate constant between oxidized azurin and reduced cytochrome c 551 was 6.8 µM c551 −1s−1 [43]. Similarly, previously reported reduction rates for Fe(III), a native S. oneidensis electron acceptor, are (1.2) × 103 µMMtrC −1s−1 [6]. Using the same assumptions as above for MtrC concentration, the reduction rate of WT‐Az at 100 µM was (4.9) × 10− 2 µMMtrC −1s−1, which is significantly lower than the reduction rate of native electron acceptor.

To determine if azurin interacts with S. oneidensis EET machinery specifically, we performed the same experiment with two EET‐deficient strains (Table S1), ΔmtrCΔomcAΔmtrF and ΔmtrCΔomcAΔmtrFΔmtrAΔmtrDΔdmsEΔSO4360ΔcctAΔrecA (ΔMtr) [6, 44]. As expected, removing the outer membrane (OM) cytochromes significantly slowed WT‐Az reduction rate ((5.0 ± 1.9) × 10− 4 s−1), while knocking out the Mtr pathway almost completely inhibited WT‐Az reduction ((3.0 ± 0.2) × 10− 4 s−1) (Figures 2A,B and S1). To rule out flavin interaction, we also measured azurin reduction using a flavin secretion knockout strain (Δbfe), significantly decreasing the concentration of soluble flavins that could be in the reactions [45]. We observed no significant difference in 30 µM WT‐Az reduction using Δbfe as compared to S. oneidensis MR‐1 (Figure S10), indicating that azurin reduction is unlikely to be mediated by flavins under our conditions. To further explore the relationship between EET and azurin reduction, we increased the concentration of WT‐Az from 10 to 40 µM and measured the reduction rate via UV–Vis (Figure 2C). Under these conditions, we observed concentration‐dependent kinetic behavior, as increasing the concentration of azurin led to an increase in reduction rate, up to 40 µM (K M = 28 ± 9 µM, V max = (7 ± 1) × 10− 3 s−1) [46, 47]. Together, these results show that S. oneidensis primarily reduces azurin through the Mtr pathway.

S. oneidensis MR‐1 Reduces Different Azurin Variants

After experimentally validating the ability of S. oneidensis to reduce WT azurin, we investigated EET from S. oneidensis to several azurin variants. We chose four azurin variants (Figure 3A and Table S2) with E°′s that are higher or lower than the E°′ of WT‐Az. The lowest potential variant, F114P, disrupts backbone hydrogen bonding between Phe114 and Cys112, weakening backbone strength. N47S‐Az modifies the hydrogen bonding to the sulfur of the conserved Cys that coordinates to the Cu center and was previously shown to increase E°′ to 396 mV [33]. NL‐Az (F114N/M121L) combines F114N, a mutation introducing a dipole interaction near T1Cu site, and M121L, a hydrophobic T1Cu axial mutation, resulting in an increased E°′ to 551 mV. SNL‐Az (N47S/F114N/M121L) combines the above N47S mutation with F114N/M121L double mutation. Together, these mutations increased E°′ to 640 mV [33]. To test the effect of amino acid substitutions on EET‐driven azurin reduction, we measured the reduction rate of each mutant (100 µM) over a period of ≈5 min (Figure 3B). We chose 100 µM due to the varying extinction coefficients of the different azurin variants, as this concentration provided high‐quality spectroscopic resolution for all variants tested. We observed significant differences in the reduction rate between the four variants (Figure 3B,C). N47S‐Az performed similarly to WT‐Az. SNL‐Az, which has the most positive E°′, exhibited a faster reduction rate than WT‐Az, while the lowest E°′ mutant, F114P‐Az, displayed significantly slower rate than any other azurin variant.

(A) Azurin mutants used in this study: F114P‐Az (Phe114Pro; E°′ = +171 mV [33]; PDB 2 GHZ), WT (E°′ = +310 [33] PDB 1AZU), N47S (Asn47Ser; E°′ = +3964) PDB 3JT2 [33]), NL (Phe114Asn/Met121Leu; E°′ = +551 mV [33]; PDB 3JTB and 3JT2 (modified)), SNL (Asn47Ser/Phe114Asn/Met121Leu; E°′ = +640 PDB 3JTB and 3JT2 [33]), and flavin mononucleotide (FMN) (E°′ = −197 mV [12]). Structures visualized using UCSF ChimeraX [48, 49]; (B) change in oxidized azurin concentration once mixed with S. oneidensis MR‐1 for each mutant over time; (C) MR‐1 rate of azurin reduction for 100 µM of each azurin mutant.

Overall, we observed reduction by S. oneidensis MR‐1 across five azurin variants, including WT‐Az, spanning greater than a 450 mV range. The reduction rate trend generally followed redox potential, with lower potential variants such as F114P‐Az being reduced at slower rates than higher potential variants.

Azurin Functions as a Mediator for Extracellular Electron Transfer‐Driven Dye Decolorization

Having confirmed that S. oneidensis MR‐1 can reduce azurin and its variants, we examined several potential applications. Shewanella spp. have been previously investigated for azo dye decolorization, with an effort to identify more efficient, stress‐tolerant strains [50, 51]. Previous work has implicated EET and the Mtr pathway [52], as well as flavins [53], suggesting that degradation of some dyes is controlled through these pathways. Thus, we hypothesized that S. oneidensis could utilize WT‐Az as an electron mediator to more efficiently decolorize azo dyes. We anaerobically incubated the azo dye reactive black 5 (RB5) with S. oneidensis MR‐1 and two different concentrations of WT‐Az while monitoring spectral changes via UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy over ≈18 h, although the reaction itself was completed over ≈5 h after a lag period. We used a calibration curve to convert UV–Vis absorption into dye concentration and calculated dye decolorization percentage from dye concentration over time using Equation (1).

S. oneidensis MR‐1 without WT‐Az decolorized RB5 at a rate of (7.2 ± 1.5) × 10− 5 s−1 (Figure 4C). The addition of WT‐Az increased the decolorization rate to (11 ± 1.4) × 10− 5 s−1 (10 µM WT) and (14 ± 1.6) × 10−5 s−1 (50 µM WT) (Figure 4C). Given that S. oneidensis reduces 10 µM WT‐Az at a rate of (1.6 ± 0.2) × 10− 3 s−1, which is faster than the decolorization rate, the bottleneck in kinetics is likely due to electron transfer to the azo bond. Given the spectral overlap, however, we cannot determine whether azurin reduction or dye decolorization occurs first. In the absence of S. oneidensis MR‐1, WT‐Az did not decolorize RB5 (Figure S4). To compare WT‐Az – mediated RB5 decolorization to literature values using riboflavin (RBF) as a mediator in the decolorization of amaranth [54], we normalized the rates to account for differing conditions by obtaining a rate ratio (Equation (2)).

Compared to RBF‐mediated dye decolorization, the normalized rate of 50 µM WT‐Az‐mediated decolorization was not significantly different (Figure S3A). The RBF‐mediated decolorization rates reported, however, used a lower concentration of RBF (10 µM). Compared to 10 µM WT‐Az‐mediated decolorization, there was not a significant difference between the normalized rate with RBF‐mediated decolorization (Figure S3A) (1.7 ± 0.7 vs. 2.7, respectively). Together, these data suggest that azurin can enhance EET‐driven dye degradation in a manner similar to flavins.

Extracellular Electron Transfer and Azurin Catalyze Atom Transfer Radical Polymerization

Azurin has previously been used as a scaffold for de novo enzyme design [55, 56, 57]. We have also previously shown that S. oneidensis MR‐1 can utilize EET to facilitate (ATRP using copper catalysts, such as Cu‐TPMA [2, 4]. Similarly, other groups have utilized alternative metalloenzymes to initiate radical polymerization, including horseradish peroxidase and laccase [58, 59]. Of the reported enzymes, laccase belongs to the class of multicopper oxidase, bearing a T1Cu center and a trinuclear copper center, where its T1Cu center is responsible for substrate recognition and electron transfer to the substrate [60]. Given these similarities, we explored azurin as a potential substitute for small‐molecule copper polymerization catalysts (Figure 5A). We inoculated an anaerobic reaction mixture containing poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate monomer (OEOMA500) and halogenated initiator with anaerobically pregrown S. oneidensis MR‐1, as previously described [2]. We added WT‐Az to each mixture and allowed reactions to proceed for 24 h, removing aliquots every 1.5 h for the initial 6 h. Polymerization activity was measured using 1H NMR spectroscopy and polymerization kinetics were fit to first‐order kinetics (Figures 5B and S5). To confirm WT‐Az‐catalyzed ATRP was driven by EET, we repeated the above‐described polymerization experiments with the ΔMtr pathway knockout [6]. As expected, removing EET machinery significantly inhibited polymerization activity (Figure 5B). Converting the polymerization rate into s−1, we calculated polymerization rate for WT‐Az ([WT‐Az] = 2 µM) as (1.3 ± 0.6) × 10− 5 s−1. Using data fit from WT‐Az concentration dependence (Figure 1C), the reduction rate for 2 µM WT‐Az is (4.3 ± 1.9) × 10− 4 s−1. Thus, the limiting factor in polymerization is likely the interaction between azurin and the growing polymer chain.

(A) Proposed ATRP mechanism with azurin as a copper catalyst; (B) WT‐Az‐catalyzed ATRP is significantly hindered without EET machinery; (C,D) Azurin mutants with different redox potentials catalyze ATRP at different rates when fit to a first‐order kinetics model. Data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA with multiple comparison correction at α = 0.05. **p < 0.01, ns p > 0.05. Data represent mean ± SD of N = 3.

Based on the correlation between azurin redox potential and S. oneidensis MR‐1 reduction rate (Figure 3C,D), we hypothesized that azurin redox potential may also influence polymerization rates. We tested one azurin mutant with a potential lower than WT‐Az, F114P‐Az, and three with potentials higher than WT‐Az, N47S‐Az, NL‐Az, and SNL‐Az. Interestingly, N47S‐Az did not catalyze ATRP using either S. oneidensis MR‐1 or a chemical reductant as a positive control. While SNL‐Az facilitated ATRP, it did not outperform WT‐Az (Figure 5B,C).

Given the results of N47S‐Az and SNL‐Az polymerizations, we hypothesized that the N47S mutation may be interfering with ATRP. To investigate this, we removed the N47S mutation from SNL‐Az, yielding F114N/M121L‐Az (E°′ = +551 mV) (NL‐Az) [33]. Using NL‐Az, we isolated the effect of increasing the enzyme redox potential while removing the potentially inhibiting N47S mutation. Over the first 6 h, NL‐Az exhibited a significantly faster ATRP rate ((2.2 ± 0.7) × 10− 3 min−1) than other variants (Figure 5C,D), whereas SNL‐Az performed about as well as WT. F114P‐Az was slower at earlier time points, particularly when compared with NL‐Az.

Relative to popular small‐molecule catalysts, such as Cu‐TPMA (E°′ = −9 mV) [2, 4, 61], all azurin mutants in this work have more positive E°′. The overall slower reaction rates are likely due to steric hindrance as azurin is bulkier than Cu‐TPMA and must align in a specific way to accept electrons from S. oneidensis MR‐1 and interact with the growing polymer chain. It is also likely that trends observed in small‐molecule catalysts will not directly transfer to enzymatic catalyst. Although redox rates and ATRP did not follow the same trend as they did with azurin reduction, we demonstrated that multiple variants could influence the polymerization kinetics when driven by an enzyme.

Azurin Increases Current Output in a Bioelectrochemical System

To further characterize interactions between S. oneidensis and azurin, we investigated how S. oneidensis‐azurin electron transfer would affect bioelectricity production in MFCs. Control fuel cells were included to provide a reference for interpreting the effect of azurin. We utilized miniature MFCs to measure changes in current output before and after azurin addition. Before introducing azurin, MFC output voltages (with 150 kW loads) were stable around 0.48 V. After 10 µM WT‐Az addition, we observed a sharp increase to 0.51 V (Δ30 mV). The increase in current stabilized back down after about 20 min (Figure 6A). As expected, we did not observe any difference in current without the addition of S. oneidensis (Figure S7). We collected polarization curves (Figure 6B) 4 h after adding WT‐Az and 20 h after WT‐Az addition. Upon the addition of 10 µM WT‐Az, there was an increase in the polarization curve to higher power densities.

(B) Polarization curves with 10 µM WT‐Az addition (blue) or 0 µM WT‐Az addition (gray) at 4 h postaddition. (C) Voltage profile with NL‐Az addition (10 µM) after MFC stabilization (green) as compared to voltage output without NL‐Az addition (gray). (D) Polarization curves with 10 µM NL‐Az addition (green) or 0 µM NL‐Az addition (gray) at 4 h postaddition. Data represent N = 1 for each condition.

We next examined how azurin variants influenced MFC performance. We decided to use NL‐Az in our bioelectrochemical system based on its higher polymerization rate and more positive redox potential. Immediately after adding 10 µM NL‐Az, we observed an increase from 0.570 V to 0.577 (Δ7.0 mV). The increase in current did not stabilize back to 0.570 after 20 min and remained steady at the new, increased current.

After 20–24 h, there was no significant difference between the steady‐state polarization curves with or without WT‐Az or NL‐Az (Figure S8). The WT‐Az is likely being used as a mediator for a short time, as evidenced by the immediate current change (Figure 6A) and a modest power increase (Figure 6B) after addition, but is likely being degraded over longer time periods according to the diminished differences in the 20 h polarization curves (Figure S8A). NL‐Az, however, produced only a small, sustained voltage increase under the fixed‐load condition and did not lead to a clear improvement in the polarization or power curves. This suggests that NL‐Az may not act as an efficient mediator for electron transfer between S. oneidensis and the anode. The small output voltage shift may instead reflect a weak interfacial effect, such as protein adsorption, double‐layer modification, or a minor shift in the anode potential. We demonstrated that azurin can be used as a redox mediator in MFCs with modest effects, and the behavior depends on azurin variants.

Discussion

Electron transfer from S. oneidensis MR‐1 to azurin was primarily dependent on azurin redox potential. S. oneidensis MR‐1 reduced the lower potential azurin mutant, F114P‐Az, significantly slower compared to its higher potential counterparts. Such a correlation of the reduction rate with azurin redox potential raises the possibility of designing specific variants with predictable reduction rates, or leveraging the wide redox window for multichannel bioelectronic sensors [62]. The reduction rates observed between S. oneidensis and azurin are slower than between their native redox partners. The discrepancy in reduction rates between S. oneidensis‐driven azurin reduction and cytochrome c 551‐driven azurin reduction can be attributed to steric and evolutionary effects. The residue responsible for electron transfer between azurin and cytochrome c 551, His35, is buried within the folded azurin protein, as well as a hydrophobic patch on the azurin protein surface that is implicated in azurin electron transfer [43]. The protein domains assumed to be responsible for electron transfer between MtrC and the final electron acceptors are residues surrounding Hemes 2, 7, and 10 [11]; however, these interactions remain poorly understood. Surrounding these three hemes are generally negatively charged amino acids, which could make close contact between azurin and MtrC more difficult than between azurin and its native electron transfer partner. Despite slower reduction rates, azurin can serve many of the same functions as flavins, such as accelerating biocatalysis.

We showed that electron transfer to WT‐Az enhances known biocatalytic properties of S. oneidensis MR‐1, including azo dye reduction and ATRP reactions. Using azurin, we could harness a previously underutilized Shewanella strain for more efficient RB5 decolorization. In dye decolorization experiments, the extended plateau, or lag time, before decolorization begins did not depend on azurin concentration (Figure S3B) and was instead likely for two reasons. First, the lag may be due to S. oneidensis preferentially utilizing sodium fumarate as an electron acceptor [54]. Excluding fumarate significantly impacted cell health (Figure S9). Additionally, the starting cell densities were low (OD600 = 0.002), to provide time to properly seal the 96‐well plate and transfer it into the plate reader. Thus, the cultures likely needed to grow to a high enough density to begin meaningfully decolorizing RB5 or reducing azurin. Azurin‐aided dye decolorization may create possibilities for similar methods to use one microbial strain with different protein mediators to decolorize dye pollutants.

Polymerization reactions were dependent on the Mtr pathway, but the rate of reaction was influenced by different azurin mutants. Increasing azurin redox potential resulted in faster ATRP rates, as indicated by the twofold ATRP rate of NL‐Az compared with WT‐Az. This aligns with the previous studies that the ATRP rate depends on the catalyst CuI/CuII ratio [63, 64]. However, introducing a N47S mutation resulted in no measurable ATRP activity with either S. oneidensis or chemical reductants, as a positive control, despite this variant's more positive redox potential. Hence, we hypothesize that other factors introduced by N47S mutation played a more significant role in the reaction. For example, mutating Asn to Ser in N47S changes the H‐bonding network near the Cu site. Asn47 forms two H‐bonds to Thr113 both with distances of 2.9 Å, as well as another H‐bond to Asp71 on an adjacent loop with distance of 3.4 Å (PDB: 4AZU [65]). Ser47 only forms one H‐bond to Thr113 with a distance of 3.5 Å (PDB: 3JT2 [33]). The change in protein rigidity might induce changes to the substrate interaction and lead to different activity. Among the mutants tested, NL‐Az exhibited the highest ATRP activity. It reached reaction conversions that were comparable to those polymerized with EET and traditional chemical catalysts after 24 h [2]. In future work, de novo enzymes inspired by both azurin and traditional copper catalysts could be used in conjunction with EET for ATRP or other reactions.

Previous work has demonstrated that the addition of flavin to a bioelectrochemical system results in a sharp decline in voltage output before returning to baseline [66]. Compared to both riboflavin and flavin mononucleotide [66, 67], the change observed with azurin addition (+30 mV) is much smaller than with other small molecules. One likely reason for this is that azurin has not evolved as a redox partner with S. oneidensis, unlike flavins, and thus is likely less primed to function as a redox mediator in an electrical system. Similarly, other common redox mediators, such as 2,7‐AQDS and 2,6‐AQDS, are structurally like flavin mononucleotide and likely undergo similar deprotonation and electron transfer steps. Alternatively, the mechanism of electron transfer from S. oneidensis MR‐1 to azurin to an electrode is likely more complicated and slower. The spike in voltage immediately after azurin addition is opposite of what is observed with other redox mediators, which usually result in an immediate decrease in voltage. This may be due to azurin having a higher redox potential compared to flavin mononucleotide, riboflavin, or other small‐molecule mediators, which are between −0.3 and −0.1 V [68, 69]. This higher potential could make electron transfer from MtrC to azurin more favorable thermodynamically, but if the electron transfer to the anode is slow or indirect, the overall effect on the current may be limited. This is consistent with the observation that NL‐Az produced only a small, yet sustained, voltage shift under fixed‐load operation, but did not substantially alter the polarization and power curves. Although the effect with NL‐Az was sustained, it was much smaller than that with WT‐Az. This could be due to NL‐Az having fewer interactions with S. oneidensis OM proteins, and thus, having fewer opportunities to transfer electrons. This is supported by a slight lagging observed in the initial reduction experiments with NL‐Az (Figure 2B), indicating that there could be some steric hindrance involved with the electron transfer process. However, the current experiment methods are not conducive to determining the rate limiting factor. There are likely two factors contributing to rate limitation. One is the redox potential of azurin used. However, based on the redox potentials of the azurin mutants, we believe that reoxidation of azurin at the electrode is more likely to be limiting than reduction by S. oneidensis MR‐1. Oxidized azurin should be thermodynamically capable of accepting electrons from the bacterial EET pathway, whereas a high‐potential mutant, such as NL‐Az, would be less favorable to reoxidize at the anode within the operating redox window of the MFC. This interpretation is consistent with the minimal improvement in voltage and power observed with NL‐Az. Additionally, there are likely concentration limitations due to the low concentration of azurin. Although lower concentrations of native mediators, such as flavins, influence the output, higher concentrations of azurin may be required as S. oneidensis has not evolved to use the protein as a mediator [17].

Although this work focuses on azurin as an exogenous mediator, we envision future work to recombinantly express and secrete azurin from S. oneidensis, lowering the overall time and energy investment while expanding the available redox range. Though this may present some challenges, azurin has been successfully expressed for whole‐cell use in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 [70]. Whole‐cell expression and secretion may mitigate limitations mentioned above, such as lower azurin concentrations in MFCs. Consistent azurin secretion could increase overall azurin concentration in the fuel cell, increasing the overall output.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the ability of S. oneidensis MR‐1 to transfer electrons to a non‐native enzyme and subsequently applied this to both biocatalysis and bioelectricity generation. Previous studies have focused on electron transfer to small molecules [12, 38, 71]. Redox‐active proteins provide a potential advantage over small molecules due to their tunability, and azurin is a particularly attractive choice given its history in protein electron transfer [32, 33, 40, 72]. As compared to other mediator pathways, such as flavins, azurin biosynthesis is much simpler and can provide a greater redox range [22, 32]. We demonstrated that azurins spanning across 500 mV can be reduced, and used biocatalytically, by S. oneidensis, with the opportunity to explore other azurin cofactors or mutations that could further expand the redox range further. Future work should include utilizing a secretion system, such as a T1SS [73, 74] in order to bypass protein purification. Combining non‐native electron transfer enzyme azurin with S. oneidensis MR‐1, we demonstrated that S. oneidensis MR‐1 EET proteins efficiently reduce and utilize these enzymes, allowing us to leverage the advantages engineered redox proteins provide over small‐molecule mediators [45, 75, 76].
