Superiorly based nasolabial flap for closure of oronasal fistula: a case report
Authors: Ji-Eun Choi, Hyuk-Jun Moon, Jin-Wook Kim, Gyu-Jo Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOronasal fistula (ONF) is an epithelialized tract between the oral and nasal cavities, for which various flaps are used in surgical closure. ONF may lead to regurgitation of food into the nasal cavity, nasal obstruction, halitosis, and other symptoms that contribute to patient discomfort and impaired quality of life. Surgical closure is challenging, occasionally requiring multiple procedures and carrying a risk of recurrence. The nasolabial flap (NLF) is a well-established local flap for soft tissue reconstruction in the orofacial region; superiorly based flaps are typically used for defects of the nasal dorsum, tip, and ala, while inferiorly based flaps are applied to defects of the nasal floor, lips, and buccal mucosa. The NLF provides dependable vascularity and favorable donor-site esthetic outcomes. Although superiorly based NLFs are not commonly used for intraoral reconstruction, this case report suggests that they can serve as a feasible option for ONF closure in carefully selected patients when the flap lies within an appropriate distance from the maxilla.Keywords: Oral fistula, Surgical flaps, Case reports

I. Introduction

Oronasal fistula (ONF) is an epithelialized tract between the oral and nasal cavities, arising from cleft palate, trauma, infection, tumor resection, or iatrogenic causes. Symptoms such as hypernasal speech, fluid leakage, and recurrent tonsillitis and rhinitis may occur, warranting surgical intervention when clinically significant1. Small fistulas that are asymptomatic or well-tolerated may not require treatment. However, larger defects or symptomatic cases necessitate surgical closure. Multiple techniques, including palatal, vomer, buccal mucosal, and tongue flaps, as well as allografts and free flaps, are commonly used for ONF repair2-4.

The nasolabial flap (NLF) is a well-vascularized, axial-pattern local flap widely used in reconstructing defects of the head and neck5. Its earliest description dates back to around 600 A.D. in the Sushruta Samhita for cutaneous reconstruction6. Subsequent contributions by Thiersch (1868), Von Langenbeck (1864), and Esser (1921) expanded its application to intraoral and palatal repair7. The flap remains valued for its reliable blood supply, color compatibility, and minimal donor-site morbidity.

When inferiorly based, the NLF is supplied by the facial artery; when superiorly based, it receives circulation from branches such as the dorsal nasal and infraorbital arteries. Superiorly based flaps are typically employed for reconstruction of the nasal ala, lower eyelid, and cheek. Conversely, inferiorly based flaps are favored for defects of the upper lip, oral commissure, and anterior mouth floor8.

This case reports a rare case of ONF closure using a superiorly based NLF, demonstrating favorable surgical and postoperative outcomes.

II. Case Report

1. Anatomy of the superiorly based nasolabial flap

The NLF functions as an axial-pattern local flap supported by a dependable blood supply. The superiorly based NLF derives its vascular supply primarily from the angular branch of the facial artery, which communicates with the dorsal nasal branch of the ophthalmic artery and facial branches of the infraorbital artery, forming a reliable axial vascular pedicle that maintains flap viability9-11. The facial artery gives rise to the inferior and superior labial arteries, the alar branch, and the lateral nasal artery before terminating as the angular artery. After passing through dense fibrous tissue near the oral commissure, it ascends along the superior border of the upper lip toward the nasal ala and then courses medially within the nasofacial groove to the medial canthus12. At the superior margin of the lacrimal fossa, the ophthalmic artery enters the orbit and branches into the supratrochlear and dorsal nasal arteries. The dorsal nasal artery anastomoses with the angular branch of the facial artery, providing perfusion to the superiorly based NLF13.

2. Case

A 73-year-old male patient presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of Kyungpook National University Dental Hospital with nasal leakage when drinking liquids. He was a non-drinker, non-smoker, and had no significant systemic conditions. Clinical examination revealed an approximately 10-mm ONF in the left maxillary premolar region. The premolars on that side had been extracted over 20 years earlier, and the patient was using a maxillary complete denture. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) confirmed the ONF, showing extensive palatal osteolysis and associated soft-tissue loss.(Fig. 1) An initial attempt at closure using a palatal rotation flap was planned and performed under local anesthesia two weeks after the first visit. However, complete fistula closure could not be achieved during follow-up. To obtain definitive repair, a NLF procedure was planned and performed under general anesthesia three months later.

3. Schema of the technique

The medial border of the flap was aligned along the nasolabial fold, and the final design was fusiform in shape. Doppler ultrasonography was used to confirm blood flow in the dorsal nasal and angular arteries, assisting in flap design. A central marking along the flap outlined the course of the angular branch of the facial artery to ensure its inclusion. A superiorly based flap with a V-shaped base was designed to minimize postoperative lower-eyelid distortion. The skin incision was extended through the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Flap elevation proceeded from superior to inferior in a deep plane beneath the facial muscles, artery, and vein. The angular artery was identified and preserved through blunt dissection; ligation of the superior labial artery was performed when required. A cutaneous flap based on the dorsal nasal artery was elevated and transposed through a tunnel created in the canine space, between the levator labii superioris and zygomaticus muscles12. The portion of the flap passing through the tunnel was de-epithelialized to prevent cyst or epithelial inclusion formation. This step reduces the bulk, facilitates tension-free passage through the canine space, and enhances connective-tissue integration and vascular stability, thereby lowering the risk of fistula or flap failure12. After preparing and exposing the intraoral defect, the flap was inset and sutured using 4-0 nylon. The donor site was irrigated and closed in layers with 4-0 Vicryl and 5-0 nylon sutures.(Fig. 2-4)

Postoperatively, both donor and recipient sites healed uneventfully with no inflammation, flap necrosis, wound dehiscence, or nasal leakage. Although postoperative CBCT was not obtained because the patient declined further imaging, the absence of nasal regurgitation over serial follow-up visits supported stable closure of the ONF. The facial scar remained minimal as it coincided with the nasolabial fold.(Fig. 5, 6)

III. Discussion

The NLF is widely used in soft-tissue reconstruction owing to its accessibility and dependable vascularity. Its primary indications include closure of intraoral defects resulting from oral cavity tumor excision, reconstruction of the floor of mouth and vestibule, and restoration of mucosal continuity in oral submucous fibrosis. It is also used to repair extraoral defects, particularly in the nasal region (including the alar subunit, nasal tip, dorsum, and soft triangle), as well as the cheek and lips. Owing to its robust vascularity, ease of harvest, and minimal donor-site morbidity, the NLF is especially advantageous for small- to moderate-sized defects in older or medically compromised patients for whom free-flap reconstruction may not be feasible14,15. In these indications, the flap usually travels a relatively short distance across the vestibule, and the pedicle lies inferior to the defect, which provides a favorable rotation but can reduce vestibular depth and interfere with subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation. This could be the indication of inferiorly based NLF. By contrast, in the present case, the defect was located in the anterior maxillary premolar region, and a superiorly based design allowed the flap to pivot around the nasofacial groove and reach the fistula without excessive twisting of flap or reducing of the vestibule. Compared with other local options such as buccal fat pad, tongue, or multilayer palatal flaps, the superiorly based NLF offers wellvascularized and sufficient tissue while leaving the palatal mucosa and buccal vestibule largely intact, which is advantageous in an edentulous patient who depends on a maxillary complete denture16,17.

Multiple techniques exist for ONF closure, selected according to the defect’s location, size, and surrounding tissue condition. Local mucosal flaps—such as palatal rotational, buccal advancement, and buccal fat pad flaps—are traditionally favored due to their reliability and proximity. The palatal rotational flap offers robust vascularity, ample keratinized mucosa, and preservation of vestibular anatomy, making it valuable for larger or posterior fistulae and for patients who may later require implant placement. However, its relative bulk and limited mobility can restrict rotation in some instances18,19.

The buccal advancement flap is simple to perform and effective for anterior fistulae; however, it may reduce vestibular depth, potentially complicating prosthetic rehabilitation in those who are edentulous. Conversely, the buccal fat pad flap provides excellent vascularity and has a high success rate with minimal donor-site morbidity; however, its mobility and perfusion can be limited by anatomical distance in some cases18.

For complex or recurrent ONFs, multilayered closure using combined intraoral and intranasal pedicled flaps (such as nasoseptal or lateral nasal wall flaps) offers additional tissue bulk and vascularity, improving the likelihood of successful closure and minimizing the recurrence risk. However, these approaches are technically demanding and may increase operative time and patient morbidity3.

After failure of the initial palatal rotation flap, additional palatal or tongue-based procedures could have resulted in further scarring of the palatal mucosa and impaired denture stability. Therefore we chose an extraoral NLF rather than repeating palatal or tongue flaps, to preserve intraoral soft tissue and vestibular depth for subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation. The NLF provides well-vascularized soft tissue with adequate bulk and permits single-stage reconstruction while preserving the buccal mucosa and vestibule, and the donor-site scar is typically concealed within the nasolabial fold, yielding favorable aesthetic outcomes16,17. In the present case, a superiorly based NLF was selected because the fistula was located relatively anterior and superior in the left maxillary premolar region, and an inferiorly based flap would have required substantial twisting of the pedicle to reach the defect. This unfavorable arc of rotation could have increased tension during mouth opening in the postoperative period and raised the risk of flap necrosis or flap rupture. Given that the superiorly based NLF is perfused through the angular branch of the facial artery and its anastomoses with the dorsal nasal and infraorbital arteries, the axial vascular pedicle lies close to the piriform aperture and anterior maxilla. This vascular anatomy allows a reliable allowing a reliable, relatively short flap to reach premolar-level defects without compromising blood supply or requiring an extended inferiorly based design1.

Inferiorly based NLFs have been widely reported for intraoral reconstruction, whereas superiorly based flaps have mainly been used for nasal and perinasal defects. To the best of our knowledge, the use of a superiorly based NLF specifically for ONF closure has not been previously documented, and this case suggests its feasibility in carefully selected maxillary defects. Despite potential drawbacks, such as possible hair growth at the recipient site and inevitable donor-site scarring, the present case achieved stable, symptom-free closure of the fistula. However, as this is a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized, and further clinical experience and larger case series are required to better define the indications, limitations, and long-term outcomes of superiorly based NLFs for ONF repair.
