Temporal network instability and low-frequency overconnectivity underlie disorders of consciousness in severe brain injury
Authors: Diletta Bartolini, Piergiuseppe Liuzzi, Sara Secci, Bahia Hakiki, Maenia Scarpino, Rachele Burali, Azzurra di Palma, Tanita Toci, Antonello Grippo, Francesca Cecchi, Andrea Frosini, Andrea Mannini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnosing patients with a Prolonged Disorders of Consciousness (pDoC) is challenging due to the inherent limitations of the behavioral assessments recommended by international rehabilitation guidelines. To address this, we applied temporal graph theory to resting-state EEG recordings from 217 patients with severe Acquired Brain Injury (sABI), with and without pDoC. Higher temporal degree centrality in the and bands, as well as a higher burstiness coefficient, were found in the pDoC group. In contrast, lower temporal correlation coefficient was observed in the band in the pDoC group compared to the eMCS group. Furthermore, the analysis of separate frequency bands revealed different behavior of the average topological overlap, with higher values in the eMCS group in the band and higher values in the pDoC group in the band. Thus, repeatability of -connectivity between t-graphlets, the capability of sustaining more periodic-like changes in all-bands connectivity, and the higher integration of temporal network were found to be correlated with consciousness, providing valuable insights on the time-varying neural architecture of pDoC patients.Keywords: Prolonged Disorders of Consciousness, Functional connectivity, Time-varying graph analysis, EEG, Severe Acquired Brain InjuriesSubject terms: Neuroscience, Neurology, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Prolonged Disorders of Consciousness (pDoC) present one of the most formidable challenges in the management of patients suffering from severe Acquired Brain Injury (sABI). Clinically, sABI is characterized by a coma lasting at least 24 hours, a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score lower than 9 (ranging up to 15, with lower scores relative to more severe consciousness impairments1), and/or complex neurological disabilities that require highly specialized neurorehabilitation2. Despite the widespread application of behavioral assessments such as the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) to diagnose disorders of consciousness, these tools are limited by inter-rater variability, fluctuations in arousal, and difficulties in detecting covert cognition3,4. However, pDoC are behaviorally classified in Unresponsive Wakefulness Syndrome (UWS, formerly known as Vegetative State with preserved arousal, yet lacking any observable signs of awareness or purposeful behavior5) and in Minimally Conscious State (MCS, with inconsistent but reproducible signs of awareness6). Emerging Minimally Conscious State (eMCS) represents a transitional phase, where patients begin to demonstrate reliable functional communication or functional object use5–7. This distinction is fundamental: while UWS and MCS (both MCS- and MCS+) are defined by the absence or inconsistency of purposeful responses, eMCS indicates that the patient has reached a critical threshold in recovery. The presence of consistent, goal-directed behaviors in eMCS not only signifies a qualitative change in the level of awareness but also carries significant implications for prognosis and rehabilitation planning.

Over the past decade, graph theory has emerged as a valuable framework for modeling and simulating the structural and functional organization of the brain. Healthy brains typically exhibit a small-world architecture, characterized by high clustering and short path lengths, which optimally balances local specialization and global integration8–12. In contrast, studies applying static graph theory—where node connectivity is assumed to remain constant over time—have demonstrated that patients with sABI exhibit fragmented connectivity, increased segregation, reduced integration, and diminished small-worldness compared to healthy controls, particularly in states of unconsciousness13–15. These network alterations have important clinical implications. Reduced global integration suggests that the brain’s capacity for widespread information sharing is compromised, potentially underlying the impaired consciousness observed in these patients. Concurrently, increased network segregation implies that while local circuits may remain active, their ability to interact with distant regions is limited. Such an imbalance between integration and segregation has been associated with poorer prognoses, as more integrated networks are generally linked to better recovery outcomes16–18. Furthermore, analyses using high-density EEG19–21 and fMRI16,17 have revealed that frequency-specific measures—such as increased integration in high-frequency bands (e.g., or higher) and increased segregation in low-frequency bands (e.g., or lower)—correlate with levels of consciousness, reinforcing the clinical relevance of these network metrics13. However, the assumption of constant connectivity inherent to static graph analyses fails to capture the dynamic fluctuations of brain networks that are critical for understanding multi-scale neural dynamics. Unlike static graphs, Time-Varying Graphs (TVGs) account for temporal fluctuations in network topology and edge weights, offering a more refined representation of brain dynamics22,23. This dynamic perspective reflects the brain’s capacity to reconfigure its network architecture in response to changing cognitive demands. Recent studies have shown that dynamic measures—such as the temporal correlation coefficient (TCC) and temporal degree centrality (TDC)—not only capture transient changes in connectivity but also bear prognostic value, as reduced dynamic integration has been linked to poorer outcomes24–26.

Time-Varying Graph Analysis (TVGA) thus emerges as a direct response to the limitations of static models, with its ability to detect subtle, time-dependent changes providing a promising clinical tool for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and prognostication. While fMRI offers excellent spatial resolution and detailed hemodynamic insights, EEG presents several critical advantages for studying sABI patients. EEG provides superior temporal resolution, enabling the capture of rapid fluctuations in neural activity; it is also more cost-effective, easily applicable at the bedside, and more comfortable for patients, thereby facilitating its use in routine clinical settings. Furthermore, EEG directly measures cortical activity and can be readily combined with dynamic graph analysis techniques to yield novel insights into the time-varying neural architecture underlying consciousness.

In this study, we applied TVGA to resting-state EEG recordings from 217 sABI patients to derive temporal network metrics—such as TCC, TDC, and burstiness—that reflect the dynamic fluctuations in connectivity across different frequency bands. By examining these metrics, our goal is to elucidate the relationship between time-varying network properties and the level of consciousness, by comparing pDoC and eMCS patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

This work is a secondary prospective analysis within the framework of the PRABI study27, a prospective observational study enrolling sABI patients admitted to IRCCS Fondazione Don Carlo Gnocchi Firenze from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2022. Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis of a severe Acquired Brain Injury (sABI), adult age (), and a written informed consent signed by the patient or a legal guardian. Approval from the local Ethical Committee was obtained (Protocol N. ), and enrollment was done following the Helsinki Declaration.

Clinical data collection

For each patient, information concerning demographic (age, sex) and clinical data (etiology, lesion site, time from onset) were included. The etiology of the injury was categorized as traumatic, anoxic, ischemic, or hemorrhagic. Among the clinical scales administered at admission, the consciousness assessment performed by the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) was used for the present analysis18. To reduce the risk of misdiagnosis due to arousal fluctuations, the CRS-R assessments were repeated five times within 7 consecutive days and the best score was retained for the analysis3,4. Also, the CRS-R scale was assessed in absence of drug sedation by skilled operators at admission to the IRU28.

EEG data collection and pre-processing

All patients underwent a standard-EEG during the first week after the study entry. EEG recordings were performed using a digital machine (Gal NT, EBNeuro) and an EEG prewired head cap, with 19 electrodes’ (10–20 International Standard System Fp1-Fp2-F7-F8-F3-F4-C3-C4-T3-T4-P3-P4-T5-T6-O1-O2-Fz-Cz-Pz) at a sample rate of 128 Hz. Closed-eyes recordings included an initial 10-minutes resting-state part and 20-minutes of randomly administered stimuli (used in clinical practice to assess reactivity, variability, and in general, cortical responsiveness of the patient). Only the resting-state portion of the recording was retained for further analysis and visually inspected. The patient was retained if at least 5 consecutive minutes of clean resting-state EEG were present. The starting and ending points of such 5-minutes section were manually selected and used to crop the EEG recording. EEG unipolar recordings were re-referenced to the grand average and high-pass filtered using the MNE library29 (zero-phase FIR filter at 1 Hz with a Hamming’s window as suggested by the PREP pipeline30). Infinite-impulse-response notch (50 Hz) filtering was then applied to further remove power line disturbance. Then, the first five seconds of the recording were discarded to eliminate filtering-related transitory artifacts. Channels with still excessive or uncorrelated noise were labeled following PREP criteria30 and interpolated by means of spherical splines31 using the MNE library. Lastly, the extended InfoMax Independent Component Analysis (ICA) method was applied. Within this step, a pre-whitening Principal Components Analysis (PCA) was applied, decreasing dimensionality from to with set to 15. Whitened data entered the ICA algorithm and Independent Components (ICs) were automatically labeled using the MNE-ICALabel library32. In particular, a neural-network classifier trained on crowd-sourced data, based on the Matlab ICLabel implementation, was used to retain only brain-related ICs labeled with a confidence higher than 80%. Lastly, channel-level data was reconstructed from the ICs space.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported in terms of medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and in terms of counts and percentages for categorical variables. The outcome was set to the presence of a pDoC classified as UWS or MCS versus eMCS patients. Given that disorders of consciousness (DoC) exist on a continuum, it is critical to clarify our operational definitions. Patients in eMCS exhibit consistent functional communication or object use, indicative of a qualitatively higher level of recovery as well as residual or recovering consciousness and a notably better prognosis, opposed to the more profoundly impaired pDoC patients. Graphs’ metrics were compared between pDoC and eMCS patients, using Mann-Whitney (or t-) tests (conditioned to Shapiro-Wilk normality results). Also, differences in metrics across electrode configurations were assessed via Repeated Measures ANOVA (or Friedman’s tests) (conditioned to Shapiro-Wilk normality results). Post-hoc analysis, in the form of pair-wise comparisons, were performed using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank post-hoc test, with correction for multiple comparisons enforced with the Bonferroni technique. All tests were performed on SPSS30 and significance level was set to 0.05 for all tests. Code and data can be obtained upon request to the corresponding author.

Discussion

In this study, we explored how the temporal dynamics of brain networks, assessed through EEG-based TVGA, relate to consciousness states in patients recovering from sABI. Using an inexpensive and non-invasive setup, we constructed temporal graph-like structures and quantified topological overlap, node centrality, and burst-like fluctuations in network connectivity. Furthermore, we examine these metrics within specific frequency bands (, , and ), providing insight into the temporal variability of different oscillatory activities in the brain. Our results reveal that eMCS patients exhibited significantly higher TCC in the band compared to pDoC patients. The TCC measures the similarity of a network’s topology across successive time windows, capturing the stability of functional connectivity over time. This finding aligns with previous studies that demonstrate that recovery of consciousness is associated with the reemergence of -band activity in patients recovering from sABI25,27,34, under anesthesia38,39, and during sleep40. The higher band TCC observed in patients with eMCS suggests that the increased temporal stability of high-frequency connectivity may reflect the preserved mechanisms of recovery of consciousness. Considering that brain dynamic in epilepsy have greater temporal variability in network structure41, suggests that TCC may not only estimate lagged topological similarity but also serve as a proxy for frequency content, with higher TCC values reflecting more structured, high-frequency brain activity in conscious states. We also observed that AvgTO and TDC were frequency-dependent markers of consciousness state. Specifically, higher integration at low frequencies () and lower integration at high frequencies () were associated with the pDoC group, consistent with prior static graph theory analyses13. This finding reinforces the concept that unconscious states are characterized by disrupted long-range cortico-thalamic connectivity, leading to an over-reliance on low-frequency synchronization. Furthermore, the higher TDC in the and bands in pDoC patients aligns with the ABCD model of consciousness proposed by Forgacs et al.42,43. Thus, the observed elevated connectivity in the and bands can be seen as a compensatory response to the impairment of higher-frequency long-range connectivity. A fundamental result of our study is the analysis of burstiness in brain network dynamics of sABI patients. Bursty temporal patterns, characterized by irregular fluctuations in connectivity, have been identified as a fundamental feature of various natural systems, including brain activity26. Previous studies have suggested that burst-like connectivity changes can be detected in resting-state fMRI and EEG data44,45, but their role in distinguishing different states of consciousness remains largely unexplored. In our study, we found that while TVGA networks were non-bursty () in all patients, pDoC patients exhibited more irregular and less periodic fluctuations in connectivity. This suggests that the unconscious brain operates in a less stable, more erratic state, potentially reflecting impaired information integration and reduced functional efficiency. This result is consistent with theories positing that the conscious brain maintains a balance between flexibility and stability, while unconscious states exhibit either excessive rigidity (e.g., deep coma, anesthesia) or excessive stochasticity (e.g., epileptic seizures)46. From a methodological standpoint, we demonstrated that TVGA-based consciousness assessment remains robust even when reducing the number of EEG electrodes from 19 to 6. This finding is paramaount for clinical translation, as it suggests that low-density EEG configurations can still capture meaningful consciousness-related brain dynamics, making this approach more feasible for bedside applications. While high-density EEG (128–256 channels) may improve spatial resolution, our results indicate that clinically practical setups retain diagnostic value. One potential limitation is the choice of thresholding strategies for constructing adjacency matrices. Rather than applying absolute thresholds, we used proportional connectivity thresholds, ensuring that network densities remained comparable across subjects. While this approach promotes consistency in network sparsity and mitigates inter-subject variability, it inherently introduces a degree of arbitrariness in the selection of a fixed density level. Future studies should explore alternative thresholding methods, particularly adaptive or data-driven approaches. Examples include the use of minimum spanning tree (MST) algorithms, which preserve the global topology of the network without requiring arbitrary cut-offs. Such strategies may enhance the sensitivity and robustness of time-varying graph analysis (TVGA), thereby improving the interpretability and generalizability of findings in disorders of consciousness. Furthermore, our cohort includes patients with a pDoC after all etiologies, differently associated with specific mechanisms of brain damage. These differences may affect EEG performance, as injuries involving deeper brain structures or the brainstem can lead to variability in cortical signal capture. While the inclusion of multiple etiologies increases the generalizability of our findings, it also introduces variability that might confound the spatiotemporal dynamics observed in our analyses. Future studies with larger cohorts should address these issues by conducting stratified analyses to determine the extent to which EEG connectivity differences are influenced by the underlying etiology. Currently, consciousness diagnosis in pDoC patients relies primarily on behavioral assessments using the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R)28, which is limited by inter-rater variability, vigilance fluctuations3,4, sensory/motor impairments47, and the challenge of detecting covert cognition42,48. The latest guidelines emphasize the need for multimodal assessments, incorporating at least one neurophysiological or neuroimaging measure to complement behavioral evaluation48,49. Although our dichotomization reduces the nuanced continuum of consciousness, it mirrors current clinical practices that recognize emerging awareness as a distinct state with its own neural characteristics. This classification enables us to directly compare the spatiotemporal dynamics associated with emerging recovery versus persistent impairment. Moreover, by comparing pDoC with eMCS rather than with healthy controls, we emphasize that the observed differences are more closely related to the severity of consciousness impairment than to the absence of structural lesions. In this context, TVGA provides a promising new framework for capturing the spatiotemporal neural mechanisms underlying consciousness states. By incorporating both temporal and spectral features, TVGA could offer a more comprehensive assessment of consciousness than traditional static graph approaches. Future studies with larger, prospectively assessed cohorts will be essential to validate these findings and further explore the potential of TVGA in clinical decision-making and within specific cohorts of pDoC patients.
