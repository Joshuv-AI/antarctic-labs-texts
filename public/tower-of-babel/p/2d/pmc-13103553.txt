Measures of resting cartilage structure and composition in runners with and without knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Michael A. Hunt, Michaela C.M. Khan, Natasha M. Krowchuk, Anthony A. Gatti, Alexander Rauscher, Jean-Francois Esculier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare measures of resting cartilage structure and composition in experienced older runners with and without knee osteoarthritis.Design47 runners (mean (sd) age = 58.0 (8.4) years) with knee osteoarthritis, as well as 27 runners (mean (sd) age = 52.2 (9.7) years) with no radiographic knee osteoarthritis or knee pain participated. Magnetic resonance imaging was used to measure cartilage structure and composition and calculate: volume and thickness, as well as T2 and T1ρ relaxation times, for eight regions of interest. Between-group comparisons were examined using analysis of covariance with body mass, years of self-reported running experience, and average weekly running volume over the three months prior to testing included as covariates.ResultsFew between-group differences were observed. Runners with knee osteoarthritis exhibited less patellar cartilage thickness (mean difference [95% confidence limits]: −0.3 [-0.4, −0.1] mm) and volume (−408 [-736, −79] mm3), but thicker lateral posterior femur cartilage (0.2 [0.0, 0.3] mm), than runners without knee osteoarthritis. Further, runners with knee osteoarthritis generally exhibited longer relaxation times in all regions of interest, though statistical significance was only observed in the lateral posterior femur (T2 relaxation time: 1.8 [0.2, 3.3] ms) and medial posterior femur (T1ρ relaxation time: 2.6 [0.8, 4.4] ms) regions.ConclusionsOur findings do not support the notion of widespread cartilage degeneration in the knees of experienced older runners with knee osteoarthritis. Longitudinal research is necessary to examine the longer-term impacts of running on general knee health in runners with and without knee osteoarthritis.

Introduction

The popularity of recreational running has risen substantially in the past decade, with many runners now continuing into their later years. While running is an inexpensive method to meet physical activity guidelines, the appropriateness of running in certain populations remains unclear. A high number of healthcare professionals and members of the general public believe that running should be avoided by individuals with knee osteoarthritis (OA), as they believe it may exacerbate pain and accelerate disease progression [1].

However, there is a growing body of evidence that has begun to question the accuracy of these perceptions, with some research now suggesting that running with knee OA may be appropriate. Data from systematic reviews and meta-analyses show that running is not detrimental to lower limb cartilage health in runners without knee OA [2,3]. Further, epidemiological research indicates lower OA rates in recreational runners compared to sedentary individuals [4]. While promising, there is a paucity of available information on cartilage health in runners with knee OA. Comparing cartilage in runners with and without knee OA would provide clinicians and runners with valuable evidence to inform decisions on the appropriateness of running after receiving a diagnosis of OA.

The purpose of this brief report was to compare resting structural and compositional measures of knee cartilage in runners with and without knee OA. We hypothesized that runners with knee OA would present with thinner cartilage than runners without knee OA, in addition to showing signs of collagen disorganization as evidenced by higher cartilage relaxation times.

Methods

This was a secondary analysis from a larger study with the objective to examine changes in cartilage outcomes after acute and repeated exposure to running in experienced masters-aged (typically ≥35 years of age) runners with and without knee OA (NCT04325334). Data presented in the current paper were from the Baseline assessment prior to any running intervention. The study was approved by the institutional Clinical Research Ethics Board, and all participants provided written, informed consent.

Participants

Community-dwelling volunteers were recruited to the larger study between February 2020 and July 2024 via print and social media, as well as through relationships with sports medicine clinics and local running groups. Recreational runners who were at least 40 years of age and ran at least twice weekly totalling 10 km or more, and had done so for 12 months, were eligible. Exclusion criteria relevant to the current analysis included: (i) any history of traumatic knee injury (defined as requiring surgical intervention or continuous treatment for more than one month) in the study knee; (ii) presence of an inflammatory arthritic condition; (iii) standard contraindications to MRI; and, (iv) any history of surgery in either knee.

Participants completed a questionnaire to determine the presence of knee pain, and underwent radiographic assessment of both knees. Radiographic images were analyzed to classify knee OA disease status according to the Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) classification scale [5] by two trained assessors (MAH and NMK). Participants were categorized into one of two groups based on the combination of pain and radiographic data: 1) the tibiofemoral OA (TFOA) group exhibited a minimum KL grade 2 in at least one knee and reported pain on most days of the previous three months in the same knee; 2) the Control group exhibited a KL grade 0 in at least one knee and were pain free for the preceding 12 months in that knee. In situations where an individual had two eligible knees, the study limb was the most symptomatic (TFOA group) or was randomly chosen (Control group).

Data collection

To ensure consistency and to minimize any effect of pre-loading, participants were instructed to avoid running and other moderate-high impact activities in the day prior to testing. Further, all imaging was conducted in the morning, and participants were seated for approximately 30 min prior to MRI scanning. Data analysis

Each image file was assigned a unique identifier that ensured images were analyzed without knowledge of group allocation. Anatomical scans were used to automatically segment bone and cartilage into femoral, patellar, and tibial regions using a previously validated deep learning algorithm [6]. Femoral cartilage was sub-divided into anterior and medial and lateral weight-bearing and posterior regions, while tibial cartilage was sub-divided into medial and lateral regions using established region definitions [7], and algorithms [8]. All segmentations and image registrations were quality controlled by an experienced, blinded assessor (AAG), and manual corrections were performed as appropriate. Outcomes of interest included: cartilage thickness and volume, as well as T2 and T1ρ relaxation times.

Cartilage thickness was computed using open-source software (https://github.com/gattia/pymskt) [8,9] for each vertex of the bone surfaces as the Euclidean distance from the bone to the articular surface of the overlying cartilage, and averaged across all vertices of each region of interest. Cartilage volume for each region of interest was calculated as the product of the number of voxels assigned to cartilage and the reconstructed volume size for each voxel.

To enable quantitative MRI analyses, the anatomical scan was rigidly registered to the first echo time (TE) of the T2 and first time of spin lock (TSL) of the T1ρ mapping sequences using a previously validated registration algorithm [10]. The quantitative MRI maps were fit voxelwise to the segmentation using a nonlinear least squares fitting algorithm [11]. T2 maps were generated by dropping the first echo to reduce bias due to the stimulated echo effect, and by fitting a monoexponential decay to signal intensity as a function of TE. For T1ρ maps, TSL images were registered to the first TSL using elastix [12], followed by fitting a monoexponential decay to signal intensity as a function of TSL. For both T2 and T1ρ, voxels with poor fit (R2 < 0.8) were excluded from the analysis, while voxels with T2 > 100 ms and T1ρ > 130 ms were considered erroneous [13] and also excluded.

Statistical analysis

Between-group differences for all outcome measures and regions of interest were assessed using analysis of covariance, with covariates being body mass (kg), self-reported running experience (years), and average weekly running volume (km) over the three months prior to testing. We also performed exploratory analyses by adding sex as an additional covariate or comparing groups based on KL grade. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used, and models were fit unadjusted for multiple comparisons. All assumptions for analysis of covariance held, and no transformation of data or use of alternative tests was employed. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Seventy-four individuals participated, comprised of 47 in the TFOA group (29 females; 37 with KL grade 2, 10 with KL grade 3; mean [95%CI] age 58.0 [55.6, 60.5] years, body mass index 23.2 [22.4, 24.0] kg/m2, running experience 18.6 [14.6, 22.7] years, and weekly running volume 24.3 [20.1, 28.6] km) and 27 in the Control group (19 females; all with KL grade 0; mean [95%CI] age 52.2 [48.4, 56.0] years, body mass index 22.5 [21.4, 23.6] kg/m2, running experience 18.2 [12.3, 24.1] years, and weekly running volume 27.9 [18.1, 37.6] km). On average, scanning commenced at a mean [95%CI] time of 08:07 [08:02, 08:11] for the TFOA group, and 08:10 [07:50, 08:31] for the Control group.

Discussion

We have presented, for the first time, structural and compositional measures of resting cartilage in experienced masters-aged runners with and without knee OA. While some trends were observed, the vast majority of comparisons resulted in small mean differences, suggesting that knee cartilage structure and composition in our sample was generally similar between groups. In addition to providing normative cartilage health data for older runners with and without knee OA, our findings challenge the long-held belief that running with knee OA should be avoided [1]. Indeed, our data suggest that runners with knee OA do not exhibit widescale knee cartilage impairments compared to runners without OA, and provide evidence towards promoting the appropriateness of running continuance in the presence of knee OA.

In contrast to our hypothesis, no meaningful differences were observed in any measure of tibiofemoral cartilage morphology. We expected to observe less cartilage in the TFOA group based on the fact that they had signs and symptoms consistent with knee osteoarthritis, while the Control group did not. However, while groups were dichotomized based on structural changes on radiography, the KL scale prioritizes osteophyte formation over joint space narrowing when determining the presence of grade 2 changes [5], and the full extent of thickness and volume throughout the regions of interest may have been difficult to ascertain through radiography. The findings of our exploratory subgroup analysis comparing outcomes across KL grades may be explained by the insensitivity of 2D radiographs in differentiating more global and three-dimensional (e.g. volume) measures of cartilage structure.

Though the findings were encouraging from a clinical perspective, some themes were observed. Those with TFOA had less patellar cartilage, measured as both mean thickness and total volume. This finding has relevance to runners with knee OA for a few reasons. First, concurrent osteoarthritic changes in the tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints are common, and those with multi-compartment involvement exhibit worse symptoms and function than those with unicompartmental involvement [14]. Second, symptoms of patellofemoral pain are common in runners across the lifespan and range of running experience. It is possible that our sample of runners with knee OA experienced a history of patellofemoral pain and joint overload, leading to cartilage loss. However, we did not collect such data, and it remains unknown if monitoring and addressing such symptoms would help reduce the rate of patellar cartilage loss. Despite being preliminary, and limited by the cross-sectional study design, our findings suggest that assessment of mechanical and clinical attributes in both the patellofemoral and tibiofemoral joints should be considered in runners with knee OA.

When comparing relaxation times, between-group differences were mostly statistically non-significant, and though any differences were of small magnitude, values were generally longer in the TFOA group in all regions of interest. It is believed that T2 and T1ρ relaxation times provide an indication of the collagen and proteoglycan structure of cartilage, respectively, and that longer relaxation times represent a more disorganized cartilage matrix that may lead to further cartilage degeneration [15]. While there are many measures of overall cartilage health, and the clinical relevance of the small relaxation time differences observed in the present study (generally less than 2 ms) is unknown, our findings do highlight the need for careful monitoring of symptoms in runners with knee OA as part of general clinical management of the disease to facilitate continued and safe running before any detrimental effects are triggered.

Our findings are not without limitations. First, all of our runners had substantial running experience, and despite the presence of clinical OA in the TFOA group, these findings cannot necessarily be generalized to novice runners. Second, the cross-sectional nature of our study does not permit any assessment of cause and effect, nor any prediction of future cartilage degradation. Third, we excluded runners with a history of knee injury or surgery to eliminate the likely confounding effect of those factors on cartilage health, and thus better examine the role of running. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to runners with previous injuries or surgeries. Finally, our sample may have been affected by survivorship bias, whereby only runners with healthier cartilage and less knee symptoms enrolled, while those with more cartilage degradation who may have stopped running prior to recruitment were not sampled. While this possibility cannot be discounted, and the risk of running on knee OA development or progression in experienced masters-aged runners cannot be negated, our findings indicate that it is possible to practice long-term running with a clinical diagnosis of knee OA and exhibit cartilage outcomes similar to a sample of runners without OA.
