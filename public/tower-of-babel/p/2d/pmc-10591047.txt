Artificial intelligence education: An evidence-based medicine approach for consumers, translators, and developers
Authors: Faye Yu Ci Ng, Arun James Thirunavukarasu, Haoran Cheng, Ting Fang Tan, Laura Gutierrez, Yanyan Lan, Jasmine Chiat Ling Ong, Yap Seng Chong, Kee Yuan Ngiam, Dean Ho, Tien Yin Wong, Kenneth Kwek, Finale Doshi-Velez, Catherine Lucey, Thomas Coffman, Daniel Shu Wei Ting
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryCurrent and future healthcare professionals are generally not trained to cope with the proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) technology in healthcare. To design a curriculum that caters to variable baseline knowledge and skills, clinicians may be conceptualized as “consumers”, “translators”, or “developers”. The changes required of medical education because of AI innovation are linked to those brought about by evidence-based medicine (EBM). We outline a core curriculum for AI education of future consumers, translators, and developers, emphasizing the links between AI and EBM, with suggestions for how teaching may be integrated into existing curricula. We consider the key barriers to implementation of AI in the medical curriculum: time, resources, variable interest, and knowledge retention. By improving AI literacy rates and fostering a translator- and developer-enriched workforce, innovation may be accelerated for the benefit of patients and practitioners.

Introduction: AI as the next frontier in healthcare innovation

The world of healthcare is changing. Artificial intelligence (AI), defined as the ability of computers to perceive, process, and utilize large quantities of information, has permeated every sector of the economy and is beginning to manifest changes in clinical work. The myriad of opportunities provided by AI range across clinical diagnosis, management, and prognosis; education of students, doctors, and allied healthcare professionals; as well as improvements and novel approaches in research.1,2,3,4 Diagnostic AI algorithms have exhibited performance levels matching or surpassing the performance of clinical experts, AI risk calculators are improving patient stratification and triage, and AI surveillance models have been used to detect and monitor disease outbreaks using X (formerly Twitter) data.5,6,7 Large language models such as GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 are emerging with the ability to replicate clinician responses to text and images, AlphaFold has revolutionized drug discovery by accurately and automatically predicting protein structures, and speech recognition applications are automating clinical documentation.8,9,10,11,12

Serious issues preclude deployment of AI applications in many contexts. These include inaccuracy, bias, ethical conundrums, unclear or unenforced governance, and uninterpretable or unexplainable model workings.13,14,15,16 AI research is frequently poorly reported, many validation studies exhibit flawed methodology, and marketing often overstates the evidenced benefit for AI applications for patients.6,17,18,19 Despite these limitations, AI applications nevertheless seem likely to proliferate in healthcare settings and provide increasing levels of support to patients and practitioners.13,20 Clinicians are likely to engage with a wide variety of AI tools throughout their career.

To ensure that AI fulfills its potential in the clinical domain, medical students—tomorrow’s doctors—must have the required understanding to use new technology appropriately, improving healthcare provision for patients and practitioners without compromising safety.21,22 Future clinicians require “AI literacy”: an understanding of the technical basis, strengths, and limitations of AI.23 Moreover, future innovators require specific education to help mitigate the limitations of AI discussed above, and all clinicians should be trained to mitigate limitations and risks of AI to ensure that their patients receive optimal care as technology is deployed at scale. AI literacy may be cultivated from the very beginning of training with incorporation of AI content into healthcare professional curricula. Most medical students agree that more AI education is necessary and desirable.24 However, there is no consensus about what should be taught and how teaching should be delivered, with no standardized curriculum.25,26

To conceptualize the aims of AI education, clinicians may be stratified according to their level of engagement with AI (Figure 1). Consumers comprise the majority of the clinical workforce; they are not involved in AI research and development but responsible for selecting and using new tools appropriately to serve their patients. Translators are responsible for bringing state-of-the-art computational methods to the bedside: validating clinical AI tools and engineering systems to allow consumers to leverage tools effectively. Developers are technically competent AI scholars engaged in the computer science underlying AI; while many developers will be computer scientists, individuals with medical expertise (e.g., physician-computer scientists) are necessary because they may use their domain-specific knowledge to guide development in clinically useful directions. As time passes, technology progresses, and familiarity with AI increases, we envisage that a greater proportion of healthcare professionals will advance into the translator and developer categories and expect the level of AI knowledge and skills to increase in all three categories. In this article, we outline an initial curriculum for students based on the continuum formed by consumers, translators, and developers, suited to the present day. We first discuss the current state of AI education at medical schools, describing the progress made with specific reference to case studies around the world. We then outline our curriculum, which aims to teach at an appropriate level to prepare clinicians without causing undue stress and placing unreasonable demands for time during the already strained period of clinical training.27 An emphasis is placed on integration with existing elements of medical education: AI should be a complementary supplement to clinical practice, education, and research rather than replacing other subjects. In particular, AI education initiatives dovetail with work to inculcate evidence-based medicine (EBM) in clinicians—critical appraisal and adoption of AI applications operate through the same principles as with drugs and other medical or surgical interventions.28 Finally, we discuss how the knowledge and skills base of the clinical workforce may change in future years as AI education is actively implemented and technology continues to permeate more aspects of life inside and outside of the clinical workplace.

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

Google Scholar, MEDLINE (via PubMed), and preprint servers (medRxiv and arxiv) were searched using the following terms: “artificial intelligence”, “AI”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “evidence based medicine”, “EBM”, “medicine”, “clinical”, “healthcare”, “education”, “curriculum”, “course”, “teacher”, and “teaching”. Papers published in English, French, or Mandarin Chinese were reviewed. Final inclusion depended on originality and relevance to the subject matter of this review in the consensus opinion of the team of authors. For examples of AI teaching at medical school, examples were selected from around the world to provide an illustration of variation in provision.

The current status of AI education and literacy in medicine

Currently, no formalized AI curriculum has been implemented in the majority of medical schools around the world.29 Consequently, knowledge and skill levels in the clinical workforce are poor; many students report never having heard of machine learning, even greater proportions report little to no AI teaching at their medical school, and students with AI knowledge have often learned in spite of, rather than thanks to, their medical course.30,31,32,33 Most students would like to learn more about machine learning but find it difficult to understand potential applications, interpret results from research articles, or understand the statistical methods used in AI research.34 These observations are consistent across other healthcare programs, with allied healthcare students similarly poorly informed about AI and how it relates to their disciplines.35 Most of those students represent consumers, whose knowledge and skills bases are currently very low because of a lack of teaching around the world.

Translators and developers also have difficulty developing their skills. Every one of eleven doctors with AI expertise interviewed in a focus group study reported minimal mentorship, which meant they had to forge their own path to develop their skills and seek opportunities.36 Nine of eleven doctors believed that AI should be introduced into medical school teaching immediately and that exposure to the field should be followed up with opportunities to explore genuine interest in the future.36 Basic AI literacy is likely sufficient for the majority of healthcare professionals—consumers—while extracurricular or optional curricular supplements may help cultivate translators and developers who may drive the field forward as they progress in their careers.23

For medical schools that have started implementing AI in their curricula, a variety of approaches have been utilized. These range on one axis, according to depth, from basic introductory sessions suited to consumers to intensive research projects for future translators and developers and vary on another axis according to the time allocated to AI education. An overview of medical school AI teaching programs

The progress made with incorporating EBM into medical education provides a roadmap for metrics of successful integration as well as challenges that must be overcome. This, in turn, provides more or less feasible aims for incorporating new concepts into medical education. EBM was only coined as a term in the 1980s but is now taught around the world in a variety of forms.45,46,47 While EBM aptitude in the medical workforce is growing, students and clinicians still exhibit deficiencies in their knowledge and skills, particularly relating to statistical analysis and critical appraisal.48,49 The Fresno test provides a validated tools for assessing EBM aptitude and has been used to demonstrate the effectiveness of EBM teaching at medical school; successful courses result in students exhibiting sustained increases in Fresno test scores.50 Successful courses require commitment from expert teachers, administration, and well-designed learning outcomes and teaching structure.50 No standardized EBM curriculum is universally accepted, and further work is required to ensure that all doctors develop sufficient EBM skills to interact effectively with the literature base and contribute to advancing clinical practice. In four decades, significant progress has been made, but some challenges remain.

The integration of emerging technologies such as AI into medical education is now recommended by the National Academy of Medicine and the American Medical Association (AMA).5,22 However, whether for lack of resources, time, or know-how, most training programs have yet to make this transition. While no validated tests of AI aptitude have been deployed, survey studies show that students and healthcare professionals around the world lack the knowledge and skills to interact with cutting-edge changes in digital health.30,31,34 By providing an AI curriculum linked to statistics and EBM, we hope that faster progress can be made to adapt clinical training to the rapidly changing environment, leveraging the progress made in EBM education over the last 40 years.

A curriculum for AI education at medical school

First and foremost, the AI curriculum aims to confer the basic AI literacy required for future consumers—the vast majority of the clinical workforce—to effectively use AI systems and communicate to patients how AI affects decision-making. While critical appraisal skills are useful to assess whether AI systems should or should not be integrated into care, consumers do not require statistical understanding beyond what is required for EBM; links between AI and EBM may be leveraged to improve teaching in both domains at medical school. Translators and developers require greater expertise, requiring additional teaching and opportunities for practical application of skills. Teaching may be structured in a variety of ways to suit faculties with different ethea and philosophies, incorporating lectures, group-based projects, or self-directed learning. Four pillars underpin the aims of AI teaching, building on the underlying clinical and scientific knowledge and skills students develop during their courses (Figure 2). Consumers, translators, and developers are considered along a continuum, with corresponding levels of conceptual complexity and detail. Consumers

Consumers require sufficient knowledge and skills to select and use AI tools effectively in clinical settings. This requires EBM skills as with conventional interventions and procedures.51 For diagnostic applications, this requires an understanding of confusion matrices and how to calculate and interpret sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and negative and positive predictive values. Consumers should be able to describe the important salient points following from high or low sensitivity and specificity and use or avoid applications accordingly depending on clinical requirements. Other clinical interventions may be appraised based on their effect on mortality or morbidity or other appropriate surrogate outcomes. Moreover, to decide which systems are worth using and in which situations use is effective, consumers should have an appreciation of evidence. At the most basic level, consumers should understand the levels of evidence and trial endpoints to gauge whether an AI intervention is justified—weighing high-quality randomized control trials measuring clinical endpoints more highly than retrospective observational data using surrogate endpoints, for instance. Teaching may extend to more advanced critical appraisal skills, evaluating study design, reporting quality, and statistical analysis for consumers to contribute to implementation, governance, and policymaking.51,52

To mitigate limitations of AI applications, consumers also require some domain-specific knowledge. A basic grounding in the principles and process of machine learning will enable consumers to develop an understanding of where pitfalls in an AI system may lie. Consumers should be taught the importance of understanding whether the AI was trained to produce outputs for particular demographics, whether bias is likely to lead to an error or unfair decision, and when not to use or to overrule AI decisions or diagnoses. While active research is underway to help automate these processes, clinicians retain responsibility for their patients and must actively consider whether use of an AI system contributes to providing better healthcare.13,53 A particularly important issue is biased performance; for example, because of training datasets lacking representation of minority groups or AI systems failing to incorporate important collateral information in their algorithms.20,54 Enhanced education is unlikely to lead to consumers adopting the technical roles of developers and translators (governed by regulatory bodies) to identify bias. However, consumers can help mitigate bias and other issues to ensure that AI innovation serves all patients by appraising the evidence base for a given intervention; models developed and validated without appreciation of minorities may entail greater risks for those neglected groups, and consumers can act appropriately to safeguard their wellbeing.20 Ultimately, consumers may be limited by the quality of published evidence, time to consider whether to use AI applications, and alternatives to AI-based care available. Good education is nevertheless essential to enable clinicians to make the most of available resources.

Patients should be involved in clinical decision-making where possible, and this requires accurate and clear communication from their care providers.55 Consumers should therefore have the necessary understanding to explain AI and machine learning, performance and model outputs, as well as limitations such as bias and inaccuracy. Developers and translators are responsible for optimizing new applications and interventions to lower the bar to integrating new applications, but training consumers to improve their baseline knowledge as AI proliferates in healthcare should nevertheless be a high priority. In short, care providers should have the necessary knowledge and skills to use AI applications where they will improve patient outcomes, avoid blind adherence to model recommendations that can be compromised, and explain processes to patients to involve them maximally in their care.

Translators

Translators are a bridge between developers, who often have no clinical expertise, and consumers with a limited understanding of AI. As such, they require a more in-depth understanding of data structures and AI schemata such as deep learning. Translators should appreciate how structured and unstructured data can be leveraged with machine learning and how neural networks develop optimal representation of data through deep learning.56 They should be able to differentiate between supervised and unsupervised training using labeled and unlabeled data, respectively, and define and apply different machine learning tasks: regression, classification, clustering, anomaly detection, etc. The pitfalls of machine learning (such as overfitting and underfitting) should also be engaged with, as well as strategies to mitigate these risks, such as good data curation and cross-validation. Translators may develop basic coding skills in R or Python or use automated machine learning (autoML) for hands-on experience to develop a practical understanding of the capabilities and limitations of ML.57

Translators may participate actively in validation projects and therefore require knowledge of how to produce and interpret receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, precision-recall curves (PRCs), and F1 scores to evaluate performance. Understanding the concept of threshold values and the benefits and pitfalls of adjusting thresholds to fulfill a defined clinical purpose is also essential. These skills may benefit from basic coding ability, and translators may make use of existing libraries and applications to interrogate models for performance and explainability analyses.58 To contribute to model training, translators should be educated on ethical data collection, data management, information governance, and patient consent. Because data quality and quantity are often the limiting factor for developing useful medical AI applications, this is one of the most impactful way for translators to contribute to innovation. Moreover, to ensure that projects are guided in a useful direction and that validated models can be implemented in clinical settings with consumers at the helm, translators should be introduced to the regulatory requirements for AI applications in their home countries or regions. Translators with clinical and AI expertise, able to design, lead, and interpret validation studies, are the best-placed stakeholders to make decisions regarding which applications should be available to clinicians; a key aim should be to provide a menu of robustly validated AI tools with as much detail as possible regarding appropriate use cases to help consumers make optimal evidence-based decisions.

For effective appraisal, translators should have a strong understanding of study design, statistical analysis, and risk of bias. The benefits and limitations of randomized control studies, cohort, studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional surveys should be emphasized alongside the requirement to test interventions in pragmatic trials to demonstrate effectiveness rather than efficacy.59 Understanding basic statistical tests corresponding to differences in means, contingency tables, correlations, and Kaplan-Meier plots is essential to conduct or appraise studies testing an AI intervention in clinical settings. Translators should be aware of frameworks to assist with assessment of study quality and risk of bias, which may temper conclusions. On one hand, all of these statistical and study design skills are necessary for any clinician scientist developing and testing interventions. The key here is to ensure that these translators connect those ideas to validation and appraisal needs associated with AI models, which are usually more complex. Critical appraisal is especially important in the AI sector, where poor study design, reporting, and overinterpretation of results are pervasive.60,61,62 All of these skills converge in systematic review, which may allow translators to gain hands-on experience as well as effectively summarize evidence to shape clinical guidelines or policymaking.

Developers

Developers have more extensive computer science knowledge and skills, which supplements their clinical and scientific background. The ability to code in Python or other languages enables them to drive primary AI research from an earlier stage than clinical implementation. This requires a firm grasp of ML architectures, including convolutional neural networks, feedforward neural networks, recurrent neural networks, generative adversarial networks, and transformers.56 More importantly, developers should have the ability to quickly understand and apply new architectures as they continue to be developed; recently, transformers have revolutionized language models and required many developers to pivot their attention accordingly.12 Developers may also be comfortable leveraging adjacent and emerging technologies to ideate and implement novel applications for healthcare; current examples include federated learning for secure inter-departmental training using patient data.63,64

Developers comprise the vanguard of AI development and must be trained to take on this role with appropriate responsibility. Wider implications for the economy, society, and safety must be considered carefully when developing new technologies because responsibility for releases currently lies with developers.12 Rigorous testing is required to ensure that models are appropriate for deployment, and developers are best placed to conduct stress tests to identify technical weaknesses or failure points; relevant techniques include adversarial attacks and out-of-distribution testing.12,65,66 Active research is required to mitigate bias and fairness concerns, particularly where limitations in training data impede improving existing architectures and training protocols.20 While most clinical implemented AI models feature no explainability, and existing explainability techniques exhibit significant limitations, developers may work on interpretable engineering and explainable AI to ameliorate the black box issue, which can threaten safety and hinder acceptance by patients and practitioners.67,68 Areas requiring research are manifold, and clinician developers are uniquely placed to work toward areas of importance for patients and practitioners because of their commitment to patient interests, knowledge and experience relating to healthcare, and AI expertise.

With sufficient knowledge and skills, developers may undertake critical appraisal of primary computer science research as reported in preprints and conference papers, perhaps evaluating reproducibility using provided source code. Moreover, to demonstrate generalizability, developers may apply new models and techniques to different cohorts, helping to justify future deployment and delineate boundaries within which applications may perform to an acceptable standard. Developers may leverage the newest techniques and technologies to produce novel clinical applications, driving innovation forward. As clinicians, developers should also have a foundational understanding of clinical practice and research to assist translators and consumers implement new applications and actively contribute to governance and policymaking to ensure patients and practitioners are safeguarded.

Delivering AI teaching for healthcare trainees

Healthcare professionals operate along a continuum as consumers, translators, or developers in medical AI, but their distribution skews heavily toward the consumer side; most current clinicians to not have the aptitude or interest to become AI experts. While the number of translators and developers may increase with time, particularly as AI applications proliferate in healthcare, curricula should generally cater primarily to consumers to prepare them for the changing work environment.69 Optional courses, modules, and sessions could enable future translators and developers to develop their interest and skills, and the most interested students may seek out their own opportunities for student-selected components and elective periods. Depending on the aims and resources of medical schools and aptitude and interest of their students, courses may select different elements of our AI curriculum to teach (Figure 2) and may have different objectives for graduation.

Many teaching formats may be employed to foster AI literacy, and medical schools are already taking a wide variety of approaches. Case-based learning is common and well accepted at medical school and could be used to teaching consumer-level AI via relevant, concrete scenarios.70 For developers and translators, project-based learning may be especially useful for developing new skills, and autoML would enable students with no coding experience to gain hands-on experience sooner.57,71 In addition to improving theoretical understanding, project-based learning facilitates insight into systems design, allowing students to learn what is required to develop AI applications as well as how they are implemented and later governed.72 Didactic teaching in large lectures or small groups may also be employed, requiring tutors with appropriate expertise. As AI literacy becomes democratized in the medical workforce, the pool of expertise to draw on will broaden with compounding benefit: students learning from peer tutors’ first-hand experience, tutors benefitting from reiteration of their knowledge, and faculties benefitting from lower demand for expert teachers.73 Finally, self-directed learning is an important way to develop competence—working at individuals’ required pace—and may be necessary to complete an AI project or study for an examination. Self-study may be augmented by emerging large language models, which exhibit a growing ability to provide accurate, tailored information in response to biomedical, coding, and ML queries.12,74,75 GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 currently exhibit state-of-the-art performance in medical tasks and are already being integrated into study tools in other subjects.12,76 GPT-4 is also being integrated into platforms such as Github, thanks to its remarkable ability to solve coding problems.12 Teachers may also leverage large language models to appraise work and produce teaching material at unprecedented scale, although frequent fact fabrication necessitates close oversight to avoid errors.12,74

Interprofessional collaboration

As healthcare becomes increasingly multidisciplinary, effective interprofessional practice patterns become more important to ensure optimal patient care is delivered.77 Physicians, allied health professionals, pharmacists, administrators, data scientists, and digital experts must all work together to provide AI-empowered healthcare. Interprofessional education, where students on different courses engage with each other to foster effective collaboration, is therefore becoming commonplace within medical curricula.78 In the context of AI education, elements of the interprofessional education approach may be incorporated to encourage a team-based approach in data bias recognition and bioethical and liability considerations as future medical teams adopt data-driven, AI-augmented approaches to clinical decision-making.79

Challenges to integration of AI in medical education

The barriers to implementation must be considered carefully because they have to be addressed directly to enable progress to be made. As discussed above, coverage of EBM education remains patchy and inconsistent after four decades; dealing with difficulties impeding implementation is the only way to promote faster, universal change.

Time

Medical training is a strenuous time with a packed curriculum, likened by some to “drinking from a fire hose.” Interventions must seek not to increase this strain. Some have argued to replace preclinical science content with the fundaments of EBM; they argue that decision-making should rely more on the academic evidence base—ideally high quality randomized control trials—than mechanistic reasoning.59 Others have suggested fitting additional AI modules as core or extracurricular courses, discussed in greater depth above. Fortunately, especially when it comes to thinking critically about study design, data collection and processing, and evaluation, there are large overlaps with EBM that ease the burden of adding AI as a new subject area. There may be many approaches that achieve the desirable end of fostering AI literacy, and the optimal approach may depend on independent medical schools’ aims, directions, and resources.

Resources

For translators and developers, AI development requires computational resources; for high-performance models, these may extend to expensive hardware, such as graphic-processing units (GPUs). While cloud-based autoML platforms ameliorate this requirement, most require payment, which would incur significant costs to cover whole cohorts of students. However, given the emphasis on conceptual learning aimed at consumers, we would suggest that physical resources are not a significant barrier to entry. Consumers are not expected to become AI developers and should therefore not be forced into developing skills using packages, platforms, and techniques that may rapidly become obsolete in the rapidly developing field.

However, teaching does require tutors with AI expertise, and this may be a limiting factor in many medical schools. Few academics and clinicians are AI translators or developers possessing both clinical expertise and familiarity with AI.80 A core curriculum may at the very least demonstrate what knowledge is required for teachers and may enable schools to identify potential teachers with the required knowledge and skills to teach who may not necessarily consider themselves AI specialists. As AI literacy improves in the workforce, peer teaching may become a larger contributory factor to education—as with clinical skills developed on placements—with senior students and qualified professionals able to impart their knowledge about how to use and appraise AI systems to their junior colleagues.

Variable baseline aptitude and interest

Students vary widely in their background knowledge and skills with respect to AI. More problematically, students also vary widely in their interest in the subject. AI teaching must cater to these interests, ensuring that sufficient knowledge is imparted without limiting students who may wish to become translators and developers while ensuring that disinterested students do not fall outside their depth and let content wash over them. Because understanding AI requires a degree of statistical understanding and familiarity with EBM, these difficulties reiterate the need to ensure that concurrent EBM teaching continues to improve. Most students will have received no prior teaching on either EBM or AI because individuals generally commence their courses straight after finishing school, and most graduate students come from backgrounds in the traditional basic sciences.81

Effort and learning may be promoted by well-designed assessments requiring students to understand and apply knowledge rather than regurgitate facts. Assessments may be formative (low-stakes guides for future self-directed development) or summative (higher-stakes tests that must be passed to demonstrate competence).82 Engagement may also be encouraged by emphasizing the clinical utility of AI; most students are not familiar with medical applications of AI, which could lead to misapprehension about the relevance of AI teaching at medical school. The extracurricular opportunities detailed above would provide opportunities for more interested students to explore the field in more depth and perhaps become future translators and developers. Medical recruitment may also be refocused to value students with experience in AI and biostatistics who are most likely to become translators and developers. This may be especially applicable in graduate entry programs where applicants with a computational background are likely to have useful skills that could be applied in clinical contexts.

Knowledge retention

Another difficulty is knowledge retention by students from these courses because of the limited opportunities to apply concepts of AI in healthcare while still in training. There needs to be continuity of AI education across the years of medical school and also between training and work as a qualified professional. Implementing a spiral curriculum, where concepts are revisited at many points during training, promotes inculcation of knowledge and skills through progressive learning and reinforcement.83 A spiral curriculum also has the benefits of allowing students to learn simpler concepts first and is already widely applied in medicine.83,84 The spiral structure is also amenable to adding further tiers for especially interested students to apply themselves, supporting future translators and developers in addition to consumers.85

Whatever the structure employed to deliver teaching, AI content should be clinically contextualized to engage students and ensure that time and effort directly improve their clinical practice. Examples of datasets, models, and studies should be clinical wherever possible. Although AI has had greater impacts in other fields, there are sufficient examples within medicine to teach student doctors, with hundreds of models already approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Conclusion: Visualizing an AI-enriched workforce

As AI applications continue to proliferate in healthcare, the need to educate clinicians to effectively appraise and use these tools is becoming more pressing. Supplementation of existing curricula with AI may prove to be the most realistic way to prepare consumers for the changing landscape of digital health, and this may be facilitated by linking AI teaching to related concepts in EBM. Although EBM teaching has not been standardized or perfected, its progress made over 40 years may enable AI teaching to be implemented quickly and with minimal friction. Two distinct but complimentary changes may be observed—as has already occurred with EBM—as AI education improves. First, knowledge and skills will increase across the workforce. Because consumers form the greatest proportion of the clinical workforce, improving the minimum standard of training will serve to have the greatest sector-wide effect in terms of improving AI literacy. Second, as more healthcare professionals are exposed to AI, more may develop and foster an interest in AI and take on the role of translators and developers. A workforce enriched by these more skilled and interested groups will accelerate progress by improving critical appraisal, driving better quality of research, and optimizing allocation of resources. Opportunities must be provided by academia and industry to allow these translators and developers to flourish.
