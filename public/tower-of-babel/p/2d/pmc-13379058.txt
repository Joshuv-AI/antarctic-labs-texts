Dopamine transporter gene (SLC6A3) polymorphism rs2963257 and chronic pain susceptibility in a Japanese population
Authors: Keiko Yamada, Daisuke Nishizawa, Hideko Arita, Kazuo Hanaoka, Choku Yajima, Jitsu Kato, Setsuro Ogawa, Ayako Hiranuma, Junko Hasegawa, Yuko Ebata, Kyoko Nakayama, Masako Iseki, Kazutaka Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.A case–control study identifies the SLC6A3 rs2963257 A allele as a significant risk factor for chronic pain susceptibility in a Japanese population.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain represents a major global health challenge, significantly impairing quality of life for a vast number of patients and imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens.2,18,39,59 Current therapeutic strategies for chronic pain, encompassing pharmacological, physical, and psychological interventions, exhibit considerable interindividual variability in efficacy, highlighting the lack of a universally effective treatment approach.31,58 This variability is thought to stem from a complex interplay of environmental, psychosocial, and genetic factors, with an individual's genetic background playing a crucial role.11,13,22,60,61

In recent years, the concept of personalized medicine, which tailors prevention and treatment strategies according to individual genetic information, lifestyle, and environmental factors, has gained traction, particularly in fields like oncology. Similarly, the field of pain management is moving toward “precision pain medicine,” aiming to identify patients at risk, predict responses to specific therapies, and optimize treatment strategies by targeting underlying pathological processes.12,30,41,47 A critical step toward achieving precision pain medicine involves identifying reliable genetic markers associated with pain susceptibility, chronification risk, or differential responses to analgesics.9,29,34,38

The neurotransmitter dopamine is well recognized for its complex role not only in reward, motivation, and motor control but also in pain perception, emotional aspects of pain, and the descending inhibitory pain pathways.27,48,49,57 Its influence extends through various brain circuits, including mesolimbic and mesocortical pathways, and regions like the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), where dysregulated dopaminergic signaling has been observed in chronic pain states.1,26,42,48 Indeed, genetic variations affecting different components of the dopaminergic system have been implicated in individual differences in pain experiences.14,20,52,54 Specifically, molecules regulating dopaminergic neurotransmission, including enzymes involved in dopamine synthesis and metabolism such as tyrosine hydroxylase (TH),7 catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT),55 the dopamine transporter (DAT), which is responsible for dopamine reuptake (encoded by solute carrier family 6 member 3 [SLC6A3]),25,50,52 and the various dopamine receptors (DRD1–DRD5),19,36,56 are considered key players in pain modulation. Genetic polymorphisms within the genes encoding these crucial proteins (TH, SLC6A3, DRD1–DRD5, and COMT) could potentially influence the function of the dopaminergic system and consequently contribute to susceptibility to or the pathophysiology of chronic pain.14,19,36,52,55

However, the association between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the genes regulating dopaminergic function and susceptibility to chronic pain remains incompletely understood, particularly across diverse populations. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between SNPs in a panel of key dopamine-related genes (TH, SLC6A3, DRD1–DRD5, and COMT) and chronic pain in a Japanese population using a case–control design. Elucidating the role of the dopaminergic system in the genetic background of chronic pain may deepen our understanding of its pathophysiology and contribute to the future development of more effective and personalized treatment strategies.12,14,21,52,60

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This study used a case–control design to investigate the association between SNPs in selected dopamine-related genes and chronic pain susceptibility in a Japanese population. The participant cohort was derived from previous studies.3,33

2.1.1. Cases

To ensure standardized phenotyping, patients with chronic pain were included if they had been experiencing persistent pain for more than 3 months and were diagnosed by board-certified pain clinicians at the participating pain centers. We aimed to investigate the genetic susceptibility to “chronic pain” as a broad phenotype, encompassing a variety of intractable conditions routinely managed in pain clinics, such as postherpetic neuralgia, spinal canal stenosis, and chronic postoperative pain (Table 1). Patients with severe coexisting complications that could severely alter pain perception were excluded.

The patient group consisted of 191 individuals suffering from chronic pain. These patients were recruited at the pain clinics of 3 hospitals in Tokyo, Japan. This sample size reflects the cohort described by Nishizawa et al. (2021), derived after applying quality control (QC) filters to an initial set of 194 patients.33 Patients with severe coexisting medical complications were excluded. Detailed clinical characteristics of the original patient cohort have been described previously.33

2.1.2. Controls

The control group comprised 282 healthy adult volunteers recruited from the Kanto area of Japan. Healthy controls were rigorously defined and recruited. The inclusion criteria for controls were individuals with no current or history of any chronic pain conditions and no history of severe neurological or psychiatric disorders. These individuals were confirmed to be free from any major diseases, including chronic pain conditions, as detailed in prior reports.33 All participants were apparently of Japanese ethnicity.

2.2. DNA extraction and genotyping data source

Genomic DNA had been previously extracted from peripheral whole-blood or oral mucosa samples using standard laboratory procedures and stored in TE buffer (10 mM tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane–hydrogen chloride, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 8.0).33

Whole-genome genotyping data for these participants were available from previous studies.33 Briefly, genotyping was performed using the Infinium assay (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with either the HumanOmni1-Quad v1.0, HumanOmniExpress-12 v1.1 (for patients), or HumanOmniExpressExome-8 v1.2 BeadChip (for controls) on an iScan system.33 For the present study, genotype data for specific candidate genes were extracted from these existing whole-genome datasets.

2.3. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms selection for dopaminergic genes

From the final quality-controlled dataset comprising 445,723 SNPs, this investigation targeted variants located within or near key genes involved in the dopaminergic neurotransmission pathway. Specifically, we focused on SNPs within the genomic regions (including coding sequences, introns, and flanking regulatory regions) of the following genes known to play crucial roles in dopamine synthesis, transport, reception, and metabolism: TH, SLC6A3 (encoding the DAT), DRD1–DRD5, and COMT. Relevant SNPs residing within these defined gene loci were extracted from the main dataset for detailed association analysis with chronic pain phenotypes.

2.4. Quality control

Stringent QC measures, similar to those described by Nishizawa et al. (2021) for case–control analyses, were applied to the extracted SNP data.33 Regarding sample QC, individuals with genotype call rates <95% had already been excluded, resulting in a final sample of 191 cases and 282 controls. Regarding SNP QC, SNPs were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: genotype call frequency <95% in the combined case–control dataset; significant deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) in either the patient or control group (P ≤ 0.001), where HWE assesses whether the observed genotype frequencies in controls match expected frequencies under random mating; minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1 in the combined dataset, where MAF represents the frequency of the less common allele (this threshold focuses the analysis on common genetic variations); and potential platform-specific genotyping errors (according to differential missingness or frequency differences between genotyping platforms used in the original studies, although stringent filtering was already applied in Nishizawa et al. 202133).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the chronic pain group and healthy controls. Age distributions were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and sex distributions (men vs women) were compared using the χ2 test, excluding participants of unknown sex.

Initial data processing and QC for SNPs were performed using PLINK software (v1.07).37 Subsequent association analyses for genotype and allele frequencies were conducted using STATA Statistical Software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX), with P-values calculated by chi-squared tests or Fisher exact tests as appropriate.

To assess inheritance patterns, we tested genetic models, using modified Poisson regression to estimate prevalence ratios and their 95% confidence intervals; P-values were derived using the Wald χ2 test. For this analysis, we defined the genetic models assuming the minor allele (or the allele showing a risk trend) as the risk allele: for example, for SLC6A3 rs2963257, the dominant model compared carriers of at least one A allele (GA+AA) with GG homozygotes, and the recessive model compared AA homozygotes with carriers of at least one G allele (GA+GG). The Cochran–Armitage test was used to assess the additive trend. All regression and trend analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Given the candidate gene approach, correction for multiple testing was performed using the Bonferroni method, adjusting the significance threshold according to the total number of SNPs successfully tested within the selected dopamine-related gene regions. A corrected P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

2.6. Ethical considerations

The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024, and received approval from the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Juntendo University (approval number, E22-0094-H03) and the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science (approval number, 23-32, 25-4). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant before enrollment.

3. Results

3.1. Participants

This study initially included 194 patients with chronic pain and 282 healthy controls (previously reported).3,33,43 Following the QC procedures detailed in the Methods section, 3 patients were excluded on the basis of the sample call rate (<95%). Consequently, the final analysis involved 191 patients with chronic pain (case group) and 282 healthy controls (control group). All participants were of Japanese ethnicity. Cases were recruited from pain clinics at multiple medical institutions, and controls were volunteers recruited from the Kanto area. Detailed demographic and clinical background information for the participants (eg, age, sex, pain diagnoses) is available in Table 1 and in previous reports by Nishizawa et al. (2021)33 and Aoki et al. (2023).3

3.2. Descriptive data and genotyping overview

Initial data derived from whole-genome genotyping underwent QC filtering, yielding a final set of 445,723 high-quality SNPs available for case–control analysis. For the present study, we extracted SNPs located within the genomic regions (gene body and ±30 kb flanking regions) of 8 candidate genes related to the dopamine pathway: TH, SLC6A3, DRD1–DRD5, and COMT. After applying further gene-specific QC to SNPs within these candidate regions (call frequency ≥95%, HWE P ≥ 0.001 in both patients and controls, MAF ≥0.1), a total of 184 SNPs were included in the final association analysis (top part of Table 2).

3.3. Main results: association of dopamine-related single-nucleotide polymorphisms with chronic pain

We assessed the association between the 184 filtered SNPs and susceptibility to chronic pain. Among all SNPs tested within the 8 candidate gene regions, the most robust association signal was observed for rs2963257, a variant located not within an exon but rather in the flanking region of the SLC6A3 gene (encoding the DAT) on chromosome 5 (bottom part of Table 2).

3.3.1. Genotypic association

A statistically significant difference was found in the genotype distribution of rs2963257 between the chronic pain patient group and the healthy control group (Fisher exact test, P = 0.00026). This association remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing across the 19 SNPs analyzed within the SLC6A3 gene regions (eg, Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold: P = 0.0026 [0.05/19]). 3.3.2. Inheritance models

To better understand the nature of this association, we analyzed the data under different genetic inheritance models. The strongest association was observed under the dominant model for the A allele (GA+AA vs GG), which showed a notable association with chronic pain (P = 0.0017). Crucially, this finding remained statistically significant after applying Bonferroni correction for the 19 SNPs tested in SLC6A3 (P < 0.0026). This result suggests that carrying at least one A allele (or specifically, the absence of the protective GG genotype) is associated with increased susceptibility to chronic pain.

In contrast, no significant associations were found under the recessive model for the A allele (AA vs GG+GA; P = 0.22) or the additive model (trend test, P = 0.59) (Table 3). Allele frequencies did not significantly differ between the groups (P = 0.63). As shown in Table 2, the G allele is the minor allele (MAF = 0.350 in controls), whereas the A allele is the major allele.

No other SNPs within the SLC6A3 gene region, nor within the other 7 investigated dopamine-related genes (TH, DRD1–DRD5, and COMT), showed a statistically significant association with chronic pain in this cohort after correcting for multiple testing (supplemental digital content, Tables S1–S8, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A427).

3.4. Other analyses

To further characterize the association signal at the SLC6A3 locus, we evaluated the pattern of linkage disequilibrium (LD) among the analyzed SNPs. The regional LD plot (Fig. 1) shows that rs2963257 is not part of a strong LD block and exhibits only weak LD with adjacent SNPs, such as rs2963259 (r2 = 0.06). This result suggests that the association signal at rs2963257 is relatively distinct from the other common variants analyzed in this region.

4. Discussion

In this case–control investigation, conducted within a Japanese cohort, we explored potential associations between genetic variations in 8 key dopaminergic pathway genes (TH, SLC6A3, DRD1–DRD5, and COMT) and susceptibility to chronic pain. Our principal finding is a statistically significant link between the non-coding SNP rs2963257, located in the flanking region of the SLC6A3 gene encoding the DAT, and chronic pain status. This association was most prominent under a dominant model for the A allele, which remained statistically significant after applying strict Bonferroni correction (P = 0.0017 < 0.0026). This finding suggests that carrying the A allele may increase susceptibility to chronic pain, or conversely, that the GG genotype confers a protective effect. In contrast, our analysis did not reveal significant associations for other SNPs within SLC6A3 or the remaining 7 candidate genes after adjusting for multiple comparisons. To date, no prior genome-wide association studies have reported an association between rs2963257 and pain-related phenotypes in public databases such as the GWAS Catalog,44 PheWeb,5 or Open Targets Genetics.15 Therefore, this study may represent the first report implicating this SNP in chronic pain susceptibility.

4.1. Role of SLC6A3 and the dopaminergic system in chronic pain

As shown in Table 1, our case cohort included multiple chronic pain etiologies. Although this heterogeneity might be considered a limitation, it also supports the hypothesis that the dopaminergic system modulates generalized pain susceptibility—such as central sensitization and the affective dimensions of pain—regardless of the peripheral nociceptive or neuropathic origin. Although previous studies linked SLC6A3 variants to postoperative or experimental pain phenotypes, including persistent breast pain after surgery,24 preoperative pain expression in elderly patients undergoing orthopedic surgery,8 and experimental cold pain responses in healthy volunteers,51 these reports examined previously recognized SLC6A3 polymorphisms, including common SNPs and the well-known 3′-UTR 40-bp VNTR,53 rather than rs2963257.

Mechanistically, how this specific noncoding polymorphism contributes to chronic pain warrants consideration. Although rs2963257 is located in the flanking region of the SLC6A3 gene and does not alter the encoded dopamine transporter sequence, noncoding variants can exert important regulatory effects on gene expression and function.23,28 In our query of the GTEx Portal (v8), based on the GTEx v8 resource described by the GTEx Consortium,17 rs2963257 showed a cis-eQTL association with LINC01511 in several tissues, including the brain cortex, whereas no significant eQTL association was identified directly with SLC6A3. LINC01511 is a long noncoding RNA gene located at 5p15.33 in close genomic proximity to SLC6A3 (NCBI Gene ID: 100506791 and 6531, respectively). Accumulating evidence indicates that cis-acting lncRNAs can regulate adjacent genes at the transcriptional and epigenetic levels,16,45 and lncRNAs can also influence RNA splicing.40

We, therefore, hypothesize that the rs2963257-associated alteration in LINC01511 expression may affect the expression or splicing of SLC6A3 (DAT) in the central nervous system. Such a mechanism is biologically plausible because dopaminergic pathways contribute to descending pain modulation,27 and impaired descending inhibitory control is thought to facilitate pain chronification.35 However, because we found no direct functional evidence that rs2963257 itself regulates SLC6A3, and our LD analysis did not place rs2963257 within a strong LD block, further functional studies, such as allele-specific reporter assays and CRISPR-based perturbation of LINC01511, are needed to determine whether rs2963257 is itself causal or instead tags an unidentified functional variant.

4.2. Considerations regarding other dopaminergic genes

Our study noted some SNPs exhibiting nominal significance (uncorrected P < 0.05), but these failed to meet the threshold after correction for multiple testing. Dopamine receptors, particularly D2 and D3, have been implicated in pain transmission and opioid analgesia.4,6,10,57 Our current findings suggest that common variants within these receptor genes do not exert a major independent effect on the overall risk for chronic pain within our cohort. However, it remains possible that associations could emerge in studies with larger sample sizes, investigations focused on specific pain subtypes, or analyses considering interactions with other genetic or environmental factors.

4.3. Functional annotation of the rs2963257 single-nucleotide polymorphisms

Functional annotation using GTEx (v8) revealed that rs2963257 is a significant cis-eQTL for LINC01511 in several tissues, including the cortex and frontal cortex. Although no significant association with SLC6A3 expression was observed, the proximity of rs2963257 to SLC6A3 and the role of dopaminergic pathways in pain modulation support further exploration. Although no prior studies directly link LINC01511 to chronic pain, this lncRNA has been implicated in neuroinflammatory and synaptic processes. These findings suggest that rs2963257 may modulate pain susceptibility through novel regulatory mechanisms, potentially involving lncRNA-mediated pathways.

4.4. Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this investigation include the use of a previously well-characterized case–control cohort; the focus on a specific ethnic group (Japanese), thereby contributing to the diversity of genetic pain research; and the application of standardized QC and analytical procedures.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the sample size (191 cases, 282 controls) might have limited statistical power to detect associations with moderate effect sizes or those involving less frequent genotypes, such as under a recessive model. Second, our case cohort consisted of heterogeneous chronic pain etiologies, including both neuropathic and nociceptive pain. Because of the limited sample size, we were unable to perform rigorous subgroup analyses stratified by specific pain origins. Although we observed a shared genetic susceptibility via the dopaminergic pathway, future studies with larger cohorts are necessary to determine whether the rs2963257 polymorphism has differential effects on specific pain subtypes. Third, our candidate gene approach inherently limits the scope of discovery to the preselected genes, potentially missing associations in other genomic regions. Fourth, to the best of our knowledge, the association between the SLC6A3 rs2963257 polymorphism and chronic pain susceptibility has not yet been investigated in other Asian populations or other ethnicities. Because genetic architectures, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and minor allele frequencies can vary significantly across different populations, the significant association observed in this study might be specific to the Japanese population. Therefore, further replication studies involving multiethnic cohorts are essential to determine whether our findings are globally generalizable. Fifth, the biological function of the identified SNP, rs2963257, remains uncharacterized; the observed association does not definitively prove causality. Finally, the present study lacks replication in an independent cohort, and genotype imputation was not conducted. The analyses were based solely on directly genotyped SNPs, which restricts our ability to capture untyped variants and comprehensively evaluate regional linkage disequilibrium. Future directions include genotype imputation using general haplotype reference panels (eg, the 1000 Genomes East Asian panel) or population-specific reference panels (eg, the ToMMo 8.3K JPN panel32,46), followed by fine-mapping and replication studies in independent multicenter cohorts. In addition, environmental factors, psychosocial factors (eg, depression and anxiety), and comorbidities were not controlled in our analysis because of retrospective data collection, which could influence the observed associations. Future prospective studies integrating comprehensive psychosocial assessments are warranted.

4.5. Clinical and research implications and future directions

Acknowledging these limitations, our results nonetheless suggest a potential link between SLC6A3 polymorphism and susceptibility to chronic pain, underscoring the importance of the dopaminergic system in pain modulation. If the association with rs2963257 is replicated in independent cohorts and its functional mechanism is elucidated, it could potentially inform future risk stratification efforts or contribute to the development of personalized therapeutic strategies targeting DAT function.

Priorities for future research include the following:(1) Replication studies in larger, independent Japanese cohorts and diverse ethnic populations.(2) Functional investigations (eg, in vitro assays, expression quantitative trait locus analyses) to determine the biological impact of rs2963257 (or tightly linked variants) on SLC6A3 expression or DAT protein function.(3) Stratified analyses to explore associations within specific pain subtypes (eg, neuropathic vs nociceptive pain).(4) Interaction analyses examining the interplay between SLC6A3 variants and other genetic or environmental factors (eg, psychosocial stress, lifestyle).(5) Prospective studies to evaluate whether this SNP predicts pain chronification or treatment response.

5. Conclusion

This study provides evidence that the SNP rs2963257 near the DAT gene (SLC6A3) is associated with chronic pain susceptibility in the studied Japanese population. Specifically, the association under a dominant model for the A allele was statistically significant (P = 0.0017) even after strict Bonferroni correction. This result suggests that carrying the A allele may increase susceptibility to chronic pain, or conversely, that the GG genotype confers a protective effect. No significant associations were identified for other dopamine-related genes after rigorous correction for multiple testing. These findings lend support to the involvement of the dopaminergic transporter pathway in chronic pain pathophysiology. Although further validation and functional exploration are warranted, this work highlights a promising candidate for advancing personalized approaches in pain medicine.
