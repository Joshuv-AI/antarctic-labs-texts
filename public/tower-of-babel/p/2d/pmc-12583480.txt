Dismantlable adhesive using coordination polymer/metal organic framework
Authors: Mitsutaro Umehara, Izuru Miyazaki, Akitoshi Suzumura, Shinya Moribe, Yumi Masuoka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explored the use of meltable metal-organic frameworks and coordination polymers (MOFs/CPs) as dismantlable adhesives that can be readily disassembled by immersion in water at room temperature. Three MOF/CP materials were synthesized, melted, and applied to copper substrates to evaluate their shear bonding strength. Among these, [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](H2Im)2 (ZPI) exhibited the highest shear strength (~ 10 MPa), indicating its suitability as a high-strength adhesive. Moreover, ZPI-bonded joints showed a marked decrease in bond strength after just one hour of immersion in water at room temperature, allowing for manual disassembly. These findings highlight the potential of ZPI as a dismantling adhesive that functions under mild conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22297-4.Keywords: Adhesive, Coordination polymer, Metal organic framework, MOF, Dismantlable adhesiveSubject terms: Chemical engineering, Organometallic chemistry, Coordination polymers, Metal-organic frameworks

Introduction

In recent years, the trend of multimaterialization —the integration of materials with various properties—has rapidly advanced for a wide range of applications, including automobiles, electronic devices, and building materials1–3. By leveraging the complementary strengths of each material, this approach provides significant benefits, such as weight reduction, enhanced functionality, and lower production costs. For example, combining a metal and a resin enables the realization of both rigidity and light weight.

The ability to bond dissimilar materials is essential for the construction of multimaterial structures, and the role of adhesive technology is becoming increasingly important. However, once these dissimilar materials are integrated into a composite structure, separating and dismantling them into individual components after product use becomes extremely challenging, posing a significant barrier to recycling.

Currently, these composites are mechanically crushed during the recycling process, followed by chemical treatments such as combustion or dissolution to extract the constituent elements. However, these processes are costly, and it is difficult to recover suitable materials for reuse. Therefore, recycling is economically viable only for metals that are relatively easy to extract, such as copper, and for expensive metals, such as gold. Most other materials are treated as slag.

Considering these issues, dismantlable adhesives that can be easily disassembled by reducing their adhesive strength through external stimuli have recently gained attention4–6. Dismantlable adhesives are designed to lose their adhesive function through physical or chemical reactions triggered by external stimuli, such as heat7, light8, mechanical stress9, acids10, or water11, thereby degrading the adhesive structure.

However, to further improve the practical applicability, simpler and safer disassembly methods are required. Specifically, adhesives that can be disassembled simply by immersion in water at room temperature, without the need for heating or solvents, are highly desirable. Room-temperature water-dismantlable bonding technology holds great potential for a wide range of applications4,6,11, including reducing the physical and chemical impact of disassembly, temporary fixation of electronic and optical components, and medical and dental applications. Conventional technologies include starch-based adhesives12,13, urethane-based adhesives that can be disassembled by pH changes14,15, and biocompatible adhesives developed for medical and dental use16,17; however, none of these offer sufficient bonding strength exceeding 10 MPa. Recently, adhesives that achieve high bonding strength (12 MPa) along with water-induced disassembly using eutectogels have been reported11, and this field is gaining increasing attention. Such water-dismantling adhesives represent promising technologies for future material design from the perspective of reducing energy consumption, lowering environmental impacts, and simplifying recycling processes.

In this study, we focused on metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and coordination polymers (CPs) for adhesion applications. MOFs/CPs have a unique structure in which organic ligands and metal nodes are assembled in a variety of ways through coordination bonds and have attracted attention in recent years owing to their structural diversity and high functionality18–21. These materials have been extensively studied for various applications, including gas adsorption and separation22, catalysis23,24, energy materials25,26, and sensing27. However, attempts to utilize MOFs/CPs as ‘bonding materials,’ i.e., materials for bonding or joining objects, have been limited. Although reports on MOFs/CPs as functional materials are widely available, applications as bonding materials have received less attention. Establishing a bonding method using MOFs/CPs might enable the development of a novel functional bonding technique, such as atmospheric reactive bonding.

As pioneering research in the field of MOF/CP bonding, Zhao et al. reported the first application of cyano-bridged coordination polymers (cyano-bridged CPs) as bonding materials in 201728. This bonding mechanism involves the face-to-face stacking of plate-like CP molecules through Van Der Waals forces, resulting in a higher bonding strength (2 MPa) than that of conventional point-contact-type MOF/CP materials. However, the bonding strength remains insufficient for practical applications, as the particles in these materials remain in a solid state during bond formation. Hence, the bonding layer consists of solid aggregates, which result in gaps between the molecules at the substrate interface. These voids inevitably reduce the bonding strength.

Considering this background, we hypothesized that wetting the substrate surface is crucial for achieving strong bonding strength. Therefore, we explored a high-strength bond formation strategy for MOF/CP, in which bonding is formed in a liquid state. Using this approach, we utilized a sol–gel solution of ZIF-67, which is a Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework (ZIF) material composed of zinc and 2-methylimidazole, applied it to the substrate, and attempted to solidify it during bond formation29. Consequently, a high bond strength of 30 MPa is achieved. This value significantly exceeds previously reported values and demonstrates the effectiveness of the material as a bonding agent. However, because ZIF-67 is stable in water, it is difficult to disassemble it by immersion in water, making it unsuitable as a water-dismantling adhesive. Therefore, we focused on the use of a meltable MOF/CP. The use of a meltable MOF/CP is an alternative strategy for forming bonds in a liquid state. Meltable MOFs/CPs refer to MOFs/CPs that exhibit the property of transitioning from a solid to liquid state at a certain temperature, and reports on such materials have increased in recent years30–35. Meltability is expected to allow the adhesive material to spread and wet the substrate surface when heated above its melting point during bonding, thereby enhancing adhesion. In this study, we synthesized three types of meltable MOFs/CPs ([Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](H2Im)2 (indicated as ZPI), [Zn(H2PO4)2(HTz)2] (indicated as ZPT), and [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](H2DMeBIm)2 (indicated as ZPD) ) introduced in a review paper by Horike et al.18,36 and conducted bonding tests using these materials. We also investigated the disassembly behavior when immersed in water at room temperature. This study explored the possibility of achieving both bonding and disassembly, which are two fundamentally contradictory properties.

Results and discussions

We synthesized ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD by mechanochemical method. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD synthesized by the mechanochemical method are shown in Figs. 1 and S1. The figures also include the patterns simulated from the CIF (Crystallographic Information File) data using the visualization software VESTA37 for each compound. The XRD patterns and the calculated patterns are in strong agreement, indicating the successful synthesis of the expected phases.

XRD patterns of synthesized powders for (a) ZPI, (b) ZPT, (c) ZPD, and (d) fracture surfaces of ZPI after performing shear test (ZPI_st) and water immersion test (ZPI_60w) along with their corresponding calculated XRD patterns.

The powders (ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD) were heated, melted, and then applied to the surface of a copper substrate to prepare the bonding test specimens (see Fig. 2). Shear strength tests were conducted to evaluate the bonding performance. All samples exhibited clear bonding, confirming the validity of the strategy proposed in this study, i.e., the presence of a liquid phase is essential for forming MOF/CP-based bonds. The results of the shear strength tests for each MOF/CP-bonded specimen are shown in Fig. 3. The shear strengths of ZPT and ZPD were 3 MPa or less. In contrast, ZPI exhibits a shear strength of approximately 10 MPa, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the highest value reported to date for melt MOF/CP-based bonding materials. This value also exceeds those of commercially available general-purpose adhesives, suggesting that ZPI has strong potential as a practical bonding material. In addition, ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD are all known as functional materials with proton conductivity32,33,35 and have the potential to impart additional properties, such as electrical and ionic conductivity, to bonding materials. Therefore, these materials are expected to be used not only for simple bonding applications but also as functional bonding materials in fields such as electrochemical devices and energy-related technologies.

Shear strength test for ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD specimens. The results after water immersion are also indicated (immersion time of 5, 15, and 60 min are denoted as ZPI_5w, ZPI_15w, and ZPI_60w, respectively).

The results of the fracture surface observations of the bonding test specimens are shown in Fig. 4a,b and S2. An important consideration during the bonding process is the potential introduction of air bubbles when molten MOF/CP is applied to the substrate surface. The presence of air bubbles within the joint layer can significantly reduce bonding strength. However, no air bubbles were observed in any of the specimens prepared in this study, and uniform, high-quality joint layers were successfully formed.

Microscopic observations for fracture surface of (a) lower and (b) upper substrate of ZPI specimens and after water immersion of (c) lower and (d) upper substrate. To align the orientation of the fracture surface, the image on the right was rotated to match the one on the left.

In addition, in all samples (ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD), MOF/CP remained on both the upper and lower substrates, indicating that the fracture mode was cohesive failure. This suggests that the adhesive strength at the interface exceeded the internal strength of the adhesive, confirming that the interfacial adhesive strength was sufficiently high.

Notably interesting features were observed in the Cu substrate. The copper surfaces exposed to air oxidize and gradually lose their metallic luster. However, the copper surfaces in contact with the bonded layer retained a more pronounced metallic luster compared to the non-contact surface (see Figs. 4 and S2). This might have resulted from the chemical interactions between the azole compounds (e.g., imidazole) in the MOF/CP and the copper surface. The azoles used in this study, such as imidazole, are known to be corrosion inhibitors38,39 that are widely employed to protect copper surfaces. Recent reports also suggested that melted MOF/CP materials have been reported as potential coating agents40. The strong interfacial adhesion to copper demonstrated in this study was likely due to the chemical bonding between the azoles in the MOF/CP and the metal atoms on the substrate surface.

The XRD patterns of the fractured surfaces after shear test are shown in Figs. 1d and S1 (subscript ‘st’ indicates ‘after shear test’). No distinct diffraction peaks were observed for the ZPT or ZPD samples, indicating that both materials were in an amorphous state (see Fig. S1). It is known that, upon melting and subsequent cooling, these materials do not return to their original crystalline phases but instead form a glass state with a local structure similar to that of the original crystal33. Therefore, these XRD patterns reflect the glass state formed during the melting and cooling processes during joint formation.

In contrast, ZPI was found to be partially crystallized. ZPI forms a glassy state upon melting but reverts to its crystalline state upon annealing34. Therefore, it is considered that ZPI did not form a fully glassy state after joint formation and remained in a partially crystallized state, although the XRD patterns did not fully match with those of crystalline ZPI.

Based on the results of the above series of joint tests, ZPI demonstrated the highest joint strength. Therefore, subsequent disassembly tests using water at room temperature were conducted on ZPI. The figure shows the joint strength before and after water immersion testing (see Fig. 3). The joint strength after water immersion decreased gradually and significantly with increasing immersion time. In the figure, specimens immersed for 5, 15, and 60 min are denoted as ZPI_5w, ZPI_15w, and ZPI_60w, respectively. After 60 min of immersion (ZPI_60w), the joint strength decreased significantly such that the samples could be easily disassembled by hand. Observations of the fracture surfaces revealed that the area around the edge of the joint had turned white (see Fig. 4c,d). To clarify the mechanism of this change, micro-Raman spectroscopy analysis was performed on the fracture surface of a specimen (ZPI_5w) (see Fig. S3). Measurements were conducted on the transparent central part and the whitish discolored peripheral part of the specimen. The results revealed that the whitish discolored region exhibited a characteristic peak at 1050 cm⁻¹. Furthermore, scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation of the same region showed that the transparent region had a uniform, smooth, glass-like structure, whereas the whitish discolored region consisted of aggregated columnar particles (see Fig. S4). and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis confirmed that the whitish discolored region had lower P/Zn and N/Zn ratios compared to the transparent region (see Fig. S5).

To investigate this color change in more detail, a model sample simulating disintegrated ZPI was separately prepared; ZPI was melted and cooled to produce a transparent sheet, which was then immersed in water overnight. After immersion, the transparent ZPI sheet transformed into a white powder, referred to hereafter as sample_i (i: immersion in water). XRD analysis of sample_i yielded a diffraction pattern consistent with zinc phosphate hydrate (Zn₂(P₂O₇)(H₂O)₅, PDF# 04-012-8139) (Fig. S6). Additionally, Raman spectroscopy of sample_i revealed a distinct peak at 1050 cm⁻¹ (Fig. S3).

Based on these results, it is considered that ZPI decomposes into zinc phosphate hydrate upon immersion in water, leading to the gradual disintegration of the joint from the outer periphery in contact with water. Since the decomposition product contains no nitrogen and has a P/Zn ratio of 1, the N/Zn and P/Zn ratios are expected to decrease as decomposition progresses at the joint. In the EDX analysis of the whitish discolored region on the fracture surface described above, the P/Zn and N/Zn ratios did not fully reach 1 and 0, suggesting that decomposition was still in progress. These findings support the proposed mechanism whereby ZPI joints undergo chemical decomposition upon contact with water, resulting in the disintegration of the bond.

These results demonstrate that ZPI as an adhesive material offers both high bonding strength and facile dismantlability at room temperature using water.

Conclusion

In this study, we focused on the use of MOFs/CPs to obtain adhesives that can be easily dismantled using room-temperature water. Three materials were synthesized from a group of meltable MOFs/CPs, melted, and applied to Cu substrates to create joints. The joints prepared with ZPI exhibited a high shear strength of approximately 10 MPa, demonstrating sufficient performance as a structural adhesive.

Moreover, when the bonded ZPI structure was immersed in room-temperature water for one hour, the bond strength significantly decreased, allowing easy disassembly by hand. The results highlighted the potential of ZPI as a bonding material that can be disassembled under mild conditions.

In addition, by combining the diverse functionalities and bonding capabilities of MOFs/CPs, this study establishes new design guidelines for developing functional adhesives with properties not found in conventional adhesives. The development of next-generation multifunctional bonding materials based on MOFs/CPs is anticipated.

Methods

Synthesis of [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](H2Im)2 (ZPI) powder

Zinc oxide (1 mmol), imidazole (HIm, 2 mmol), and phosphoric acid (3 mmol) were mixed in a mortar for 15 min, and the reaction product was obtained via a mechanochemical reaction34. The mixture was then washed with methanol by centrifugation and vacuum-dried at 100 °C for more than 24 h to obtain a white powder.

Synthesis of [Zn(H2PO4)2(HTz)2] (ZPT) powder

ZPT was synthesized similarly to ZPI: Zinc oxide (1 mmol), 1,2,4-triazole (HTz, 2 mmol), and phosphoric acid (2 mmol) were mixed in a mortar for 15 min, and the reaction product was obtained via a mechanochemical reaction33. The mixture was then washed with methanol by centrifugation and vacuum-dried at 100 °C for more than 24 h to obtain a white powder.

Synthesis of [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](H2DMeBIm)2 (ZPD) powder

ZPD was synthesized using the same procedure as ZPI: Zinc oxide (1 mmol), 5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole (HDMeBIm, 2 mmol), and phosphate (3 mmol) were mixed in a mortar for 15 min, and the reaction product was obtained via a mechanochemical reaction41. The mixture was then washed with methanol by centrifugation and vacuum-dried at 100 °C for more than 24 h yielding a slightly brown powder.

Preparation of bonding test specimens

The substrate materials used in this study were oxygen-free copper (C1020, Ra = 0.8, Engineering Test Service Co., Ltd.) with dimensions of φ10 × t5 mm (lower part) and φ5 × t2 mm (upper part). Prior to bonding, the substrates were ultrasonically cleaned with acetone for 10 min, acid washed with 7% hydrochloric acid for 30 s, again ultrasonically cleaned with acetone for 3 min, and dried with nitrogen gas.

The following bonding process was performed: a glass plate was placed on a hot plate, and ZPI, ZPT, and ZPD powders were individually heated to their respective melting points. The substrate was then preheated on a hot plate. A few milligrams of the melted material were collected using a spatula and applied to the surface of the lower substrate. The upper substrate was placed on top. The assembly was held at the target temperature for 5 min, removed from the hot plate, and rapidly cooled to produce the bonding test specimens. All the bonding procedures were conducted in a nitrogen atmosphere.

Bonding test

The shear bond strength was measured using a shear strength testing machine comprising a force gauge (model 5566, Imada) integrated with an automatic stage. In this configuration, the lower part was fixed, and the upper part was loaded by moving a rod connected to the load cell of the force gauge. The stroke speed was set at 1 mm/min. To ensure repeatability, each test was performed three times (N = 3). This test method complies with JIS Z 3198-5 (Japanese Industrial Standard for Test Methods for Soldered Joints).

Water immersion test

The disassembly of a joint is defined as the point at which the joint strength approaches zero, whereas dismantlable bonding refers to a significant reduction in joint strength within a short period of time due to external stimuli, such as temperature or atmosphere. To determine whether disassembly was possible in room-temperature water, the bonding test specimens were immersed in ion-exchanged water for one hour, after which the bonding strength tests were conducted.

X-ray diffraction pattern measurement and observation of fracture surfaces

The XRD patterns of the synthesized powder samples and the fracture surfaces of the bonded test specimens were recorded. The Rigaku Ultima IV (Cu Kα) or MiniFlex XpC (Cu Kα) was used for the measurements. The powder samples were analyzed using Ultima IV, and the fracture surfaces of the test specimens were measured using MiniFlex XpC. The scan speed was set at 5°/min, with a step size of 0.02°. The measurements were performed in Bragg–Brentano geometry using the θ-2θ scan mode.

A digital microscope manufactured by Yashima was used to observe the fracture surfaces.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis

SEM images and EDS analysis were obtained using a JEOL JSM-7000 F field-emission scanning electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Raman spectroscopy

For Raman spectroscopy, a RAMANtouch Raman spectrometer (Nanophoton; excitation wavelength: 532 nm, output power: 0.1 mW) was used. For sample_i, a 1.0 mW of output power was used.
