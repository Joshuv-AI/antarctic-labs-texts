The impact of loneliness on alexithymia among Chinese adolescents: the mediating role of problematic smartphone use and the moderating effect of fear of negative evaluation
Authors: Xiaowen Wu, Zihui Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research investigates how loneliness influences alexithymia among Chinese adolescents, drawing on Internet Compensation Theory and the Social Compensation Hypothesis. The study highlights the mediating role of problematic smartphone use (PSU) and the moderating effect of fear of negative evaluation (FNE). Using a cross-sectional design, data were collected from 688 secondary school students via the online Questionnaire Star platform. The results indicate that loneliness is positively associated with both PSU and alexithymia. PSU partially mediates the relationship between loneliness and alexithymia (based on statistical models). Additionally, FNE plays a moderating role in the relationship between loneliness and PSU or alexithymia. Subgroup analysis revealed that male students reported significantly higher levels of alexithymia than females. Among individuals with higher levels of FNE, the positive correlation between loneliness and alexithymia was significantly stronger. This study proposes a mechanism involving internal emotion (loneliness), external behavior (PSU), and cognitive evaluation (FNE), offering a systematic theoretical basis for adolescent mental health interventions.Keywords: Loneliness, Alexithymia, Problematic smartphone use, Fear of negative evaluation, Adolescents

Introduction

Alexithymia, originally conceptualized by Sifneos [52], describes a psychological phenomenon wherein individuals struggle to identify, articulate, and manage their emotional experiences. According to Hébert et al. [18], the incidence of alexithymia among adolescents has been estimated to vary between 7.3% and 29.9%, a range that underscores the emotional immaturity often seen during adolescence. This developmental vulnerability has drawn growing concern in the field of adolescent mental health [64]. Increasingly, alexithymia is being acknowledged as a critical psychological vulnerability, exerting considerable influence on adolescents’ emotional adjustment and cognitive-emotional growth [32]. Research indicates that alexithymia is highly correlated with emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety in adolescents and may hinder the development of social skills [58]. Since adolescents are at a critical stage of emotional and cognitive development, failure to identify and intervene in alexithymia in a timely manner may lead to long-term psychological maladaptation. Therefore, exploring its influencing factors is crucial for early prevention and intervention.

The formation and development of alexithymia are influenced by a combination of various internal and external factors. Loneliness, as a typical internal psychological experience, has been confirmed to be closely related to alexithymia. Studies have shown that the higher the level of loneliness in adolescents, the higher their alexithymia scores [26]. Individuals who are in a prolonged state of loneliness often lack effective emotional expression and social interaction opportunities, which further exacerbate their difficulties in emotional recognition and expression. Among the external factors, problematic smartphone use (PSU) has become a significant focus in recent years due to its profound impact on adolescents’ mental health [15]. The Internet Compensation [25] Theory proposes that individuals facing emotional neglect or social stress in real life may turn to smartphone use as a compensatory outlet. Although such use can momentarily relieve emotional strain, it may impair their capacity to manage emotions in real-world settings over time. Adolescents, who tend to be frequent users, are particularly vulnerable to negative outcomes associated with smartphone dependency, both psychologically and behaviorally [34]. Empirical studies have consistently linked PSU with alexithymia. For example, a large-scale survey by Jin et al. [23] involving 748 Chinese adolescents revealed that individuals with difficulties in emotion identification and higher levels of externally oriented thinking were more prone to smartphone addiction. Similarly, research by Gündogmus et al. [14] reported that increased severity of PSU was associated with more pronounced alexithymic features. Consequently, investigating the underlying pathways and designing targeted interventions for alexithymia holds crucial value for improving youth mental health outcomes.

Loneliness and alexithymia

Loneliness, widely recognized as a prevalent emotional condition, has attracted growing scholarly interest due to its significant impact on psychological well-being [74]. It generally reflects a person’s sense of dissatisfaction stemming from perceived inadequacies in the number or depth of their interpersonal connections. Such perceptions, rooted in personal evaluation, are frequently linked to emotional unease and psychological strain [47]. When there is a mismatch between one’s desired level of social engagement and actual interpersonal experiences, this disconnect can lead to adverse emotional states and cognitive responses, ultimately jeopardizing mental health.

Prior studies have identified a direct link between loneliness and alexithymia. The association between loneliness and alexithymia in adolescents has been validated in multiple studies. Studies have shown that adolescents with significant loneliness exhibit obvious barriers in emotion regulation, struggling to effectively employ appropriate strategies to express their emotional states [59]. For instance, Zhang et al. [72] found that individuals with higher levels of loneliness experienced more significant difficulties in emotional recognition and expression. In addition, from the perspective of Emotion Socialization Theory, an individual’s ability to recognize and express emotions is gradually developed through ongoing interactions with others [5]. However, loneliness, as a subjective experience of social disconnection, limits the opportunities for individuals to receive emotional feedback and emotional support, thereby weakening their emotion regulation and expression abilities. Research indicates that this lack of social interaction not only diminishes the pathways through which individuals gain emotional regulation experience but also may affect the development of their emotional vocabulary, thereby increasing the risk of alexithymia [17].

In addition to the direct path, loneliness may also indirectly affect alexithymia through external behavioral factors, especially through PSU as a key mediating variable. According to the theory of compensatory internet use, when individuals experience emotional, social, or identity deficits in real life, they may seek psychological compensation through media such as the internet [25]. Research by Snodgrass et al. [53] has confirmed the practical application of this theory, where the anonymity, controllability, and immediate interaction available in the online environment provide individuals with alternative social experiences, allowing them to alleviate feelings of loneliness, stress, or social exclusion. According to the Social Compensation Hypothesis, people with limited offline social support or underdeveloped interpersonal skills are more inclined to establish connections in virtual environments, which can offer a sense of inclusion and emotional satisfaction [24, 57]. In this context, those experiencing heightened loneliness may turn to smartphone engagement as a substitute for face-to-face social interaction. Research has identified a significant association between PSU and alexithymia [14]. Individuals who use smartphones more frequently often show greater difficulty in recognizing and managing emotions.

In conclusion, loneliness affects alexithymia not only directly through internal psychological mechanisms but also indirectly through PSU as an external behavioral pathway. Understanding this dual mechanism helps further elucidate the occurrence of alexithymia and provides a theoretical basis and practical guidance for interventions in adolescent emotional health.

The potential mediating role of problematic smartphone use

As mobile internet use has surged, smartphones have evolved into essential devices in daily life. Data from the “Statistical Report on the Development of the Internet in China” indicate that by December 2024, mobile internet users in China had exceeded 1.1 billion, comprising nearly 99.7% of the national population. Notably, approximately 21.1% of these users are between the ages of 6 and 19. Within this context, PSU has become increasingly prominent, often manifesting as difficulties in managing smartphone usage duration and frequency. Such patterns of overuse have been linked to disruptions in students’ academic engagement, interpersonal relationships, and emotional well-being [35]. Empirical studies suggest that excessive smartphone dependency among adolescents is frequently associated with a range of psychological conditions, including heightened levels of anxiety, depression, and perceived social isolation [55]. In addition, it is often accompanied by maladaptive coping styles, such as avoidant behavior and emotional dysregulation [9]. These outcomes not only compromise mental health but also undermine adolescents’ social competence and life satisfaction. Therefore, in-depth exploration of PSU in youth populations is essential, especially in relation to their psychological adjustment and social development.

A large body of research indicates that PSU may play a mediating role in the mechanism of alexithymia. On one hand, existing studies consistently point out a significant positive correlation between PSU and alexithymia. For instance, Yang et al. [69] reported that excessive smartphone dependence is a notable predictor of elevated alexithymia levels. This finding indicates that high-frequency device use may disrupt individuals’ ability to recognize and express emotions, potentially intensifying alexithymic tendencies. In addition, Mei et al. [42] linked PSU to increased risks of loneliness, anxiety, and depression. Additionally, Su and He [54], along with Sarman and Çiftci [50], found robust associations between loneliness and PSU. Research by Wang et al. [63] confirmed that loneliness predicts greater reliance on smartphones, and Kingston and Shekhar [28] further demonstrated that escalating loneliness intensifies dependence on digital devices. In conclusion, according to the Compensatory Internet Use Theory [25], adolescents with high levels of loneliness tend to seek emotional compensation through smartphones. However, this compensatory behavior may weaken emotional expression skills due to reduced real-life social interactions. Specifically, excessive reliance on virtual socializing may lead to a poor emotional vocabulary [5] and hinder the development of emotion regulation strategies, thereby exacerbating alexithymia.

Building upon previous findings, it is reasonable to propose that PSU may act as an intermediary mechanism linking loneliness with alexithymia, as supported by prior studies [62, 71]. More specifically, loneliness may not only have a direct effect on the development of alexithymia but could also intensify alexithymic traits by promoting increased engagement in problematic smartphone behaviors.

The moderating role of fear of negative evaluation

The conceptual framework of fear of negative evaluation (FNE), originally introduced by Watson and Friend [65], highlights individuals’ heightened concern about being judged unfavorably during interpersonal encounters—a key indicator of social anxiety. This theoretical perspective posits that individuals experiencing high levels of FNE are prone to heightened psychological distress and may engage in avoidant responses when faced with social exposure. In more severe cases, such fear can be accompanied by physical symptoms, including increased heart rate and perspiration. Moreover, FNE has been shown to undermine self-worth and impair one’s ability to engage effectively in social contexts [43].

According to the theory of compensatory internet use, loneliness is often viewed as a key factor in promoting PSU [50]. When individuals experience high levels of loneliness, they often seek to fill emotional gaps through external tools such as social media or smartphones, which exacerbates PSU. Kardefelt-Winther [25], in his research on online gaming, found that players with psychosocial issues (such as high stress and low self-esteem) tend to over-engage in online games due to escapist motivations, which eventually leads to negative consequences. However, FNE may alter an individual’s cognitive and emotional responses to social interactions, thus influencing whether they rely on smartphones for emotional regulation. Particularly, the pressure felt during social interactions and the excessive concern about others’ evaluations may make individuals more likely to escape through smartphone use, reducing direct social contact [44].

Moreover, FNE may influence individuals’ emotional and behavioral responses while using smartphones [40]. Specifically, when individuals have higher FNE, they may be more inclined to use smartphones to avoid facing social interactions and emotional expressions in real life. Through smartphones, individuals can maintain social connections in a safer environment, reducing the risk of facing negative evaluations in face-to-face interactions [40]. However, this reliance on smartphones may lead to further deterioration of an individual’s social skills, particularly in emotional expression [70, 73]. Studies have confirmed that excessive smartphone reliance may reduce real-life social interactions, making individuals more dependent on virtual communication tools when facing emotional expression needs, ultimately exacerbating the manifestation of alexithymia [7].

Finally, loneliness is widely considered an important factor in adolescent emotional problems. Research shows that when individuals have higher FNE, they tend to exhibit stronger avoidance tendencies toward social interactions, unwilling to face others’ evaluations, which limits their emotional regulation and alexithymia [3]. Specifically, when individuals experience high loneliness, accompanied by high FNE, they may excessively worry about social interactions and avoid emotional expression and communication with others, which may further impair their ability to express emotions [11]. Studies suggest that individuals tend to display emotional avoidance or suppression when facing potential negative evaluations from others, and this behavioral pattern is closely related to the formation of alexithymia [39]. In other words, FNE not only directly affects an individual’s emotional experience but also exacerbates the impact of loneliness on alexithymia by influencing their behavioral choices in social situations.

In conclusion, FNE may serve as a moderator in various pathways. Exploring the moderating mechanism of FNE is of great significance for deeply understanding the formation process of adolescent emotional disorders and developing targeted intervention strategies.

Research objectives and hypotheses

Loneliness, PSU, and FNE are three key factors influencing alexithymia in adolescents. First, loneliness may reduce opportunities for emotional expression by limiting real-life social interactions [59]. Second, PSU, as a common compensatory behavior, may replace face-to-face communication, further restricting the development of emotional recognition abilities [23]. Finally, individuals with high FNE are more likely to suppress emotional expression due to the fear of negative evaluation, thereby exacerbating alexithymia [43]. Although previous studies have made preliminary explorations into variables such as loneliness, alexithymia, PSU, and FNE in the context of adolescent mental health, there are still significant gaps in both the depth and breadth of research.

Firstly, most studies currently focus on one specific variable, such as loneliness, alexithymia, PSU, or FNE, and analyze its direct effect on alexithymia outcomes, without conducting a systematic investigation of the interactive mechanisms among these variables from multidimensional perspectives, such as internal emotional experiences, external behavioral patterns, and cognitive assessments. Secondly, while existing research has found associations between PSU, loneliness, and alexithymia, there is a lack of systematic empirical support regarding whether PSU serves as a mediator between these variables. Additionally, the moderating role of FNE in this chain of relationships has almost been overlooked, and its potential effects in direct and indirect pathways remain unclear. Finally, current research based on the theory of compensatory internet use mainly focuses on explaining the triggering factors of PSU, but has not integrated these factors and their consequences into an overarching framework for study.

To address these deficiencies, the present study aims to systematically explore the mechanism by which loneliness affects alexithymia in the adolescent population of China, constructing a comprehensive model that integrates three dimensions: internal emotion (loneliness), external behavior (PSU), and cognitive assessment (FNE). This study will delve into the potential mediating role of PSU and examine the moderating effect of FNE in each pathway, thereby revealing how loneliness influences adolescents’ emotional expression abilities through behavioral and cognitive aspects. Moreover, in line with the theory of compensatory internet use, this study also addresses an important yet neglected issue: the triggers of PSU and the psychological and social consequences it generates. Therefore, the study will include both loneliness and alexithymia in the same causal chain, clarify the mediating role of PSU, and comprehensively present its impact on adolescents’ psychological and social functioning.

Based on the theoretical relationships outlined above, this study aims to further investigate the mediating mechanism of PSU between loneliness and alexithymia, and to examine whether this mechanism is moderated by FNE. Based on previous empirical research, we have established the theoretical hypothesis model as shown in Fig. 1, and propose the following hypotheses: (1) Loneliness is positively correlated with alexithymia; (2) Loneliness is positively correlated with PSU; (3) PSU is positively correlated with alexithymia; (4) PSU mediates the relationship between loneliness and alexithymia; (5) FNE moderates the direct or indirect paths from loneliness to alexithymia.

Materials and methods

Participants

This investigation adopted a cross-sectional approach and utilized a non-probability sampling method to recruit participants via the online platform Sojump (www.Sojump.com). Data were gathered from adolescents enrolled in various secondary schools across different regions of China. To enhance the representativeness and external applicability of the findings, researchers worked closely with school administrators who explained the purpose and significance of the study to potential participants and facilitated their voluntary involvement. Participants completed the survey independently, and data collection was carried out between March and May 2025. All responses were recorded in an anonymous and confidential manner. The research team obtained both the informed consent from the adolescents themselves and the written consent from their parents or legal guardians prior to the survey. According to conventional standards for sample estimation, each survey item was expected to have at least 10 responses. Given the anticipated dropout rate of approximately 20%, a minimum of 624 valid responses was required [29]. In total, 723 questionnaires were disseminated and received. A multi-step screening protocol was implemented to ensure data integrity. First, entries with duplicated IP addresses were excluded to prevent redundancy. Second, cases with completion durations exceeding three standard deviations from the mean were removed to control for response anomalies. Third, any submission with over 70% identical responses on the same scale was flagged and discarded [8]. These procedures ensured the robustness and reliability of the dataset. After quality filtering, 688 valid responses remained, yielding an effective response rate of 95.16%. Among the final sample were 305 male and 383 female participants. These individuals constituted the dataset for subsequent analysis (see Table 1).

Measurement tools

Smartphone addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV)

This study measured adolescents’ problematic smartphone use using the SAS-SV developed by Kwon et al. [30]. One sample item is: I delay my planned tasks because of using my smartphone. The scale comprises 10 positively phrased items, each evaluated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 6 = strongly agree), yielding total scores between 10 and 60. Higher scores denote more pronounced PSU symptoms. Liu et al. [38] provided evidence for the scale’s cultural relevance and measurement stability in Chinese adolescent groups. In this study, the SAS-SV showed strong reliability, reflected by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.955.

Toronto alexithymia Scale-20 (TAS-20)

To assess levels of alexithymia, the TAS-20 was employed. An example item is: I often have difficulty understanding how I feel. The instrument consists of 20 items, with responses rated on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Scores are totaled, ranging from 20 to 100; higher scores reflect greater alexithymia. Its effectiveness has been validated in studies involving Chinese adolescents [22]. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.955.

University of California Los Angeles loneliness Scale-6 (ULS-6)

Adolescents perceived loneliness was measured using the ULS-6. A representative statement includes: I lack companionship. Participants responded using a four-point scale (1 = never; 4 = always), yielding scores between 6 and 24. Higher totals indicate greater perceived loneliness. The ULS-6 has been widely applied in Chinese research contexts, with prior work [67] affirming its reliability and validity. In the present dataset, the scale achieved good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.943).

Brief version of the fear of negative evaluation scale (BFNES)

To assess adolescents’ sensitivity to negative social appraisal, this study employed the BFNES. One representative item reads: Even though I know others’ opinions are not important, I still worry about how others perceive me. The scale includes 12 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores for affirmatively phrased items are calculated as-is, while those worded in the negative are reverse-coded before being totaled. Overall scores can range from 12 to 60, with higher totals indicating more intense fear of being judged by others. Previous studies involving Chinese adolescents have demonstrated the BFNES’s validity and internal reliability [46]. In the present dataset, this instrument showed strong psychometric performance, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.934.

Statistical analysis

This study employed a two-stage approach for hypothesis testing and model prediction. First, a theory-driven structural equation model (SEM) was constructed to test the linear relationships between exogenous and endogenous variables. SEM analysis was then conducted using Smart PLS 4.0 software, with hypothesis testing performed through path analysis and Bootstrap resampling methods. Next, independent sample t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to conduct statistical analysis of group differences. Finally, multi-group analysis was further performed using the multi-group PLS analysis method.

A p-value less than 0.05 suggests that the data possess sufficient correlation structure to support factor analysis. SEM analysis

This study used Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to test both the measurement model and the structural model. PLS-SEM offers several advantages: first, it is a prediction-oriented data analysis technique that effectively enhances the explanatory power of the model; second, PLS-SEM has relatively low sample size requirements and can still operate stably even when the data do not meet the normality assumption; lastly, this method is suitable for complex models that include multiple latent variables and a large number of observed variables. The objective of this study is to explore the relationship between adolescent loneliness and alexithymia. The research model includes four constructs and a total of 52 measurement items, with a sample size of 688 participants. Therefore, choosing PLS-SEM as the analysis method for this study is both reasonable and appropriate [16].

Evaluation of common method Bias (CMB)

To assess whether the data were affected by common method bias, this study utilized Harman’s one-factor test. Through exploratory factor analysis, seven independent components were identified, each with eigenvalues above 1. The initial unrotated factor accounted for 26.305% of the total variance, which is notably lower than the 40% threshold typically considered indicative of serious bias [48]. Therefore, it can be inferred that the dataset is not substantially compromised by common method variance, and the overall validity of the results remains intact.

Group difference analysis

Measurement invariance analysis

Participants were ranked from high to low based on their FNE scores and the sample was divided into two equal groups: the high FNE group (top 50%) and the low FNE group (bottom 50%). Subsequently, this study conducted a multi-group partial least squares (PLS) analysis to further compare the structural path coefficients. This method has been widely applied in previous studies [75].

Before the comparison, measurement invariance was assessed using the Measurement Invariance of Composite Models (MICOM) procedure proposed by Henseler et al. [19]. According to Hair [16], two conditions must be met for valid multi-group analysis: structural invariance, ensured by using identical model specifications and estimation methods, and compositional invariance, assessed through a permutation test.

In Smart-PLS 4.0, structural invariance is automatically maintained, and compositional invariance is evaluated based on whether the correlation of construct scores exceeds the 5% quantile of the empirical distribution. As shown in Table 12, the results confirmed that measurement invariance was established between the high and low FNE groups, supporting the reliability of the subsequent multi-group comparison.

Discussion

This study is consistent with the research by Zhang et al. [72], both finding a significant positive correlation between loneliness and alexithymia in adolescents. Additionally, Vescan et al. [61] emphasized that adolescents experiencing higher levels of loneliness often encounter difficulties in regulating their emotions and tend to lack effective coping strategies. According to the Emotion Regulation Process Model proposed by Gross [12], emotion regulation involves a set of psychological processes through which individuals observe, assess, and adapt their emotional responses to meet contextual demands and personal objectives. Adolescents who report persistent loneliness are often found to possess weaker emotion regulation skills, which can reduce their willingness and ability to express emotions. Over time, this leads to a decline in emotional expressiveness and intensifies alexithymic tendencies [49]. For instance, Lamprou et al. [31] observed that many individuals in lonely states tend to suppress their emotions instead of seeking social support. Such suppression, when prolonged, may diminish their ability to recognize and articulate feelings. In the present research, adolescents in long-term loneliness lacked adequate emotional and social interaction opportunities, resulting in limited chances to develop and practice emotion recognition and communication skills. The Emotion Regulation Theory introduced by Gross [12] underscores the importance of frequent interpersonal interactions and emotional feedback for cultivating emotion regulation abilities. When adolescents are socially isolated for extended periods, they miss out on essential experiences such as observing others’ emotional expressions and practicing their own. This absence of social-emotional engagement likely contributes to the worsening of alexithymia symptoms. In addition, from a social environment perspective, family and school are key places where adolescents learn emotional expression. If parents or teachers encourage less emotional sharing (such as saying “boys don’t cry” or “don’t overthink”), adolescents may suppress their emotions, leading to alexithymia [56].

In addition, the analysis revealed a clear positive association between adolescents’ loneliness and PSU, thereby supporting Hypothesis 2. This observation aligns with prior work by Hu and Xiang [20], who demonstrated that feelings of loneliness significantly predict increased PSU among adolescents. A follow-up comparison based on loneliness levels further indicated that adolescents with higher loneliness scores reported markedly elevated PSU compared to their peers in the lower loneliness group. These outcomes not only reinforce the understanding of PSU antecedents but also lend empirical support to the compensatory internet use framework and the Social Compensation Hypothesis. By exploring these associations from the perspective of behavioral mechanisms, this study offers deeper insights into how loneliness may contribute to problematic technology engagement in youth populations. From the perspectives of the theory of compensatory internet use and the Social Compensation Hypothesis, adolescents with high levels of loneliness tend to use smartphones as emotional regulation tools, compensating for the lack of real-life social interaction through instant virtual interactions. This compensatory usage pattern is prone to develop into over-reliance [25]. It is particularly noteworthy that this dependency may form a self-reinforcing cycle: while smartphone use may temporarily alleviate loneliness, in the long term, it occupies time and energy that would otherwise be spent on real-life social interactions, ultimately exacerbating social isolation [24, 57]. Adolescents’ dependence on smartphones is also influenced by their social environment. When friends in real life are using social media to chat or play games, individuals who do not participate may be excluded (e.g., “If you don’t play, there’s no common topic”). Adolescents with strong feelings of loneliness are more likely to overuse their smartphones in order to fit in with the group [45]. This finding provides important implications for mental health interventions for adolescents in the digital age: in reducing PSU, it is essential to also address the underlying emotional needs and the lack of social connections.

The current results contribute not only to the identification of PSU’s predictors but also to a better understanding of its psychological implications. Consistent with our research findings, Huang et al. [21] reported a robust link between alexithymic traits and tendencies toward excessive smartphone use. These findings imply that individuals with difficulties in emotional awareness and expression may be more inclined to rely heavily on smartphones. Such reliance may serve as a means of avoiding unpleasant emotions or compensating for internal emotional deficiencies. A follow-up study by Jin et al. [23] further supports this conclusion, noting that the close connection between adolescent smartphone addiction and alexithymia may stem from the fact that smartphone use provides an instant and convenient form of emotional regulation. However, this alternative regulation ultimately exacerbates individuals’ alienation from their own emotions, creating a vicious cycle. In this study, adolescents with prolonged PSU exhibited noticeable emotional avoidance tendencies and social withdrawal behaviors. They were more likely to use instant gratification methods such as short videos, social media, or gaming to alleviate negative emotions rather than actively confronting and resolving emotional issues in real life [76]. This externally driven emotional regulation pattern led them to gradually lose awareness and expression of their own emotions, thereby worsening the symptoms of alexithymia. Additionally, excessive smartphone use also resulted in a decline in adolescents’ real-life social skills, further reducing opportunities for emotional expression, creating a closed loop of “smartphone dependence—emotional numbness” [69]. From a sociological perspective, prolonged dependence on smartphone socializing may alter adolescents’ emotional expression. For example, many people have become accustomed to using emojis to replace genuine emotions, leading to vague emotional language (e.g., not knowing how to express oneself). This digital socializing habit may make it increasingly difficult for adolescents to clearly express their feelings in real life, exacerbating alexithymia [1]. These findings reveal the complex interplay between PSU and alexithymia and provide a critical foundation for developing subsequent intervention strategies.

This study found that PSU partially mediates the relationship between loneliness and alexithymia, supporting the Compensatory Internet Use Theory [25]. From an emotional perspective, loneliness drives adolescents to replace real-life socializing with smartphone use, leading to reduced opportunities for emotional expression [36]. From an attention perspective, excessive smartphone use may occupy time for emotional reflection, hindering the development of emotional recognition abilities [4]. Prior literature has underscored the intermediary function of smartphone overuse in bridging emotional distress and psychological maladjustment. For instance, Kayis et al. [26] found that heightened loneliness increases vulnerability to smartphone addiction, which adversely affects mental health outcomes. Their findings emphasized that excessive reliance on smartphones may evolve into a coping mechanism aimed at avoiding emotional discomfort, thereby weakening individuals’ connection with their inner experiences—a conclusion that resonates with the present findings. In a similar vein, Wang et al. [62] demonstrated that smartphone dependency acts as a mediating factor in the association between psychological stress and impaired sleep, suggesting that adverse emotional states contribute to elevated smartphone usage, which may further deteriorate psychological and behavioral health. These studies collectively demonstrate that smartphone addiction may be a key mechanism through which negative emotions (such as loneliness and stress) affect mental health. In addition, Yue et al. [71] further complemented this view, finding that smartphone addiction mediates the relationship between parent-child relationships and social media use, emphasizing the core impact of emotional factors on adolescents’ smartphone use behaviors. These findings are consistent with the logical framework of this study, which suggests that negative emotions (such as loneliness) may be associated with increased dependence on smartphones. In the long term, this maladaptive behavioral pattern may be accompanied by greater difficulty in emotional recognition and expression, thus leading to an association with alexithymia.

The moderation analysis revealed the complex role of FNE in adolescent mental health issues. The results indicate that the level of FNE is positively correlated with the strength of the association between loneliness and PSU. First, from the perspective of emotion regulation [13], individuals with high FNE are overly sensitive to social evaluations and often experience stronger psychological discomfort when facing loneliness. The immediacy, controllability, and low-risk nature of smartphones make them an effective emotional regulation tool. Second, from the perspective of social compensation mechanisms, these individuals use virtual social interactions to avoid potential negative evaluations in real-life social situations. While this pattern temporarily alleviates psychological discomfort, it further weakens social skills. Over time, it may create a vicious cycle where loneliness leads to PSU, and PSU, in turn, causes social skill degradation [6, 41]. Regarding the relationship between loneliness and alexithymia, FNE also showed a significant moderating effect. Our subgroup analysis further indicated that in the high FNE group, the positive association between loneliness and alexithymia was significantly stronger than in the low FNE group. This suggests that individuals with high FNE, due to their excessive focus on others’ evaluations, may actively suppress emotional expression, thereby impairing their ability to recognize and describe emotions. First, according to the emotion processing theory [2], individuals with high FNE often over-control their emotional experiences. This emotional suppression strategy interferes with the normal process of emotional recognition and expression. Second, from the perspective of developmental psychology, prolonged emotional suppression may lead to dysfunction in the emotional cognition system [51]. It is particularly noteworthy that in the Chinese cultural context, this moderating effect may be amplified by cultural traits such as “face consciousness,” leading adolescents to be more inclined to suppress emotional expression to avoid negative evaluation. It should be noted that the moderating effect of FNE on the relationship between PSU and alexithymia was not significant. This finding may suggest that the association between PSU and alexithymia is relatively independent of an individual’s sensitivity to FNE. From the perspective of behavioral addiction [25], problematic smartphone use may affect emotional recognition abilities through mechanisms such as direct attention capture and social deprivation, which may not be moderated by cognitive appraisal factors like FNE. Additionally, according to the emotion regulation process model [13], the instant gratification feature of smartphones may directly replace the need for emotion regulation, forming a habitual emotional avoidance pattern. This automated behavioral process is likely less influenced by cognitive appraisal factors such as FNE. This finding suggests that, when intervening in the relationship between problematic smartphone use and alexithymia, greater attention may need to be paid to correcting behavioral habits rather than solely adjusting cognitive factors. In conclusion, the results of this study strongly support the applicability of the theory of compensatory internet use and the Social Compensation Hypothesis in the digital age, revealing the psychological mechanism by which adolescents with high FNE use smartphones to compensate for deficiencies in real-life social interaction.

Furthermore, loneliness, PSU, and FNE do not independently influence adolescent alexithymia; instead, they form a closed-loop system through the dynamic interaction of emotion, behavior, and cognition. Specifically, individuals with high loneliness are more likely to rely on smartphones to compensate for emotional gaps, driven by emotion. This process is further reinforced by FNE, which causes cognitive factors to further modulate the relationship between emotion and behavior [37]. Meanwhile, PSU reduces real-life social interactions, thereby weakening emotional expression abilities, and this influence ultimately feeds back into emotional and cognitive development, exacerbating alexithymia [68]. This chain mechanism reveals the complex causes of adolescent mental health in the digital age and provides a theoretical basis for intervention designs that integrate emotion regulation, behavioral habits, and cognitive evaluation.

The analysis results also revealed that male adolescents had significantly higher levels of alexithymia compared to females, which is consistent with existing research. Garaigordobil [10] found that males scored significantly higher than females on alexithymia measures, such as difficulty in expressing emotions and externally oriented thinking. Zhu et al. [77] also observed that the male group performed worse than females in terms of alexithymia. According to the Normative Male Alexithymia (NMA) hypothesis [33], traditional male gender role socialization makes it difficult for men to express emotions verbally. In traditional concepts of masculinity, expressing negative emotions such as stress and anxiety is often viewed as a sign of weakness or dependency. Therefore, men lack the vocabulary to express negative emotions, leading to higher scores on alexithymia scales. For example, when communicating with others, men may be unable to accurately express their inner feelings due to the constraints of this belief system, which manifests as a higher degree of alexithymia [33]. From a sociological perspective, gender role pressures during adolescence may exacerbate this difference. Boys face more “strong” expectations in academics and social interactions, which may force them to cope with loneliness through “outward thinking” defense mechanisms (such as gaming addiction and ignoring emotions), thereby reinforcing tendencies toward alexithymia [66].

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study offers theoretical value by systematically examining how loneliness (as an internal emotional factor), problematic smartphone behavior (external), and FNE are linked to the development of alexithymia. By incorporating direct, mediating, and moderating effects into a unified framework, this research contributes a novel viewpoint on the multifaceted origins of alexithymia among adolescents, while enriching the theoretical discourse within mental health studies.

The findings also confirmed that PSU acts as a partial intermediary in the link between loneliness and alexithymia. This outcome lends empirical support to the compensatory internet use framework, which proposes that adolescents may resort to excessive smartphone engagement as a response to unsatisfied social needs in offline life. Over time, such dependence may heighten difficulties in emotional communication. By jointly modeling loneliness, smartphone use, and alexithymia, this study provides a coherent explanatory structure that clarifies the interactions among these psychological dimensions.

Moreover, the results further expand the theoretical reach of cognitive evaluation theory by identifying FNE as a moderating variable in social-emotional contexts. This advances the theory’s utility in explaining emotional functioning in digital interaction scenarios. The study shows that FNE indirectly shapes an individual’s mental health by influencing their behavioral choices and emotion regulation, emphasizing the crucial role of cognitive factors in the psychological adaptation process. Furthermore, the study found significant differences in the main pathways based on gender and grade level, suggesting that developmental stages and socio-cultural factors (such as academic pressure and gender roles) may play important roles in the mechanisms outlined above.

Practical implications

Firstly, parents should pay attention to whether their children often feel lonely or rely too much on their phones. For children who feel lonely, they can arrange regular daily parent-child activities, such as cooking or taking walks together, where the whole family puts away their phones to focus on communication and companionship. This helps reduce children’s loneliness and dependence on phones. For children who are afraid of being criticized, parents should pay attention to their communication methods, using encouragement instead of blame. This can reduce children’s psychological stress and make them more willing to put down their phones voluntarily.

Secondly, schools should design targeted interventions based on the group differences identified in the study. To address the issue of emotional expression difficulties in boys, emotional expression training programs (such as role-playing and emotional vocabulary exercises) can be implemented to improve their emotional recognition and expression abilities. Additionally, cognitive restructuring training can be incorporated into mental health courses to help them view others’ evaluations more rationally. Schools should also promote digital literacy education, using case discussions and other methods to help students understand the potential impact of excessive smartphone use on emotional health.

Finally, communities could organize small-scale offline activities (such as interest groups and volunteer services) to provide adolescents with genuine social interaction opportunities. Technology companies may consider developing features that promote healthy usage habits, such as contextualized break reminders tailored to different usage patterns. Meanwhile, society should foster an inclusive environment to create supportive conditions for adolescents’ mental health development.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contributions, this study is not without certain methodological constraints. To begin with, the use of a cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer temporal or causal directionality among variables, even though the associations observed remain informative. Future research could consider longitudinal tracking or experimental interventions to enhance causal inference. In terms of data acquisition, the findings were primarily based on self-administered surveys, which may introduce response distortions, including those caused by the desire to present oneself in a favorable light. Moreover, the reliance on purely quantitative techniques may overlook nuanced psychological patterns. To address this, it is recommended that future work incorporate qualitative inquiry, observational protocols, and physiological measures to offer a more multidimensional view of the constructs involved. Furthermore, with regard to construct definition, the PSU measurement tool used in this study did not differentiate among various usage behaviors. Given that users may engage with smartphones for a variety of purposes—such as entertainment, communication, or information-seeking—each of which may stem from different psychological motivations, future studies should refine their measurement instruments to reflect such contextual distinctions, thereby improving theoretical clarity and applicability. In addition, the participants in this study were limited to Chinese adolescents, and the study has not incorporated a cross-cultural comparative perspective. In different cultural contexts (such as collectivist and individualist cultures), adolescents may exhibit systematic differences in their expressions of loneliness, smartphone usage patterns, and emotional regulation strategies. Future research could examine the cross-cultural applicability of the findings by conducting cross-cultural comparisons. Finally, in terms of research design, this study’s sampling method primarily relied on online platforms and school management channels, which may not have fully captured certain special groups (such as school dropouts or individuals with restricted smartphone usage). To improve the external validity of the research, future studies are recommended to adopt more diverse sampling methods, including adolescent groups from different socioeconomic statuses, educational backgrounds, and geographical distributions, to obtain more generalizable findings.

Conclusion

This study explored the mechanism by which loneliness affects alexithymia in Chinese adolescents, examining the mediating role of PSU and the moderating effect of FNE. Based on a survey of 688 adolescents, the study found that loneliness was significantly positively correlated with both alexithymia and PSU. PSU was also positively correlated with alexithymia and partially mediated the relationship between loneliness and alexithymia (based on statistical models). Additionally, FNE played a moderating role in the association between loneliness and PSU or alexithymia. Finally, group analyses showed that males had significantly higher TAS scores than females and the positive correlation between loneliness and alexithymia was more pronounced among individuals with higher levels of FNE. This study supports the ‘loneliness-problematic smartphone use-alexithymia’ association path, revealing the dynamic interactive mechanisms between internal emotional experiences, external behavioral patterns, and cognitive evaluations. It provides practical references for adolescent interventions and theoretical support for the development of intervention strategies based on enhancing psychological resources.
