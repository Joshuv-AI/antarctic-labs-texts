A novel rat model of neurotrophic keratopathy complicated with limbal stem cell deficiency induced by trigeminal ophthalmic branch injury
Authors: Xiaorong Sun, Yue Chen, Dan Su, Feng Luo, Min Zhang, Yuwei Xu, Jinliang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnimal models that simultaneously reproduce neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) and limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD) are scarce, despite the frequent clinical coexistence of these two conditions. We aimed to establish a reproducible rat model showing a combined NK/LSCD phenotype by selectively injuring the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve (V1) within the orbit.MethodsAdult male Sprague–Dawley rats underwent intraorbital V1 transection or V1 exposure without transection. Corneal sensitivity was evaluated qualitatively using a cotton-swab touch response and quantitatively using a Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer before surgery and on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21. Tear secretion, fluorescein staining, ocular surface abnormalities, and corneal neovascularization were assessed longitudinally. Corneal and limbal tissues were examined histologically and by PAS staining, CD31, K12, K14, and P63 immunostaining, and TUNEL analysis.ResultsCochet–Bonnet esthesiometry confirmed profound corneal hypoesthesia after V1 injury. Corneal sensitivity remained at 60 mm before surgery and in sham eyes at all postoperative time points, whereas the mean thresholds in the V1-injury group were 3.5 mm, 4.0 mm, and 5.5 mm on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21, respectively. V1 injury was also associated with reduced tear secretion, persistent epithelial compromise, progressive corneal neovascularization, stromal fibrosis, PAS-positive goblet-cell invasion, reduced limbal P63 and K14 expression, peripheral loss of K12 expression, and increased corneal apoptosis.ConclusionsIntraorbital V1 injury induces a denervation-associated ocular surface disease phenotype with overlapping features of neurotrophic keratopathy and secondary limbal epithelial failure in rats. Within the 21-day observation period, this model provides a practical platform for investigating early-to-intermediate interactions between corneal sensory denervation, epithelial dysfunction, and limbal niche disturbance. Its long-term stability and the course of corneal reinnervation remain to be determined.Keywords: Neurotrophic keratopathy, Limbal stem cell deficiency, Corneal sensation, Trigeminal nerve injury, Conjunctivalization, Goblet cells

Introduction

The ocular surface is a highly specialized and integrated biological system composed of the cornea, limbus, conjunctiva, tear film, and their associated neural and immune microenvironments. The cornea and conjunctiva are separated by the limbal junctional zone, where limbal epithelial stem cells reside within the basal epithelial compartment and support continuous renewal of the corneal epithelium throughout life [1, 2]. In addition to serving as the source of corneal epithelial regeneration, the limbal region functions as a structural and functional barrier that helps prevent conjunctival epithelial ingrowth and vascular invasion into the normally avascular cornea [1–4]. Loss or dysfunction of limbal stem cells disrupts this barrier and leads to limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), which is characterized clinically and histopathologically by persistent epithelial defects, conjunctivalization, goblet cell invasion, corneal neovascularization, stromal scarring, chronic inflammation, and loss of vision [5–12].

Neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) is a degenerative corneal disease caused by impairment of trigeminal sensory innervation and its trophic support to the ocular surface [13, 14]. The ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve provides the principal sensory input to the cornea and is essential not only for nociception, but also for maintenance of epithelial integrity, blink and tear reflexes, wound healing, and epithelial–stromal homeostasis [15]. A wide range of clinical insults, including herpetic disease, ocular surgery, central nervous system disorders, diabetes, topical toxicity, and chemical injury, may disrupt corneal innervation and precipitate NK [16–20]. In experimental settings, NK has been modeled through trigeminal ganglion injury, ciliary nerve injury, stereotactic trigeminal approaches, and chemical or genetic neuronal ablation [21–24].

Although NK and LSCD are often discussed as distinct disease entities, increasing evidence indicates that they may overlap biologically and clinically. Reduced corneal sensation and abnormalities of the subbasal nerve plexus have been documented in eyes with progressive LSCD, and a number of etiologies, including herpetic infection, diabetes, chronic topical toxicity, contact lens wear, and ocular surface burns, may contribute to both conditions simultaneously [25–28]. In such cases, sensory denervation, epithelial instability, tear film dysfunction, inflammation, limbal niche disruption, and pathological vascularization may interact to drive progressive ocular surface failure. Despite this recognized overlap, experimental models that reproducibly recapitulate both the neurotrophic and limbal stem cell-deficient components of disease remain limited.

Here, we established and characterized a novel rat model in which selective intraorbital injury of the trigeminal ophthalmic branch induces a combined NK/LSCD phenotype. Using serial functional and clinical assessments together with histopathologic, immunophenotypic, and apoptotic analyses, we demonstrate a reproducible sequence of events from sensory loss and tear dysfunction to epithelial breakdown, centripetal corneal neovascularization, conjunctivalization, stromal fibrosis, depletion of limbal stem/progenitor markers, and disruption of corneal epithelial identity. This model provides a practical platform for investigating denervation-associated ocular surface failure and the pathophysiologic interface between NK and secondary LSCD.

Methods

Animals

Twenty male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 180–220 g; SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions (20–25 °C; 60% ± 10% humidity; 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All procedures complied with the Chinese Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chengdu Bio-HT Company Limited (Approval No. 2024IINHT01R01). Following intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital at 40 mg/kg, rats were euthanized via cervical dislocation during deep anesthesia. Throughout the entire process, these methods adhere to ethical standards by prioritizing the welfare and dignity of animals.

Trigeminal ophthalmic branch (V1) injury

Rats were divided into a V1 injury (model) group or a sham group. Rats were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane by inhalation. Under sterile conditions, a curvilinear skin incision (~ 5–6 mm) was made parallel and slightly superior to the upper eyelid margin of the right eye. Following blunt dissection, the intraorbital space was approached as illustrated in the surgical diagram (Fig. 1) and anatomical illustration (Fig. 2). Critical neurovascular structures, particularly the venous sinus, were identified and carefully avoided. The V1 branch was isolated using fine microsurgical techniques. In the model group, V1 was transected at two distinct points approximately 2 mm apart using microsurgical scissors under high-magnification microscopy to ensure complete interruption. Tissues were then closed in layers. To reduce postoperative corneal desiccation and minimize exposure-related surface injury, the eyelids of the operated eye were temporarily sutured closed. In sham animals, V1 was exposed and isolated without transection. Postoperatively, animals were monitored during recovery and returned to clean cages after full recovery from anesthesia. After completion of the surgical procedure, the eyelids of the operated eye were temporarily sutured closed to reduce postoperative corneal desiccation. This measure was intended to mitigate, but not completely eliminate, exposure-related ocular surface stress.

Corneal sensitivity assessment

Corneal sensitivity was assessed before surgery and on postoperative days 7, 14, and 21. A cotton-swab test was used as a qualitative supportive assessment of the corneal touch-evoked blink response. In conscious rats, a sterile cotton tip was gently applied to the corneal surface, and the presence or absence of an observable blink response was recorded. Blink latency was not timed as an independent quantitative endpoint, and no numerical latency cutoff was used to define corneal hypoesthesia.

Quantitative corneal sensitivity was measured using a Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer [29]. The nylon filament was initially extended to 60 mm and was subsequently shortened in 5-mm increments. At each filament length, the cornea was stimulated three times. The longest filament length that elicited a blink response in at least two of the three stimulations was recorded as the corneal sensitivity threshold. If no blink response was elicited at a filament length of 5 mm, the sensitivity threshold was recorded as 0 mm.

Neovascularization measurement

Under isoflurane anesthesia, corneal neovascularization (NV) and epithelial integrity were assessed using a slit-lamp microscope (YZ5T, Chengdu 66 Vision Medical Device Co., Ltd., China). The cornea was divided into four quadrants. NV was quantified as the ratio of the longest vessel length to the corneal radius [30].

Fluorescein staining and ocular surface score

Liquid fluorescein sodium was instilled into the conjunctival sac. After ~ 30 s, epithelial damage was graded under slit-lamp illumination [30]. A maximum fluorescein score of 16 was calculated by summing quadrant scores. Ocular surface score was assessed by established criteria [31] incorporating corneal opacity, iritis, and conjunctival hyperemia; higher scores indicated more severe disease.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

On post-surgery day 21, rats were euthanized and the operated eyes were enucleated. Eyeballs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked, and incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Signals were developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. H&E staining evaluated overall morphology and epithelial thickness. Masson’s trichrome staining assessed stromal collagen deposition and fibrosis. PAS staining identified goblet cells. Apoptosis was evaluated using TUNEL staining (G1507, Servicebio, China). Immunohistochemistry was performed for anti-CD31 (GB11063-2), anti-K12 (GB111820), anti-K14 (GB11803), and anti-P63 (GB11396-1) (all Servicebio, China).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests were applied as appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.01 was considered statistically significant.

Results

V1 injury caused profound and persistent corneal hypoesthesia

The corneal touch-evoked blink response assessed qualitatively with a cotton swab was markedly impaired after V1 injury. Because this assessment was not based on a timed latency endpoint, quantitative corneal sensitivity was determined using Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometry.

Before surgery, corneal sensitivity was 60 mm in both groups and remained at 60 mm in sham eyes at all postoperative time points. In the V1-injury group, the mean corneal sensitivity thresholds were 3.5 mm on day 7, 4.0 mm on day 14, and 5.5 mm on day 21 (Fig. 3M). These findings demonstrate profound and persistent corneal hypoesthesia during the 21-day observation period. Although a modest increase in the mean threshold was observed between days 7 and 21, the values remained markedly lower than those in sham eyes. In the absence of direct structural assessment of corneal nerves, this change was not interpreted as evidence of anatomical nerve regeneration.

Progressive corneal neovascularization and stromal remodeling

Corneal neovascularization emerged at the limbus and progressed centripetally by day 7 (Fig. 3C), extending further toward the central cornea on days 14 and 21 (Fig. 3E–H). Neovascularization scores increased over time (Fig. 3J). Histologically, the limbal and peripheral cornea showed epithelial thickening, epithelial disorganization, and increased stromal cellularity. In the central cornea, fibroblast-like stromal cells and vascular lumens were observed (Fig. 4E-H). Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 4M-P) demonstrated dense and irregular collagen deposition, consistent with stromal fibrosis and scarring. These changes indicate progressive stromal remodeling following sensory denervation and ocular surface instability.

Corneal conjunctivalization and stromal fibrosis after V1 injury. In the sham group, stromal collagen fibers were regularly arranged and the corneal architecture was preserved (A–D, I–L). After V1 injury, the limbal corneal epithelium was thickened, with increased epithelial density, additional epithelial layers, disordered polarity, and numerous presumed goblet cells (E, G). In the central cornea, epithelial cells were disorganized, fibroblast-like stromal cells were increased, and vascular lumens were visible (F, H). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated dense and irregular collagen deposition consistent with stromal fibrosis and scarring (M–P). Black arrows indicate presumed goblet cells; red arrows indicate corneal neovascularization

Corneal conjunctivalization and LSCD features

PAS staining revealed abundant goblet cells within the corneal epithelium after V1 injury, indicating conjunctivalization, a hallmark of limbal barrier failure and LSCD (Fig. 5C, D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in PAS-positive goblet cells across the cornea (Fig. 5E). CD31 immunostaining identified extensive corneal neovessels extending from the subepithelial region into the stroma (Fig. 5H, I), with a significantly greater vascular area fraction than in sham eyes (Fig. 5J). These findings support the development of a secondary LSCD-like phenotype following V1 denervation, characterized by conjunctival epithelial encroachment, vascular invasion of the normally avascular cornea, and disruption of limbal epithelial barrier function.

Depletion of limbal stem/progenitor markers and disruption of corneal epithelial identity

In sham eyes, small basal cells in the limbal epithelium exhibited strong nuclear P63 staining and robust K14 expression, consistent with a limbal stem/progenitor-enriched epithelial compartment (Fig. 6A, B, E, F). After V1 transection, the population of small basal limbal cells was reduced, and both P63 and K14 staining were markedly diminished in the limbal basal epithelium (Fig. 6C, D, G, H). K12 immunostaining, a marker of differentiated corneal epithelial identity, was uniformly present across the corneal epithelium in sham eyes (Fig. 7A–D). In contrast, after V1 injury, K12 expression was lost in the limbus and peripheral cornea and was preserved mainly in the central cornea, forming a distinct K12-positive/K12-negative boundary (Fig. 7E–H). Collectively, these findings indicate disruption of limbal epithelial maintenance and altered corneal epithelial lineage identity after V1 denervation.

Increased apoptosis in the cornea after V1 injury

TUNEL staining showed few apoptotic cells in sham corneas (Fig. 8A), whereas numerous TUNEL-positive cells were present in both the corneal epithelium and stroma after V1 injury (Fig. 8B). Quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly increased apoptosis in the model group (P < 0.01; Fig. 8C). These findings are consistent with persistent epithelial stress, impaired trophic support, and active stromal remodeling in the denervated ocular surface (Fig. 3L).

Discussion

This study establishes a novel rat model in which intraorbital transection of the trigeminal ophthalmic branch (V1) induces a combined ocular surface phenotype encompassing key features of both neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) and limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD). By integrating serial in vivo assessments with endpoint histopathology, we observed a coherent sequence of denervation-associated events, including profound corneal sensory loss, reduced tear secretion, persistent epithelial compromise, progressive corneal neovascularization, conjunctivalization, stromal fibrosis, depletion of limbal stem/progenitor markers, altered corneal epithelial identity, and increased apoptosis.

A major strength of the present model is that it links anatomically targeted trigeminal sensory injury with a reproducible ocular surface phenotype. From an anatomical and pathophysiological perspective, the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve is the principal sensory source for the cornea and plays a central role not only in nociceptive transmission, but also in trophic maintenance of the corneal epithelium, epithelial wound healing, tear reflex regulation, and preservation of ocular surface homeostasis. Loss of corneal sensory input can therefore disrupt both epithelial integrity and the microenvironment of the limbal niche. In the present study, persistent corneal anesthesia documented by both the cotton-swab test and Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometry, together with tear reduction and sustained fluorescein staining, provides functional evidence that the model successfully reproduces a neurotrophic ocular surface state.

V1 injury may affect the ocular surface through several interrelated mechanisms. Direct loss of trigeminal sensory input can impair epithelial trophic support, wound healing, epithelial–stromal homeostasis, and limbal niche maintenance. In parallel, disruption of corneal afferent pathways may reduce reflex tearing and blinking, thereby increasing evaporative and exposure-related stress. The profound corneal hypoesthesia demonstrated by Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometry, together with reduced tear secretion, persistent epithelial compromise, and increased epithelial and stromal apoptosis, supports a substantial denervation-associated neurotrophic component. PAS-positive goblet-cell invasion, reduction of limbal P63 and K14 expression, peripheral loss of K12 expression, and corneal vascularization further support secondary disruption of limbal epithelial maintenance. Nevertheless, corneal opacity, neovascularization, epithelial breakdown, and fibrosis are not specific to neurotrophic keratopathy and may also be aggravated by exposure-related injury. Temporary eyelid closure was used to reduce postoperative desiccation; however, spontaneous blink frequency, palpebral exposure, and exposure duration were not quantitatively assessed, and an exposure-matched control condition was not included. The relative contributions of direct sensory denervation and exposure-related ocular surface stress therefore cannot be fully separated in the present study.

Importantly, the model did not merely show epithelial injury, but also demonstrated secondary limbal epithelial failure. From a cell biological and histopathologic standpoint, the appearance of PAS-positive goblet cells on the corneal surface indicates conjunctivalization, while the marked reduction of limbal P63 and K14 suggests impairment of the limbal stem/progenitor compartment. In parallel, the peripheral loss of K12, a differentiated corneal epithelial marker, indicates disruption of corneal epithelial lineage identity. Together with progressive CD31-positive corneal neovascularization and stromal fibrosis, these findings support the interpretation that V1 denervation destabilizes both corneal epithelial maintenance and limbal barrier function, producing an overlap phenotype with features of both NK and LSCD (Fig. 9).

The present findings are consistent with secondary limbal epithelial failure rather than direct evidence of complete anatomical depletion of all limbal stem cells. Conjunctivalization, indicated by PAS-positive goblet cells on the corneal surface, together with reduced P63 and K14 expression in the limbal basal epithelium and peripheral loss of K12 expression, suggests disruption of limbal epithelial maintenance and corneal epithelial lineage identity. These changes should therefore be described as an LSCD-like or secondary LSCD phenotype within the present model, rather than as proof of complete and irreversible limbal stem cell loss.

Mechanistically, this phenotype is likely multifactorial. Sensory denervation may impair epithelial trophic support directly, alter blinking and reflex tearing, reduce epithelial wound healing capacity, and perturb the limbal microenvironment [32]. The observed neovascularization may also reflect altered neurovascular balance after denervation, as corneal nerves and blood vessels normally maintain a mutually inhibitory relationship in the avascular corneal milieu. Once this balance is disrupted, vascular ingrowth, stromal remodeling, and conjunctival epithelial encroachment may together promote progressive ocular surface failure.

At the same time, the current model should be interpreted with appropriate caution. We agree that V1 transection may influence not only corneal trophic innervation but also reflex pathways involved in blinking and tear secretion. Therefore, not all observed pathology should be attributed exclusively to a pure neurotrophic ulcer mechanism. To reduce postoperative desiccation, the eyelids of the operated eye were temporarily sutured closed. Nevertheless, a contribution from exposure-related ocular surface stress cannot be completely excluded. For this reason, we consider the present model best interpreted as a denervation-associated ocular surface disease model with overlapping NK and LSCD features, rather than as a model in which every component is mechanistically isolated from exposure-related factors.

Compared with previously reported approaches such as intracranial trigeminal manipulation, trigeminal ganglion injury, ciliary nerve injury, or chemically induced denervation, the present intraorbital V1 transection strategy avoids craniotomy and directly targets the relevant peripheral sensory pathway [11, 21–35]. This may reduce procedural complexity while preserving robust phenotype induction. In addition, the simultaneous emergence of neurotrophic epithelial dysfunction, corneal vascularization, limbal marker depletion, and conjunctivalization increases the translational relevance of the model for studying clinically overlapping disease states rather than isolated corneal denervation alone.

The present study was limited to a 21-day observation period. This interval was sufficient to demonstrate the development of profound corneal hypoesthesia and a pronounced ocular surface phenotype with overlapping features of neurotrophic keratopathy and secondary limbal epithelial failure. However, it is insufficient to define the complete chronic natural history of the model, the long-term durability of the phenotype, or the timing and extent of corneal nerve regeneration. The modest increase in the Cochet–Bonnet threshold between days 7 and 21 may reflect partial functional recovery, but this interpretation cannot be confirmed without direct assessment of corneal nerve density and morphology. Longer-term longitudinal studies incorporating in vivo confocal microscopy, corneal nerve immunostaining, or quantitative nerve morphometry will be required to characterize reinnervation, chronic remodeling, and long-term model stability. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as characterizing the establishment and early-to-intermediate evolution of the phenotype within 21 days, rather than a fully validated chronic disease course.

This study has several limitations. First, the cotton-swab test was a qualitative supportive assessment and did not provide a timed measurement of blink latency; quantitative sensitivity was therefore determined by Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometry. Second, although temporary eyelid closure was used to reduce corneal desiccation, exposure-related ocular surface stress could not be completely excluded. Spontaneous blink frequency, palpebral exposure, and exposure duration were not quantified, and an exposure-matched control condition was not included. Third, corneal nerve density and morphology were not directly assessed. Finally, the 21-day observation period does not establish the chronic natural history, long-term stability, or reinnervation characteristics of the model. These questions require dedicated longer-term investigation.

Despite these limitations, the combined functional, histologic, and phenotypic evidence supports the value of this model as a practical platform for studying the interface between corneal sensory denervation and limbal epithelial failure. This may be particularly useful for evaluating therapies aimed at restoring corneal innervation, improving epithelial trophic support, limiting conjunctivalization and neovascularization, or preserving the limbal epithelial stem/progenitor niche.

Conclusion

Intraorbital V1 injury induces a reproducible denervation-associated ocular surface disease phenotype characterized by profound corneal hypoesthesia, tear dysfunction, persistent epithelial compromise, conjunctivalization, altered limbal and corneal epithelial marker expression, corneal neovascularization, stromal remodeling, and increased apoptosis. These findings support overlapping features of neurotrophic keratopathy and secondary limbal epithelial failure within the 21-day observation period. The model may therefore provide a practical platform for investigating early-to-intermediate interactions between corneal sensory denervation and limbal epithelial dysfunction. Further longitudinal studies are required to determine its chronic stability and to characterize corneal nerve regeneration.
