Impact of bispectral index monitoring on dose requirement of dexmedetomidine during total parotidectomy under general anesthesia without muscle relaxants
Authors: Sunil Rajan, Maharnab Bhuyan, Gayathri Sreekumar, Mahesh Madhu, Lokeshshiva Arul, Roniya Ann Roy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:For facial nerve monitoring during parotidectomy, neuromuscular blockers should be avoided. Assessment of depth of anesthesia based on clinical signs or end-tidal anesthetic gas concentration is unreliable. We compared intraoperative dexmedetomidine requirement with and without bispectral index (BIS) monitoring in patients undergoing total parotidectomy under general anesthesia (GA) without muscle relaxants.Material and Methods:This was a prospective, randomized study conducted in 60 patients undergoing total parotidectomy under GA with nerve conduction studies. Following induction and intubation, all patients received dexmedetomidine 1 µg/kg bolus. In Group A, BIS values were continuously monitored. Following initial bolus, only when the BIS value was >50, dexmedetomidine infusion was restarted at 0.5 µg/kg/h and adjusted to maintain BIS between 40 and 50. In Group B, dexmedetomidine 1 µg/kg bolus was given, followed by 0.5 µg/kg/h initially and infusion. Infusion rate was titrated based on hemodynamic variables and patient immobility. If patients in any group moved intraoperatively, propofol 0.5 mg/kg, not >30 mg, was given, and dose of dexmedetomidine infusion was increased by 0.1 µg/kg/h increments to a maximum dose of 0.7 µg/kg/h.Results:Rate of dexmedetomidine infusion was significantly lower in Group A compared to Group B (0.058 ± 0.011 vs. 0.547 ± 0.133 µg/kg/h). Extubation time was significantly lower in Group A (14.40 ± 8.845 vs. 30.27 ± 9.903 min). Hemodynamic responses, sevoflurane and propofol consumption, incidence of intraoperative patient immobility, hypotension, and use of vasopressors were comparable in both groups.Conclusions:Use of BIS monitoring in patients undergoing total parotidectomy under GA without muscle relaxants resulted in significant reduction in intraoperative dexmedetomidine consumption with significantly shorter extubation time.

Introduction

The risk of facial nerve injury during parotidectomy is high, which can be prevented to a great extent by use of intraoperative facial nerve monitoring. However, for effective nerve conduction studies, neuromuscular blockers should be avoided intraoperatively. Dexmedetomidine 1 µg/kg body weight followed by 0.3–0.7 µg/kg/h infusion is considered as the standard care for providing adequate depth of general anesthesia in the absence of muscle relaxants.[1] But relying entirely on the clinical signs or end-tidal anesthetic gas concentration for titration of depth of anesthesia may result in either overdosing or underdosing of anesthetic agents. Monitoring bispectral index (BIS) intraoperatively provides an objective assessment of the depth of anesthesia and helps in titration and optimization of anesthetic dosing. Though BIS monitoring is used in various surgeries, its usefulness in parotid surgeries remains unexplored. We hypothesized that during total parotidectomy with intraoperative facial nerve conduction study, the use of BIS monitoring might help to reduce the dose requirement of dexmedetomidine infusion.

The primary objective of our study was to compare the intraoperative dexmedetomidine requirement with and without BIS monitoring in patients undergoing total parotidectomy under general anesthesia without the use of muscle relaxants. The secondary objectives were to compare intraoperative patient immobility, hemodynamic responses, sevoflurane and propofol consumption, need for intervention if patient moves, incidence of hypotension, use of vasopressors, and time taken for extubation in both groups.

Material and Methods

This was a prospective, randomized, single-blinded study conducted from March 2024 to July 2024. The study was initiated after obtaining institutional ethical committee clearance (IEC-AIMS-2024-ANES-034, dated 06-03-2024) and was registered in Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2024/03/064505). Patients with parotid tumors undergoing total parotidectomy under general anesthesia requiring intraoperative nerve conduction studies were recruited in the trial. Patients aged 18–70 years, of American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA PS) class I–III, were included. The study excluded participants who had a resting heart rate of less than 60 beats per minute, were taking beta-blockers, had any type of heart block, were allergic to dexmedetomidine, or needed nasal or fiberoptic intubation.

All procedures of the clinical trials involving human participants in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee, as well as the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and its later 2013 amendments and good clinical practice were followed in our study. Written informed consent was taken from all patients for participation in the study and use of their clinical data for research and educational purposes.

As there were no similar studies published earlier, we conducted a pilot study with 20 patients in two groups to calculate the sample size. In one group, dexmedetomidine infusion was titrated to maintain BIS values between 40 and 50, while in the other group, dexmedetomidine dose was maintained taking into account patient immobility and hemodynamic status. Depth of anesthesia was ensured with end-tidal sevoflurane 1 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) in air oxygen mixture in both groups. Based on the mean and standard deviation of dexmedetomidine infusion dose adjusted with and without BIS monitoring (0.137 ± 0.075 vs. 0.561 ± 0.116 µg/kg/h) with 99% confidence and 90% power, the minimum sample size was calculated to be less than five per group. However, during our study period, we recruited 60 patients with 30 in each group.

The patients were randomly allotted to either Group A or Group B using computer-generated random sequence of numbering. Concealment of allocation was ensured by sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Following adequate fasting, patients in both groups received intravenous glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg, fentanyl 2 µg/kg, and midazolam 2 mg and anesthesia was induced with propofol 1.5–2 mg/kg till loss of response to verbal commands. After ensuring mask ventilation, suxamethonium 2 mg/kg was given and the patients were orally intubated with 6.5 mm internal diameter (ID) for females or 7.0 mm ID cuffed flexometallic endotracheal tube for males. The endotracheal tube was secured and an oropharyngeal airway was placed to prevent intraoperative biting of the tube. The mouth was then covered using a transparent adhesive hydrocolloid dressing to avoid interference to the surgical field. Patients in both groups were mechanically ventilated with sevoflurane in air oxygen mixture (1:1) at a fresh gas flow of 1 l/min. Sevoflurane vaporizer dial settings were kept between 2% and 3% to maintain 1 MAC end-tidal, age-adjusted sevoflurane levels. Positive pressure ventilation was used to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) levels between 25 and 30 mmHg. Patients were mechanically ventilated with a higher respiratory rate, aiming for a lower EtCO2 to prevent spontaneous respiratory efforts intraoperatively. Patients in both groups received fentanyl 0.5 µg/kg intravenously every 30 min during the surgery.

In Group A, BIS monitor (Covidien BIS Complete Monitoring System 194-0405 Rev C; Covidien IIc, MA, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) was used to monitor the depth of anesthesia using BIS electrodes (Medtronic BIS Quatro Sensor 186-0106’ M/s Covidien IIc, USA). Patients of Group A received dexmedetomidine (Dexem™; Themis Medicare Limited, Vapi, Gujarat, India) 1 µg/kg bolus over 10 min, and the BIS values were continuously monitored. Following the initial bolus, only when the BIS value was >50, dexmedetomidine infusion was restarted at a rate of 0.5 µg/kg/h. The dose was then adjusted to maintain the BIS values between 40 and 50. If the BIS value remained >50, the infusion rate of dexmedetomidine was increased by 0.1 µg/kg/h increments to a maximum of 0.7 µg/kg/h. With BIS values <40, the infusion rate was reduced by 0.1 µg/kg/h increments and even stopped if the BIS values remained persistently <40 in Group A. In Group B, dexmedetomidine 1 µg/kg bolus was given over 10 min, followed by 0.5 µg/kg/h infusion initially. The infusion rate was titrated based on hemodynamic variables like heart rate, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and patient immobility. Continuous electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry, noninvasive blood pressure, EtCO2, end-tidal sevoflurane, and temperature were monitored intraoperatively.

Tachycardia (heart rate >100/min), hypertension (increase in MAP >20% from baseline), and/or patient movements were initially managed as follows. If patients in any group moved intraoperatively, propofol 0.5 mg/kg not exceeding 30 mg as a bolus was given. If any patient required more than one propofol bolus in 10 min, dexmedetomidine infusion was allowed to be used at 0.5 µg/kg/h in Group A and the dose of dexmedetomidine infusion was increased by 0.1 µg/kg/h increments in Group B. The maximum dose of dexmedetomidine used was 0.7 µg/kg/h in both groups. The minimum dose of dexmedetomidine infusion in Group A could be zero, whereas in Group B, it was 0.2 µg/kg/h.

Fall in MAP (>20% from the baseline value) was considered as hypotension and was initially managed with 250 ml bolus of Ringer’s lactate. If hypotension persisted even 5 min after fluid bolus, ephedrine 3 mg or phenylephrine 50 µg bolus was administered depending on the heart rate. Generally, patients with heart rate <60/min received ephedrine and those with heart rate >60/min were given phenylephrine. If hypotension was persistent, dexmedetomidine dose was reduced by 0.1 µg/kg/h. If any patient required more than four doses of vasopressors in 30 min, noradrenaline infusion was started at 0.05 µg/kg/min and titrated to a maximum of 0.2 μg/kg/min. Bradycardia with heart rate <50/min was treated with glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg or atropine 0.6 mg as appropriate.

Facial nerve identification was done to avoid nerve injury with the help of a peripheral nerve stimulator using single twitch current strength 0.4–0.5 mA. Any structures resembling facial nerve branches were directly stimulated. A positive identification of facial nerve was made if stimulation elicited a visible twitch in the facial muscles. Intraoperative facial nerve conduction study was done in all patients in our study. Dexmedetomidine infusion was stopped 30 min before the end of surgery, once the parotid gland was removed. Sevoflurane was discontinued at the end of skin suturing. Patients were extubated when they were awake and obeying commands with regular spontaneous breathing with adequate tidal volume. Time taken for extubation was noted as the time from stopping sevoflurane till the endotracheal tube was removed from the oral cavity. Total dose of dexmedetomidine, sevoflurane, and propofol used, number of patient movements, number of times propofol bolus was given, interventions used to treat hypotension and bradycardia, and the time taken for extubation were documented. The heart rate and MAP before induction, after induction, 1, 3, and 5 min after intubation, then every 15 min till the end of surgery were also noted. Incidence of intraoperative awareness was assessed on the first postoperative day, 24 h after surgery. All patients were quizzed about any recall of events that occurred under anesthesia.

To test the statistically significant difference in the mean dosage of dexmedetomidine, number of patient movements, hemodynamic parameters, and time taken for extubation between the two groups, independent sample test was applied. To test the statistically significant difference in the proportion of hypotension and use of vasopressors between the two groups, Pearson’s Chi-squared test was used. To test changes in hemodynamic parameters between the two groups, independent sample t-test was applied for normal data and Mann–Whitney U test was applied for skewed data. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Version 20.0 for Windows (IBM Corporation Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

For statistical analysis, the data of 60 patients were used [Figure 1]. The demographic variables, except the gender distribution and the distribution of ASA PS, were comparable in both groups [Table 1]. The duration of surgery, sevoflurane and total propofol consumption, as well as the volume of intravenous fluid (IVF) used were comparable in both groups. Total dexmedetomidine consumption was significantly higher in Group B (79.35 ± 26.469 vs. 147.47 ± 52.040 µg, P < 0.001). Dexmedetomidine infusion rate was significantly lower in Group A compared to Group B (0.058 ± 0.011 vs. 0.547 ± 0.133 µg/kg/h, P < 0.0001) [Table 2]. Extubation time was significantly lower in Group A (14.40 ± 8.845 vs. 30.27 ± 9.903 min, P < 0.0001) [Table 2].

Subgroup analysis of both groups with equal number of females and males (13 males and 13 females in each group) showed that total dexmedetomidine consumption rate was significantly lower in Group A compared to Group B (76.81 ± 19.372 vs. 145.09 ± 49.348 µg, P < 0.001). Dexmedetomidine infusion rate was also significantly lower in Group A than Group B (0.053 ± 0.032 vs. 0.551 ± 0.027 µg/kg/h, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Most of the published trials had not investigated BIS-guided titration of dexmedetomidine requirement during surgeries with nerve monitoring. Instead the effect of addition of dexmedetomidine on the dose reduction of other agents like propofol,[23] sevoflurane,[456] and isoflurane[7] while maintaining an optimal BIS was assessed in most of them.

We observed that dexmedetomidine infusion of 0.058 ± 0.01 µg/kg/h was only needed to maintain BIS values between 40 and 50 during total parotidectomy. This dosage was significantly lower than many of the commonly followed regimens of dexmedetomidine infusion, like 0.5 µg/kg/h,[4] 0.4 µg/kg/h,[56] and 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h.[8] The drastic reduction in dexmedetomidine requirement shown in our study compared to that in studies by Magalhães et al.,[4] Quan et al.,[5] and Nunes and Cavalcante[6] could be because in these studies, the authors maintained a constant infusion rate of dexmedetomidine while the dose of volatile agent was adjusted based on the BIS values. However, in our study, the dose of sevoflurane was kept constant at 1 MAC and the dose of dexmedetomidine was titrated.

Though BIS values between 40 and 60 provide an optimal depth to prevent anesthesia awareness,[9] we chose to maintain BIS in the range 40–50 during our study. We decided on a BIS value of 50 instead of 60 to restart dexmedetomidine infusion in Group A, to make sure that patients did not start to move due to a lower depth of anesthesia in the absence of muscle relaxants. If we had waited for a BIS value of 60 to intervene, the chances of patient movement would have been high.

For calculation of intraoperative dexmedetomidine requirement, we did not include the initial bolus of 1 µg/kg as the dose used was same in both groups. We maintained the concentration of sevoflurane at 1 MAC to reduce the possibility of intraoperative patient movements to the minimum. Moreover, by keeping a constant MAC, finding out the exact effect of BIS monitoring on the dose requirement of dexmedetomidine was easy. Since neither the duration of surgery nor sevoflurane consumption differed significantly between the groups, the dexmedetomidine dose established in our study most likely reflected an accurate equivalent to BIS-guided titration.

Various trials have investigated different modalities to ensure patient immobility during parotidectomy with propofol infusion,[10] dexmedetomidine infusion,[1] and supplemental transtracheal block.[10] Dexmedetomidine infusion was found to be superior to propofol infusion with respect to shorter extubation time during surgeries requiring intraoperative nerve stimulation studies.[11] Though transtracheal block before induction of anesthesia was shown to facilitate better hemodynamic stability and patient immobility intraoperatively by many studies,[1011] we did not adapt that technique to ensure better patient comfort, as the block has to be given when the patient is awake.

The hemodynamic effects of dexmedetomidine, like bradycardia and hypotension, are dose dependent.[12] Hence, titration of the dose to the safe minimum level should presumably reduce intraoperative hemodynamic side effects and the use of inotropes and/or vasopressors. In our study, we had observed a lower heart rate in Group B compared to Group A. However, the difference was not found to have any statistical significance, except toward the end of surgery. This could be because of the higher dexmedetomidine dose the patients in Group B were receiving. However, MAP remained comparable in both groups. There was no significantly higher incidence of hypotension in Group B as well. A prompt and accurate titration of dexmedetomidine dose during our study might have helped to avert development of hemodynamic side effects of dexmedetomidine requiring intervention. However, the extubation time was significantly prolonged in Group B. This could be the result of the added sedative effects of the volatile agent, along with the higher dexmedetomidine consumption in Group B.

In our study, most patients in both groups had movements before surgery while positioning, that is, during neck extension and head rotation to the contralateral side, which could be the reason for comparable patient movements in both groups. This also explains one of the gross findings of our study that in spite of using a higher dose of dexmedetomidine, there was no decrease in the number of movements. In Group A, 86% patients had moved and were managed with a single bolus of propofol, which was mostly during positioning. Movements during positioning before surgery could be due to delay in attaining adequate end-tidal inhalational agent concentration. However, there were minimal to absent patient movements intraoperatively in both groups. It was also observed that 80% (n = 24) patients in Group A did not require dexmedetomidine infusion after the initial bolus of 1 µg/kg as the BIS values remained <50. But in Group B, all patients continued to receive dexmedetomidine throughout surgery, which led to a significantly higher consumption.

Based on our observation that dexmedetomidine 0.058 ± 0.011 µg/kg/h was only needed to maintain BIS values between 40 and 50 during total parotidectomy, it could be inferred that intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine at 0.06 µg/kg/h after an initial bolus of 1 µg/kg, while maintaining end-tidal sevoflurane at 1 MAC with liberal use of opioids, could be enough to provide optimal depth of anesthesia. While providing general anesthesia without using muscle relaxants, use of a reliable depth of anesthesia monitor is mandatory. However, many a time, it may not be available. Based on the results of our study, it is possible to explore the feasibility of reducing the usual dose recommendations of dexmedetomidine during surgeries with nerve conduction studies when BIS monitoring is unavailable.

Female gender has shown to have preponderance for development of intraoperative awareness and faster recovery from general anesthesia.[13] Therefore, to rule out the possibility of gender bias in our study due to a higher number of females in Group A, we recalculated the total dexmedetomidine consumption and infusion rate in both groups with 13 males and 13 females in each group. It showed that total dexmedetomidine consumption rate was significantly lower in Group A compared to Group B (76.81 ± 19.372 vs. 145.09 ± 49.348 µg, P < 0.001). Similarly, the dexmedetomidine infusion rate was also significantly lower in Group A (0.053 ± 0.032 vs. 0.551 ± 0.027 µg/kg/h, P < 0.001). These findings support the observations of lesser dexmedetomidine consumption and infusion rate in Group A of our study.

Facial nerve monitoring was used for all patients in our study, and none developed nerve injury. Though patients in both groups received significantly different doses of dexmedetomidine, there was no difference in the findings of nerve monitoring in both groups. Intraoperatively, we kept oropharyngeal airways in all patients to avoid biting of endotracheal tube. Therefore, relatively smaller sized tubes were used to reduce the possibility of tongue edema. Hypocapnia can lead to reduced stimulation of central chemoreceptors, resulting in diminished respiratory drive.[14] Therefore, we hyperventilated our patients and the EtCO2 was maintained between 25 and 30 mmHg. To ensure adequate intraoperative pain relief and to avoid patient movements in the absence of muscle relaxants secondary to inadequate analgesia, fentanyl 0.5 µg/kg was administered intravenously to all patients every 30 min during the surgery.

The major drawback of our study was that it was a single-blinded trial. As BIS monitor was used in one group, double blinding was not possible. The gender distribution was not comparable in our study as majority of patients in Group B were females.

Conclusion

Use of BIS monitoring in patients undergoing total parotidectomy under general anesthesia with facial nerve monitoring resulted in a significant reduction in intraoperative dexmedetomidine consumption with significantly shorter extubation time.
