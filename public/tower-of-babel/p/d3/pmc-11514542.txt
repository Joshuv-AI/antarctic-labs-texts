Clinical application of ctDNA in early diagnosis, treatment and prognosis of patients with non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Shenyu Zhu, Rongqian Wu, Xiangjin Liu, Bin Xie, Chunfa Xie, Shulin Li, Zhicheng Wu, Zuxiong Zhang, Zhixian Tang, Liang Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) being the most common type. As understanding of precise treatment options for NSCLC deepens, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a potential biomarker that has become a research hotspot and may represent a new approach for the individualized diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC. This article reviews the applications of ctDNA for the early screening of patients with NSCLC, guiding targeted therapy and immunotherapy, evaluating chemotherapy and postoperative efficacy, assessing prognosis and monitoring recurrence. With the in-depth study of the pathogenesis of NSCLC, plasma ctDNA may become an indispensable part of the precise treatment of NSCLC, which has great clinical application prospects.Keywords: : circulating tumor DNA, effect monitoring, immunotherapy, minimal residual disease, non-small cell lung cancer, targeted therapy

Background

Lung cancer has the highest worldwide morbidity and mortality rates of all malignancies. According to the latest global cancer data released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer in 2020, there were approximately 19.3 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer related deaths worldwide in 2020. Among them, the new cases of lung cancer are second only to breast cancer, accounting for 11.4% of all new cancer cases, whereas the number of deaths from lung cancer ranks first, accounting for 18% of all cancer deaths [1,2]. Most patients with lung cancer are histologically diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for 85% of all lung cancer cases [3,4]. Adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma are the two most common histological subtypes of NSCLC [5]. Surgery remains the cornerstone of early-stage NSCLC treatment, often combined with adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy to reduce postoperative recurrence rates [6,7]. However, even after R0 tumor resection, 30–50% of patients with NSCLC experience relapse [8–10]. Some patients considered successfully treated with surgery might have developed micrometastases before the surgery. Unfortunately, detecting micrometastases in current clinical practice is challenging, although they could persist during surgery or postoperative adjuvant therapy, becoming potential sources of local or distant metastases [11]. One study found that a worse clinical NSCLC stage correlated with a lower postoperative survival rate [12]. Liang et al. reported that timely postoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy or targeted therapy, improved the long-term survival rate of patients with advanced NSCLC [13]. However, effective means for diagnosing and monitoring the recurrence of NSCLC are lacking in clinical practice. Traditional tumor markers (such as carcinoembryonic antigen, neuron-specific enolase and progastrin-releasing peptide), imaging approaches (computed tomography and positron emission tomography-computed tomography) and pathological biopsy analysis all have their limitations. In light of this, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in the peripheral blood has gained increasing attention for the precise treatment of NSCLC.

Biological characteristics of ctDNA

In 1948, Mandel and Metais first reported the presence of cell-free circulating DNA (cfDNA) in the blood [14]. This type of DNA is actively secreted into the blood by apoptotic or necrotic cells [15]. It is highly fragmented double-stranded DNA with a length of approximately 167 bp and a half-life between 16 min and 2.5 h [16]. The concentration of cfDNA in the blood of healthy individuals typically fluctuates between 1 and 10 ng/ml [17,18]. Leon et al. first found increased concentrations of cfDNA in blood samples from patients with cancer, compared with cancer-free individuals [19]. Blood cfDNA levels in patients with cancer are typically 4–40-times higher than those in unaffected individuals [20,21]. An analysis of ctDNA based on an earlier study of cfDNA reported that ctDNA is a component of cfDNA, with a length of approximately 134–144 bp. This ctDNA is a fragment released into the blood by tumor cells, mainly through apoptosis or necrosis (Figure 1) [22,23]. Chen et al. showed that the half-life of blood ctDNA in patients with NSCLC is approximately 35 min [24]. The concentration of blood ctDNA in patients with NSCLC is associated with tumor diameter and clinical stage [25,26]. In addition, the genetic information present in ctDNA, such as chromosome rearrangements, gene mutations and DNA methylation, corresponds to that of the source tumor [27–30]. Therefore, tumor-specific genetic information carried by ctDNA is helpful for the early diagnosis of malignant tumors, guiding drug selection to treat patients with cancer, evaluating postoperative efficacy and chemotherapy effects, making prognoses and monitoring recurrence.

After entering blood vessels, tumor cells travel to multiple organs such as the bone, liver, brain and adrenal gland via the circulatory system. Tumor cells in the blood release circulating tumor DNA following necrosis and apoptosis.

Detecting ctDNA

Although ctDNA carries tumor-specific genetic information and is a specific biomarker, its general detection is challenging owing to its low concentration in peripheral blood and short half-life. Four traditional first-generation sequencing methods have been used for the early detection of ctDNA: RNA–DNA hybridization, the ethidium bromide method, counter immunoelectrophoresis and the diphenylamine method. Owing to the extremely low ctDNA content in peripheral blood, it was difficult to detect using previous-generation sequencing technology, with positive results only seen in the peripheral blood of a small number of patients with advanced tumors and large tumor burdens. With the development of new molecular biology technologies, current detection methods for plasma ctDNA are mainly based on next-generation sequencing (NGS) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Figure 2) [31]. NGS detection methods include whole-genome sequencing, tagged amplification deep sequencing (TAM-Seq) and targeted deep sequencing. PCR detection methods include bead emulsion amplification magnetics, droplet digital PCR, amplification refractory mutation system-PCR and real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR [32–35]. NGS is currently recognized as the most effective analytical DNA detection method. By increasing the depth of sequencing, its detection sensitivity can reach 0.01% or more [36,37].

Detection of circulating tumor DNA.

In current second-generation sequencing, cancer personalized profiling by deep sequencing (CAPP-Seq) has achieved even higher sensitivity and specificity at a lower cost, making it more suitable for clinical applications [38–40]. TAM-Seq has higher sensitivity and specificity for tumors and is more affordable than whole-exome sequencing. Unknown mutations can be detected by both whole-genome sequencing and whole-exome sequencing. CAPP-Seq and TAM-Seq are primarily used to detect known mutations. However, NGS technology must overcome challenges related to obtaining high-quality samples, high detection costs and difficult data processing, before it can achieve more widespread use in clinical practice. Although there are many methods for extracting and analyzing plasma ctDNA, standardized detection methods and guidelines are lacking, which hinders the routine implementation of plasma ctDNA detection in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of patients with NSCLC. To this end, a significant number of standardized case studies are needed as soon as possible to aid the development of unified and standardized detection methods and guidelines.

Clinical use of ctDNA detection for the diagnosis & treatment of NSCLC

Early screening & diagnosis of lung cancer

Early interventions for lung cancer can significantly improve patient survival. The 5-year average survival rate in patients with early-stage lung cancer is 91%, while that in patients with advanced lung cancer is 26% [41], indicating that early screening and diagnosis of lung cancer is critical for improving patient quality of life. The widespread use of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) for the clinical screening of lung cancer has led to a notable increase in the detection rate of pulmonary nodules. However, LDCT screening is currently overused in asymptomatic patients who have never smoked or quit smoking many years earlier, resulting in a false-positive rate of 96% [42]. Incorporating ctDNA into lung cancer screening may help improve these detection rates. Chen et al. showed that the detection of ctDNA mutations in peripheral blood is highly feasible for the early screening and diagnosis of lung cancer [43]. Newman et al. reported a 47% detection rate of plasma ctDNA in patients with clinical stage I NSCLC [44]. Additionally, other studies have reported that plasma ctDNA concentration is associated with tumor diameter and clinical stage [24,25]. Abbosh et al. observed that the detection rate of ctDNA differed among the different pathological types of NSCLC [25], with those in squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma being 97 and 19%, respectively. One study combined methylation levels of plasma ctDNA with CT imaging of pulmonary nodules to develop an artificial intelligence-based analysis that can identify malignant pulmonary nodules. This approach is expected to greatly improve the detection rate of early lung cancer [45–47]. Plasma ctDNA detection is minimally invasive and suitable for a wide range of patients. In the future, we expect to further optimize screening for early lung cancer by integrating plasma ctDNA detection and LDCT.

Guiding immunotherapy & targeted therapy in patients with NSCLC

ctDNA & immunotherapy

Although immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICS)-based immunotherapy is a new treatment method that has shown significant efficacy for the treatment of patients with NSCLC, current data indicate that this efficacy does vary significantly. Thus, the populations that benefit most from these treatments merit further study. Plasma ctDNA mutation levels are associated with TMB [48–50], which is currently recognized as a clinical biomarker for evaluating the efficacy of immunotherapy. Therefore, plasma ctDNA, as a new biomarker, has great significance for evaluating the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with NSCLC [51–53]. A recent study discovered that patients with locally advanced unresectable NSCLC who had higher ctDNA mmVAF and received a combination of chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy exhibited better outcomes compared with those who did not receive immunotherapy [54]. The study confirmed the significant role of ctDNA in guiding immunotherapy for locally advanced NSCLC, specifically in terms of improving progression-free survival (PFS). A clinical trial demonstrated that plasma ctDNA may be a dynamic biomarker in patients with lung cancer receiving immunotherapy [55]. Plasma ctDNA levels decrease significantly after immunotherapy, indicating their efficacy in many patients with NSCLC. By contrast, no significant change after treatment indicates a poor response to immunotherapy [56–58]. Wang et al. reported that reduced plasma ctDNA levels in the early stages of immunotherapy have a more significant effect on immunotherapy [59]. The detection of dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA can be used to evaluate the effect of immunotherapy earlier than traditional imaging studies [60,61], which can allow for earlier adjustment of clinical treatment strategies. Moreover, detecting dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA levels can better identify pseudoprogression following immunotherapy, compared with traditional imaging examinations [60,62,63]. Despite these advantages for evaluating the efficacy of immunotherapy and identifying pseudoprogression, sufficient clinical research data are still lacking to establish plasma ctDNA as a biomarker for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy and identifying pseudoprogression. Further clinical studies are required to validate its potential role in these areas.

ctDNA & targeted therapy

The survival benefit of targeted therapy for patients with NSCLC has been verified in clinical trials and can significantly reduce the mortality of these patients, as well as improve their long-term quality of life [64]. A prospective non-randomized clinical trial showed that ctDNA-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (EGFR) genotyping aids clinical decision-making in patients with advanced lung cancer who are clinically diagnosed but whose pathological status is unknown [65]. A Phase II clinical trial showed that, in patients with operable stage IIA and IIIB cell lung cancer after preoperative targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy, the plasma ctDNA level after preoperative neoadjuvant therapy was associated with a postoperative pathological response [66]. Therefore, for patients with NSCLC who consider preoperative targeted therapy neoadjuvant, the optimal surgical timing may be evaluated based on changes in plasma ctDNA levels. Analyzing plasma ctDNA gene mutations can be used to monitor driving mutations and possible evolutionary directions of tumors following targeted therapy, to predict which targeted therapy will be most effective [67–71]. For patients with advanced NSCLC who are not eligible for surgery, there may be complications in finding mutation targets by biopsies of tumor tissues. Thus, the detection of peripheral blood ctDNA carries the advantage of being simple and noninvasive.

NGS of plasma ctDNA in patients with advanced NSCLC that is resistant to targeted therapy has revealed that 88% of these patients typically have at least one gene mutation and that 50% of them have at least one feasible site for targeted therapy [72,73], indicating that the sequencing of plasma ctDNA can be used to identify appropriate drugs for targeted therapies. Phallen et al. reported significantly prolonged PFS in patients who responded to targeted therapy, and that plasma ctDNA was almost completely removed following treatment [68]. By contrast, the PFS of patients who did not respond to targeted therapy was significantly shorter and there was no significant change in plasma ctDNA following treatment, indicating that the effect of targeted therapy can be evaluated based on dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA. With the increasing use of targeted drugs, drug resistance has become increasingly prevalent. Detecting mutations in multiple genes and sites of plasma ctDNA using high-throughput sequencing is beneficial for uncovering mechanisms behind resistance to targeted drug therapies. A prospective single-arm study found that for patients with advanced unresectable NSCLC, the therapeutic effect can be monitored by changes in plasma ctDNA. This study found that the addition of apatinib improved the use time of the first generation of epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and the duration was better than that of the third generation of epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors [74]. Lee et al. performed a sequence analysis of plasma ctDNA before and after treatment and reported a new kinase fusion following targeted therapy, which may represent a mechanism leading to drug resistance [75]. Consequently, detecting plasma ctDNA holds the potential for guiding treatment termination and determining recovery times after targeted therapy, to prevent the emergence of new mutations and thus influence the efficacy of this treatment approach.

Evaluation of chemotherapy efficacy

Adjuvant chemotherapy for advanced NSCLC can significantly improve the long-term survival of patients. However, the economic burden on patients and the excessive medical treatments that are necessary for this approach are becoming increasingly evident. Therefore, prompt evaluation of the efficacy of chemotherapy, and corresponding adjustments to chemotherapy regimens, are particularly important and highlight the goal of precision medicine. Currently, imaging examinations are commonly used in clinical practice to evaluate the efficacy of chemotherapy. However, these have long lags and low sensitivities/specificities. These are areas in which plasma ctDNA detection carries significant advantages [76–78]. Ramirez et al. reported a significantly longer survival time in patients with lung cancers that were positive for methylation of the 14-3-3σ gene following platinum-based chemotherapy compared with the survival time in patients without this methylation. This finding indicates that deep sequencing of plasma ctDNA methylation has the potential to identify specific gene methylations that can impact the survival and prognosis of patients with lung cancer who are undergoing chemotherapy [79]. Zhang et al. measured the plasma methylation levels of Ras-associated domain-containing protein 1 (RASSF1A) and adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) in patients with lung cancer before and 24 h after chemotherapy and reported significantly higher methylation levels of both genes after cisplatin chemotherapy [80]. If tumor cells are sensitive to chemotherapy drugs, they get destroyed and their DNA is released into the blood [81], resulting in increased plasma ctDNA levels. This ctDNA release suggests that the increased plasma RASSF1A and APC gene methylation levels observed 24 h after chemotherapy indicate that these patients were responsive to cisplatin chemotherapy. Therefore, monitoring dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA methylation levels may be an effective index for evaluating the effects of chemotherapy.

Evaluation of postoperative efficacy

The effects of surgery in patients with NSCLC are generally evaluated based on the results of histopathological examination of intraoperative frozen specimens of the tumor resection margin. However, patients have a risk of postoperative recurrence. Many studies have found that the dynamic changes of plasma ctDNA during perioperative period can be used to evaluate the postoperative efficacy of NSCLC patients (Table 1). The detection of minimal residual lesions based on plasma ctDNA can predict the recurrence of NSCLC during the perioperative period [82,83]. Prospective studies have shown that plasma ctDNA status on the third day after surgery can predict lung cancer recurrence [84–86]. A clinical application study that used NGS to evaluate the dynamic recurrence risk of ctDNA following the resection of NSCLC tumors and the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy found that patients who were ctDNA-positive postoperatively and those who were ctDNA-positive following adjuvant chemotherapy were significantly associated with worse relapse-free survival outcomes [87]. A number of studies have demonstrated the value of plasma ctDNA detection in predicting the benefit of adjuvant therapy and the risk of postoperative NSCLC recurrence [86,88]. Patients with ctDNA gene mutations or higher postoperative plasma ctDNA levels have been found to have higher recurrence rates [31,89]. In one clinical trial, 14 of 24 patients with operable early-stage NSCLC experienced recurrence following surgery. Of these, 13 had detectable plasma levels of ctDNA before the recurrence of the disease [25]. The results of these studies indicate value in the evaluation of postoperative efficacy in patients with NSCLC by detecting dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA during the perioperative period, to allow for earlier clinical interventions and improve long-term survival rates. However, relatively few multicenter prospective studies have been performed in this vein, so more clinical studies are needed to verify these findings.

Assessing prognosis & monitoring recurrence

Currently, the prognostic evaluation and monitoring of recurrence in patients with NSCLC are determined using a combination of imaging and tissue biopsies. However, with the in-depth study of minimal residual disease (MRD), an expert consensus has gradually been formed to evaluate prognosis and monitor recurrence in patients with NSCLC, based on MRD [90]. The analysis of ctDNA can be used to detect MRD. It can also be used as an alternative detection method for MRD after surgery or adjuvant therapy, which plays a critical role in the prognoses of patients with NSCLC, and in monitoring recurrence [91–93]. Studies have shown that ctDNA is a prognostic factor in patients with Stage I NSCLC. The pre-surgical detection of ctDNA in the peripheral blood of these patients is associated with a higher risk of recurrence compared with patients with Stage I NSCLC without preoperatively detected ctDNA [31,39,86]. A meta-analysis of the prognostic value of ctDNA in operable NSCLC showed that changes in plasma ctDNA levels before and after surgery could well evaluate the therapeutic effect of surgery, and could guide the adjuvant treatment of patients with NSCLC according to the level of postoperative ctDNA [94]. Tran et al. found that patients with positive plasma ctDNA after surgical resection had a worse clinical prognosis by combining plasma ctDNA with radiation tumor volume measurement. This study shows that the combination of ctDNA and radiological monitoring is a valuable tool to guide the follow-up treatment decision-making of patients with early NSCLC [95]. Through the dynamic monitoring of plasma ctDNA in patients with advanced NSCLC during radiotherapy and chemotherapy, it was found that the plasma ctDNA concentration of patients showed a downward trend with the prolongation of treatment time. This study found that the dynamic changes of plasma ctDNA can predict the clinical treatment effect of patients with locally advanced NSCLC [96]. Patients with lung cancer who have post-surgical detection of ctDNA in the peripheral blood generally have a high recurrence rate; moreover, the detection of ctDNA in peripheral blood is more sensitive than imaging examination, and can guide postoperative management approaches to avoid excessive medical treatments [40,97,98]. Zhang et al. studied the prognoses of patients with NSCLC following surgery, referencing gene mutations in plasma ctDNA on the 30th day after surgery [99]. A multicenter Phase II clinical trial was conducted on patients with stage IIA-IIIB NSCLC to investigate the impact of negative plasma ctDNA after surgery and neoadjuvant therapy on pathological and survival outcomes. The study findings suggested that negative plasma ctDNA could potentially serve as a useful indicator for improved pathological and survival benefits [100]. A retrospective study found that long-term elevated plasma ctDNA levels in patients with NSCLC following chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy strongly suggested a risk of lung cancer progression [101]. Patients with NSCLC who received targeted therapies with no mutations in resistance genes present in their plasma ctDNA after treatment showed longer survival times [102,103]. The results of these studies demonstrate that ctDNA detection may soon be used clinically to evaluate prognoses and monitor recurrence in patients with NSCLC.

Discussion

The discovery of ctDNA represents a promising new tumor marker for the diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC. It has the advantages of simple sampling, as it is non-invasive, repeatable and can truly reflect dynamic changes in tumors and their gene mutation maps. It has great clinical value for the early screening of patients with NSCLC in terms of guiding targeted therapy and immunotherapy, evaluating therapeutic efficacy and monitoring recurrence. Although some patients with early NSCLC have relapsed after radical surgery, others have been cured without recurrence. In the future, we may be able to identify these patients through ctDNA detection and avoid the unnecessary administration of systemic chemotherapies with large adverse reactions after surgery. It is believed that soon, long-term ctDNA monitoring will help us determine whether patients who experience initial adjuvant chemotherapy resistance can receive subsequent intensive treatments. More importantly, no matter which treatment method is selected for patients with NSCLC, if increased ctDNA content is detected it may indicate disease progression. Using ctDNA data, clinicians can adjust treatment plans and prolong adjuvant treatment times to improve the prognoses of these patients.

Detection technology for ctDNA has made great progress in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and detection depth. With increases in clinical detection methods, ctDNA will likely continue to gain attention as a significant tumor marker. Plasma ctDNA is an ideal tumor marker that reflects changes in tumor gene expression in patients with NSCLC. If plasma ctDNA is thoroughly understood in the future, drug researchers may develop targeted inhibitors and immunotherapeutic drugs. Simultaneously, clinicians can treat the source of NSCLC, choose better chemotherapy regimens, monitor the effect of treatment in real-time, evaluate the prognosis of patients in time, and finally achieve better clinical treatment for patients with NSCLC. For ctDNA detection to be widely adopted in clinical practice, it is essential to conduct prospective studies with large sample sizes. These studies can confirm and develop unified detection methods and standards, continuously improve the sensitivity and specificity of detection technology, and ensure economic analysis options for patients with NSCLC. With an in-depth study of the pathogenesis of NSCLC and the rapid development of molecular biology techniques, ctDNA may become an indispensable part of precision treatment approaches for NSCLC, making it clinically applicable to both diagnosis and treatment.

Plasma ctDNA detection technology faces limitations and challenges in its clinical application for NSCLC. In patients with early NSCLC, the concentration of plasma ctDNA is very low due to the small amount of DNA shedding from small tumors. Therefore, improving the sensitivity of plasma ctDNA detection remains a challenge. Although the sensitivity of NGS to detect ctDNA has significantly improved, there is still a risk of false positive results, necessitating further improvement in the specificity of the technology. Additionally, the process of ctDNA detection is complex, highlighting the importance of optimizing the detection steps for better clinical application. The lack of a standardized protocol for the extraction and analysis of plasma ctDNA, along with the absence of unified data interpretation, makes it difficult to incorporate plasma ctDNA detection into clinical diagnostic procedures. At last, addressing the economic factors is crucial. Less affluent countries may not prioritize the purchase of expensive detection instruments, hindering the global dissemination and application of plasma ctDNA detection.

Conclusion

In the future, the integration of plasma ctDNA detection and LDCT is further expected to optimize the early screening and diagnosis of NSCLC. For patients with NSCLC who require preoperative neoadjuvant therapy, in the future, we may choose the best surgical timing according to the change in plasma ctDNA content after neoadjuvant therapy to avoid excessive adjuvant therapy. In the future, we may be able to identify the pseudo-progression of NSCLC after immunotherapy by detecting plasma ctDNA. We are expected to identify the mechanism of drug resistance in patients with NSCLC after targeted therapy, which may solve many problems in patients with NSCLC during adjuvant therapy. In the future, by improving the level of ctDNA detection technology, establishing standard detection methods, and expanding the scope of clinical trials, it is expected to make plasma ctDNA detection truly beneficial in patients with NSCLC.

Future perspective

In the upcoming 5–10 years, personalized ctDNA detection is poised to become the predominant focus of research. This approach offers greater reliability in identifying potential cures for lung cancer, and by integrating personalized ctDNA detection with clinical staging, more precise prognostic information can be achieved.

In the upcoming years, it is anticipated that ctDNA monitoring will allow lung cancer patients to discontinue ineffective drug treatment promptly, thereby minimizing the toxic and side effects of chemotherapeutic drugs and alleviating the financial burden on patients. This will enable the clinical identification of drug holidays following adjuvant chemotherapy and immunotherapy for lung cancer patients.

Significant advancements in plasma ctDNA detection are expected in the next 5–10 years. ctDNA is anticipated to be a key player in early screening of NSCLC patients, guiding targeted therapy and immunotherapy, assessing chemotherapy and postoperative outcomes, predicting prognosis and monitoring recurrence. It is foreseeable that ctDNA detection will play a crucial role in guiding the comprehensive management of lung cancer patients in the future.
