Myoepithelial tumor of the bone: radiologic and histopathologic correlation
Authors: Mohammed Alharthi, Maram Alothman, Athbi A. Naief
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myoepithelial tumors of the bone (BMET) are rare and diagnostically challenging due to their nonspecific clinical and radiologic features. Herein, we report the case of a 19-year-old patient who presented with progressive left hip pain for 1 year. Pelvic radiography revealed an expansile osteolytic lesion of the left iliac bone, abutting the articular surface of the left sacroiliac joint. Cross-sectional imaging demonstrated an aggressive expansile mass with cortical destruction and adjacent soft tissue involvement, raising concern for a primary malignant bone tumor. Imaging differential diagnoses included Ewing sarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and other round cell neoplasms. Image-guided biopsy and histopathological analysis confirmed a BMET. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the radiographic appearance of this rare entity and the role of multimodality imaging and histopathologic correlation in establishing the diagnosis of atypical pelvic lesions in young patients.

Introduction

Myoepithelial tumors of the bone (BMETs) are exceptionally rare neoplasms [1] that share histologic and immunophenotypic characteristics with their soft tissue and salivary gland counterparts [2]. They represent a spectrum from benign myoepitheliomas to malignant myoepithelial carcinomas and have been recognized as distinct entities only in recent decades. To date, only a limited number of intraosseous cases have been reported, involving both long bones and axial sites, including the pelvis, sacrum, and vertebrae [3].

These tumors affect patients of any age, including adolescents and young adults, and do not demonstrate a strong sex predilection. Because they are extremely rare and imaging findings are frequently nonspecific; thus, BMETs are often not included in the initial differential diagnosis of osteolytic lesions. Radiologically, these lesions typically appear as lytic, expansile masses with variable cortical involvement and possible soft tissue extension, which may mimic more common primary bone tumors [4].

Radiologic features alone are not specific; thus, accurate diagnosis depends on a combination of imaging, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry. This diagnostic challenge underscores the importance of case reporting to expand clinicians” and radiologists” understanding of the presentation, imaging spectrum, and behavior of BMETs [5].

She reported no history of trauma or surgery involving the affected region. The patient denied weight loss or other constitutional symptoms. Discussion

BMETs are extremely rare, with the literature reporting only a limited number of cases. They are classified under the spectrum of myoepithelial neoplasms, which are more commonly observed in salivary glands and soft tissues, but can rarely originate in bone. These tumors may present at any age, although adolescents and young adults are frequently affected, as in this case [6].

Comparable to our case, patients frequently present with insidious, progressive pain or swelling, which is nonspecific and may delay diagnosis. The differential diagnosis includes more common aggressive primary bone tumors, including Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, and chondrosarcoma, as well as metastatic lesions. Imaging alone is insufficient to definitively diagnose myoepithelial tumors, and histopathology with immunohistochemistry is crucial [7,8].

Radiologically, myoepithelial tumors are characterized by lytic, expansile lesions with cortical thinning or breach. MRI typically shows low T1 and high T2 signal intensity with heterogeneous postcontrast enhancement, reflecting their cellularity and variable myxoid stroma. Bone marrow replacement and cortical destruction, as observed in our patient, are common features. Soft tissue extension is variable and may not always be present [9].

Conversely, primary bone malignancies, including osteosarcoma, are characterized by osteoid matrix production, frequently visible as cloud-like or dense mineralization within the lesion. Furthermore, the surrounding soft tissue demonstrates more aggressive radiologic features, including poorly defined margins, cortical destruction, and prominent periosteal reactions such as a sunburst appearance and Codman triangle. Similarly, chondrosarcoma may demonstrate chondroid matrix calcifications [10].

Other lytic bone lesions, including Ewing sarcoma, may present with a dominant large extraosseous soft tissue component disproportionate to the osseous involvement with permeative bone destruction and layered periosteal reaction, whereas metastases and lymphoma may demonstrate variable lytic or mixed patterns but are usually correlated with clinical history and multifocal disease [10].

The histologic classification of myoepithelial tumors relies on their location, cell composition, and biological behavior. Myoepithelial tumors containing an epithelial component by light microscopy are known as pleomorphic adenoma, mixed tumor, and chondroid syringoma, depending on their site of origin. Neoplasms that are composed solely of myoepithelial cells are known as myoepithelioma when benign, and myoepithelial carcinoma or malignant myoepithelioma when malignant [11].

Historically, benign myoepitheliomas are characterized by bland spindle or epithelioid cells with myoepithelial differentiation, frequently expressing cytokeratin, S100, and Epithelial Membrane Antigen. Malignant tumors are comprised of spindle cells intermingled with epithelioid eosinophilic cells, showing mild pleomorphic, occasional mitoses, and areas of necrosis [7,8,11].

Due to the rarity of the condition, management strategies are not standardized. Surgical resection is the primary treatment for localized disease, whereas palliative options, including embolization, may provide symptomatic relief in unresectable cases or when surgery is deferred. In our patient, transarterial embolization was successfully performed, leading to significant pain relief. The prognosis varies and appears to correlate with histologic grade and surgical excision completeness [12].

This case highlights the importance of including myoepithelial tumors in the differential diagnosis of osteolytic pelvic lesions in young adults, emphasizing the key role of multimodality imaging and histopathologic confirmation for accurate diagnosis and management planning.

Conclusion

BMETs are rare and present with nonspecific symptoms and imaging findings, frequently mimicking more common aggressive bone lesions. Accurate diagnosis requires correlation of radiologic features with histopathology. Awareness of this entity is crucial for radiologists and clinicians to guide appropriate management. In select cases, interventions, including tumor embolization, may provide effective symptomatic relief.
