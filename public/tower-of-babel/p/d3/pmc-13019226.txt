Development and Study of a Salt and Oil Tolerant Foamer System for Tight Gas Well Foam Drainage Gas Recovery
Authors: Jian Yan, Xuan Yang, Tianyi Wang, Qing Yang, Kai Qi, Meiqi Dang, Mingyao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the technical challenge of severe performance degradation of foaming agents caused by high-salinity formation water and condensate during drainage gas recovery in tight gas wells, this study developed a composite foam system (BLZP) with excellent salt and oil tolerance. Through chemical modification, zwitterionic groups (sulfonate and quaternary ammonium) were introduced into the poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) backbone to synthesize a zwitterionic polymer ZPVA, which served as a stabilizer. ZPVA was then optimized via orthogonal experiments with the zwitterionic primary foaming agent dodecyl dimethyl betaine (BS-12) and the nonionic cofoaming agent lauryl dimethyl amine oxide (LAO). Sodium perfluorononyloxybenzenesulfonate (OBS) was subsequently incorporated as an enhancer. Systematic characterization and evaluation demonstrated that ZPVA effectively suppresses polymer aggregation in 10 wt % NaCl or CaCl2 solutions, maintaining smaller hydrodynamic sizes and higher solution viscosities. The optimized BLZP system exhibited significantly enhanced interfacial dilatational viscoelasticity in simulated high-salinity formation water (TDS 78,050 mg/L). Oil-tolerance tests (with condensate content up to 60%) further revealed that BLZP, owing to its compact and highly elastic composite interfacial adsorption layer, effectively inhibits oil droplet spreading and bridging-induced foam rupture. The decrease in initial foam height (H 0) and 5 min foam height (H 5) was substantially lower than that of two commercial foaming agents. Even at 60% oil content, BLZP maintained H 5 > 150 mm, liquid carrying ratio >40%, and foam water content >4%, demonstrating outstanding potential for field applications. This study provides an innovative chemical solution for efficient foam-assisted drainage gas recovery under challenging reservoir conditions.

Introduction

Currently, the development of tight gas resources , is advancing intensively worldwide, driven by the growing demand for cleaner energy and the continuous innovation in extraction technologies. As a key unconventional resource, tight gas plays an increasingly vital role in the global energy landscape. − However, efficient recovery of tight gas is often hindered by the rapid decline in reservoir pressure and the subsequent accumulation of high-salinity formation water and condensate in wellbores, − which severely impairs the performance of conventional foam drainage agents. Foam-assisted drainage gas recovery has been widely implemented as an effective technique to alleviate liquid loading in gas wells, − particularly in unconventional reservoirs such as tight gas formations. The principle relies on the injection of aqueous foaming agents to reduce the density of the liquid column and facilitate its lift to the surface by the produced gas. − However, the practical efficacy of this technology is often severely compromised by the harsh downhole conditions, notably high-salinity formation brines and the presence of condensate oil.

High concentrations of mono- and divalent ions (e.g., Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+) can cause charge shielding, precipitation, or dehydration of conventional surfactants, − leading to a drastic reduction in foamability and foam stability. Simultaneously, condensate oil presents a more complex challenge. It can spread at the gas–liquid interface, destabilize the pseudoemulsion film, and act as an antifoaming agent, accelerating foam coalescence and collapse. − Zwitterionic surfactants (e.g., betaine) are commonly used for their excellent salt tolerance, particularly their resistance to multivalent cations. However, as small molecules, they offer limited reinforcement to the gas–water interface, resulting in inferior foam stability. Moreover, achieving simultaneous high performance in both oil tolerance and salt resistance remains a significant challenge for these surfactants. , Most commercial foamers are optimized for either salinity or oil tolerance, leaving a significant performance gap in environments where both contaminants coexist, which is typical in many mature tight gas fields.

To address this dual challenge, the design of a robust foamer must integrate multiple functionalities: salt-tolerant moieties to resist ionic strength, oil-resistant groups to inhibit oil spreading, and a stable interfacial architecture to maintain film elasticity. Recent advances have explored the use of amphoteric surfactants, polymers, and fluorinated compounds. Amphoteric surfactants like betaines show inherent salt tolerance due to their zwitterionic nature. Polymers, such as modified poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), can enhance foam stability by increasing bulk viscosity and interfacial viscoelasticity. Fluorosurfactants are renowned for their ability to both dramatically reduce surface tension and create low-energy surfaces, which are resistant to wetting by hydrocarbons.

In this study, we report the development and comprehensive evaluation of a novel, salt- and oil-tolerant foamer system (BLZP) specifically designed for tight gas well foam drainage gas recovery. The system is engineered through a multistep approach: First, a zwitterionic poly­(vinyl alcohol) (ZPVA) was synthesized via sequential grafting of anionic sulfonate and cationic quaternary ammonium groups onto the PVA backbone to serve as a salt-tolerant stabilizer. Second, an optimized blend of a betaine-based primary foamer (BS-12) and a nonionic cofoamer (LAO) was identified for superior performance in brines. Finally, a fluorosurfactant (OBS) was incorporated as an enhancer to impart exceptional condensate oil resistance by modifying the spreading and bridging coefficients at the foam film interface.

The performance of the BLZP system was rigorously benchmarked against two commercial foamers under simulated downhole conditions. Key evaluation metrics including initial/5 min foam height, liquid-carrying capacity, foam water content, interfacial dilatational rheology, and pseudoemulsion film stability, collectively demonstrate that the BLZP system maintains exceptional foam stability and liquid-carrying efficiency in high-salinity brines, even with high condensate oil concentrations. These results highlight its promise as a chemical strategy for enhancing gas recovery in challenging tight gas reservoirs.

Experimental Section

Materials

Poly­(vinyl alcohol) PVA (1788), sodium 2-chloroethanesulfonate, (2-chloroethyl) trimethylammonium chloride, acetone, sodium hydroxide, sodium chloride, calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate, and sodium bicarbonate were all analytical grade (purity >99.7%) and purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium perfluorononyloxybenzenesulfonate (OBS), dodecyl dimethyl betaine (BS-12), sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (SDBS), alpha-olefin sulfonate (AOS), lauryl alcohol ethoxylate (LAE), lauryl dimethyl amine oxide (LAO), and lauryl glucoside (LPG) were all analytical grade (purity >99.7%) and obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Condensate oil and formation water were sampled from tight gas wells in the Ordos Basin. Two commercial foaming agents, Foamer 1 and Foamer 2 (surfactant content >90%), were provided by an oilfield chemicals company.

Methods

PVA Modification

The modification of PVA was carried out via a two-step nucleophilic substitution reaction, as illustrated in Figure . Briefly, the process involved partial etherification of hydroxyl groups on the PVA backbone to introduce anionic and cationic functional groups, respectively.

Route for PVA modification.

Introduction of Anionic Sulfonate Groups

A total of 100 g PVA (1788) was fully dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) under nitrogen atmosphere at 90 °C. After cooling the solution to 70 °C, 50 mL of a 10% aqueous NaOH solution was added dropwise. Subsequently, 15.8 g of sodium 2-chloroethanesulfonate was introduced into the reaction system. The mixture was stirred continuously at 70 °C for 6–8 h. Upon completion, the mixture was poured into a large volume of cold acetone or ethanol to precipitate the product. The precipitate was collected by filtration and dried via rotary evaporation to obtain the intermediate anionic PVA (PVA-SO3 –).

Introduction of Cationic Ammonium Groups

The dried PVA-SO3 – was redissolved in a mixed solvent of DMSO/water and heated to 75 °C. Stoichiometric NaOH (1.0 equiv relative to the target hydroxyl groups) was added, followed by (2-chloroethyl) trimethylammonium chloride (1.1 equiv relative to the target hydroxyl groups). The reaction was maintained at 75 °C for 5–7 h. The final zwitterionic polymer (ZPVA) was isolated using the same precipitation/purification procedure as in the first step: acetone precipitation, extensive dialysis, and lyophilization.

FTIR Characterization

The chemical structures of PVA before and after modification were characterized using a Nicolet iS50 Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were prepared by mixing the dried polymers with KBr and pressing them into pellets for measurement.

Composition Analysis of Condensate Oil

Carbon number distribution of n-alkanes in the condensate oil was analyzed via gas chromatography following the standard SY/T 0542-2008, “gas chromatographic analysis of stabilized light hydrocarbon components.”

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS)

The test samples with specified salt concentrations were prepared by direct dilution of the ZPVA stock solution (1 mg/mL) with corresponding aqueous salt solutions, followed by vortex mixing and equilibration at room temperature. The hydrodynamic diameter (D h) of polymer aggregates was determined using a BI-200SM goniometer laser scattering system (Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, USA). The measurements were conducted with an incident laser beam wavelength of 532 nm and a scattering angle of 90°. All experiments were performed at a constant temperature of 25.0 ± 0.1 °C. For each sample, the intensity-weighted size distribution was derived from the correlation function using the instrument’s built-in analysis software. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Apparent Viscosity

The salt tolerance of the PVA samples was evaluated by measuring their apparent viscosity in brine solutions. Solutions of PVA (1 wt %) were prepared with varying salinities. Apparent viscosity was measured at room temperature using a six-speed rotational viscometer (NDJ-95 A, Shanghai, China). The viscosity retention at 6 rpm upon salt addition served as the key indicator of salt tolerance.

Foaming and Foam Stability Test

Foaming performance was assessed using a Ross-Miles foam meter per industry standard SY/T 6465-2000. The initial foam height (H 0) and height after 5 min (H 5) were recorded to evaluate foaming ability and stability, respectively. Temperature was controlled via a water jacket to simulate downhole conditions. To simulate the actual reservoir temperature of 60 °C, all experiments were conducted with the temperature set at and maintained at 60 °C.

Liquid-Carrying Capacity

After foaming, air was injected into the graduated tube at 5 L/min for 15 min. The residual liquid volume (V 1) was measured. All experiments were conducted at 60 °C. Liquid-carrying efficiency (φ) was calculated as 1 φ=250−V1250×100 where φ is the liquid-carrying efficiency, %; and V 1 is the volume of the residual liquid, mL.

Foam Water Content

Following the start of air injection (5 L/min), initial foam (first minute) was discarded. Stable foam was collected into a dry beaker until reaching 1000 mL. After complete collapse, the liquid volume (V 2) was measured. All experiments were conducted at 60 °C. Foam water content (ψ) was calculated as 2 ψ=V21000×100 where ψ is the foam water content, %; and V 2 is the volume of the collapsed liquid, mL.

Interfacial Property Characterization

Interfacial Tension Measurement

The interfacial tension (IFT) of oil–water, gas–water, and oil–gas systems was measured at room temperature using a Sigma700 automatic tensiometer (Biolin Scientific) via the platinum plate method.

Oil Droplet Contact Test at the Gas–Water Interface

A single oil droplet (approximately 30 μL) was slowly extruded from a syringe at the bottom of a container filled with the foaming solution. The droplet rose through the solution and contacted the gas–liquid interface, forming a pseudoemulsion film. The time from initial contact to film rupture was recorded to evaluate the stability of the pseudoemulsion film and investigate the influence of condensate oil on the interfacial stability of different foam systems. Results and Discussion

FTIR Results

The FTIR spectra (Figure ) reveal distinct new absorption peaks in ZPVA compared to unmodified PVA, confirming the successful grafting of functional groups onto the poly­(vinyl alcohol) backbone. New peaks at 2015.67 cm–1 and 1975.37 cm–1 are attributed to C–H stretching vibrations, likely associated with structural changes in the grafted side chains. A prominent peak at 1718.65 cm–1 corresponds to CO stretching, indicating the presence of ester or carboxyl groups, possibly formed as intermediates or byproducts during modification. The peak at 1538.06 cm–1 is assigned to N–H bending or aromatic CC vibrations, confirming the introduction of nitrogen-containing groups. A strong absorption at 1137.3 cm–1 is characteristic of asymmetric SO stretching from sulfonate groups (–SO3 –), verifying the successful grafting of sodium 2-chloroethanesulfonate. Additionally, the peak at 839.56 cm–1 is attributed to C–N+ stretching or N–CH3 deformation in quaternary ammonium groups, indicating the incorporation of (2-chloroethyl) trimethylammonium chloride. Collectively, these features confirm the synthesis of zwitterionic ZPVA, bearing both anionic sulfonate and cationic quaternary ammonium groups on the PVA backbone.

Salt Tolerance of Modified PVA

DLS Analysis

The salt tolerance of poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and its modified derivative (ZPVA) was evaluated using dynamic light scattering (DLS) by monitoring the changes in hydrodynamic diameter (D h) in NaCl and CaCl2 solutions at varying concentrations.

As shown in Figures a–d and , unmodified PVA exhibited significant salt-induced aggregation. With increasing salt concentration, the D h of PVA increased dramatically, with CaCl2 causing more pronounced aggregation than NaCl. At 10 wt % NaCl, the average D h of PVA increased from approximately 100 nm to over 4000 nm; in 10 wt % CaCl2, it further exceeded 5000 nm. This behavior can be attributed to charge screening by salt ions and enhanced intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Salt ions neutralize surface charges on PVA chains, reducing electrostatic repulsion, while Ca2+, due to its higher charge density, can coordinate with hydroxyl groups, promoting chain aggregation and entanglement, leading to significant swelling or aggregation. ,

Size distributions of the 0.1 wt % ZPVA solutions with various NaCl or CaCl2 concentrations at 25 °C: (a) without NaCl; (b) with 2 wt % NaCl or 2 wt % CaCl2; (c) with 6 wt % NaCl or 6 wt CaCl2; (d) with 10 wt % NaCl or 10 wt % CaCl2.

Mean hydrodynamic diameters of PVA or ZPVA aggregate in the solutions with different salt concentrations.

In contrast, ZPVA demonstrated excellent salt tolerance. As shown in Figures a–d and , its D h increased only slightly with rising salt concentration. In 10 wt % NaCl, the average D h increased gradually from 100 to 480 nm; in 10 wt % CaCl2, it reached only about 530 nm. This indicates that structural modification effectively suppressed salt-induced aggregation in ZPVA. The introduced functional groups (e.g., hydrophobic moieties or steric hindrance groups) likely enhance the steric stability of the polymer chains, weakening interactions between ions and hydroxyl groups, thereby maintaining a more extended conformation and preventing excessive aggregation in saline environments.

In summary, compared with unmodified PVA, ZPVA maintains a smaller hydrodynamic size and better dispersion under high ionic strength, demonstrating significantly improved salt tolerance.

This exceptional performance serves as strong evidence for a high and well-balanced degree of substitution in PVA. This can be attributed to the high density of paired zwitterionic groups, which generates a robust hydration layer through electrostatic interactions, thereby effectively shielding the polymer chains from salt ions.

Apparent Viscosity

The effect of NaCl and CaCl2 on the apparent viscosity of PVA and ZPVA solutions was further investigated to evaluate their salt tolerance (Figure ).

Apparent viscosity of 1 wt % PVA or ZPVA solutions with various NaCl or CaCl2 concentrations: (a) NaCl; (b) CaCl2.

As shown in Figure a, the apparent viscosity of PVA initially increased to a maximum (∼40 mPa·s) at low NaCl concentrations, then decreased sharply to ∼8 mPa·s at 10 wt % NaCl. This trend reflects dual ionic effects: charge screening enhances intermolecular hydrogen bonding at low salt levels, temporarily raising viscosity; at higher concentrations, dehydration and ionic cross-linking lead to chain contraction and aggregation, resulting in a significant viscosity drop. In contrast, ZPVA exhibited a gradual viscosity increase up to ∼45 mPa·s at 6 wt % NaCl, followed by only a slight decrease to ∼28 mPa·s at 10 wt % NaCl. This indicates better structural stability of ZPVA in saline solutions, likely due to steric hindrance from the introduced functional groups, which mitigates excessive dehydration and aggregation, maintaining higher hydrodynamic volume over a wide salt concentration range.

In CaCl2 solutions (Figure b), PVA viscosity also peaked (42 mPa·s) at low Ca2+ concentrations, then dropped sharply to 4 mPa·s at 10 wt % CaCl2. This is attributed to strong Ca2+ coordination with hydroxyl groups, which initially reinforces the network but ultimately induces aggregation and phase separation at higher concentrations. ZPVA, however, showed a more stable response: viscosity increased to 44 mPa·s at 4 wt % CaCl2, decreasing only moderately to 30 mPa·s at 10 wt % CaCl2. The improved stability likely arises from functional groups that inhibit intense Ca2+ coordination and enhance steric repulsion, thereby preserving chain hydration and maintaining an extended conformation. ,

In summary, ZPVA maintains more stable viscosity and demonstrates superior salt tolerance in both NaCl and CaCl2 solutions compared with PVA, confirming that structural modification effectively enhances performance retention in saline environments. Furthermore, the pronounced thickening effect of ZPVA in high-salinity environments significantly contributes to foam stabilization. The increased bulk viscosity substantially retards liquid drainage from foam lamellae and decelerates bubble coalescence and Ostwald ripening, thereby extending foam lifetime under challenging saline conditions.

Optimization of Salt-Resistant Foaming System

Screening of Primary Foaming Agents

Three surfactants alpha-olefin sulfonate (AOS), sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (SDBS), and dodecyl dimethyl betaine (BS-12), were prepared as 0.4 wt % solutions to evaluate their foaming performance under varying electrolyte concentrations. The effect on initial foam height (H 0) is shown in Figure .

Foaming capacity of different primary foaming agents as a function of NaCl or CaCl2 concentration: (a) NaCl; (b) CaCl2.

Figure a indicates that with increasing NaCl concentration, H 0 values of BS-12 and AOS remained relatively stable, while that of SDBS dropped sharply above 4 wt % NaCl. Figure b shows that in CaCl2 solutions, both AOS and SDBS exhibited significant H 0 reduction at 2 wt % CaCl2; in contrast, BS-12 maintained a stable H0 across the tested CaCl2 range. Based on these results, BS-12 demonstrated superior resistance to electrolyte interference and was therefore selected as the primary foaming agent in this study.

The observed differences can be attributed to surfactant molecular structure and electrolyte-tolerance mechanisms. AOS and SDBS are anionic surfactants, whose foaming performance is sensitive to counterions (e.g., Na+, Ca2+) in electrolytes: counterions shield electrostatic repulsion between head groups, promoting micellization and reducing surface activity; additionally, multivalent ions such as Ca2+ can complex or precipitate with anionic head groups, further destabilizing the adsorption layer and deteriorating foam performance. BS-12, as a zwitterionic surfactant, carries both positive and negative charges within the same molecule, maintaining charge balance in electrolyte solutions. This structure makes it less susceptible to changes in ionic strength or valence, thereby delivering stable foaming performance across a wide range of NaCl and CaCl2 concentrations.

Screening of Co-foaming Agents

To enhance the salt tolerance of the foaming system, three nonionic surfactants with good salt resistance-lauryl alcohol ethoxylate (LAE), lauryl dimethyl amine oxide (LAO), and lauryl glucoside (LPG)-were selected as candidate cofoaming agents. Foaming performance was evaluated using blends of 0.4 wt % primary foaming agent (BS-12) and 0.2 wt % cofoaming agent at varying salt concentrations (Figure ).

Variation in initial foaming capacity with NaCl or CaCl2 concentration for systems containing different cofoaming agents: (a) NaCl; (b) CaCl2.

While all three cofoaming agents improved foaming capacity, the LAO-containing system exhibited the most stable performance with the gentlest decline under increasing NaCl and CaCl2 concentrations. Consequently, LAO was identified as the optimal cofoaming agent.

The superior salt tolerance of LAO is attributed to its molecular structure and interfacial behavior. As a nonionic surfactant, LAO lacks strongly ionizable groups, minimizing interference from electrolyte ions or precipitation. Its amine oxide group exhibits moderate polarity, facilitating the formation of a stable composite adsorption layer with the primary foaming agent at the interface, thereby enhancing film elasticity. Moreover, LAO possesses an appropriate molecular structure that promotes close packing at the interface, improving foam structural stability under high-salinity conditions. In contrast, the polyoxyethylene chains of LAE are prone to dehydration and shrinkage in high-salt environments, while the interfacial activity of LPG is more susceptible to disruption of hydrogen bonds by electrolytes, resulting in inferior stability compared with LAO.

This order of influence can be explained by the functional roles of each component. The results indicate that the solution containing only the primary foaming agent BS-12 exhibited the lowest interfacial dilatational elastic modulus (E′) and viscous modulus (E″), suggesting weak interfacial film strength and limited foam stability. The addition of the cofoaming agent slightly increased E′ and E″, but the improvement was modest, indicating its limited role in reinforcing the interfacial film despite assisting foaming. Notably, the marked increase in interfacial dilatational moduli (both E′ and E″ more than doubled) upon adding ZPVA points decisively to its role in reinforcing the gas–liquid interface. This enhancement is attributed primarily to the adsorption of ZPVA molecules at the interface, where they undergo intermolecular interactions to form a viscoelastic film with denser packing and greater mechanical strength. The resulting barrier effectively retards liquid drainage and inhibits gas diffusion between bubbles, thereby substantially improving foam stability. While an increase in bulk viscosity may contribute, the direct and pronounced effect on interfacial rheology strongly suggests that interfacial adsorption and film formation are the dominant stabilization mechanisms.

Furthermore, comparisons between systems prepared with pure water (Figure ) and simulated formation water (Figure ) showed that all three formulations maintained relatively high interfacial dilatational moduli in the simulated brine, demonstrating good salt tolerance. This can be attributed to the systematic salt-tolerance optimization during the earlier screening of the primary agent, coagent, and stabilizer. The selected surfactants and polymer retain effective interfacial adsorption and molecular arrangement even in electrolyte-rich environments, resisting salting-out or conformational collapse, thereby ensuring foam stability in high-salinity formation water. ,

In summary, interfacial dilatational rheology elucidates the contribution of each component to foam stability at the molecular level. Together, these findings demonstrate that the stabilizer ZPVA significantly enhances the mechanical strength of the interfacial film, whereas the system’s salt tolerance originates from the salt-resistant structural design of its components. This provides a theoretical basis for applying the system under high-salinity reservoir conditions.

Garrett Spreading Coefficient

To further improve the condensate tolerance of the foaming system, Sodium perfluorononyloxybenzenesulfonate (OBS) was introduced as an enhancer. Based on the previously optimized salt-tolerant formulation (0.4% BS-12 + 0.3% LAO + 0.15% ZPVA), the surface tension and interfacial tensions with two condensate oils (JX6 and CX3) were measured at different OBS dosages (Figure a).

Using the Garrett spreading model, ,, the entering (E), spreading (S), and bridging (B) coefficients were calculated via eqs ,, to quantitatively describe droplet behavior at the foam interface. As shown in Figure b, for JX6 condensate, S became negative at 0.03% OBS, indicating effective suppression of droplet spreading; B also remained negative, suggesting that droplets tend to form stable bridges within the film rather than causing rupture. For CX3 condensate (Figure c), a similar effect required 0.04% OBS. This difference may be attributed to the carbon-chain composition: CX3 oil is dominated by C8–C9 chains, whereas JX6 oil has hydrocarbon lengths closer to those of the main surfactant, enabling spreading inhibition at a lower OBS concentration.

To satisfy the antioil requirements for both condensates, the final OBS dosage was set at 0.04%. Thus, the optimized oil- and salt-tolerant foaming formulation is 0.4% BS-12 + 0.3% LAO + 0.15% ZPVA + 0.04% OBS. By incorporating the fluorosurfactant OBS, this formulation forms a highly elastic mixed adsorption layer at the gas–liquid interface, effectively reducing oil-liquid interfacial tension. Stability of the Liquid Film against Oil Droplets

The oil tolerance of the system was evaluated using a microscopic film stability test, which measures the time (Ts) from when an oil droplet contacts the gas–liquid interface until film rupture. The salt-tolerant foamer system was incrementally supplemented with Sodium perfluorononyloxybenzenesulfonate (OBS), and the Ts values for JX6 and CX3 condensates were recorded. As shown in Figure a, Ts increased significantly with higher OBS concentration, confirming that OBS is critical for enhancing film stability against oil. Moreover, JX6 condensate (with longer carbon chains) exhibited longer Ts than CX3, indicating the influence of oil molecular structure on film stability.

Oil droplet stability time test: (a) effect of OBS concentration on oil droplet stability time; (b) comparison between the optimized formulation and commercial systems.

This behavior can be explained from two perspectives: interfacial adsorption and film mechanical strength. On one hand, as an anionic fluorosurfactant, OBS strongly adsorbs at both gas–liquid and oil–water interfaces, markedly reducing oil–water interfacial tension and driving the spreading coefficient (S) negative, thereby suppressing droplet spreading. On the other hand, OBS forms a dense composite adsorption layer with the surfactants in the base formulation, increasing the elastic modulus and mechanical strength of the interfacial film, which effectively slows down film thinning and rupture after oil droplet intrusion. For the longer-chain JX6 condensate, its higher hydrophobicity and more ordered alignment at the oil–water interface likely contribute to a denser and rheologically stronger interfacial film, resulting in longer Ts under identical conditions.

In comparison with two commercial foamers (Foamer 1 and Foamer 2) as shown in Figure b, the fluorinated BLZP formulation exhibited a Ts value more than ten times greater. This is attributed to the high-strength, low-energy composite film formed by OBS at the interface, which effectively suppresses oil droplet spreading and bridging, thereby significantly delaying film rupture.

Comprehensive Foam Performance Analysis

Foam Height

The oil- and salt-resistant foam formulation BLZP was evaluated for foaming and foam stability in simulated formation water containing varying concentrations of JX6 condensate. The condensate oil was added to the aqueous surfactant solution at a specific ratio. The mixture was then agitated using a magnetic stirrer at a low speed of 50 rpm for 1 min to achieve droplet dispersion without emulsification, thereby preparing the liquid for foam drainage. Compared with two commercial foaming agents, BLZP demonstrated superior performance. As shown in Figure , with increasing condensate content, both the initial foam height (H 0) and 5 min foam height (H 5) of the two commercial agents decreased sharply, whereas the decreases for BLZP were much more moderate, indicating excellent oil tolerance. Notably, even at 60% condensate content, BLZP maintained H 5 above 150 mm, far exceeding the performance of comparable commercial products. This advantage is attributed to the rigid hydrophobic segments and special hydrophilic groups introduced in the molecular structure of BLZP, which enable effective arrangement at the oil–water interface and the formation of a stable interfacial film. This suppresses oil-induced foam rupture and drainage, significantly enhancing foam stability in oil-containing environments.

Liquid-Carrying Capacity and Foam Water Content

The liquid-carrying capacity (φ) and foam water content (ψ) were employed as key indicators to systematically evaluate the effect of condensate oil content (0–60%) on foam stability. As shown in Figure , both φ and ψ of the two commercial foamers decreased rapidly with increasing condensate concentration, indicating severe structural destabilization and accelerated drainage under oil interference. In contrast, the optimized formulation BLZP demonstrated excellent oil tolerance. Even at 60% condensate content, φ remained above 40% and ψ exceeded 4%. This performance is attributed to the synergistic effect of the rigid hydrophobic groups and hydrophilic head groups in BLZP molecules, which form a dense, highly elastic adsorption layer at the oil–water interface. This layer effectively resists oil droplet penetration and retards film drainage, thereby preserving the foam’s microstructure and macroscopic stability in oil-containing environments.

Conclusion

A zwitterionic polymer (ZPVA) was successfully synthesized by simultaneously introducing sulfonate and quaternary ammonium groups onto the PVA backbone via a two-step nucleophilic substitution. ZPVA demonstrated remarkable structural stability in 10 wt % NaCl or CaCl2 solutions, effectively suppressing salt-induced chain aggregation and dehydration. Its hydrodynamic diameter varied only moderately, and its apparent viscosity retention was significantly higher than that of unmodified PVA.1.Orthogonal experiments identified an optimal formulation consisting of the zwitterionic surfactant BS-12 as the primary foaming agent (0.4 wt %), the nonionic surfactant LAO as the cofoaming agent (0.15 wt %), and ZPVA as the stabilizer (0.3 wt %). This combination, through molecular synergy, formed a stable foaming system in simulated formation water.2.Interfacial dilatational rheology analysis revealed that the adsorption of ZPVA at the gas–liquid interface significantly enhanced the viscoelasticity of the interfacial film. The underlying mechanism involves the formation of a spatial network by polymer chains at the interface, which increases the mechanical strength and disturbance resistance of the film, thereby effectively retarding liquid drainage.3.The addition of 0.04 wt % fluorosurfactant OBS markedly improved the oil tolerance of the system. Mechanistic studies confirmed that OBS competitively adsorbs at the oil–water interface, substantially reducing interfacial tension and shifting the spreading coefficient to negative values, thus inhibiting oil-droplet spreading and bridging-induced rupture at the gas–liquid interface. Microscopic film-stability tests showed that the oil-droplet stability time of the BLZP system was more than ten times longer than that of commercial foamers.4.Under simulated oily (up to 60% condensate) and high-salinity formation-water conditions, the BLZP system exhibited outstanding overall performance. It maintained a 5 min foam height above 150 mm, a liquid-carrying efficiency >40%, and a foam water content >4%, significantly surpassing two commercial foaming agents. This performance is attributed to the dense, highly elastic composite adsorption layer formed at the gas–liquid interface, which simultaneously resists charge-shielding by salt ions and interfacial disruption by condensate oil.
