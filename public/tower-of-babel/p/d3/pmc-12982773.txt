1,1-Diethoxyethane enhances aerobic respiration in human mitochondria via activation of AMP-activated protein kinase
Authors: Thang Nguyen Huu, Hien Duong Thanh, Min-Kyu Kim, Dhiraj Kumar Sah, Vu Hoang Trinh, Hyun Joong Yoon, Jin Myung Choi, Geun-Haeng Lee, Seon-Young Kim, Seung-Rock Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enzyme 5ʹ-adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key regulator of cellular energy metabolism, playing a protective role in cardiovascular remodeling and diseases. Here, 1,1-Diethoxyethane (1,1-DEE; Biosacetalin), a compound traditionally known as a flavoring agent in wine and distilled spirits, was identified as an AMPK activator. By analyzing AC16 cardiomyocytes with the Seahorse Mito Stress Test, we found that 1,1-DEE induced a transient inhibition of both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis, resulting in reduced ATP production. This metabolic disturbance triggered AMPK activation in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, leading to adaptive increases in the phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase and 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 2, which promotes fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis. AMPK activation also enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis by upregulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha and mitochondrial transcription factor A. These alterations led to a sustained increase in both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis. Overall, our findings unravel the important physiological role of 1,1-DEE in energy homeostasis through AMPK activation and aerobic respiration. This mechanism may hold therapeutic potential for the prevention and treatment of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases.Subject terms: Molecular biology, Diseases

Introduction

Epidemiological research has highlighted the beneficial effects of moderate alcohol consumption on cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), particularly coronary heart disease (CHD)1. A notable example is the French paradox, which refers to the low incidence of CHD-related mortality in France despite a high intake of saturated fats, a phenomenon often attributed to wine consumption2. Various bioactive compounds have been identified in wine, such as resveratrol, quercetin, and catechin3,4, which contribute to cardioprotective effects5,6. However, less is known about the potential health benefits of aroma compounds in alcoholic beverages. 1,1-Diethoxyethane (1,1-DEE) is a major aroma compound responsible for the fruity flavor of wine. The concentration of 1,1-DEE has been found to be the second highest in fino sherry, measuring up to 448 mg/L7. Moreover, the concentration of 1,1-DEE in aged Chinese rice wine has been reported to be four times higher than that in young Chinese rice wine8. 1,1-DEE is also present in potato wine9, red wine10, wine vinegar11, and aged rum12 and is considered one of the most odor-active compounds. In sherry wine, this compound is produced through a biochemical pathway catalyzed by flor yeast13. Despite its widespread presence, the biological functions of 1,1-DEE in vitro or in vivo, particularly in relation to cardiovascular health, remain unexplored.

The enzyme 5ʹ-adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) has been shown to protect against several cardiovascular conditions, including atherosclerosis14,15, myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury16,17, hypertrophy18, and cardiac contractile function impairment19. AMPK is a serine (Ser)/threonine (Thr) kinase that was originally identified as the key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis20,21. Structurally, it is composed of three subunits: a catalytic α-subunit, a regulatory β-subunit, and a non-catalytic γ-subunit, each of which plays distinct roles in regulating physiological functions22. The α-subunit is responsible for the activation of AMPK via phosphorylation at the Thr172 residue. The β-subunit facilitates the association of AMPK with glycogen, and the γ-subunit binds to AMP and, to a lesser extent, to ADP, stimulating AMPK phosphorylation by upstream kinases23. AMPK activation leads to the promotion of catabolic processes and inhibition of anabolic processes, resulting in the generation of ATP and improving cellular energy balance. Therefore, AMPK activation has the potential to prevent various diseases, ranging from CVDs to cancer24. Given the cardioprotective effect of AMPK activation and the presence of 1,1-DEE in wine, we hypothesize that 1,1-DEE may activate AMPK, thus modulating metabolic pathways and offering potential therapeutic benefits in cardiovascular diseases. This study aims to investigate the role of 1,1-DEE in activating AMPK and its downstream effects on metabolic processes in cardiomyocytes.

AMPK activation regulates various downstream effectors that promote catabolic processes such as fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) is a biotin-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the carboxylation of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, a precursor for the biosynthesis of fatty acids25. Phosphorylation of ACC by AMPK at Ser79 (ACC1) or Ser212 (ACC2) inhibits its activity, ultimately reducing fatty acid synthesis in the cytosol and increasing fatty acid oxidation in mitochondria20. In the heart, fatty acid oxidation accounts for 70–80% of energy production. AMPK-ACC signaling is involved in regulating platelet phospholipids and thrombus formation26, cardiac hypertrophy and contractility27, and ischemia28. AMPK regulates glycolysis through the phosphorylation of 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase-2 (PFKFB2), a bifunctional enzyme involved in the formation and degradation of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (Fru-2,6-P2), a potent activator of phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK1). Specifically, the phosphorylation of PFKFB2 at Ser466 and Ser483 enhances the production of Fru-2,6-P2 and upregulates PFK1, promoting the progression of glycolysis. These two phosphorylation sites are regulated by protein kinase B (Akt) and AMPK. PFKFB2 activation supports cardiac remodeling29, inhibits ferroptosis in I/R injury30, and alleviates hypoxia-induced cardiac dysfunction31. Therefore, understanding the relationship between 1,1-DEE and AMPK-related fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis is essential for elucidating its potential therapeutic effects.

Mitochondrial biogenesis, the process by which new mitochondria are formed from pre-existing ones to meet increased cellular energy demands, is another critical function regulated by AMPK21. Chronic AMPK activation can promote mitochondrial biogenesis, whereas AMPK knockout can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction32,33. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, is activated downstream of AMPK21. In particular, AMPK promotes the activation of transcription factor EB, which can directly bind to and activate the promoter of PPARGC1A, the gene encoding PGC-1α34. AMPK also directly phosphorylates PGC-1α at Thr177 and Ser538 to enhance its activity35. Given the importance of PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis in promoting cardioprotection36,37, the relationship between 1,1-DEE and AMPK-PGC-1α signaling warrants investigation.

We recently identified 1,1-DEE as a bioactive compound that enhanced insulin sensitivity and ameliorated obesity and dyslipidemia in the mice fed high-fat diet38–42. We also proposed Biosacetalin as a common name of 1,1-DEE that may be potentially beneficial for human health and disease39.

This study aimed to examine the activation of AMPK by 1,1-DEE and its effects on metabolic pathways using the human cardiomyocyte cell line AC16. The mechanism of AMPK activation was assessed through the Seahorse Mito Stress Test and reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements. The acute effect of 1,1-DEE on the phosphorylation of ACC and PFKFB2 was also evaluated, which regulate fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis, respectively. Additionally, the prolonged effects of 1,1-DEE on mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α activation and on mitochondrial respiration were assessed.

Results

Transient inhibition of the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in AC16 cardiomyocytes following short-term 1,1-DEE treatment

The chemical structures of 1,1-DEE, 1,2-Diethoxyethane (1,2-DEE), and metformin are presented in Fig. 1a. To investigate the relationship between 1,1-DEE and cellular bioenergetics, mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Agilent Seahorse XF96 Cell Mito Stress Test Kit. The OCR and ECAR were monitored during acute treatments with various concentrations of 1,1-DEE, 1,2-DEE, and metformin for 30 min. 1,1-DEE treatment caused an immediate decline in the basal OCR (Fig. 1b, red arrow), which was reversible after a few minutes. The transient decrease in the OCR was concentration-dependent. In contrast, treatment with 1,2-DEE did not induce significant changes in the basal OCR and acute response (Fig. 1c). Metformin treatment resulted in a gradual decline in the basal OCR after 30 min, which also contributed to a decrease in acute response in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1d).

After 30 min of acute treatments, Oligomycin (Oligo), carbonyl cyanide-4 (trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and Rotenone/Antimycin (R/A) were added to evaluate mitochondrial function. 1,1-DEE treatment reduced ATP-linked production, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity (%), non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption, and coupling efficiency at 10–20 mM concentrations, and proton leak values were moderately increased at 5–15 mM concentrations (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1a). On the other hand, 1,2-DEE treatment did not show significant differences in these parameters (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1b); however, metformin downregulated ATP-linked production, proton leak, and maximal respiration at 5–20 mM concentrations (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1c).

The ECAR, a measure of extracellular acidification resulting from glycolysis and commonly used as an indirect indicator of the cellular glycolytic rate, was also evaluated. 1,1-DEE treatment caused a reversible decline in the ECAR at 5–15 mM; however, 1,1-DEE irreversibly reduced the ECAR at 20 mM after 30 min (Fig. 1h). No significant changes in the ECAR were observed after 30 min of treatment with 1,2-DEE or metformin (Fig. 1i, j). Collectively, these findings suggest that 1,1-DEE acutely regulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and cellular glycolysis, leading to a reversible decline in both pathways.

Inhibition of human respiratory complex I (hRCI) by 1,1-DEE: molecular docking simulation for the interaction of 1,1-DEE with hRCI

We hypothesized that 1,1-DEE treatment may target hRCI, which favors mitochondrial inhibition. As shown in Fig. 2, molecular docking studies were conducted to elucidate the atomic-level mechanism of hRCI inhibition by 1,1-DEE using the cryo-EM structure of hRCI43. Docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina44. The docking was focused on three domains of human respiratory complex I that configure the binding site of piericidin A, a known inhibitor of complex I in Mus musculus: NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] iron-sulfur protein 2 (NDUFS2), NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] iron-sulfur protein 7 (NDUFS7), and NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase chain 1 (ND1). Our results revealed that 1,1-DEE successfully docked at the top of the ubiquinone-binding site, positioning itself between two highly conserved residues—His92 and Tyr141 of NDUFS2—which are essential for catalysis (Fig. 2a, b)45. This binding mode suggests that 1,1-DEE may interfere with ubiquinone binding, thereby disrupting the electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone and leading to mitochondrial inhibition (Fig. 2c). In addition, 1,1-DEE formed 17 specific contacts with surrounding amino acids, including a polar interaction with His92 within a 4 Å distance (Fig. 2d). In contrast, 1,2-DEE formed only 8 contacts with no polar interaction with the surrounding residues in NDUFS2 (Fig. 2e). These findings provide insight into the molecular mechanism by which 1,1-DEE may inhibit hRCI, offering a potential explanation for its observed effects on cellular energy metabolism.

Enhancement of AMPK activation by 1,1-DEE treatment in a concentration- and time-dependent manner

Eukaryotes contain highly conserved homeostatic systems that are rapidly activated to cope with decreased ATP levels caused by mitochondrial dysfunction or glycolytic inhibition46. AMPK is a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, which becomes fully activated within minutes of OXPHOS inhibition to strengthen catabolic processes and suppress anabolic processes20,21. To investigate whether 1,1-DEE affects AMPK activation, AC16 cells were incubated with various concentrations of 1,1-DEE for 10 min, and the phosphorylation of AMPK (phospho-AMPK) was assessed. Phospho-AMPK was gradually increased starting at 5 mM (Fig. 3a). Increased phosphorylation was also observed in other cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, treatment of AC16 cells with 1,2-DEE did not alter AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. 3b). The results suggest that 1,1-DEE specifically induces AMPK activation in a concentration-dependent manner.

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA (a, c, e): nsp > 0.05, *p ≤ 0.05, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001.

We further examined the temporal dynamics of AMPK activation. The expression of phospho-AMPK was transiently increased at 5 and 10 min but declined thereafter, indicating that AMPK activation by 1,1-DEE is reversible (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 2b). 1,2-DEE exposure caused no significant changes in phospho-AMPK after 30 min (Fig. 3d). However, metformin treatment led to a gradual increase in phospho-AMPK over 60 min (Fig. 3e). These results suggest the conformation-dependent activation of AMPK by 1,1-DEE but not 1,2-DEE. Moreover, the findings highlight differences in the activation patterns of AMPK between 1,1-DEE and metformin.

AMPK is activated not only by changes in the ADP/ATP and AMP/ATP ratios but also by ROS, particularly H2O247,48. Therefore, we assessed whether 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation is mediated by ROS generation. ROS levels were increased after 10 min of treatment with 1,1-DEE (Fig. 4a, b). In addition, pretreatment with the ROS scavenger N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) reduced ROS generation (Fig. 4a, b). Western blot analysis revealed that pretreatment with NAC and the antioxidant ebselen reduced the phosphorylation of AMPK after 30 min of 1,1-DEE exposure, indicating that ROS scavengers decreased AMPK activity (Fig. 4c). The findings suggest that ROS generation plays a partial role in AMPK activation by 1,1-DEE.

Enhancement of phosphorylation of ACC and PFKFB2 by 1,1-DEE treatment

AMPK plays a crucial role in regulating fatty acid metabolism by phosphorylating ACC. ACC phosphorylation inhibits its activation and reduces the production of malonyl-CoA, a substrate of fatty acid synthase (FASN)20,25. Moreover, malonyl-CoA suppresses mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation. Therefore, activation of AMPK promotes mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation via phosphorylation of ACC. To determine the effect of 1,1-DEE on the downstream targets of AMPK, AC16 cells were treated with various concentrations of 1,1-DEE for 30 min, and the phosphorylation levels of ACC and PFKFB2 were assessed. 1,1-DEE treatment increased the phosphorylation of ACC at the Ser79 residue, suggesting the inhibition of fatty acid synthesis and promotion of fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 5a). AMPK is also known to regulate glycolysis by activating PFKFB2. Our results revealed an increase in PFKFB2 phosphorylation at Ser466 and Ser483, suggesting enhanced PFKFB2 activation and glycolysis following 1,1-DEE exposure (Fig. 5a). To determine whether the phosphorylation of ACC is regulated by AMPK, WT and AMPK DKO MEF cells were exposed to 1,1-DEE for 30 min. Similar to the findings for AC16 cells, 1,1-DEE treatment stimulated AMPK phosphorylation and subsequent ACC phosphorylation in WT MEF cells. However, this increase in phosphorylation was absent in AMPK DKO MEF cells (Fig. 5b). Pretreatment of AC16 cells with compound C (an AMPK inhibitor) reduced the phosphorylation of PFKFB2 at Ser483, confirming the role of AMPK in regulating PFKFB2 activity (Fig. 5c). Further analysis showed that 1,1-DEE decreased the expression of the sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBF1) and FASN mRNA, supporting the inhibition of ACC activity and fatty acid synthesis (Fig. 5d, e). These results suggest that acute exposure to 1,1-DEE enhances fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis through AMPK activation.

Enhancement of PGC-1α/mitochondria transcription factor A (TFAM) signaling by prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment

Mitochondrial biogenesis is a prolonged effect of AMPK activation32. Several downstream targets of AMPK are involved in the promotion of mitochondrial biogenesis; however, the underlying mechanism has not been fully elucidated. Among these targets, PGC-1α is a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis21. In AC16 cells, prolonged exposure to 1,1-DEE for 8 h slightly increased the mRNA expression of PPARGC1A (Fig. 6a). Western blot analysis also revealed the upregulation of PGC-1α expression following 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation (Fig. 6b). To determine whether the increase in PGC-1α expression is AMPK-dependent, WT and AMPK DKO MEF cells were treated with 15 mM 1,1-DEE for up to 24 h. PGC-1α expression was increased after 2 h of 1,1-DEE exposure; however, this increase was not observed in AMPK DKO MEF cells after 24 h (Fig. 6c). The results highlight the importance of 1,1-DEE in regulating PGC-1α, an important indicator for mitochondrial biogenesis.

PGC1-α regulates mitochondrial biogenesis through its transcriptional machinery, which contributes to the enhancement of mitochondrial mass36. PGC-1α can bind to nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), which in turn upregulates mitochondrial genes associated with electron transport chain (ETC) complexes36. Concurrently, this binding upregulates the expression of TFAM, a key factor for mitochondrial DNA replication and transcription49,50. To determine whether 1,1-DEE induces the upregulation of the NRF1/TFAM axis, AC16 cells were exposed to 15 mM of 1,1-DEE for up to 8 h. mRNA expression of NRF1 was upregulated after 8 h of 1,1-DEE exposure, and TFAM expression showed a significant increase as early as 4 h (Fig. 6d, e). Western blot analysis confirmed that TFAM protein levels were also significantly upregulated, whereas NRF1 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 6f, g). Moreover, in AC16 cells, pretreatment with compound C inhibited the protein expression of TFAM during 24 h of 1,1-DEE exposure (Fig. 6h), collectively supporting the role of 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation of TFAM. Furthermore, mitochondrial OXPHOS subunit expressions, including complex I-NDUFB8, complex II-SDHB, complex III-UQCRC2, complex IV-COXII, and complex V-ATP5A, were also enhanced after 1,1-DEE treatment (Fig. 6i). Mitochondrial membrane potential was then assessed using MitoTracker Red CMXRos. Compared with the untreated group, 1,1-DEE increased fluorescent signals. However, these signals decreased when the cells were pretreated with compound C (Fig. 6j, k), indicating a regulatory role of AMPK on 1,1-DEE-induced enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential. In summary, 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation promotes mitochondrial biogenesis via the PGC-1α/TFAM signaling pathway and enhances mitochondrial membrane potential.

Enhancement of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2/NFE2L2) by prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment

Given the enhanced ROS generation induced by 1,1-DEE treatment, we next assessed whether 1,1-DEE can regulate redox homeostasis. NRF2, encoded by NFE2L2, is a master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis that is canonically activated through the Kelch-like-ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)/NFE2L2 pathway and drives transcription of antioxidant and detoxification genes via antioxidant response elements (AREs)51. Here, we observed that 1,1-DEE enhanced NFE2L2 mRNA expression (Fig. 6e). A gradual increase in NRF2 protein level was also detected during 8 h of 1,1-DEE treatment (Fig. 6f). In addition, studies have demonstrated a crosstalk between AMPK and NRF252,53. Consistent with this notion, AMPK DKO MEFs largely abolished 1,1-DEE-induced NRF2 upregulation, suggesting that AMPK contributes to the regulation of NRF2 stabilization (Fig. 6g). Thus, 1,1-DEE treatment can potentially participate in balancing redox homeostasis through NRF2 signaling, which is contributed by AMPK activation.

Improvement of mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis by prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment via AMPK activation

To assess the effect of prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment on mitochondrial function, we evaluated mitochondrial respiratory capacity using the Seahorse XF96 Mito Stress Test Kit after 8 h of treatment. Treatments with various concentrations of 1,1-DEE resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in the mitochondrial OCR (Fig. 7a), reflected by increased mitochondrial basal respiration, ATP-linked production, and maximal respiration (Fig. 7b–d). Similarly, the ECAR was also enhanced following treatment (Fig. 7m). These results suggest that prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment induces both mitochondrial OXPHOS and glycolysis pathways for ATP generation. In contrast, 1,2-DEE treatment did not significantly alter either mitochondrial OCR or ECAR values (Fig. 7e–h, n). Metformin treatment reduced the mitochondrial OCR in a concentration-dependent manner, including basal respiration, ATP-linked production, and maximal respiration, while increasing the ECAR (Fig. 7i–l, o), suggesting a shift toward glycolysis. These findings indicate that 1,1-DEE improves mitochondrial respiration and capacity.

To assess whether enhanced mitochondrial respiration by 1,1-DEE is AMPK-dependent, we pretreated cells with compound C. In comparison with 1,1-DEE treatment only, pretreatment with compound C reduced the mitochondrial OCR, resulting in decreased basal respiration, ATP-linked production, and maximal respiration (Fig. 8a–d and Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). The ECAR was also suppressed in compound C-treated cells (Fig. 8m and Supplementary Fig. 3e). In addition, 1,1-DEE treatment upregulated the OCR and ECAR in WT MEF cells compared with untreated WT MEF cells, as evidenced by enhancements in basal respiration, ATP-linked production, and maximal respiration (Fig. 8e–h, n). However, these enhancements were abolished in MEF AMPK DKO cells (Fig. 8i–l, o). The results suggest that cellular bioenergetic improvements induced by 1,1-DEE, including mitochondrial respiration and cellular glycolysis, are mediated through AMPK activation.

To investigate the effect of 1,1-DEE on mitochondrial respiratory function in vivo, C67BL/6 male mice were treated with 1,1-DEE for 8 weeks, and heart tissues were utilized for RNA-seq analysis. GSEA preranked analysis demonstrated that 1,1-DEE treatment induced enrichments in a wide range of mitochondrial biological processes, including “GOBP NADH dehydrogenase complex assembly” (NES = 4.1, FDR = 0.0, nom p value = 0.0), “GOBP oxidative phosphorylation” (NES = 6.8, FDR = 0.0, nom p value = 0.0), “GOBP aerobic respiration” (NES = 5.0, FDR = 0.0, nom p value = 0.0), “GOBP mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly”, “GOBP aerobic electron transportation chain”, “GOBP ATP biosynthetic process”, “GOBP ATP synthesis coupled electron transport” (Supplementary Fig. 4a–g). These findings support the enhancement of mitochondrial respiration and capacity by 1,1-DEE.

Discussion

1,1-DEE is an aroma compound formed during the biological aging in winemaking by a specific flor yeast strain known as Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae)13,54. In the primary process of Sherry wine making (base wine), particularly Fino Sherry, cells prefer anaerobic fermentation to aerobic respiration, fermenting sugars (glucose and fructose) from grapes to produce ethanol and CO2 (Crabtree effect)55. At the end of this step, once the sugars in the base wine are depleted, a dry base wine with around 12–13% alcohol content is obtained. Then, the wine is fortified with neutral grape spirit up to 15–15.5% alcohol content, which is still low enough for cells to survive and thrive56. This wine is placed in partially filled barrels, allowing biological aging to start. In the sugar-free and oxygen-rich environment, cells adaptively switch from anaerobic fermentation to aerobic respiration, utilizing oxygen and alternative carbon sources such as ethanol. This shift promotes the production of acetaldehyde and its derivatives, such as 1,1-DEE56, which facilitates the formation of a white biofilm (flor yeast) on the wine’s surface55. The thick flor yeast protects the wine from oxidation and increases acetaldehyde content. This biological aging process has been well studied, with a focus on the role of sucrose non-fermenting 1 (Snf1; a yeast homolog of AMPK). Under glucose-depleting conditions, Snf1 is activated by the phosphorylation of Thr210 in its activation loop, resulting in enhanced respiratory gene expression and increased oxidative respiration57,58. In our study, we demonstrated that 1,1-DEE, a product generated from the biological aging of wine, could activate AMPK in mammalian cells, suggesting that 1,1-DEE influences metabolic processes by maintaining or reinforcing Snf1 activity to regulate energy balance in yeast during aerobic respiration.

Mitochondria are central to cellular energy production, particularly in cardiomyocytes, where they generate approximately 90% of ATP via OXPHOS through the ETC59. Acetyl-CoA, derived from mitochondrial fatty acid and glucose oxidation, is critical for fueling the ETC via the tricarboxylic acid cycle. In this study, acute exposure to 1,1-DEE transiently suppressed the OCR, indicating a temporary inhibition of mitochondrial function. This inhibition was linked to a decrease in mitochondrial ATP production and disrupted ETC activity, as evidenced by reductions in mitochondrial maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity. Molecular docking simulations revealed that 1,1-DEE can interact with the ubiquinone-binding site of hRCI, thereby disrupting electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone. In addition to mitochondrial respiration, 1,1-DEE treatment also affected glycolysis, as indicated by a concentration-dependent decrease in the ECAR during short-term 1,1-DEE treatment. The results indicated fewer protons were being secreted in treated cells than in untreated cells, which reduced glycolytic activity in the cytosol and glucose oxidation in the mitochondria. Overall, the transient suppression of both mitochondrial and glycolytic ATP production suggests that 1,1-DEE induces a temporary metabolic stress that triggers adaptive responses to restore energy levels. Further studies are needed to validate the interaction between 1,1-DEE and hRCI using biochemical assays.

While 1,1-DEE reversibly inhibited mitochondrial respiration within minutes, decreased OCR values were more delayed and gradual with metformin treatment. This differential response in transient treatment likely reflects differences in their physicochemical properties. We recently assessed the potential permeability of 1,1-DEE dependent on two critical parameters, including logP and polar surface area (PSA)39. In comparison with metformin, 1,1-DEE is a more lipophilic compound with a higher logP, a key indicator of membrane permeability (1,1-DEE: 1.15, metformin: -2.31), and lower polar surface area (1,1-DEE: 18 Å2, metformin: 89 Å2), which predicts a rapid membrane diffusion and cellular entry39. In addition, the previous study has shown that metformin requires specific cation transporters to enter cells and mitochondria, suggesting that its uptake may be more gradual and delayed compared with 1,1-DEE60. Further studies are necessary to understand the cellular and mitochondrial transport mechanisms of 1,1-DEE.

AMPK is a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis and contains a γ-subunit that binds AMP, promoting AMPK activation through allosteric mechanisms and inhibiting dephosphorylation23. An increased AMP/ATP ratio, therefore, activates AMPK, leading to the stimulation of ATP-generating pathways and suppression of ATP-consuming processes. In this study, 1,1-DEE-induced transient mitochondrial dysfunction lowered ATP production and increased the AMP/ATP ratio. Consistent with this mechanism, AMPK was robustly and transiently activated during periods of reduced OCR, indicating that AMPK activation may be directly linked to 1,1-DEE-induced mitochondrial inhibition. Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial stress elevates mitochondrial ROS production through the ETC48,61. 1,1-DEE induced transient mitochondrial inhibition and increased ROS generation, suggesting that mitochondria may be the potential source for 1,1-DEE-mediated ROS by inducing electron leakage from mitochondrial respiratory complex I. Further studies are necessary to clarify detailed mechanisms of 1,1-DEE-induced ROS generation. 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation promotes catabolic processes, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of ACC and PFKFB2. Phosphorylation of ACC inhibits its enzymatic activity, thereby reducing malonyl-CoA production and enhancing mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. This process is particularly important in the heart, where fatty acid oxidation accounts for approximately 70–80% of mitochondrial ATP production under normal conditions, while glucose oxidation contributes the remaining 20–30%62. Additionally, phosphorylation of PFKFB2 at Ser466 and Ser483 promotes glycolytic flux, consistent with the observed reversible changes in the ECAR. In summary, 1,1-DEE induces transient inhibition of mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis, leading to reduced mitochondrial and cytosolic ATP levels. To restore energy homeostasis, AMPK is rapidly activated, increasing phosphorylation of ACC and PFKFB2, thereby enhancing fatty acid oxidation and glycolysis to support cellular energy demands.

Previous studies have shown that PGC-1α knockout mice exhibit mitochondrial defects in skeletal muscle, reduced cardiac contractility, and decreased ATP production, underscoring the critical role of PGC-1α in mitochondrial biogenesis and cardiac function63. Increased PGC-1α activity has been reported to prevent cardiomyopathy, highlighting its cardioprotective potential in diabetic hearts64. In the present study, 1,1-DEE induced PGC-1α expression, suggesting a role in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis. AMPK is known to regulate PGC-1α through phosphorylation at Thr177 and Ser53835. Notably, our findings demonstrate that 1,1-DEE-induced AMPK activation increased both the mRNA and protein expression levels of PGC-1α. Supporting this mechanism, a recent study identified folliculin-interacting protein 1 as a direct AMPK substrate that mediates AMPK-induced PGC-1α-dependent mitochondrial and lysosomal biogenesis65. PGC-1α interacts with nuclear respiratory factor NRF1 to promote the expression of TFAM, a key regulator of mitochondrial transcription and genome replication. Consistent with the idea of 1,1-DEE-enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, exposure to 1,1-DEE increased TFAM expression. Enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis improved cellular OXPHOS and ATP production, as reflected by the increased OCR and OXPHOS component expressions during prolonged 1,1-DEE treatment via AMPK activation.

Under basal conditions, NRF2 is constitutively ubiquitinated by the KEAP1/Cullin 3 (CUL3) E3 ligase and degraded by the proteasome51. Oxidative or electrophilic stress modifies redox-sensitive cysteines in KEAP1, impairing NRF2 ubiquitination and allowing newly synthesized NRF2 to accumulate and translocate to the nucleus51. Previous studies have shown that NRF2 is also regulated by a KEAP1-independent pathway in which glycogen synthase kinase 3 (including GSK-3β) phosphorylates NRF2 within the Neh6 domain, promoting β-TrCP–dependent ubiquitination66,67. Activation of AMPK suppresses GSK-3β activity, thereby stabilizing NRF2 independently of KEAP1 and linking metabolic stress to antioxidant signaling52,53. Our data indicated that 1,1-DEE elevated ROS levels and promoted NRF2 expression, leading us to propose that 1,1-DEE regulates redox homeostasis by enhancing NRF2 stabilization and nuclear localization. Moreover, ROS generated in response to 1,1-DEE partially activated AMPK, and AMPK signaling was required for NRF2 induction, supporting a model in which NRF2 is stabilized through the ROS/AMPK axis. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to fully characterize this signaling cascade.

Despite the prominent effects of 1,1-DEE on regulating mitochondrial respiration and energy homeostasis contributed by AMPK activation in vitro, linking this mechanism to in vivo is essential. GESA of heart RNA-seq data suggested a potential application of 1,1-DEE to the mouse model, particularly genes associated with aerobic respiration and mitochondrial capacity. Nevertheless, definitive validation in an in vivo model, including physiologically relevant dosing and an appropriate delivery route of 1,1-DEE, will be required in future studies to assess cardiac mitochondrial function and AMPK/PGC-1α/TFAM signaling. In addition, more in-depth studies for the biological safety are considered necessary to ensure human clinical use of 1,1-DEE in the future.

In conclusion, this study highlights the effects of 1,1-DEE on AMPK activation and its downstream pathways, including fatty acid oxidation, glycolysis, mitochondrial biogenesis, aerobic respiration, and redox homeostasis (Fig. 9). Accumulating evidence underscores the importance of mitochondria in heart failure, atherosclerosis68, and other CVDs69, suggesting that mitochondria represent a promising therapeutic target. By enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis signaling and respiratory capacity, 1,1-DEE may serve as a potential agent for the treatment of CVDs.

Methods

Materials and reagents

1,1-Diethoxyethane(A902), 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; 35845), metformin (#317240), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (A9165), and hydrogen peroxide solution (H2O2; 88579) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 1,2-Diethoxyethane (D0456) was acquired from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). Mitotracker® Red CMXRos (#9082) and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (100X; #5872) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

The following antibodies were used: AMPK (1:1000; #2532), phospho-AMPK (Thr172) (1:1000; #2535), ACC (1:1000; #3676), phospho-ACC (Ser79) (1:1000; #11818), PFKFB2 (1:1000; #13029), phospho-PFKFB2 (Ser483) (1:1000; #13064), PGC-1α (1:1000; #2178), NRF1 (1:1000; #46743), NRF2 (1:1000; #12721), β-actin (1:1000; #4970), anti-rabit IgG (1:5000; #7074) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Additional antibodies included PGC-1α (1:1000; #PA5-72948), phospho-PFKFB2 (Ser466) (1:1000; #PA5-64738), and TFAM (1:1000; #MA5-16148) from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Total OXPHOS Human antibody (1:1000, #ab110411) was from Abcam (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The GAPDH antibody (1:1000; LF-PA0212) was purchased from Ab Frontier (Daejeon, Korea).

Animals and treatment

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Damool Science (Daejeon, Korea) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal facility at the Hwasun Biomedical Convergence Center, Chonnam National University Medical School (Republic of Korea). All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Use and Care Committees at Chonnam National University Medical School and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care, as well as the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (n = 3) and fed a diet consisting of 70 kcal% carbohydrate, 20 kcal% protein, and 10 kcal% fat. 1,1-DEE was orally administered every other day at a concentration of 100 mg/kg, and the untreated group received equivalent volumes of water. After 8 weeks of treatment, the mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia using isoflurane with no distress, and heart tissues were collected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

AC16 cells (#SCC109; Millipore, CA, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12; LM002-05), supplemented with 12.5% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; S001-01) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; LS 202-02) (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea). At 70% confluency, the cells were subcultured by trypsinization with 1× trypsin-EDTA (#25300-062). For specific experiments, the cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. 1,1-DEE was diluted in serum-free medium to the desired concentrations before incubation with cells.

Wild-type (WT) and AMPKα double-knockout (DKO) mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cell lines were provided by Dr. Eui-Ju Yeo (Department of Biochemistry, College of Medicine, Gachon University, Korea)70. The cells were cultured in DMEM (LM001-05) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1× P/S (Welgene).

OCR and ECAR assessment

The mitochondrial OCR and ECAR were evaluated using the Agilent Seahorse Mito Stress Test Kit (#103015-100; Seahorse Bioscience, Houston, TX, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Before the assay, AC16 cells were seeded into a 96-well Seahorse cell culture microplate at 1.8 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. The sensor cartridge was hydrated by incubation in 200 µL of Seahorse XF calibrant (#100840-000) overnight in a non-carbon dioxide (CO2) incubator at 37 °C. In the standard protocol, the cells were exposed to the indicated concentrations of 1,1-DEE, 1,2-DEE, or metformin for 8 h. On the day of assay, the medium was replaced with 180 µL/well Seahorse XF DMEM (#103575-100). The OCR and ECAR were then measured by the sequential addition of Oligo (1.5 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and R/A (0.5 μM) into injection ports using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). In the modified protocol, the cells were washed and substituted with 180 µL/well Seahorse XF DMEM on the day of assay. The OCR and ECAR were evaluated by sequentially adding the indicated concentrations of 1,1-DEE, 1,2-DEE, metformin, Oligo, FCCP, and R/A into injection ports using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer. The results were exported and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Immunofluorescence staining for the detection of ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates or coverslips in 12-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. The cells were then exposed to 1,1-DEE at the indicated concentrations and for the indicated time courses. For ROS detection, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (#18912-014; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) after the specified treatments and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min with 5% CO2. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS. Live cell images were taken by fluorescence microscopy. For mitochondrial membrane potential assessment, cells were incubated with 200 nM MitoTracker® Red CMXRos at 37 °C for 30 min with 5% CO2. The cells were washed twice with PBS. Live cell images were obtained by fluorescence microscopy.

Western blot analysis

After the indicated treatments, cells were washed twice with cold PBS. Cell lysates were obtained using a lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, and 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were placed on ice and vortexed every 5 min for 30 min. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The lysates were mixed with sample buffer containing 60 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 25% glycerol, 2% SDS, 5% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 0.5% bromophenol and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred electrophoretically to Immobilon PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and immunoblotted overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. On the next day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein expression was detected using the Immobilon Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (#P90720; Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and imaged using the Fusion Solo Vilber Lourmat system (Vilber GmbH, Eberhardzell, Germany). Protein levels were quantified using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.5i; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA extraction, semiquantitative PCR, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (#155960626; Ambion, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 indicating purity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using Improm-IITM Reverse Transcriptase (A3802; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Semiquantitative PCR was performed using AccuPower® PCR Premix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea), and qRT-PCR was conducted using TOPrealTM SYBR Green qPCR Premix (#RT500S; Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea). Primers were purchased from Bioneer and are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Molecular docking of the interaction of 1,1-DEE and 1,2-DEE with hRCI

Molecular docking analysis was performed using the cryo-EM structure of hRCI (PDB ID: 5XTD). Three domains of hRCI that configure the binding site for piericidin A, a known inhibitor of complex I in Mus musculus NDUFS2, NDUFS7, and ND1, were selected for docking analysis using AutoDock Vina software. The input data for ligands (1,1-DEE and 1,2-DEE) were retrieved from the ChEBI database. The macromolecular target was prepared by selecting the above-mentioned three subunits. The docking grid was centered on the region corresponding to the piericidin A binding site, with the following grid parameters: 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å and a sampling exhaustivity of 4.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) with an RNA Integrity Number ≥ 8. The mRNA-seq library was constructed using the TruSeq mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. HiSeq 4000 sequencing system was utilized for sequencing, resulting in 2 × 100 bp sequencing reads.

Bioinformatic analysis of RNA-seq data

Raw sequencing data were assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.11.7). The reads were trimmed using TrimGalore with a quality threshold of >30. Trimmed reads were aligned to the GRCm38 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.0). The HTseq-count algorithm and ensemble Gencode m25 gene annotations were used to profile the gene expression from the raw read counts. Count data were converted to counts per million (CPM) using EdgeR, with the merging of redundant genes to calculate the average CPM. RNA expression data were normalized as described in a previous study71. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted with GSEA desktop v4.4.0 using preranked lists generated from log2 fold-change values. This GSEA of Gene Ontology biological processes (GOBPs) was carried out using the m5.go.bp.v2025.1.Mm.symbols.gmt gene set.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of 2–3 biological replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA. p values of ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant.
