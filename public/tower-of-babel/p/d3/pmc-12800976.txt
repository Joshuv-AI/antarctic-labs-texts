Arthroscopic Excision of the Juxta-Articular Osteoid Osteoma From the Calcaneus at the Sinus Tarsi
Authors: Mikołaj Wróbel, Marta Kubisa, Katarzyna Zielińska, Monika Dorocińska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoid osteoma (OO) is a benign bone tumor that can rarely occur in the sinus tarsi, where its nonspecific symptoms may lead to delayed or incorrect diagnosis. When identified in this location, arthroscopic excision offers a minimally invasive and precise approach for management, although reports of its use remain limited. When OO is suspected in the sinus tarsi, based on clinical findings and confirmed through imaging, arthroscopic resection may be considered. Using standard anterior ankle portals, the surgeon can access and excise the lesion with minimal disruption to surrounding structures. Histological confirmation should be obtained, and thorough debridement of the nidus is essential to prevent recurrence. Arthroscopic excision of osteoid osteoma in the sinus tarsi is a safe and effective technique that facilitates accurate lesion removal while preserving adjacent anatomy. This method serves as a viable alternative to open excision or radio frequency ablation in appropriate cases.

Osteoid osteoma (OO) is a nonmalignant bone-forming tumor that comprises roughly 10–20% of all benign bone lesions.1 Although OO primarily affects other parts of the skeleton, up to 10% of OOs are found in the foot and ankle region, with incidence rates reported between 1% and 16%.2,3 In rare cases, the tumor can be located in the sinus tarsi, but the initial pain is typically nonspecific, poorly localized, and diffusely distributed, making diagnosis difficult.4 On clinical examination, tumor presence is indicated by persistent pain in the sinus tarsi, unrelated to physical activity. Although constant, the pain typically worsens at night and is notably relieved by aspirin, whereas other NSAIDs are generally less effective.5, 6, 7 Diagnostic imaging typically reveals the nidus as a well-defined, round, or oval radiolucent area surrounded by dense sclerotic bone on x-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans.8,9 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) shows diffuse bone marrow edema around the subchondral nidus, while ultrasound demonstrates diffuse synovial inflammation in the sinus tarsi. A treatment option for OO is minimally invasive ablative techniques, such as laser photocoagulation and radio frequency ablation (RFA).10 To the best of our knowledge, the first documented cases of the sinus tarsi osteoid osteoma removal via arthroscopic techniques were published in 2016.11,12 Since then, only two additional publications on this topic emerged, also describing an OO located in the dorsal calcaneus surface and presenting with sinus tarsi syndrome, which were successfully treated arthroscopically.13 Therefore, we would like to present our modified arthroscopic technique of OO removal from sinus tarsi.

Clinical Considerations

Persistent, unexplained lateral foot pain—especially when localized to the sinus tarsi and unresponsive to conservative management—should raise suspicion for rare causes such as juxta-articular osteoid osteoma. Symptoms may include deep aching pain, often worse at night, with limited or no response to NSAIDs, except partial relief from aspirin.

When initial imaging, such as MRI, shows nonspecific bone marrow edema without a clear lesion, follow-up CT is critical for detecting small cortical or subcortical lesions characteristic of osteoid osteoma. Lesions located near the sinus tarsi, particularly adjacent to the cervical ligament attachment on the calcaneus, may be overlooked or misdiagnosed as sinus tarsi syndrome.

In such cases, repeat MRI and CT imaging should be correlated with localized tenderness and persistent symptoms. When a nidus is identified, surgical excision—preferably via a minimally invasive arthroscopic technique—should be considered. The surgical technique is explained in Video 1.

Surgical Technique

Under spinal anesthesia, the patient is positioned in the lateral decubitus position with the operative foot freely suspended off the edge of the operating table (Fig 1A). A pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the proximal thigh and inflated to 280 mmHg. Gravity-assisted fluid inflow is used, without the need for joint distraction devices. A 30°, 4.0-mm arthroscope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) and a 4.5-mm shaver (Smith & Nephew, Watford, UK) are used for visualization and tissue resection, respectively. Portal Placement

Under fluoroscopic guidance (Fig 1C), portal positioning is confirmed using 12-mm needles (Fig 1D). The primary posterolateral portal is established ∼1 cm distal and 1.5 cm anterior to the tip of the fibula, serving as the viewing portal. The anterolateral portal, located 2 cm anterior to the posterolateral portal, functions as the working portal. This two-portal approach allows optimal visualization and instrumentation within the sinus tarsi (Fig 1E). Before cutting the skin, 3 to 5 mL of saline is injected into the sinus tarsi to expand soft tissues.

Sinus Tarsi Debridement

The procedure begins with debridement of hypertrophic synovium and fatty tissue from the sinus tarsi using a shaver and vaporizer (WereWolf Coblation System, Smith & Nephew, Watford, UK) (Fig 2A). Localization and Identification of Osteoid Osteoma

Once the sinus tarsi is cleared, the superficial cortical bone of the calcaneus is exposed and partially removed with a motorized burr to access the lesion site (Fig 3A). Osteoid Osteoma Removal

The nidus is gently separated from surrounding soft tissues (Fig 4, A and B), and the superficial portion is removed with arthroscopic forceps (Fig 4C). Complete excision of the nidus is performed, and the specimen is sent for histopathological confirmation. Additional debridement of the cavity and surrounding sclerotic bone are carried out with a motorized burr to ensure complete removal and minimize the risk of recurrence (Fig 4D). Finally, a 1.5-mm K-wire is used to drill into the base of the cavity, stimulating bone marrow access and promoting healing (Fig 4E). Discussion

Arthroscopic removal is a feasible and effective option for excising superficial bone lesions of the sinus tarsi, particularly those consistent with osteoid osteoma (OO). This minimally invasive approach provides direct visualization of the nidus and surrounding tissue, allowing precise resection while minimizing disruption to adjacent structures.

When considering imaging, CT remains the standard diagnostic tool for OO and other small osseous lesions.14 MRI may be indicated when clinical and radiographic findings are nonspecific, as it offers a broader assessment to exclude alternative diagnoses. However, MRI can be limited in diagnosing OO, as reactive marrow changes may mimic other pathologies, such as Brodie abscess.15

While spontaneous regression of OO can occur, the prolonged NSAID therapy required—often several years—generally favors surgical management.16 Historically, open en bloc resection has been the mainstay of treatment, offering a 95–100% success rate,17, 18, 19 but with notable drawbacks: loss of bone integrity, increased fracture risk, need for internal fixation or bone grafting and extended postoperative immobilization.20 In a systematic review of 223 foot and ankle OOs, en bloc resection accounted for 36.9% of cases.10 Less invasive alternatives, such as RFA, achieve comparable short-term success rates21, 22, 23; however, incomplete ablation may result in persistent or recurrent pain,24 and for intra-articular lesions, there is a risk of delayed cartilage damage.25

However, there are limitations. Arthroscopy requires familiarity with sinus tarsi anatomy and careful portal placement to avoid injury to the cervical and interosseous talocalcaneal ligaments. Deep or sclerotic lesions may be less accessible, increasing the risk of incomplete excision. Although early results are favorable, long-term data remain limited, and recurrence cannot be excluded.

Optimal surgical exposure can be achieved with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, allowing the foot to hang freely in slight supination.11, 12, 13 Standard arthroscopic equipment (4.0-mm arthroscope, 4.5-mm shaver) is generally sufficient for maneuvering within the sinus tarsi. A vaporizer can be used for precise synovectomy, improving visualization before bone work. Curettes and small burrs are suitable for nidus removal, with care taken to avoid excessive bone resection that could compromise structural integrity.

In summary, arthroscopic excision of OO in the sinus tarsi is a technically demanding, but valuable, alternative to open surgery and ablative methods, combining precise resection with minimal invasiveness. Surgeons should weigh its advantages against potential limitations, tailoring the approach to lesion location, accessibility, and available expertise (Table 2).
