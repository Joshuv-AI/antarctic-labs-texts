Revealing spatiotemporal neural activation patterns in electrocorticography recordings of human speech production by mutual information
Authors: Julio Kovacs, Dean Krusienski, Minu Maninder, Willy Wriggers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Spatiotemporal mapping of neural activity during continuous speech production has been traditionally approached using correlation coefficient (CC) analysis between cortical signals and speech recordings. A prior study employed this approach using electrocorticography (ECoG) data from participants who underwent invasive intracranial monitoring for epilepsy. However, CC cannot detect nonlinear relationships and is dominated by the correspondence between periods of silence and of non-silence.New Method:We introduce the mutual information (MI) measure, which can capture both linear and nonlinear dependencies. We validated CC and MI on the sub-second spatiotemporal brain activity recorded during continuous speech tasks. To refine the results, we also implemented a novel “masked analysis”, which excludes periods of silence, and compared it with the standard (unmasked) analysis.Results:Our findings show that previous results, obtained through more complex statistical methods, can be reproduced using CC with an appropriate threshold cutoff. Moreover, both standard MI and CC are influenced by broad transitions between silence and speech, but masking allows the detection of intrinsic correspondences between the two signals, revealing more localized activity.Comparison with existing methods:Compared to the standard CC, masked MI highlights early prefrontal and premotor activations emerging ∼440 ms before speech onset. It also identifies sharper, anatomically coherent activations in key speech-related areas, demonstrating improved sensitivity to the fine-grained spatiotemporal dynamics of continuous speech production.Conclusion:These findings deepen our understanding of the neural pathways underlying speech and underscore the potential of masked MI for advancing neural decoding in future speech-based braincomputer interface applications.

Introduction

Various forms of noninvasive neuroimaging, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), positron-emission tomography (PET), electroencephalography (EEG) and magneto-encephalography (MEG), have facilitated the understanding of speech processes in the brain [1–3]. Despite the remarkable advances enabled by these techniques, none of them provides the simultaneous spatial and temporal resolution necessary to elucidate the full range of the dynamics of brain activity during continuous speech. For example, fMRI provides a detailed topography of the neural correlates of speech, but its temporal resolution is of the order of seconds, which prevents its use in capturing intra-word dynamics. In contrast, scalp EEG and MEG are well-suited for analyzing millisecond-scale neural dynamics but both are highly susceptible to electromagnetic interference from muscular activity during overt speech, limiting their application to covert speech or pre-vocalization stage [4–6]. However, some studies have applied scalp EEG and MEG to overt speech [7–9], including one [10] that combined eye movement tracking with EEG to elucidate spatiotemporal causal interactions among brain regions during overt speech, revealing signal dynamics similar to those described in [11].

On the other hand, intracranial recordings, including electrocorticography (ECoG) [12], stereo electroencephalography (sEEG) [13], and microelectrode arrays (MEAs) [14] offer both high spatial and high temporal resolutions, as well as being relatively insensitive to interference artifacts. These qualities make this modality ideal for acquiring rich neural data, enabling detailed analysis and visualization of speech-related activity—from speech preparation (before onset) to speech perception, as participants hear themselves speak.

While stereotactic EEG (sEEG), with its array of deeply implanted albeit sparse electrodes, has provided useful insights into speech-related activity in the brain [15,16], these electrodes are currently only implanted for clinical purposes and generally provide suboptimal sampling of brain areas related to speech processing. In contrast, long-term ECoG and MEAs have been implanted in targeted speech-related areas solely for the purposes of speech research, specifically for the development of speech neuroprothetics [17,18]. ECoG and MEAs each have unique tradeoffs for the development of practical speech neuroprostheses, but remain the gold standard for detailed investigation and decoding of speech processes in the brain.

The seminal study by Brumberg et al. [11] used ECoG to characterize the spatiotemporal electrophysiological representations of continuous speech and identified a shared network of brain areas involved in both overt and covert speech processes. Several studies have used ECoG in more or less controlled settings, focusing on the production of isolated words or syllables rather than natural, fluent speech [19–21].

Recent advancements in ECoG-based research have leveraged deep-learning techniques, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and transformers for characterizing, decoding, and synthesizing both overt and covert speech [22–24,21,25–28].

However, despite these advances, artificial neural networks provide limited insight into the spatiotemporal structure and mechanisms of neural activity during speech processing. For example, deep-language models have recently been shown to align closely with large-scale ECoG recordings during natural speech [29], but such alignment does not imply interpretability: the internal representations of these models remain opaque, particularly in how they relate to cortical spatial organization or known functional hierarchies. While some efforts have begun to address this gap [30,31], deep-learning models continue to prioritize decoding accuracy over interpretability. This underscores the need for robust statistical approaches that offer both accuracy and insight into how speech-related information is encoded across the cortex.

Mutual information (MI) offers such an approach. As a non-parametric measure, MI is sensitive to both linear and nonlinear relationships between neural and behavioral signals. By estimating shared information directly from empirical time series, MI captures interactions that extend beyond the linear associations measured by the traditional correlation coefficient (CC). This makes MI particularly well-suited for analyzing speech-related neural activity, which often involves complex and nonlinear dynamics that CC may not fully capture.

Several studies have shown this distinction, demonstrating that MI can reveal neural interactions beyond what correlation-based methods can detect. For example, Hassan et al. [32] showed that MI was more effective in identifying long-range neural dependencies during interictal periods in epilepsy, while Ma et al. [33] used it to track nonlinear seizure propagation. More recently, De Clercq et al. [34] applied MI to EEG data and demonstrated its superiority over linear methods while not losing sight of the spatial and temporal characteristics of speech processing –features often hidden in more complex deep neural networks.

While MI has shown utility in characterizing neural dynamics in domains like seizure propagation and attention, its application to speech production has been limited. In this study, we apply MI to speech production using ECoG data –not to decode language directly, but to uncover the spatiotemporal structure of cortical activity during continuous speech. Unlike recent approaches that rely on deep neural embeddings to model speech processing [29], our use of MI offers a model-free statistical approach to elucidate the relationships between speech and brain signals. To refine this analysis further, we introduce a novel masking procedure that removes silent periods, allowing us to isolate the neural dynamics strictly associated with speech production.

In this study, we compare Mutual Information (MI) and the Pearson correlation coefficient (CC) in ECoG studies, expanding on the seminal work of [11] and related investigators [10,35]. We are motivated for two main reasons:

As mentioned before, the MI is a preferable similarity measure, as the CC captures only the linear relationships between neural signals and may underestimate the complexity of speech-related brain activity.

Methods

We utilized ECoG recordings from eight participants (four males, four females; corresponding to participants A–H in [11]) with intractable epilepsy who performed a continuous (paragraph-length) speech production task. All participants in the study were right-handed and had implants on the left-brain hemisphere, as shown in Fig. 1. Given that Broca’s area is lateralized, this left-hemisphere implantation is particularly relevant for investigating speech-related neural dynamics. In addition to Broca’s area, these implants consistently sampled other speech relevant areas including premotor and motor (M1) cortex, posterior superior temporal gyrus (pSTG), the middle section of the superior temporal gyrus (mSTG), supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and the middle temporal gyrus (MTG), as shown in Fig. 1a. Pre-operative magnetic resonance images and post-operative computed tomography images were used to identify electrode locations for each participant, which were collectively placed in a common frame of reference [36], as shown in Fig. 1b. These are the same data sets used in [11], and the reader is referred to that paper for details on data acquisition and preparation. Since the analysis explicitly uses speech, it was only done for overt speech.

Here, we introduced an additional preconditioning step for the speech signals: as is typically the case with audio signals, they are of very high dynamic range, hence we used their logarithms instead of the raw signals. (Even though the MI is theoretically invariant under monotonic transformations such as the logarithm, applying this transformation allows a more accurate calculation of the MI.)

Standard analysis.

For each electrode of each participant, the ECoG signal was bandpass filtered from 70–170 Hz to isolate broadband high-gamma activity, and notch filtered at 120 Hz to remove the harmonic of power line interference. The broadband high-gamma amplitude envelope for each electrode and the corresponding speech intensity time series were computed as the magnitude of the analytic signal obtained from the Hilbert transform [11]. The resulting high-gamma envelope time series, referred herein as “gamma”, was correlated to the participant’s speech intensity time series, across a range of relative time offsets from −500 ms and 500 ms, in 10 ms increments. This analysis was done using both the CC and the MI.

Masked analysis.

This analysis was done similarly to the standard case, with the addition of a “speech mask” for each participant, which was provided by the experimentalists who acquired the data. This speech mask was used to remove the periods of silence from each participant’s time series (both gamma and speech). For gamma, the masking was done after each time shift was applied. Following this, we proceeded as in the standard case, generating sequences of CC and MI versus time lag for each electrode.

Each of the four experimental conditions (CC or MI, standard or masked) resulted in a heat-map movie of “activation indices” on a brain model. To allow meaningful comparisons across the four cases, we define our activation indices as follows.

For the CC analysis, we used the absolute value of the CC as the activation index. This choice, while straightforward, is well justified: Notably, Brumberg et al. [11] defined activation as −logp, where p is the p-value of the unscrambled CC values relative to a beta distribution fitted to the scrambled data. However, it is possible to show that for the unscrambled data, −logp is approximately proportional to |CC|.

For the MI analysis, we used raw values directly as the activation indices, as they are inherently non-negative and reflect the mutual dependence between neural and behavioral signals.

To determine the statistical significance of neural activity for each of the electrodes, we applied a threshold on the |CC| and MI measures. (In contrast, Brumberg et al. [11] used p-values from a fitted distribution of scrambled data as a measure of statistical significance.) Fig. 2 demonstrates why this is justified. We observed a noise floor (the aggregate level of unwanted background noise when no signal is present) for each recording, which allowed us to differentiate between signal S and noise σ≪S by thresholding (see below for numerical values).

Calculation of Mutual Information.

Let f, g be discrete, real-valued random variables. In our context, these variables will have finite sets of values, say Vf and Vg, respectively. (And in the case of continuous variables, these would be discrete grids of values.) The entropy of f is defined as (1) H(f)=−∑s∈Vfpf(s)logpf(s), where pf is the probability density function of f. The joint entropy of the pair (f,g) is defined similarly as (2) H(f,g)=−∑s∈Vft∈Vgpf,g(s,t)logpf,g(s,t),

where pf,g is the joint probability density function. The mutual information of the variables f, g is defined as (3) MI(f,g)=H(f)+H(g)−H(f,g),

which can easily be rewritten as (4) MI(f,g)=∑s,tpf,g(s,t)logpf,g(s,t)pf(s)pg(t).

Further details of these concepts are available in Cover and Thomas [37]. To compute the probability density functions needed to evaluate the above formulas, we used the BADE method [38], which provides accurate estimates even for highly uneven distributions.

Results

The primary results of our analyses are represented in Figs. 3–6. Corresponding videos are provided as supplementary material, showing the continuous progression of neural activity, from −500 ms to +500 ms time lags, as the average activation indices over all eight participants on a standardized cortical template. We used the thresholded CC or MI values directly. We then calibrated our heat maps against Figure 4 of [11], achieving a close match. In particular, Fig. 3 and supplementary video 1 (CC for the standard case) demonstrate why this calibration is justified. In Brumberg et al. [11], the activation indices (derived from Bonferroni-corrected −logp values) begin to exceed a certain threshold (−logp>9.53) around −400 to −300 ms in frontal-motor areas and near 0 to +100 ms in the posterior temporal region (pSTG). Our results in Fig. 3 show a similar progression, where activation in Broca’s area, premotor, and motor cortex emerges by −440 ms, and posterior temporal areas becoming active near 0 ms and strengthening through +220 ms. This pattern is consistently observed in our standard MI results (Fig. 4) as well. Furthermore, the masked analyses (Fig. 5 and Fig. 6) reveal additional early activations–particularly in prefrontal regions–that are not evident in either the standard data or in previous studies, highlighting the increased sensitivity of our approach for detecting subtle preparatory neural dynamics.

Comparison of the various experiments

CC vs MI, standard

In the standard condition, CC and MI yield similar time-evolution patterns of cortical activity. This similarity appears to be driven by both measures capturing the broad temporal structure in the data—namely, intervals of silence and non-silence—rather than reflecting distinct or fine-grained neural interactions. While CC captures linear correlations, MI measures overall shared information, including nonlinear dependencies. The similarity between the two in this setting suggests that previously reported ECoG correlations during speech may, for the most part, correspond to large-scale temporal dynamics.

These findings indicate that both CC and MI are sensitive to coarse-grained features such as the presence or absence of speech. Without accounting for this, there is a risk of misinterpreting broad temporal alignment as evidence of meaningful neural connectivity. This underscores the importance of preprocessing —particularly masking—as well as cautious interpretation, when analyzing data with strong task-related structure.

This hypothesis is further explored through comparison with the masked results below.

CC vs MI, masked

Here we can see two key differences between CC and MI:

Earlier Activation in MI: The MI exihibits an earlier and more distinct activation than the CC, particularly in the auditory association area, e.g., middle section of the superior temporal gyrus (mSTG, lower left in the snapshots). While CC-based activation typically begins around −240 ms, MI reveals significant activation already by −440 ms in frontal and prefrontal areas, and by 0 ms in temporal regions. This approximately 200 ms earlier onset suggests that MI is capturing second-order shared information, likely reflecting earlier phonological or semantic processing, more effectively than CC.

This advantage could be particularly valuable for improving the timing and accuracy of real-time speech-decoding in real-time BCI applications.

CC, standard vs masked

The masked CC analysis reveals subtle task-related activations that are largely absent in the standard condition. Notably, masking highlights the weak but persistent activations in the prefrontal cortex, a region associated with higher-order cognitive and planning processes, more clearly, although these activations are less prominent than those observed with masked MI. The masked CC also shows more defined responses in the auditory association cortex (mSTG), in contrast to the more diffuse and less interpretable patterns seen with standard CC.

These effects are consistent with our MI findings (below), reinforcing that masking helps uncover speech-related neural dynamics that are otherwise obscured by global fluctuations during silence periods in the standard conditions. Importantly, even a basic linear metric like CC benefits from masking. Compared to previous work based on standard analyses [11], our masked CC results reveal earlier onset of frontal activations by approximately 100–200 ms and greater spatial specificity in auditory and prefrontal areas. These refinements suggest that masking not only clarifies the spatiotemporal dynamics of speech production but also enhances sensitivity to subtle cognitive processes that were less accessible with earlier methods.

MI, standard vs masked

Among all the methods explored in our study, the comparison between masked and standard MI reveals the most substantial differences, emphasizing the benefit of masking in isolating neural activity associated with continuous speech production. Although both conditions use the same raw data, the masked approach yields clearer, more focused patterns of speech-related cortical dynamics.

Three key effects emerge from this comparison:

Stronger and Earlier Prefrontal Cortex Activations: Masked MI reveals a distinct pattern of high neural activity in frontal regions, particularly within inferior frontal (Broca’s area) and ventral premotor cortex. These activations emerge well before speech onset, typically around −440 ms, and are consistent with internal stages of speech planning and motor preparation. In contrast, standard MI delays or suppresses these effects, likely because silence periods contribute disproportionately to the global MI signal levels, thereby obscuring early, speech-relevant signals.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that MI is highly sensitive to presence of silence in ECoG signals, and that masking is essential for uncovering temporally and spatially organized neural processes associated with speech production. When applied to masked data, MI captures structured cortical dynamics that are otherwise obscured in the standard condition.

The improved spatial precision and earlier temporal dynamics observed in masked MI suggest that analyses using standard data may overestimate the extent and timing of cortical activity during speech. For real-time brain-computer interface (BCI) design applications, which depend on early and specific detection of task-relevant signals, masking enhances interpretability by eliminating silence-related contributions. Our findings, therefore, reinforce the value of masking as a standard preprocessing step, and highlight MI’s advantage over the traditional CC for speech decoding in epilepsy patients.

Signal-to-noise ratio

To quantify the quality of the results, we computed the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) on a per-participant basis. Most of the CC and MI curves of each participant remain inactive most of the time, thereby creating a background layer that provides a convenient baseline for estimating the noise level (σ ). The signal (S) was defined as the maximum value of all the curves, measured from the center of the background layer. (Fig. 2, which shows the standard progression of MI for all the electrodes in participant A, illustrates this.)

We performed this calculation across all four experiments, for each of the eight participants in this study. The results (Fig. 7) show that the MI consistently yields higher SNR values than CC in both masked and standard conditions. Additionally, the masked conditions always yield lower SNRs than the standard cases, pointing again to the weakness of the true signals involved — and confirming that the higher values for the standard cases are likely spurious.

The fact that MI yields higher SNR than CC does suggest that it captures richer neural dynamics beyond simple linear correlations. This aligns with our observations from the preceding section, where MI was more effective at detecting early speech planning activity. Additionally, the fact that masked conditions yield lower SNRs than standard ones suggests that a significant portion of the standard measures may be due to spurious correlations linked to silent vs. non-silent fluctuations rather than intrinsic speech-related neural interactions. This confirms that masking removes non-informative global fluctuations, revealing weaker but more biologically meaningful cortical activity.

Conclusions

In this work, we introduce a novel analytic method for studying the spatiotemporal dynamics of speech-related cortical activity using electrocorticography (ECoG) data. Our approach combines two key innovations: (1) the use of mutual information (MI) as a non-parametric similarity measure between neural signals and behavioral features, and (2) a novel masking procedure that eliminates periods of silence from the time series, thereby avoiding spurious correlations. Together, these methods yield a more precise and functionally specific mapping of speech-related neural activity than was possible in earlier approaches.

Our findings demonstrate that in standard data, the MI and the CC produce similar activation patterns—primarily reflecting the global structure of silence and speech. However, in the masked condition, MI shows a clear difference from CC: it reveals earlier, stronger, and more localized activations in speech-related regions, including Broca’s area, premotor cortex, and the prefrontal cortex, providing a more detailed mapping of the areas involved in speech planning and production stages. These differences likely reflect nonlinear components of statistical dependencies that are not captured by linear measures such as the CC. While CC also benefits from masking, the improvements are more subtle and spatially diffuse.

We also find that masking leads to improved spatial localization, revealing more focal cortical engagement and minimizing the spread of activation seen in standard results. This increased spatial precision may enable more detailed investigations of speech networks and could be applied to other cognitive domains such as memory, attention, or decision-making.

To assess robustness, we quantified the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) across methods and participants. MI consistently yielded higher SNR than CC, reinforcing its value for capturing speech-relevant neural information, with less influence from sources of noise that may be present during data acquisition. Importantly, the high correlations observed in standard data appear to be largely spurious, driven by broad alignment with speech-silence transitions rather than true task-related dynamics. Masking helps correct for this, allowing both CC and MI to better reflect the true relationship between neural signals and the behavioral task.

These findings have practical implications for BCI design applications, where accurate and early decoding is essential, especially in clinical populations. In epilepsy, where pathological activity and background noise often obscure relevant signals, MI’s sensitivity to subtle neural patterns and higher SNR suggests its advantage for real-time speech decoding. By revealing early, nonlinear neural responses, this approach may improve the reliability and speed of neural prosthetics and assistive communication systems, including BCIs for speech synthesis or text generation in individuals with motor impairments.

Limitations of this study:

While our study provides valuable insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of neural activity during speech, nonetheless, certain limitations should be considered. The data were obtained from patients undergoing invasive ECoG monitoring as part of their clinical care. This approach offers exceptional temporal resolution and spatial precision on the order of a few millimeters, making it highly suited for investigating cortical dynamics. However, electrode placement is determined by clinical necessity, which introduces variability in coverage across participants and may limit the ability to sample all speech-relevant brain areas. Despite this, the inclusion of a cohort of eight participants constitutes a robust sample size for studies using this methodology, where participant availability is inherently limited. Additionally, the masking procedure, while effective in reducing spurious correlations, also removes periods of silence that might contain subtle preparatory or resting-state neural activity.

Despite these considerations, our results highlight the advantages of MI over CC in detecting nonlinear neural dynamics and improving SNR, demonstrating its potential for more accurate assessments of cortical activity. Future research should refine masking strategies, include more diverse participants, and explore additional statistical methods to further advance our understanding of speech-related neural dynamics.
