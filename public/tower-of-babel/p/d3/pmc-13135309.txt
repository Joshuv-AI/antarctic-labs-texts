Impact of intensive care unit versus general ward monitoring on length of stay in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients after bronchoscopic lung volume reduction
Authors: Andrew M. Rabah, Jeffrey H. Jennings, Medha R. Cherabuddi, Rebecca A. Priebe, Joseph F. Hanania, Avi Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) offers several advantages over lung volume reduction surgery, such as faster recovery, fewer complications, and reduced infection risk; however, it also carries a risk of pneumothorax (PTX). This risk has led to the routine practice of admitting patients for observation to the intensive care unit (ICU) across various hospitals. However, the impact of care level, whether in the ICU or general ward, on length of stay (LOS) and postprocedural complications in BLVR patients remains unknown. This study aims to determine whether acuity of care affects LOS and postprocedural complication rates in patients who underwent BLVR.METHODS:We retrospectively reviewed 240 patients’ electronic medical records, calculated median LOS, and compared factors that influence LOS in patients admitted to the pulmonary ward (PW) and the ICU.RESULTS:Our data revealed no statistically significant difference between the median LOS between patients who were admitted to the PW (median 3 days, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 3–5) versus the ICU (median 3 days, 95% CI: 3–5). Postprocedural complications, including PTX, respiratory failure, arrhythmias requiring intervention, rates of valve removal during admission, and time from identification of PTX to chest tube placement, were similar between PW and ICU.CONCLUSIONS:With structured nursing education, monitoring post-BLVR patients in a nonhigh-acuity setting appears to be a safe alternative to ICU admission in facilities that perform BLVR.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains one of the most consequential comorbidities worldwide, affecting an estimated 480 million people in 2020.[1] Despite the wide availability of medical treatments such as inhaled antimuscarinics, beta antagonists, and inhaled steroids, which have demonstrated benefits in reducing morbidity and mortality;[2] there are limited options for those afflicted with severe emphysematous COPD.[34]

Lung volume reduction surgery, a surgical procedure that involves the removal of severely damaged lung tissue,[5] has been shown to improve exercise capacity[67891011] and mortality[812] in carefully selected patients. However, postoperative complications have posed a significant barrier to its wider adoption.[3] Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) has emerged as a less invasive substitute for lung volume reduction surgery, offering a swifter recovery period, diminished complication rates, and lowered susceptibility to infections.[1314]

Despite the lower complication rates compared to surgical procedures, BLVR still carries risks such as pneumothoraxes (PTXs). While immediate post-procedure sedation-associated hypoventilation may occur in patients with severe COPD,[15] there are no studies indicating the utility of longer observation in an intensive care unit (ICU). Due to significant demand on healthcare resources,[161718] we transitioned to managing post-BLVR patients in the ICU to the general care pulmonary ward (PW). Before this change was implemented, a nursing educational plan was developed and gradually introduced over a 6-month period with the goal of educating nurses on the PW to rapidly identify and manage common complications associated with BLVR. To ascertain whether the intensity of care impacts the length of stay (LOS) in these patients, we initiated a comparative study between patients managed in the PW and those who remained in the ICU. To assess the efficacy and safety of less intensive care observational settings, we evaluated the LOS and complication rates among post-BLVR patients monitored in the ICU for the first 24 h compared to those in the PW.

Materials and Methods

Ethics

The study was completed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Convention of 1975, revised in 2000. The Institutional Review Board waived patient consent due to the minimal risk involved. All data were obtained through a retrospective chart review and were deidentified after the collection phase of the study was complete. Analysis was conducted at the population level, with no identifiable patient information included in the study. Data access was restricted to personnel necessary for completing the study, and all data were stored on an encrypted, password-protected network. At no point was data stored on a personal device. Deidentified data were retained after the study’s completion to allow for potential audits.

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent a BLVR at a single institution between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023. Subjects were identified for this study using an internal database of patients who underwent BLVR at our institution. The study protocol was approved by our institution’s institutional review board (#16802-1). Patients who underwent a BLVR between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022, were admitted directly from the operating room to the ICU following the BLVR procedure. After December 31, 2022, patients were observed for 1 h in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and then admitted to the PW. We excluded patients who underwent BLVR between December 1, 2022, and January 31, 2023, to prevent the “washout” and “run-in” periods from confounding the study. Patients who underwent a repeat BLVR procedure during a different hospital admission were also excluded.

With respect to nursing education, the interventional pulmonology team developed and implemented an educational program for nursing staff on the PW. Over 6 months, nurses were required to attend presentations, participate in didactic sessions, and complete a series of online learning modules. The educational objectives included gaining a broad overview of BLVR and understanding the pathophysiology and early recognition of PTX.

In conjunction with the educational initiative, policy updates were introduced to enhance nursing autonomy and patient safety. Nurses received training on proper chest tube flushing techniques, and protocols were established to enable continuous pulse oximetry monitoring and to authorize nursing staff to independently order chest radiographs.

In both the PW and ICU periods, the protocol mandated a 3-day observation stay, regardless of complications. Patients received a daily chest X-ray (CXR), or an earlier CXR if they exhibited respiratory symptoms. If a PTX was identified by CXR, the patient was assessed to determine whether chest tube placement or continued observation was necessary. The time to chest tube placement for PTX was calculated for all such patients, unless the PTX occurred during the procedure or within the 1st h postprocedure while in the PACU. Beginning on November 11, 2020, all patients who underwent BLVR at our institution were given 5 days of oral steroids per a new BLVR procedure protocol. Therefore, COPD exacerbation (COPDE) after November 11, 2020, was defined as a patient requiring more than 5 days of oral steroid therapy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Stata statistical software (StatCorp, College Station, TX). The significance threshold was set using an α = 0.05 for the following statistical tests: t-test, Chi-squared, and Kruskal–Wallis rank sum. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test (for nonnormally distributed variables) and expressed as means ± standard deviation or as medians with a 95% confidence interval (CI), respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and analyzed using a Chi-squared test.

Demographic variables were collected to ensure patients admitted to the PW or the ICU were equivalent with respect to age, body mass index, ethnicity, pack-years, lung function, and inhaler use. Race was collected from self-reported data on the electronic medical record, and all patients identified as either African American or white. We used univariate linear regression to identify independent risk factors for increased LOS. Due to the nonnormal distribution of LOS, a logarithmic transformation was applied to the LOS variable before performing linear regression analysis. In addition to the primary variable of interest (location of patient ICU or PW), the variables used in these analyses included whether the patient developed a PTX, respiratory failure, arrhythmia requiring intervention, valve revision, valve removal, hemoptysis, or COPDE. Variables with a P ≤ 0.1 were included in the multivariate regression model. Sample size calculations were performed assuming a power of 0.8 and an alpha of 0.05, resulting in a required sample size of 144.

Patients in the PW group were older than those in the ICU group (69.80 ± 0.79 years versus 66.37 ± 0.55 years, respectively; t-test, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In 2018, BLVR emerged as a less invasive method for reducing lung volume in patients with severe emphysema. However, close monitoring is still required due to the risks associated with the procedure. In our institution, patients were initially monitored in an ICU setting for the first 24 h before being transferred to a PW. In close collaboration between the interventional pulmonology team and the nursing staff on the PW, a nursing educational curriculum was designed, developed, and implemented with the goal of training nurses to promptly identify and manage common complications associated with BLVR, namely PTX. Following the implementation of this curriculum, the post-procedural protocol was modified such that post-BLVR patients were observed in the PACU for 1 h before being transferred directly to the PW. We found that the overall LOS was similar for patients admitted to the ICU and those admitted to the PW after the new protocol was implemented. In addition, the overall incidence of PTXs during hospitalization following BLVR was comparable between the two groups.

The finding that there was no difference in LOS or PTX rates when patients were transferred to a general ward versus an ICU is significant because high-acuity units, such as an ICU, typically impose a higher resource burden and incur greater daily costs compared to non-ICU units.[161718]

The LOS after BLVR can vary depending on the occurrence of complications such as PTX. Our median LOS of 3 days was consistent with findings from other studies. Karambelkar et al. reported a mean hospital stay for patients undergoing BLVR of 5.3 days, with a range of 3–24 days, and a median LOS of 3 days with an interquartile range of 3–5 days.[19] Fiorelli et al. found that in uncomplicated cases, the median hospital stay was 2 days, with a significant increase to a median of 11.5 days in cases complicated by PTX.[20] In our cohort, most patients without complications were discharged on day 3 of hospitalization. For those who developed a PTX, the LOS increased two- to threefold, and this increase was similar regardless of whether the patients were in the PW or ICU group.

Overall, the rate of PTX in our cohort was 28.75%, which is consistent with the range in other studies. While rates have been reported as low as 5.9%,[21] they have generally ranged from 17.3%[19] to as high as 34%[22] and 36%.[23]

Given the differences in the availability of personnel and equipment in an ICU setting as compared to the general ward, it is reasonable to infer that chest tube placement might be performed more rapidly in the ICU. We found no difference between the two groups in the time it took from identification of a PTX to chest tube placement, indicating that patients on a general floor received chest tubes just as promptly as those in the ICU when a PTX occurred. Patients destined for the PW had a 1-h observation period in the PACU, which may explain the similarities in the time it took to place a chest tube despite differences in acuity of care between the 2 units. However, after accounting for these more immediate PTXs, there remained no difference in time-to-tube placement for the patients assigned to the PW. Another possible explanation is that the interventional pulmonology team was readily available in both groups, ensuring immediate involvement, including CXR and chest tube placement if a patient developed symptoms.

We observed a higher rate of chest tube malfunctions in the PW versus the ICU. We defined a chest tube malfunction as a dislodgment, occlusion, kink, re-suture, or flush. This could be attributed to the different nurse-to-patient ratios in each setting; in the ICU, the nurse-to-patient ratio was 2:1, compared to 4:1 in the PW. A higher nurse-to-patient ratio may increase the chances of chest tube malfunction because nursing staff are required to split their time between more patients, thus increasing the chances of chest tube malfunction.

COPDE can occur in up to 21.2% of patients following BLVR.[24] This aligns with our findings, where 15%–28% of patients reported a COPDE and fewer than 10% experienced hemoptysis. Although the incidence of these events was statistically significantly higher in the PW group, the severity of these events was likely low, as neither COPDE nor hemoptysis significantly predicted LOS.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Additionally, the study utilized a historical control design, meaning that the level of experience with the BLVR program was lower during the ICU phase. Over time, as familiarity with the post-procedure observation protocol increased, it may have influenced the outcomes observed during the PW phase. However, in both ICU and PW time frames, the protocols were standardized and strictly followed, with comprehensive initial training and continuous monitoring to ensure consistency in patient care.

Conclusions

Our study found no significant differences in LOS, incidence of PTX, or other complications between patients managed in the ICU and those managed on the PW following BLVR. The incidence of PTX observed was consistent with rates previously reported in the literature. Although chest tube malfunctions occurred more frequently among patients in the PW, this observation warrants further investigation to elucidate potential contributing factors.

These findings suggest that with appropriate monitoring, standardized management protocols, and targeted nursing education, post-BLVR patients can be safely managed in less resource-intensive settings. Moreover, similar educational and protocol-driven approaches could be implemented across institutions caring for BLVR patients in the immediate post-procedural period. Such initiatives have the potential to reduce healthcare costs while maintaining high standards of patient safety and clinical outcomes at BLVR-capable facilities worldwide.
