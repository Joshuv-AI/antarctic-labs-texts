Real-time location system implementation in dementia care: Stakeholder perspectives
Authors: Alisa Grigorovich, Kelsey Harvey, AnneMarie Levy, Lynn Haslam-Larmer, Leia C Shum, Andrea Iaboni, Josephine McMurray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesDespite interest in using real-time location systems (RTLS) to improve quality and efficiency of care and to collect data for the development of clinical algorithms, research on their implementation and use in long-term care homes is scarce. This study examines RTLS implementation in one long-term care home identifying failure points, ethical tensions, and sociotechnical misalignments that led to abandonment.Research Design and MethodsSemistructured interviews were conducted with 47 participants (residents, care partners, direct care staff, managers, administrators) across two time points; all also completed a demographic survey. Thematic analysis of the data was guided by the Non-Adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread and Sustainability framework, and Sociotechnical Systems theory.ResultsInitial enthusiasm for RTLS stemmed from safety and efficiency expectations, but misaligned functionalities, limited staff engagement, and ethical tensions undermined adoption. Stakeholders anticipated real-time monitoring and fall detection, neither of which were provided. Residents frequently removed the wearables, citing discomfort and privacy, while staff encountered barriers due to the limited integration of the RTLS into workflows. Ethical tensions emerged as residents’ autonomy and preferences were overridden by care partners.Discussion and ImplicationsThese failures underscore the need for iterative and participatory approaches to implementation, transparent communication, and stakeholder alignment. The findings highlight the sociotechnical complexities of implementing surveillance technologies in long-term care. Ethical concerns surrounding resident autonomy, workforce surveillance, and data governance, must be addressed in future implementations to ensure that RTLS supports rather than compromises the dignity and rights of residents and staff.

Autonomy is a core human capability (Jennings, 2016) and an ethical value that is recognized within culture change efforts to improve quality of care provided to older adults and enhance their health and wellbeing (Grabowski et al., 2014; Hande et al., 2021). In the context of residential long-term care (LTC) homes, promotion of autonomy can be in conflict with professional and organizational obligations to protect older adults from risk of harm as the majority of those who live in these settings have dementia (Estabrooks et al., 2020). These types of congregate living environments (also known as nursing homes) provide “24-hr functional support” for individuals with complex health needs and/or disabilities (Sanford et al., 2015, p. 183).

In both Canada and the United States, the concept of risk and corresponding focus on safety tends to dominate with LTC, which can undermine older adults’ autonomy and negatively impact quality of life by leading to excessive social control and restrictions on their freedom (Hande et al., 2021; Steele et al., 2023). The overemphasis on safety is in part related to the fact that these are highly regulated and litigious environments with historic and contemporary examples of excessive use of chemical and physical restraints, neglect, abuse and resident to resident violence (Estabrooks et al., 2020; Hande et al., 2021). Achieving an adequate balance between safety and autonomy is thus an important consideration, and an ethical tension that has been widely recognized as relevant to understanding the full impacts of considering how and whether artificial intelligence (AI) and data intensive technologies can be used to improve quality of care and quality of life within these settings (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Lukkien et al., 2023).

One example of a technology that has gained interest as a tool to improve quality of care in LTC by mitigating risks to safety such as falls and delayed response to health-related events are real-time location systems or RTLS (Bowen & Cacchione, 2022; Grigorovich et al., 2021; Loveys et al., 2022; Lukkien et al., 2023). RTLS can be used to track the physical location of people or assets across time and space indoors and in LTC these are primarily implemented to help staff promptly locate residents within the home, especially those living with dementia. RTLS are also expected to provide movement data that could help identify patterns and to develop algorithms to detect clinically significant changes in residents’ health status (Bowen & Cacchione, 2022; Hasan et al., 2025; Shum et al., 2025). Additionally, proponents argue RTLS can enhance efforts to support freedom of movement indoors for residents with dementia (Grigorovich et al., 2021; Haslam-Larmer et al., 2024). Finally, there is an interest in using this technology to reduce the costs of care by identifying cost saving and cost efficiencies through automation of care documentation and monitoring of health status, thus reducing the demand for human resources, which aligns with a broader shift toward using surveillance technologies for risk mitigation and operational oversight, particularly in under-resourced and complex health care environments (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Chen, 2020; Lee et al., 2024).

Despite the anticipated benefits, no clinical algorithms have been successfully implemented, no significant operational efficiencies have been realized, and empirical research on RTLS use in practice in LTC remains limited (Grigorovich et al., 2021). A more recent review that explored use of RTLS more broadly across all health care environments similarly found that there is a lack of comparative studies on implementation, maintenance, and long-term savings, and concluded that “the real advantage and feasibility of RTLS use [is] open to debate” (Lopes et al., 2025, p. 9). Furthermore, growing evidence highlights unintended consequences of these and similar technologies in health care, including high implementation costs and need for customization, disruptions to existing work practices, increased staff workload, and potential resident harms (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Grigorovich et al., 2021; Hall et al., 2019; Lopes et al., 2025; Niemeijer et al., 2015). While the use of surveillance technologies in dementia care is not new, AI-enabled, data-intensive technologies raise additional ethical concerns. Specifically, algorithmic decision making can exacerbate vulnerabilities and perpetuate discrimination, including by further undermining personal autonomy and increasing staff burden (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Berridge & Wetle, 2020; Hall et al., 2019; Niemeijer et al., 2015). As RTLS adoption becomes increasingly linked to risk management, it is vital to evaluate whether these technologies truly mitigate risk or create new ethical and operational dilemmas.

Research on the implementation of RTLS in LTC remains scarce, leaving the balance of benefits and harms largely speculative. This highlights the need for empirical research to inform decision making regarding RTLS adoption. While implementation science provides a well-documented framework for adoption in health care (Cresswell & Sheikh, 2013; Greenhalgh & Abimbola, 2019), LTC has historically lagged in technology adoption due to resource limitations and organizational complexities (Cruise et al., 2025). This gap underscores the importance of understanding how implementation science applies specifically to LTC environments (Wilson et al., 2022). This study explores the intended and unintended consequences of RTLS implementation in a LTC home from the perspectives of residents, care partners, staff, and organizational decision makers. Our goal was twofold:

To provide practical, policy-oriented guidance for stakeholders considering RTLS adoption, including strategies for mitigating unintended consequences.

To identify the social and technical factors influencing RTLS success or failure, and particularly how individual and organizational factors shape adoption decisions.

Understanding these factors is critical for fostering acceptance and long-term sustainability of such technologies (Greenhalgh et al., 2017; Kronlid et al., 2024). The study contributes to existing literature by examining sociotechnical implications of RTLS deployment, addressing ethical challenges, organizational readiness, and stakeholders’ lived experiences. By capturing stakeholder perspectives, our findings highlight critical tensions, technological innovation, resident rights, and the promotion of supportive care environments. This aligns with the broader need to evaluate implementation of digital and AI-based solutions in complex health care systems where outcomes are “nonlinear, dynamic, and unpredictable” (Lipsitz, 2012).

Methods

The study was reviewed and received ethical clearance from the Brock University (#22-070), and Wilfrid Laurier University (#8294) Research Ethics Boards.

Study design

This paper reports on findings of a qualitative study that involved interviews with three types of stakeholders in one residential LTC setting that implemented an RTLS. All data were collected between November 2022 and December 2023.

Setting

The study took place in a 128-bed LTC home in urban southern Ontario, Canada. The multilevel home was typical of these types of care settings in Canada and offers 24-hr supervision and access to skilled care and services (e.g., nursing, personal care, recreation, etc.) for primarily older adults with complex health conditions and cognitive impairment (Estabrooks et al., 2020). Residents of this home are similar to those in other Ontario LTC homes, in that they are primarily older women (average age of 83 years) with moderate to severe cognitive impairment (62% have a diagnosis of dementia), three or more different medical conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, depression), and need extensive support with their activities of daily living (Flanagan et al., 2021).

The LTC home where the study was conducted was operated under a charitable, “not-for-profit” status, but was managed by a private, for-profit health company. As with other LTC homes in Canada, the care of residents in this home is partly publicly funded with residents contributing toward the cost of accommodation and meals via a copayment fee, which is income-tested (Woolley, 2023). The amount of the copayment fee is also based on whether they are in a basic, semiprivate or private room; however, all receive the same level of care.

Following the advice of the management company, the home implemented an RTLS to improve the accuracy and efficiency of tracking residents within the home (e.g., to enable quicker localization of residents in the home by staff and to reduce reliance on in-person observation of residents’ activities and burden of documentation of activities) and to explore whether location data can be used to derive clinical insights and guide data-driven care planning and quality improvement initiatives. While there were no specific inefficiencies identified, the organization expressed an interest in reducing the amount of time staff spent on locating residents (e.g., for medication administration) and exploring whether summarized location data trend reports provided by the RTLS software could be used to infer residents’ activity patterns to monitor their health status (e.g., sleep disturbance, mobility), determine need for specific interventions (e.g., diet, activity), and evaluate their impacts; this was considered to have the potential to reduce the cost of human resources (e.g., by automating documentation and/or monitoring) and improve quality of care. They were also interested in contributing to the development of clinical algorithms based on movement data and allowed the researchers involved to use the RTLS to collect data from the residents for this purpose.

Real-time location system

This was a commercially available ultra-wideband system that involved bracelets that residents wore on their wrists that transmitted signals to beacons installed on walls and ceilings throughout the LTC home. The RTLS software uses the proximity of the bracelets to multiple beacons to triangulate the location of the resident wearing the bracelet within the home, and data were displayed on a facility-wide map within an application accessible to staff on a tablet or desktop computer. Data were either displayed in aggregate (all residents’ location on a map) or summarized for each resident (e.g., total time or number of times spent in a location, total walking distance, speed, etc.). The bracelets’ built-in “nurse call bell system,” that featured a press button, was disabled due to the presence of an existing bedside call system, the lack of required backup electrical power, and staffing constraints. This technology was implemented alongside existing technologies in the LTC home used to support quality of care, including password protected electronic door locks with alarms (e.g., at entrances to outside and stairwells), a nurse call bell system, various alarms (e.g., bed alarms, WanderGuard TM), and video surveillance in public areas of the home (e.g., lobby).

An administrator in the LTC home approached residents and/or their substitute decision makers (SDMs) to notify them about the installation of the RTLS and to provide them with the opportunity to consent (or refuse) to the resident wearing the bracelet and to the collection of location data. For residents where it was established that they needed support in decision making (i.e., they had a SDM in place for clinical care decisions), the SDM provided consent to RTLS. Staff then approached these residents to ask them to wear the bracelet. All of this was done prior to the SDMs and residents being approached about participating in a research study about the RTLS implementation as described below. All residents living in the home were provided with an opportunity to agree to wear the RTLS bracelet unless their SDM refused to grant consent (which was rare) and the majority of residents in the home was wearing the RTLS bracelet at the start of the research study.

Recruitment and data collection

Eligible participants were all residents who lived in the LTC home, their care partners, and organizational decision makers (e.g., direct care staff, manager/administrator) who spoke and understood English. Digital informed consent to participate in the research was obtained from all stakeholders except the residents, for whom the SDM provided proxy consent on their behalf. Following established guidelines for involvement of persons living with dementia in minimal risk research (Holden et al., 2018; Slaughter et al., 2007), we followed a deferred two-step consent model. Residents were approached with information about the study after their SDM provided informed consent on their behalf and to provide them with an opportunity to assent (or refuse) participation; their assent was also continuously evaluated throughout all research interactions. Residents were ineligible to participate in the study if they were not yet approached by the LTC home about wearing the RTLS bracelet (this was deferred until they were approached), or were unable to assent to an interview, even if their SDM had provided written informed consent.

To examine participants’ experiences and preferences over time, the study included residents and care partners of residents across different engagement scenarios. This included residents who: (1) agreed to wear the RTLS bracelet initially, regardless of whether they continued to wear the bracelet or later expressed dissent or refusal to wear the bracelet; (2) refused to wear the bracelet initially but later agreed; and (3) consistently refused to wear the bracelet (or their SDM refused consent on their behalf). This comprehensive approach ensured diverse perspectives on the implementation and use of the RTLS.

Interviews

The interviews ranged from 30 to 60 min in length and were conducted shortly after the RTLS was implemented to capture participants’ initial impressions. Typically, organizational decision makers and care partners were interviewed twice: initially within 2 months of being approached about the RTLS bracelet, and again after 6 months. Residents, however, participated in only one interview, conducted within 2 months after being approached to wear the bracelet. Interviews were scheduled at a time and location mutually agreed upon by participants and the research team. Interviews with care partners and decision makers were conducted either in person at the LTC home, by phone, or using Microsoft Teams (see Table 1 for examples of interview questions). All resident interviews took place in person at the LTC home by one member of the research team and followed recommendations for establishing assent throughout the interaction (Tatzer and Reitinge, 2023). Where possible, documents (e.g., strategic plans, organization value statements, training materials related to the RTLS) were collected to supplement the interview data. All interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and deidentified before analysis.

Data analysis

Analysis was iterative and ongoing, commencing with the first data collected and continuing throughout the data collection period. Specifically, we used a hybrid thematic analysis approach (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006) that involved both data driven inductive and theoretically informed deductive reasoning with multiple coding levels to generate novel themes that both explained identifiable patterns and provided practical insights.

To facilitate analysis, interview transcripts, institutional documents, and electronic artefacts were imported into NVivo 12. Data analysis proceeded in two phases. We then followed a multiple phase approach that is presented as a linear procedure below for simplicity but was in fact an iterative and reflexive process that entailed reflection and reflexivity.

The first phase was both deductive and inductive, with the former involving development of a set of a priori (or pre-empirical) codes based on the Non-Adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework (Greenhalgh et al., 2017) to establish an analytical foundation (Bowen, 2006). The NASSS is an evidence-based, theory-informed, and pragmatic framework that was developed to guide evaluation of the success of technology-related innovations and was selected based on our interests in a theoretically informed approach to examining sociotechnical implications of RTLS deployment. The NASSS framework examines seven interrelated domains: health-related condition, the technology, the value proposition, the adopter system (stakeholders), the organization, broader sociocultural and institutional contexts, and adaptation over time. These domains were used as sensitizing concepts to inform the initial development of codes: condition; technology; micro-individual adoption of technology over time; meso-organizational; and macro-environmental. As the NASSS framework is not specific to a type of technology, health care setting and/or end-user, we developed additional codes relevant to our study (e.g., knowledge to use technology required by staff vs organization, value proposition to resident, care partner, staff and LTC organization). All the data were then coded using this framework with new inductive codes assigned to segments of data that described a new concept observed in the data that was not covered by the NASSS framework (e.g., values tensions between stakeholders). The second phase of analysis was again both deductive and inductive in that it involved interpretation of the coded data to connect the codes and to create themes to identify patterns and relationships based on the narratives of participants and engagement with the wider sociotechnical and critical data studies approaches to AI and data-intensive technologies scholarship (Lukkien et al., 2023; Neves et al., 2023). We specifically drew on the sociotechnical systems approach that views organizations as composed of two interconnected subsystems: the technical (technology, infrastructure, and tasks) and the social (people, culture, organizational structure) (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977; Cherns, 1987; Sittig & Singh, 2010). This perspective posits that organizational performance is optimized when social and technical components are aligned. In this phase, this theoretical perspective was used as an “interpretative lens” to further deepen our analysis and enhance reflexive engagement with the data, which is consistent with how theory is typically employed in reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021). As an example of how we moved from codes to themes in this phase, the use of this perspective enabled us to identify value tensions between stakeholders related to the technology (as captured in codes ‘value proposition,’ ‘technology,’ and ‘condition’), which in turn were identified as barriers to adoption when expectations about use of RTLS were not realized in practice (e.g., Misaligned expectations and perceptions of RTLS capabilities theme). This phase involved identifying themes, defining and naming them and refining them through ongoing reflection and collaborative discussion.

Initial acceptance was driven by perceived safety and efficiency benefits

Initially, most participants accepted the RTLS due to the common perception that it enhanced the (physical) safety of residents. Care partners and staff emphasized the importance of locating residents promptly to ensure they were “safe.” One manager explained, “We’re responsible for resident safety, so even though you don’t have to … see them in person … it’s good to know their whereabouts” (D4-T1). Another manager stated that it was “very efficient, especially if you are trying to locate a resident” (D3-T1). Similarly, care partners reported feeling reassured, with one stating it gave him “a peace of mind … almost like an added person looking after her, just monitoring her 24/7” (C2-T1). A resident agreed: “It doesn’t bother me … Because [if I fall], and … can’t [call for help] … that’s good, as far as I’m concerned” (R1-T1).

However, stakeholders struggled to articulate how RTLS improved care beyond existing safety measures, including an open plan layout, open doors on all rooms, electronic locks on elevators and doors, bed sensors, and controlled access (e.g., residents could not leave the unit or go on the unit balcony unaccompanied). While the RTLS provided general surveillance of residents while in the home, including bathrooms and bedrooms, many could not identify the specific, measurable benefits of this. As an example, when asked if they felt the RTLS was useful to her or her grandmother, one care partner stated: “Not really. It didn’t really—doesn’t really affect me at all. No, I’m not worried about my grandmother wandering really anywhere. I have to look in two places for her. It’s either her room or she’s stuck in front of that TV. Oh no. Three places, and the dinner area. [S]he doesn’t really need it” (CP4-T1). Similarly, an administrator who was asked to reflect on this topic was not able to explain the added benefit of this system: “I think it would be used best for falls, and those who are on the first floor… because we have a lot of wanderers … That’s our locked unit. Actually all of our floors are locked units technically … If we see a resident in a room for too long of a period of time, and we know that they’re not the person to be in their room for a long period of time, we can at least go and do a check. Although we should already be doing our hourly checks anyways” (D14-1).

Misaligned expectations and perceptions of RTLS capabilities

A recurring theme was the gap between stakeholders’ initial expectations and actual RTLS functionality that became more evident over time. Many participants mistakenly believed the RTLS generated automated alerts in the case of falls or episodes of prolonged inactivity, or that staff constantly monitored these alerts and residents’ movement data. One care partner assumed: “It’s a great safety feature for people who just wander or even if they’ve fallen” (C4-T1). Similarly, another care partner expected staff to use the RTLS proactively: “Presumably, someone’s monitoring it, the information, and so that if by chance he [father] did get loose or whatever, it’d be easy to locate him” (C21-T2). Similarly, another stated:So, it will help you in case of a fall or in case of some kind of medical emergency … If you’re just wearing it for data collection purposes, then I don’t know if my mother is the right person to be wearing that bracelet (C20-T2).

So, it will help you in case of a fall or in case of some kind of medical emergency … If you’re just wearing it for data collection purposes, then I don’t know if my mother is the right person to be wearing that bracelet (C20-T2).

In practice, the RTLS did not automatically detect falls and geofencing alerts for most residents were largely not implemented (and even when implemented the sound for the alerts was not enabled), and the call bell on the bracelet that residents wore was not turned on. Instead, the RTLS was primarily used as a location tracker with information displayed on a map app at a central nursing desk, accessed reactively by staff when prompted or needed. As one direct care staff explained: “Initially no one really used it. Over time … [staff would check if] if they were trying to administer a medication and that person sometimes goes off units, they couldn’t easily find them” (D1-T2). That such expectations existed was likely because when the RTLS and its purpose were explained to care partners and residents when they were asked to wear the bracelet, all of its potential uses were identified, without providing specific information about which features would be used, how, or when. This was in part because even the administrators were not sure as to how exactly the RTLS or the collected data would be used in practice beyond asking staff to use it to locate residents as needed: “Right now, we’re actually still in talks about what specifically we would want to use that data for. A few things we’ve thrown around has been infection prevention, falls, and we’re still as a team brainstorming where we’d want to see that go. That’s where we’re at is just trying to figure out within the home how this tool can be most beneficial to us” (D14-T1).

The gap between expectations and reality not only created dissatisfaction but also eroded trust in the RTLS, especially among care partners. When explaining the RTLS technology to residents and care partners, direct care staff, administrators and mangers emphasized the potential future anticipated benefits, particularly if all RTLS functions were implemented and it was used alongside clinical algorithms, rather than clarifying the RTLS’ current uses in the home: the “[RTLS] is not too invasive, and it’s used to get more information to better support your [loved one]” (D9-T1). As stakeholders’ understanding of the RTLS limitations grew, disillusionment increased. For example, many care partners had initially believed that RTLS data would be shared with them to improve accountability and care transparency. One care partner explained:I think the bracelet can monitor where my mom would be in the home, on the floor, whether in her room, in a hallway, and for how long … I went to a family council meeting where someone from [the RTLS company] came, and they showed us what could be gleaned from the data … it occurred to me that maybe people weren’t being able to go to the washroom whenever they wanted. I thought, ‘Hey, this is kind of interesting. Maybe we can, as family members … have a peek into how often they actually get to go to the washroom (C17-T1).

However, no such data sharing mechanism existed. This unmet expectation not only undermined trust in the RTLS but also reinforced concerns about whether the technology was implemented to address residents’ care needs or serve organizational efficiency goals. Even without expectation of such sharing of individual data, care partners expected to be updated on an ongoing basis as to how the home was using the collected data to improve quality of care in the home. As one care partner noted: “There were a couple of updates from the monthly [Resident ad Family Council] meetings that they had, but nothing [like] what I would’ve expected it to be. Like, ‘This is what we’re doing with the bracelet, this is how we’re using it, and this is how we’re making improvements in the home with this information.’ I didn’t feel that there was any of that” (C15-T2).

Administrators acknowledged the challenge of meeting care partners’ expectations: “Families want the reports of their family member. How do I deal with that at the home level? … [we don’t have the] resources” (D5-T2). Finally, both administrators and direct care staff also expressed disappointment with not knowing how to use the collected data to inform decisions about clinical care and the added burden of figuring this out on their own, which in turn eroded trust in the RTLS and the company who provided it. As one direct care staff stated: “I would say that I had higher expectations of [RTLS company] to be much further along with their data and with their analysis of that data and with their reporting … I think that people gather a lot of data, but the data is not useful when you’re a nurse on the unit. How do I interpret that data, and how do I make sense of it for the resident and the family? The other thing about the system is, in order for someone to do that, you’re really looking at extra resources on the unit. Remember, you have one unit supervisor (nurse) for 32 people” (D5-T2). This disconnect highlighted broader tensions between stakeholder expectations and organizational capacity to use the RTLS and the collected data in real time and to share this information with care partners as demonstrated in the quotes above, particularly given the LTC home’s already limited resources.

Tensions between stakeholders that reflect conflicting perceptions and needs and lead to abandonment

Despite initial enthusiasm and acceptance, the implementation ultimately failed due to persistent operational challenges and unmet expectations. Key sociotechnical misalignments contributed to this outcome. The organization installed RTLS primarily to enhance staff efficiency in locating residents, but in practice, the benefit of this was minimal. Direct care staff and managers reported that the LTC home’s small size and open concept layout made manual tracking easier and more reliable. RTLS data were accessible only through a stationary nursing desk computer; for a short period of time at the end of the study, the application was added to one tablet in the locked dementia care unit to accommodate reduced staffing on night shifts. The general lack of mobile accessibility and disabled automated alerts were impediments for staff needing real-time access to residents’ location while on the move. As one direct care staff explained, “if the system [RTLS] made a noise or [was on] on a tablet … [it] would have been more helpful” (D11-T1). Care partners observed residents removing the wearables, and we observed that this behavior was not consistently monitored reliably, nor reliably addressed.

While RTLS was not used to track staff, some management staff chose to voluntarily wear the wristband to try out the technology. Both the management staff and care partners saw its potential for use in workforce surveillance. They also envisioned it as a tool to demonstrate compliance with mandated hours of care or identify instances of neglect or abuse. One manager stated:[I’d be very] interested … in the data, how staff are spending their time, where they’re spending their time…You’ve got a lot of action happening in certain moments. Then there are probably lulls… Those lulls, I'm not sure how qualitatively they’re filled from a work perspective both in terms of fulfillment of our staff and the quality, obviously, of companionship and interaction with our residents (D7-T1).

However, this idea was met with skepticism and resistance by direct care staff, who expressed concerns about potential misuse of the technology for monitoring their activities. They indicated that this would impact their acceptance and use of the technology. One participant explained:Everybody was like, it’s okay for the residents … [but] You can’t come with me with a vague technology … I need transparency [about] why would the staff be tracked (D17-T1).

Power relations and missed opportunities for shared decision making

Real-time location systems adoption relied on the cooperation and consent of residents, care partners, and staff. However, meaningful engagement was often lacking, contributing to frustration and noncompliance. Many care partners consented to RTLS because they believed that they would receive movement data collected on their loved ones, only to be disappointed by the lack of communication and access to the information collected, which reflects existing hierarchical power relations where direct care staff have positional power over residents and care partners. One participant stated: “I thought there’d be more … back and forth about it, but there was nothing … Not that I necessarily needed that information, but it would’ve been interesting” (C3-T2). This lack of transparency eroded trust in the RTLS and highlighted gaps in shared decision-making processes.

Residents’ autonomy, interests and values were similarly overlooked. Some residents explicitly rejected the idea of sharing location data with family or care staff, stating: “I don’t think that’s their business” (R1-T1). Such remarks illustrated a broader tension between residents’ desire for autonomy and the RTLS’ design, which inherently prioritized monitoring and perceived safety over personal privacy. Many residents resisted wearing the RTLS bracelet, even if consent was given by their SDM, due to its lack of functionality and poor physical design. While it resembled a smartwatch, it lacked any direct value to the resident and was described as uncomfortable and too heavy to wear for long periods of time. One care partner explained:In the beginning, [my mother] was quite fine to wear it, [but] because she has skinny arms, it started to rub … so she’d take it off for a couple of days and then put it back on. Then take it off and put it back on … Right now, I know that she hasn’t had it on for, I'm going to say maybe a month or a month and a half (C1-T2).

The disconnect between the wearable’s design and residents’ preferences further exacerbated resistance to its use, highlighting a missed opportunity to involve end users in the development and refinement of the technology.

Another significant issue was the delegation of consent primarily to SDMs, who were often responsible for informing the residents about the RTLS and encouraging them to wear it but made little effort to involve residents directly in the consent process. Even when care partners were encouraged to reflect on the values and preferences of residents in the interview, including their desire to retreat from public view or have control over their activities, their overriding focus on physical safety often minimized these considerations, eroding residents’ self-determination. Some care partners openly acknowledged residents’ desire for privacy and autonomy yet ultimately placed their safety concerns above all else. One care partner admitted: “Oh, my mother wouldn’t want you to tell her what to do [laughs]” (C8-T1). Staff also reported that some residents explicitly resisted wearing the RTLS because they did not want their movements monitored. One manager recalled: “Sometimes when I approached residents about the RTLS they would say, ‘I don’t want people to know where I am all the time’ … but [we always emphasized] resident safety, and for the most part, they’ve agreed” (D1-T1).

This consistent framing of the RTLS as a “safety measure” and “good for you,” without explicitly acknowledging its implications for privacy and autonomy, illustrates how power dynamics shaped the adoption process. Care partners and staff justified the use of RTLS by emphasizing residents’ cognitive impairments, arguing these made privacy concerns irrelevant or impractical. As one care partner remarked: “Well, they track where she is, so what? She’s under their care to begin with. She’s already lost some … independence and privacy that she had at home …Tracking her movement … is nothing to be concerned about” (C16-T1). Such justifications reveal how safety and surveillance were prioritized over residents’ rights to autonomy, reflecting a broader cultural tendency in LTC to subordinate individual preferences to institutional safety and risk management objectives.

Additionally, some care partners and staff actively misled residents about the purpose of the RTLS to ensure compliance. For example, one care partner stated:I don’t think [privacy] would be a problem … It all depends how much information I feed my mom [about] the bracelet. If I told her that she’s being watched constantly, then maybe it would bother her, but if I kept the information to a minimum … once she got used to wearing it, it’d be like a watch. (C1-T1)

The reliance on proxy consent from SDMs and the minimization of residents’ input reflect broader systemic issues regarding power distribution and decision-making processes within these settings.

Both care partners and staff justified their decisions to not fully respect the residents’ preferences, suggesting their frailty and cognitive impairment made them incapable of making any decisions. They also argued that support of privacy was irrelevant or impractical in the context of institutionalized care. Participants generally expressed the belief that by moving into LTC and accepting support with everyday activities, older adults de facto traded their right to autonomy. As one care partner explained about her father: “He may feel like privacy is an issue but honestly as difficult as it is when he moved into this place, he had to give up a lot of his privacy in order to keep [him] safe” (C6-T1). These attitudes were further compounded by a lack of clear organizational dialogue about how to balance monitoring technologies with respect for residents’ autonomy and dignity.

A lack of awareness and concern for data protection, ownership, and use

Another significant theme was the near-total absence of concern or discussion regarding data protection, storage, and ownership among all stakeholder groups. Both residents and care partners demonstrated limited awareness of how RTLS data were stored, who owned it, or how it might be used beyond immediate care purposes. Care partners commonly assumed data collection primarily benefited residents’ safety or care coordination, rarely considering or questioning broader implications of its use. One care partner stated: “I don’t really know who looks at the data, but I assumed it’s just for the staff to know where she is” (C10-T1). Similarly, another stated: “I’m assuming it’s … used solely by the home at the moment and for research purposes, and I have no objections to that. I guess I’m of the understanding that the data is stored securely and is only being used with non-identifiers…You’re not tracking my mom around the home and saying, ‘[she] likes to do this’” (C9-T1).

This lack of inquiry extended to the potential monetization of data or its use for operational decisions, such as reducing staffing costs or rationing care. Some administrators expressed interest in using RTLS data to “document the amount of [staff] interaction or the participation time of residents [in activities] into their chart” (D3-T1) or to identify ways to enhance staff efficiency by using the location data to infer whether they have enough human resources: “Do we have enough resources? How busy are those resources during each shift? How frenzied are the movements? I mentioned it before we build to peaks and so from a management productivity perspective, work patterns, how can we lessen the peaks to reduce the strain at certain times” (D7-T1). However, there was no transparent discussion about whether this, or other types of data about how and when staff interact with residents, might eventually be used to inform resource allocation decisions. This oversight raises significant ethical questions about the ownership and the potential misuse of data generated by surveillance technologies in LTC.

Discussion

The findings from this study highlight complex sociotechnical challenges associated with implementing RTLS in LTC settings. Stakeholders initially perceived RTLS as beneficial, primarily due to its potential to enhance resident safety and operational efficiency. However, the gap between stakeholder expectations and actual technological capabilities, both based on the functions enabled and used in practice as well those potentially created in the future (e.g., clinical algorithms), created dissatisfaction and distrust, ultimately resulting in system abandonment. These findings echo previous research emphasizing that misalignment between technological promises and practical utility often underlies technology nonadoption in health care settings (Greenhalgh et al., 2017).

Sociotechnical interdependencies and the complexity of RTLS adoption in LTC

The implementation of the RTLS underscores the inherently complex and interdependent nature of sociotechnical systems in LTC. The NASSS framework (Greenhalgh et al., 2017) highlights the necessity of aligning the technology with individual, organizational, and contextual factors to ensure sustainable adoption. In this study, RTLS introduction revealed misalignments between technical design, organizational goals, existing workflows, and stakeholder expectations, leading to its eventual failure. Although the RTLS used in this study (and similar commercially available ones) is marketed as a tool to enhance operational efficiency by reducing the time staff spent locating residents and resident safety, the LTC home’s choice to limit its full functionality (e.g., lack of mobile access and disabled alerts and call button) disrupted established routines and resulted in minimal value for the staff. Moreover, staff were largely unprepared to cope with the added workload of determining the clinical significance of the collected data, or developing processes for integrating these data into existing quality improvement mechanisms and sharing information about this with care partners and residents. These findings align with the broader sociotechnical systems literature that emphasizes the necessity for technological systems and social practices to coevolve for successful implementation (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977; Cherns, 1987). In LTC, where workflows are often constrained by resource limitations and hierarchical structures, the introduction of disruptive technologies can negatively impact existing routines unless accompanied by iterative user-centered design (Grigorovich et al., 2021; Hall et al., 2019). Moreover, while the RTLS in this study was deployed primarily as a location tracking tool and/or to augment existing in-person documentation and monitoring of residents’ activities and health status, its predictive capabilities through AI-enabled applications, hold potential for improving care quality and operational efficiency (Wilson et al., 2022; Haslam-Larmer et al., 2022; Loveys et al., 2022). However, achieving these future benefits still requires buy-in from all stakeholders, and in particular those consenting to wear the bracelet (residents and care partners), to ensure consistent collection processes and equitable distribution of its current and future benefits to individuals and organizations. Otherwise, the integration of RTLS and other types of surveillance technologies has the potential to further erode quality of care and lead to algorithmic harms (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022) undermining otherwise good intentions. While residents largely did not voice concerns about privacy and data security, there was a gap in the organization’s development of safeguards and transparent policies around the use and ownership of location data. There was also a gap in the education provided to help all stakeholders understand the risks and data protection protocols and policies in place to mitigate these. As the broader scholarship on the use of AI in health care demonstrates, such education and policies are crucial not only for informed consent and compliance with existing regulations regarding health information but also to align use of technologies in LTC with older adults’ and caregivers’ needs and values (Lindeman et al., 2020; Lukkien et al., 2023; Mohsin Khan et al., 2025) to ensure foundational trust and acceptance. Without such trust, the RTLS’ ability to generate comprehensive, rich, longitudinal data for predictive analytics is significantly impeded.

Ethical concerns: Privacy, autonomy, consent, and transitions

Ethical concerns were pervasive, particularly in relation to resident autonomy and the implications of surveillance. SDMs consented to the technology, but residents were often excluded from these decisions, perpetuating power imbalances and marginalizing residents’ voices in favor of organizational, staff, and care partners’ priorities. The lack of resident involvement in consent processes reflects a broader trend in LTC, where institutional safety and efficiency goals can override individual rights (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Hande et al., 2021; Liougas et al., Submitted). The framing of RTLS as a “safety tool” justified compromises to privacy and autonomy. Care partners often minimized privacy violations, emphasizing that residents had “traded” autonomy by entering the LTC facility. This aligns with critiques that surveillance technologies in care settings often reproduce paternalistic attitudes, reinforce systemic ageism and ableism, and erode residents’ agency (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Berridge & Wetle, 2020; Neves et al., 2023; Niemeijer et al., 2015).

One underexplored aspect of RTLS is its use across fluctuating cognitive states, particularly with dementia progression. This “cognitive liminal state” (McMurray, 2023) raises unique ethical challenges as residents’ initial consent (or assent) to surveillance technologies may no longer reflect their evolving comfort or capacity. Neubauer et al. (2021) identified similar issues in the community context, noting RTLS supported independence for some persons living with dementia but was perceived as intrusive by others. Our findings also align with Berridge et al.’s (2022) and Chien et al.’s, (2024) research suggesting that a one-size-fits-all approach to RTLS implementation is unlikely to succeed. Stakeholders’ perceptions of surveillance and data collection evolve alongside cognitive changes, highlighting a need for more nuanced ethical frameworks that include ongoing consent processes, regular reevaluations of residents’ preferences, and adaptive RTLS that align with their changing needs and preferences (Liougas et al., Submitted). Such approaches can enhance autonomy and promote equity and inclusivity in LTC.

Finally, the identified ethical challenges also intersect with the goals of using technologies like RTLS to design and use predictive analytics in LTC. Without early engagement in data collection and usage, the introduction of advanced AI applications will likely face even greater resistance (Berridge et al., 2022; Chien et al., 2024). Tools that continuously monitor patterns of movement or behavior amplify privacy concerns, underscoring that transparency and efforts to promote stakeholder trust in data governance are critical steps to bridge current usage and future predictive potential (OECD, 2015).

The role of expectations and communication

Misalignment between stakeholder expectations and actual RTLS functionality significantly contributed to its failure. Care partners and staff initially believed that the RTLS provided advanced functionalities, such as fall detection and real-time monitoring alerts, and that they would easily be able to integrate collected data into care planning, leading to frustration and eroded trust when these expectations were not met (Greenhalgh et al., 2017). The NASSS framework emphasizes managing stakeholder expectations through clear communication and transparency about the technology’s capabilities and limitations (Greenhalgh et al., 2017). However, presenting RTLS inspirationally rather than realistically, created a “design-reality gap” (Heeks, 2006), which undermined stakeholder confidence and willingness to adopt it. Studies confirm that overpromising technology functionality without adequate stakeholder education can lead to disillusionment and abandonment (Haslam-Larmer et al., 2024; Lipsitz, 2012; Neves et al., 2023). Given growing attention and criticism of AI “hype” in health care, our findings provide further support for calls for a more responsible approach to policy and regulation of AI that involves rigorous evaluation of functionality of new technologies with those directly involved to mitigate potential harms—before their deployment in LTC (Lukkien et al., 2023; Strange, 2024).

Furthermore, aspirational framing of RTLS as a pathway to predictive analytics created unrealistic expectations. Care partners expressed interest in using RTLS data to improve daily care, such as automating monitoring toileting frequency or identifying early health risks. However, insufficient communication about achievable goals with the use of RTLS in the home deepened their dissatisfaction, emphasizing the importance of aligning technological narratives with realistic implementation plans.

Power relations and missed opportunities for shared decision making

The RTLS implementation revealed the hierarchical power imbalances within LTC. Managers held disproportionate control over technology selection and implementation, with minimal consultation or ongoing engagement of direct care staff, residents, or care partners. This dynamic reflects broader critiques of LTC technology adoption, where organizational priorities often overshadow the voices of those most directly affected (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022; Niemeijer et al., 2015). Excluding residents from decision-making processes is particularly problematic given the deeply personal implications of surveillance technologies. Residents expressed discomfort and privacy concerns with the wearable and yet their perspectives were frequently dismissed or rationalized by care partners and staff as secondary to safety. This marginalization of resident voices underscores the need for participatory approaches to technology prioritizing shared decision making and respect for individual autonomy (Berridge et al., 2022; Grigorovich et al., 2022).

Theoretical contributions and implications for policy

This theoretically informed study provides further support for the importance of holistic and participatory approaches to adoption of technology that integrates technical, social, and organizational factors (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977; Cherns, 1987; Greenhalgh et al., 2017). Although RTLS has been promoted for its potential to support clinical workflows and collect longitudinal data, findings from this study suggest that without meaningful stakeholder engagement and acceptance, RTLS may not currently possess any transformative potential. Specifically, the study highlights three critical conditions necessary to realize those benefits:

Iterative and collaborative design processes: All stakeholders must be meaningfully engaged throughout the design and implementation phases to ensure the technology genuinely aligns with their needs and expectations. Without such involvement, RTLS will likely continue to be misaligned with user requirements, limiting its practical utility and sustainability.

From a policy perspective, these findings suggest that current regulatory frameworks for RTLS may be insufficient and should explicitly mandate stakeholder involvement in technology design and implementation processes, clear data governance protocols, and education about technology functionality. Absent of these requirements, implementation of RTLS will continue to face challenges that significantly limit its value and sustainability in LTC settings.

Limitations and directions for future research

Study limitations include having a small number of resident participants, and it being conducted in a single LTC home in Canada with one type of commercially available RTLS. We did not collect comprehensive demographic information, including on factors known to influence experiences with technology (e.g., digital literacy) nor explored in our analysis how historical and contemporary conditions of inequality and oppression influence experiences with RTLS. We also did not include residents who were not able to participate in an interview, which includes those who live with advanced cognitive decline and represent a substantial portion of the LTC home population. Future multi-site research studies with more varied populations and alternative methods of data collection are thus needed to evaluate RTLS use and impact more comprehensively, including how these can exacerbate systemic inequalities.

Another limitation of this study was that we did not explore cost-related considerations, and thus conducting economic analyses of RTLS in LTC, including identifying its financial costs and benefits to LTC homes and ultimately to residents, will be an important future direction. Current research suggests that implementation of RTLS can involve high costs for hardware, customization and ongoing maintenance (Lopes et al., 2025), and thus cost–benefit analyses will be particularly important to identify the opportunity costs of investment in RTLS within the resource restricted environment of LTC over alternative approaches to improve quality of care (Berridge & Grigorovich, 2022). These studies should also explore the long-term implications of RTLS and similar surveillance technologies on resident well-being, staff workflows, and organizational outcomes. Finally, more research is needed to investigate whether and how AI-enabled predictive tools based on location data obtained using RTLS could be used to enhance care delivery in LTC, addressing ethical concerns and participatory approaches to data collection and implementation processes that foster stakeholder trust.

Conclusion

This study illustrates that implementing RTLS technology in LTC settings is not merely a technical challenge but fundamentally a sociotechnical endeavor demanding thoughtful alignment between innovation and human values. The implementation and eventual abandonment of the RTLS in this LTC home reveals critical insights into the complex interplay between technological innovation, organizational contexts, and stakeholder expectations. While stakeholders initially accepted RTLS due to perceived enhancements in resident safety and operational efficiency, substantial gaps between anticipated benefits and actual system function/implemented features undermined sustained adoption. These findings align with existing sociotechnical and critical research on AI and data intensive technologies in health care and provide a useful empirical basis for future studies to ensure a deeper alignment with users’ real-world practices, values, and expectations and to advance quality of care in LTC settings.
