Walnut kernel pellicle polyphenols: subcritical water extraction, digestive characteristics, and their effect on gut microbiota and metabolites
Authors: Xinyu Yuan, Qiqi Feng, Qing Wang, Tao Wang, Zhu Qiao, Hongyu Pan, Yujie Zhong, Yongliang Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWalnut kernel pellicle, a by-product of the walnut industry, is rich in polyphenols. However, research on walnut kernel pellicle polyphenols (WPP) remains limited, and the composition and bioactivity of WPP are influenced by extraction methods, gastrointestinal digestion, and colonic fermentation. Therefore, this study aimed to optimize subcritical water extraction (SWE) and NKA-9 resin purification of WPP. Next, the changes in WPP throughout simulated gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation were evaluated. The results demonstrated that SWE achieved higher polyphenol yield compared to ultrasound-assisted extraction, with ellagic acid and ellagitannins as the main components. Although digestion and fermentation reduced WPP contents and antioxidant activities, they facilitated the biotransformation of ellagic acid and ellagitannins into urolithins. Meanwhile, WPP modulated the gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria. Additionally, WPP enhanced beneficial microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids and indole derivatives. These findings highlighted the potential of WPP as a functional ingredient for gut health.Keywords: Walnut pellicle polyphenols, Gastrointestinal digestion, Colonic fermentation, Intestinal microbiota, Short-chain fatty acids, Indole derivatives

Introduction

Yunnan walnuts (Juglans sigillata D.) are recognized for their rich content of bioactive compounds with significant health-promoting properties (Pycia & Ivanisova, 2020). Among walnut tissues, the kernel pellicle, a thin outer layer surrounding the kernel, represents a particularly valuable but underutilized components. Despite accounting for only 10 % of the total kernel mass, it contains 3–7 times higher polyphenol content than walnut shells, 4–9 times greater concentration than green husks, and 60–67 times more polyphenols than peeled kernels (Fang et al., 2021). This remarkable polyphenol enrichment makes walnut kernel pellicle polyphenols (WPP) a compelling subject for nutritional and pharmacological research. Currently, the primary methods for extracting polyphenols include solvent extraction, ultrasound-assisted extraction, and enzyme-assisted extraction, etc., which present several limitations, such as solvent residues and suboptimal efficiency. Subcritical water extraction (SWE) has emerged as a green and efficient alternative, offering high extraction yield and industrial scalability potential (Zhang et al., 2020). Moreover, SWE can modify the structure of bioactive compounds, enhancing their functionality. For instance, Sedem Dzah et al. (2020) compared four methods for extracting buckwheat polyphenols and found that SWE exhibited a higher extraction yield (53.3 mg/g), and the antioxidant activity of SWE-extracted polyphenols was superior to that of ultrasound-assisted solvent extraction, ultrasound-assisted SWE, and hot water extraction. Although SWE has demonstrated efficacy for various plant polyphenols, its application to WPP extraction remains unexplored, particularly concerning optimal extraction parameters, resultant polyphenol profiles, and bioactivity preservation.

Polyphenols exhibit diverse physiological benefits, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, anticarcinogenic potential, cardiovascular protective effects, and metabolic regulatory properties (Aziz et al., 2024). However, their bioavailability and efficacy are influenced by gastrointestinal digestion. During the gastric digestion phase, the highly acidic environment and proteolytic action of pepsin can induce molecular degradation of polyphenols (Morzel et al., 2022). Subsequently, in the intestinal digestion phase, the neutral to weakly alkaline environment, along with the emulsifying effect of bile salts and the catalytic action of pancreatic enzymes, further affects the chemical stability of polyphenols (Wojtunik-Kulesza et al., 2020). This pH- and enzyme-dependent instability represents a major limitation for polyphenol bioavailability.

Although a proportion of polyphenols is degraded during gastrointestinal digestion, the majority will reach the colon intact, where they undergo complex interactions with gut microbiota. Studies have shown that polyphenols can promote the growth of beneficial microbes and inhibit the abundance of detrimental bacteria (Nash et al., 2018). For example, red wine polyphenols can increase the abundance of beneficial bacteria, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Roche in the intestines of patients with metabolic syndrome, while reducing the relative abundance of harmful bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Enterobacter cloacae (Nash et al., 2018). Quercetin can increase Akkermansia muciniphila abundance, thus mitigating obesity (Tan et al., 2021). In addition to regulating gut microbiota, polyphenols can stimulate the gut microbiota to produce beneficial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), indoles, and their derivatives. SCFAs play crucial roles in maintaining gut health. Butyric acid, for instance, serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes, helps maintain gut barrier integrity, reduces inflammation, and regulates the immune system (Kalkan et al., 2025). Indoles are aromatic compounds, have been shown to exhibit a variety of biological activities such as modulating immune response and effecting the central nervous system through the gut-brain axis (Gao et al., 2020). While polyphenols influence gut microbiota, bioactive derivatives with enhanced absorption and efficacy can also be produced from polyphenols through gut microbiota metabolism. For instance, ellagitannins and ellagic acid are converted into urolithins by gut microbiota (Hua et al., 2024). The urolithins exhibit anti-inflammatory and colon cancer preventive properties (Massaga et al., 2025). Although these mechanisms are well-established for common polyphenols, the specific interactions between WPP and gut microbiota require further investigation, particularly regarding their effects on gut microbiota composition, microbial metabolites, and the bio-transformation of WPP.

Thus, to evaluate the potential of WPP as a functional bioactive compound, the present study aims to 1) optimize the parameters for extracting WPP by SWE, 2) purify WPP using NKA-9 macroporous resin, 3) assess the gastrointestinal digestive stability of WPP, 4) examine how WPP affects gut microbiota composition and metabolism, and 5) investigate the transformation of WPP by gut microbiota.

Materials and methods

Materials

Folin-Ciocalteu's phenol reagent, indole-3-acrylic acid, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, indole-3-pyruvic acid, and indole-3-butyric acid were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Yeast extract, heme chloride, bile salt, L-cysteine salt, bladed azurite, vitamin K1, artificial gastric fluid, artificial intestinal fluid, indole-3-carboxaldehyde, tryptophan, 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridinyl)-S-triazine (TPTZ), 2,2-azinoline (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox), syringic acid, protocatechuic acid, quercetin, ellagic acid, and kaempferol were purchased from Yuanye Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, valeric acid, isobutyric acid, and isovaleric acid were obtained from Aladdin Biochemistry Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of WPP

Walnut kernel pellicle was provided by the Yunnan Academy of Forestry and Grassland Science. The walnut kernel pellicle powder was prepared by drying, crushing, and then sieving it through a 60-mesh screen. Subsequently, single-factor experiments and response surface methodology (RSM) were employed to determine the optimal conditions for extracting WPP from the powder using SWE. Three single factors are as follows: Temperatures (100, 110, 120, 140, 160, and 180 °C), time (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min), and solid-liquid ratios (1:30, 1:40, 1:50, 1:60, 1:70, 1:80 g/mL). The three factors and levels for response surface methodology (RSM) are as follows: temperatures (100, 110, and 120 °C); time (5, 15, and 25 min); solid-liquid ratios (1:40, 1:50, and 1:60 g/mL). After extraction, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation (3000 g, 10 min) and freeze-dried to obtain the crude WPP.

Purification of WPP

Macroporous resin was used to purify the crude WPP. Firstly, the optimal resin for purifying crude WPP was screened by evaluating its adsorption and desorption rates. Then, the concentration and pH of crude WPP, and the ethanol volume fraction for washing WPP, were determined using static adsorption-desorption experiments. Subsequently, dynamic adsorption-desorption experiments were carried out to derive the up-sampling flow rate and volume. Briefly, macroporous resin was packed into a φ1.6 cm × 26 cm glass column, and the crude WPP solution (2 mg/mL, pH = 2) was loaded onto the column at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, 2 mL/min, and 3 mL/min. Subsequently, WPP was eluted with 45 % ethanol at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Effluent was collected until the polyphenol content reached the leakage point of 1/10. The collected eluate fractions were concentrated and freeze-dried to obtain purified WPP.

Identification of WPP components

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole Orbitrap tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS2) (Q Exactive Focus, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, America) equipped with a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.9 μm) was used to identify WPP components. The injection volume was 2 μL with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phases consisted of (A) 0.1 % formic acid and (B) acetonitrile, with the following gradient program: 0–1 min, 5 % B; 1–5 min, 15 % B; 5–10 min, 38 % B; 10–15 min, 65 % B; 15–18 min, 80 % B; 18–20 min, 80 % B; 20–22 min, 100 % B. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in negative ion mode with electrospray ionization (ESI) under the following conditions: 3.5 kV spray voltage, 320 °C capillary temperature, full scan mode (100–1000 m/z). Data analysis was performed with XCalibur software. External standards, including syringic acid, protocatechuic acid, quercetin, ellagic acid, gallic acid, and kaempferol, were used for quantification. The contents of ellagic acid pentoside, ellagic acid hexoside, galloyl-HHDP-glucose, trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose, HHDP-glucose, galloyl-bis-HHDP-glucose, bis-HHDP-glucose, digalloyl-HHDP-glucose, and casuarinin were expressed as μg/mg ellagic acid. The contents of monogalloyl-glucose, digalloyl-glucose, and 1,3,6-Tri-O-galloyl-β-d-glucose were expressed as μg/mg gallic acid.

Detection of polyphenol contents

The total WPP content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. Briefly, WPP solution (0.5 mL, 0.01 mg/mL) was mixed with 10 % Folin-Ciocalteu's phenol reagent (2.5 mL) and allowed to react for 5 min. Subsequently, 2.0 mL of 7.5 % Na2CO3 was added and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (T9CS, Persee General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The results were expressed as milligram equivalents of gallic acid per gram of WPP (mg GAE/g DW).

Antioxidant activity assay

The capacity of WPP to scavenge DPPH free radicals

WPP (0.4 mL, 0.01 mg/mL) was thoroughly mixed with 0.1 mM/L DPPH solution (2 mL). The mixture was then incubated in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm by a UV spectrophotometer (T9CS, Persee General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

The capacity of WPP to scavenge ABTS free radicals

A 0.5 mL of WPP solution (0.01 mg/mL) was thoroughly combined with 4 mL of 0.1 mM ABTS solution, and incubated in the dark at 30 °C for 6 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm by a UV spectrophotometer (T9CS, Persee General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

The capacity of WPP to reduce iron ions

A 150 μL of WPP (0.01 mg/mL) was uniformly combined with 4.5 mL of 0.1 mM FRAP solution and then incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min. The absorbance was measured at 593 nm by a UV spectrophotometer (T9CS, Persee General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Simulated in vitro digestion of WPP

The methods of in vitro simulated digestion were referred to as those of Tan et al. (2023) with slight modifications. For simulated gastric digestion, 0.5 g of WPP was mixed with 35 mL ultrapure water and 15 mL artificial gastric fluid in an anaerobic incubator (YQX-T, Longyue Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) under 10 % oxygen concentration. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 2 using 1 M HCl. Then the anaerobic incubator was sealed and stirred at 37 °C, 100 rpm for 1 h in the dark. Next, 10 mL of the gastric digestion mixture was immediately frozen at −80 °C and then centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze-dryer (LGJ-10 FD, Songyuan Huaxing Technology Develop Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to obtain gastric digestion products (GD).

A gastric digestion mixture was used to simulate intestinal digestion. Briefly, 40 mL of gastric digestion mixture was transferred into an anaerobic incubator and mixed thoroughly with 20 mL of artificial small intestine solution. Then, 6 M NaOH was used to adjust the pH to 7. Next, the anaerobic incubator was sealed and incubated at 37 °C, 100 rpm, 8 % O₂ for 3 h. The supernatant was obtained by centrifuging the digested mixture (3000 g, 10 min) and then freeze-dried to make the intestinal digestion product (ID).

In vitro simulated fermentation

The method of in vitro simulated fermentation was referred to the research conducted by Tan et al. (2023). In addition, the fermentation conditions including duration and WPP concentration described in the above literature were optimized by other members of our research team. In in vitro simulated fermentation experiments to evaluate the fermentation characteristics of active substances including polyphenols and polysaccharides, three fecal samples from healthy donors are sufficient to meet experimental requirements. Numerous authoritative studies have confirmed that using fecal samples from three healthy donors yields stable and reproducible data, including consistent shifts in microbial community structure and metabolic profiles (Cui et al., 2024; Ge et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2022). Accordingly, fecal samples were provided by three healthy volunteers who were free from metabolic and gastrointestinal diseases and had not taken antibiotics for at least 3 months. To create an enteric bacterial solution, fecal samples from three healthy volunteers were mixed in a ratio of 1:1:1 and then blended with phosphate buffer (pH = 7, 0.1 M) at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio. Next, the mixture was filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to get enteric bacterial solution. Subsequently, a mixture of 0.4 g WPP, 18 mL of the enteric bacterial solution, and 22 mL brain heart infusion agar was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. The samples were taken at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after fermentation for subsequent analysis.

16S rRNA sequencing

The 16S rRNA sequencing was performed by Marjorie Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The DNA concentration and purity were checked on 1 % agarose gel and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States). The V3-V4 variable region was amplified using upstream primer 27F (5’-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGGGGGGCTCAG-3′) and downstream primer 1492R (5’-RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The quality of amplification products was first evaluated using 2 % agarose gel electrophoresis. Following this, the desired bands were excised from the gel, and the products were recovered using the AxyPrepDNA Gel Recovery Kit (AXYGEN, NY 14831, USA). Quantification of the recovered products was then performed with the QuantiFluor™-ST Blue Fluorescent Quantification System (Corporate Campus, Madison, USA). DNA libraries were constructed from equimolar mixtures of purified products using the SMRTbell Pretreatment Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, California, USA). The purified libraries were sequenced by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Then, optimized HiFi reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1 with 97 % sequence similarity. Classification analyses were performed on each OTU representative sequence.

Determination of SCFAs

The fermentation supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (4 °C, 11000 g, 5 min). For extraction, the supernatant was mixed with 0.2 mL of 50 % H2SO4 and 0.4 mL of ethyl acetate, then incubated at 4 °C for 10 min. After subsequent centrifugation (6000 g, 5 min), the organic phase was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS-QP2010 Ultra, SHIMADZU, Oregon, USA) with an HP-FFAP column (0.25 μm, 30 m × 0.32 mm). The column oven temperature was initially set at 100 °C, then increased to 140 °C at a rate of 6 °C/min, to 200 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, and held at 200 °C for 3 min. He was used as the carrier gas, and the sample was injected at a split ratio of 1:100 with a volume of 1 μL. Quantification was done using external standards including acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, and isovaleric acid.

Determination of indole metabolites

The fermentation solution was centrifuged (4 °C, 11000 g, 5 min) to obtain the supernatant. Then, 2 mL of the supernatant was vigorously mixed with 2 mL of ethyl acetate acidified with 1.5 % formic acid by vortexing for 2 min. After phase separation by centrifugation (4 °C, 11,000 g, 10 min), the organic phase was collected, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen stream, and reconstituted in 150 μL of methanol. The extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to analysis. Analysis was performed using UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS2 (Q Exactive Focus, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, America) with chromatographic conditions as described in section 2.4. The mass spectrometry parameters were as follows: electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive ion mode, 3.5 kV spray voltage, 320 °C capillary temperature, and full scan mode (100–1000 m/z). Quantification was performed using external calibration curves of authentic standards including indole-3-carboxaldehyde, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, indole-3-acrylic acid, indole-3-pyruvic acid, and indole-3-butyric acid.

Statistical analysis

As for multiple comparisons, the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 25 to analyze significant differences between different groups, and Duncan's test was applied to analyze differences between groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Optimization of WPP extraction process: one-way and response surface experiments

SWE offers significant advantages, including environmental friendliness and high efficiency. Therefore, SWE was employed to extract polyphenols from walnut kernel pellicle. Firstly, the optimal temperature, time, and solid-liquid ratio were determined through single-factor experiments. The highest polyphenol yield (332.47 mg GAE/g) was achieved at 110 °C (Fig. 1A), consistent with findings from chestnut husk extraction (Ferreira et al., 2022). Temperature exceeding 110 °C led to a significant decrease in yield, likely due to thermal degradation. The initial increase in yield up to 110 °C may result from the disruption of lignin-phenolic acid bonds. The optimal extraction time was 15 min (Fig. 1B), while the optimal solid-liquid ratio was 1:50 (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, RSM was used to further optimize these parameters (Table S1). Three-dimensional plots (Fig. 1D-F) revealed the maximum yield conditions, with temperature being the most influential factor-consistent with previous studies on SWE extraction of polyphenols (Çalhan et al., 2023). A quadratic regression model was derived as follows: polyphenol content (mg GAE/g) = 365.29–7.72 A - 5.08B - 0.9166C - 5.25AB + 10.79 AC + 2.48 BCE - 17.84A2–11.83B2–11.36C2 (where A = temperature, B = time, C = solid-liquid ratio). The model was highly significant with p < 0.05, and the lack of fit was not significant (p = 0.8953), confirming its reliability. The optimal extraction parameters predicted by the model were 112 °C, 11 min, and 1:48 solid-liquid ratio. Three validation experiments yielded an average polyphenol content of 358.79 ± 0.85 mg GAE/g, which was close to the predicted value of 365.29 mg GAE/g, with a relative error of 1.81 %. Furthermore, this study showed that SWE achieved a higher polyphenol yield compared to ethanol extraction (polyphenol contents: 316.07 mg GAE/g) (Fig. S1A). Other studies have also shown that SWE is more efficient than traditional extraction methods, such as hot water or methanol extraction (Wang et al., 2021). Overall, the results above showed that SWE is an efficient method for extracting polyphenols from walnut kernel pellicles.

Optimization of the polyphenol purification process

Macroporous resins are ideal for polyphenol purification due to their ability to effectively adsorb polyphenols while removing impurities like sugars, fats, and inorganic salts (Chen et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 2A, NKA-9 macroporous resins demonstrated superior performance with adsorption and desorption rates of 74.98 % and 88.69 %, respectively. This high efficiency is likely attributed to its high polarity, which complements the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups in WPP. Subsequently, the optimal conditions for NKA-9 resin purification of WPP were studied. As shown in Fig. 2B, the highest adsorption rate (40.53 %) was observed at 2 mg/mL WPP. Beyond this concentration, adsorption rates declined greatly due to the saturation of the resin's active sites. pH also significantly influenced adsorption performance, with optimal results obtained under acidic conditions with 61.26 % adsorption rate (Fig. 2C). This may because alkaline conditions induce polyphenol polycondensation, reducing their binding affinity. For elution optimization, the highest desorption efficiency (96.52 %) was obtained with 45 % ethanol solution (Fig. 2D), consistent with findings by Hou et al. (2025), where desorption rates decreased at ethanol concentrations exceeding 50 %. Dynamic adsorption studies revealed flow rate dependence (Fig. 2E). At higher flow rates (3 mL/min), the leakage point occurred at just 4 BV, indicating insufficient adsorption. Conversely, a low flow rate will lead to decreased adsorption efficiency. Therefore, a loading flow rate of 2 mL/min was chosen as the optimal loading flow rate. According to the elution curve, there was no trailing phenomenon at the end of elution, confirming effective separation (Fig. 2F). After purification, the WPP content increased from 358.79 ± 0.85 mg GAE/g to 585.30 ± 2.54 mg GAE/g (Fig. S1B).

Effect of in vitro simulated digestion and colonic fermentation on WPP content and activity

Polyphenols are highly sensitive to gastrointestinal conditions. To evaluate the impact of digestion on the stability and bioactivity of WPP, we measured the changes in WPP content and antioxidant activity during in vitro simulated digestion and colonic fermentation. Before the study, we assessed the safety of WPP by administering it to C57BL/6 mice at 300 mg/kg daily for four weeks. Results showed no adverse effects on body weight gain or overall health status (Fig. S2A). Histopathological examination of liver tissues, a key organ for metabolic and toxicological evaluation, revealed no signs of hepatocellular damage, inflammation, or other pathological alterations (Fig. S2B, C). During in vitro digestion, total polyphenol content exhibited a slight decrease in the gastric phase but declined sharply in the intestinal phase (from 553.80 to 224.75 mg GAE/g). This observation aligns with the lower stability of polyphenols under alkaline vs acidic conditions (Shang et al., 2022). During colonic fermentation, the contents of WPP continued to decrease (from 221.20 at 0 h to 145.60 mg GAE/g at 48 h) (Fig. 3A).

Consistent with the observed reduction in WPP content, DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging capacity, as well as FRAP iron ion reducing activity, decreased during in vitro simulated digestion and colonic fermentation. After gastrointestinal treatment, DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging capacities decreased by 78.5 % and 70.1 %, respectively (Fig. 3B, C), while FRAP iron ion reducing activity decreased by 70.1 % (Fig. 3D). These results are consistent with previous findings for Aronia melanocarpa extract, where the antioxidant capacity of the extracts is reduced after intestinal digestion (Li et al., 2024). After colonic fermentation for 48 h, the DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging capacity, as well as FRAP iron ion reducing activity, continue to decline by 25.20 %, 40.15 %, and 23.63 %, respectively, suggesting that WPP may be metabolized by intestinal microbiota.

Effect of in vitro simulated digestion and colonic fermentation on WPP components

The variations in WPP content and antioxidant activity during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and fermentation likely reflect compositional changes in WPP components. Therefore, the changes in WPP components during simulated digestion and fermentation were studied. As shown in Table 1 and Table S2, the major components of WPP include phenolic acids (protocatechuic acid, syringic acid, gallic acid, and ellagic acid), ellagic acid derivatives (ellagic acid pentoside and ellagic acid hexoside), ellagitannins (HHDP-glucose, trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose, galloyl-HHDP-glucose, etc.), gallic acid derivatives (monogalloyl-glucose), and flavonoids (quercetin). Among them, ellagitannins are the most abundant, consistent with previous reports that walnuts and their by-products are rich sources of ellagitannins (van der Beek et al., 2015). Notably, our findings align with existing literature documenting the presence of phenolic acids and their derivatives, including gallic acid and catechin derivatives (Shang et al., 2022).

Ellagitannins are hydrolyzable tannins characterized by a glucose/polyol core esterified with gallic acid-derived hexahydroxydibenzoyl (HHDP) units (Lipinska et al., 2014). Under physiological conditions, ellagitannins can be hydrolyzed to ellagic acid. After simulated gastrointestinal digestion, the contents of most polyphenolic components decreased significantly. Especially, trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose, galloyl-HHDP-glucose, and bis-HHDP-glucose contents were reduced by 86.03 %, 57.65 %, and 54.27 %, respectively. This may be due to the conversion of these components into ellagic acid. However, the contents of ellagic acid, ellagic acid pentoside, and ellagic acid hexoside also decreased by 50.92 %, 32.92 %, and 16.62 %, respectively, suggesting their instability in the gastrointestinal environment.

After colonic fermentation, most WPP components exhibited further degradation, presumably due to microbial utilization. While there were still some fractions that increased first and then decreased. The content of trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose increased from 20.54 to 50.91 μg/mL after 6 h of fermentation, and then decreased to 19.91 μg/mL after 48 h of fermentation. The content of bis-HHDP-glucose increased from 14.39 to 23.09 μg/mL at 12 h fermentation, and then decreased to 19.11 μg/mL at 48 h fermentation. Ellagic acid was increased from 0 to 24 h, while it decreased greatly at 48 h, which may be due to some ellagitannins being converted into ellagic acid at 0–24 h, while ellagic acid was metabolized into urolithins by the intestinal microbiota. Urolithins are products of ellagic acid metabolism by the gut microbiota. In support of this metabolic pathway, we detected several urolithin metabolites. There was a significant accumulation of urolithins (especially urolithin A, urolithin C, urolithin M5, and urolithin M6), with urolithin M5 showing the most marked accumulation during fermentation (Table 2). In addition, flavonoids including quercetin and kaempferol, remained relatively stable throughout the digestion and fermentation. The proposed bio-transformation pathways of WPP through gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation can be seen in Fig. 4.

Proposed biotransformation pathways of walnut kernel pellicle polyphenols (WPP) through gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation. Trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose and galloyl-bis-HHDP-glucose undergo initial hydrolysis, facilitated by enzymatic activity, gut microbiota, or acidic/alkaline conditions, releasing galloyl units and yielding intermediates such as galloyl-HHDP-glucose and HHDP-glucose. The HHDP is highly unstable and rapidly undergoes spontaneous lactonization to form ellagic acid. Additionally, ellagic acid derivatives, including ellagic acid hexoside and ellagic acid pentoside, can be hydrolyzed under similar conditions (acidic/alkaline environments or microbial action) to release ellagic acid along with their corresponding sugar moieties. Subsequently, ellagic acid can be further metabolized by the gut microbiota through a series of dehydroxylation and decarboxylation reactions, ultimately yielding bioactive urolithins such as Urolithin A, B, and C.

Bioaccessibility of WPP

Bioaccessibility refers to the proportion of food-derived bioactive compounds that are released from the food matrix during digestion and become available for intestinal absorption (Dominguez-Fernandez et al., 2021). The gastrointestinal bioaccessibility of WPP during gastrointestinal digestion varies significantly, mainly depending on their chemical structure, substituent type, and molecular size (Table 3). Among phenolic acids, syringic acid (containing methoxy groups) demonstrated the highest bioaccessibility (95.17 %), attributed to the steric hindrance effect of its methoxyl groups. In contrast, free phenolic acids such as gallic acid and protocatechuic acid exhibited moderate stability with bioaccessibility of 61.00 % and 60.98 %, respectively, which may be due to the susceptibility of their phenolic hydroxyl groups to oxidation or binding with intestinal contents. Among ellagic acid derivatives, hexoside forms (bioaccessibility of 83.40 %) displayed greater stability than pentosides (bioaccessibility of 67.00 %) or free ellagic acid (bioaccessibility of 49.10 %), suggesting glycosylation confers intestinal protection. Polymeric tannins like trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose (bioaccessibility of 13.98 %), bis-HHDP-glucose (bioaccessibility of 45.77 %), and galloyl-HHDP-glucose (bioaccessibility of 42.36 %) displayed low bioaccessibility due to the hydrolysis-prone ester bonds. In contrast, digalloyl-HHDP-glucose (bioaccessibility of 99.71 %) exhibited relatively high bioaccessibility, possibly because partial hydrolysis of trigalloyl-HHDP-glucose converted it into digalloyl-HHDP-glucose. Additionally, casuarinin (bioaccessibility of 94.39 %) demonstrated exceptionally high stability, likely due to its C-glycosidic bond or steric protection. While monogalloyl-glucose demonstrated exceptional stability (99.78 % bioaccessibility), its more acylated counterparts, digalloyl-glucose (42.74 % bioaccessibility) and tri-O-galloyl-β-d-glucose (19.43 % bioaccessibility), showed significantly reduced stability. This pattern can be attributed to the increased susceptibility of multiply esterified compounds to hydrolytic cleavage. Importantly, the hydrolysis of these higher galloylated forms (digalloyl-and trigalloyl-glucose) likely contributes to the sustained high bioaccessibility of monogalloyl-glucose through enzymatic conversion pathways.

Effect of WPP on the diversity of intestinal microbiota

Polyphenols retained during the gastrointestinal digestion stage will reach the colon, where they play a pivotal role in modulating intestinal microbiota (Zhao et al., 2021). To investigate the effects of WPP on gut microbial diversity and composition, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed. As shown in Fig. 5A, B, there were significant differences in microbial diversity and community structure between the Ctrl and WPP groups, demonstrating WPP's substantial impact on gut microbiota composition. At the phylum level, the dominant gut microbes were Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota in all groups, consistent with typical human gut microbiota profiles (Chen et al., 2021). The Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio is a notable marker of gut microbiota dysbiosis (Stojanov et al., 2020). After 48 h of fermentation with WPP, the abundance of Bacillus gradually increased (from 63.35 % at 0 h to 85.56 % at 48 h), while the abundance of Bacteroides decreased (from 34.79 % at 0 h to 0.74 % at 48 h), increasing the ratio of Bacillus to Bacteroides (Fig. 5C). This suggested that WPP has the potential to promote the growth of the Firmicutes phylum and alleviate intestinal microbial dysbiosis.

From the genus level analysis, fermentation with WPP caused a significant rise in the abundance of Megamonas (peaking at 42.97 % at 48 h) and Faecalibacterium (peaking at 11.57 % after 6 h), while reducing the levels of Ruminococcus, Dorea, Mediterraneibacter, and Blautia (Fig. 4D and Fig. 6A). Similarly, species-level analysis revealed a marked rise in Megamonas rupellensis (peaking at 42.96 % after 48 h) and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (peaking at 9.00 % after 6 h) (Fig. 5E and Fig. 6B). Study has shown that Megamonas rupellensis can metabolize diverse carbohydrates into acetic, propionic, and lactic acids, and these SCFAs are crucial for maintaining intestinal homeostasis (Jia et al., 2022). Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is considered a potential probiotic and has been associated with the protective effects against colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, type II diabetes, and metabolic syndrome (Leylabadlo et al., 2020). Conversely, WPP fermentation reduced the abundance of Blautia wexlerae, Dorea longicatena, Ruminococcus sp. ZG-924, Mediterraneibacter faecis, Ruminococcus sp. AF45-4BH, and Dorea formicigenerans. Dorea formicigenerans is considered one of the potential disease biomarkers. For example, in a study on Alzheimer's disease, the increase in Dorea formicigenerans has been used to construct disease prediction models (Ferreiro et al., 2023). Dorea longicatena is more abundant in obese individuals, which may be associated with fat accumulation and metabolic disorders (Liu et al., 2017). Some species of Ruminococcus, such as Ruminococcus gnavus, are associated with colitis. However, the function of Ruminococcus sp. AF45-4BH and Ruminococcus sp. ZG-924 remains to be studied. Blautia wexlerae is often considered a potentially beneficial microbe, but may also exhibit harmful effects in certain situations, especially in specific diseases such as colitis (Yan et al., 2023). In addition, LDA effect size (LEfSe) analysis further identified that g_Megamonas and s_Megamonas_rupellensis are specific taxons after 24 h of WPP fermentation (Fig. 6C).

Effect of WPP on intestinal microbiota metabolites

SCFAs contents

Polyphenols engage in a bidirectional relationship with gut microbiota: while undergoing microbial metabolism, they simultaneously regulate microbial composition and metabolic activity. A key consequence of this interaction is the production of SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate. SCFAs confer multiple health benefits by strengthening intestinal barrier integrity, reducing systemic endotoxin levels, and suppressing inflammatory responses (Parada Venegas et al., 2019). Prior studies have demonstrated that polyphenol supplementation elevates SCFAs production. For example, Lonicera caerulea L. polyphenol extract significantly increased acetic acid, propionate, and butyrate concentrations while improving gut microbiota composition in hyperglycemic mice (Cao et al., 2023). Arctium lappa L. polyphenol extract reduced serum lipids by enriching SCFA-producing bacteria, thereby boosting acetate production (Lou et al., 2024).

Our results agreed with these findings, demonstrating that WPP fermentation significantly altered SCFAs profiles. As shown in Fig. 7A, fermentation with WPP yielded significantly lower pH vs. control (Fig. 7A), reaching minima at 48 h, which may be due to the production of SCFAs. In addition, this acidic environment may potentially inhibit pathogenic microorganisms while promoting beneficial bacterial growth. During the fermentation process, the total SCFAs contents in the WPP group were significantly higher than that in the Ctrl group and reached its peak (378.80 mM/L) at 12 h (Fig. 7B). Notably, acetic acid levels showed particularly pronounced increases in the WPP group, attaining maximum concentrations of 348.50 mM/L at 12 h. This elevation is physiologically significant as acetic acid enhances insulin sensitivity, reduces postprandial glucose levels, and promotes satiety to support weight management (Ban et al., 2023). Propionic acid exhibited progressive accumulation during WPP fermentation (increasing from 1.48 mM/L at 0 h to 20.01 mM/L at 24 h) (Fig. 7D). The propionic acid is a key regulator of hepatic glucose metabolism and systemic energy homeostasis (Ding et al., 2019). Butyric acid concentration was significantly higher in the WPP group versus Ctrl, reaching maximum levels (13.36 mM/L) at 24 h (Fig. 7E). This metabolite functions as a key energy source for colonocytes and a critical maintainer of intestinal barrier function (van der Beek et al., 2015). Besides, WPP increased isovaleric acid contents (from 0.76 mM/L at 0 h to 2.54 mM/L at 48 h) (Fig. 7F), a metabolite with demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties (Liu et al., 2024). Overall, these findings indicated that WPP effectively boosted SCFAs production, thereby mediating multiple beneficial health effects through its microbial metabolic interactions.

Indole derivatives

Indole and its derivatives constitute a class of biologically active metabolites produced through gut microbial metabolism of tryptophan. These compounds serve as critical mediators in host physiology and have been associated with various pathological conditions, including neuropsychiatric disorders, inflammatory bowel diseases, colorectal cancer, metabolic syndrome, and immune dysregulation (Ala, 2022). Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota-derived indole derivatives, particularly indole-3-lactic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, and indole-3-acetic acid, exert substantial immunomodulatory effects and contribute to intestinal homeostasis (Wang et al., 2024). Consequently, we quantified these indole metabolites. As shown in Fig. 8, the WPP group demonstrated progressive increases in indole-3-carboxaldehyde, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, and indole-3-pyruvic acid levels compared to the control group. Among them, WPP exerted the most pronounced effects on indole-3-pyruvic acid (increased from 5.86 μg/mL (0 h) to 8.10 μg/mL after 24 h fermentation) and indole-3-propionic acid (increased from 0.22 μg/mL (0 h) to 2.57 μg/mL) (Fig. 8C, E). The observed increases in indole-3-pyruvic acid and indole-3-propionic acid are particularly noteworthy due to their well-documented biological functions, which include up-regulating tight junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1), reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α), and activating both aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) pathways (Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, indole-3-propionic acid has been inversely correlated with type 2 diabetes risk and may enhance insulin sensitivity through multiple mechanisms (Sehgal et al., 2022). The above results highlighted the specificity of WPP's influence on microbial metabolic pathways.

Correlation analysis between gut microbiota and microbial metabolites

To evaluate the relationship between gut microbiota and microbial metabolites, we conducted a correlation analysis. As shown in Fig. 9A and B, there are significant microbial-metabolite associations at both taxonomic levels. At the genus level, Gemmiger, Ruminococcus, Mediterraneibacter, Anaerobutyricum, Blautia, Fusicatenibacter, Dorea, and Coprococcus exhibit negative correlations with SCFAs (propionic acid, butyric acid, and isovaleric acid) and indole derivatives (indole-3-carboxaldehyde, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, indole-3-pyruvic acid, and indole-3-butyric acid), while Clostridium, Phascolarctobacterium, and Megamonas show positive associations with these metabolites. These patterns were further corroborated at the species level, where Blautia wexlerae, Fusicatenibacter saccharivorans, Mediterraneibacter faecis, Anaerobutyricum hallii, and several other species maintained negative correlations, while potentially beneficial species including Faecalibacterium sp. I4.3.84, Phascolarctobacterium succinatutens, and Megamonas rupellensis showed positive metabolic associations. These findings suggest complex, taxonomy-dependent relationships between gut microbiota and microbial metabolites that may have important physiological implications. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these associations (especially the beneficial species and metabolisms) remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further mechanistic investigation through targeted culturing studies and metagenomic functional analyses.

Furthermore, as WPP has excellent antioxidant and gut microbiota-regulating activities, it can be used in the food industry, such as fortification of walnut-based products (e.g., walnut milk) or incorporation into functional beverages and dietary supplements targeting gut health. While promising, the application of WPP in the food industry still faces the following challenges: 1) Regulatory approval requires compliance with novel food regulations and robust clinical validation of health claims; 2) Key hurdles include overcoming WPP's inherent bitterness, enhancing processing stability, and improving bioavailability.

Conclusion

Our study developed an optimized extraction and purification protocol for WPP, yielding a high-purity polyphenol containing 585.30 mg GAE/g, predominantly composed of ellagic acid and ellagitannins. After gastrointestinal digestion, WPP contents and antioxidant activity decreased to some extent. During in vitro simulated fermentation, ellagic acid and ellagitannin of WPP can be converted to highly active urolithins. Simultaneously, WPP significantly modulated gut microbiota through increasing Megamonas rupellensis and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, while reducing Dorea longicatena and Blautia wexlerae. In addition, the beneficial gut microbiota metabolites, including SCFAs (notably propionic acid) and indole derivatives (notably indole-3-pyruvic acid and indole-3-propionic acid), increased greatly after WPP treatment. Although WPP can improve gut microbiota and increase their beneficial metabolites, the corresponding relationship between specific gut strains (e.g., Megamonas rupellensis) and metabolites (e.g., indole-3-pyruvic acid and indole-3-propionic acid) remains to be studied. In addition, the study utilized fecal samples from healthy adults, which may not adequately represent the vast inter-individual variability in gut microbial composition. Besides, although in vitro digestion and fermentation models provide unique advantages for polyphenol metabolism research, it may not fully replicate the complexity of the human gut due to simplified physiological conditions (e.g., lack of hormonal regulation, immune responses, and peristalsis), unrepresentative microbiome dynamics (e.g., static fecal inoculate failing to replicate spatial heterogeneity or long-term host-microbe adaptation), and the absence of post-digestive processes. Thus, future research should incorporate more diverse donor samples and advanced models, such as rats, mice or even humans.
