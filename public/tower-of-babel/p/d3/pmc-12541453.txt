A multilayered gap junction network is essential for social decision-making
Authors: Airi Nakayama, Hiroo Kuroyanagi, Hironori J. Matsuyama, Ikue Mori, Naoki Hisamoto, Shunji Nakano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEarly social experiences strongly influence emotions and behaviors, but the underlying neural mechanisms are unclear. This study shows that early experience of crowding exerts lasting effects on foraging behavior and promotes dispersal. This behavioral shift depends on a neural network connected via gap junctions, channel structures that directly link two adjacent cells. This network spans multiple neural circuit layers, connecting density-sensing neurons to a decision-making circuit. Notably, these same gap junction components also regulate entry into developmental diapause in response to high density. The findings reveal that the multilayered gap junction network enables early social cues to shape adult behaviors, highlighting its crucial role in developmental and behavioral plasticity within social contexts.

Animal behavior is influenced by the social context. A classic example is density-dependent dispersal behavior, in which animals in small groups tend to exploit local resources, whereas those in high-density populations are more likely to explore new habitats. Such density-dependent behavioral control is observed across a wide range of species, including invertebrates (1), fish (2), and mammals (3, 4). A comprehensive understanding of decision-making and its neural basis in complex social contexts will also provide key insights into the development of theoretical and computational frameworks for information processing (5).

Social cues modulate behavior in genetically tractable model organisms such as fruit flies and nematodes. In Drosophila, foraging strategy has been shown to be modulated by population density (6, 7). Similarly, exposure to a crowded larval environment results in adult male flies with increased courtship behavior (8). Research on the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans has shown that these animals sense population density by detecting the nematode pheromones (9, 10): C. elegans constantly produces and secretes several derivatives of the dideoxy sugar ascarylose into its environment (11–13), and the concentrations of these ascarosides serve as a measure of the population density. These pheromones are detected by chemosensory neurons and influence larval development. When high pheromone levels accumulate in the environment, combined with other adverse conditions such as food scarcity and high temperature, C. elegans enters a diapause state known as dauer larva. This nonfeeding, stress-resistant, developmentally arrested stage is specialized for survival and dispersal under harsh conditions. Nematode pheromones also regulate adult behavior. For instance, they elicit acute chemotactic responses of attraction or aversion (14–18) and modulate nematode foraging strategy (19, 20). Together with the aforementioned research on Drosophila, these studies demonstrate that innate behaviors are regulated by social cues.

In this study, we investigate whether early-life social experiences modulate learned behavior, focusing on C. elegans thermotaxis (21). In this behavioral paradigm, C. elegans is grown from embryos to adulthood at a constant temperature with food. When adult animals are placed on a spatial temperature gradient, they exhibit a preference for and navigate toward the temperature at which they were previously cultivated. Thus, C. elegans utilizes past experiences to associate temperature information with food availability. The neural circuitry underlying thermotaxis has been extensively characterized (22–27). The core components of this circuit are the AFD thermosensory neurons and their postsynaptic partners, the AIY interneurons (28–34). AFD neurons respond to thermal inputs (35–40), and their intracellular calcium dynamics are phase-locked to oscillatory temperature changes (41). AIY neurons receive input from AFD (30, 31), and their activity correlates with the valence of temperature stimuli (28–30, 42).

Here, we show that C. elegans thermotaxis is modulated by social contexts. While animals grown at moderate population density prefer their previous cultivation temperature, exposure to high population density during the larval stage induces a subset of adults to exhibit exploratory behavior and disperse from this temperature. This social modulation requires a neural network connected via gap junctions, channel structures that directly link the cytoplasm of adjacent cells, allowing the passage of ions and small molecules (43). This gap junction network spans multiple layers of neural circuitry, connecting pheromone-sensing neurons to the circuit that processes thermal information. Analysis of temperature-evoked neural activity reveals that high population density modulates circuit dynamics through this network, altering the perception of stimulus valence and driving dispersal from the previous cultivation temperature. Furthermore, components of this gap junction network also mediate the decision to enter the dauer diapause state. Our results suggest that the multilayered gap junction network is essential for social decision-making and governs both developmental and behavioral plasticity in response to high-density environments.

Results

C. elegans Thermotaxis Behavior Requires rdl-1.

We previously conducted genetic screens to identify mutants defective in thermotaxis behavior and isolated nj262 mutants (42). Under our standard cultivation conditions, where 100 to 300 adult animals were present per plate (medium density, corresponding to 4 to 13 animals/cm2) and were cultivated at 20 °C, wild-type animals preferred their previous cultivation temperature. In contrast, nj262 mutants migrated toward a colder temperature region and displayed negative thermotaxis (Fig. 1 A and B: Note that all the experiments in this study were conducted with animals cultivated under this population density unless otherwise stated). We mapped nj262 to an interval on chromosome IV and identified a mutation within this region that affects the rdl-1 gene. Specifically, nj262 carries a G-to-A transition mutation that disrupts a splice donor site in rdl-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). To confirm that this mutation is responsible for the thermotaxis defect, we introduced the same mutation into the rdl-1 locus and observed that the resulting rdl-1(nj337) animals also exhibited a cryophilic phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Furthermore, we generated a deletion allele of rdl-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Animals carrying this deletion allele, rdl-1(nj335Δ), also dispersed from the cultivation temperature, and this defect was rescued by introducing a genomic fragment containing the rdl-1 locus (Fig. 1C). rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants also displayed cryophilic phenotypes when they were cultivated at 23 °C or when they were placed at a lower temperature region of the temperature gradient (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C–E). These results indicate that rdl-1 is required for proper thermotaxis behavior. rdl-1 encodes the C. elegans ortholog of the mammalian RD3 (Retinal Degeneration 3) protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F) (44). RD3 was originally identified as a regulator of the subcellular localization of guanylate cyclases (45).

rdl-1 Functions in Chemosensory Neurons to Regulate Thermotaxis.

To determine the expression pattern of rdl-1, we generated a strain in which the gfp coding sequence was inserted immediately before the stop codon of the endogenous rdl-1 locus, creating an in-frame rdl-1::gfp knock-in. Unlike rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants, animals carrying the rdl-1::gfp gene did not exhibit a cryophilic phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), indicating that rdl-1 function remained intact in this strain. Examination of GFP expression revealed that rdl-1::gfp was expressed in 11 classes of sensory neurons in the head: ADF, AFD, ASE, ASG, ASI, ASJ, ASK, AWA, AWB, AWC, and BAG (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). This expression pattern is consistent with a previous report using a fosmid-based rdl-1 reporter transgene (44).

To identify the neurons in which rdl-1 functions to regulate thermotaxis, we performed cell-specific rescue experiments. We fused rdl-1 cDNA with various promoters known to drive expression in specific neurons and tested whether expression of rdl-1 in any of the 11 sensory neuron types could rescue the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants. Surprisingly, expression of rdl-1 in any one of four chemosensory neurons, ADF, ASI, ASJ, or ASK, was sufficient to rescue the rdl-1(nj335Δ) defect (Fig. 1 D–G). In contrast, AFD-specific expression of rdl-1 rendered animals thermophilic (positive thermotaxis), likely due to an artifact associated with overexpression (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D). A strain in which rdl-1 is specifically knocked out in AFD by flanking the rdl-1 locus with loxP sequences and expressing Cre recombinase specifically in AFD did not exhibit thermotaxis defect (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Expression in ASE, ASG, AWA, AWB, AWC, or BAG did not rescue the cryophilic defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F–I). Simultaneous expression of rdl-1 in AFD, ASE, ASG, AWA, AWB, AWC, and BAG also caused a thermophilic defect (SI Appendix, Fig. S2J). These results indicate that rdl-1 functions in the four chemosensory neurons, ADF, ASI, ASJ, and ASK, to regulate thermotaxis.

Thermotaxis Behavior Is Modulated by Population Density.

The four chemosensory neurons in which rdl-1 functions have previously been shown to detect nematode pheromones (46–51), which serve as indicators of population density (9–12, 52). Given this, we examined whether thermotaxis behavior is influenced by population density. As described above, when animals were cultivated at a medium population density, wild-type animals preferred the cultivation temperature, whereas rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants dispersed from it (Figs. 1C and 2A). However, when animals were cultivated at a low population density (1 to 10 animals/plate: 0.04 to 0.4 animals/cm2), rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants no longer exhibited a cryophilic phenotype and instead preferred the cultivation temperature, similar to wild-type animals (Fig. 2A). Conversely, when animals were cultivated at a high population density (500 to 1,000 animals/plate: 21 to 42 animals/cm2), wild-type animals exhibited behavior similar to that of rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals: A fraction of the population dispersed from the cultivation temperature and migrated toward the colder side of the temperature gradient (Fig. 2B). To determine whether population density during cultivation or during the thermotaxis assay affected behavior, we cultivated rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals at low or medium density and then subjected them to the thermotaxis assay under a constant low density (four animals per assay plate). We found that population density during cultivation continued to affect thermotaxis behavior, even when the assay was conducted under uniform low density (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). These results indicate that thermotaxis is modulated by population density during cultivation and suggest that rdl-1 mutants have an altered perception of population density, interpreting it as abnormally high even under moderate conditions.

To confirm that the social modulation of thermotaxis involves nematode pheromones, we examined daf-22 mutants, which lack an enzyme required for pheromone synthesis (53, 54). We found that daf-22(m130) mutants preferred the cultivation temperature and that their thermotaxis phenotype was insensitive to high population density (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the daf-22(m130) mutation suppressed the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants cultivated at medium population density (Fig. 2C), and this effect was reversed by introducing a daf-22 genomic clone (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Additionally, when wild-type animals were cultivated in the presence of crude pheromone extracts, they dispersed along the temperature gradient (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). These results indicate that social modulation of thermotaxis is mediated by nematode pheromones.

To identify the developmental time window during which population density experience affects thermotaxis behavior, we conducted population shift experiments, exposing rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals to up- or down-shifts in density at different developmental stages (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). When animals were grown at low density and shifted to medium density immediately before the thermotaxis assay (Day 4), they still preferred the cultivation temperature, similarly to animals that had never experienced medium density (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Likewise, animals exposed to medium density for only 1 d by shifting them on Day 3, when most had reached the fourth larval stage or early adulthood, also preferred the cultivation temperature. By contrast, animals exposed to medium density from Day 2 or Day 1, corresponding to the second (or third) and first larval stages, respectively, exhibited altered thermotaxis behavior. These results suggest two possible requirements for medium-density experience to induce behavioral change: 1) animals need to be exposed to medium density for longer than 1 d, or 2) animals must experience medium density at a developmental stage earlier than the fourth larval stage. To distinguish between these possibilities, we shifted animals to medium density on Day 2, maintained them at medium density for 1 d, and then assessed their thermotaxis behavior on Day 3. These animals dispersed toward the colder side of the temperature gradient (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D), suggesting that medium density experience during the second (or third) larval stage is critical for modifying thermotaxis behavior.

We also conducted downshift experiments. When animals were cultivated at medium density and then shifted to low density immediately before the thermotaxis assay, they still dispersed from the cultivation temperature, similarly to animals continuously raised at medium density (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). However, animals shifted to low density on Day 3 altered their behavior and preferred the cultivation temperature (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). These observations suggest an asymmetric effect of population density shifts: Animals switch from exploitation to exploration when exposed to medium density from the second larval stage, but they can revert from exploration to exploitation at a later stage (fourth larval or young adult stage) if density decreases.

rdl-1 Regulates the Localization of DAF-11 Guanylate Cyclase.

Previous studies have shown that pheromone signaling is mediated by daf-11, which encodes a guanylate cyclase expressed in chemosensory neurons, including ASI, ASJ, ASK, and ADF (55–57). DAF-11 is thought to be constitutively active in animals grown at low population density, whereas its activity is inhibited by pheromones in high-density environments (58). Given that the RD3 proteins control the trafficking of guanylate cyclases (44, 45), we investigated whether rdl-1 regulates the subcellular localization of DAF-11. To address the localization of DAF-11 in single neurons, we generated daf-11a cDNA fused with a gfp coding sequence and attempted to express this fusion gene by the control of an ASI or AWC-specific promoter. We found that injecting these plasmids into the wild-type animals yielded no line with GFP fluorescence. We constructed multiple transgenes with distinct linker sequences or with amino-terminal or carboxy-terminal gfp fusion, but none of these daf-11 cDNA constructs gave GFP fluorescence. When we generated a genomic translational daf-11::gfp gene, we observed that animals carrying this transgene exhibited GFP signals at the sensory endings of chemosensory neurons in wild-type animals (Fig. 3A). We therefore used this transgene to address whether rdl-1 regulates localization of DAF-11. In contrast to the wild-type animals, the DAF-11::GFP signals at the sensory endings were markedly reduced in rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants (Fig. 3A). This defect was rescued by introducing a genomic fragment of rdl-1 (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the previous report (55), the DAF-11::GFP localization was not altered by the population density (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). These results suggest that rdl-1 is required for the proper subcellular localization of DAF-11.

To determine whether daf-11 is involved in the regulation of thermotaxis, we generated a mutation corresponding to a previously characterized hypomorphic allele of daf-11 (Materials and Methods). We observed that animals carrying this mutation, daf-11(snj2), exhibited a cryophilic phenotype (Fig. 3B), indicating that daf-11 is crucial for thermotaxis regulation. These results provide a molecular basis for the abnormal perception of population density in rdl-1 mutants. Specifically, the mislocalization of DAF-11 likely reduces its activity, mimicking the effects of a high-pheromone environment. Consequently, rdl-1 mutants grown at moderate density behave as if they were exposed to high population density. In a subsequent experiment, we used these mutants as a proxy for animals cultivated under high-density conditions.

Identification of a Neural Circuit Required for the Social Modulation of Thermotaxis.

We next aimed to identify the neural circuitry that mediates the social modulation of thermotaxis. Previous connectome analyses (59–61) have shown that each of the four chemosensory neurons, ADF, ASI, ASJ, and ASK, forms synaptic connections with AIA interneurons, which, in turn, extend their network to other neurons, including ASG, ASH, AIB, AWA, and AIZ (Fig. 4A). To determine whether these neurons are required for the social modulation of thermotaxis, we utilized strains in which these neurons were selectively ablated via cell-specific expression of caspase (23), and asked whether ablating individual neurons could make the resulting animals’ thermotaxis phenotype insensitive to rdl-1(nj335Δ). We first observed that ablation of either ASI or ASK suppressed the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 4 B and C), indicating that these chemosensory neurons are required for social modulation. Similarly, in animals lacking AIA, AWA, or AIZ neurons, rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants no longer affected thermotaxis behavior (Fig. 4 D–F), demonstrating that these neurons are also essential for social modulation. By contrast, ablation of AIB, ASG, or ASH did not occlude the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), suggesting that these neurons are dispensable for social modulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that the social modulation of thermotaxis relies on a neural circuit composed of the chemosensory neurons ASI and ASK, as well as the AIA, AWA, and AIZ neurons.

A Multilayered Gap Junction Network Is Required for the Social Modulation of Thermotaxis.

An inspection of the C. elegans wiring diagram (59–61) revealed that each of the chemosensory neurons, ADF, ASI, ASJ, and ASK, connects to the AIA interneuron via a gap junction (Fig. 4A), a channel structure that directly links the cytoplasm of two adjacent cells. Connections via gap junctions extend further into the circuit: AIA is coupled to AWA, which, in turn, is connected to AIZ. Furthermore, AIZ neurons are linked via gap junctions to AFD (Fig. 5A), the major thermosensory neurons essential for thermotaxis behavior (22). To determine whether gap junctions are pivotal for the social modulation of thermotaxis, we examined whether innexins, the invertebrate counterparts of connexins and core components of gap junctions, are required for this process. A previous genome-wide study identified the expression patterns of C. elegans innexin genes in the nervous system (62). We therefore focused on a subset of innexins reported to be expressed in the neural circuit involved in social modulation. Our analysis revealed that two innexin genes, inx-1 and che-7, are essential for the social modulation of thermotaxis. A deletion allele of inx-1, inx-1(tm3524Δ), suppressed the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants, while inx-1(tm3524Δ) animals alone exhibited thermotaxis behavior similar to that of wild-type animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Unlike the wild-type animals, inx-1(tm3524Δ) animals did not alter their thermotaxis behavior when grown at the high population density (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). We previously showed that che-7, formerly known as inx-4, is required for thermotaxis and that che-7(ok2373Δ) animals displayed a thermophilic phenotype (63). Notably, rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants could no longer alter thermotaxis behavior in animals lacking che-7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Thermotaxis phenotype of che-7 mutants was also insensitive to the high population density (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). By contrast, mutations in two other innexin genes, inx-7 and inx-18, did not suppress the effects of social modulation by the rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F). These results suggest that inx-1 and che-7 are specifically required for the social modulation of thermotaxis and underscore the essential role of gap junction–mediated signaling in this process.

Next, we aimed to identify the specific neuronal sites where these innexins function. Specifically, we examined whether cell-specific knockout of inx-1 and che-7 in key circuit components, ASI, AIA, AWA, AIZ, and AFD, abolishes social modulation. To this end, we generated cell-specific conditional knockout strains for inx-1 and che-7 by inserting two loxP sequences flanking their endogenous loci, combined with cell-specific expression of Cre recombinase under promoters that drive expression in candidate neurons. We found that knockout of inx-1 in ASI suppressed the thermotaxis defect of rdl-1(nj335Δ), whereas neither the floxed allele of inx-1 alone nor Cre expression in ASI alone restored the preference for the cultivation temperature in rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 5B). Similarly, knockout of inx-1 in AIA or AIZ also allowed rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals to prefer the cultivation temperature, whereas Cre expression in these neurons alone did not (Fig. 5 C and D). We next examined the effect of che-7 knockout in AWA and found that it suppressed the rdl-1(nj335Δ) defect, while neither the che-7 floxed allele alone nor Cre expression in AWA alone altered the rdl-1(nj335Δ) phenotype (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we investigated the effect of inx-1 and che-7 knockout in AFD. While eliminating either inx-1 or che-7 individually from AFD did not suppress the rdl-1(nj335Δ) defect, simultaneous knockout of inx-1 and che-7 in AFD restored the preference for the cultivation temperature in rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 5F). These results indicate that inx-1 and che-7 function redundantly in AFD to mediate social modulation.

Previous studies have indicated that AFD neurons connect to AIY neurons via a gap junction (59–61) (Fig. 5A) and that inx-1, but not che-7, is expressed in AIY (62). A recent study also showed that elimination of inx-1 from AIY caused abnormal isothermal tracking behavior (64). Given these observations, we tested whether eliminating inx-1 specifically in AIY could also suppress the rdl-1(nj335Δ) defect. We found that rdl-1(nj335Δ); inx-1(nj374, floxed) animals expressing Cre in AIY did not alter the rdl-1 phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G), suggesting that inx-1 does not function in AIY to mediate the social modulation of thermotaxis. Furthermore, these results indicate that the abnormal isothermal tracking behavior observed in inx-1 mutants does not account for the suppression of the rdl-1(nj335Δ) defect. Taken together, our findings highlight the roles of inx-1 and che-7 in ASI, AIA, AWA, AIZ, and AFD, and suggest that a multilayered gap junction network spanning these neural circuits is essential for thermotaxis decision-making in response to social contexts.

Social Contexts Modulate the Processing of Temperature Inputs.

How do social cues alter neural operation and influence the processing of temperature information? To address this question, we conducted calcium (Ca2+) imaging to monitor circuit dynamics in response to temperature stimuli. Previous studies have shown that temperature changes are detected by AFD thermosensory neurons and that Ca2+ levels in AFD increase as temperature rises (35–37, 39, 41, 65). To investigate whether social modulation affects this response, we recorded Ca2+ dynamics in immobilized animals under a microscope while exposed to temperature ramps. Our analysis revealed that AFD Ca2+ levels increased similarly in both wild-type and rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 6A), suggesting that social modulation does not affect the temperature-induced Ca2+ responses in AFD neurons.

To determine whether social contexts influence temperature-evoked neural responses in neurons downstream of AFD, we focused on AIY interneurons, the primary chemical postsynaptic partners of AFD (59–61). AIY is required for thermotaxis behavior (22, 23, 30–32), and we have previously shown that its neural activity correlates with the valence of thermal stimuli (28, 29, 42): Ca2+ levels in AIY increase when the temperature moves toward the cultivation temperature (positive valence) and decrease when the temperature shifts away from it (negative valence). We monitored AIY Ca2+ responses in freely moving animals subjected to a temperature ramp approaching the cultivation temperature (Fig. 6B). Consistent with our previous reports, AIY neurons in wild-type animals predominantly exhibited increased Ca2+ levels (Fig. 6C). By contrast, in rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants, AIY neurons primarily showed a decrease in Ca2+ signals. This defect was rescued by rdl-1 expression in ASI chemosensory neurons (Fig. 6C), indicating that social contexts influence temperature-induced AIY responses. To further support this finding, we monitored AIY responses in wild-type animals cultivated at medium and high population densities. While animals raised under medium-density conditions exhibited AIY Ca2+ increases, those grown at high density predominantly displayed Ca2+ decreases (Fig. 6D). These results suggest that experiencing a high population density alters the perception of stimulus valence and modulates the temperature-evoked activity of AIY interneurons.

We next asked whether the modulation of AIY activity by social contexts requires circuit components identified above. To address this, we recorded AIY Ca2+ responses in animals with genetically ablated AIA neurons and found that rdl-1(nj335Δ) failed to affect AIY responses in this genetic background (Fig. 6E). These results suggest that AIA is essential for transmitting social cues sensed by chemosensory neurons to the temperature-processing circuit (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, we investigated the role of gap junctions in this modulation. We found that inx-1(tm3524Δ) restored excitatory AIY responses in rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 6F), suggesting that the gap junction network facilitates the transfer of social information to the AFD-AIY circuit, thereby altering how thermal inputs are evaluated within this temperature-processing network.

To further investigate whether neurons within this network respond to thermal stimuli and whether their responses are influenced by social contexts, we examined temperature-evoked Ca2+ responses in ASI, ASK, AIA, AWA, and AIZ. We found that AIA, AIZ, and to a lesser extent, ASI exhibited increased Ca2+ levels in response to warming stimuli, with similar responses observed in rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (Fig. 6 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), suggesting that social modulation does not affect the temperature-induced Ca2+ responses in these neurons. When animals expressing Ca2+ indicators in ASK or AWA were subjected to temperature ramps, no significant Ca2+ responses were detected in either wild-type nor rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These results suggest that the social modulation does not affect the temperature-evoked Ca2+ transients in ASI, ASK, AIA, AWA, and AIZ.

Gap Junction Influences the Decision to Enter Developmental Diapause.

C. elegans enters a developmentally arrested stage, known as dauer larva, in response to adverse conditions such as high pheromone concentrations, elevated temperature, and food scarcity (10, 66). The daf-11 guanylate cyclase gene was originally identified as a regulator for dauer development, and daf-11 mutants exhibit a dauer-constitutive (Daf-c) phenotype, in which animals enter diapause independently of environmental cues (67). Given these findings, we investigated whether rdl-1 and gap junction components contribute to the decision-making process for developmental diapause. While rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants did not exhibit an apparent Daf-c phenotype at 20 °C, they showed enhanced dauer formation at 27 °C (68) (Fig. 7A). These results suggest that, similar to daf-11, rdl-1 plays a role in regulating dauer formation. A previous study also showed that daf-11 is required for 2-nonanone avoidance behavior (55). We observed that rdl-1(nj335Δ) mutants were normal in this behavior (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Since rdl-1 mutations likely result in a partial reduction of daf-11 activity, it remains to be determined whether rdl-1 affects only a subset of daf-11-mediated processes.

We further examined the genetic interactions between daf-11 and gap junction components and found that inx-1(tm3524Δ) suppressed both the thermotaxis defect and the Daf-c phenotype of daf-11 mutants (Fig. 7 B and C). Similarly, che-7(ok2373Δ) suppressed the Daf-c phenotype of daf-11(sa195) (Fig. 7D). These observations suggest that a shared genetic program regulates both developmental and behavioral plasticity in response to social cues. Furthermore, they indicate that the gap junction network is a crucial component of this program, facilitating social decision-making.

Discussion

Social interactions represent some of the most intricate behaviors in the animal kingdom. The presence of conspecific or heterospecific individuals often influences an animal’s behavioral strategy to optimize fitness and reproductive success. Social stimuli and constraints exert strong selective pressures on brain evolution, leading to the development of neural mechanisms that regulate behavior in specific social contexts. Here, we demonstrate that C. elegans thermotaxis, a reward-seeking behavior in which animals learn to associate temperature with food, is modulated by competition for available resources. Animals integrate prior experiences into their decision-making: Individuals reared at low population density preferentially migrate toward their cultivation temperature, whereas those exposed to high population density during larval development tend to disperse from it. This social modulation is implemented by a multilayered gap junction network that connects population-density-sensing neurons to the thermal processing circuit. Notably, components of this gap junction network also regulate developmental diapause. These results suggest that the gap junction network serves as a central framework for social decision-making, coordinating both developmental and behavioral plasticity in response to social cues.

Our observations indicate that exposure to high population density during early development causes animals to disperse from the cultivation temperature and instead migrate toward colder regions. This behavioral shift corresponds to changes in AIY activity: In animals reared at low density, a warming stimulus toward the cultivation temperature evokes excitatory responses in AIY, whereas under high-density conditions, AIY exhibits inhibitory responses. Since reduction in AIY activity correlates with reorientation maneuvers such as reversals and turns (69, 70), this shift in neural activity likely directs animals away from the cultivation temperature and toward colder environments. Given that C. elegans lifespan and reproductive period are extended at low temperatures (71), this shift in thermal preference may facilitate habitat exploration and enhance long-term survival. This behavioral plasticity, together with the developmental diapause program, likely supports population survival in dynamic environments characterized by ephemeral resources.

Our results demonstrate that animals exposed to high population density during the second or third larval stage disperse from the cultivation temperature. However, this exploratory behavior can be reversed if population density decreases during the fourth larval stage or later. This density-dependent plasticity shares both similarities and differences with the dauer decision. Both processes exhibit reversibility: For example, the decision to enter dauer, essentially made during the first larval stage, can be reversed in the second larval stage if environmental conditions improve (10). In contrast, the timing of decision-making differs between two processes: Dauer commitment occurs during the first larval stage, whereas commitment to density-dependent behavioral changes occurs during the second or third larval stage. These findings suggest that C. elegans continuously monitors population density, even in nondauer reproductive cycles, and that high-density signals modulate neural circuits to promote exploratory behavior.

Our findings delineate a neural circuit that conveys population density information to the thermotaxis circuit (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This circuit involves four pheromone-sensing chemosensory neurons—ASI, ASJ, ASK, and ADF. Analyses using rdl-1 mutants suggest that pheromone signaling in all four of these chemosensory neurons must be active to modulate thermotaxis behavior. In rdl-1 mutants, the localization of DAF-11 at the sensory endings is attenuated, leading to enhanced pheromone signaling in these neurons. Restoring rdl-1 expression in any one of the four chemosensory neurons was sufficient to rescue the thermotaxis defect of the mutant, suggesting that the elevated pheromone signaling in all four neurons is necessary to alter thermotaxis. Consistently, genetic ablation of ASI or ASK suppressed the thermotaxis phenotype of rdl-1 mutants, supporting the notion that all four chemosensory neurons must be active to modulate thermotaxis. These neurons likely transmit population density information to AIA interneurons, which then relay the signal through AWA and AIZ neurons to ultimately influence the activity of the AFD-AIY thermosensory circuit.

How does high population density regulate the thermotaxis circuit? We observed that animals previously exposed to high population density exhibited altered thermotaxis behavior even when tested under low-density conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Since the assay plates lack nematode pheromones, animals likely retain a “memory” of their high-density experience, which subsequently affects temperature processing. Our imaging studies reveal that AIY interneuron responses to warming stimuli are altered by population density, whereas the responses of the presynaptic AFD thermosensory neuron remain unchanged. These results point to two potential sites of pheromone action: presynaptic modulation of AFD output or postsynaptic modulation within AIY. Although these possibilities are not mutually exclusive, we speculate that presynaptic modulation is more likely, given AFD’s well-documented plasticity, including changes in gene expression (36, 37, 41, 72–74). In fact, numerous factors regulate AFD neuronal output (28, 29, 31, 42). Our results suggest that pheromone signaling induces long-lasting modifications in the temperature-processing circuit, ultimately promoting exploratory behaviors.

Our results demonstrate that the social modulation of thermotaxis relies on a multilayered gap junction network. This network connects the pheromone-sensing chemosensory neurons to thermosensory circuits through interconnecting neurons, including AIA, AWA, and AIZ (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Given that temperature-evoked Ca2+ transients in these interconnecting neurons remain unaffected by social modulation, gap junctions likely influence the temperature-processing AFD-AIY circuit through alternative mechanisms. Since gap junction components also regulate dauer larva formation, we propose that this network acts during early development to transmit population-density information to the downstream circuits. Pheromone stimuli can elicit Ca2+ responses in chemosensory neurons such as ASK (49, 50), suggesting that gap junctions may facilitate electrical coupling, thereby transducing pheromone-induced activity into long-lasting modifications of the thermotaxis circuit. Alternatively, given that many pheromone responses in C. elegans chemosensory neurons involve gene expression changes (47, 75–80), gap junctions may enable metabolic coupling between chemosensory and thermosensory neurons, transferring population-density information to induce cellular remodeling.

Early-life social experiences have profound effects on emotions and behaviors across species (81), and abnormal social interactions can trigger long-lasting neurochemical, neuroendocrinological, and immunological changes (82). In C. elegans, pheromone exposure has been shown to drive brain-wide circuit reconfiguration (83, 84). Our results underscore the crucial role of gap junction networks in social decision-making by mediating such circuit remodeling. Future research in this area will provide deeper insights into how gap junction networks define brain states and regulate neural development and function.

Materials and Methods

C. elegans Strains.

The C. elegans strains were cultured on NGM plates with the OP50 Escherichia coli as food (85). All strains were cultured at 20 °C unless otherwise indicated. N2 (Bristol) was used as the wild-type strain. Germline transformation was performed by microinjection as previously described (86). CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing was performed as previously described (87). Mutations, extrachromosomal arrays, integrated transgenes used in this study are described in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Mapping of nj262.

We conducted a SNP mapping of nj262 using a polymorphic wild-type strain CB4858. We crossed nj262 with CB4858 (88) and recovered F2 lines that exhibited the cryophilic phenotype. Genomic DNA was prepared from these lines, and patterns of SNP were determined. nj262 was mapped into a 1.2 Mbps interval of chromosome IV between the nucleotides of 6,805949 and 8,093583.

Generation of daf-11(snj2), rdl-1(nj337), and rdl-1(nj335Δ).

To generate a reduction-of-function allele of daf-11, we created a previously identified mutation associated with daf-11(m87) that would alter the serine 866 codon into phenylalanine (55) by using a CRISPR-based homology-directed repair, as previously described (87). The DNA sequence of the resulting daf-11(snj2) allele was confirmed to harbor the S866F mutation. A similar approach was used to create rdl-1(nj337), which alters the splice acceptor site that proceeds the second intron (Fig. 1C). The DNA sequence of rdl-1(nj337) was confirmed.

To generate rdl-1(nj335Δ), we induced double-strand breaks at two sites within the rdl-1 locus and simultaneously introduced a single strand repair template that would join the two sites. rdl-1(nj335Δ) eliminates a 1,970 bp sequence that corresponds to the nucleotides of 20,407 and 22,376 of cosmid C46A5.

Generation of inx-1(nj374, floxed) and che-7(snj1, floxed).

To generate inx-1 and che-7 alleles that are flanked by loxP sites, we conducted a CRISPR-Cas9-based homology-directed repair to insert loxP sequence into these loci. The loxP sequences were inserted into the third and eighth introns of inx-1a and the fifth and the ninth introns of che-7a. The DNA sequences of these alleles are described in SI Appendix.

Thermotaxis Assay.

Thermotaxis assays were performed as previously described (89). For our standard condition (medium density), we placed two hermaphrodites onto an NGM dish (5.5 cm in diameter, 23.7 cm2) and allowed them to lay eggs for 1 d. The plates were incubated at 20 °C for 4 d. The F1 progeny, which we typically find 100 to 300 animals per plate, were collected, were washed with NG buffer, and were transferred onto the center of a thermotaxis assay plate that had been placed onto a temperature gradient from 17 °C to 23 °C with the gradient steepness of 0.5 °C/cm. The animals were allowed to freely move on the temperature gradient for 1 h. The assay plate was divided into eight sections along the temperature gradient. The number of animals in each section was counted.

To test the thermotaxis behavior of animals grown under low density, we picked 1 to 10 freshly laid embryos onto an NGM dish and allowed these embryos to develop into adults. To examine the effect of upshifting population density, rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals grown at low density were transferred to an NGM plate that contained rdl-1(nj335Δ); dpy-11(e224) animals at medium density. The Dpy and non-Dpy animals were collected and were subjected to the thermotaxis assay. We counted only non-Dpy animals to examine the effect of density upshift.

To compare the thermotaxis behaviors of animals grown under medium and high density, we obtained embryos by bleaching gravid hermaphrodites. We placed 100 to 300 embryos (medium density) or 500 to 1,000 embryos (high density) onto an NGM dish spotted with 20 × concentrated OP50 to prevent animals from starvation. To conduct thermotaxis assays with a similar number of animals across the conditions, we used a fraction of animals grown under high-density conditions. Thermotaxis assays were performed as described above.

To investigate thermotaxis behaviors of animals mutant for daf-11, we allowed animals of daf-11(sa195 or snj2)/tmC12[egl-9(tmIs1197[Pmyo-2::mCherry])] (90) or daf-11(sa195 or snj2)/tmC3[egl-9(tmIs1230[Pmyo-2::mCherry])] animals to self-fertilize, and their F1 mCherry-negative progeny was examined for thermotaxis behaviors.

Determination of Subcellular Localization of DAF-11::GFP.

We generated an extrachromosomal array containing a translational daf-11::gfp by injecting into the wild-type animals. We observed that this transgene expressed GFP in ASI and AWC. This extrachromosomal array was transferred into an rdl-1(nj335Δ) background by crossing. The GFP signals were observed by Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscopy with Plan-Apochromat 63× objective. Maximum intensity projection of z-stack was analyzed with ImageJ, and the mean fluorescence intensities at the sensory ending and cell body in each animal were calculated.

Crude Pheromone Preparation.

Crude pheromone was prepared as previously described (10). The wild-type animals were grown in two litters of liquid culture supplemented with OP50. The exhausted culture medium was reduced to 50 mL in volume by heating and was centrifuged at 1,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was dried down, and the resulting residues were extracted with 95% ethanol. The extracts were dried at 60 °C, and the residues were then extracted into distilled water. The activity of the partially purified pheromone was measured by dauer-forming assays. The concentration that gave rise to 4 to 15% of dauer animals was determined. We added the pheromone extract at the half of this concentration to NGM plates to examine the effect on thermotaxis behavior.

Dauer Formation Assay.

Gravid hermaphrodites were placed onto an NGM plate seeded with OP50 for a few hours and allowed to lay eggs. The plates were incubated for a few days, and the F1 progeny was observed under a dissecting microscope. The number of dauer and nondauer animals was counted. To score animals homozygous for daf-11 mutations, we placed animals heterozygous for daf-11(sa195 or snj2)/tmC12[egl-9(tmIs1197[Pmyo-2::mCherry])] (90) onto NGM plates and allowed them to lay eggs. The F1 mCherry-negative progeny was scored to determine the fraction of animals that developed into dauer.

Calcium Imaging from Immobilized Animals.

Calcium imaging experiments of AFD, ASI, ASK, and AWA neurons were conducted with immobilized animals expressing calcium indicators (GCaMP6m or YCX1.6) in these neurons. Animals were immobilized by placing on a 5 to 10% agarose pad with polystyrene beads and were subjected to temperature ramps. Fluorescent images were captured every second. Fluorescence intensities of GCaMP6m and RFP or of YFP and CFP from the cell body were measured by ImageJ, and the ratio of GCaMP6m/RFP or YFP/CFP was calculated. The ratio change was defined as (ratio − baseline ratio)/baseline ratio, where baseline ratio corresponds to the mean of the ratio values during the first 30 s of the recording.

Calcium Imaging from Freely Moving Animals.

Animals expressing YCX1.6 in AIY, AIA, or AIZ were placed on a 2% agarose pad on a coverslip. The sample was covered by another coverslip and was placed on a motorized stage (HawkVision Inc., Fujisawa) that keeps the target image of animals in the field of view while animals are moving on the agarose pad. Animals were subjected to a warming stimulus. Fluorescent images were captured twice a second, and YFP and CFP intensities were measured by a custom-written MATLAB program. The ratio values of YFP/CFP were used to calculate the standardized ratio change, which was defined as (ratio − minimum ratio)/(maximum ratio − minimum ratio). The baseline standardized ratio, which was the mean of the standardized ratios of the first 10 frames, was subtracted from the standardized ratio change of each frame. The area under the curve of this standardized ratio change was calculated for the entire time window after the warming stimulus was applied.

Statistics.

One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer test was used for multiple comparisons of the dispersion index or TTX index. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the fraction of animals that entered dauer. Brunner–Munzel test with Holm correction was applied to the rest of the analyses, including DAF-11::GFP localization and calcium imaging.

To compare the thermotaxis behavior of animals grown under low density, we performed Bootstrap analysis of the dispersion index. We randomly sampled animals based on the combined data of the observed thermotaxis distribution of two groups (e.g., the wild-type animals grown under low density versus rdl-1(nj335Δ) animals grown under low density) and simulated the entire dataset, with identical numbers of animals and trials to those in our experimental dataset. We repeated the simulation for 100,000 times and calculated each time the difference of the mean dispersion indexes between the two simulated groups. The distribution of this value from 100,000 simulations was compared to the experimental value and was shown as a histogram.
