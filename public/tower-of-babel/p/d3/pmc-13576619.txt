Single‐Cell Transcriptomics and Mendelian Randomization Analysis Reveal Key Genes in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Xiangpeng Chen, Hongquan Chen, Shiguang Xu, Shumin Wang, Huishan Wang, Hao Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the most common cardiac arrhythmias. It reduces quality of life and increases the risk of complications such as stroke. Although progress has been made in understanding its pathogenesis, the key cellular subtypes and therapeutic targets remain unclear.MethodsWe applied single‐cell transcriptomics to identify critical cellular subtypes in AF. High‐dimensional weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) and machine learning were used to screen AF‐related genes. Mendelian randomization (MR) and colocalization analyses were performed to assess causal relationships between these genes and AF.ResultsSingle‐cell analysis showed a significant increase in macrophages in AF, especially SPP1‐expressing macrophages, which may drive AF onset and progression. HdWGCNA identified AF‐related gene modules. Three genes, LRCH1, RSRC2 and VAMP2, were found to be causally associated with AF. MR analysis confirmed their significant causal effects.ConclusionsThe accumulation of SPP1‐expressing macrophages may drive the onset and progression of AF. Furthermore, LRCH1, RSRC2, and VAMP2 were identified as key causal genes for AF, providing novel insights into its molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, hdWGCNA, key genes, Mendelian randomization, single‐cell transcriptomics

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is characterized by rapid and disorganized electrical activity in the atria, leading to irregular heart rhythms [1]. AF substantially reduces quality of life and increases morbidity and mortality, primarily through higher risks of stroke and heart failure [2]. Established risk factors include age, hypertension, diabetes, male sex, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, and long‐term endurance exercise [3]. Despite extensive efforts to clarify the molecular and cellular mechanisms of AF, its multifactorial and complex nature continues to hinder research progress [4]. Although substantial efforts have been made to elucidate the molecular and cellular mechanisms of AF, its multifactorial nature as a complex arrhythmia poses significant challenges to research progress. Current pharmacological therapies for AF, particularly persistent AF, remain suboptimal. Patients often rely on traditional antiarrhythmic medications such as amiodarone and sotalol, which have limited efficacy and significant side effects [5, 6]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of AF and identifying novel therapeutic targets remain urgent research priorities.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) is a powerful tool for characterizing disease‐associated gene expression at the single‐cell level [7]. It enables the identification and comparison of cellular subtypes across conditions, providing insights into cellular changes during disease progression [8]. Mendelian randomization (MR), which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables, offers a robust framework to infer causal relationships and helps overcome biases inherent in observational studies [9, 10]. By integrating scRNA‐seq with genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data, gene expression regulation can be mapped to specific loci with higher precision. This integrative approach facilitates the systematic identification of key genes influencing AF risk and provides new opportunities for early diagnosis and therapeutic development.

Through scRNA‐seq analysis, we observed a significant increase in macrophage populations in patients with AF. Among these cells, SPP1 was one of the most highly upregulated genes. SPP1+ macrophages contribute to AF progression by promoting fibrosis, activating inflammatory responses, and disrupting interactions between cardiomyocytes [11]. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a significant increase in SPP1 expression was observed in specific macrophage subtypes, particularly in the fibrotic lower lobes of the lungs. This was associated with a markedly increased proliferation rate of SPP1+ macrophages [12]. This suggests that these cells play a pivotal role in the progression and maintenance of fibrotic lesions through their high proliferative capacity [12]. Research into the relationship between SPP1 and macrophages has been more prominent in oncology. Studies indicate that in colorectal cancer, SPP1+ macrophages may regulate FAP+ fibroblasts via TGFB1, promoting the secretion of MMPs and collagen, which collectively contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Patients with high FAP or SPP1 expression were less likely to benefit from anti‐PD‐L1 therapie [13, 14, 15, 16]. In lung adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, SPP1 was shown to upregulate PD‐L1, mediating macrophage polarization and facilitating immune evasion in the tumor microenvironment [17, 18].

Materials and Methods

Single‐Cell Transcriptomic Data Analysis

Single‐cell transcriptomic profiles from AF and normal tissue samples were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically dataset GSE197518, which included 7 AF patients and 5 normal controls. Data quality control (QC) was performed using the R package Seurat (v4.3.0). After stringent quality control, which included the removal of doublets with the DoubletFinder package, along with the application of thresholds for nFeature_RNA (number of detected genes > 200) and mitochondrial content (percent.mt < 15%), a total of 41 286 high‐quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Subsequently, 2000 highly variable features were selected for downstream analysis. To remove batch effects, the Harmony R package (v0.1.1) was employed. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) based on the top 10 principal components. Clustering and subclustering analyses were conducted using Seurat's FindClusters function with appropriate resolution settings. Cell clusters were annotated based on highly expressed genes and known cell markers.

High‐Dimensional Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis (hdWGCNA)

HdWGCNA was used to normalize and reduce single‐cell gene expression data, minimizing technical biases while highlighting biological differences [19], A soft thresholding approach was applied to construct a scale‐free gene co‐expression network, ensuring biological relevance and statistical stability. Modules of co‐expressed genes were identified using a topological overlap measure. This process enabled the characterization of single‐cell gene expression patterns and regulatory networks.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To evaluate associations between key genes and AF, relevant datasets were obtained from the FinnGen database (finngen_R11_I9_AF), comprising genome‐wide association study (GWAS) data from 55 853 AF cases and 231 952 controls, and from the IEU Open GWAS platform (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), which provided eQTL data for key genes (Supporting Information S2: Table 1). All included samples were of European ancestry. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were performed using the R package TwoSampleMR. Selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) met the following assumptions: 1. Relevance: IVs were associated with the exposure of interest. 2. Independence: IVs were independent of confounding factors. 3. Exclusion Restriction: The IVs influence the outcome solely through the exposure. To minimize pleiotropy and satisfy this assumption, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (R 2 < 0.3, clump window = 500 kb) using the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel to ensure the independence of IVs. IVs strength was maximized to explain the variance in drug targets, focusing on SNPs within ±100 kb of key genes. F‐statistics > 10 indicated no weak instrument bias. Five MR methods were employed: inverse variance weighted (IVW, primary method), MR‐Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across all 41 tested genes based on the IVW results. For single IV analyses, the Wald ratio method was applied. Heterogeneity among IVs was assessed using Cochran's Q test (p > 0.05 indicated no significant heterogeneity). Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated via MR‐Egger intercept tests (p < 0.05 suggested pleiotropy).

Colocalization Analysis

Colocalization analysis determines whether two genetic association signals originate from the same or nearby genetic variants within a specific locus [20]. Bayesian colocalization methods estimate the probability of shared causal variants between traits. The Coloc R package was used for Bayesian colocalization analysis. The four hypotheses tested were: H0: No SNPs within the region are associated with either trait. H1/H2: SNPs are associated with either trait A or trait B. H3: SNPs are independently associated with both traits. H4: SNPs are shared between the two traits [21]. A posterior probability for colocalization (PP4) ≥ 0.75 was set a priori as the threshold for strong evidence of a shared causal variant.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were conducted using R software (v4.3.1). For normally distributed data, independent t‐tests and one‐way ANOVA were used for two‐group and multigroup comparisons, respectively. Non‐normally distributed data were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests) and Kruskal–Wallis H tests. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with differences evaluated via chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. For multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

Increased Proportion of Macrophages in AF

The GSE197518 dataset contained 12 samples with approximately 43 000 cells after stringent quality control. Batch effects were corrected, followed by dimensionality reduction and clustering. UMAP identified 16 distinct cell clusters (Figure 1A). These clusters were annotated using canonical marker genes and classified into six major cell types: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, monocytes, and T/NK cells (Figure 1B). Marker gene expression profiles for each cell type are shown in Figure 1C. Comparative analysis of cell type composition revealed a 2.8‐fold increase in macrophage proportion in AF samples (12.2% ± 10.3%) compared with controls (4.42% ± 1.50%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.202) due to substantial inter‐individual variability in the AF group (Figure 1D,E, Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

Identification of SPP1+ Macrophages

Macrophages are key contributors to AF pathogenesis. To examine their changes under disease conditions, macrophages were isolated and subdivided into seven subtypes (Figure 2A). Previous studies have shown that macrophages with high SPP1 expression are involved in atrial remodeling and conduction abnormalities in AF [11]. Analysis of SPP1 expression across subtypes revealed increased levels in clusters 2 and 4 (Figure 2B). SPP1+ macrophages from these clusters were extracted and re‐clustered, generating three subtypes (Figure 2C). Among them, cluster 2 showed the highest SPP1 expression and was selected for further analysis (Figure 2D).

Identification of Key Gene Modules in SPP1+ Macrophages Using hdWGCNA

Given the central role of SPP1+ macrophages in AF, we used hdWGCNA to identify key gene modules in these cells. Raw cells were aggregated into pseudo‐cells, and a soft threshold of 3 was applied to construct a scale‐free network (Figure 3A). In total, 24 gene modules were detected. Among them, module 17 showed specific high expression in Cluster 2 macrophages, suggesting that its genes may be critical for SPP1+ macrophage function in AF (Figure 3B). The top 15 genes from each module, ranked by correlation (Figure 3C). Module activity was evaluated using AUCell scoring and visualized with UMAP and dot plots (Figure 3B,D). Finally, the top 50 genes from module 17 were selected for downstream functional analysis.

Causal Relationship Between LRCH1, RSRC2, VAMP2, and AF

Two‐sample MR analysis was used to assess the causal relationships between the top 50 genes in module 17 and AF. SNPs related to these genes were extracted, yielding valid instrumental variables for 41 genes (Supporting Information S2: Table 2). Multiple MR methods were applied, and results were corrected using FDR. Three genes showed significant positive causal associations with AF: LRCH1 (OR = 1.1724 per 1 SD increase in genetically predicted LRCH1 expression, 95% CI: 1.072–1.283, p = 0.011), RSRC2 (OR = 1.452 per 1 SD increase in genetically predicted RSRC2 expression, 95% CI: 1.197–1.763, p = 0.006), and VAMP2 (OR = 1.102 per 1 SD increase in genetically predicted VAMP2 expression, 95% CI: 1.032–1.1765, p = 0.049) (Figure 4A, Table 1). Cochran's Q test showed no significant heterogeneity, and MR‐Egger and MR‐PRESSO indicated that the findings were not affected by horizontal pleiotropy (Table 1). Funnel plots also suggested a low risk of substantial heterogeneity (Figure 4B). Leave‐one‐out analysis confirmed that higher expression of these genes was consistently associated with increased AF risk under the IVW method (Figure 4C). To further evaluate their genetic relationships with AF, colocalization analysis was performed. We defined a priori a posterior probability for shared causal variants (PP4) ≥ 0.70 as strong evidence of colocalization. RSRC2 showed strong evidence of colocalization with AF (PPH4 = 0.813), indicating that shared genetic variants contribute to both RSRC2 expression and AF (Table 1). This supports a close genetic link between RSRC2 and AF. In contrast, LRCH1 (PPH4 = 0.039) and VAMP2 (PPH4 = 0.103) exhibited lower PP4 values, suggesting weaker or no evidence for a shared causal variant. Therefore, only RSRC2 met the prespecified colocalization threshold, supporting a more confident genetic link between RSRC2 and AF.

Discussion

In this study, scRNA‐seq analysis showed a significant increase in macrophages in AF samples, with SPP1 identified as the most upregulated gene in these cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that SPP1+ macrophages play a central role in atrial remodeling and conduction disturbances in AF [11]. These macrophages represent a pro‐fibrotic subgroup regulated by CXCL4, and they promote multiorgan fibrosis through interactions with platelets and fibroblasts [22]. In AF, they accelerate disease progression by enhancing fibrosis and activating inflammatory pathways [11]. Similar findings have been reported in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, where high SPP1 expression in macrophages contributes to the formation and maintenance of fibrotic lesions [12]. In cancer, including colorectal and lung adenocarcinoma, SPP1+ macrophages support disease progression by modulating fibroblasts and promoting immune evasion [13, 17]. Building on the critical role of SPP1+ macrophages in fibrotic and inflammatory diseases, our study used hdWGCNA to identify three key genes associated with AF: LRCH1, RSRC2, and VAMP2.

LRCH1, a member of the LRCH protein family, functions as a cytoskeletal regulator during cell division and has been linked to osteoarthritis and hip joint degeneration [23, 24]. It also acts as a negative regulator of Cdc42 activation, contributing to autoimmune encephalomyelitis [12]. Studies suggest that LRCH1 may influence ulcerative colitis (UC) development by modulating CD4+ T cell migration [25]. In addition, LRCH1 inhibits LAT signaling and suppresses CD8+ T cell activity, thereby affecting anti‐infective and antitumor immune responses, which makes it a potential immunotherapeutic target [26]. These findings highlight the role of LRCH1 in immune regulation and suggest that it may contribute to AF pathogenesis through mechanisms such as immune modulation and cell migration. Although its direct role in AF remains unclear, its functions in other immune‐related diseases provide valuable insights for future research.

RSRC2 was first identified in 2007 as a gene associated with esophageal cancer [27]. Subsequent studies have shown that reduced RSRC2 expression is linked to greater tumor invasiveness, advanced clinical stage, and chemotherapy sensitivity in esophageal, pancreatic, and gastric cancers [27, 28, 29]. However, its role in cardiovascular diseases remains largely unexplored. In our analysis, RSRC2 showed a significant positive causal association with AF, particularly within the SPP1+ macrophage subgroup. Colocalization analysis further indicated that RSRC2 and AF share common genetic variants, suggesting a genetic link. While the role of RSRC2 in cancer is relatively well established, additional studies are needed to clarify its expression, function, and mechanisms in cardiomyocytes and to determine its potential contribution to AF.

VAMP2 is a core component of the SNARE complex that mediates vesicle fusion and intercellular communication [30, 31]. It plays important roles in neurotransmitter release, endocrine regulation, and immune responses. Recent studies have also highlighted its involvement in neural conduction and synaptic plasticity [32, 33]. However, its role in the cardiovascular system, especially in arrhythmias such as AF, remains poorly understood. By regulating vesicle‐membrane fusion, VAMP2 is essential for intracellular transport and signaling [31]. In macrophages, VAMP2 expression may influence immune responses, potentially modulating the immune microenvironment and contributing to AF development [11]. Although direct evidence linking VAMP2 to AF is limited, its established functions in immune regulation and cell signaling suggest important research potential. We hypothesize that VAMP2 may regulate macrophage secretory activity and cytokine release, thereby influencing AF pathogenesis and progression.

This study highlights the potential link between SPP1+ macrophages and AF, identifying LRCH1, RSRC2, and VAMP2 as candidate genes. These findings provide novel perspectives on the molecular mechanisms underlying AF and lay the foundation for future investigations into therapeutic targets.
