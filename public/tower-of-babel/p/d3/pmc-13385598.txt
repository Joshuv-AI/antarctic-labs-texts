Comparative evaluation of CNN architectures for detection of dental pulp calcifications on CBCT slices
Authors: Arham M. Alkherbash, Basheer H. Al-shameri, Mohammed Alsabri, Ayman Alsabry, Raghda Alnozaily, Abdullah F. Alshammari, Ahmed A. Madfa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental pulp calcifications (DPCs) can complicate endodontic procedures by obstructing root canals and increasing the risk of procedural errors. This preliminary comparative study evaluated fifteen pretrained convolutional neural network architectures for binary detection of DPCs on two-dimensional cone-beam computed tomography slices and examined how the data-partitioning and augmentation workflow influenced apparent model performance.MethodsThe dataset comprised 58 original grayscale CBCT slices, including 30 slices with pulp calcification and 28 without calcification. Two analytical approaches were evaluated. In the primary leakage-controlled approach, the original slices were partitioned before augmentation into 46 training, six validation, and six test slices; augmentation was then restricted to the training subset, producing 278 training image instances. In a secondary exploratory analysis reproducing the original workflow, the 58 slices were augmented to 290 image instances before an 80:10:10 training, validation, and test split. Fifteen pretrained CNN architectures were fine-tuned using transfer learning for up to 40 epochs with early stopping. Performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices, and empirical ROC-AUC. Exact binomial 95% confidence intervals were calculated for accuracy in the primary approach, and Grad-CAM was used for qualitative interpretation of EfficientNet-B3 predictions.ResultsIn the primary leakage-controlled analysis, test accuracy ranged from 50.00% to 100.00%, and F1-scores ranged from 0.0000 to 1.0000. DenseNet169, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ResNet18, and CoAtNet-0 correctly classified all six test slices; however, the exact 95% confidence interval for an observed accuracy of 100% was 54.1%–100.0%, reflecting substantial uncertainty. Empirical AUC values ranged from 0.444 to 1.000 and were interpreted descriptively. In the secondary exploratory workflow, EfficientNet-B3, EfficientNet-B5, and EfficientNetV2-S achieved test accuracies of 93.10% and F1-scores of 0.9333–0.9375. Because augmentation preceded partitioning in this workflow, these estimates may be optimistic. Model rankings differed substantially between the two approaches.ConclusionThe apparent performance and ranking of CNN architectures were sensitive to the data-partitioning and augmentation strategy. Although several architectures showed promising classification performance, the small number of original slices and the very limited independent test set preclude definitive claims of model superiority or clinical applicability. These preliminary findings require confirmation using larger patient- or scan-level datasets, volumetric analysis, repeated validation, and independent external testing.

Introduction

Dental pulp calcifications (DPCs) are mineralised deposits that develop within the pulp chamber or root canal system. 1 Pulp calcifications are often asymptomatic and may be detected on routine dental radiographs. 2 When extensive, pulp stones or pulp canal obliteration may obscure the pulp space and complicate access preparation, canal location, and instrumentation during endodontic treatment.2,3 Their occurrence has been associated with ageing and local pulpal insults, including caries and trauma. Reliable radiographic identification is therefore relevant to preoperative assessment and endodontic treatment planning.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides volumetric visualisation of maxillofacial structures with reduced anatomical superimposition and can improve assessment of the pulp chamber and root canal anatomy compared with conventional projection radiography.4–6 Nevertheless, accurate identification of DPCs on CBCT may remain challenging, particularly for less-experienced observers and when calcifications are small or morphologically variable, image resolution is limited, or high-density root canal fillings, anatomical variations, and artefacts complicate interpretation.7–9 Although DPCs are inherently three-dimensional findings, the present study used two-dimensional CBCT slices as a preliminary and computationally tractable framework for a controlled comparison of multiple pretrained CNN architectures. This slice-based design enabled standardised image-level classification across the evaluated models but did not preserve inter-slice continuity or the full volumetric context of the calcification. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence for slice-level classification rather than as validation of a complete volumetric diagnostic system.

Deep learning and convolutional neural networks have demonstrated considerable potential in dental image analysis, including the detection, classification, and segmentation of calcifications and other dental abnormalities.10–18 However, the existing evidence for automated dental pulp calcification detection remains methodologically heterogeneous. Studies based on bitewing and panoramic radiographs have commonly used object-detection or segmentation architectures, including YOLO, ResNeXt, and Mask R-CNN, and have reported relatively high accuracy, precision, and recall values.19–22 In contrast, CBCT-based investigations have generally used smaller datasets and computationally demanding volumetric or slice-based approaches, with more variable performance.6,18 Earlier hybrid CNN–SVM methods have also produced lower accuracy than end-to-end deep learning approaches. 23

Direct comparison of the reported performance across previous studies remains difficult because of substantial heterogeneity in imaging modality, diagnostic task, unit of analysis, image-cropping strategy, dataset size, augmentation protocol, partitioning method, and evaluation metrics. In particular, studies based on small single-centre datasets are vulnerable to overfitting and unstable performance estimates. When augmented derivatives or multiple slices originating from the same patient, tooth, or CBCT volume are allocated to different data subsets, information leakage may occur and may produce overly optimistic validation or test results. The absence of independent external validation, repeated training runs, confidence intervals, and formal paired statistical comparisons further limits the strength of claims regarding model superiority. In addition, explainability methods and sufficiently detailed reporting of preprocessing, model implementation, and computational settings have been used inconsistently, restricting reproducibility and clinical interpretation. Consequently, the available literature demonstrates technical feasibility but provides limited evidence regarding which CNN architecture performs most reliably under controlled and leakage-aware experimental conditions.

Against this background, the present preliminary comparative study compared fifteen pretrained CNN architectures from the ResNet, DenseNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, and CoAtNet families for binary detection of dental pulp calcifications on uniformly labelled two-dimensional CBCT slices. All architectures were trained and evaluated under standardised experimental conditions using consistent preprocessing, optimisation settings, stopping criteria, and performance metrics. The primary analysis used a leakage-controlled workflow in which the original slices were partitioned before augmentation and augmentation was restricted to the training subset. A secondary exploratory analysis reproduced the original workflow in which augmentation preceded partitioning, allowing assessment of how data-preparation strategy influenced apparent performance.

The study hypothesized that classification performance would differ across CNN architectures under otherwise comparable experimental conditions and that the estimated performance and model ranking would be sensitive to the partitioning and augmentation workflow. The objective of this study was to compare the classification performance of fifteen pretrained CNN architectures for detecting dental pulp calcifications on two-dimensional CBCT slices and to examine the influence of leakage-controlled versus augmentation-before-partitioning workflows on the resulting performance estimates.

Methods

Study design and analytical framework

This preliminary comparative deep-learning study evaluated the image-level classification performance of fifteen pretrained convolutional neural network architectures for detecting dental pulp calcifications on two-dimensional CBCT slices. All architectures were trained and evaluated using standardised preprocessing, optimisation, early-stopping, and performance-evaluation procedures to support direct comparison across models.

Two analytical workflows were examined. In the primary leakage-controlled workflow, the 58 original CBCT slices were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets before augmentation, and augmentation was applied exclusively to the training subset. In the secondary exploratory workflow, the original dataset was augmented before partitioning, reproducing the initial experimental procedure and allowing assessment of the extent to which the data-preparation sequence influenced the observed performance and model ranking. The leakage-controlled workflow was regarded as the primary analysis, whereas the augmentation-before-partitioning workflow was retained only as a methodological and exploratory comparison.

Ethical approval and data source

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Sana’a University, Yemen (approval No. 550). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable institutional ethical requirements. The study dataset comprised 58 original de-identified grayscale two-dimensional CBCT slices obtained from multiple dental clinics in Sana’a, Yemen. Thirty slices showed dental pulp calcification and 28 did not. All images were anonymised before data preparation and model development.

Image annotation and reference standard

All 58 original CBCT slices were independently reviewed by six dental clinicians who participated in the study. Each clinician classified the slices as positive or negative for dental pulp calcification. A positive label was assigned when a clearly identifiable radiopaque deposit was present within the pulp chamber or root canal space, whereas a negative label was assigned when no such intrapulpal radiopaque deposit was identified. The researcher responsible for artificial intelligence implementation and computational model development did not participate in the clinical image-labelling process.

No discordant classifications were reported among the six clinical assessors during the labelling process; therefore, no adjudication procedure was required. The concordant clinical classification was adopted as the reference standard for dataset labelling and was applied consistently across all fifteen CNN architectures.

Data partitioning and augmentation strategies

Two data-partitioning and augmentation workflows were evaluated. The first was designated as the primary leakage-controlled approach. In this workflow, the 58 original CBCT slices were stratified by class and partitioned before any augmentation was performed. Of the 30 original positive slices, 24 were allocated to the training subset, three to the validation subset, and three to the test subset. Of the 28 original negative slices, 22 were allocated to training, three to validation, and three to testing. Data augmentation was then applied exclusively to the original training slices, generating 120 additional positive and 112 additional negative training samples. The resulting training subset contained 278 image instances, comprising 144 positive and 134 negative samples. The validation and test subsets each contained six unaugmented original slices, equally distributed between the positive and negative classes. Thus, no augmented derivative of a validation or test slice was included in the training subset.

The allocation of the original slices and their augmented derivatives was documented in a split manifest. This record was used to verify that each original slice was assigned to only one subset and that all augmented derivatives remained within the training subset. This leakage-controlled workflow was treated as the primary analysis because the validation and test subsets contained only original images that were not used to generate training samples.

The second workflow reproduced the original data-preparation procedure and was retained as a secondary exploratory analysis. In this approach, all 58 original slices were first expanded to 290 image instances through augmentation, after which the expanded dataset was randomly divided using an 80:10:10 ratio. This produced 232 training images, 29 validation images, and 29 test images. Because augmentation preceded partitioning, transformed derivatives originating from the same original slice may have been allocated to different subsets. The resulting estimates were therefore considered potentially optimistic and were interpreted only as an exploratory comparison with the primary leakage-controlled workflow.

Image preprocessing and augmentation pipeline

Before model training, the images were resized according to the input-resolution requirements of each CNN architecture. Image preprocessing and augmentation were implemented using the Albumentations library. The augmentation pipeline included brightness adjustment, rotations of up to ±15°, Gaussian blurring following rotations of up to ±30°, horizontal flipping, and random cropping followed by resizing after rotations of up to ±15°. These transformations were selected to increase visual variability in the training data while retaining the image features used for class assignment.

In the primary leakage-controlled workflow, the augmentation pipeline was applied only to the original training slices after dataset partitioning. The original validation and test slices underwent only the resizing and normalization required for model input and were not augmented. In the secondary exploratory workflow, the same augmentation procedures were applied before the expanded dataset was partitioned. CNN architectures and training procedure

Fifteen pretrained deep-learning architectures were evaluated: ResNet18 and ResNet50; DenseNet121, DenseNet169, and DenseNet201; EfficientNet-B0, EfficientNet-B1, EfficientNet-B3, EfficientNet-B4, EfficientNet-B5, tf-EfficientNet-B5, and EfficientNetV2-S; ConvNeXt-Tiny and ConvNeXt-Base; and CoAtNet-0. The architectures represented residual, densely connected, compound-scaled convolutional, modern convolutional, and hybrid convolution–attention model families. The models were implemented using the torchvision and timm libraries within the PyTorch framework.

All models were initialized with weights pretrained on ImageNet. Their original classification heads were replaced with binary output layers corresponding to the calcified and non-calcified classes, and the complete networks were fine-tuned using transfer learning. Architecture-specific input resolutions and batch sizes were used to accommodate differences in model design and GPU memory requirements. Input resolution ranged from 128 × 128 to 456 × 456 pixels, and batch size ranged from 8 to 32.

For each analytical workflow, all architectures were trained using the same class labels, data partitions, loss function, optimizer, learning rate, maximum number of epochs, and early-stopping rule. Binary classification was optimized using cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0003. Training was allowed to continue for a maximum of 40 epochs. Early stopping with a patience of five consecutive epochs without improvement in validation accuracy was used to reduce the risk of overfitting and unnecessary training.

A fixed random seed of 42 was applied to Python, NumPy, and PyTorch operations, including the training-data loader, to improve reproducibility. Deterministic CUDA settings were enabled where supported. During each epoch, model parameters were updated using the training subset, whereas the validation subset was used only to monitor classification accuracy and select the best-performing checkpoint. The checkpoint achieving the highest validation accuracy was saved and reloaded before final evaluation. The test subset was not used for parameter optimization, early stopping, or checkpoint selection and was evaluated only after completion of model training.

A single seeded training run was performed for each architecture within each analytical workflow. No architecture-specific hyperparameter search was conducted beyond the use of model-appropriate input resolutions and batch sizes. This standardized procedure was selected to support direct comparison among architectures; however, the absence of repeated independent runs was considered when interpreting the stability of the observed performance estimates.

Computational environment and model execution

All model-development experiments were conducted in Google Colab using a GPU-enabled runtime with an NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics processing unit. The dataset was manually uploaded as a compressed archive, extracted within the Colab environment, and organised into class-specific directories corresponding to the training, validation, and test subsets. For the primary leakage-controlled workflow, the resulting directory structure was organised as ‘Split/train’, ‘Split/val’, and ‘Split/test’, with separate Positive and Negative folders within each subset. Dataset allocation and the relationship between original and augmented images were documented in a split manifest.

The models were implemented using the PyTorch framework and loaded through the torchvision or timm libraries, depending on architecture availability. The software environment comprised PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128, timm 1.0.27, Albumentations 2.0.8, OpenCV 4.13.0, and scikit-learn 1.6.1. CUDA acceleration was enabled when available. A fixed random seed of 42 was applied to Python, NumPy, PyTorch, and the shuffled training-data loader. Deterministic CUDA settings were enabled where supported to improve the reproducibility of model execution.

For each architecture, pretrained weights were loaded, the final classification layer was adapted for binary classification, and training was performed using the workflow-specific data partitions. Model performance on the validation subset was monitored after every epoch. The checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy was saved in PyTorch `.pth’ format and reloaded before final evaluation on the test subset. The test subset was not used during parameter updating, early stopping, or checkpoint selection.

In addition to the predicted class labels, the softmax probabilities assigned to the negative and positive classes were retained for every test image. The image path, reference label, predicted label, and class probabilities were exported to model-specific comma-separated value files. These outputs were subsequently used to construct receiver operating characteristic curves and calculate empirical area-under-the-curve estimates. The same execution sequence and reporting structure were applied across all evaluated architectures to support transparent comparison, although exact numerical replication may still be affected by the stochastic nature of neural-network training and variability in cloud-based runtime environments.

Performance evaluation and visualisation

Model performance was evaluated after reloading the checkpoint that achieved the highest validation accuracy during training. For binary classification, CBCT slices containing dental pulp calcification were designated as the positive class, whereas slices without calcification were designated as the negative class. The final predicted class was assigned according to the class with the highest model output probability.

Performance on the test subset was summarised using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Accuracy represented the proportion of all test slices classified correctly. Precision represented the proportion of slices predicted as calcified that were truly positive, whereas recall represented the proportion of reference-positive slices correctly identified by the model. The F1-score was calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall and was used to describe their balance. When a model produced no positive predictions, undefined precision- or F1-related values were reported as zero rather than omitted.

Confusion matrices were generated for each architecture using a fixed class order of negative followed by positive. These matrices were used to present the numbers of true-negative, false-positive, false-negative, and true-positive classifications and to characterise the direction of classification errors. Because the primary leakage-controlled test subset contained only six original slices, the confusion matrices and derived metrics were interpreted at the individual-image level, and small numerical differences between architectures were not assumed to represent stable differences in generalisation.

Training behaviour was visualised using epoch-wise learning curves displaying training loss, training accuracy, and validation accuracy. These curves were examined descriptively to identify convergence patterns, fluctuations in validation performance, and possible divergence between training and validation accuracy. They were not used independently to establish the presence or absence of overfitting. The same evaluation definitions and visualisation procedures were applied to all architectures within each analytical workflow. Results from the primary leakage-controlled workflow and the secondary augmentation-before-partitioning workflow were reported separately because their test subsets differed in independence and composition.

Illustrative Grad-CAM visualisation

Grad-CAM was used as an illustrative post hoc visualization to demonstrate the image regions that influenced the predictions of EfficientNet-B3. This architecture was selected as a representative model because it showed comparatively strong performance among the evaluated architectures. The visualization was not intended as a systematic comparison of model explainability or as a quantitative localization analysis.

Representative examples included one correctly classified positive CBCT slice, one correctly classified negative slice, and one misclassified slice, as shown in Figure 2. For each example, the gradient of the predicted class score was propagated to the final convolutional feature layer, and the resulting activation map was resized and overlaid on the original grayscale image. Statistical analysis

Classification performance was analysed separately for the two data-preparation workflows. For each architecture, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated from the test-set predictions, with dental pulp calcification designated as the positive class. Because each architecture was evaluated using a single seeded training run within each workflow, the reported metrics represent single-split performance estimates rather than means obtained from repeated runs or cross-validation folds. Model rankings were therefore interpreted descriptively, and no predefined performance tiers were used to imply statistically distinct groups of architectures.

For the primary leakage-controlled workflow, receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed from the positive-class probabilities retained for each original test slice. The empirical area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was calculated as a threshold-independent measure of the models’ ability to rank positive slices above negative slices. Because the primary test subset contained only six original slices, including three positive and three negative cases, AUC estimates were interpreted descriptively and were not considered definitive evidence of model superiority.

Uncertainty in test accuracy under the primary workflow was quantified using two-sided exact binomial 95% confidence intervals. Exact intervals were selected because of the very small test sample and the discrete nature of the observed proportions. Confidence intervals were not used to declare statistically significant differences between architectures, because the intervals were expected to be wide and the estimates were derived from the same limited test set.

Bootstrap confidence intervals for AUC and pairwise DeLong tests were considered but were not used as confirmatory analyses. With only three observations in each class, bootstrap resampling would frequently generate samples containing one class only or repeated combinations with limited independent information, resulting in highly discrete or degenerate estimates. Similarly, the variance estimates required for reliable paired DeLong comparisons would be unstable, particularly for models showing complete separation or identical predictions. Any resulting p-values would therefore be insufficiently robust for inferential claims.

A Friedman test was not performed for accuracy, precision, recall, or F1-score. This test requires repeated paired performance measurements across independent experimental blocks, such as multiple cross-validation folds or repeated training runs. In the present study, each architecture produced only one value for each metric within each workflow, and the individual test slices could not be treated as independent replications of accuracy or precision. Accordingly, the available data did not satisfy the structure required for a valid repeated-measures comparison.

The secondary augmentation-before-partitioning workflow was summarized using descriptive classification metrics and confusion matrices only. Formal inferential comparisons were not conducted for this workflow because augmented derivatives originating from the same original slice may have appeared in different subsets, violating the assumption that all test observations were independent of the training data. No formal claims of statistically significant superiority were therefore made for either workflow. The statistical analyses were intended to characterize observed performance and its uncertainty rather than to establish a definitive ranking of the evaluated architectures.

Results

The results are presented according to two methodologically distinct analytical workflows. Approach 1 represents the primary leakage-controlled analysis, in which the original CBCT slices were partitioned before augmentation and only the training subset was augmented. Approach 2 represents a secondary exploratory analysis reproducing the original workflow, in which augmentation preceded dataset partitioning. Because the two approaches differed in test-set size, composition, and independence from the training data, their performance estimates were interpreted separately rather than compared as directly equivalent measures of model generalisation. Classification metrics and confusion matrices are reported for both approaches, whereas ROC-AUC estimates and exact confidence intervals are presented for the primary analysis. Illustrative Grad-CAM findings are reported separately as a qualitative example of model behaviour.

Approach 1: Primary leakage-controlled hold-out analysis

In the primary analysis, the 58 original CBCT slices were stratified and partitioned before any augmentation was performed. The training subset contained 46 original slices, including 24 positive and 22 negative slices. The validation and test subsets each contained six original slices, with three positive and three negative cases in each subset. Augmentation was subsequently restricted to the training data, generating 120 additional positive and 112 additional negative samples. The final training subset therefore comprised 278 image instances, including 144 positive and 134 negative samples, whereas the validation and test subsets remained unaugmented. The split manifest confirmed that each original slice was assigned to only one subset and that augmented derivatives were retained exclusively within the training subset.

DenseNet169, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ResNet18, and CoAtNet-0 correctly classified all six test slices, yielding an observed accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score of 1.000. Their confusion matrices showed three true-negative and three true-positive classifications, with no false-positive or false-negative predictions.

EfficientNet-B3, ConvNeXt-Base, and tf-EfficientNet-B5 each achieved an accuracy of 83.33%, precision of 0.7500, recall of 1.0000, and F1-score of 0.8571. Each model correctly identified all three positive test slices but misclassified one negative slice as positive. DenseNet201 also achieved an accuracy of 83.33%; however, its error pattern differed, with a precision of 1.0000, recall of 0.6667, and F1-score of 0.8000. It produced no false-positive predictions but failed to identify one positive case.

EfficientNet-B4 and EfficientNet-B5 achieved an accuracy of 66.67%, precision of 0.6000, recall of 1.0000, and F1-score of 0.7500. Both models detected all positive slices but produced two false-positive classifications. ResNet50 also achieved 66.67% accuracy, with precision, recall, and F1-score values of 0.6667, corresponding to one false-positive and one false-negative prediction. EfficientNet-B1 achieved the same accuracy but showed a different classification profile, with precision of 1.0000, recall of 0.3333, and F1-score of 0.5000.

EfficientNetV2-S achieved an accuracy of 50.00%, precision of 0.5000, recall of 0.6667, and F1-score of 0.5714. DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0 also achieved 50.00% accuracy; however, both models classified all six test slices as negative. Consequently, they correctly classified the three negative slices but missed all three positive slices, resulting in precision, recall, and F1-score values of 0.0000.

Although several architectures achieved apparently perfect classification in this analysis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the test subset contained only six original slices. Each individual misclassification altered the estimated accuracy by 16.67 percentage points. Accordingly, the observed performance was treated as preliminary single-split evidence, and no definitive conclusion regarding model superiority was drawn from the classification metrics alone.

Statistical results of the primary leakage-controlled analysis

Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed from the positive-class probabilities generated for the six original test slices. The corresponding ROC curves are presented in Figures 4–6, while the empirical AUC estimates and accuracy confidence intervals are summarized in Table 1 and Supplemental Material 1.

CoAtNet-0, ConvNeXt-Base, ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet121, DenseNet169, DenseNet201, and ResNet18 each achieved an empirical AUC of 1.000. EfficientNet-B0 and tf-EfficientNet-B5 achieved an AUC of 0.889. EfficientNet-B1, EfficientNet-B3, EfficientNet-B4, and EfficientNet-B5 each achieved an AUC of 0.778, whereas ResNet50 and EfficientNetV2-S achieved AUC values of 0.667 and 0.444, respectively.

The AUC results did not always correspond directly to classification accuracy at the fixed decision threshold. For example, DenseNet121 achieved an AUC of 1.000 despite an accuracy of 50.00% because the positive-class probabilities assigned to the positive slices were consistently higher than those assigned to the negative slices, although none exceeded the classification threshold required for a positive prediction. This finding illustrates that AUC reflects relative discrimination across thresholds, whereas accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score depend on the selected classification threshold.

Two-sided exact binomial 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the observed accuracy values. For architectures achieving an accuracy of 100.00%, the 95% confidence interval was 54.1%–100.0%. The corresponding intervals were 35.9%–99.6% for an observed accuracy of 83.33%, 22.3%–95.7% for an accuracy of 66.67%, and 11.8%–88.2% for an accuracy of 50.00%. The substantial overlap and width of these intervals demonstrate the high uncertainty associated with performance estimates derived from only six test slices.

Pairwise DeLong tests and bootstrap confidence intervals for AUC were not treated as reliable confirmatory analyses because the test subset contained only three positive and three negative observations. Under these conditions, variance estimates and bootstrap distributions would be highly discrete and potentially degenerate, particularly for models showing complete separation. A Friedman test was also not performed because each architecture produced only one performance estimate from a single hold-out partition, rather than repeated paired measurements across independent folds or training runs. Accordingly, the ROC-AUC values and classification metrics were interpreted descriptively, and no statistically significant superiority of one architecture over another was claimed.

Approach 2: Secondary exploratory augmentation-before-partitioning analysis

In the secondary exploratory analysis, the 58 original CBCT slices were augmented to produce 290 image instances before the expanded dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets using an 80:10:10 ratio. This procedure resulted in 232 training images, 29 validation images, and 29 test images. Because transformed derivatives originating from the same original slice may have been allocated to different subsets, the validation and test observations could not be considered fully independent of the training data. The resulting performance estimates were therefore regarded as exploratory and potentially optimistic. Because augmentation preceded partitioning, the reported performance may partly reflect similarity between transformed samples derived from the same original slices. These results were therefore retained only as a secondary exploratory comparison and were interpreted alongside the primary leakage-controlled analysis.

However, this interpretation remains qualitative and hypothesis-generating.

These qualitative observations illustrate selected activation patterns but do not establish precise calcification localization or clinically valid diagnostic reasoning.

Discussion

The principal finding of this preliminary comparative study was that the apparent performance and ranking of the evaluated CNN architectures were strongly influenced by the sequence of dataset partitioning and augmentation. In the primary leakage-controlled analysis, in which the original CBCT slices were partitioned before training-only augmentation, observed test accuracy ranged from 50.00% to 100.00%. DenseNet169, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ResNet18, and CoAtNet-0 correctly classified all six original test slices. However, the exact 95% confidence interval associated with an observed accuracy of 100% was 54.1%–100.0%, demonstrating that apparently perfect performance on such a small test subset remains highly uncertain. In the secondary exploratory workflow, in which augmentation preceded partitioning, ResNet50, EfficientNet-B3, EfficientNet-B5, and EfficientNetV2-S achieved the highest observed test accuracies. The substantial change in model ranking between the two approaches indicates that performance estimates from small augmented datasets are highly sensitive to data-preparation procedures and should not be interpreted as stable evidence of architectural superiority.

The ResNet results illustrate this instability. ResNet18 achieved perfect classification in the primary analysis but an accuracy of 82.76% in the secondary workflow, whereas ResNet50 achieved 66.67% and 93.10%, respectively. Residual connections may facilitate optimization and gradient propagation in deep networks 24 ; however, the contrasting results across the two workflows suggest that architectural depth alone did not determine performance in the present dataset. The higher secondary-workflow performance of ResNet50 may partly reflect its greater representational capacity, but it may also have been influenced by similarity between augmented derivatives appearing across the data subsets. Conversely, the strong primary-workflow result of ResNet18 was based on only six test slices and therefore cannot establish that the shallower residual model generalizes more reliably.

DenseNet architectures also showed heterogeneous behaviour. DenseNet169 was among the strongest models in both workflows, achieving perfect classification in the primary analysis and 89.66% accuracy in the secondary analysis. DenseNet201 produced intermediate performance in both approaches, whereas DenseNet121 achieved only 50.00% accuracy in the primary analysis despite an empirical AUC of 1.000. This apparent discrepancy occurred because DenseNet121 assigned higher positive-class probabilities to the positive slices than to the negative slices, although all probabilities remained below the fixed threshold for a positive classification. The finding highlights the distinction between threshold-independent discrimination and classification performance at a predefined decision threshold. It also suggests that probability calibration and threshold selection may materially influence the apparent clinical behaviour of a model, particularly when validation data are extremely limited.

The EfficientNet family showed some of the largest differences between the two workflows. EfficientNet-B3, EfficientNet-B5, and EfficientNetV2-S were among the highest-performing models in the secondary exploratory analysis, but their primary-workflow accuracies were 83.33%, 66.67%, and 50.00%, respectively. EfficientNet-B0 and EfficientNet-B1 also showed comparatively limited classification performance in the primary analysis. Although compound scaling of network depth, width, and input resolution can improve accuracy and computational efficiency, 25 the current findings do not support a simple or monotonic relationship between model scale and classification performance. In the present small-sample setting, variants with larger input dimensions and greater model capacity may have been more difficult to fine-tune stably. Moreover, applying a common optimization protocol across all architectures supported a controlled comparison but may not have yielded the optimal fine-tuning configuration for every model, because effective learning rates, regularization strategies, and training schedules can vary across datasets and transfer-learning settings. 26

ConvNeXt and CoAtNet require similarly cautious interpretation. ConvNeXt-Tiny and CoAtNet-0 correctly classified all six test slices in the primary workflow, while ConvNeXt-Base achieved 83.33%. In the secondary workflow, ConvNeXt-Tiny and ConvNeXt-Base achieved 89.66%, whereas CoAtNet-0 achieved 82.76%. Therefore, these architectures did not consistently underperform across both analyses. Their lower performance relative to the highest secondary-workflow models may nevertheless reflect the interaction between model design and dataset scale. ConvNeXt uses a modern convolutional design with larger receptive fields and architectural elements derived from contemporary vision models, whereas CoAtNet combines convolutional operations with attention mechanisms. Such architectures may require larger and more heterogeneous datasets for stable fine-tuning than were available in this study. Their results may also have been influenced by architecture-specific input resolution, batch size, transferability of ImageNet features to grayscale dental imaging, and the use of a common optimization strategy. These explanations remain hypotheses and cannot be confirmed without repeated runs and architecture-specific hyperparameter optimization.

The differences between the two workflows also demonstrate why augmentation should not be interpreted as an increase in the number of independent clinical observations. Although the augmented dataset contained 290 image instances, it was derived from only 58 original CBCT slices. When augmentation was performed before partitioning, transformed copies of the same original slice could contribute to both model training and evaluation, potentially allowing the network to exploit highly similar visual features. The higher and more stable-looking performance observed for several architectures in the secondary workflow may therefore partly reflect information leakage rather than improved generalizability. By contrast, the primary workflow reduced this threat by keeping all augmented derivatives within the training subset, although its very small validation and test subsets resulted in imprecise and unstable performance estimates. These complementary findings reinforce the need to distinguish between dataset size expressed as image instances and the number of independent patients, scans, teeth, or original images.

A recent preliminary study by Shetty et al. 27 evaluated VGG16, ResNet101V2, and MobileNetV2 for detecting pulp calcifications in 240 cropped panoramic radiographs. VGG16 achieved a test accuracy of 85%, an F1-score of 0.86, and an AUC of 0.93. The present study evaluated a broader range of architectures using two-dimensional CBCT slices, but direct numerical comparison is not appropriate because the studies differed in imaging modality, sample composition, cropping strategy, partitioning procedures, and evaluation design. In particular, the larger apparent performance values observed in the secondary workflow of the present study should not be interpreted as superiority over the findings of Shetty et al., because the augmentation-before-partitioning strategy may have produced optimistic estimates. Nevertheless, both investigations support the technical feasibility of transfer learning for pulp-calcification classification and highlight the importance of dataset design and architecture selection in determining reported performance.

The illustrative Grad-CAM examples suggested that correctly classified positive predictions were associated with greater activation within or near the pulp space, whereas the misclassified example showed a more diffuse pattern. These observations are hypothesis-generating and do not establish that the model consistently localized calcifications or relied on clinically relevant features.

Overall, the findings demonstrate the technical feasibility of comparing pretrained CNN architectures for dental pulp calcification classification on two-dimensional CBCT slices. However, the marked change in model ranking between the two workflows indicates that the observed performance was strongly influenced by the data-preparation strategy. The study therefore does not identify a single architecture that can be considered definitively superior.

Clinical implications

Reliable identification of dental pulp calcifications on CBCT images may support preoperative endodontic assessment by alerting clinicians to calcifications that could complicate access preparation, canal localization, and instrumentation. The present findings indicate that pretrained CNN architectures can recognize image patterns associated with the presence or absence of pulp calcification on two-dimensional CBCT slices within the present dataset, supporting further investigation of AI-assisted image review in endodontic practice.

A future system based on this approach could function as a decision-support aid by flagging selected slices for closer clinician assessment rather than replacing professional interpretation. However, the present study did not evaluate clinician–AI interaction, changes in diagnostic decision-making, workflow efficiency, treatment planning, or patient outcomes. Therefore, no claim can currently be made regarding clinical benefit, autonomous diagnosis, or the use of dental pulp calcification detection as a screening tool for systemic disease.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

A principal strength of this study was the controlled head-to-head evaluation of fifteen pretrained deep-learning architectures under standardized training and evaluation procedures. The revised primary workflow addressed the risk of augmentation-related data leakage by partitioning the original CBCT slices before applying augmentation exclusively to the training subset. A split manifest was retained to document the allocation of original and augmented images. Additional methodological strengths included the use of fixed randomization settings, validation-based checkpoint selection, consistent performance definitions, retention of image-level prediction probabilities, empirical ROC-AUC analysis, exact confidence intervals for accuracy, and transparent reporting of the original augmentation-before-partitioning workflow as a secondary exploratory analysis rather than primary evidence.

Several important limitations must nevertheless be acknowledged. First, the dataset comprised only 58 original two-dimensional CBCT slices. Although augmentation increased the number of training image instances, it did not increase the number of independent clinical observations. The primary validation and test subsets each contained only six original slices, including three positive and three negative cases. Consequently, individual classification errors produced large changes in the reported metrics, and the wide exact confidence intervals demonstrate substantial uncertainty. Performance estimates from a single hold-out partition should therefore not be interpreted as stable estimates of architectural superiority.

Second, the secondary exploratory workflow involved augmentation before partitioning. Transformed derivatives originating from the same original slice may therefore have appeared in different subsets, creating a risk of information leakage and optimistic performance estimates. These results were retained solely to demonstrate the sensitivity of model performance to the data-preparation sequence and should not be used as evidence of independent generalization.

Third, the analysis was based on isolated two-dimensional CBCT slices rather than complete volumetric examinations. The models could not use spatial continuity across adjacent slices or assess the three-dimensional extent and location of calcifications. Detailed scanner and acquisition parameters were also unavailable for retrospective reporting, which limits reproducibility and prevents assessment of the influence of device-specific imaging characteristics. Moreover, the dataset was not externally validated using images from independent centers, patient populations, CBCT systems, or acquisition protocols. The absence of external validation represents a major limitation of the study.

Fourth, each architecture was evaluated using a single seeded training run and one predefined data partition. Repeated independent runs, stratified cross-validation, and architecture-specific hyperparameter optimization were not performed. The common optimisation protocol supported procedural comparability but may have favored some architectures over others. The observed differences may therefore reflect not only architectural characteristics but also stochastic training variation, input resolution, batch size, pretrained-feature transferability, and model-specific optimization requirements.

Fifth, the six dental clinicians independently classified the original slices and reported concordant labels; however, their individual ratings were not retained as separate records. Consequently, a formal inter-observer agreement statistic could not be calculated retrospectively. The reference standard was based on image interpretation rather than histopathological confirmation. In addition, the illustrative Grad-CAM examples were not validated using lesion-level annotations, quantitative localization metrics, random case selection, or an independent clinician-scoring protocol.

Finally, the study evaluated technical image-level classification and did not assess clinical utility or clinician–AI performance. Accordingly, the findings should not be extrapolated to autonomous diagnosis or immediate clinical deployment.

Future investigations should use substantially larger datasets with clearly documented patient-, tooth-, and scan-level identifiers to permit grouped partitioning and prevent related observations from appearing across subsets. Repeated stratified cross-validation and independent multi-center external validation should be incorporated, with sample-level prediction probabilities retained for robust confidence intervals and paired model comparisons. Complete volumetric CBCT analysis, architecture-specific optimization, probability calibration, and prespecified operating-threshold selection should also be examined. Explainability findings should be validated against lesion-level annotations and independent clinician assessments. Prospective studies comparing unaided and AI-assisted clinical interpretation will ultimately be required to determine whether such systems provide measurable diagnostic or workflow benefits.

Conclusions

This preliminary comparative study evaluated fifteen pretrained CNN architectures for detecting dental pulp calcifications on two-dimensional CBCT slices and examined the influence of the dataset-preparation workflow on model performance. The substantial changes in performance and model ranking between the leakage-controlled analysis and the exploratory augmentation-before-partitioning analysis demonstrate that the reported outcomes were highly sensitive to the sequence of data augmentation and partitioning.

Although several architectures achieved high or apparently perfect classification performance, the limited number of independent original slices and the small primary test subset preclude conclusions regarding definitive architectural superiority, reliable generalization, or clinical readiness. Performance obtained from the augmentation-before-partitioning workflow should be interpreted cautiously because similarity between derivatives of the same original images may have produced optimistic estimates.

Overall, pretrained CNNs demonstrated preliminary technical feasibility for slice-level identification of dental pulp calcifications. Confirmation in larger, independently validated patient- or scan-level datasets is required before clinical utility or comparative architectural superiority can be established.
