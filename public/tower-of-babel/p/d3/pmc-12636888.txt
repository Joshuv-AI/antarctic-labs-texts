Co-transcriptional modifications of 2′-hydroxyls on synthetic mRNA enhance ribonuclease resistance and lipid nanoparticle thermostability
Authors: Kohki Yamada, Yun-Chieh Sung, Tsung-Ying Lee, Daniel J. Siegwart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The innate fragility of messenger RNA (mRNA) stems from 2′-hydroxyls on the ribose that are responsible for making mRNA sensitive to enzymatic and thermal degradation, posing formidable challenges in efficacy, in vivo stability, global-scale distribution, and long-term storage of mRNA nanomedicines. However, 2′-OH modifications are difficult to introduce, both enzymatically and synthetically, and their potential impact on mRNA translation remains poorly understood. Here, we synthesized 2′-O-methyl (2′-O-Me)- or 2′-fluoro (2′-F)-substituted mRNA through in vitro transcription processes by employing T7 RNA polymerase mutants, aiming to reveal how these 2′-modifications dictate the stability and activity of modified mRNAs. We found that 2′-O-Me and 2′-F modifications protected mRNA from enzymatic degradation, thereby extending the lifetime of intact mRNA in RNase-abundant environments. We observed that higher stability was conferred mostly in exchange for a decrease in translatability, but 2′-modified mRNA still showed detectable activity both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, when formulated into lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and stored at ambient temperature, 2′-modified mRNA led to significantly slower decay in activity. Our findings demonstrate how co-transcriptional 2′-modifications can enhance the stability of synthetic mRNA with application in mRNA LNP formulations.

Introduction

As recently evidenced by the successful approval of multiple SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, messenger RNA (mRNA) draws growing attention as a novel therapeutic modality. Indeed, mRNA-based genetic medicines offer immense potential for vaccines, cancer therapy, and gene editing among other applications.1,2,3,4 Despite these promises, the susceptibility of mRNA to enzymatic degradation by ribonuclease (RNase) ubiquitously existing in vivo has been a substantial barrier to its efficacious delivery to target sites of therapeutic interest. This issue was addressed by encapsulating mRNA molecules into lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations to sterically block RNase-mediated mRNA cleavage. However, mRNA packaged inside LNPs still suffers from low thermostability, which is in part attributed to mRNA’s intrinsic propensity to in-line hydrolysis, requiring storage at extremely low temperatures to preserve mRNA LNP activity.5,6,7,8,9,10

To tackle those challenges associated with mRNA instability, we hereby report an in vitro transcription (IVT)-based approach to mRNA 2′-ribose modifications. Mechanistically, the 2′-hydroxyl group of the ribose sugar is involved in both RNase-mediated degradation and in-line hydrolysis;11,12,13,14 therefore, the substitution of 2′-hydroxyls is hypothesized to prevent mRNA degradation across both pathways. However, this strategy has been underappreciated for the stabilization of mRNA body sequences, with preceding reports confined to chemically synthesized minimal mRNA,15 too short to encode most therapeutically relevant genes, and post-transcriptional chemical functionalization.16 Other works alternatively focused on region-specific end modifications on the 5′ cap17,18 and 3′ poly(A) tail19,20,21 or backbone modifications including phosphorothioates.19,22

Due to its large size ranging from hundreds to thousands of nucleotides in length, mRNA synthesis in general requires IVT, as opposed to the solid-phase chemical synthesis of oligonucleotides and relatively short RNA fragments. IVT is an enzymatic reaction whereby RNA transcripts are produced by a bacteriophage RNA polymerase (RNAP) using an engineered DNA template containing the corresponding promoter sequence. Chemical modifications via IVT are primarily limited by the substrate range of the IVT system. Although several nucleobase modifications, including N1-methyl-pseudouridine (N1-Me-psU), have been identified to be relatively well tolerated by common IVT polymerases such as T7 RNAP,23,24,25,26 the enzyme does not typically accept 2′-ribose-modified substrates for transcription.27,28 In this study, we generated 2′-modified mRNA by utilizing T7 RNAP mutants in a modified IVT protocol. We synthesized and characterized a library of 2′-modified mRNA and discovered that our co-transcriptional modification strategy successfully improved the enzymatic and thermal stability of IVT mRNA.

Results

T7 RNAP mutants enable 2′-O-Me and 2′-F modifications on IVT mRNA

Among the possible 2′-ribose modifications, 2′-O-methyl (2′-O-Me) and 2′-fluoro (2′-F) are the two representative examples that have been most extensively studied in clinically approved RNAi therapeutics, such as GalNAc-conjugated small interfering RNA (siRNA) that targets the liver.29,30,31,32,33,34 Considering their established utility in therapeutic siRNA design, we focused on incorporating these modifications into IVT mRNA by substituting 2′-O-Me ribonucleoside triphosphates (NTPs) or 2′-F deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) for canonical 2′-OH-NTPs in IVT reactions (Figure 1A). The following T7 RNAP mutants were employed: MuIII (hereafter referred to as Mutant X) for 2′-O-Me modification35,36,37 and Y639F mutant (hereafter referred to as Mutant Y) for 2′-F modification38 (Figure 1B). IVT was performed under optimized buffer conditions and for an extended duration of time to ensure maximum transcriptional yield.37 For the remainder of this study, NanoLuc (Nluc) luciferase was chosen as the gene of interest for its small size and superior bioluminescence performance, which provides a sensitive reporter system for detecting the translational activity of mRNA in vitro and in vivo.39,40 Thus, we designed and cloned a plasmid DNA template such that the T7 promoter is located upstream from the Nluc sequence and it also has a 120 nt poly(A) tail at the 3′ end.Figure 12′-O-Me- or 2′-F-modified mRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase mutants(A) Chemical structures of canonical or 2′-ribose-modified triphosphates used as precursors of mRNA transcripts (2′-O-Me and 2′-F are highlighted in pink and blue, respectively). First, we sought to understand the altered substrate specificities of Mutants X and Y by substituting each 2′-OH-ATP, CTP, GTP, and N1-Me-psUTP with their 2′-O-Me or 2′-F counterparts in a systematic, base-by-base fashion. The transcripts were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis (CE) to determine size and integrity. We found that Mutant X can incorporate all four 2′-O-Me-NTPs, 2′-OH-CTP, and 2′-OH-GTP, while rejecting 2′-OH-ATP and 2′-OH-UTP (Figure 1C). Note that 2′-OH-N1-Me-psUTP was replaced with 2′-O-Me-UTP here, and we observed that Mutant X failed to transcribe either 2′-OH- or 2′-O-Me-modified N1-Me-psUTPs (Figure S1). Mutant Y showed its capability of incorporating all 2′-OH-NTPs and 2′-F-dNTPs as long as one or two NTPs were substituted at a time (Figure 1D). Based on estimated IVT yields, Mutant Y may favor canonical 2′-OH-NTPs over 2′-F-dNTPs, and we also observed that the transcriptional activity was almost entirely lost when all four NTPs were substituted altogether (Figures 1D; S2B). Overall, these findings validate the utility of T7 RNAP variants for transcribing 2′-modified mRNA.

2’-O-Me and 2’-F modifications effectively protect mRNA from RNase-mediated degradation

The key question we initially asked was whether 2′-OH substitution with 2′-O-Me or 2′-F provides mRNA with stronger resistance to RNases. To begin with, we generated a small library of 2′-O-Me mRNAs 2–5 with different combinations of 2′-O-Me substitution for 2′-OH-NTPs, along with unmodified 2′-OH mRNA 1 as a control (Figure 2A). Because of the limited substrate range of Mutant X (Figure 1C), mRNAs 2–5 were made with all conceivable patterns of 2′-O-Me-NTP substitution. mRNAs 1–5 were treated with RNase T1, RNase A, and the cocktail of both. RNase T1 is an endoribonuclease that specifically cleaves phosphodiester bonds of single-stranded RNA after G residues, whereas RNase A is known to cut at C and U residues. Following RNase treatment, the mRNA samples were analyzed by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. For RNase T1, a dramatic stability enhancement was seen with mRNAs 3 and 5, in which G residues are completely 2′-O-Me modified (Figure 2B). On the other hand, mRNAs 4 and 5 exhibited improved resistance to RNase A, presumably because both C and U are fully substituted in those mRNAs (Figure 2C). When treated with the cocktail of RNases T1 and A, mRNA 5 showed exclusively higher stability than the rest (Figure 2D), in accordance with the earlier observations in single RNase treatment. Then, to illuminate mRNA stability in a more physiologically relevant context, we tested 2′-O-Me mRNA in serum-containing buffer. mRNAs 1–5 were mixed in PBS with 0.1% FBS and incubated at 37°C for different periods of time. Strikingly, we found that heavily 2′-O-Me-modified mRNA 4 (A, U, C-substituted, which corresponds to approximately 75% of 2′-OHs being modified) and fully modified mRNA 5 (corresponding to 100% of 2′-OHs being modified) remained intact for at least 72 h (Figure 2E), representing the great promise of 2′-O-Me modification to augment mRNA stability.Figure 22′-O-Me modification enhances mRNA stability against various RNases and in serum-containing buffer(A) Pie charts describing the different modification patterns of 2′-O-Me mRNAs synthesized in this study. Next, we investigated the enzymatic stability of 2′-F-modified mRNA. Given the wider substrate acceptance of Mutant Y that allows for simultaneous incorporation of 2′-OH and 2′-F nucleotides into transcripts (Figure 1D), we were inspired to study how a gradient of partial 2′-F modification impacts stability. Thus, two sets of 2′-F mRNA were devised: the first group (mRNAs 6–9) of GTP-specific 2′-F substitution with 25% increment up to 100% and the second group (mRNAs 10–13) of all-NTP 2′-F substitution with 20% increment up to 80% (Figures 3A and 3B). Regarding this approach, it should be noted that the population of modified mRNA may not be completely homogeneous, considering the stochastic nature of nucleotide incorporation by Mutant Y. Acknowledging that possibility, the successful synthesis of mRNAs 6–13 at high integrity and purity was confirmed by CE, and the transcriptional yield was estimated to be comparable across the board (Figures S2C and S2D, S3). The obtained mRNAs 6–13 were each treated with either RNase T1 or RNase A, in parallel with unmodified mRNA 1, followed by denaturing gel electrophoresis to quantify the relative band intensity of undegraded mRNA at the RNase concentrations of 0.01 U/μL for RNase T1 and 10 ng/mL for RNase A (Figures 3C–3F). For mRNAs 6–9, 2′-F modification of G residues led to a linear increase in stability against G-specific RNase T1 in proportion to substitution percentage, but the correlation was less pronounced for C- and U-specific RNase A, consistent with the base specificity of each RNase species. By contrast, 2′-F modification of all four NTPs at once in mRNAs 10–13 resulted in stronger protection from both RNases T1 and A, highlighting the comparative advantage of all-NTP 2′-F substitution. We thus picked mRNAs 10–13 for further analysis in serum stability assays. FBS-treated mRNAs were incubated at 37°C for 10, 30, and 120 min. The quantification of relative band intensities on the denaturing gels showed a delayed degradation for heavily modified mRNAs (such as 60%-substituted mRNA 12 and 80%-substituted mRNA 13) compared to unmodified or lightly modified mRNAs (Figures 3G and 3H). 2′-modified mRNAs are less efficient but active in protein translation

While the series of mRNA stability assays demonstrated the practicality of our co-transcriptional modification strategy, 2′-ribose modifications may potentially interfere with ribosomal translation processes.41,42 Accordingly, the translation activity of 2′-modified mRNA was systematically examined by detecting Nluc luciferase activity in cultured cells. The entire library of 2′-modified mRNAs 1–13 was transfected into HEK293T cells with cationic lipid reagents, and bioluminescence was measured at 24 h after treatment (Figure 4A). 2′-O-Me mRNAs 2–5 showed comparable levels of translation among one another, with approximately 100-fold decrease in efficiency compared to the control mRNA 1 regardless of modification patterns (Figure 4B). Of note, we found that even the fully 2′-O-Me-substituted mRNA 5 was translationally active, albeit at a significantly reduced level. As for 2′-F modification, transfection efficiencies for both mRNAs 6–9 (G-specific substitution) and 10–13 (whole substitution) steadily decreased in a substitution percentage-dependent manner (Figures 4C and 4D). A similar trend of translational activity was confirmed with a longer mRNA encoding firefly luciferase, validating the broader applicability of this approach (Figure S10). In contrast to mRNA 5 (100% 2′-O-Me-modified), 80% 2′-F-modified mRNA 13 did not yield detectable signals at the same dose. For comparison, we also tested an additional group of 2′-F mRNA wherein A, C, G, U, pyrimidine (Y), or purine (R) were each substituted completely with 2′-F and noticed that the 2′-F substitution of even single NTP species can lead to a total loss of mRNA activity (Figure S4), which indicates that 2′-F may not be well tolerable by translation machinery.43,44 These studies collectively suggest that 2′-O-Me and 2′-F can indeed have certain deleterious effects on mRNA translation activity, whereas their extent can be flexibly controlled by modulating the substitution percentage of either modification introduced into the mRNA.Figure 42′-O-Me and 2′-F mRNAs showed translation activity in vitro and in vivo(A) A library of unmodified or 2′-modified mRNA was transfected into HEK293T cells, and Nluc activity was detected by measuring bioluminescence. Subsequently, based on mRNA translatability in vitro, we narrowed the list of 2′-modified mRNA candidates down to 2′-O-Me mRNA 5 and 2′-F mRNA 10 based on their relatively high activity balanced with greater stability. To elucidate the in vivo activity of 2′-modified mRNA, four-component LNPs were prepared to individually encapsulate mRNA 1 (2′-OH unmodified), 5 (2′-O-Me 100% modified), and 10 (2′-F 20% modified) (Figure 4E). The ionizable amino lipid 4A3-SC8 was used along with DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 (molar percentage: 23.8/23.8/47.5/5.0).45 Each mRNA LNP was administered via intramuscular injection to C57BL/6 mice at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg, and bioluminescence images of luciferase activity were recorded at 6 h post-injection. In correspondence with in vitro screening results, 2′-modified mRNAs 5 and 10 led to reduced yet detectable mRNA activity in vivo (Figure 4F). We further tracked the bioluminescence of mRNA 1 and 10 Nluc LNPs over the time course from 6 h to 4 days following single injections. Interestingly, unmodified mRNA 1 LNPs quickly dropped in bioluminescence, while 2′-F-modified mRNA 10 LNPs maintained at a comparable level to eventually reach almost on par with mRNA 1 LNPs (Figure S5). These data suggest that the kinetic profile of in vivo mRNA translation might be altered by 2′-modifications.

Incorporation of 2′-modified mRNAs into LNP formulations improves their shelf stability

Finally, we explored the possibility that 2′-modifications might impart higher thermostability during shelf storage to mRNA LNPs. To test this hypothesis, we chose ionizable lipids ALC-0315 and SM-102, both employed in COVID-19 mRNA vaccines by Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna, respectively. For the subsequent LNP stability study, the molar ratios of LNP components were maintained consistent regardless of the ionizable amino lipid used, to enable a direct comparison of the different ionizable lipids under otherwise identical conditions. It should be noted that this experimental design, in turn, resulted in deviation from the actual formulas used for Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna’s mRNA vaccines, as well as the use of vortex mixing for LNP formulation instead of the industry-standard controlled T mixing method. LNPs with mRNAs 1, 5, or 10 were prepared anew, followed by dialysis in PBS to remove any residual ethanol carried over from LNP synthesis procedures, and stored at 25°C for up to 7 days (Figure 5A). The basic physicochemical properties of mRNA LNPs, including hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta-potential, and encapsulation efficiency, were measured on days 4 and 7 (Figures 5B an 5C; S6). No obvious change in these parameters was confirmed throughout 1 week of storage. On day 7, HEK293T cells were treated with each LNP in parallel. Owing to different baselines for 2′-modified mRNA activity than 2′-OH mRNA, the activity decay was normalized by calculating relative bioluminescence in proportion to that of freshly prepared LNPs on day 0 without any storage. Notably, 2′-O-Me mRNA 5 and 2′-F mRNA 10 LNPs gave significant enhancement in thermostability compared to unmodified mRNA 1 LNPs for both ionizable lipid species (Figures 5B and 5C). We further continued to monitor mRNA LNP activity until day 14 to characterize the rate at which activity declines over time. The non-linear fitting based on a one-phase decay model yielded greater half-life values for mRNAs 5 and 10 than for mRNA 1 (Figure S7). We were also able to confirm that the stability enhancement by 2′-modifications was correlated with maintained mRNA integrity after storage, by using CE traces of mRNA samples extracted from those shelf-stored LNPs to quantify the change in the amount of partial RNA fragments (Figures 5B, 5C, and S8). Furthermore, a similar benefit of enhanced storage stability was confirmed with an additional ionizable lipid, DLin-MC3-DMA, a clinically approved platform for an siRNA-LNP therapy, supporting the generality of our approach (Figure S16). Discussion

Our investigation confirmed that both 2′-O-Me and 2′-F modifications significantly enhance mRNA stability against enzymatic degradation in a base-specific manner, with the degree of protection depending on the extent of modification. Although RNases used in this study (RNase T1 and RNase A) are not naturally occurring in humans, the relevance of our findings is supported by our use of RNase concentrations (0.1–1 μg/mL) similar to those found in blood plasma for one of their human counterparts, RNase 1 (approximately up to 0.5 μg/mL).46,47

We observed that 2′-modified mRNAs exhibited a reduction in translation efficiency, which we propose is a critical design parameter. This trade-off between stability and translational activity can be fine-tuned by adjusting the stoichiometry of the modified and unmodified NTPs during IVT. More importantly, the altered translation kinetics of the modified mRNAs may allow for a more sustained, lower-level expression over a prolonged period, which could lead to potential therapeutic benefits in certain settings, such as durable efficacy and less frequent dosing.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the impact of 2′-ribose modifications on the intricate process of translation remains less understood within the scope of our current work. However, we hypothesize that these modifications interfere with the ribosome’s function by either sterically hindering the decoding process or impeding the translocation step.41 While our current study does not directly provide the mechanistic evidence to distinguish between these two possibilities, future studies utilizing techniques such as ribosome profiling could provide critical insight by precisely mapping the locations of ribosome pauses on modified versus unmodified mRNAs. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of these molecular-level interactions is crucial for optimizing the design of therapeutic mRNA, where a balance between stability and translation efficiency is paramount.

A critical consideration for the clinical translation of modified mRNA is the fidelity of the transcription process, particularly when utilizing mutant T7 polymerases to incorporate non-canonical 2′-modified nucleotides. These altered enzymes may exhibit reduced specificity or processivity compared to their wild-type counterparts, raising valid concerns about the potential for unintended nucleotide misincorporation or the generation of truncated transcripts. While our initial quality control, including CE analysis, confirmed the synthesis of a full-length mRNA product, we acknowledge that a more comprehensive analysis of transcript homogeneity would be beneficial. High-resolution sequencing, for instance, could provide definitive validation of transcription fidelity and is a key step for future studies aiming to advance this technology toward clinical application.

The therapeutic application of mRNA hinges on minimizing the innate immune response, which can cause rapid mRNA degradation and systemic inflammation. Our study utilized 2′-O-Me modifications, a proven strategy for suppressing immunogenicity by inhibiting Toll-like receptor (TLR) activation. In contrast, 2′-F modifications may also abrogate TLR responses but could enhance the sensing of RNA by cytoplasmic helicases like RIG-I and MDA-5, highlighting the importance of selecting modifications based on the desired immune profile.48,49

Finally, we believe the scalability and manufacturability of this approach under guanosine good manufacturing practices (GMP) conditions are achievable. The path toward GMP production of the 2′-modified mRNA drug substance would build upon the existing, well-established framework for IVT-based mRNA manufacturing. The core process, including DNA template preparation, IVT reaction, DNase treatment, and purification, remains largely unchanged, with specific validation steps for the use of mutant polymerases and modified nucleotides. The mutant T7 RNAP, which enables the incorporation of 2′-O-Me and 2′-F-modified NTPs, is currently a specialized research-grade enzyme. Nevertheless, its production can be scaled using established recombinant protein expression and purification workflows that are already well defined and widely employed in the biopharmaceutical industry. Similarly, while 2′-modified NTPs are not yet readily available in large quantities from commercial vendors, their synthesis relies on well-understood organic chemistry principles that are amenable to industrial-scale production. As the demand for these modified reagents grows, dedicated chemical synthesis routes could be established to provide the necessary materials for clinical and commercial manufacturing.

In conclusion, we employed an IVT-based, co-transcriptional approach to introduce 2′-O-Me and 2′-F modifications into synthetic mRNA. Both types of modifications enhanced mRNA’s RNase resistance while reducing translation efficiency. Selected candidates of 2′-O-Me and 2′-F mRNA from in vitro mRNA delivery screening showed detectable levels of activity in vivo. We also demonstrated that, using formulations based on the ionizable amino lipids found in worldwide distributed COVID-19 vaccines, these mRNA modifications have the potential to achieve greater LNP thermostability during storage, thereby extending their shelf life post-formulation. Despite these promising results, our study has certain limitations. We acknowledge that our LNP stability data, while encouraging, represent a preliminary short-term assessment of only 2 weeks. It is important to emphasize that we conducted this analysis under accelerated conditions of 25°C, which provides a more rigorous test than the typical refrigerated storage (4°C). To fully evaluate the commercial and clinical viability of these formulations, future studies will need to extend these stability analyses over a longer duration under relevant storage conditions. Additionally, while we focused on a specific set of mRNA modifications, a comprehensive screening of other modified nucleotides and their combinations could reveal even more effective strategies. Future research should also explore the long-term safety and biodistribution of these optimized mRNA LNP formulations. Importantly, our work on mRNA LNP stability expands the toolbox available to refine their design to streamline manufacturing processes, simplify handling, and improve accessibility to a broader population, which may inspire further examination of mRNA modifications in vaccines and therapeutic applications.

Materials and methods

IVT and capping

T7 RNAP MuIII is a mutant form of T7 RNAP purchased from Hongene Biotech (Union City, CA). It is specifically engineered to incorporate 2′-O-Me-modified ribonucleoside-5′-triphosphates, enabling unique applications in RNA synthesis. This T7 variant will be referred to as Mutant X in this section. T7 RNAP Y639F was purchased from LGC Biosearch Technologies (United Kingdom), along with its 5× reaction buffer and 100 mM DTT. This T7 variant will be referred to as Mutant Y in this section. mRNA-CleanCap-NanoLuc was a gift from Howard Chang (Addgene plasmid #188247; http://n2t.net/addgene:188247; RRID: Addgene_188247).50 The coding fragment of NanoLuc (Nluc) luciferase (603 bp) was first cloned into a pCS2+MT plasmid backbone featuring an SP6 promoter, customized 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions as well as a 120 nt poly(A) segment through NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Its SP6 promoter sequence (5′ ATTTAGGTGACACTATAG 3′) was then converted into the T7 promoter sequence (5′ TAATACGACTCACTATAG 3′) by Q5 site-directed mutagenesis (NEB). For 2′-O-Me mRNA IVT using Mutant X, 5× transcription buffer was prepared according to the following recipe: 1 M HEPES/KOH (pH 7.6), 0.2 M DTT, 10 mM spermidine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 0.05% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). The unit reaction was assembled with 0.25 U T7 Mutant X, 1 μg linearized DNA template, 2 mM NTPs (2′-OH or 2′-O-Me), 1 mM GMP, 6 mM Mg(OAC)2, 1.5 mM Mn(OAC)2 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 U RNase inhibitor (Hongene) in 1× transcription buffer. The IVT mix was incubated overnight at 37°C, followed by column purification using the Monarch spin RNA cleanup kit (NEB). For 2′-F mRNA IVT using Mutant Y, the unit reaction was assembled with 1.25 U T7 Mutant Y, 1 μg linearized DNA template, 2 mM NTPs (2′-OH or 2′-F), 10 mM DTT, and 1 U RNase inhibitor in 1× reaction buffer. The IVT mix was incubated overnight at 37°C, followed by column purification. Following each IVT, a Cap1 structure was post-transcriptionally introduced at the 5′ end by using the Cap1 Capping System (Hongene) according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Capped mRNA was then purified by LiCl precipitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific), after which the length and integrity of the purified mRNA transcripts were analyzed by CE. CE described in this study was all conducted on the 4150 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies, part no. G2992AA) with high-sensitivity RNA ScreenTape, ladder, and sample buffer (part no. 5067-5579, 5067-5581, and 5067-5580). Data were processed and visualized using TapeStation analysis software (Agilent Technologies). As for IVT materials, N1-methyl-pseudouridine triphosphates (sodium salt solution), 2′-O-Me-ATP/GTP/CTP (lithium salt solution), and 2′-F-dATP/dGTP/dCTP/dUTP (lithium salt solution) were all purchased from Hongene Biotech, whereas 2′-O-Me-UTP and N1-methyl-2′-O-Me-N1-methyl-pseudoUTP (lithium salt solution) were purchased from TriLink BioTechnologies (San Diego, CA).

mRNA enzymatic stability

For protection of mRNA from RNase T1, RNase A, and their cocktail (all purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific), first, 1 μg of each mRNA was diluted in either RNase T1 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5 plus 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; both purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific) or UltraPure DNase/RNase-free distilled water (Invitrogen). To it was added 1 μL of RNase solution in the corresponding buffer (RNase T1: 1 or 0.1 U/μL, RNase A: 1 or 0.1 μg/mL, the cocktail: 2,000× or 20,000× dilution from the master stock as received from the vendor. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 10 min, immediately followed by the addition of 2× glyoxal gel sample loading dye (premixed with ethidium bromide; purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific) and heating at 95°C for 5 min. The sample was then loaded into each well for electrophoresis in 1× NorthernMax-Gly gel running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For protection of mRNA from FBS, 1 μg of each mRNA was first diluted in 1× PBS. To it was added 1 μL of 1% FBS solution in 1× PBS. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for different time spans (10 min–72 h). Once the incubation was complete, 2× glyoxal gel sample loading dye was added to the solution, and the sample was heated at 95°C for 5 min. The sample was then loaded into each well for electrophoresis in 1× NorthernMax-Gly gel running buffer. All the denaturing agarose gel images were obtained with the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The RiboRuler high-range RNA ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was run alongside the experimental samples on the gel to estimate the size of each band. Data were processed using Image Lab 6.1 Software, and band intensities were calculated by manually detecting the bands in each lane and adjusting them by subtracting the lane background.

In vitro luciferase expression and cell viability in HEK293T cells

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) at 37°C under 5% CO2 atmosphere in a humidified incubator. All cells were used with low passage numbers after the initial thawing of frozen vials. To test mRNA transfection activity in vitro, Lipofectamine MessengerMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Briefly, Nluc mRNA and lipid reagents were individually diluted in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium and then hand-mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio to allow the mRNA-lipid complexes to self-assemble. After a 5-min incubation at room temperature, the samples were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 2 ng/μL mRNA. HEK293T cells were seeded into a white 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well 24 h prior to treatment. On the day of treatment, the medium was first replaced afresh with 90 μL of the complete culture medium, and 10 μL of Nluc mRNA was added at a dose of 20 ng per well. After a 24-h incubation, the Nano-Glo luciferase assay (cat. no. N1130, Promega) and CellTiter-Fluor cell viability assay (cat. no. N7120, Promega) were performed to measure Nluc mRNA expression and cytotoxicity, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols. Luminescence and fluorescence in 96-well plates were measured on an Infinite F/M200 Pro microplate reader (Tecan).

mRNA LNP preparation and characterization

mRNA LNPs were formulated using the ethanol dilution method.45 4A3-SC8 was synthesized and purified according to previously published lab protocols.51 For LNP preparation, ionizable lipids (4A3-SC8, ALC-0315, or SM-102), DOPE (Avanti Polar Lipids), cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich), and DMG-PEG2000 (NOF America Corporation) were dissolved in pure ethanol at a molar ratio of 23.8:23.8:47.5:5.0. Nluc mRNA was dissolved in 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 4.2 (Sigma-Aldrich). The weight ratio of total lipids to mRNA was fixed at 40:1. The ethanol phase was added to the aqueous phase by vortex mixing at the ethanol-to-aqueous volume ratio of 1:3, followed by 15-min incubation at room temperature. The synthesized LNPs were dialyzed (Pur-A-Lyzer Midi Dialysis Kit, MWCO: 3.5 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich) against 1× PBS for 2 h at 4°C before subsequent use. To evaluate the physicochemical properties of LNPs, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was performed by using 100 μL samples diluted in PBS to measure hydrodynamic diameter and PDI. Particle size distribution was also monitored, and single peaks were confirmed with no obvious sign of aggregation. Zeta-potential of LNPs was analyzed by electrophoretic light scattering (ELS), using 750 μL samples diluted in ultrapure water. DLS and ELS were both performed on Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments; He-Ne laser, λ = 632 nm). To measure mRNA encapsulation efficiencies, the Quant-it RiboGreen RNA assay (Invitrogen) was conducted according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Briefly, LNPs were diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng/mL mRNA in 1× TE buffer. Additionally, mRNA standards for each sample were prepared individually at concentrations of 0, 2, 10, 50, and 100 ng/mL in 1× TE buffer. All LNP samples and mRNA standards were transferred to a 96-well black opaque polystyrene microplate, with 100 μL of each in three replicates per group (n = 3). To each well, 100 μL of the RiboGreen reagent (diluted 2,000-fold in 1× TE buffer) was added and mixed thoroughly by shaking. Fluorescence was measured with the Tecan microplate reader (excitation: 485 nm, emission: 535 nm) to quantify the amount of free mRNA. Standard curves were generated for each of the unmodified and 2′-modified mRNAs to facilitate accurate estimation of encapsulation efficiencies.

In vivo luciferase expression in mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and were conducted in accordance with local, state, and federal regulations as applicable. C57BL/6J mice (4–6 weeks old, female, 18–20 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free, barrier animal facility at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, with 30%–50% air humidity, and on a 12/12-h dark/light cycle. They were fed standard Teklad Global 2916 chow. To test mRNA activity in vivo, each mouse received PBS or a 0.2 mg/kg dose of Nluc mRNA LNPs via intramuscular (i.m.) injection. After 6, 24, 48, and 96 h, the mice were injected with Nano-Glo Fluorofurimazine (FFz) in vivo substrate (cat. no. N4110, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and then euthanized. The right leg, where the injection site was located, was harvested and imaged. Bioluminescence imaging with animals was performed on a PerkinElmer IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). The total flux was calculated by drawing a region of interest (ROI) around the injection site.

LNP thermostability

Nluc mRNA LNPs were prepared and dialyzed as described earlier. For standardization across all groups, freshly prepared LNPs were characterized by DLS, ELS, and RiboGreen assay, as well as tested for mRNA in vitro activity in HEK293T cells, which was regarded to be 100% activity as the reference point (day 0). For shelf storage, LNP samples were aliquoted into separate tubes for later analyses on days 4 and 7, after which each tube was tightly sealed with Parafilm. Until further use, LNPs were placed inside a bench-top incubator, where the temperature was consistently maintained at 25°C. After certain lengths of storage, LNPs were re-characterized and used for transfection experiments. To evaluate mRNA integrity, Nluc mRNA LNPs were stored at 25°C for an extended period of time (3 weeks) under the same conditions as described earlier. After the shelf storage, LNP samples (containing 500 ng mRNA in 100 μL PBS solution) were dissociated by adding 100 μL of 0.1% Triton X-100. Then, mRNA was purified using the Monarch spin RNA cleanup kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted mRNA samples were analyzed using CE, and the obtained traces were subsequently analyzed using software to calculate the relative amount (or percentage) of partial RNA fragments, determined by the length of nucleotides (see also Figure S8).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 was used to perform statistical analyses. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s test for multiple comparisons was used to calculate statistical significance (ns: p > 0.05; ∗ denotes p < 0.05; ∗∗ denotes p < 0.01; ∗∗∗ denotes p < 0.001).
