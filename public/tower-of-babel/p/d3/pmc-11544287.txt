Activating autophagy improves paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy in chemotherapy
Authors: Jin Zhang, Yelan Huang, Xiaohan Sun, Xiya Chen, Xi Zhao, Chenqiu Ran, Bo Liu, Yue Hao
Published: 2024

To the editor:

Paclitaxel is one of the most widely used chemotherapy drugs for treating several types of cancer, including lung, breast, and cervical cancers1,2. Clinical application of paclitaxel is closely associated with a serious adverse effect known as chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), which affects 44%–98% of patients treated with paclitaxel, causing symptoms such as loss of sensation, burning or tingling sensations, abnormal pain, and difficulties in performing daily activities3. It is worth noting that the pathogenesis of CIPN remains unclear, and currently there is a lack of effective treatment strategies. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the underlying mechanisms of CIPN to identify and develop potential therapeutic approaches. Based on this, using in vivo and in vitro rats CIPN models, we discovered for the first time that dysregulation of autophagy serves as a significant underlying factor contributing to CIPN. The involvement of autophagy in numerous diseases has been extensively investigated, however, its role in CIPN remains largely unexplored. We found that paclitaxel disrupts the fusion process between autophagosomes and lysosomes in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, resulting in the accumulation of damaged mitochondria. This disruption ultimately contributes to the onset and progression of CIPN. By utilizing 3-methyladenine (3-MA), an autophagy inhibitor, and Acadesine (AICAR), an autophagy activator4, we identified that AICAR exerts promising improvement on CIPN by inducing autophagy and promoting lysosomal clearance of damaged mitochondria, which offers a novel approach to address the quandary encountered in the clinical application of paclitaxel.

Paclitaxel-induced CIPN exhibited evident mitochondrial damage and dysregulation of autophagy in DRG neurons

We first established paclitaxel-induced CIPN model in rats, the paw withdrawal thresholds and the paw liking latency in rats treated with paclitaxel (2.0 mg/kg, 4 injections) were remarkably lower than that of the control on both Days 8 and 14 (Fig. 1A), indicating paclitaxel-induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity. We then examined changes in intraepidermal nerve fibers (IENFs) in both control and paclitaxel-treated rats using immunohistochemistry. In the control group, the nerve fibers (stained green) were abundant in length and density, extending from the basement membrane to the epidermis. On the contrary, paclitaxel significantly reduced the density of IENFs. Further, the correlation analysis illustrated that the relative IENFs density was positively correlated with the paw withdrawal threshold (Supporting Information Fig. S1A) and paw licking latency (Fig. S1B). Importantly, we found that paclitaxel caused mitochondrial swelling and vacuolation, as well as a significant increase in the number of abnormal mitochondria (Fig. 1B). The correlation analysis illustrated the relative abnormal mitochondria number was positively correlated with IENFs density and the paw withdrawal threshold (Fig. 1C and D). In DRG neurons, mitochondrial damage was also significantly correlated with the loss of nerve fibers (Fig. S1C–S1E). Therefore, we focused on mitochondria to explore the potential mechanisms underlying CIPN. Through RNA-seq in the DRGs of paclitaxel-induced CIPN rats, 287 genes were found to be down regulated; 97 genes were demonstrated to be up regulated, of which 53 genes changed significantly (P < 0.05). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment for these significantly altered genes demonstrated that paclitaxel caused significant damage to DRG tissues, including changes in morphology or physiology, cell death, autophagy, dysregulation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) pathway, and abnormal function of neurons (Fig. 1E, Supporting Information Fig. S2). The process of autophagy, as a crucial biological mechanism, is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases5. However, its involvement in CIPN remains unexplored. We subsequently assessed the expression of key autophagy-related proteins in DRG tissues and observed that paclitaxel treatment elicited significant accumulation of LC3II, while failing to activate Beclin1. Notably, paclitaxel did not induce the degradation of p62 (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that paclitaxel may impair the autophagy process.

The DRG neurons treated with paclitaxel were subsequently examined, revealing the induction of prominent autophagosomes by paclitaxel; however, concomitant with this effect, a substantial accumulation of damaged mitochondria was also observed (Fig. 1G). Similarly, paclitaxel did not induce the degradation of p62 in DRG neurons, but instead promoted the accumulation of p62, and there was no further accumulation of p62 and LC3 II in DRG neurons following the administration of the autophagy inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Baf.A1), indicating that paclitaxel may cause the inhibition of autophagy on substrate degradation (Fig. 1H). To validate this, we transfected DRG neurons with a GFP-RFP-LC3 plasmid to monitor the presence of autophagosomes and autophagolysosomes. Paclitaxel treatment facilitated an increase in autophagosome formation (Yellow) but did not stimulate the generation of autophagolysosomes (Red). Similarly, employing LC3 and LAMP2 as markers for autophagosomes and lysosomes respectively, we observed no significant co-localization between LC3 and LAMP2 upon paclitaxel exposure (Fig. 1I), also illustrating the formation of autophagolysosomes was failed. Then, we examined the expression of key proteins regulating lysosomal function in DRG neurons, lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), LAMP2, Ras-related protein Rab-5 (RAB5), RAB7 and transcription factor EB (TFEB)6, after treatment of paclitaxel. The findings demonstrated that paclitaxel significantly downregulated the expression levels of LAMP1, LAMP2, RAB5, RAB7, and TFEB (Fig. 1J), further indicating the damage of lysosomes. In DRG tissues, paclitaxel significantly decreased the expression levels of LAMP1 and RAB7 while also downregulating TFEB (Fig. 1K). Overall, in the CIPN model, paclitaxel induced significant autophagy dysfunction in DRG neurons.

AICAR repairs paclitaxel-induced lysosomal dysfunction and promotes autophagolysosome formation to clear damaged mitochondria

Given that paclitaxel induces defective autophagy in DRGs, we explored whether inhibiting autophagy or activating intact autophagy could serve as potential treatments for CIPN. To investigate this, we used the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA and the autophagy activator AICAR in a rat CIPN model, respectively. Both 3-MA and AICAR improved paw withdrawal thresholds in paclitaxel-treated rats; however, only AICAR alleviated the paw licking latency (Fig. 1L). We subsequently examined plantar epidermal nerve fibers in rats and observed a significant restoration of paclitaxel-induced nerve fiber loss with AICAR treatment, whereas 3-MA did not exhibit the same effect (Fig. 1M). Similarly, AICAR, but not 3-MA, reduced mitochondrial damage in DRG tissues induced by paclitaxel (Fig. 1N). Subsequently, we investigated the impact of 3-MA supplementation on cell viability and ATP production in DRG neurons following treatment with Paclitaxel and AICAR. The addition of 3-MA partially counteracted the decrease in neuronal activity induced by paclitaxel, while AICAR exhibited a more pronounced neuroprotective effect. Notably, 3-MA failed to ameliorate the reduction in ATP levels caused by paclitaxel, whereas AICAR significantly restored neuronal ATP production capacity. Importantly, the co-administration of 3-MA and AICAR resulted in a significant attenuation or even abrogation of the protective effect exerted by AICAR (Fig. 1O and P), thereby substantiating that autophagy serves as the underlying mechanism through which AICAR exerts its protective effects.

Furthermore, we conducted additional investigations to examine the impact of AICAR on CIPN. AICAR demonstrated a significant enhancement in the cellular viability, ATP production capacity, and mitochondrial membrane potential of DRG neurons damaged by paclitaxel, but did not affect the anti-cancer activity of paclitaxel (Supporting Information Figs. S3 and S4), which confirming the protective effect of AICAR on DRG neurons and its potential in alleviating nerve damage caused by paclitaxel chemotherapy. The examination of mitochondrial membrane potential and nerve fibers from DRG neurons also confirmed that AICAR effectively reversed the paclitaxel-induced mitochondrial damage and loss of nerve fibers (Fig. 1Q and R). Sustained mitochondrial membrane potential reduction often induces apoptosis, but interestingly, paclitaxel did not induce significant apoptosis in DRG cells in our model (Supporting Information Fig. S5). Importantly, AICAR facilitated the fusion between autophagosomes and lysosomes and reduced the accumulation of paclitaxel-induced ubiquitination proteins (Fig. 1S, Supporting Information Fig. S6A and S6B), thereby demonstrating the restoration of autophagic functionality. Subsequently, by detecting key lysosomal regulatory proteins in DRG neurons and DRG tissues, AICAR was found to restore the expression of RAB7 and TFEB (Fig. 1T). In addition, AICAR restored the autophagy degradation function of p62 after paclitaxel injury (Fig. 1U and V).

Considering that mitochondrial damage can directly initiate mitochondrial autophagy, we further investigated the involvement of mitophagy in paclitaxel-induced injury of DRG and AICAR-mediated protection of DRG. By utilizing Mdivi-17, an inhibitor of mitophagy, we observed a reduction in paclitaxel-induced damage to DRG cells upon inhibition of mitophagy. Furthermore, the protective effect of AICAR on DRG cells was also diminished with inhibition of mitophagy (Fig. S6C and S6D). Moreover, we conducted a preliminary exploration of the mechanisms by which paclitaxel and AICAR regulate autophagy/mitophagy. We found that paclitaxel promoted the formation of autophagosomes by activating the PINK1-Parkin pathway through mitochondrial damage but concurrently impaired lysosomal function, hindering autophagolysosome formation (Supporting Information Fig. S7). AICAR activates unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1)–FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1)-mediated autophagy8 and promotes lysosomal function by activating AMPK (Supporting Information Fig. S8). Notably, both the autophagy induced by AICAR and the protective effect on DRG cells were due to the activation of AMPK (Supporting Information Fig. S9A–S9E). We also assessed the mitochondrial number and ATP production in DRG tissues of the paclitaxel-induced rat model and AICAR-treated groups. The results revealed no significant disparity in the overall mitochondrial count among the groups; however, there was a substantial increase in damaged mitochondria within the paclitaxel group, accompanied by a decline in ATP production. Conversely, in the AICAR treatment group, there was a notable reduction in damaged mitochondria along with a significant restoration of ATP production (Supporting Information Fig. S9F–S9H), indicating that AICAR facilitated the clearance and regeneration of paclitaxel-damaged mitochondria. In addition, the role of autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), autophagy activator rapamycin9 and lysosomal activator EN610 in paclitaxel induced CIPN was also preliminally investigated, and it was found that CQ did not show protective activity, while rapamycin and EN6 showed certain protective effect (Supporting Information Fig. S10), however, the specific mechanism also needs to be further studied. Nevertheless, this further demonstrated the therapeutic potential of activating autophagy and lysosome functions for paclitaxel-induced CIPN.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate for the first time that paclitaxel-induced disfunction of autophagy plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy. Despite inducing autophagosome formation, paclitaxel impairs autophagolysosome formation, thereby exacerbating the accumulation of damaged mitochondria. The activation of autophagy represents a promising and effective therapeutic strategy for chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, offering a bright prospect for resolving the clinical application dilemma associated with paclitaxel.
