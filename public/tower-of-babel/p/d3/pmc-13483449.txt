Decoding cancer circulating transcriptomic signatures with language models
Authors: Siwei Deng, Lei Sha, Yongcheng Jin, Tianyao Zhou, Chengen Wang, Qianpu Liu, Hongjie Guo, Chengjie Xiong, Yangtao Xue, Xiaoguang Li, Yuanming Li, Yaping Gao, Mengyu Hong, Junjie Xu, Shanwen Chen, Pengyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current liquid biopsy methods for multi-cancer detection using plasma cell-free RNA (cfRNA, short RNA fragments circulating in blood that can reflect disease states) typically rely on gene annotations, which can overlook signals from unannotated or repetitive genomic regions. We present GeneLLM, a Transformer-based model that directly processes the nucleotide sequences of human-mapped cfRNA reads to identify cancer-indicative signatures. By bypassing gene-level quantification, the model retains signals from transcriptomic dark matter. The model learns latent pseudo-biomarkers (prototype representations from aggregated cfRNA read embeddings) that serve as discriminative features for cancer classification, rather than corresponding to explicit genomic sequences. Here we show that, in a multi-centre cohort, GeneLLM achieves ROC-AUC values ranging from 0.9250 to 0.9962 across several cancers, while maintaining comparable performance at one-sixth of the typical sequencing depth. These results suggest that sequence-level modelling of plasma cfRNA can capture diagnostically relevant information beyond annotation-dependent approaches, enabling more cost-efficient and scalable cancer screening.

Introduction

Early cancer detection is crucial for improving treatment efficacy and patient outcomes1,2. Non-invasive, cost-effective techniques, such as those leveraging cell-free RNA (cfRNA), are increasingly important for this purpose. CfRNA provides advantages over cell-free DNA (cfDNA) for solid tumour detection by reflecting dynamic gene expression profiles in response to disease3–9. This approach addresses limitations of existing methods like colonoscopy and mammography, which can be invasive, expensive, and resource-intensive10,11.

Nonetheless, cfRNA-based detection encounters challenges of sensitivity and specificity, arising from fluctuating cfRNA levels and the difficulty in differentiating cancerous and normal profiles. To address this, studies have integrated machine learning methods, performing differential expression (DE) analysis to discover differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and identify potential biomarkers with methods such as random forest12. However, these methods are still reliant on annotated genes.

Research has uncovered sequences from cfRNA that do not usually reside in gene regions, such as repetitive RNA regions, which show promise as biomarkers5. Other transcripts, such as transcribed ultraconserved regions, also exhibit promise in cancer detection13. These sequences, often called the dark matter of the genome, are gaining recognition in cancer detection14. Despite their potential, these sequences are not annotated and thus cannot be included in traditional bioinformatics pipelines dependent on genome annotations. Consequently, the insights from the sequences transcribed from the genome’s dark matter are frequently overlooked. The first sequence-based pretrained model, Evo, was published in Science15, and an updated version (Evo 2) followed16. However, despite its design, Evo 2 is not well-suited to process raw sequencing data, a format common in experimental settings, due to the complexity of such data.

We introduce Gene Large Language Model (GeneLLM), a pretrained large language model for cfRNA analysis. It directly analyses raw sequencing data to discover short, cancer-indicative sequences, called pseudo-biomarkers, without relying on existing genome annotations. In this study, we show that GeneLLM classifies multiple cancer types from multi-centre samples with comparable performance at one-sixth standard sequencing depth. GeneLLM identified cfRNA molecules transcribed from unannotated regions of the genome, which are undetectable using conventional methods. Because each cancer type was predominantly recruited at a specific centre, we mitigated potential site confounding by centre-matching non-cancer controls (Fig. 1B–E) and by applying a source-bias elimination step during disease tuning (Section “Disease tuning”, Eq. (10)) for de-biased patient-level features. Different from Evo and Evo2, our architecture in GeneLLM represents a foundation model that is trained directly on raw sequencing data and processes these data at the patient scale. These results suggest three main implications. First, GeneLLM advances marker discovery by directly identifying short cfRNA sequences (pseudo-biomarkers) without prior knowledge. It also identifies sequences from unannotated regions (the dark genome), often overlooked by traditional methods. Second, GeneLLM improves detection accuracy and reduces costs by requiring only plasma samples to detect multiple cancers simultaneously. Its versatility across different cancer types demonstrates diagnostic potential and may advance cancer screening by unlocking dark genome potential, improving accessibility and affordability. Results

Study design and sample characteristics

We performed cfRNA sequencing and analysis with the pretrained GeneLLM model on samples from four cancer types and matched non-cancer controls, collected across three centres: Sir Run-Run Shaw Hospital, Beijing Hospital, and Peking University First Hospital (Fig. 1A–E). Figure 1F and Supplementary Data 1 summarise the 496 cases included in the study, with the majority of cancer samples (67.8%) being early-stage disease (stages I and II). Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion before sequencing resulted in low rRNA content within the samples (Fig. 1G). The PCA projections in Fig. 1B–E are unsupervised summaries of batch-corrected, normalised gene-level counts adjusted with the limmaremoveBatchEffect function17; these plots are purely illustrative and do not reflect the source-bias elimination module used for model training. Because 67.8% of cancers are stages I or II, overlap with controls is expected and reflects biological proximity rather than technical artefact.

Model design

GeneLLM uses the sequential data processing capabilities of large language models to improve the accuracy of early cancer detection. We constructed and pretrained a cfRNA-based large language model using unlabelled cfRNA reads to model relationships within these sequences. Given the large volume of cfRNA reads per patient (approximately 40 million), we trained a disease head on patient-level features, with the pretrained backbone kept fixed. This head extracts features from the cfRNA reads. It then generates cancer detection predictions.

GeneLLM’s architecture processes each patient’s extensive cfRNA reads, compares them, and identifies minor anomalies indicating potential disease. The model operates in three stages. First, we pretrained the Transformer architecture using cfRNA reads (Fig. 2A). Since disease labels are not required at this stage, we shuffled each patient’s cfRNA reads, then tokenised them into 7-mer sequences. The pretraining process follows the same methodology as language models18. Second, once GeneLLM is pretrained, it transforms each cfRNA sequence into a vector using the output hidden layer. We then combined cfRNA vectors from training patients only to mine pseudo-biomarkers. These are the most representative cfRNA sequences. Third, we constructed a disease prediction head (Fig. 2B) and conducted disease tuning based on the pseudo-biomarkers while keeping the pretrained encoder frozen. GeneLLM performance evaluation

A comprehensive dataset of 496 cfRNA cases, comprising both cancer patients and non-cancer individuals, was assembled for this study. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets with a 5:1:4 ratio to evaluate GeneLLM’s performance. The split was stratified by disease status within each centre to balance class membership across partitions. All pretraining of GeneLLM and the subsequent pseudo-biomarker mining are performed exclusively on the training set to avoid data contamination. The validation set is used only for hyperparameter tuning in the disease tuning stage for pseudo-biomarker count, multi-scale feature extractor architecture, pivot calibration thresholds, and early stopping. The test set is reserved for the final performance evaluation after all training and tuning are completed. The number of pseudo-biomarkers (n) was treated as a hyperparameter. Candidate values (200, 400, 600, 1000 and 1200) were evaluated on the validation set. We selected n = 1000 based on the highest mean Area Under the Curve (AUC) within the allocated computational resources.

GeneLLM performance with shallow sequencing depth

Standard cfRNA-seq data from plasma samples typically yields 6 GB of data, equivalent to approximately 40 million paired-end reads. We investigated GeneLLM’s performance using cfRNA-seq data with reduced sequencing depth. This simulated shallow sequencing conditions.

We generated the shallow sequencing dataset by re-sequencing the same cfRNA libraries. This involved a lower depth (1 GB per sample versus 6 GB for standard depth) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. In silico subsampling of the standard-depth libraries (20, 40, 60, and 80% of full depth) also assessed performance limits. We used the same hyperparameters and evaluated performance on the held-out test set. This followed the 5:1:4 split previously described. AUCs remained above 0.96 for all cancer types even at 20% depth (Supplementary Fig. S2. This approach replicates realistic shallow-sequencing conditions and prevents biases from artificial read removal.

Despite the substantial reduction in sequencing depth, GeneLLM maintained its predictive accuracy. The results in Fig. 3H, I demonstrate performance comparable to that observed with the higher sequencing depth data.

These findings underscore GeneLLM’s applicability in clinical environments where shallow sequencing is employed due to cost or resource limitations. The model’s robustness at reduced sequencing depth indicates its ability to make accurate predictions even in demanding situations. Furthermore, GeneLLM’s consistent performance with limited sequencing data demonstrates its efficient use of the most informative features within the cfRNA-seq data, which is beneficial when high-depth sequencing is impractical.

Comparison of GeneLLM with existing methods on public datasets

To benchmark GeneLLM’s performance, the method was compared with published results on publicly available cfRNA datasets. These included datasets from Yu et al. 2020 (NCBI BioProject PRJNA552230, comprising 284 plasma cfRNA samples in extracellular vesicles (EVs) with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), 100 patients with chronic pancreatitis, and 117 healthy controls)7, and Chen et al. 2022 (NCBI BioProject PRJNA729258, containing plasma cfRNA samples from 54 colorectal cancer, 37 stomach cancer, 27 liver cancer, 35 lung cancer, 31 oesophageal cancer, and 46 healthy controls)6. For each external cohort, we used the exact training, validation and test partitions released in the original studies and trained GeneLLM de novo on each dataset. Raw fastq data from these studies were preprocessed using our established pipeline, and GeneLLM was retrained on the processed reads. The results are presented in Fig. 3J, K. To isolate the contribution of the learned pseudo-biomarker features from the GeneLLM architecture, we performed an additional experiment. For each external cohort, we generated pseudo-biomarkers using only that dataset’s training split and then trained the same classifier type as used in the original study (support vector machine for Yu et al.7 and random forest for Chen et al.6) on the resulting patient-level features. This approach controls for classifier choice while testing the value of our feature representation. The results are shown in Fig. 3L, M. Using GeneLLM-derived pseudo-biomarkers improved AUC from 0.936 to 0.957 on the Yu et al. PDAC dataset7 and from 0.910 to 0.934 on the Chen et al. pan-cancer dataset6, demonstrating that the features themselves carry substantial predictive information beyond what the original studies captured.

GeneLLM demonstrated improved performance in cancer detection. For pan-cancer detection using the Chen et al. dataset conditions, GeneLLM achieved a median AUC of 0.96 (IQR: 0.94 to 0.97). This compares to 0.91 (IQR: 0.89 to 0.93) reported by Chen et al. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielded Z = 3.09, with p = 0.001 (n = 92); the effect size was r = 0.32. The median AUC difference was 0.05 (95% CI: 0.02–0.08), based on bootstrap estimation (n = 92). For pancreatic cancer detection using the Yu et al. dataset conditions, GeneLLM achieved a median AUC of 0.99 (IQR: 0.98 to 1.00). This compares with 0.95 (IQR: 0.93 to 0.96) reported by Yu et al. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielded Z = 4.63, with p = 3.6 × 10−6 (n = 160). The effect size was r = 0.38. The median AUC difference was 0.04 (95% CI: 0.03–0.06), based on bootstrap estimation (n = 160).

GeneLLM identifies genomic dark matter for cancer detection

The pseudo-biomarkers identified by GeneLLM are not literal biological markers corresponding to annotated genes. They are latent prototype vectors learned during pretraining on approximately 40 million cfRNA reads per patient. These vectors capture high-dimensional sequence patterns that discriminate disease classes. Each pseudo-biomarker links to cfRNA fragments in the latent space. These fragments typically arise from unannotated, intergenic, or repetitive genomic regions, often termed transcriptomic dark matter.

This design is intentional and central to the model’s performance. GeneLLM avoids gene-level annotation constraints. It exploits previously overlooked cfRNA signals from vast, underexplored transcriptome regions. Conventional bioinformatics pipelines filter out unmapped or unannotated reads, rendering such regions invisible. The abstract nature of pseudo-biomarkers reflects the representational power of large language models. Predictive capacity arises from high-dimensional latent embeddings rather than direct sequence interpretation. These model-derived abstractions capture subtle, cancer-type-specific transcriptomic signatures. Annotation-dependent methods do not recover these signatures. This capability contributes significantly to the observed performance gains.

To determine pseudo-biomarker genomic origins, we extracted the pseudo-biomarker sequences. The pseudo-biomarker mining stage produces 1000 pseudo-biomarkers in the full model configuration. This number was determined through validation set tuning to balance classification accuracy and computational efficiency. All pseudo-biomarkers contribute jointly to the disease tuning module during prediction. For interpretability, we present the top three cancer-type-specific pseudo-biomarkers per class. These were selected based on the highest selectivity scores computed on the training split only (Equation (6), Section “Pseudo-biomarker mining”). These three are illustrative and do not represent the full set used during training and inference. The full set of 1000 pseudo-biomarker sequences is available in the Supplementary Data 2.

To quantify the contribution of pseudo-biomarkers from non-gene regions, all 1000 pseudo-biomarkers were classified by their top Nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn) alignments (Supplementary Data 2). Most sequences were not encoded by a gene. Performance was then evaluated using only the gene-aligned set on the held-out test set. The non-gene set yielded higher AUCs across tasks. For pan-cancer, the non-gene set achieved 0.889 versus 0.792 for the gene set (Supplementary Fig. S1). This indicates that GeneLLM leverages information from unannotated genomic regions, termed genomic dark matter, for cancer identification.

This capability distinguishes GeneLLM from traditional gene expression-based methods. Those methods are limited by their reliance on existing annotations. Further investigation into these unannotated regions may yield insights into cancer development, progression, and biomarker discovery19–21.

To assess pseudo-biomarker specificity, we examined the scores assigned by GeneLLM to the top three pseudo-biomarkers for each cancer type across all samples (Fig. 4). The method for calculating these scores is illustrated in Section “Pseudo-biomarker mining”. We observed a clear association between specific pseudo-biomarkers and their corresponding cancer types. For example, pseudo-biomarkers identified for stomach cancer exhibited high scores in stomach cancer samples but significantly lower scores in other samples. This pattern was consistently observed across all cancer types. Comparison with existing methods

Finally, we benchmarked GeneLLM’s performance against established machine learning methods. These included support vector machine, random forest, XGBoost, logistic regression, elastic net, and a plain neural network. Software versions used were scikit-learn 1.5.0 for support vector machine, random forest, logistic regression, elastic net, and the plain neural network (neural_network.MLPClassifier). XGBoost was implemented with xgboost 2.1.0.

GeneLLM consistently outperformed all other methods across various performance metrics. The baseline methods used normalised expression matrices derived from the same cfRNA sequencing data. On the held-out test set, the AUC gap ranged from about 6 to 15 percentage points across pan-cancer and the four disease-specific tasks. The gap persisted on the most challenging task, liver cancer, where GeneLLM reached a test AUC of 0.924. This enhanced performance, combined with its ability to leverage information from unannotated genomic regions, positions GeneLLM as an effective tool for cancer detection and biomarker discovery.

Experiments also used random forest classifiers on aggregated pseudo-biomarker features. These features correspond to frozen-backbone representations. A random forest classifier trained on these features achieved AUCs of 0.928 for colorectal cancer and 0.920 for liver cancer. It also reached 0.946 for lung cancer, 0.932 for stomach cancer, and 0.936 for pan-cancer. PCA-based baselines were also trained using sklearn.decomposition.PCA (version 1.5.0). These pipelines used the top 50 principal components of the latent feature matrix as inputs (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). The resulting components served as features for GeneLLM’s downstream disease tuning module (Table 1 and Fig. S4). All benchmarks used the same train, validation, and test partitions. These models consistently underperformed across all cancer types and pan-cancer settings. This was compared to our proposed disease tuning module (Table 1 and Fig. S4). GeneLLM achieved AUCs on the held-out test set of 0.985 for colorectal and 0.924 for liver cancer. It reached 0.98 for lung, 0.98 for stomach, and 0.985 for pan-cancer. This represents an AUC gain for GeneLLM. The gain ranged from 0.4 percentage points for liver cancer to 5.7 percentage points for colorectal cancer. This performance gap arises because standard classifiers lack architectural capacity. They cannot capture hierarchical dependencies, mitigate source-specific biases, or calibrate multi-disease decision thresholds. By contrast, our disease tuning module incorporates pseudo-biomarker summarisation, source bias elimination, multi-scale feature extraction, and calibrated multi-head classification. Discussion

Applying GeneLLM to cancer detection advances the use of information encoded within cfRNA. The model achieved high performance, with AUC values exceeding 0.9 across multiple cancer types. It also demonstrated consistent accuracy in classifying individual cancers. This indicates a reliable approach for early detection. This performance arises from GeneLLM’s ability to capture subtle patterns within cfRNA data. Traditional methods, relying on DE analysis of known genes, often overlook these patterns. Similar to language model training18, the pretraining strategy allows GeneLLM to learn intrinsic relationships between cfRNA sequences. This discerns minute, disease-indicative variations. This ability to learn contextual information directly from raw sequencing data distinguishes GeneLLM from annotation-dependent methods.

GeneLLM maintained high performance even with significantly reduced sequencing depth (one-sixth of standard depth). This robustness reduces costs and computational burden. It also suggests efficient identification and utilisation of the most informative cfRNA features. This efficiency stems from the model’s focus on short, indicative sequences, termed pseudo-biomarkers. This contrasts with reliance on entire gene expression levels. This targeted approach is advantageous when cfRNA is fragmented or at low concentrations. These are common challenges in liquid biopsy diagnostics. Further investigation into pseudo-biomarker characteristics, such as length distribution, sequence composition, and genomic origin, will provide insights into cancer biology and inform the development of more targeted diagnostic tools. These pseudo-biomarkers represent fragments of larger transcripts from unannotated regions. Alternatively, they may arise from previously uncharacterised, cancer-specific transcriptional events.

Many pseudo-biomarkers originate from unannotated genomic regions, referred to as ‘dark matter’. This discovery has significant implications. GeneLLM accesses a previously unexplored source of diagnostically relevant biological information. These unannotated regions show high conservation across samples, with high coverage and identity in BLASTn searches. Their lack of association with known genes raises questions about their function and role in cancer development. Understanding these unannotated regions could reveal additional avenues for cancer biology and may also lead to alternative therapeutic strategies. GeneLLM’s ability to identify these sequences demonstrates the potential of AI-driven approaches. Such approaches can uncover hidden biological signals and advance scientific understanding.

The identified pseudo-biomarkers show specificity, demonstrating distinct scoring patterns across different cancer types. This strengthens their diagnostic potential. This specificity indicates that different cancers may exhibit unique cfRNA signatures from these unannotated regions. These signatures could reflect distinct molecular mechanisms driving cancer development and progression. This observation suggests the development of highly specific diagnostic tests tailored to individual cancer types. Such tests could enable earlier and more accurate diagnosis22,23.

Applying standard machine learning classifiers, such as support vector machines or random forests, to the learned patient-level features is theoretically possible. However, these approaches are impractical for our latent representations. Each patient in our dataset has 40 million cfRNA sequences. These are first transformed into high-dimensional, context-dependent embeddings during pretraining. These embeddings vary in length and exhibit token-level dependencies. They encode multi-scale contextual information. This information is not preserved when aggregated into fixed-length vectors. Training a supervised classifier directly on raw reads is also impractical. A single epoch would require streaming terabytes of sequence per pass. This would also involve maintaining gradients over billions of tokens. Such requirements exceed the feasible memory and compute capacity of our hardware.

Single-cell foundation models operate on per-cell gene expression matrices. Our cfRNA pipeline consumes raw sequencing reads. These approaches are not directly comparable. Applying single-cell models requires aggregation to bulk gene counts. This process discards non-gene and repeat-derived signals used by our approach. It also introduces a modality shift from intracellular single-cell to plasma cfRNA. Ranking-based quantification discards continuous signal intensity, whereas GeneLLM’s disease tuning exploits quantitative magnitude after source-bias elimination. We did not benchmark these models here. This avoided confounding by input format and pre-processing. The current scope focuses on baselines sharing the same cfRNA data modality and published cfRNA studies. Future work will explore fair interfaces when foundation models expose bulk-compatible inputs.

GeneLLM outperforms traditional machine learning methods trained on annotated gene expression data. It extracts and utilises more informative features from cfRNA. Information from unannotated regions, combined with GeneLLM’s pattern recognition, provides a deeper understanding of disease states.

Public dataset comparisons further validate GeneLLM’s performance against existing methods. GeneLLM achieved improved AUC values for pan-cancer and pancreatic cancer detection. These results surpass previously published findings6,7. This demonstrates an enhanced ability to detect cancer using existing cfRNA data. This improved performance, alongside reduced sequencing depth requirements, positions GeneLLM as a cost-effective and accurate approach for early cancer detection. Consistent performance across multiple datasets suggests that the model identifies informative cfRNA patterns reliably. This indicates its generalisability and potential clinical applicability.

Cross-species and cross-disease stage comparisons have limitations. Further validation requires larger, more diverse cohorts. These should include different cancer stages and diverse ethnic backgrounds. This will fully assess clinical utility and ensure equitable performance across populations.

While large-scale DNA language models, such as DNABERT24 and Evo 216 have demonstrated strong performance in genomic feature prediction, their design goals, training data, and input characteristics differ fundamentally from those of GeneLLM. Evo 216 is pretrained on whole-genome sequences from diverse species, paired with organism-level context, to predict functional annotations, conservation scores, and evolutionary constraints at the nucleotide level. DNABERT24, similarly, learns k-mer-based embeddings from genomic DNA to capture local sequence patterns.

In contrast, GeneLLM is trained directly on raw cfRNA sequencing reads, which are short, fragmented sequences with variable coverage that reflect the transcribed and expressed portion of the genome, including both annotated and unannotated regions. These reads inherently encode expression-level information, RNA degradation patterns, and sample-specific signals that are absent from static genomic DNA. Our pretraining corpus comprises approximately 20 trillion cfRNA reads, enabling the model to learn fine-grained, transcriptome-specific representations that are critical for detecting subtle, disease-associated patterns.

Because of these differences, direct initialisation from DNA-based language models is not optimal: the mismatch in input modality (DNA vs. cfRNA), sequence structure (long contiguous vs. short fragmented), and biological signal type (static genomic content vs. dynamic expression) would limit transferability. Nevertheless, hybrid strategies, for example initialising the nucleotide embedding layer from a DNA language model and then continuing pretraining on cfRNA reads, may be worth exploring in future work, provided appropriate domain adaptation techniques are applied to mitigate the distribution shift. We did not initialise GeneLLM from DNABERT24 or Evo 216 and did not use these DNA models as baselines, because they do not operate on raw cfRNA reads without converting them into genomic windows or heavy aggregation that removes expression and sample context.

The implications extend beyond cancer diagnostics. GeneLLM analyses raw sequencing data and identifies informative sequences without existing annotations. This opens possibilities for biomarker discovery in other diseases and biological contexts. The framework adapts to other sequencing data types. These include intracellular bulk RNA-seq or single-cell RNA-seq. It can identify biomarkers or therapeutic targets. For example, applying GeneLLM to single-cell RNA-seq data could reveal cell-type-specific pseudo-biomarkers associated with disease progression or treatment response. This technology has broad applications. It can advance understanding of complex biological processes. It also accelerates the development of personalised medicine.

While GeneLLM demonstrates considerable potential, potential limitations and future research directions warrant attention. Biological characterisation of the identified pseudo-biomarkers is crucial. Determining their origin and function is essential for understanding the mechanisms driving their association with cancer. This could involve experimental validation using techniques such as quantitative PCR, in situ hybridisation, or functional assays. Investigating the roles of these sequences in cancer development and progression could lead to the identification of therapeutic targets. The potential impact of technical artefacts or biases in the sequencing data on GeneLLM’s performance requires careful evaluation of the model’s robustness to variations in sequencing platforms, library preparation methods, and data processing pipelines to ensure reliable and reproducible results. Finally, integrating GeneLLM with other data modalities (clinical, imaging, or proteomic data) could further enhance diagnostic accuracy and provide a more holistic view of the disease state. Addressing these limitations and pursuing these future research directions can unlock GeneLLM’s full potential and pave the way for advances in precision medicine. We will expand external validation to further cfRNA cohorts as raw read data become available and will preregister the evaluation protocol to ensure comparability across studies. Looking ahead, we plan to aggregate multiple high-scoring pseudo-biomarkers per cluster to derive consensus strings and then apply standard enrichment tests to assess whether any emergent sequence patterns show association with disease selectivity scores (Section “Pseudo-biomarker mining”). We outline this plan here to define a clear path for motif-level investigation in future work. De novo retraining on the Chen et al. dataset6, including independent pre-training and pseudo-biomarker mining from its training split alone, confirms the generalisability of these phases beyond the primary cohort’s site effects.

A further limitation is cross-cohort transfer at the raw-read level. Low-biomass plasma libraries are highly sensitive to centre- and protocol-specific effects, including rRNA depletion chemistry, fragmentation profiles, background contamination and the handling of exogenous reads, which together induce substantial distribution shifts that can negate naive weight transfer25–27. Our ablation on the Chen et al.6 cohort reflects this: initialising from our pre-trained backbone did not surpass de novo training on that dataset. Looking ahead, we plan to pre-train on more heterogeneous cfRNA corpora and to incorporate domain-adaptation objectives (for example, adversarial alignment of pseudo-biomarker summaries) to encourage domain-invariant representations28.

Methods

Ethics approval

The Ethics Committee and the Institutional Review Board of Peking University First Hospital under approval 2022-132, Beijing Hospital under approval 2022BJYYEC-420-02, and Sir Run-Run Shaw Hospital under approval 2023-0300 approved this study. We conducted all procedures in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. All participants provided written informed consent to participate and for the publication of their anonymised data after being fully informed of the study aims and risks. All cases were de-identified before analysis, and no personally identifiable information was accessible to the research team. Data were handled in accordance with institutional data governance policies, and only anonymised sequencing data were used for model development and evaluation.

Cohort design

The cohort for this study comprised 496 de-identified plasma samples, including 102 colorectal cancer and 102 stomach cancer samples from Peking University First Hospital, 81 liver cancer samples from Sir Run-Run Shaw Hospital, 47 lung cancer samples from Beijing Hospital, and 164 non-cancer controls. Supplementary Data 1 provides the clinical characteristics of these participants in aggregate. The non-cancer controls were obtained from all three centres to calibrate potential batch effects. No formal prospective sample size calculation was performed. The 496 cases correspond to all eligible and available de-identified samples provided by the three collaborating hospitals during the data collection period. The cohort size was determined by data availability. This sample is sufficient for the analyses performed because the dataset provides broad representation across clinical categories to support model development and validation. For all datasets used in this study, including our primary multi-centre cohort and the external datasets from Chen et al.6 and Yu et al.7, the training, validation and test sets were defined exactly as in the original publications for the external datasets, and by a 5:1:4 ratio for our primary dataset. De novo training on external datasets validates pre-training and pseudo-biomarker generation phases independently of the primary cohort. The primary dataset split was stratified by disease status within each centre. Participant sex was determined by self-reporting during recruitment. We did not perform a sex or gender analysis because the study focused on identifying cancer-specific signatures across the entire clinical cohort. Recruitment was conducted randomly and continuously without selection based on sex or gender. Given that cancer types were not uniformly distributed across centres, we explicitly modelled source effects in the disease tuning stage using the source-bias elimination module (Section “Disease tuning”, Eq. (10)).

In the pretraining and pseudo-biomarker mining phase, only the training set was used to learn latent representations from raw cfRNA reads; no information from the validation or testing sets was accessed. In the disease tuning phase, the training set was used for parameter estimation, while the validation set was used exclusively for hyperparameter selection, including the number of pseudo-biomarkers, multi-scale feature extractor architecture, pivot calibration thresholds, and early stopping criteria. The testing set was held out entirely and used only for final performance evaluation. No technical replicates were performed; each profile represents one biological replicate from an independent patient. Unless otherwise specified, all reported metrics in this study are from the held-out testing set.

Sample collection

Peripheral whole blood samples were taken from subjects before treatment using EDTA-coated tubes. The tubes were inverted between eight and ten times to ensure the mixing of the blood with the anticoagulant. Plasma separation was performed following a standard clinical protocol for blood centrifugation within two hours of blood collection. After separation, the plasma samples were divided into aliquots and preserved at − 80 °C until the extraction of cfRNA. All plasma aliquots were destroyed following cfRNA extraction and library preparation in accordance with institutional biobanking protocols.

CfRNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Total cfRNA was isolated from 200 μL of plasma utilising the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit (Qiagen), which employs Qiagen’s unique resin as the separation matrix. This kit is capable of extracting all RNA molecules from approximately 18 nt upwards. DNase I (NEB) was used to eliminate remaining DNA.

To assess potential contamination in the RNA extraction and library preparation processes, two types of negative controls were utilised. These included libraries generated from RNA/DNA-free water (Invitrogen) and solutions eluted from the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit with RNA/DNA-free water as input. A non-reverse transcriptase control was implemented to assess DNA contamination. The positive control comprised human brain RNA provided with the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit, and was processed using the same protocol as that for the cfRNA library preparation.

The comprehensive cfRNA library (>50 nt) was constructed using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2-Pico Input Mammalian (Takara), adhering to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. This kit employs a CRISPR/DASH technique to eliminate ribosomal cDNA. Each library was sequenced to a depth of approximately 40 million reads on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform using a PE150 strategy.

Data quality control and preprocessing

To ensure high-quality data, we employed strict quality control processes. First, all raw data were trimmed using fastp v0.23.329 with default parameters to automatically trim low-quality reads and adaptors. The trimmed reads were then mapped to the human genome (GENCODE human release 4330) using STAR v2.7.10b31 with the encyclopaedia of DNA elements (ENCODE) standard options32,33. The mapped reads were used as input for GeneLLM. Unless otherwise stated, we retained all reads mapping to the human genome irrespective of alignment uniqueness or mapping quality to preserve signals from repetitive and unannotated regions. We discarded unmapped reads to limit exogenous contamination. Fastp default polyX and polyG trimming mitigated instrument-specific low-complexity artefacts. Mapping statistics were then retrieved using the flagstat function in SAMtools v1.1734.

Notation used in this paper

Our model is composed of a core LLM and a set of task-specific modules organised in a sequential pipeline. The pipeline consists of two major stages: pretraining and post-training disease tuning. In the pretraining stage, the LLM is first trained on cfRNA sequences using a standard autoregressive language modelling objective. This is followed by a pseudo-biomarker mining step, which aligns the learned representations with a set of latent prototype vectors to enable downstream interpretability and compact feature summarisation. In the post-training disease tuning stage, the pretrained LLM remains frozen, and we apply several modules: a disease-specific feature summarisation module based on pseudo-biomarker matching, a source bias elimination mechanism to align distributions across different data sources, a multi-scale feature extractor that enriches representation capacity, and a calibrated multi-head disease classifier for robust multi-class prediction. We will introduce each step in detail in this section. The complete algorithmic pipeline is summarised in Box 1.

Inputs and data sources

The input for GeneLLM is a sequence of tokens derived from the cfRNA sequencing reads of both cancer patients and individuals without cancer. Each patient contributes approximately 40 million cfRNA reads. During the pretraining phase, GeneLLM focuses on understanding the internal relationships within the cfRNA reads and storing pertinent information without using cancer labels. These cfRNA reads are then converted into 7-mers, each considered a token. To avoid annotation bias, no alignment coordinates, mapping metadata, or gene labels are provided to the model; it consumes only the nucleotide sequences of human-mapped reads. This design preserves repeat-derived and multi-mapping content without inducing locus-specific artefacts.

For batch processing, the lengths of different ID sequences are standardised by padding, inserting [PAD] tokens to ensure all token sequences in a batch are of equal length. Consistent with Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, specific tokens [SOS] and [EOS] are employed to mark the start and end of each sequence, respectively.

Pretraining

During the pretraining phase described in Fig. 2A, each cfRNA sequence is treated as an independent sentence, with 7-mer sequences acting as tokens. The core model architecture is a deep Transformer decoder network. To distinguish between input and target sequences during training, a special [SOS] (start of sequence) token is prepended to the input tokens, and an [EOS] (end of sequence) token is appended to the target tokens. The model employs a standard language modelling objective, learning to predict the next token in the target sequence based on the input sequence and preceding target tokens. In total, pretraining processed approximately 20 trillion cfRNA reads from the training split and ran for about 15 days on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Detailed hardware and scheduling are reported in the Training regimen. All parameters were randomly initialised; no weights from DNA language models were used in this study. Pretraining on unlabelled cfRNA sequencing data enables the model to learn meaningful representations that can be fine-tuned for downstream tasks.

The model is composed of six Transformer blocks stacked sequentially. Each block comprises a self-attention sublayer and a feed-forward sublayer, adhering to the original Transformer architecture proposed by Vaswani et al. (2017)35. A triangular masking pattern is applied to the self-attention matrices within each block. This masking enforces the autoregressive property of the decoder, ensuring that predictions for a given target token depend only on previous tokens in the sequence and the input tokens, never on future tokens.

Pseudo-biomarker scoring

Standard ML classifiers require fixed-length feature vectors and do not directly operate on the large, variable-length embedding sets produced by GeneLLM pretraining. Given the scale of our data (40 million cfRNA reads per patient), we found that directly aggregating embeddings into simple statistical summaries for use with standard classifiers led to substantial loss of diagnostic information. This motivated the design of the disease tuning module, which is tailored to the structure of the pseudo-biomarker space and integrates bias correction, multi-scale feature extraction, and calibrated decision-making to achieve optimal performance.

Multi-scale feature extractor

The feature vector, derived from the vast amount of cfRNA reads, exhibits a rich set of multi-scale features that capture intricate patterns and variations at different levels of granularity. These multi-scale features are crucial for understanding the complex nature of the cfRNA sequences and their potential role in disease detection.

Inspired by the Dense Convolutional Network architecture proposed by ref. 37, we propose to extract multi-scale features by employing stacked skip-connection layers. As illustrated in Fig. 2B, we utilise multiple Multi-scale Feature Extractor modules with activation functions, such as ReLu( ⋅ )38 and the Tanh( ⋅ ) to extract multi-scale features. Within each module, we iteratively apply a feedforward layer to the input feature vector and concatenate the output to the input, effectively preserving information from previous layers and enabling the network to learn multi-scale representations.

The proposed architecture leverages the power of skip connections, which allow the network to propagate information from earlier layers to later layers, mitigating the vanishing gradient problem and facilitating the learning of multi-scale features. By iteratively applying feedforward layers and concatenating the outputs, the network can capture a wide range of patterns and variations present in the cfRNA sequences at different scales. This approach enables the model to effectively learn and represent the complex characteristics of the cfRNA reads, which is important for accurate disease detection.

By incorporating multiple Multi-scale Feature Extractor modules, the proposed architecture can extract a comprehensive set of multi-scale features that encapsulate the rich information present in the cfRNA sequences. These features serve as a robust foundation for subsequent analysis and disease detection tasks, enabling the development of more accurate and reliable diagnostic tools based on cfRNA sequencing data.

Training regimen

To train the GeneLLM, cfRNA reads were preprocessed as described in Section “Inputs and data sources”.

We trained the GeneLLM on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, utilising their high-performance computing capabilities. To optimise memory usage and training efficiency, we used a batch size of 1 per GPU. The model was pre-trained using the entire training set of cfRNA sequences, allowing it to learn the underlying patterns and characteristics of the data. The pre-training process was computationally intensive and took approximately 15 days to complete.

After pre-training, we utilised only the training split to extract pseudo-biomarkers; the validation and test splits were not accessed at this stage.

To further refine the GeneLLM for disease prediction, we performed disease tuning using labelled patient cfRNA sequential data. Each patient in the dataset has a massive amount of cfRNA sequences associated with their condition. By leveraging this labelled data, we trained the downstream disease head and calibration modules while keeping the pretrained backbone fixed to capture disease-specific patterns and characteristics. The disease tuning process adapts the model to better discriminate between different disease states and improves its performance in downstream tasks, such as disease prediction.

The combination of pre-training on a large cfRNA dataset and subsequent disease tuning using labelled patient data enables the GeneLLM to learn both general and disease-specific representations of cfRNA sequences. This approach harnesses the power of transfer learning, where the knowledge gained from pre-training on a diverse dataset is transferred and specialised for specific disease applications through fine-tuning. The resulting model is expected to capture relevant biomarkers and patterns associated with various diseases, potentially enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of disease diagnosis and monitoring using cfRNA data.

Inference regimen

During the inference process, we sequentially input the cfRNA sequences into the GeneLLM, followed by the disease judgement head. The GeneLLM encodes each cfRNA sequence, capturing its relevant features and representations. The encoding time varies depending on the length of the cfRNA sequences, as longer sequences require more computational processing.

On average, the GeneLLM takes approximately 2.16 × 10−5 s to encode a single cfRNA sequence. However, it is important to note that each patient typically has a massive amount of cfRNA sequences associated with their sample. Therefore, the total encoding time for a single patient is around 14.4 minutes, considering the processing of all their cfRNA sequences.

After encoding the cfRNA sequences, the disease judgement head comes into play. This component of the model is responsible for making predictions or classifications regarding the presence or absence of specific diseases based on the encoded representations. To enhance the robustness and reliability of the disease judgement, we employ an ensemble approach. We use ten ensemble models, each trained on different subsets or variations of the data, to make predictions. The ensemble approach helps to mitigate potential biases or variances in individual models and improves the overall accuracy of the disease judgement.

The inference time for the disease judgement head, using the ten ensemble models, takes approximately 5.6 minutes on average for one patient. This inference time includes the processing of the encoded cfRNA sequences by each ensemble model and the aggregation of their predictions to reach a final judgement.

Compared to previous methods for cancer screening, the combination of the GeneLLM and the disease judgement head offers a rapid and efficient approach. Traditional cancer screening methods often involve invasive procedures, extensive laboratory tests, and manual analysis, which can be time-consuming and resource-intensive. In contrast, our proposed method leverages the power of machine learning and natural language processing techniques to analyse cfRNA sequences rapidly and accurately.

The reported times reflect the hardware and computational resources used in our experiments. Runtimes will vary with infrastructure and cohort size. Nevertheless, the efficiency and speed of our method highlight its potential for widespread adoption in clinical settings, enabling large-scale cancer screening programmes and improving patient care.

Metrics

To evaluate the architecture’s performance in detecting diseases, we calculated the confusion matrix and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC). The confusion matrix provides a tabular summary of the model’s classification results, displaying the counts of true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) predictions. These counts offer insights into the model’s ability to correctly identify positive and negative samples and the types of errors it makes. The AUC-ROC value represents the probability that a randomly selected positive sample will have a higher score assigned by the model than a randomly selected negative sample. A larger AUC value, ranging from 0 to 1, suggests better model performance in distinguishing between positive and negative samples.

Furthermore, we plotted the ROC curve to visually assess the model’s performance. The ROC curve illustrates the trade-off between the true positive rate (TPR) and the false positive rate (FPR) at various classification threshold values. An ideal ROC curve would hug the top-left corner of the plot, indicating high TPR and low FPR across a wide range of thresholds. The closer the ROC curve is to the top-left corner, the better the model’s performance. The AUC value summarises the overall performance of the model across all possible thresholds, providing a single scalar value for comparison. By evaluating the architecture’s performance using these metrics and visualisations, we gain a comprehensive understanding of its effectiveness in detecting diseases.

The algorithm takes raw cfRNA reads as input and first encodes each sequence using GeneLLM to obtain vector representations that capture underlying biological semantics. Based on these representations, a pseudo-biomarker mining module-formulated similarly to a mixture model—is introduced to iteratively summarise massive reads into a limited set of pseudo-biomarker prototypes, effectively transforming high-dimensional and noisy data into a structured feature space. At the patient level, responses to these pseudo-biomarkers are aggregated to form individual feature vectors. To mitigate distribution shifts introduced by different sequencing sources, a source-wise mean alignment strategy is applied for bias correction. The aligned features are then processed by a DenseNet to extract multi-scale representations, enhancing expressive capacity. Finally, a multi-head classification framework models different disease types independently, followed by a calibration step to adjust prediction confidence. If the maximum calibrated probability falls below a predefined threshold, the sample is classified as non-cancer; otherwise, the most probable disease type is returned, enabling robust and interpretable disease detection.

Ablations for gene versus non-gene reads

To quantify the predictive contribution of non-gene reads, we classified pseudo-biomarker sequences by their top BLASTn hit against the NT database (updated 23rd June 2024)39, separating those aligning to annotated gene regions from those outside. We evaluated three training regimes on the same stratified train, validation and test partitions: genes-only, non-gene-only and all-reads. Performance was summarised as AUC and ΔAUC relative to the all-reads model, with percentile bootstrap 95 per cent confidence intervals over patients.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of AUC values between GeneLLM and previously published methods (Chen et al.6 and Yu et al.7) on their respective datasets were performed using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. This non-parametric test was chosen as the normality of the differences in AUCs could not be assumed, and it is suitable for paired observations (e.g., comparing AUCs across multiple specific cancer subtypes or cross-validation folds from the same dataset). Measurements were taken from distinct analytical results (AUCs derived per specific subgroup/condition being compared).

For these null hypothesis tests, the test statistic, the exact p-value, and the number of pairs (n) are reported. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. No corrections for multiple comparisons were applied for these specific benchmark comparisons as they were considered distinct hypothesis tests against prior art.

Descriptive statistics for the AUC values being compared include the median and interquartile range (IQR). Effect sizes for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were calculated as Rosenthal’s r (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=Z/\sqrt{N}$$\end{document}r=Z/N, where Z is the Z-statistic derived from the test and N is the total number of observations, i.e., twice the number of pairs). An r value of 0.1 is considered a small effect, 0.3 a medium effect, and 0.5 a large effect.
