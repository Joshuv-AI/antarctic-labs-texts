Developmental Milestones for Children With Down Syndrome: Revised Estimates Using Moving Average Summaries
Authors: Rahaf Tarawneh, Katherine Pawlowski, Nicole Baumer, Bo Zhang, Maitreyi Mazumdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveChildren with Down syndrome follow distinct developmental trajectories that require specialized monitoring and counseling. We aimed to provide updated estimates of developmental milestone attainment using a nonparametric approach and to compare these results with previously reported generalized linear mixed‐effects model (GLMM) estimates.MethodsWe reanalyzed developmental data from 842 children with Down syndrome (ages 2 months to 24 years). For each milestone, achievement rates were calculated within overlapping 0.5‐year time windows, and shape‐constrained additive models were used to derive monotonic regression curves.ResultsCompared with GLMM‐based estimates, the nonparametric approach predicted milestone attainment at least 1 year earlier for 17 of 25 milestones at the highest comparable percentile. Early gross motor milestones showed consistent achievement patterns across methods, whereas more complex adaptive, language, and academic skills demonstrated greater variability and were more sensitive to modeling assumptions. Females achieved 17 of 25 milestones at least 1 year earlier than males.ConclusionsThese findings highlight substantial developmental heterogeneity and support the potential for continued skill acquisition throughout childhood and adolescence in individuals with Down syndrome. The updated estimates provide clinically relevant reference points to guide individualized monitoring, anticipatory guidance, and family counseling.

Introduction

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that children and adolescents with Down syndrome receive anticipatory guidance regarding development and behavior, as well as screening for co‐occurring neurodevelopmental conditions [1]. Because individuals with Down syndrome have different patterns of development than children without Down syndrome, developmental monitoring tools based on the general population have limited utility for children and adolescents with Down syndrome. We recently published developmental milestones based on a large hospital‐based sample of children and young adults with Down syndrome, using generalized linear mixed‐effects models (GLMMs) that were similar to those used to create the Denver Developmental Screening Test [2, 3]. Our report presented 15%, 30%, 45%, 60%, 75%, and 90% probabilities of having achieved certain developmental milestones by a specific age. For some milestones, the age range for predicted attainment was wide, especially when the milestone was attained at older ages.

We received many responses to our report, including questions about whether there might be alternatives to using GLMMs [4], and questions about the performance of GLMMs for milestones attained during adolescence. GLMMs impose strong parametric assumptions, including a linear increase in the log‐odds with age, which in turn restricts the probability of attaining developmental milestones to an S‐shaped trajectory. In contrast, nonparametric models offer greater flexibility and therefore may be more appropriate for capturing complex or irregular developmental patterns. Recent population‐based studies of milestone attainment have utilized a nonparametric regression method based on moving average summary statistics, providing an alternative that addresses some of the limitations of GLMMs [5].

Methods

Study Population and Questionnaires

Children and adolescents with Down syndrome (n= 842) ages 2 months to 24 years visited the Boston Children's Hospital Down Syndrome Program between 2018 and 2023. As part of their clinical care, caregivers completed standardized forms regarding the development of children that included information about language, motor skills, social communication, adaptive tasks, and behavioral functioning, as reported in Baumer et al. [2]. In addition, caregivers of children over age 3 years completed the Neurodevelopmental Parent Report for Outcome Monitoring (ND‐PROM), a standardized developmental monitoring questionnaire [6]. During clinical visits with children under age 3 years, physical therapists completed a Physical Therapy Clinical Form that recorded information obtained by caregiver report or direct observation by the physical therapist. Some developmental milestones were asked about on more than one questionnaire (e.g., “Engages in pretend play” appears on both the Developmental Intake Form and the ND‐PROM); in these situations, responses were combined into one variable. Questions about expressive communication were phrased using each child's preferred communication strategy (e.g., spoken language, sign language, assistive communication device). Participants were included if they had a clinical diagnosis of Down syndrome and at least one completed developmental assessment during a program visit; no additional exclusion criteria were applied. Developmental regression, including Down syndrome regression disorder, was not systematically screened during the study period. Participants with mosaic Down syndrome were included in the cohort, with a prevalence of less than 2%. This study was approved by the Boston Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board.

Statistical Analysis

For this analysis, we modified a nonparametric regression approach from the one reported by Sudry and colleagues [5] as follows: we created a series of overlapping 0.5‐year time windows from 0.35 to 24.55 years: 0.35 ± 0.25, 0.40 ± 0.25, ⋯, 24.55 ± 0.25. For each developmental milestone, we used only the first evaluation for each child within a time window. We then grouped children by their age on the date of the documentation of attainment of the milestone and calculated the attainment rate for the visits within each time window. The attainment rate within each time window was calculated by dividing the number of children who had attained the milestone by the total number of children with a clinical visit within each time window, resulting in a series of attainment rates in each time window. As a result, a series of success rates were obtained at 0.35, 0.40, 0.45 years of age, and so on. To avoid generating estimates in regions where the data were sparse, time windows in which five children or fewer contributed data were not included in the model. We then fit the success rates to a shape constrained additive model [7] (the maximum basis dimension was set to k=50) to derive monotonic smoothing regression curves of success rates on age and calculated the ages at which the success rate surpassed 15%, 30%, 45%, 60%, and 75% for the first time.

The moving‐average summaries were used as an intermediate step to obtain stable, age‐specific estimates of milestone attainment rates from irregularly spaced clinical visit data. Because milestone status was assessed at discrete clinical encounters with uneven age coverage, calculating attainment rates within overlapping age windows allowed us to aggregate information locally across nearby ages, reduce the influence of sparse or noisy observations, and produce empirical success‐rate estimates that could then be modeled as a function of age. These nonparametric estimates are intended to complement, rather than replace, the previously published GLMM‐based milestone norms, and illustrate how alternative modeling assumptions applied to the same cohort can influence age‐at‐attainment estimates without altering the underlying developmental patterns.

To compare results from this nonparametric approach with those from the previously published GLMM method [2], we identified the highest percentile for which an age was reported in both models for each milestone and compared the predicted ages at that percentile. Nonparametric monotonic regression was preferred for this analysis because developmental milestone achievement naturally progresses over time in a nondecreasing manner. In other words, once a child reaches a developmental milestone, the model assumes that they remain at or above that level, and that the proportion of children achieving a milestone only increases as age increases.

Early gross motor milestones, including sitting, crawling, and standing, showed the earliest and most consistent achievement, with the 75th percentile attained between approximately 1.06 and 2.4 years. In contrast, more complex adaptive skills, such as drinking from an open cup and independent dressing, and academic abilities, including reading, writing, and arithmetic, demonstrated wider age ranges and greater variability.

Sex‐specific analyses demonstrated broadly similar developmental patterns across domains. Females achieved 17 of the 25 milestones at least 1 year earlier than males at the highest comparable percentile. The magnitude of these differences varied by domain and percentile. Females achieved reading 10 words and brushing teeth substantially earlier than males, with differences of 8.28 and 6.96 years, respectively, at the 60% probability level. Overall, the direction and pattern of sex differences in estimated ages were consistent across modeling approaches.

Compared with the previously reported GLMM‐based estimates, the nonparametric approach yielded earlier predicted ages for 17 of the 25 milestones, defined as ≥ 1‐year differences at the highest comparable percentile. At these percentiles, the magnitude of earlier attainment ranged from approximately 1.7–5.3 years. Milestones such as walking around the home, writing one's name, reading 10 words, using utensils to feed, brushing teeth, using two‐word phrases, and following one‐ and two‐step directions showed reductions of approximately 2 years, ranging from 1.7 to 2.3 years, in the predicted age of attainment. Larger differences, averaging approximately 4 years and ranging from 2.94 to 5.3 years, were observed for running, walking independently in the community, basic arithmetic skills, expressive vocabulary milestones (≥ 11, ≥ 21, and ≥ 50 words), three‐word phrases, and conversational ability.

For some milestones, particularly those achieved at younger ages, the nonparametric method produced earlier estimates at the lower percentiles. The 15th percentile ages were 0.69 years for use of at least one word, sign, or picture, 0.71 years for sitting unsupported for at least 10 min, and 0.81 years for scooting, creeping, or crawling across the floor. These findings likely reflect the influence of local data density and heterogeneity in caregiver‐reported early skill recognition. We were unable to estimate higher percentiles of 90% for many later‐developing milestones because of data sparsity at older ages.

Discussion

Children with Down syndrome learn, grow, and achieve on their own trajectory. Developmental milestones can offer clinicians and families an understanding of common developmental patterns, allowing insights about progress, strengths, and areas for growth and enabling better detection of needs and opportunities for targeted and tailored supports for all individuals. Our motivation in reporting the findings for our experience in our setting, and our motivation in this reanalysis, is to provide a population‐based description of the variability of ages at which skills are attained to help pediatricians and other providers prioritize competing concerns and develop tailored recommendations for children in their care.

In our reanalysis using nonparametric methods, we found very similar findings to what we previously reported using GLMMs [2]. For some milestones, however, applying a nonparametric method yielded earlier ages for predicted attainment of milestones, most notably for early language milestones. In many instances, we did not have enough data to reliably estimate age of attainment for the 75th and 90th percentiles. The difference between the two statistical methods arises from the strong assumptions of S‐shaped developmental trajectories imposed by the GLMMs, which are not required by the nonparametric regression approach [7]. We continue to see a wide range of functioning across developmental domains with substantial heterogeneity. These findings align with a recent study of parent‐reported developmental skill attainment in 543 individuals aged 0–21 years, which also demonstrated marked heterogeneity and wide variability in the timing of skill acquisition [8].

We posit that our updated analyses may be most useful for milestones achieved at younger ages, where more data are available. Our findings also suggest that many children with Down syndrome obtain skills at later ages, supporting the notion that children and young adults with Down syndrome continue to learn and develop new skills throughout childhood and adolescence. As with our previous report, this analysis is limited by our setting, a single pediatric tertiary hospital‐based clinic. Families with children with more significant challenges may be more likely to seek out a specialty clinic, and those with more complex needs may visit more often. Using a clinical sample may provide the benefits of capturing and characterizing the wide range of developmental heterogeneity in Down syndrome while also establishing a foundation to better understand how specific medical conditions or other risk factors may shape development. Drawbacks of this approach include potential over‐representation of individuals with complex medical conditions. In the future, additional data from more diverse sources, collected prospectively across multiple sites, will elucidate a more comprehensive understanding of patterns of development in Down syndrome.

The earlier ages of attainment observed for some milestones using the nonparametric approach should be interpreted with caution and do not imply accelerated or precocious development relative to the general population. Rather, these differences reflect the influence of alternative modeling assumptions applied to the same caregiver‐reported data. Nonparametric, moving‐average‐based methods emphasize local data density and are less constrained by global parametric trajectory assumptions, which can shift early‐percentile estimates for certain milestones, particularly in terms of heterogeneous domains such as language. Importantly, the overall ordering of milestone acquisition, domain‐specific patterns, and sex differences were consistent across modeling approaches, supporting the interpretation that these findings represent statistical effects rather than true changes in developmental capability. As in our prior work, caregiver report remains a key data source and may introduce variability in the timing of early skill recognition; however, this limitation applies equally across analytic approaches. Clinically, these estimates should be viewed as complementary reference points that highlight variability and methodological sensitivity, rather than as revised expectations for early developmental performance.

Conclusion

In this analysis of developmental milestones in a large clinical cohort of children and adolescents with Down syndrome, nonparametric methods produced estimates that were broadly consistent with our prior GLMM‐based findings while highlighting the influence of modeling assumptions on predicted ages of attainment, particularly for language, adaptive, and academic skills. Early gross motor development appeared robust across approaches, whereas later and more complex skills demonstrated greater variability and sensitivity to statistical methods. These results reinforce the substantial heterogeneity in developmental trajectories and underscore the importance of individualized monitoring and anticipatory guidance.
