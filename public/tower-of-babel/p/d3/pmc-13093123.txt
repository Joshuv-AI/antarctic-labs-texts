Combined Effects of Protein/Fat Deficiency and Disuse on Skeletal Muscle Mass and Function in Mice
Authors: Koichi Toyoshima, Bo‐Kyung Son, Miya Oura, Zehan Song, Michiko Nanao‐Hamai, Sumito Ogawa, Masahiro Akishita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSarcopenia is driven by multifactorial insults, including undernutrition and disuse; however, the causal links between inadequate nutrition and the loss of muscle mass and strength remain unclear. This study aimed to establish mouse models of protein and/or fat deficiency and to investigate their interaction with disuse on skeletal muscle.MethodsNine‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice fed isocaloric diets for 8 weeks: normal chow (NC), low fat (LF), low protein (LP), or low protein and low fat (LPLF). Outcomes included body weight, grip strength test, gastrocnemius and soleus muscle weights, cross‐sectional area (CSA) of gastrocnemius muscle fibers, and gastrocnemius muscle mRNA expression of ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) markers (atrogin‐1, MuRF1) and inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β). After the 8‐week diet phase, a disuse model using bilateral hindlimb immobilization for 1, 3, or 7 days was applied under continued isocaloric feeding to assess diet–disuse interactions.ResultsCompared with NC, the LF, LP, and LPLF groups showed reduced body weight, grip strength, muscle mass, and myofiber CSA. Upregulation of UPS‐related genes was observed in LPLF, whereas the expression of inflammation‐related genes did not differ from NC in LF, LP, and LPLF. When combined with immobilization, LP and LPLF diets further exacerbated the decreases in muscle mass and strength compared with NC, accompanied by increased expression of both UPS‐ and inflammation‐related genes.ConclusionsAn animal model of diet‐induced reduction in muscle mass and strength was established, which will be useful for investigating the effects of protein and fat deficiency on skeletal muscle. Elucidating the detailed molecular pathways involved remains an important goal for future research and may provide new insights into nutritional approaches to prevent or treat sarcopenia.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive disorder of skeletal muscle characterized by the loss of muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance [1]. It is associated with physical disability, morbidity, and mortality, leading to loss of independence and an increased health care burden, which is a critical issue in aging societies [2].

Undernutrition, characterized by inadequate intake of protein and/or energy, is an important contributing factor to the onset and progression of sarcopenia [1, 3]. It is often caused by age‐related physiological changes such as loss of appetite, impaired taste and smell, and reduced gastrointestinal motility [4]. Therefore, adequate intake of dietary protein is recommended to prevent the onset and progression of sarcopenia [5]. On the other hand, some clinical studies have reported an association between higher dietary protein intake and sarcopenia [6], while the evidence remains inconsistent. Associations between fat intake and the prevalence of sarcopenia have also been reported, but the direction of this association remains controversial [7, 8]. In summary, although previous studies have examined the relationships between dietary protein and fat intake and muscle health, the causal mechanisms have not yet been established. Therefore, establishing an appropriate animal model is warranted to elucidate the causal links and underlying mechanisms between dietary protein and fat intake and muscle health.

Inflammation and activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) in skeletal muscle are considered key molecular mechanisms underlying sarcopenia. Elevated inflammation, characterized by increased levels of cytokines such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), contributes to the development and progression of sarcopenia [9]. In addition, the UPS, whose key markers include the E3 ubiquitin ligases atrogin‐1 and MuRF1, mediates muscle protein degradation and plays a central role in the development of sarcopenia [10].

Disuse is another important factor contributing to sarcopenia [1]. Prolonged unloading or immobility leads to disuse muscle atrophy, particularly, in older adults with reduced physical activity [11]. In clinical practice, undernutrition and disuse act as mutually aggravating factors, synergistically accelerating the loss of muscle mass and strength [12, 13]. Inflammation and activation of the UPS within skeletal muscle are also considered key molecular mechanisms underlying the decrease in muscle mass and strength induced by disuse [14].

In this study, we first aimed to establish an animal model by feeding mice a low‐protein and/or low‐fat (LPLF) diet to investigate its effects on muscle mass and strength, as well as on UPS activation and inflammation. We then combined this with a hindlimb immobilization model, which simulates disuse syndrome observed in clinical settings, to examine the combined effects of undernutrition and disuse on muscle atrophy and weakness.

Methods

Animals and Materials

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Sankyo Labo Service Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). All mice were individually housed in a controlled environment (21°C ± 3°C, 40% ± 10% humidity) under a 12‐h light/dark cycle, with free access to water and food.

Two series of experiments were conducted (1) a model of protein and/or fat deficiency using four types of diet and (2) a model combining protein and/or fat deficiency with immobilization using three types of diet.

The mice were fed one of four diets: normal chow (NC), low‐fat (LF) diet, low‐protein (LP) diet, or low‐protein and low‐fat diet (LPLF). All diets were obtained from Research Diets Inc. (New Brunswick, NJ, USA), and their compositions are shown in Table 1. The energy percentage of protein and fat in the LP, LF, and LPLF was set to 5%, based on previous reports [15, 16, 17]. Mice were fed isocaloric diets once daily at the same time of day. The total energy intake was matched to the ad libitum intake of the LPLF group, which exhibited the lowest energy consumption among the four diets. After a one‐week acclimation period, mice were assigned to the experimental diets and maintained for 8 weeks. For the immobilization groups, feeding continued for an additional 1, 3, or 7 days. To compare with the 7‐day immobilization group, additional mice were fed for 9 weeks without immobilization.

For sample collection and immobilization procedures, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture containing medetomidine hydrochloride (0.3 mg/0.3 mL/kg), midazolam (4 mg/0.8 mL/kg), and butorphanol (5 mg/1 mL/kg). Food was removed from cages at 18:00 on the day before euthanasia, which was performed between 9:00 and 10:00, resulting in a fasting period of no more than 16 h.

All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of the Graduate School of Medicine, The University of Tokyo, and were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Department of Medicine, The University of Tokyo.

Hindlimb Immobilization

To induce immobilization, both hindlimbs of anesthetized mice were wrapped with surgical tape (3 M Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and suspended using aluminum wires (DAIDOHANT Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan), as previously described (Figure 4A) [18, 19, 20]. Immobilization was performed after 8 weeks of dietary intervention and maintained for 1, 3, or 7 days. During immobilization, mice had free access to food and water, and isocaloric feeding was continued.

Grip Strength Test

Fore‐ and hindlimb grip strength was assessed using a grip strength meter (MK‐380Si, Muromachi Kikai, Tokyo, Japan). Mice were allowed to grasp a 12 × 8 cm wire mesh with all four paws and were gently pulled horizontally at a constant speed until their grip was released. Each mouse underwent five trials, and the mean of the three middle values (excluding the maximum and minimum) was used for analysis.

Measurement of Muscle Weight

After euthanasia, the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles were dissected and weighed. The mean weight of the bilateral muscles was calculated for each muscle group.

Immunofluorescence Staining, ATPase Staining and Assessment of Muscle Fiber Cross‐Sectional Area (CSA)

The gastrocnemius muscle was rapidly frozen, and 10‐μm‐thick cryosections were prepared for immunofluorescence staining (Applied Medical Research Laboratory, Osaka, Japan). Sections were prefixed in 100% ethanol, treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol (#S2023; Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), endogenous avidin/biotin blocking solution (#X0590; Agilent Technologies), and 2% bovine serum albumin (Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA). Slides were incubated overnight with a rabbit polyclonal anti‐laminin antibody (1:100, #L9393; Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA), followed by Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) (#A11037; Invitrogen, MA, USA). ATPase staining was performed to identify fast (Type II, ATPase‐positive) and slow (Type I, ATPase‐negative) myofibers, as previously described [21]. Images were captured using a BZ‐X810 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Tokyo, Japan). CSA was measured using ImageJ 1.52 (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA) in five random fields per mouse, and the mean CSA was calculated.

Quantitative PCR Analysis

Gastrocnemius muscle samples were immersed in RNA later (#AM7020; Invitrogen, CA, USA) to stabilize RNA. Tissues were homogenized using a cell destroyer (#PS1000; Bio Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan), and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (#74704; Qiagen). Complementary DNA was synthesized using the Omniscript RT Kit (#205110; Qiagen) with oligo (dT) primers (#18418020; Invitrogen). Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green method with Fast SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (#4385612; Applied Biosystems) on a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization.

Primer sequences were as follows:

Statistical Analyses

Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Between‐group comparisons were conducted using unpaired Student's t‐tests, and multiple‐group comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer post hoc tests. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of LFLP Diets on Muscle Health

A comparative analysis of the dietary groups revealed that body weight, muscle strength, muscle mass, and the CSA of the fast‐twitch (Type II, ATPase‐positive) myofibers of gastrocnemius muscle were all reduced in the LP, LF, and LPLF groups compared with the NC group (Figure 1A–E). The analysis of slow‐twitch (Type I, ATPase‐negative) myofibers showed similar trends, but the effects did not reach statistical significance (Figure 1E). These findings indicate that diets deficient in fat and/or protein, even under isocaloric conditions, have detrimental and additive effects on muscle health.

Further investigation into the molecular mechanisms showed that the LPLF diet specifically caused a marked increase in the expression of UPS‐related genes, particularly atrogin‐1 and MuRF1 (Figure 2A,B), suggesting that enhanced muscle protein degradation was associated with the pronounced atrophy observed in this group.

In contrast, the expression levels of inflammation‐related genes such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β did not differ significantly among any of the diet groups compared with the NC group (Figure 3A–C).

Effects of LPLF Diet Combined With Immobilization on Muscle Health

Body weight, muscle strength, gastrocnemius and soleus muscle weights, and gastrocnemius muscle CSA of fast‐twitch (Type II) myofibers were all lower in the LP and LPLF groups than in the NC group, and the CSA of slow‐twitch (Type I) myofibers in the LPLF group than in the NC group throughout the immobilization period (Figure 4B–F).

Following immobilization, the LP and especially the LPLF diets further increased the expression of muscle protein degradation–related genes atrogin‐1 and MuRF1 (Figure 5A,B).

Moreover, mRNA expression of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β was significantly upregulated in these groups after immobilization, particularly in the LPLF group (Figure 6A–C). These results suggest that protein deficiency, and more markedly the combination of protein and fat deficiency, exacerbates the inflammatory response to immobilization, thereby contributing to accelerated muscle deterioration.

Discussion

This study aimed to establish a mouse model of decreased muscle mass and strength induced by insufficient intake of protein and/or fat. Reductions in muscle mass and strength were observed in the LP, LF, and LPLF groups, indicating that these dietary interventions successfully reproduced features of muscle deterioration. However, none of the three diets caused significant changes in skeletal muscle inflammation, whereas only the LPLF diet‐induced upregulation of the UPS. When combined with the hindlimb immobilization model, further aggravation of muscle atrophy and weakness was observed, reflecting the combined effects of undernutrition and disuse commonly seen in clinical settings. Upregulation of skeletal muscle inflammation and the UPS was detected in the LP and LPLF groups after immobilization, which contrasted with findings under dietary intervention alone.

In addition, CSA analyses of fast‐ and slow‐twitch myofibers indicated that the decrease in muscle mass induced by LF, LP, and LPLF, as well as by LP and LPLF combined with immobilization, was largely attributable to atrophy of fast‐twitch myofiber, and is in line with reports that nutrient‐induced [22] and disuse‐induced [23] atrophy preferentially affect fast‐twitch myofibers.

Under isocaloric conditions, reductions in muscle mass and strength were observed in mice fed LP and LF diets, indicating the successful establishment of an animal model of diet‐induced muscle loss and weakness. Although adequate protein intake is generally considered important for the prevention of sarcopenia [24], some studies have reported conflicting results. For example, in a study of 3302 community‐dwelling twins aged 60 years or older in the UK, higher protein intake (> 1.3 g/kg/day) was associated with reduced muscle mass, whereas lower protein intake was associated with increased muscle mass [6]. Similarly, the causal relationship between fat intake and sarcopenia remains controversial. Among community‐dwelling older Japanese adults, higher total fat intake was associated with a lower prevalence of sarcopenia [7], whereas among Chinese older adults, a lower percentage of energy derived from fat was associated with a lower prevalence of sarcopenia [8].

Several animal studies have investigated the effects of protein restriction. A comprehensive study in which mice were fed 25 different diets with stepwise variations in energy and macronutrient composition throughout their lifespan reported that LP diets were associated with extended lifespan and improved metabolic parameters [16]. Conversely, other studies have shown that LP diets increase renal fibrosis and leukocyte infiltration, leading to glomerular injury [25], suggesting that the effects of protein restriction may vary across organs. Middle‐aged mice fed a high‐fat diet for 5 months have been reported to exhibit reductions in muscle mass and strength [26]. To date, no animal model has specifically reflected the effects of a LP or LF diet on skeletal muscle. Therefore, the present findings provide new insights and may contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the relationships among protein intake, fat intake, and sarcopenia.

In the LPLF group, enhanced muscle degradation mediated by the E3 ubiquitin ligases atrogin‐1 and MuRF1 was observed compared with the LF or LP groups. This finding suggests that the simultaneous deficiency of both protein and fat, rather than either alone, activates distinct catabolic mechanisms that promote muscle degradation and consequent loss of muscle mass and strength. The underlying mechanisms may involve upstream signaling pathways of atrogin‐1 and MuRF1, such as the AMPK (AMP‐activated protein kinase) and Akt/FOXO (forkhead box protein O) pathways [27], which warrant further investigation.

In the hindlimb immobilization model, muscle atrophy was further exacerbated by the LP and LPLF diets, indicating a synergistic or additive interaction between nutritional deficiency and disuse. This experimental combination reproduces the clinical concept of the “frailty cycle” [28], demonstrating the utility of LP and LPLF diet models for studying sarcopenia. These models may also help to elucidate the pathophysiology of frailty, for which only a few animal models have been reported [29].

The coexistence of nutritional deficiency and immobilization appeared to promote muscle degradation through UPS activation, which was not significantly observed with LP diet alone. A previous study reported that lifelong feeding of a LP diet increased UPS activity in skeletal muscle, which contrasts with our findings [30]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in the duration of dietary intervention and the age of the mice, although further research is warranted. Previous studies have also demonstrated that UPS activation occurs in skeletal muscle during disuse atrophy [14]. Although the detailed mechanisms remain unclear, our results suggest that the effect of protein deficiency on skeletal muscle is modulated by immobilization, which enhances catabolic signaling through proteasomal degradation. This mechanism may underlie the combined effects between nutritional deficiency and disuse.

Similarly, immobilization modified the effects of LP and LPLF diets on inflammation‐related gene expression in skeletal muscle, which remained unchanged under dietary intervention alone. This also indicates that nutritional deficiency and physical inactivity act synergistically to enhance inflammatory responses. Previous studies have reported that inadequate nutrition can promote inflammation through multiple pathways [31], although this was not observed in the present dietary intervention alone. Nutrient stress has also been shown to induce a senescence‐associated inflammatory phenotype, promoting chronic low‐grade inflammation [32]. The lack of inflammation observed in our dietary models may be attributable to differences in the intervention duration or the age of the mice, which should be clarified in future studies.

Inflammation is known to exert dual effects on skeletal muscle, acting both as a promoter of anabolism and a trigger of catabolism [33, 34]. Therefore, the causal relationships among inflammation, muscle mass, and strength observed in this study warrant further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, the most important limitation is the use of young mice. Because sarcopenia is a geriatric syndrome that mainly develops in older adults, the present findings should be validated in aged mouse models. Second, in the immobilization model, the LF diet was not implemented without a specific scientific rationale. Future studies are needed to investigate the effects of LF on disuse‐induced muscle atrophy. Additionally, the roles of other molecular mechanisms remained unknown in the present study. We preliminarily examined the mRNA expression using qPCR, including autophagy, which has been reported to be upregulated in a state of protein and fat deficiency [35], and OXPHOS, which has been reported to be affected by malnutrition in skeletal muscle [36]. These analyses were conducted to investigate the mechanisms underlying each dietary model and their combination with immobilization; however, no consistent results were observed (data not shown). Incorporating additional assays to further assess autophagy and OXOPHOS may help clarify these mechanisms in future studies.

In conclusion, the animal models established in this study provide a useful platform for investigating the effects of protein and fat deficiency on skeletal muscle. Our findings suggest that simultaneous deficiency of both nutrients may induce muscle atrophy and weakness through distinct molecular mechanisms. Further elucidation of these mechanisms is warranted and may offer novel insights into nutritional strategies for the prevention and treatment of sarcopenia. Although dietary management is widely recognized as essential for preventing sarcopenia, research focusing on specific nutrient components remains limited. While the role of protein has been extensively studied, the impact of dietary fat on skeletal muscle maintenance is still unclear and requires further investigation in both animal and human studies.
