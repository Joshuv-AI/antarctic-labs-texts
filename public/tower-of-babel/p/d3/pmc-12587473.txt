Postoperative rehabilitation interventions in adults after anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction
Authors: Vitor M Vaz, Murillo Mateus Ismail, Paulo Ricardo Giusti da Silva, Aline M Mizusaki Imoto, Gustavo J Almeida, Maria S Peccin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To assess the effectiveness and safety of postoperative rehabilitation interventions in adults undergoing anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. This systematic review will compare the effects of various interventions on key recovery outcomes, including pain, muscle strength, range of motion, knee joint laxity, return to daily activities and sports, functional performance, and adverse events.

Background

Description of the condition

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are a significant concern in sports involving pivoting or rapid directional changes, with an estimated 120,000 cases annually in the USA [1]. People commonly report knee instability or a sensation of the knee "giving way" following injury [2]. These injuries predominantly occur through non‐contact mechanisms, such as jump landings, deceleration, or pivoting, accounting for approximately 88% of cases among professional soccer players [3]. Female athletes are at higher risk, experiencing ACL injuries 1.6 to 3 times more frequently than males in similar sports, likely due to biomechanical and hormonal differences [4, 5].

ACL injuries often require surgical reconstruction to restore knee stability and function, particularly in active individuals. Recovery and return to pre‐injury activity levels depend on several factors. Younger patients (aged 15 to 24 years) typically exhibit better tissue healing and higher return‐to‐sport rates than those aged 25 years and older [2, 6]. Sex influences outcomes, with females facing distinct rehabilitation challenges due to differences in neuromuscular control and hormonal profiles [7]. Pre‐injury activity level also shapes recovery expectations, as professional athletes have greater functional demands than people involved in recreational activity or sedentary individuals [8]. In addition, outcomes may vary by time from injury to surgery (e.g. within three months versus more than six months) [9], graft type (hamstring tendon, bone–patellar tendon–bone, or quadriceps tendon)[10], and whether injuries are isolated or accompanied by meniscal or chondral damage [11].

Other factors that can impede recovery and delay a return to sport, even when physical recovery is adequate, include fear of reinjury, reduced confidence, kinesiophobia, and low psychological readiness to return to sport (self‐readiness) [2, 12]. Therefore, comprehensive postoperative care is needed to optimize outcomes after ACL reconstruction.

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Postoperative rehabilitation following ACL reconstruction involves a multifaceted approach [13]. Interventions aim to promote tissue healing, restore knee function, reduce pain, and facilitate a return to pre‐injury activity levels, including a return to sports [2]. Broadly, rehabilitation strategies fall into three categories: exercise‐based (active) interventions, adjunctive ('exercise‐plus') interventions, and passive interventions (see Table 1).

Exercise‐based interventions are structured, progressive programs designed to restore range of motion (ROM), muscle strength, neuromuscular control, and functional capacity. Typical components include flexibility/ROM work, open‐ and closed‐kinetic‐chain strengthening, and proprioceptive/balance or perturbation training, progressing to functional and sport‐specific drills. These programs may be delivered under supervision or as minimally supervised/home‐based care, with dosing defined by FITT (frequency, intensity, time, type).

Adjunctive (exercise‐plus) interventions deliver the above structured exercise together with an adjunct intended to augment activation, loading, or recovery—such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation (to facilitate quadriceps activation), blood‐flow restriction, whole‐body vibration, aquatic therapy, or continuous passive motion (CPM) when used as an adjunct to exercise.

Passive interventions are therapist‐ or device‐applied modalities without voluntary muscle contraction, aimed mainly at reducing pain and swelling, and promoting early joint mobility (e.g. CPM used alone, cryotherapy, manual therapy/mobilizations). By themselves, passive modalities do not build strength or motor control.

By integrating these interventions, rehabilitation programs address both biological tissue repair and functional recovery. Evidence suggests that combined approaches can reduce pain, improve physical function, and support a successful return to pre‐injury activities [13]. In our review, we will evaluate components individually and, where trials deliver multi‐component packages, we will evaluate combined approaches. The effectiveness of these interventions likely depends on their tailored application, considering factors such as participant age, sex, activity level, and psychological readiness.

Why it is important to do this review

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are common, particularly in sports, and often require surgical reconstruction to restore knee function [14]. Effective postoperative rehabilitation is critical to optimize recovery, minimize reinjury risk, and prevent complications such as early osteoarthritis [15]. However, clinical practice in ACL rehabilitation varies widely, with uncertainty surrounding the efficacy and safety of many postoperative interventions [16, 17]. Despite advancements in ACL reconstruction techniques, there is no consensus on optimal postoperative rehabilitation strategies. Existing systematic reviews and clinical guidelines often have methodological limitations and fail to provide clear, evidence‐based recommendations [13, 17, 18]. This variability can lead to inconsistent patient care and suboptimal outcomes.

This systematic review aims to critically evaluate and synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials on the safety and efficacy of postoperative interventions following ACL reconstruction. By addressing gaps in current knowledge, the review seeks to inform the development of standardized, evidence‐based rehabilitation protocols. Its findings have the potential to guide clinical practice, shape healthcare policies, and identify priorities for future research, and ultimately to improve outcomes for individuals recovering from ACL reconstruction.

Types of studies

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs), both published and unpublished, evaluating postoperative interventions following anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. Cluster‐randomized trials and cross‐over trials will also be included. Given that we know there are multiple RCTs on the subject, we will exclude quasi‐randomized studies (i.e. trials in which allocation to groups is not strictly random, such as allocation by date of birth, day of the week, medical record number, or alternation).

Types of participants

We will include individuals aged 15 to 65 years who have undergone primary ACL reconstruction, either isolated or combined with meniscal or other ligament injuries. Participants may include competitive athletes or those engaged in recreational sports. We will exclude studies involving participants with prior knee surgeries (other than ACL reconstruction), severe osteoarthritis, or complex multi‐ligament injuries. We will define osteoarthritis using clinical criteria, imaging (e.g. X‐rays or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)), or established guidelines, such as those from the American College of Rheumatology [19]. We will identify other ligament injuries through clinical examination, imaging, or surgical findings [20].

For studies with mixed populations (i.e. that include participants with conditions that are ineligible for inclusion in the review), we will include the study if data specific to the eligible subgroup can be extracted or obtained from the authors. If disaggregated data are unavailable, we will include studies where eligible participants constitute more than 80% of the sample, and undertake sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of mixed populations.

Types of interventions

We will evaluate postoperative rehabilitation interventions following ACL reconstruction, focusing on commonly used, evidence‐supported modalities [13, 18]. We will categorize interventions into three groups, which we selected for their real‐world prevalence and reported therapeutic relevance: exercise‐based interventions, adjunctive (exercise‐plus) interventions, and passive interventions.

Exercise‐based interventions

Exercise‐based interventions are interventions that encompass systematic and methodical physical exercises tailored to each phase of ACL rehabilitation, potentially integrating evidence‐based models of physiological change to optimize recovery. Outcome measures

In this systematic review, we will prioritize outcomes deemed critical or important to patients, clinicians, and policymakers, as informed by the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET Initiative, accessed May 2025) and the Panther Symposium consensus on clinical outcomes following anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (see Supplementary material 2) [2, 21]. We will prioritize outcomes based on their clinical significance and relevance for informed decision‐making rather than their prevalence in published research.

We will group outcomes into time bands, based on the work of Andernord and colleagues [22].

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

Two review authors (VV, MI) will independently screen titles and abstracts of all records identified through database searches against the eligibility criteria, using Covidence software to remove duplicates and manage study selection. We will code records as 'retrieve' (eligible or potentially eligible/unclear) or 'do not retrieve.' For non‐English language records, we will use translation software (ChatGPT 5‐thinking) to assess eligibility. Full‐text reports of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and independently screened by the same authors (VV, MI) to confirm inclusion, with reasons for exclusion documented. We will resolve disagreements through discussion or consultation with a third author (MSP).

If published reports lack sufficient information to determine eligibility, we will contact study authors for clarification (e.g. about intervention details, participant demographics, study design, or outcomes). We will make up to two contact attempts—an initial request and one reminder after two weeks; if there is no reply within four weeks of the first contact, we will consider the data unavailable and proceed with the available data.

We will collate multiple reports of the same study to ensure each study, not each report, is the unit of interest. We will document the selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram [28, 29, 30], as well as in tables of excluded studies, ongoing studies, and studies awaiting classification.

The RoB 2 tool has five domains: bias arising from the randomization process; bias due to deviations from intended interventions; bias due to missing outcome data; bias in measurement of the outcome; and bias in selection of the reported result [32, 33]. We will assign ratings of ‘low risk,’ ‘some concerns,’ or ‘high risk’, based on structured signaling questions. We will document assessments using the RoB 2 Excel tool. We will justify any deviations from algorithmic guidance with evidence from study reports. For outcomes primarily derived from medical records or registries, we expect a lower risk of bias in the measurement domain when ascertainment is objective, standardized, and non‐differential; nonetheless, we will evaluate and report all domains and any residual concerns (e.g. missingness (loss or lack of outcome data); inconsistent definitions (outcomes defined or measured differently across sources or participants); differential surveillance (systematic between‐group differences in how intensively outcomes are sought or recorded)).

Measures of treatment effect

For dichotomous outcomes, we will report risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as the primary effect measure; odds ratios (ORs) or risk differences (RDs) may be used when methodologically justified [34]. For continuous outcomes, we will use mean differences (MDs) when outcomes are measured on the same scale and standardized mean differences (SMDs) when different scales are used, each with 95% CIs [34]. For time‐to‐event outcomes, we will report hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs, without converting to risk ratios unless justified [34].

Unit of analysis issues

The unit of analysis will be the participant. For cross‐over RCTs, we will use first‐period data analyzed as a parallel‐group comparison; if an adequate washout is prespecified and no carryover/period effects are evident, we may use paired within‐participant estimates. For cluster‐randomized trials, we will account for clustering using reported intracluster correlation coefficients (ICCs) or an assumed ICC with sensitivity analyses. In multi‐arm trials, we will combine or appropriately split arms to avoid double‐counting. When outcomes are reported per knee (e.g. bilateral cases), we will use a participant‐level summary (e.g. average across knees) or select one knee at random; if knees are analyzed as clusters, we will adjust as for cluster designs.

Dealing with missing data

We will contact study authors regarding missing data; if there is no reply, we will send a reminder after two weeks. If there is no response within four weeks of the initial contact, we will deem the data unavailable. In such cases, we will proceed with the available data and assess the impact of doing this through sensitivity analyses. For dichotomous outcomes, we will calculate loss rates using the number of randomized participants as the denominator. For continuous outcomes, we will calculate MD or SMD based on the number of participants analyzed at the relevant time point, or the number randomized if the number analyzed is not specified. We will calculate missing standard deviations from other statistics (e.g. standard errors, CIs, probability (P) values), according to guidance in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [35], or impute from other trials in the meta‐analysis if necessary. We will scrutinize missing data listed in published protocols for potential bias (e.g. exclusions due to adverse events).

Reporting bias assessment

If we include more than 10 RCTs in the review, we will generate funnel plots to explore small‐study biases, considering multiple causes of asymmetry [36]. We will conduct formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry following the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [35]. To assess selective outcome reporting, we will compare published reports with trial protocols on the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (www.who.int/ictrp/en), noting any discrepancies that are indicative of reporting bias.

Statistical synthesis (meta‐analysis)

We will conduct meta‐analysis according to guidance in chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [35]. For meta‐analysis of dichotomous data, we will use RR primarily and OR/RD when justified, for continuous data, MD or SMD, and for time‐to‐event data, HR, all of these with 95% CIs. For primary pooling, we will use an inverse‐variance random‐effects model. The between‐study variance (τ²) will be estimated with REML (RevMan default) [31, 35]; if REML fails in a specific analysis, DerSimonian–Laird will be used for that analysis only. Confidence intervals will be Wald‐type (RevMan); in supplementary analyses outside RevMan, we will apply Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustments as a sensitivity check. We will present 95% prediction intervals where feasible. Our primary syntheses will be run in RevMan [31]; if required for multivariate/robust models, we will use R (metafor, ≥ 2.1 to 0) [37].

Synthesis without meta‐analysis (SWiM)

When meta‐analysis is not appropriate due to limited or incompatible data, we will undertake SWiM according to chapter 12 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [28]. We will group studies by the same synthesis PICOs and time windows, apply consistent effect‐direction rules, summarize patterns considering study size/precision, and avoid vote counting by statistical significance. We will present results in structured text and tables; non‐standard plots will be justified.

Equity‐related assessment

We are aware that most RCTs on postoperative rehabilitation interventions following ACL reconstruction are conducted in high‐income or upper‐middle‐income countries, and primarily involve populations with high socioeconomic status and access to specialized care (e.g. private hospitals or sports medicine centers). Therefore, we will collect data on study setting and country income level (World Bank classification); we will also collect data on participant sex/gender and consider equity implications narratively.

There is a critical lack of data from low‐income or underserved populations, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries, which precludes a meaningful equity analysis. Additionally, existing studies from high‐income settings indicate that lower socioeconomic status is associated with reduced access to ACL reconstruction and poorer postoperative outcomes, highlighting disparities that remain under‐explored in the current literature [38, 39, 40].

In the SoF tables, we will report relative effects (e.g. RRs, HRs), absolute effects, and—when estimable and interpretable—the NNTB or NNTH. These results will be reported irrespective of statistical significance; where applicable, we will indicate when estimates are statistically significant (e.g. 95% CI not crossing the null). We will derive the baseline risk for absolute effects and NNTB/NNTH from the median control group event rate across included studies, or from high‐quality external sources if internal estimates are inappropriate.

We will distinguish between absence of evidence and evidence of no effect, avoiding clinical practice recommendations. In the implications for research section of the review, we will highlight priorities and uncertainties.

Consumer involvement

We have not involved consumers directly, but our outcomes align with core outcome sets from the COMET initiative, which were developed with consumer input [2].
