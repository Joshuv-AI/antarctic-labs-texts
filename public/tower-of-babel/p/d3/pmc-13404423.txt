Intratumoral TIGIT blockade augments antitumor responses and bypasses increased functionality of peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells in mouse and human models of bone and soft tissue sarcoma
Authors: Marshall L Lammers, Cyrus J Sholevar, Makan Karimzadeh, Aryana M Razmara, Sylvia M Cruz, Megan C Purl, Natalie M Liu, Danika Bakke, Rosemary N Plagens, Andrew Elliott, Michael K Sheng, Clemens Van Dongen, Trung Tran, Annie Truc Trinh, Ryan N Nielsen, Tushar Kotamraju, Lauren E Farley, Khurshid R Iranpur, Tasneem Mukarrama, Alessandro Gronchi, Richard F Riedel, Arta M Monjazeb, Steven W Thorpe, Morgan A Darrow, Jinhwan Kim, William J Murphy, Sean J Judge, Robert J Canter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTIGIT (T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains) has emerged as a key exhaustion marker of intratumoral natural killer (NK) cells, but results from clinical trials with TIGIT blockade have been largely negative. Recent data have suggested that a subset of TIGIT-expressing NK cells can show increased functionality. We hypothesized that there are differences in function between peripheral and intratumoral TIGIT-expressing NK cells in patients with sarcoma and preclinical sarcoma models, which undermine the efficacy of systemic TIGIT blockade. We sought to investigate differences in TIGIT+ NK cells using systemic versus intratumoral TIGIT-blocking strategies.MethodsPeripheral and intratumoral NK cells were analyzed from human patients and mice with osteosarcoma (OSA) and soft tissue sarcoma (STS). NK phenotype and function were evaluated using flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, live-cell imaging, and RNA sequencing. Mouse antimouse-IgG1 TIGIT blockade was delivered systemically or intratumorally in flank models of OSA (K7M2) and STS (MCA-205). Clinical and genomic data were evaluated using Caris CODEai. Expression of the TIGIT ligand CD155 on myeloid and tumor cells was evaluated as a marker of TIGIT function.ResultsUsing multiple readouts, TIGIT+ NK cells from the spleen of tumor-bearing mice or peripheral blood of patients with STS and OSA showed increased functionality compared with TIGIT− NK, while TIGIT+ NK cells from the sarcoma tumor microenvironment (TME) of mice and humans were dysfunctional, with upregulated senescence and inhibitory gene pathways. Systemic TIGIT blockade reinvigorated intratumoral NK cell function but inhibited peripheral NK cells, while intratumoral administration of TIGIT blockade significantly delayed tumor growth and prolonged survival with partial reversal of NK dysfunction in the TME. Clinical and genomic data demonstrated that more NK cell infiltration in human STS was prognostic of improved overall survival only when intratumoral CD155 expression was low, indicating that elevated CD155 expression is correlated with greater NK cell dysfunction.ConclusionsWe identified a cross-species role of TIGIT expression dependent on location, where peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells showed evidence of enhanced functionality, while intratumoral TIGIT+ NK cells were dysfunctional. These differences impacted antitumor effects of TIGIT blockade, suggesting that intratumoral delivery of TIGIT blockade may be a novel translational strategy in high-risk bone and soft tissue sarcomas.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been practice-changing for an ever-increasing number of cancers.1 2 Yet, despite these advances, there remain subsets of cancers that respond poorly, and these patients are in need of improved treatment options. Bone and soft tissue sarcomas, a complex family of mesenchymal tumors with >100 subtypes, represent a family of largely immunotherapy-resistant cancers with few options outside of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy.3–8 Although there are subtypes of sarcomas that have shown encouraging responses to immunotherapy, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade in undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma and engineered T-cell receptor therapy in synovial sarcoma, current response rates to immunotherapy in sarcomas remain modest, and novel treatments are needed.8–10

Natural killer (NK) cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes capable of killing malignant cells without prior sensitization. They are attractive targets for immunotherapy using strategies aimed at both boosting endogenous NK cells as well as adoptive cellular therapy.11 12 Preclinical studies and observational data have provided evidence that increased frequency of intratumoral NK cells are associated with clinical outcomes in both osteosarcoma (OSA) and soft tissue sarcoma (STS).11–15 Studies have also shown that the tumor microenvironment (TME) of sarcomas and other solid tumors is hostile to cytotoxic immune cells.16–19 NK cell dysfunction and exhaustion in the TME, defined by decreased cytotoxicity, cytokine production, and proliferation, have been identified as a key barrier to successful immunotherapy.16–19 We and others have shown that TIGIT is a critical marker of immune exhaustion in NK cells, including sarcomas, and clinical trials have evaluated both Fc-active and Fc-silent TIGIT-blocking antibodies as a strategy to reinvigorate NK and T cells for therapeutic effects.13 20–22 Specifically, Fc-silent antibodies were designed to reinvigorate dysfunctional NK and T cells in the TME and Fc-active modalities were engineered to simultaneously reinvigorate these immune populations and deplete certain TIGIT-expressing cells, such as immunosuppressive Tregs or intratumoral dysfunctional/exhausted lymphocytes.23–25 However, despite promising data from preclinical studies, clinical results of TIGIT blockade have been modest to date, especially when given as monotherapy.23–25

TIGIT is an inhibitory surface protein expressed on NK and T cells that binds to CD155 with high affinity, in addition to CD112 and CD113, on antigen-presenting cells and tumor cells.26 27 Classically, this binding sends negative stimuli to the TIGIT-expressing cell through TIGIT’s intracellular immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) domain.26 27 Importantly, however, TIGIT expression on NK cells has not always been correlated with immune dysfunction. Vendrame et al observed that TIGIT-expressing NK cells in patients with HIV were paradoxically associated with highly functional and adaptive/memory-like features.28 Similarly, other groups have demonstrated that TIGIT-expressing NK cells exhibit greater antitumor activity compared with TIGIT-negative NK cells when exposed to lung cancer and glioblastoma cells ex vivo.29 30

We sought to evaluate the impact of TIGIT expression on NK cells in both the periphery and TME of patients with OSA and STS and preclinical models. We aimed to investigate the impact of TIGIT blockade on TIGIT+ NK cells in the periphery versus the sarcoma TME, hypothesizing that TIGIT blockade would have opposite effects on peripheral compared with intratumoral NK cells. Ultimately, we sought to test whether intratumoral TIGIT blockade would be better than systemic administration in sarcoma-bearing mice to provide proof-of-concept evidence that intratumoral TIGIT blockade is an effective immunotherapy strategy in bone and soft tissue sarcomas.

Methods

Human sample preparation

Whole blood was processed into peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and plasma as described previously.13 31 32 Plasma was stored at −80°C for batched analyses. Patient tumors were collected from surgical specimens in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium at 4°C. Tumor tissue was digested mechanically for downstream assays.13

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on tissue microarrays using a rabbit antihuman NCR1 (NKp46) antibody (Abcam: ab224703). Slides were analyzed in a blinded fashion by a sarcoma pathologist (MAD). IHC scores were calculated as described previously and dichotomized into high or low using the median value.31

Caris Life Sciences CODEai data

A retrospective analysis was conducted on tumor samples submitted by clinical physicians for molecular profiling at Caris Life Sciences (Phoenix, Arizona, USA), a College of American Pathologists/Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified laboratory. Real-world overall survival (OS) information was obtained from insurance claims data. OS was calculated from the time of tissue collection until the date of death or last contact. Patients without contact/claims data for a period of at least 100 days were presumed deceased. Conversely, patients with documented clinical activity within 100 days prior to the latest data update were censored in the analysis. A total of 2810 samples with associated whole transcriptome sequencing were identified as STS tumors using histology and OncoTree codes, which were used for subsequent survival analyses. Kaplan-Meier estimates, HRs as determined by Cox proportional hazards model, and log-rank p values were calculated for molecularly defined patient cohorts using the Python package lifelines (V.0.30.0), where significance was determined as p≤0.05.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were stained for cell surface and intracellular targets.13 31–33 Flow cytometry was performed using either BD LSRFortessa or Sony ID7000 analyzer. A complete list of flow cytometry antibodies is available in online supplemental table 1. For fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), cells were stained with surface antibodies and collected in RF10C at 4°C. Representative flow cytometry gating for human and mouse NK cell gating are shown in online supplemental figures 1 and 2. Human NK cell subsets were gated for FACS using the following gating strategy: singlets, live cells, CD45+CD3−CD56+TIGIT+/–. Mouse NK cell subsets were gated for FACS using the following strategy: singlets, live cells, CD45+CD3−CD49b+TIGIT+/–, as shown in online supplemental figure 3.

Tumor cell lines and in vitro culturing

K562 human erythroleukemia (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC): CCL-243), K562 clone 9 (kind gift from Dr Dean Lee), MCA-205 mouse fibrosarcoma (Sigma-Aldrich: SCC173), M3-9-M murine rhabdomyosarcoma (kind gift from Dr Crystal Mackall), and transduced cell lines were cultured in complete RF10C media. A673 human Ewing sarcoma (CRL-1598), K7M2 murine OSA (ATCC: CRL-2836), and transduced cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Transduced cells expressed a puromycin selection gene and were incubated with 5 µg/mL of puromycin every 3 weeks. Mycoplasma testing and cell line authentication were performed routinely.

CD107a assays

Human and murine immune cells were co-cultured with tumor targets or 1X phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and ionomycin (Invitrogen: 00497093). For co-culture experiments, NK cells, PBMCs, or splenocytes were first stimulated with 100 IU/mL of recombinant human interleukin 2 (rhIL-2) (National Cancer Institute: Ro 23-6019). One hour into the stimulation, Golgi plug (BD Biosciences: 51-2301 KZ) and Golgi stop (BD Biosciences: 51-2092 KZ) were added, followed by antihuman CD107a BV605 (BioLegend: 328634) or antimouse CD107a PE-Dazzle 594 (BioLegend 121624). Cells were then stained for flow cytometry.

Mouse models

Female or male C57BL/6, BALB/c, and RAG mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed in an American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care-approved, specific pathogen-free facility.

Mouse sample collection and tissue preparation

Mouse spleens and tumors were harvested after euthanasia, mechanically digested, and processed.32–34 Mouse blood was collected from the tail vein using a heat lamp and a small incision. 100 µL of blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes, and gentle pressure was applied for hemostasis. Blood was then subjected to two rounds of red blood cell lysis.

Mouse tumor studies

Mice were injected in the right flank with 1×106 K7M2 cells or 5×104 MCA-205 cells. Orthotopic injections of 1×106 K7M2 were injected into the tibia of BALB/c mice after mechanical insertion of a syringe parallel to the bone.35 Mice were followed for survival, and terminal end points were used per the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines.

Ultrasound imaging

For two-dimensional ultrasound imaging, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and hair overlying the tumors was removed.36 A thin layer of commercial ultrasound gel was applied to the exposed skin, and imaging was performed using a Vevo F2 (Fujifilm VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) equipped with a 128-element linear array transducer operating at 25–57 MHz frequency. All acquired B-mode images were reconstructed and analyzed in Vevo LAB software (V.5.10.0).36

NK depletion studies

BALB/c mice were injected intraperitoneally with 10 µL of stock anti-ASGM1 (Wako: 014-09801) on days 0, 7, 14, and 21.34 37 C57BL/6 mice were injected intraperitoneally with a loading dose of 200 µg of anti-NK1.1 (clone PK136) (BioXCell: BE0036) on day 0 followed by 100 µg of anti-NK1.1 on days 7 and 14.38 39

Incucyte assays

For killing assays using live-cell imaging, 2×103 fluorescently labeled tumor target cells were plated in 96-well flat-bottom plates overnight. For TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cell killing assays, NK cells were isolated using FACS flow sorting gated on TIGIT+ and TIGIT− populations using a fluorescence-minus-one control. After sorting, effector cells were counted and plated at the indicated effector-to-target (E:T) ratios with 1000 IU/mL rhIL-2 before collecting at least five images per hour for the duration of the assay. Specific killing was calculated by analyzing green or red object counts and graphed as a fold change ratio using the Sartorius Incucyte.40

RNA sequencing

10x Genomics single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed as described previously.32 33 Briefly, NK cells were isolated using FACS sorting or magnetic bead isolation kits (STEMCELL Technologies: 19855), counted, and concentrated between 700 and 1500 cells/µL for sequencing using the 10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single-Cell 3’ Gene Expression V.3.1 protocol.32 33 Additional details are provided in online supplemental methods. Publicly available RNA-seq data for both peripheral and intratumoral NK cells can be found at BioProject accession number PRJNA1417271.41

TIGIT block experiments

After tumors reached a volume of 100–300 mm3, mice were injected with either 200 µg or 100 µg of an IgG1 antimouse TIGIT antibody (Absolute Antibody: Ab01258-1.1, clone 1B4) intraperitoneally or intratumorally twice a week for a total of five doses. Given volume limitations, specifically for intratumoral injections, 100 µg doses of TIGIT-blocking antibodies were predominantly compared. Control mice were injected intratumorally with 100 µg of an IgG2a isotype diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (InVivoMab: BE0089).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

Human CD155 ELISA kits from Thermo Fisher Scientific (EH79RB) and R&D Systems (DY253005), and murine IgG Rapid ELISA kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (EELR010), were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, plasma from STS and OSA cohorts was incubated with anti-CD155 monoclonal antibodies for 2 hours followed by a sandwich ELISA including streptavidin horseradish peroxidase (HRP). RAG or C57BL/6 mouse plasma was collected via tail vein bleed before similar incubation with anti-IgG monoclonal antibodies for 1 hour before sandwich ELISA also using streptavidin HRP. Samples were analyzed using a microplate photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific: 51119000) at an optical density of 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Excel, GraphPad Prism software, and R V.4.3.3 were used for statistical analysis. We analyzed the differences between two groups using appropriate paired or unpaired Student’s t-test for parametric data, and the Mann-Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-parametric data. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test. Tumor growth and Incucyte cytotoxicity curves were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance.

Results

NK cells are prognostic in human soft tissue and bone sarcomas but are dysfunctional

We and others have previously shown that the presence of NK cells in the TME of STS and OSA correlates with improved clinical outcomes.11–13 Analyzing a cohort of 69 patients with STS treated from October 2018 to April 2025 (see online supplemental table 2 for patient clinical and pathological data), we observed that infiltration of NK cells in STS, as measured by IHC for NKp46, was associated with significantly improved OS (p≤0.05, figure 1A,B). We then queried the Caris CODEai clinico-genomic platform to assess if molecular/genomic markers of NK cells in the STS also correlated with OS. Analyzing data from 2810 patients with STS (see online supplemental table 3 for patient data) stratified into quartiles (figure 1C), we observed that NCR1 (NKp46) RNA expression in STS tumors correlated with superior OS, with longer survival seen with each increasing quartile of NCR1. These data also showed a significant difference when using an aggregated NK gene signature composed of multiple NK-related phenotypic and cytotoxic markers (online supplemental figure 4).42 Although these data support that intratumoral NK cells are associated with superior survival in patients with STS, it is well established that the TME of solid tumors, including sarcomas, fosters immune dysfunction and exhaustion.18 19 To reconcile this, we next examined the immune phenotype and function of NK cells from PBMCs and the TME of our STS cohort and observed similar evidence of NK dysfunction in tumor-infiltrating NK cells. Figure 1D shows positron emission tomography (PET)/CT images and histology of a representative patient with STS, along with representative flow cytometry analyses of matched blood and tumor samples (figure 1E). Overall, we observed significantly lower frequencies of cytotoxic CD56dim NK cells in the STS TME compared with PBMC NK cells (figure 1F), along with significantly lower expression of activation markers CD16, granzyme B, and DNAX accessory molecule-1 (DNAM-1) compared with PBMC subsets (figure 1G,I). To assess functional differences, we then compared CD107a expression between intratumoral and PBMC NK cells in patients with STS (figure 1J). Consistent with our phenotype data, we observed that NK cells from the STS TME expressed significantly lower CD107a after co-culture with K562 prototypical NK cell targets (figure 1K) or PMA (figure 1L,M). Despite known differences in the immune TME of OSA and STS, NK cells have also been shown to correlate with clinical outcomes in OSA.11 12 We next evaluated similar immune readouts in a cohort of patients with OSA (n=7) undergoing surgery and chemotherapy for primary disease (see online supplemental table 4 for patient data). Figure 1N shows representative bone scan and histopathology from a patient with conventional OSA of the distal femur. Similar to STS, we observed numerically lower frequencies of cytotoxic CD56dim NK cells in the OSA TME, although this difference was not statistically significant (figure 1O). Furthermore, CD16, granzyme B, and DNAM-1 expression were all significantly lower on NK cells in the OSA TME compared with PBMCs (figure 1O–R). Taken together, these data indicate that NK cells are clinically relevant tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the STS and OSA TME despite evidence of intratumoral dysfunction.

NK cells are critical for delaying tumor growth in murine STS and OSA but are dysfunctional in the TME

To examine the role of NK cells in the TME of syngeneic murine models, we established both subcutaneous and intratibial models of K7M2 (OSA) and subcutaneous/flank MCA-205 (STS) using ultrasound to complement caliper-based measurements of tumor growth (figure 2A–F). We observed histological evidence of highly cellular and pleomorphic cells in all tumors, with intratibial tumors also showing the presence of mature bone (figure 2C,D). We then performed immune characterization of K7M2 and MCA-205 tumors, observing comparable proportions of NK cells in the TME of both subcutaneous and intratibial OSA models (figure 2G). Analysis of MCA-205 tumors showed similar NK frequencies in the TME compared with the spleen of C57BL/6 mice (figure 2H). When we analyzed the absolute number of NK cells in these compartments, there were significantly more NK cells in the spleen per mg of tissue compared with the MCA-205 tumor, as shown in online supplemental figure 5. Notably, K7M2 OSA tumors also contained more NK cells/mg of tissue when compared with MCA-205 STS tumors (p≤0.05) (online supplemental figure 5).

We then depleted NK cells from tumor-bearing mice. In K7M2 mice, tumors grew significantly faster in NK-depleted mice (figure 2I) with significantly shorter survival (figure 2J). Consistent with successful NK depletion, we observed a significant reduction in the frequency of NK cells in the blood of mice at day 9 when growth curves were starting to separate (figure 2K). We also evaluated the effect of NK depletion in MCA-205 fibrosarcoma in C57BL/6 mice. Interestingly, despite lower frequencies of NK cells in MCA-205 tumors, NK depletion in MCA-205 mice also led to more rapid tumor growth (figure 2L), shorter survival (figure 2M), and a corresponding significant reduction in NK cells in the blood (figure 2N). In untreated tumor-bearing mice, we also observed significantly lower CD107a expression by NK cells following PMA stimulation in the TME compared with the spleen in both K7M2 BALB/c mice (figure 2O,P) and MCA-205 C57BL/6 mice (figure 2Q,R), consistent with NK dysfunction in the murine sarcoma TME. Thus, similar to our human data, NK cells in both murine OSA and STS appear to mediate significant antitumor effects despite evidence of TME-induced NK dysfunction.

Human peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells show evidence of increased functionality

Prior publications have shown TIGIT to be a critical exhaustion marker for NK cells, but recent data have suggested that a subset of TIGIT+ NK cells have increased function.28–30 We therefore set out to compare TIGIT+ NK cells in the blood versus the sarcoma TME in our human cohort. Using flow cytometry (figure 3A), we observed significant upregulation of TIGIT on NK cells in the STS TME (figure 3B).13 Importantly, we observed significantly higher expression of granzyme B on TIGIT+ NK cells in the blood and significantly lower expression of granzyme B on TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME (figure 3C). We also observed a significantly higher proportion of CD56dim NK cells among the TIGIT+ NK cells in the blood compared with TIGIT− (p≤0.05), while there was no difference between TIGIT+ and TIGIT− subsets in the STS TME (figure 3D). Similarly, we observed greater frequencies of NKp46 expression on peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells compared with TIGIT− (p≤0.05), while there was no difference on TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME (online supplemental figure 6).

Next, we evaluated functional characteristics of TIGIT+ NK cells in the periphery. We started by assessing apoptosis following ex vivo expansion of human NK cells (figure 3E). After 7 days of co-culture with irradiated feeder cells, we sorted TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells and re-stimulated using K562 clone 9 cells and low-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2). Representative TIGIT staining on day 7 pre-sorting (figure 3F) demonstrates characteristic upregulation of TIGIT on NK cells following stimulation. Postsorting, PBMC-derived TIGIT+ NK cells expanded more rapidly than TIGIT− subsets and maintained greater viability (figure 3G.H). Postsorting, TIGIT+ NK cells also showed significantly lower apoptosis compared with TIGIT− NK cells (figure 3I,J). We then tested differences in apoptosis under different stimulatory conditions. PBMCs from a healthy human donor were cultured overnight with 100 IU/mL of IL-2 prior to annexin V staining. Similar to our prior data, we again observed significantly greater apoptosis in the TIGIT− NK subsets (figure 3K,L).

Finally, we investigated the cytotoxic potential of TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from the blood compared with NK cells isolated from the STS TME. Sorted TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells were cocultured with A673 Ewing sarcoma targets and 1000 IU/mL of IL-2 for 48 hours (figure 3M). Notably, as shown in figure 3N, PBMC-derived TIGIT+ NK cells from a healthy donor were significantly more cytotoxic than TIGIT− at multiple E:T ratios. Similarly, peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells from a patient with STS were significantly more cytotoxic compared with TIGIT− NK cells at multiple E:T ratios (figure 3O). Conversely, sorted TIGIT+ NK cells isolated from the TME of the same patient with STS were significantly less cytotoxic compared with TIGIT− NK cells (figure 3P). Importantly, both TIGIT+ and TIGIT− subsets in the STS TME showed limited overall killing compared with targets only (figure 3P), indicative of overall NK dysfunction in the TME. Taken together, these data suggest that while TIGIT expression is associated with NK cell dysfunction in the TME, it also correlates with enhanced functionality in peripheral blood.

Murine peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells display increased functionality while intratumoral subsets are dysfunctional

To determine if differences between TIGIT+ peripheral and tumor-infiltrating NK cells extended to immunocompetent mice, we then examined our murine models of STS and OSA. As in our human subjects, we observed robust upregulation of TIGIT on intratumoral NK cells compared with spleen NK cells in both K7M2 and MCA-205 (figure 4A–C). We then examined CD107a degranulation from both TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from the spleen and TME as shown in figure 4D. Importantly, peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells from the spleens of K7M2 BALB/c mice (figure 4E) expressed significantly more CD107a compared with TIGIT− NK cells, whereas TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME expressed significantly less. Notably, we observed similar results for MCA-205 C57BL/6 mice (figure 4F). We then sorted splenic and intratumoral TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from both BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice prior to coculturing with syngeneic tumor targets. We observed that peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells were significantly more cytotoxic than TIGIT− NK cells from both BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (figure 4G,H). We also found C57BL/6 peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells were more cytotoxic toward syngeneic M3-9-M rhabdomyosarcoma cell targets when compared with TIGIT− (online supplemental figure 7). Using scRNA-seq, we then analyzed differential gene expression between TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from both the periphery and TME (figure 4I,J). We observed increased gene expression of anti-apoptotic genes in peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells compared with TIGIT− while opposite patterns were shown in tumor-infiltrating TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells, which correlated with our human data.

NK TIGIT expression reveals compartment-dependent gene expression profiles in the periphery compared with the TME

To expand on our murine scRNA-seq data, we then analyzed TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from both the blood and tumor of a patient with STS. While populations did not cluster from TIGIT expression alone (figure 5A), peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells expressed 710 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) when compared with TIGIT− subsets, while the tumor had 864 DEGs (p≤0.05). Analyzing the top DEGs between TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells, peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells upregulated transcripts associated with activation such as FASLG, NKG7, and FCGR3A compared with TIGIT− cells (figure 5B). In contrast, TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME did not contain these activating transcripts in the top DEGs (figure 5C). To expand on this analysis, we examined the top 20 DEGs for blood and sarcoma TIGIT+ NK cells. Interestingly, TIGIT+ NK cells in the periphery expressed more of the inhibitory protein lymphocyte activation gene-3, when compared with TIGIT− groups, despite the increased functionality we observed in our other assays (figure 5D).

We then analyzed TIGIT+ and TIGIT− NK cells from the periphery and the TME of mice with STS (figure 5E). Consistent with our hypothesis, TIGIT+ NK cells in both the spleen and the tumor clustered into separate and distinct sets of NK cells. Peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells clustered to a subset that was more mature with higher expression of transcripts such as Eomes (figure 5F). However, TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME downregulated functional RNA transcripts such as LAMP1 (CD107a) (figure 5G). Additional DEGs in both the spleen and tumor are shown in figure 5H.

We next used Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes to examine patterns in the top 100 DEGs within our human STS scRNA-seq data. In support of our phenotypic and functional data, peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells upregulated genes associated with greater activation and cytotoxicity. Categories such as NK cell degranulation, positive regulation of adaptive immune responses, and Fc-gamma receptor signaling were among the top 10 biological processes identified (figure 5I). In contrast, TIGIT+ NK cells in the TME aligned with more dysfunctional categories such as positive regulation of cellular senescence and negative regulation of NK cell-mediated cellular immunity among the top categories (figure 5J). These data support location-based gene profiles of TIGIT+ NK cells impacting phenotype and function.

Intratumoral TIGIT blockade improves survival in STS-bearing and OSA-bearing mice

We next compared the effects of an IgG1 antimouse TIGIT-blocking antibody administered intraperitoneally or intratumorally in murine OSA (figure 6A). As expected, OSA-infiltrating NK cells expressed significantly more CD107a ex vivo after TIGIT blockade administered either intraperitoneally or intratumorally compared with IgG controls (figure 6B). However, peripheral NK cells expressed significantly less CD107a following intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade compared with both intratumoral TIGIT blockade or intratumoral IgG treatment (figure 6C), providing evidence that peripheral and intratumoral TIGIT+ NK cells have discordant responses to TIGIT blockade.

Based on these data, we hypothesized that local administration of TIGIT blockade would improve antitumor effects compared with systemic administration. As shown in figure 6D,E, intratumoral TIGIT blockade led to significantly slower tumor growth and superior OS when compared with both intraperitoneal and IgG intratumoral controls, including complete regression in 1/7 tumors treated with intratumoral TIGIT blockade in K7M2-bearing male BALB/c mice. To determine how robust these findings were, we then repeated this experiment in MCA-205-bearing C57BL/6 female mice (figure 6F,G), observing similar results with significant tumor growth delay and improved survival in the intratumoral TIGIT blockade cohort. We also observed a trend for dose-dependent effects when administering intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade with a trend toward slower tumor growth in both K7M2 and MCA-205 tumors with 200 µg intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade compared with 100 µg dosing (online supplemental figure 8). Moreover, in MCA-205-bearing mice, OS was significantly longer with the 200 µg dosing, although in both models, the results of the higher-dose intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade remained inferior to those of 100 µg intratumoral TIGIT blockade. We also observed inferior results with higher-dose intratumoral TIGIT blockade at 200 µg, but this was likely technical in nature since the higher dose required a higher volume of injection leading to ulceration and leakage of fluid contents in many animals (data not shown).

To evaluate pharmacodynamics of our approach, we next measured serum IgG levels in RAG mice bearing MCA-205 tumors following administration of intraperitoneal or intratumoral TIGIT blockade given that anti-TIGIT antibody clone 1B4 is a recombinant murine antibody limiting our ability to detect specific IgG isotypes. We observed a near-linear increase in serum IgG levels following sequential doses of intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade (figure 6H), reaching a peak of 113.3±28.8 µg/mL, whereas peak serum IgG levels in the intratumoral-treated cohort were approximately 80 µg/mL lower (p<0.05, figure 6H). Notably, serum IgG levels did demonstrate a slower decay in the intratumoral cohort compared with the intraperitoneal cohort, such that absolute values were numerically lower in the intratumoral cohort by day 18, suggesting a potential depot effect of intratumoral administration (figure 6H). Importantly, these serum IgG concentrations were overall approximately three logs lower than those in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice (online supplemental figure 9). We then tested if this TIGIT antibody depleted TIGIT-expressing T and NK cell subsets, which has been seen by other Fc-active modalities, although data from Dixon et al suggest that this particular TIGIT blocking clone does not deplete NK or T cells.43 44 Similarly, in the BALB/c K7M2 model, we did not observe evidence of NK or T cell depletion following intraperitoneal or intratumoral TIGIT blockade (online supplemental figure 10).44 We also did not observe that TIGIT blockade altered expression of DNAM-1 or CD69 when given systemically or locally (online supplemental figure 10). It is important to note, however, when we performed a parallel experiment using intraperitoneal and intratumoral TIGIT-blocking antibodies with MCA-205-bearing C57BL/6 mice, mice receiving intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade did exhibit lower numbers of NK and T cells in the periphery compared with intratumoral TIGIT groups, suggesting this antibody has the potential to partially deplete TIGIT-expressing populations in selected circumstances (online supplemental figure 11).

We then evaluated the effects of intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade in vivo on peripheral and intratumoral NK cell cytotoxicity in MCA-205-bearing mice (figure 6I). Similar to our OSA model, peripheral NK cells showed decreased cytotoxicity following intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade compared with control groups (figure 6J), while intratumoral NK cells showed augmented cytotoxicity (figure 6K). In addition, peripheral NK cells from MCA-205-bearing mice receiving intraperitoneal TIGIT blockade were also less cytotoxic compared with intratumoral TIGIT-blockade groups, supporting local intratumoral injections of TIGIT-blocking antibodies bypass the inhibition of peripheral NK cells in preclinical models of fibrosarcoma (online supplemental figure 12).

CD155 expression mediates functional implications of TIGIT expression on NK cells in periphery and TME

We next hypothesized that CD155 expression is an important mediator of TIGIT+ NK cell function.45 First, we assessed soluble CD155 (sCD155) in the plasma of our STS and OSA cohorts (online supplemental figure 13). As shown in figure 7A, when dichotomized by the median, high sCD155 levels were associated with significantly worse distant recurrence-free survival in patients with STS (9.4±4.3 months vs not reached, p≤0.05). Next, we analyzed the Caris Life Science STS patient cohort (n=2810), observing that higher CD155 RNA expression in tumors was associated with significantly worse OS (figure 7B). Among patients with STS with CD155 expression in the lowest quartile, we observed that higher NCR1 expression, as assessed by median cut-off, was associated with significantly better OS compared with lower NCR1 expression (p≤0.05, figure 7C). However, among patients with STS with CD155 expression in the highest quartile, no difference in survival was observed between NCR1 high and low expression (figure 7D).

We then determined that CD155 expression was upregulated in our STS patient cohort on both monocytes and STS cells in the TME when compared with the blood (figure 7E, online supplemental figure 14). Similar results were observed in both STS and OSA mouse models analyzing macrophages (figure 7F–M, online supplemental figure 15 for parent gating). In addition, the frequency of CD155+ macrophages was significantly greater in the TME compared with the spleen and represented a larger percentage of overall immune cells (figure 7K,M). These data suggest that an increased frequency of CD155 expression in the TME, on both tumor cells and myeloid populations, correlates with the transition from the increased functionality of peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells to the dysfunctional state of intratumoral TIGIT+ NK cell subsets.

Discussion

Although recent advances in cancer immunotherapy have translated to dramatic survival benefits for multiple cancers, STS and OSA remain a family of tumors with an unmet need for new and improved immunotherapies. Recent strategies, such as PD-1/programmed death ligand 1 blockade and engineered T-cell receptor therapy targeting MAGEA4, while successful, have delivered benefits to only a small subset of patients with sarcoma.8–10 Here, we show in both human patients and murine models that NK cells are important mediators of tumor control in both bone and soft tissue sarcomas, reinforcing data from other studies that NK cells remain an important target for immunotherapy translation in patients with sarcoma. However, while NK cells are important prognostically in both patients with sarcoma and sarcoma-bearing mice, they are also dysfunctional and strategies to augment their antitumor function are needed.

Importantly, although intratumoral NK cells significantly upregulate TIGIT in patients with STS and OSA and mouse models, and TIGIT-expressing NK cells in the TME are dysfunctional, we have identified that peripheral TIGIT-expressing NK cells are distinct from intratumoral subsets, demonstrating a more activated phenotype, greater cytotoxicity, and resistance to apoptosis. Although immune checkpoints, such as PD-1, have been associated with early activation in addition to immune dysfunction, data illustrating significant differences between increased functionality and dysfunction among peripheral versus intratumoral NK and T cells have not previously been characterized in detail, especially for TIGIT+ NK cells in bone and soft tissue sarcomas.46 Taken together, these data support that TIGIT expression on its own is not an accurate indicator of NK cell dysfunction in patients with sarcoma and must be combined with functional analysis to confirm an exhausted phenotype. These data are supported by other researchers in recent publications showing peripheral TIGIT+ NK cells can exhibit increased functionality towards lung cancer or glioblastoma targets ex vivo compared with TIGIT− NK subsets.29 30

Since TIGIT-blocking antibodies are standardly given intravenously in clinical trials, we examined how TIGIT blockade interacted with peripheral versus tumor-infiltrating TIGIT+ NK cells.23–25 We show that when mice are given systemic TIGIT blockade, NK cells in the TME are reinvigorated and become more cytotoxic, while peripheral NK cells paradoxically become less cytotoxic in both OSA and STS models. These data suggest that TIGIT is correlated with NK cell hyperfunction/dysfunction and is in part driving differences between these NK subsets. Furthermore, we observed that IT treatment increased NK cell CD107a degranulation of intratumoral NK cells without inhibiting peripheral NK cells in OSA mice and simultaneously led to significantly slower tumor growth and improved survival. Taken together, these data suggest that local TIGIT blockade may offer superior antitumor effects when compared with systemic injections in patients with sarcoma. Critically, higher dosing may further improve local TIGIT blockade as our data show a trend toward decreased tumor growth and increased OS when intraperitoneal TIGIT block was administered at a significantly higher concentration, although not to an equivocal magnitude of the lower intratumoral TIGIT block dose. These data also support that, while systemic TIGIT blockade in preclinical models of STS and OSA is not as effective as local treatment, the rate of tumor growth can be affected at certain concentrations. These data are consistent with the findings of other studies demonstrating that systemic TIGIT blockade can decrease the rate of tumor growth and improve OS in certain malignancies.20 25 26 Our data simultaneously reflect other researchers’ work demonstrating that systemic TIGIT blockade preclinical and clinical efficacy can greatly vary depending on the type of cancer.20 25 26

Importantly, our data also suggest that CD155 is an important mediator of TIGIT+ NK cell dysfunction in the sarcoma TME. Analysis of real-world evidence from Caris Life Sciences shows that NK cells are only prognostic for improved OS when CD155 expression is low, while there was no difference in survival when CD155 expression was high. We also observed that higher sCD155 in the blood was associated with worse survival. While TIGIT and the activating surface protein, DNAM-1, both compete for CD155, the binding affinity of TIGIT is predicted to be approximately 100 times stronger than DNAM-1, suggesting that NK cell TIGIT-CD155 binding is heavily favored.27 47 These data suggest that higher CD155 expression may drive the transition from increased functionality to dysfunction among TIGIT-expressing NK cells, especially in the TME where CD155 expression is significantly more prevalent.

Despite these exciting data, it is important to acknowledge limitations of our work. Since TIGIT expresses intracellular ITIM domains, it is expected that TIGIT receptor-ligand binding would lead to downstream inhibitory signals even in a setting where TIGIT+ NK cells have enhanced functionality. However, similar to NKG2A and killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), initial TIGIT binding has been shown to augment NK cell cytotoxicity via contributions to NK cell education.48 49 Frequent TIGIT signaling leads to dysfunction in the TME, but early expression appears to correlate with increased functionality, consistent with our data. It is also important to mention that we did not analyze T cells in our study. We recognize that T cells are a critical effector cell in many cancers and that TIGIT is a key immunoregulatory checkpoint for T cells with important crosstalk with PD-1.22 27 50 51 However, PD-1 blockade and other T cell-mediated therapies are generally less effective in sarcomas, thereby limiting the impact of TIGIT-mediated T cell effects.8–10 Thus, although TIGIT is a key regulator for T cell activation, and combining PD-1 and TIGIT blockade has shown promising results in several clinical trials, T cell biology is significantly different from NK cells, making the effects of peripheral versus intratumoral TIGIT signaling on T cells an important focus for follow-up studies.22 52 53

Critically, intratumoral administration of ICIs for non-disseminated solid tumors has also been used in other preclinical and clinical studies and is currently being investigated as an exciting potential cancer immunotherapeutic strategy in cancers such as breast, head and neck cancer, gynecological malignancies, and B cell lymphomas.43 54 55 In addition to our data demonstrating the effects of local TIGIT blockade on NK cells in sarcoma models, intratumoral administration has been associated with increased penetrance into solid tumors, a higher local concentration of antibody, and decreased on-target/off-tumor effects that are commonly associated with ICIs.56–58 Furthermore, local administration of other immunotherapies has led to immediate access to tumor-draining lymph nodes and lymphoid structures in the TME, which have been associated with improved OS and may be contributing factors in the efficacy of this study.59 60 Despite these exciting data, we recognize there are practical issues with delivering intratumoral TIGIT blockade to patients with sarcoma, and it is unclear if intratumoral TIGIT blockade would translate to improved antitumor effects in patients with metastatic disease.5 8 44

Although these practical questions are critically important to potential clinical translation, our proof-of-concept in vivo results provide exciting data regarding the potential for intratumoral TIGIT blockade to translate to significant sarcoma control, including the potential for complete regression of established tumors in a subset of mice. As TIGIT blockade has thus far disappointed when given systemically in the clinic, our data may partially explain how global blockade of TIGIT affects TIGIT-expressing NK cell populations’ functionality separately in the periphery compared with the TME, leading to confounding clinical results. Further studies are needed to understand how robust these phenomena are, but our data have potentially significant implications for the TIGIT-blocking field.

Finally, we also acknowledge the diversity of bone and soft tissue sarcomas with respect to their TME and immune contexture.5–8 We recognize there are differences in histology with over 100 subtypes of STS alone, in addition to OSA, which can show dramatically separate responses to immunotherapy.5–10 Nevertheless, our data show important commonalities between bone and soft tissue sarcoma across human samples and mouse models with respect to the role of NK cells, including TIGIT-expressing NK cells. Ultimately, further studies are needed to determine the extent to which TIGIT+ NK cell anatomic compartment differences in patients with sarcoma can inform the feasibility of intratumoral TIGIT blockade.5 6 9

Overall, our data highlight an important dichotomy between the increased functionality seen in TIGIT+ NK cells in the periphery of patients with bone and soft tissue sarcoma compared with dysfunctional TIGIT+ NK cells within the TME, leading to divergent responses to systemic TIGIT blockade. Local administration of TIGIT blockade bypassed effects of systemic TIGIT blockade on the enhanced functionality displayed by peripheral NK cells, and the efficacy of systemic blockade may be correlated to the concentration of CD155 in the tumor and/or sCD155 in the blood. Ultimately, we provide proof-of-concept evidence that intratumoral TIGIT blockade translates to superior antisarcoma effects in vivo with potential clinical applications.
