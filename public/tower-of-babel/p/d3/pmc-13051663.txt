The craniopharyngeal canal (CPC) is a rare midline cranial anomaly resulting from incomplete involution of Rathke’s pouch during embryogenesis. During normal development, Rathke’s pouch separates from the stomodeum to form the adenohypophysis, while its residual lumen regresses to form Rathke’s cleft. Failure of this regression process may result in a spectrum of persistent remnants, including Rathke’s cleft cysts (RCCs), ectopic adenohypophyseal tissue, and, in rare cases, a persistent CPC extending through the sphenoid bone toward the nasopharynx.1

Previous studies have demonstrated that the remnants of Rathke’s pouch should be viewed as a developmental continuum rather than as discrete pathological entities.1 Within this framework, partial persistence of the cleft may manifest as RCC, whereas more extensive persistence of the craniopharyngeal duct can give rise to CPC, occasionally forming a complete transsphenoidal canal.1,2 This concept supports the notion that RCC and CPC represent related outcomes of a shared embryological process that differs primarily in the extent and direction of ductal persistence.

Clinically, persistent CPC is associated with a wide range of conditions including CSF leakage, recurrent meningitis, endocrine dysfunction, and midline craniofacial or ocular anomalies.3–7 Imaging-based classifications have further characterized CPC as a spectrum ranging from small incidental bony canals to larger defects containing ectopic adenohypophyseal tissue.3,4 In addition, pathological and fetal anatomical studies have suggested that adenohypophyseal elements may extend along the course of the CPC, reinforcing their interpretation as developmental remnants of Rathke’s pouch.8

Despite these observations, the direct pathological characterization of CPC remnants in adult patients remains limited. It is unclear whether CPC tissues contain differentiated adenohypophyseal endocrine cells representing multiple pituitary lineages, as defined by lineage-specific transcription factors. Although a developmental relationship between CPC and RCC has been proposed on embryological and radiological grounds,1,2 intraoperative endoscopic findings supporting a direct anatomical link between these entities in adult patients have rarely been documented.

This report describes the case of an adult patient with RCC associated with persistent CPC and addresses whether CPC remnants harbor heterogeneous adenohypophyseal endocrine components representing multiple pituitary lineages. By integrating detailed intraoperative endoscopic observations with immunohistochemical analysis using pituitary lineage–specific transcription factors, this report aims to clarify the developmental nature of CPC and its pathological relationship with RCC.

Illustrative Case

A 40-year-old woman with a history of morning glory disc anomaly (MGDA) resulting in a left-sided visual field deficit presented to our hospital with a persistent headache. Ophthalmological examinations, including Goldmann perimetry, revealed a severe visual field deficit in the left eye and enlargement of the blind spot in the right visual field (Fig. 1A). Fundoscopic examination revealed optic disc atrophy in the left eye consistent with MGDA (Fig. 1B).

A: Goldmann perimetry chart demonstrating a severe visual field defect in the left eye and enlargement of the Mariotte blind spot in the right eye. B: Funduscopic examination images revealing marked atrophy of the left retina associated with an MGDA.

MRI demonstrated a cystic sellar lesion that was hyperintense on T1-weighted images and isointense on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2A and B). No contrast-enhancing mural nodules were observed on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images (Fig. 2C and D), which was consistent with RCC. In addition, CT of the skull base revealed a canal-like bony structure extending from the sellar floor to the posterior pharyngeal wall (Fig. 2E), suggesting a persistent remnant of the CPC remnant. The RCC caused upward displacement of the optic chiasm, resulting in newly developed visual field impairment of the right eye. Given the patient’s preexisting severe visual impairment of the left eye due to MGDA and her monocular status, an endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal surgery was performed to relieve chiasmal compression and prevent further deterioration of visual function in the remaining eye.

A and B: Sagittal (A) and coronal (B) MR images demonstrating a T1 hyperintense and T2 isointense cystic sellar lesion. C and D: Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MR images showing enhancement of the cyst wall without a mural nodule, with upward displacement of the optic chiasm (arrows). E and F: CT images revealing a midline canal-like bony structure extending from the floor of the sella turcica to the posterior wall of the nasopharynx (arrows).

Intraoperatively, a bony prominence extending from the sellar floor to the posterior pharyngeal wall was noted (Fig. 3A). After careful drilling of the overlying bone, a cord-like structure extending from the sellar floor dura mater to the posterior pharyngeal soft tissue was clearly visualized (Fig. 3B). The sellar cyst was subsequently opened and drained, yielding thick, whitish mucoid contents (Fig. 3C). Inspection of the cystic cavity using a 70° endoscope revealed a dimple-like depression on the cystic floor at the site corresponding anatomically to the cord-like structure (Fig. 3D). After exposure from behind the clival bone, the cord-like structure was ligated and transected (Fig. 3E and F). No intraoperative CSF leakage was observed (Esposito grade 0).

Intraoperative endoscopic views obtained during endoscopic endonasal surgery. A: The sphenoid sinus was widely opened until the bilateral opticocarotid recesses (asterisks) were clearly identified, revealing a CPC (arrow) extending from the sellar floor toward the posterior pharyngeal wall. B: The bony covering of the canal was carefully drilled away. C: After opening RCC wall, mucoid cyst contents were evacuated. D: Inspection from within the cyst cavity using a 70° endoscope revealed a small dimple-like structure on the cyst wall at the level of the sellar floor (arrow). E and F: The CPC remnant was completely detached from the clival bone, ligated, and excised (arrow).

Histopathological examination of the cyst wall revealed ciliated columnar epithelium with areas of stratified squamous epithelium, consistent with RCC (Fig. 4A). The resected cord-like tissue contained nests of endocrine cells embedded within a fibromuscular stroma (Fig. 4B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed strong PIT-1 positivity with focal expression of SF-1 and T-Pit in these endocrine cells, indicating heterogeneous adenohypophyseal lineages within the CPC remnants (Fig. 4C–E).

Histopathological findings. A:Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrating an RCC wall composed of stratified squamous epithelium with adjacent inflammatory stroma (original magnification ×50). B and C: The excised CPC tissue consisted of a cord-like structure composed of fibromuscular stroma containing centrally located pituitary-like cell nests (original magnification ×25 [B] and ×100 [C]). D–F: Immunohistochemical staining showed that the majority of endocrine cells were positive for PIT-1, with focal positivity for SF-1 and T-Pit, indicating multiple adenohypophyseal lineages (original magnification ×100).

Postoperatively, the patient’s clinical course was uneventful, and she was discharged without complications on postoperative day 10.

Discussion

The present case provides rare immunohistochemical evidence of a persistent CPC containing adenohypophyseal endocrine cell nests that represent multiple pituitary lineages. Anatomical, embryological, and radiological studies have established that the CPC represents a developmental remnant of Rathke’s pouch and may harbor ectopic pituitary tissue as part of a continuous spectrum of sellar developmental anomalies.3 However, detailed pathological characterization of such tissue in adult patients has remained limited, despite the central role of pituitary lineage–specific transcription factors in contemporary pituitary classification systems.9,10

Histologically, the resected canal tissue consisted of a cord-like structure embedded within a fibromuscular stroma containing variably dilated small blood vessels with central collections of endocrine cells arranged in alveolar nests. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated strong PIT-1 expression in a substantial proportion of cells with focal expression of SF-1 and T-Pit in smaller subsets, indicating heterogeneous adenohypophyseal differentiation within the CPC remnant. These findings are consistent with the classic embryological concept wherein remnants of Rathke’s pouch may persist along the craniopharyngeal duct and retain differentiated anterior pituitary elements,1 a notion supported by fetal pathological studies demonstrating adenohypophyseal tissue distributed along the course of the canal.8

According to the imaging-based classification proposed by Abele et al., CPCs can be categorized into three types: small incidental canals without ectopic tissue (type 1), medium-sized canals containing ectopic adenohypophyseal tissue (type 2), and large canals associated with cephaloceles and/or tumors (type 3).3 This classification reflects a spectrum of developmental persistence and has been widely used to correlate canal morphology with associated pathology.3 According to this classification, the present lesion corresponds to a type 1 CPC, which is generally described as lacking pituitary tissue. The identification of pituitary lineage–positive cells within the residual canal tissue in this case suggests that this classification may represent a spectrum of congenital malformations influenced by the developmental stage at which canal regression is arrested. This observation further indicated that CPCs appearing as type 1 on imaging may harbor intrinsically differentiated pituitary tissues.

Beyond pathological characterization, operative findings support a developmental relationship between CPC and associated RCC. Intraoperatively, a bony prominence extending through the sphenoid sinus from the floor of the sella turcica to the posterior pharyngeal wall was directly identified, within which a continuous cord-like structure extended from the sellar floor dura mater to the posterior pharyngeal soft tissues. From an intracystic perspective, endoscopic inspection of the RCC cavity revealed a distinct dimple-like structure on the cyst wall at a site corresponding anatomically to the sellar attachment of the canal. Although direct luminal communication between the cyst cavity and the canal could not be demonstrated, the spatial concordance observed from both the extracystic and intracystic perspectives suggested a close anatomical and developmental relationship between the two lesions. Previous embryological and radiological studies have proposed that RCC and CPCs represent different phenotypic manifestations along the shared developmental continuum of Rathke’s pouch remnants,1,3 and sporadic clinical reports describing their coexistence support this concept.2 In addition, inflammation of the RCC wall has been attributed to epithelial secretions acting as local tissue irritants, leading to secondary epithelial changes and inflammatory reactions;11 in the present case, the presence of pituitary-like residual tissue within the CPC and its close anatomical proximity to the cyst may have contributed to cyst wall inflammation.

Because CPC remnants may traverse the sphenoid sinus and extend to the sellar floor, careful intraoperative identification and appropriate management are required to avoid CSF-related complications, as previously reported.5,7,12 A nasopharyngeal mass considered to be continuous with the CPC was also identified intraoperatively. Such nasopharyngeal lesions related to CPC have been described under various terms in the literature, and the concept of CPC-associated malformations has been proposed to encompass these anomalies.6,13,14 Although the nasopharyngeal mass in our case was not resected and no pathological examination was performed, previous reports have documented both neoplastic lesions and remnants of ectopic pituitary tissue arising along the CPC, suggesting the need for careful long-term follow-up.6 Furthermore, unrecognized or aggressive surgical manipulation of CPC-related structures has been associated with an increased risk of CSF leakage and subsequent meningitis, underscoring the importance of cautious drilling, precise anatomical identification, and avoidance of unnecessary resection.7,12,15,16

MGDA has been reported to be associated with various midline developmental abnormalities, and previous studies have suggested that approximately 50% of patients with MGDA may have an associated persistent CPC. In addition, congenital anomalies such as hypopituitarism, cleft lip and/or palate, and meningoencephalocele have also been described in association with MGDA.6 Although no specific common genetic background has been identified, a shared disturbance in early mesodermal and ectodermal development has been proposed as a possible underlying mechanism.3,6,13 In the present case, preoperative endocrine evaluation revealed no evidence of pituitary dysfunction, and no facial or other midline congenital anomalies were identified. To the best of our knowledge, no previous case reports have described the coexistence of MGDA, persistent CPC, and symptomatic RCC in a single patient.

This study has several limitations. First, it describes a single case, which limits generalizability. Second, although spatial concordance between the CPC remnant and intracystic dimple was demonstrated intraoperatively, direct luminal communication could not be confirmed. Third, although immunohistochemical analysis using pituitary lineage–specific transcription factors enabled the characterization of adenohypophyseal differentiation within the canal remnant, additional analyses, such as hormonal immunostaining or proliferation indices, were not performed. Finally, genetic or molecular investigations were not conducted. Despite these limitations, the present case provides pathological documentation that supports the previously proposed embryological and radiological concepts regarding CPC remnants in adult patients.1,3

In conclusion, immunohistochemical characterization of pituitary lineage markers within a persistent CPC provides pathological support for its developmental origin and suggests that even type 1 CPCs may retain differentiated adenohypophyseal elements.

Observations

This case demonstrated that a persistent CPC classified as type 1 on imaging can contain differentiated adenohypophyseal tissue identifiable by pituitary lineage–specific transcription factors. Intraoperative endoscopic findings further suggested a developmental anatomical relationship between the CPC and the associated RCC.

Lessons

CPC remnants may retain differentiated pituitary elements even in cases without radiological evidence of ectopic tissue. Detailed pathological evaluation can provide important insights into the developmental spectrum of Rathke’s pouch remnants. In addition, careful intraoperative identification of CPC structures and avoidance of unnecessary manipulation are essential to prevent CSF-related complications.
