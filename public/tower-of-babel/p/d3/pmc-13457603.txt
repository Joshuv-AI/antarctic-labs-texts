Predictors and outcomes of hepatic decompensation following transarterial locoregional therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Faisal M Sanai, Ahmed Saati, Hamdan Alghamdi, Saad Aldosari, Adnan Alzanbagi, Ali Albenmousa, Khalid I Bzeizi, Faisal Abaalkhail, Bandar AlJudaibi, Saleh A Alqahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransarterial radioembolization (TARE) and transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) are recognized locoregional therapies (LRTs) for patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). We assessed the predictors of hepatic decompensation (HD) and its impact on overall survival (OS) in patients with uHCC undergoing TACE and TARE.We analyzed patients treated with TARE (n=135) or TACE (n=177) at four tertiary centers. HD was defined as ascites, variceal bleeding or portosystemic encephalopathy at baseline or its occurrence ≤6 months post LRT. Median OS and objective response (per mRECIST) were assessed.The cohort was predominantly male (73.9%), with a mean age of 67.1 ± 10.5 years; most had intermediate-stage (BCLC B, 54.5%), followed by early-stage (BCLC 0/A, 34.0%) and locally advanced-stage disease (BCLC C, 11.5%). HD was present at baseline in 36 patients (11.5%) and occurred after LRT in 48 patients (15.4%). The post-LRT HD group showed lower complete response rates (10.9%) than those without HD (31.4%; P = 0.002). The mOS was longer in patients without HD (41.0 months) than in those with HD at presentation (15.0 months [HR 2.35; 95% CI: 1.37–4.01) or after LRT (14.0 months [HR, 2.66; 95% CI: 1.68–4.26]). Multivariable analyses revealed hepatitis C etiology (OR 0.28, 95% CI: 0.11–0.66, P = 0.006), ALBI score (OR 2.12, 95% CI: 1.38–3.32, P = 0.001) and intervention with TARE (OR 2.20, 95% CI:1. 11–4.44, P = 0.026) as independent predictors of HD post-LRT.Post-LRT HD is common and strongly associated with reduced survival in patients with uHCC. Baseline hepatic reserve and intervention with TARE are crucial factors in predicting post-LRT HD. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1. For a substantial proportion of patients within Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stages A, B, and selected stage C, locoregional therapies (LRTs) such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and transarterial radioembolization (TARE) represent key treatment modalities aimed at achieving tumor control while preserving hepatic function2,3. Contemporary practice guidelines and the updated BCLC recommendations designate TACE as the standard of care for intermediate-stage (BCLC B) HCC, whereas TARE is increasingly used as an alternative in intermediate and locally-advanced disease (BCLC C), particularly in patients being considered for downstaging or bridging to transplantation2–5. These evolving therapeutic roles highlight the need to clarify the comparative indications, outcomes, and selection considerations associated with transarterial approaches across BCLC stages.

Although both TACE and TARE are delivered via the hepatic artery, their mechanisms of action differ in ways that have distinct implications for hepatic tolerance. TACE induces ischemic and cytotoxic injury through embolic occlusion and intra-arterial chemotherapy, whereas TARE delivers high-dose intra-arterial radiation using yttrium-90 (Y90) microspheres, exposing both tumor and non-tumoral parenchyma to ionizing radiation4–6. Efficacy outcomes across cohorts demonstrate heterogeneous survival outcomes associated with different LRTs. In a previous study, TACE was associated with superior survival compared with TARE, particularly in early-stage (BCLC 0/A) disease; however, neither intervention independently predicted survival on multivariable analysis7,8.

The complex interaction between tumor burden, vascular invasion, portal hypertension, and baseline liver function further complicates risk stratification in patients undergoing transarterial therapies. Large multicenter cohorts of patients treated with TACE have highlighted the central role of liver function indices, such as Child–Pugh class and albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) grade, alongside tumor characteristics in determining survival and guiding patient selection9–13. Despite the expanding use of TARE across the BCLC spectrum, important questions remain regarding its hepatic safety profile relative to other LRTs, particularly in patients with marginal hepatic reserve. More recently, dedicated cohorts focusing on hepatic outcomes after these LRTs have demonstrated that post-procedural hepatic decompensation (HD) occurs in a substantial proportion of patients and may portend worse survival14–17.

Many earlier studies have prioritized radiologic response and overall survival as primary endpoints, with liver-related outcomes variably reported or captured as secondary safety signals. As therapeutic strategies evolve, incorporating LRTs in earlier stages, in combination with systemic therapies, or as a bridge to transplantation, understanding predictors of HD, and its association with survival has become increasingly relevant for clinical decision-making. Complementary evidence from multicenter studies has begun to define clinical and biochemical predictors of post-TARE HD and mortality; however, these analyses have largely focused on patients treated with TARE alone and have not systematically compared hepatic and survival outcomes with other transarterial modalities within a broad, real-world HCC population14,15.

In addition to tumor burden and vascular invasion, preservation of hepatic reserve is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of treatment tolerance and survival following locoregional therapy. The ALBI grade has emerged as an objective and reproducible tool for stratifying liver function and predicting outcomes across multiple HCC treatment modalities, including TACE and TARE. Nevertheless, despite growing recognition of the prognostic importance of hepatic reserve, predictors of post-treatment HD remain insufficiently characterized in real-world populations undergoing transarterial therapies18,19.

We therefore conducted this multicenter retrospective study to determine (1) the incidence of HD following TACE and TARE, (2) independent clinical and biochemical predictors of post-LRT HD, and (3) the prognostic impact of decompensation on overall survival in patients with HCC.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, multicenter observational cohort study was conducted across four tertiary referral centers in Saudi Arabia. Consecutive adult patients with unresectable HCC (uHCC) who underwent transarterial LRT were identified from institutional databases at each participating center. Patients treated between January 2016 and May 2024 were eligible for inclusion if they received TACE or TARE as part of routine clinical care. Treatment decisions, including modality selection, were made by institutional multidisciplinary tumor boards in accordance with prevailing clinical guidelines and local expertise. The study was designed to reflect real-world clinical practice, encompassing a broad spectrum of tumor burden, liver function, and disease stage. Data was collected retrospectively from electronic medical records, imaging archives, and procedural reports using a standardized data collection framework used across participating centers.

Study population

Adult patients with uHCC treated with transarterial LRT were eligible for inclusion. Patients were consecutively enrolled if they met all of the following criteria: (1) radiologic or histologic confirmation of HCC according to accepted diagnostic standards; (2) classification as BCLC stage 0, A, B, or C; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0–2; (4) preserved hepatic reserve defined as Child–Pugh class A or class B (score of ≤ 9, at the discretion of the treating multidisciplinary team; (5) deemed ineligible for curative therapies including surgical resection or percutaneous ablation based on tumor characteristics, hepatic function, or comorbid conditions; (6) presence of at least one measurable tumor lesion assessed by contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) according to modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST); and (7) receipt of treatment with either TACE or TARE during the study period. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) receipt of concurrent locoregional or curative therapies at the time of transarterial treatment, including surgical resection, percutaneous ablation, or transarterial bland embolization; (2) incomplete or missing clinical data precluding reliable assessment of outcomes; (3) previous receipt of stereotactic body radiation therapy; (4) advanced disease defined as BCLC stage D; (5) presence of extrahepatic metastatic disease at baseline; (6) previous or concurrent malignancies distinct from HCC; (7) advanced chronic kidney disease; (8) Child–Pugh class C liver status; (9) high-grade hepatic encephalopathy at baseline; (10) prior liver transplantation; or (11) human immunodeficiency virus infection.

Treatment protocols

TACE was performed using either conventional TACE (c-TACE) or drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE), according to operator discretion and institutional protocols at each participating center. For c-TACE, procedures involved super-selective catheterization of tumor-feeding hepatic arteries, followed by intra-arterial infusion of an emulsion containing 5–30 mg of doxorubicin (Actavis, Dublin, Ireland). For DEB-TACE, 100–300 μm drug-eluting microspheres loaded with 75 mg of doxorubicin were administered to achieve targeted tumor embolization. All TACE procedures were performed with the intent of maximizing tumor devascularization while preserving uninvolved hepatic parenchyma.

TARE was performed using Y90 microspheres, including both glass microspheres (TheraSphere; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) and resin microspheres (SIR-Spheres; SIRTex, Woburn, MA, USA). Treatment planning and delivery were conducted in accordance with institutional standards, following angiographic mapping and assessment of vascular anatomy. TARE was delivered either in a super-selective fashion (radiation segmentectomy) with curative intent or via non-selective lobar infusion with palliative intent, depending on tumor distribution and clinical context.

Data collection and variables

Clinical data were retrospectively abstracted from electronic medical records by trained investigators at each participating center using a standardized data extraction form. Collected variables included demographic characteristics, baseline laboratory parameters, and relevant clinical data, including baseline performance status assessed by the ECOG scale, and treatment-related characteristics. Radiologic and procedural data were also systematically recorded. Tumor-related variables of prognostic relevance included maximum tumor diameter, presence of a lesion larger than 5 cm, number of tumor lesions, lobar distribution, and fulfillment of the up-to-seven criteria, defined as the sum of the number of tumors and the diameter of the largest lesion being ≤ 7. HCC stage was assigned according to the BCLC staging system.

Treatment allocation to either TACE or TARE was determined through consensus-based decision-making during regularly scheduled multidisciplinary tumor board meetings, with representation from medical oncology, hepatology, transplant or hepatobiliary surgery, and interventional radiology. To ensure data accuracy, lead investigators at each site independently reviewed the extracted data and resolved discrepancies prior to analysis.

Definition of hepatic decompensation

HD at baseline was defined as the presence of clinically significant complications of portal hypertension including variceal bleeding, ascites, or hepatic encephalopathy at any point in the period preceding the LRT. Ascites presence was confirmed by imaging studies (such as ultrasonography, CT or MRI) and graded based on the International Ascites Club consensus grading system (Grade 1: Mild ascites only detectable by ultrasound, Grade 2: Moderate ascites evident by moderate symmetrical distension of abdomen, and Grade 3: Large or gross ascites with marked abdominal distension)20. Hepatic encephalopathy (West Haven grade ≥1) was diagnosed on a clinical basis. Variceal bleeding was defined as development of gastrointestinal bleeding, confirmed endoscopically as being related to gastroesophageal varices. HD following LRT was defined as the new-onset of these hepatic events occurring within six months following LRT.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the initial transarterial treatment to death from any cause or to the last available follow-up, with HD serving as the primary exposure of interest. Secondary outcomes included tumor response, assessed according to mRECIST. Treatment response and the need for retreatment were assessed using contrast-enhanced multiphase CT or MRI performed at 8–12 weeks following treatment during the first year, and subsequently at 12–24-week intervals thereafter. Complete treatment was defined as embolization of all tumor lesions identified on baseline imaging. In patients with multifocal bilobar disease, a staged approach was employed, whereby one hepatic lobe (right or left) was treated initially, followed by embolization of the contralateral lobe during a subsequent session to complete therapy. All imaging studies were reviewed by experienced body radiologists, and treatment response was categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) based on changes in tumor enhancement and size. Treatment was repeated according to institutional practice and clinical judgment, with a maximum of three sessions for TACE and two sessions for TARE, or until the occurrence of unacceptable toxicity, radiological disease progression, or treatment futility, as determined by the treating physician. Safety outcomes, including all-cause and treatment-related adverse events, HD and hospitalizations, were evaluated for up to six months following the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution and normality assumptions. Median OS (mOS) was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with survival time defined from the date of initial transarterial therapy to death or last follow-up. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank test and were stratified by HD status. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To explore factors associated with post-LRT HD, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between each clinical and laboratory variable and the occurrence of HD. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis and those considered clinically relevant were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported. A forest plot was used to visualize the adjusted effect estimates. Model performance and internal validity were assessed using the concordance index (C-index) and information criteria, including the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort

Between January 2016 and May 2024, a total of 378 patients with uHCC were screened across the four participating centers, and 312 patients were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure 1), with a median follow-up duration of 24.0 months (IQR: 11–42). The mean age of the cohort was 67.1 ± 10.1 years, and 228 patients (73.1%) were male. Median alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was 12.0 ng/mL; 53 patients (17.2%) had AFP ≥ 400 ng/mL. The mean ALBI score was −2.3 (IQR: −2.6 to −1.7); 28.8% were ALBI grade 1, 57.7% grade 2, and 13.5% grade 3. Hepatitis C (33.0%) and hepatitis B (30.8%) were the most common etiologies of liver disease, followed by metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (24.0%). Most patients had ECOG performance status 0–1 (87.8%). Portal vein thrombosis (PVT) was present in 54 (17.3%) patients, 12 (33.3%) of whom had HD at baseline. The median number of tumor lesions was 1.0 (IQR 1.0 – 2.0), and the median diameter of the largest lesion was 4.0 cm (2.5 – 6.1); 33.1% of patients had tumors > 5 cm. Overall, 69.5% were within the up-to-seven criteria. Regarding tumor stage, 54.5% were classified as BCLC stage B, 34.0% as stage 0/A and 11.5% as stage C. Overall, 177 patients (56.7%) underwent TACE and 135 patients (43.3%) received TARE.

Hepatic decompensation and outcomes

HD was present in 36 patients (11.5%) at baseline, and occurred in an additional 48 patients (15.4%) following LRT; 117 (37.5%) were alive at last follow-up and 195 (62.5%) had died. By May 2024, the median follow-up duration of the cohort without HD was 27.5 months (IQR: 14.0–46.8), 13.0 months (7.0–27.5) for those with baseline HD, and 14.0 months (7.3–25.0) for those with post-LRT HD.

By cohort definition, patients with baseline HD had worse baseline liver biochemistry including higher aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, and international normalized ratio (INR), as well as lower albumin and platelet levels compared to the non-decompensated group (all P < 0.05). Markers of hepatic reserve differed significantly between groups, with patients having HD exhibiting worse median ALBI scores (−1.8 vs. −2.3, P < 0.001), and a higher proportion of PVT (33.3% vs. 15.2%, P = 0.007). Patient demographics, disease etiology, tumor-related characteristics, ECOG status and AFP levels were similar between cohorts (Table 1).

Treatment modality was similar between patients without HD at baseline and those who remained compensated post-LRT (TARE, 43.1% vs. 39.0%); however, treatment distribution differed significantly in patients who developed post-LRT HD, with TARE used more frequently in this group compared with those who did not develop HD (62.5% vs. 39.0%, P = 0.004). Radiologic outcomes also differed between groups; patients with post-LRT HD had higher rates of PD compared with those who did not develop HD (30.4% vs. 16.6%), with an overall significant difference in response distribution (P = 0.009). Consequently, disease control was achieved less frequently in the decompensated cohort compared with the non-decompensated one (69.6% vs. 83.4%, P = 0.038). Neither baseline alanine aminotransferase (ALT) nor elevation in ALT post LRT were significantly different between the groups (Table 3). Similarly, treatment modality did not differ between patients with HD at baseline and those with post-LRT HD (Supplementary Table 2). Rates of CR were higher in patients with HD at baseline than those with post-LRT HD (39.4% vs. 10.9%, P = 0.003), while disease control rates remained similar (63.6% vs. 69.6%, P = 0.632).

Predictors of hepatic decompensation

In univariate logistic regression analysis, BCLC stage B and C, AST level, serum albumin, up-to-seven criteria, treatment modality, ALT elevation post LRT, treatment response, and ALBI score were significantly associated with post-LRT HD (all P < 0.05). In addition, HCV etiology was associated with a lower risk of post-LRT HD compared with hepatitis B virus etiology. In multivariable analysis, HCV etiology (OR 0.28, 95% CI: 0.11–0.66, P = 0.006), TARE treatment (OR 2.20, 95% CI: 1.11–4.44, P = 0.026), and ALBI score (OR 2.12, 95% CI: 1.38–3.32, P = 0.001) remained independently associated with post-LRT HD (Fig. 3). Other variables lost statistical significance after adjustment. The multivariable logistic regression model demonstrated good discrimination, with a C-index of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.66–0.82). The AIC and BIC values were 238.4 and 260.1, respectively. The Brier score was 0.13, indicating good overall predictive accuracy.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of patients with HCC undergoing transarterial therapies, we observed that post-LRT HD was common and carried profound prognostic implications. It developed in approximately one in six patients and was associated with a more than two-fold increase in mortality compared with patients who remained compensated. Importantly, patients treated with TARE were more likely to experience post-LRT HD, and TARE remained independently associated with decompensation, even after adjustment for liver function, tumor characteristics, and burden. These findings highlight that liver failure, not tumor progression, often becomes the dominant determinant of outcome in this population.

Our results complement prior reports demonstrating that radiation-induced parenchymal injury after TARE may precipitate non-classic radiation-induced liver disease (RILD), which results primarily from injury to hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, leading to sinusoidal obstruction, microvascular congestion, and impaired hepatic outflow, rather than direct hepatocyte necrosis21. This endothelial damage triggers hepatic venous outflow obstruction, parenchymal hypoxia, and progressive fibrosis, ultimately manifesting clinically as ascites, jaundice, and liver failure in the absence of tumor progression22. Earlier studies have linked this risk to impaired baseline liver function, advanced portal hypertension, and extensive treated liver volume14,15,22. However, many earlier analyses assessed TARE in isolation or reported hepatic outcomes as secondary endpoints. By directly comparing outcomes across transarterial modalities, our study shows that clinically meaningful HD occurs more frequently following TARE than TACE, underscoring the need to weigh hepatic safety as carefully as tumor control when selecting therapy. In our previous analysis, we have shown that survival was significantly shorter in patients treated with TARE7. Nonetheless, TARE did not serve as a predictor of OS, where variables such as up-to-seven criteria (aHR: 2.21, 95% CI: 1.17–4.17), BCLC stage B (aHR: 2.20, 95% CI: 1.27–3.83), and post-intervention HD (aHR: 3.29, 95% CI: 1.52–7.10), and treatment response (aHR: 2.78, 95% CI: 1.30–5.94) predicted reduced survival8. However, in this analysis, we have shown that patients with HD have reduced survival, and the association between TARE and HD, suggests that the reduced survival observed with TARE may be mediated through TARE-associated RILD causing HD, in turn potentially limiting any further treatment.

The mechanistic basis for this observation likely reflects fundamental differences between the two modalities. TACE induces localized ischemic injury that may spare uninvolved parenchyma when delivered super-selectively23. In contrast, TARE delivers diffuse segmental radiation exposure, which may potentially exceed hepatic tolerance thresholds, especially in patients with impaired liver function24. In patients with underlying cirrhosis, reduced hepatic reserve and pre-existing portal hypertension markedly lower radiation tolerance, rendering even segmental or lobar radiation exposure sufficient to precipitate clinically significant decompensation25.

Another marker of hepatic reserve is the ALBI grade, which provides an objective and continuous measure of hepatic reserve derived solely from albumin and bilirubin levels. It has been validated as a prognostic liver function tool in HCC18,19. In patients undergoing TACE, higher (worse) ALBI grade has been associated with significantly worse OS, supporting its utility for risk stratification even among individuals considered to have “preserved” liver function26,27. Similarly, in patients treated with TARE, ALBI grade has demonstrated clear survival discrimination and can outperform Child–Pugh class in prognosticating OS, particularly within Child–Pugh A subgroups28. Taken together, these data support incorporating ALBI into pre-procedural risk assessment to better anticipate survival outcomes following TACE and TARE. Consistent with prior literature, we found that ALBI score is a strong independent predictor of post-LRT HD, reinforcing that treatment selection must be guided not only by tumor characteristics but also by the underlying liver substrate.

Interestingly, HCV etiology was independently associated with a lower risk of post-LRT HD in multivariable analysis. One potential explanation for this finding relates to the profound therapeutic impact of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy in HCV-related cirrhosis. In our cohort, all patients with HCV infection had achieved sustained virologic response (SVR) prior to study enrollment, and the majority had been treated during the DAA era. SVR following DAA treatment has been associated with reductions in hepatic inflammation, improvement in portal hypertension, partial regression of fibrosis, and recovery of hepatic synthetic function in a subset of patients, which may collectively enhance tolerance to LRTs. Several studies have demonstrated that successful HCV eradication can reduce hepatic venous pressure gradients and lower the long-term risk of decompensation, although these benefits are most pronounced in patients without advanced irreversible architectural distortion29,30. Nevertheless, fibrosis regression after viral cure remains heterogeneous and often incomplete, with persistent microvascular remodeling, clinically significant portal hypertension and development of HCC still observed in many compensated cirrhosis patients despite SVR31–33. Importantly, detailed antiviral treatment histories, timing of SVR, and longitudinal fibrosis assessments were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort, limiting mechanistic interpretation of this finding. Therefore, the apparent protective association observed in our cohort should be interpreted cautiously and may additionally reflect residual confounding, selection bias, or immortal time bias related to treatment-era differences and patient selection34. Further prospective studies incorporating portal hypertension metrics and antiviral treatment history are needed to better clarify the interaction between HCV biology, hepatic reserve, and post-LRT outcomes.

Finally, TARE (as opposed to TACE) independently predicted post-LRT HD. While radioembolization remains valuable in settings such as PVT, where studies have highlighted the safety and efficacy in patients with segmental or lobar portal vein invasion35, our findings indicate that its use in patients with marginal hepatic function should be approached conservatively. While overall PVT was more frequent in patients with HD prior to the intervention, this did not influence post-LRT HD. On the other hand, tumoral PVT was more frequent in patients with post-LRT HD. However, neither PVT or locally-advanced tumor predicted post-intervention HD in multivariable analysis. Additionally, it is important to note that radiological disease progression was also more frequent in patients with HD. While it is feasible that disease progression may have precluded further interventions and contributed to reduced survival, it remains difficult to retrospectively ascertain if the disease progression contributed to the decompensation, or if it occurred as a direct consequence of Y90-related hepatic toxicity. Nevertheless, practical strategies may include tighter eligibility thresholds, selective or reduced-dose approaches, and closer post-treatment surveillance when TARE is chosen.

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the rapidly changing landscape of HCC management. Although the present study examined conventionally recognized prognostic determinants, emerging evidence has identified novel markers, such as increased lymphatic vessel density, as predictors of post-resection outcomes, supporting the use of adjunctive biologic and histopathologic tools to refine individualized treatment strategies36. In parallel, therapeutic approaches for HCC have expanded considerably in recent years. Whereas management of intermediate-stage disease has traditionally centered on TACE or TARE, more recent studies evaluating the combination of LRT with immune checkpoint inhibitors have demonstrated improved outcomes compared with LRT alone37. Overall, these developments underscore the increasing need for personalized treatment selection informed by prognostic stratification, rather than reliance solely on conventional BCLC-based treatment allocation.

This study has important strengths, including its multicenter design, real-world inclusion across BCLC stages, and explicit integration of hepatic outcomes with survival. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding, particularly with respect to treatment allocation, which was determined by multidisciplinary tumor board decisions rather than randomization. Although multivariable modeling was employed to adjust for clinically relevant covariates, residual confounding, especially related to subtle differences in hepatic reserve, portal hypertension severity, or institutional practice patterns, cannot be fully excluded. Second, despite the multicenter nature of the cohort, heterogeneity in treatment protocols across participating centers may have influenced outcomes. Variability in embolization technique (degree of selectivity), local operator expertise, dosimetry approaches for TARE, retreatment thresholds, and post-procedural monitoring could not be completely standardized and were not consistently available, and may have affected the risk of HD. In particular, detailed radiation dosimetry parameters (such as non-tumoral liver absorbed dose and treated liver volume) were not uniformly available, limiting more granular assessment of dose–toxicity relationships, and therefore could not be incorporated into the multivariable analysis. Additionally, direct standardized measures of portal hypertension severity were limited. These factors may have contributed to residual confounding. Third, although all patients with HCV infection had achieved SVR prior to enrollment, detailed antiviral treatment data, including treatment regimen, timing of SVR, and interval between antiviral therapy and LRT, were not consistently available. Longitudinal fibrosis assessments following antiviral therapy were also not uniformly performed. Consequently, the potential influence of fibrosis regression, residual portal hypertension, treatment-era effects, and immortal time bias on the observed association between HCV etiology and reduced risk of post-LRT HD could not be fully evaluated. Fourth, the study period spanned a decade during which the therapeutic landscape of HCC evolved substantially, including the introduction and increasing use of immunotherapy-based systemic regimens. These advances may have influenced post-progression management, hepatic tolerance, and overall survival outcomes, particularly in patients treated during the later years of the cohort. Since subsequent systemic therapies were not modeled as time-dependent variables, their potential impact on long-term outcomes cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, while HD was defined using clinically meaningful endpoints, subclinical portal hypertension markers (such as hepatic venous pressure gradient measurements or spleen stiffness) were not explicitly captured. As a result, the study may underestimate the contribution of baseline portal hypertension severity to post-LRT decompensation risk. Similarly, dynamic changes in liver function over time, beyond distinct decompensation events, were not fully characterized. Sixth, although the cohort included patients across BCLC stages and liver function strata, sample sizes within certain subgroups, particularly patients with (locally advanced) BCLC stage C disease or decompensated cirrhosis at baseline, were relatively small. This may limit statistical power for subgroup analyses and restrict the generalizability of findings to patients with more advanced disease. Seventh, given that treatment allocation was not randomized, baseline differences between TARE and TACE groups may have introduced residual selection bias. This aspect could have been potentially overcome with propensity score matching. However, we did not perform propensity score matching as this study was not designed as a comparative effectiveness analysis, and performing matching could substantially reduce sample size and statistical power for the primary outcome of identifying predictors of HD.

Despite these limitations, the study provides real-world evidence that post-LRT HD, particularly following TARE, is a critical determinant of prognosis and it highlights clinically actionable predictors that may inform patient selection and risk stratification. Prospective studies incorporating standardized dosimetry, objective portal hypertension assessments, and longitudinal liver function metrics are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

HD after transarterial LRT is common and associated with significant reduction in OS. Post-LRT HD occurred more frequently in patients treated with TARE compared to those who received TACE. Baseline indicators of hepatic reserve, such as ALBI score, along with treatment modality and HCV etiology, independently predicted the risk of decompensation. These findings underscore that treatment-related hepatic injury may outweigh tumor-related benefits when hepatic reserve is marginal. When considering LRTs, particularly in patients with impaired liver function, individualized risk assessment and judicious treatment selection are essential. Future prospective studies should refine risk-stratification tools and evaluate strategies to mitigate hepatic toxicity, ensuring that LRT maximizes tumor control while preserving liver function and survival.
