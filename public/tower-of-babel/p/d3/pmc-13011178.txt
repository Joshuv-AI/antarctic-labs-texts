Quantification of CO2 and NH3 emission by the entomopathogenic nematode, Heterorhabditis indica in heterogenous tomato rhizosphere infested with root knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita
Authors: Jeevan H, Asik Dutta, Chris John, Shilpa, TJ Purakayastha, Sharad Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoils generate numerous volatile gases, primarily carbon dioxide (CO₂) and ammonia (NH₃), as byproducts of microbial metabolism, organic matter decomposition, and biochemical processes. CO₂ mainly originates from plant root, microbial, and nematode respiration, while microbial and nematode excretions contribute significantly to NH₃. Elevated CO₂ and NH₃ levels disrupt the host-finding mechanism of plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN) by altering chemical cues from plant roots, reducing invasion and suppressing populations. High concentrations can also directly impair nematode physiology, leading to mortality.This study estimated CO₂ and NH₃ emissions under increasing concentrations of Heterorhabditis indica, an entomopathogenic nematode (EPN) applied in large numbers for pest management. Based on our hypothesis that high EPN concentrations suppress PPN invasion, we introduced varying H. indica levels into airtight pots containing tomato plants in unsterile field soil. Volatiles were collected in vials with boric acid and sodium hydroxide and quantified via titration. Unlike previous protocols, our setup included live plants, improving accuracy by accounting for root exudate influence on rhizosphere activity. This method provides a robust approach to estimating soil volatiles in heterogeneous environments, addressing key challenges in soil ecology.Simulates a natural rhizosphere for studying field-like microbial interactionsEstimates CO₂ and NH₃ emissions in soil with H. indicaCost-effective method for measuring CO₂ and NH₃ in a controlled setup with live plantsKeywords: Volatile compounds, Entomopathogenic nematodes, H. indica, Soil rhizosphere, CO2, NH3

Background

The decomposition of organic matter in soil by microbes is primarily governed by the carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio. A higher C:N ratio slows microbial decomposition as microbes require additional nitrogen to break down carbon-rich substrates, whereas a lower C:N ratio accelerates decomposition. Soil microbes are key drivers of nutrient cycling, contributing to the release of carbon and nitrogen compounds through the breakdown of organic matter. In this study, we focus on the role of soil nematodes, particularly plant-parasitic nematodes (PPN) and entomopathogenic nematodes (EPN), in soil gas emissions. These nematodes primarily release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and ammonia (NH₃) through cuticular diffusion and excretion. The PPN locate and invade plant roots using chemical cues from root exudates, feeding on plant tissues and causing substantial economic losses in agriculture. Conversely, EPN play a beneficial role in the rhizosphere as they naturally infect and kill soil-dwelling insect pests, recycling within their cadavers to persist in the environment. As commercial biocontrol agents, EPNs are typically applied in large quantities, often reaching 2.5 billion nematodes per hectare, for effective pest suppression. Given their high population densities in treated soils, EPN activity significantly increases CO₂ and NH₃ emissions, which can potentially impact PPN survival, host-finding efficiency, and overall soil microbial dynamics. Additionally, plant root exudates and rhizosphere microbial communities further influence the levels of these volatiles, necessitating a holistic approach to measuring their fluxes. Advanced techniques such as Infrared Gas Analysis (IRGA), Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (PAS), and Tunable Diode Laser Spectroscopy (TDLS) offer highly precise, real-time quantification of gaseous emissions. However, their application in controlled pot experiments, where individual plants are subjected to various treatments with replications, presents significant logistical and financial challenges. Implementing these sophisticated instruments across multiple experimental units requires specialized gas probes, complex instrumentation, and continuous monitoring, making them prohibitively expensive for routine studies. Given these limitations, we have developed a cost-effective, moderately accurate alternative using chemical trapping and titrimetric quantification of CO₂ and NH₃. Although this method does not match the precision of high-end analytical instruments, it provides reliable, long-term insights into gaseous fluxes, allowing for the detection of overall trends. By employing selective chemical absorbers and standardized titration protocols, this approach offers a practical yet scientifically robust solution for investigating soil-plant-atmosphere interactions in controlled environments. Traditionally, soil gas estimation methods, such as the Ausmus & O’Neill [1] protocol, rely on airtight glass jars to trap volatiles, but these systems do not accommodate live plants, limiting their ecological relevance. In this study, we have redesigned the experimental setup to include a live plant within an airtight pot, thereby capturing the interactive effects of plant root exudates, rhizosphere microbes, and nematode activity on CO₂ and NH₃ emissions. This novel approach enhances the accuracy of volatile quantification, providing a more ecologically relevant assessment of soil gas dynamics (Fig. 1).

Description of protocol

Preparing the pots: Pots (10 × 9.5 × 8.5 cm – upper diameter x height x base diameter) having a capacity of 250 g were filled with 150 g of soil (pH 7.93, EC 1.23, sand/silt/clay % = 66.2:19.8:15.2), leaving 3–4 cm space above the soil surface (Fig. 2A). Space above the soil surface is important to assist proper aeriation for the root system and provide enough space for accommodation of the gases to be trapped for titration. To keep the soil moist, light watering of the plants was done at every three days interval (i.e. on days 4,7,10,13) from the start of the experiment. The pots were placed in a growth facility room maintained at 28 °C with 12 h alternate light and dark periods.

Stepwise procedure for preparing an airtight closed chamber for trapping CO2 and NH3. A- Pot filling with field soil; B-Preparation of transparency plastic cover; C-Wrapping pot rim with parafilm tape; d-Inserting empty vials into the pot; E-Insertion of plant through the hole ‘P’ in the transparency cover; F-Transplant the plant and allow the seedling to establish without any sealing; G-Seal the transparency cover by wrapping additional layers of parafilm tape around the pot rim; H-Seal with 2 rounds of BOPP tape and fill the hole around the stem with non-absorbent cotton, and cover it with a layer of parafilm; I-Add chemical traps in to the vials followed by sealing the ‘L’-shaped slits using BOPP tape; J-Air tight closed chamber for trapping CO2 and NH3.

Raising tomato seedlings: Tomato seedlings (cv Pusa Ruby) were raised in sterilized soil-sand mixture in 3:1 ratio at 28 °C. Healthy 21 days seedlings were selected for the experiment.

Preparing transparent plastic covers: To cover the pot, transparency sheet of 0.1 mm thickness was cut measuring 11 cm diameter (may vary as per the diameter of the pot). Three opening were provided in the cover. A hole was made in the center ‘P’ to insert the seedling. The diameter of the hole matched with the diameter of the plant stem. Two ‘L’ shaped slits were made in the two sub-polar ends to operate as an ‘open and close lid’ to insert and take out the vials during the observation without disturbing the plant (Fig. 2B).

Wrapping pot rim with parafilm tape: Parafilm was wrapped tightly around the pot rim, covering small gaps and irregularities to smoothen the surface and to create an adhesive surface for better fixing of the transparent cover, thereby preventing the volatiles leakage. (Fig. 2C).

Inserting the vials: Two empty 5 mL glass vials were placed inside the pot with 3/4th of their length embedded in the soil and 1/4th exposed above the surface. The vials were gently rotated clockwise and anticlockwise 2–3 times to loosen the surrounding soil, creating a proper cavity for easy insertion and removal without disturbing the plant or soil structure. This positioning was designed to maximize the diffusion of volatiles into the chemical traps (Fig. 2D). Under airtight conditions, the emitted volatiles remain confined within the pot, accumulating near the soil surface rather than circulating freely. By strategically placing the vials at the specified depth, we ensured optimal capture efficiency, allowing for effective quantification of CO₂ and NH₃ emissions.

Note: while removing the vials from the pots for titration care should be taken, not to disturb the plant root system, by gently rotating the vials in clock and anti-clock directions, on the axis, and then lifting it slowly.

Transplanting tomato seedlings: Seedlings were transplanted into the pot, firstly, by gently inserting the root system though the hole made in the center of the transparency sheet cover (Fig. 2E). Secondly, the roots were placed in the center of the pot and covered with the soil (Fig. 2F). The pots were left overnight or till the seedlings got established.

Sealing the pots: Once the plant was established in the pot, to ensure airtight conditions inside the pot and prevent any gaseous exchange between the internal and external environments, a systematic sealing process was followed. Transparency cover adjustments were made to align the l-shaped slits directly above the two vials placed inside the pot. This was followed by wrapping additional two layers of Parafilm tape to secure the transparency cover in place (Fig. 2G). To protect the seedling and further reinforce the sealing, the area where the stem emerged through the sheet (‘P’) was wrapped with non-absorbent cotton, followed by a layer of Parafilm (Fig. 2H). This not only prevented air leakage but also minimized friction between the stem and the plastic edges, reducing the risk of mechanical injury.

Finally, two rounds of 1-inch BOPP transparent tape were applied over the Parafilm to ensure a robust and airtight seal (Fig. 2H). Just before sealing the two l-shaped openings, 4 mL of 0.5 N NaOH and 4 mL of 2 % boric acid were separately added to two vials. Immediately after adding the chemicals, the l-shaped openings were sealed by applying a 1-inch BOPP transparent tape to the outer surface (Fig. 2I), ensuring that no air bubbles were trapped beneath the tape.

Titration: The titration for CO2 and NH3 estimation was carried out at 24 h interval for 10 days. On each day, the vials were carefully removed from the pot by opening the ‘L’ shaped slits and replaced with fresh set of vials containing 4 mL of 0.5 N NaOH and 2 % boric acid. The slits were immediately sealed with BOPP transparent tape. The entire process was carried out swiftly in minimum possible time. Two 100 mL burette were filled with 0.5 N HCl (to titrate against the NaOH for estimating trapped CO2) and 0.005 N H2SO4 (to titrate against the 2 % boric acid for estimating trapped NH3), respectively.

CO2 estimation: The NaOH from the vial was transferred in to 100 mL conical flask and the vials were rinsed once with distilled water. 2–3 drops of saturated BaCl2 were added resulting in a milky white solution. Further, 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator were added which turned the solution pink. The solution was titrated by slowly releasing 0.5 N HCl till the pink color turned back to milky white. The titration was stopped immediately and the reading was recorded by considering the lower meniscus.

NH3 estimation: The H3BO3 from the vial was transferred into 100 mL conical flask. The titration was started against 0.005 N H2SO4 till the purplish green solution turned into wine red color.

Protocol validation

We refined our methodology by drawing insights from several key studies. Chakraborty et al. [4] conducted laboratory incubation experiments to quantify NH3 emission from nitrogenous fertilizers applied to soil in a 10-year residue management field experiment within a rice-wheat cropping system. Parkin et al. [5] developed a sampling protocol for measuring N₂O, CO₂, and CH₄ fluxes using the soil chamber method. Rao et al. [6] provided a detailed illustration of the titrimetric method for estimating CO₂ and NH₃, while Purakayastha and Chhonkar [3] evaluated NH₃ volatilization from urea-micronutrient fertilizers. Building on these works, we developed a protocol, for the first time, that involves collecting CO₂ and NH₃ volatiles using chemical traps in a sealed chamber (closed pot) with a live plant, followed by quantification through titration. Limitations

1.Wilting symptoms in plants can often be attributed to excessive watering, which disrupts the balance of air and water in the soil, leading to root stress and reduced oxygen availability. To mitigate this issue, soil moisture should be carefully managed and maintained slightly below field capacity to ensure optimal plant health.

Wilting symptoms in plants can often be attributed to excessive watering, which disrupts the balance of air and water in the soil, leading to root stress and reduced oxygen availability. To mitigate this issue, soil moisture should be carefully managed and maintained slightly below field capacity to ensure optimal plant health.

2.In case the boric acid vials appear greenish-blue instead of the expected reddish-purple, it may indicate the presence of a contaminant acid that has altered the pH. To prevent such inconsistencies, acids and vials should be handled with precision, ensuring that all materials remain uncontaminated.

In case the boric acid vials appear greenish-blue instead of the expected reddish-purple, it may indicate the presence of a contaminant acid that has altered the pH. 3.This efficiency of this method is not at par with the highly sophisticated and expensive instruments like Infrared Gas Analyzers (IRGA), Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (PAS), and Tunable Diode Laser Spectroscopy (TDLS).
