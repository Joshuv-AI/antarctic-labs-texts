Genetic diversity of Tomistoma schlegelii in Malaysia using microsatellite and mitochondrial DNA markers
Authors: Taranjeet Kaur Awtar Singh, Gideon Khoo, Khairul Nizam Abdul Maulud, Khairul Amirin Mohamed, Alan Han Kiat Ong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rising incidence of human–crocodile conflict involving the endangered Sunda gharial, Tomistoma schlegelii, has led to a growing number of wild-caught individuals being placed in conservation centres and zoos. But the genetic diversity and coancestry of the captive individuals is unknown which could result in an increased inbreeding or outbreeding, defeating the purpose of ex situ conservation efforts. Therefore, this study analysed the genetic diversity, population structure and identification of important clusters for future breeding programs. Blood samples from 38 individuals were analysed using 14 species-specific microsatellite loci and the partial mitochondrial DNA sequences of ND6–tRNAGlu–cyt b region and control region. The STRUCTURE results revealed six clusters displaying low nuclear gene diversity, with mtDNA diversity noted in only one cluster. Two important clusters were identified to facilitate future breeding programs of this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17481-5.Keywords: Endangered species, Coancestry, Breeding program, Ex situ conservation, Population structureSubject terms: Genetics, Zoology

Introduction

Maintaining genetic diversity is crucial to enable a genetic response to ecological variability, particularly in endangered species1–3. Various genetic markers have been developed for animals including crocodilians to facilitate this purpose4–12. Among these, short tandem repeats (STRs), also known as microsatellites, are popular genetic markers in population studies due to their high mutation rate (ranging from 10−6−10−2 per generation) and ease of detection via electrophoresis13–15. Mitochondrial markers are also popular for intraspecific variation because of their abundance, easy isolation, maternal inheritance, lack of recombination, small effective population size, and high mutation rate in general16. Collectively, these two markers have provided genetic support to conservation strategies, especially where population fragmentation, mating systems, and genetic variability intersect with management needs of crocodilians. Region-specific conservation strategies were recommended in French Guiana, when a dam-induced population fragmentation of Schneider’s dwarf caiman was identified17,18. Similarly, the mainland–island model in Morelet’s crocodile of Belize19, and the metapopulation structures with historical gene flow in the West African and Nile crocodiles20–22 warranted regional population management approaches with emphasis on smaller water bodies conservation. Mating strategies have also been identified to address inbreeding in captivity when genetic variation within a clutch was attributed to a polyandrous breeding behaviour23,24 while a guarded translocation protocol was suggested in the case of the Indian gharial to preserve genetic identity of two separate populations with low genetic variations25.

So far, genetic studies involving Tomistoma schlegelii have focused on captive held individuals due to the dwindling numbers of this endangered species26 and the difficulty in navigating the habitat of this elusive species27. This sole surviving member of Tomistominae28,29 inhabits freshwater and peat swamps of Indonesia, Malaysia and Brunei26,30–35 (Fig. 1). In Indonesia, confirmed occurrences have been reported in the highly acidic rivers (pH 3–4) of Sumatra and central Kalimantan linked to peat swamps32,34–36, while in east Kalimantan, it has been found in the neutral waters of Lake Mesangat (pH 7)37. Similar habitats were associated with sightings and captures in Sarawak, Malaysia as well26,38. The main threat to this large crocodilian is habitat destruction26. In Peninsular Malaysia, T. schlegelii occurs in both eastern and western regions based on past and recent captures of wild individuals. Over the past decade, four adults were captured from the Sungkai River (Perak), Dusun River (Selangor), Jengka River (Pahang), and Tekah River (Terengganu) either by accidentally getting trapped in fishing nets (in Perak and Terengganu) or washed ashore during floods (in Pahang). While the individual caught during floods was immediately relocated back to the river, the other three individuals were placed in captivity.

In Greek, Tomistoma means “cutting mouth,” referring to its long, slender snout and sharp teeth39. It is also known as the false gharial due to the similarity in the appearance of its rostrum and jaws to that of the Indian gharial. Despite this resemblance, morphological characters and fossil evidence have placed T. schlegelii closer to Crocodylus40–42, while molecular data placed it as a sister taxon to Gavialis43–46. At the population level, mtDNA studies have shown population differentiation between regions while nuclear gene studies are lacking47–49. A preliminary assessment of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) control region (CR) variation identified four haplotypes (A–D) from 11 wild-caught T. schlegelii sampled across five river systems in Kalimantan. Phylogenetic analysis revealed two distinct geographic groupings; one representing Central and Eastern Kalimantan, and the other Western Kalimantan. The observed structure was hypothesised to reflect historical landmass fragmentation and limited contemporary gene flow47. A subsequent study analysed mtDNA sequences from four geographic regions across two countries namely Malaysia (Peninsular Malaysia and Sarawak) and Indonesia (Sumatra and East Kalimantan) using concatenated mtDNA sequences from the ND6–tRNAGlu–cyt b region and cyt b–CR markers48. A high nucleotide and haplotype diversity was detected with the identification of six haplotypes, H1-H6. Phylogenetic and nearest-neighbour analyses revealed clear genetic differentiation among individuals from the four geographic regions (Fig. 1). All haplotypes were geographically restricted, with the exception of H2, which was shared between Peninsular Malaysia and Sarawak. The H4 haplotype formed a basal clade in all phylogenetic reconstructions and remained unconnected in the haplotype network at the 95% reconnection limit. The genetic distance between this lineage with the others was as high as between two morphologically distinct Cuban crocodile in which hybridization was confirmed. Hence, further investigation was suggested to identify if there was a potential cryptic lineage or management unit for T. schlegelii48.

Nuclear gene diversity had also been carried out using a combination of microsatellite markers developed for Crocodylus and Alligator, and inter-simple sequence repeats (ISSRs)49. With only half of the sample size from the previous mtDNA study48, two clusters were detected: one consisting exclusively of Peninsular Malaysian individuals and the other comprising individuals from both Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia. The authors concluded that ISSRs were more informative than microsatellite markers49. Although microsatellite markers have been developed for T. schlegelii50, they have not been used on the Malaysian samples. These markers were reported to be polymorphic, with observed heterozygosity ranging from 0.105 to 0.684 and showed high exclusion probabilities of excluding false parental pairs (99.9999%) rendering them valuable tools for assessing breeding success, relatedness, and future population management50. Given the scarcity of population-level data derived from nuclear genetic markers, this study aims to assess the genetic variation of T. schlegelii in Malaysia to help in future breeding programs. Microsatellite and mtDNA markers developed for this species48,50 were used on wild-caught individuals from the previous study48 and new individuals caught till date in this study.

Population structure

Bayesian clustering analysis using STRUCTURE indicated the presence of genetic differentiation among the sampled T. schlegelii individuals (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). The method by Evano revealed a clear peak in delta K at K = 2 (Fig. 2a), suggesting the existence of two primary inferred population, one comprising Peninsular Malaysian individuals, and another with both Peninsular Malaysia and Sarawak individuals. This most likely reflects the broad-scale genetic division across the sampled populations. However, the highest mean log-likelihood values, [Ln (Pr Data)], was six (Fig. 2b). When individuals were assigned to clusters based on the highest admixture coefficient (Q-matrices), the K = 6 indicated a finer scale differentiation within the primary clusters. This was seen when despite the genetic contribution of inferred population K1 in the Peninsular Malaysia individuals (TK009, TK015, TK030 and TTK066) the highest genetic contribution was from inferred population K2, separating them from the Sarawak individuals (TK002-TK005, TK020-TK023) (Fig. 2c). Similarly, while also showing genetic elements from other clusters, genetic contribution of inferred population K5 was highest in individual labelled TK067, while inferred population K3 dominated genetic contribution in TK014 and TK058. The Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analyses when considering K = 2, found seven and 20 loci pair in disequilibrium at 5% and 1% significance respectively, suggesting a potential population substructure. However, when considering K = 6, all loci pairs were in equilibrium at 1% significance and only one pair in disequilibrium at 5% significance. (See Supplementary Table S1 online). Hence the downstream analyses were carried out assuming the [Ln (Pr Data)] of six clusters (Cluster A-F), comprising individuals grouped by the highest contribution from an ancestral population.

Geographically, individuals in Clusters A and D were from one state each. Cluster A (TK002-TK005 and TK021-TK023) are exclusively from Sarawak (Table 2). Cluster B comprised four individuals from Peninsular Malaysia, with two sampled from Selangor (TK009 and TK015) and two of unknown origin. Individuals TK058 from Perak and TK014 formed Cluster C. The latter was held in a pen with another adult female of similar size post capture. As microchip tagging of the two individuals (TK014 and TK016) occurred after a lapse of time, identification of the individuals from Terengganu and Perak was not possible due to similarities in size and physical characteristics. Therefore, it is uncertain whether Cluster C exclusively consists of individuals from Perak. Cluster D comprised individuals from a known maternal parent (TK008) and all her offspring (TK007, TK008, TK024, TK025, TK026, TK027, TK028) and an adult male (TK007). The adults in this cluster are from Selangor. Cluster E consisted primarily of individuals from Selangor and Terengganu, all belonging to the previously described H4 lineage48, whereas Cluster F included individuals from Selangor, Terengganu, Pahang, and unknown origins. Except for Cluster B, all the other clusters consisted of individuals with only one mtDNA haplotype.

Genetic diversity

Except for loci TM8 and TM15, the polymorphic information content (PIC) was relatively high for all the other loci, indicating that the markers were informative (Table 3). The FSTAT results revealed that at least seven loci were in Hardy‒Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), namely, TM2, TM7, TM10, TM15, TM21, TM23, and TM24, but TM2, TM7, TM10, TM15 and TM21 deviated from equilibrium when overall samples were considered after randomisation at 5% nominal level. TM23 had the highest allelic richness, followed by TM24, while TM8, TM13, and TM15 showed the lowest allelic richness. These loci exhibited similar trends for HO and HE, although the latter parameter for locus TM13 was comparable to that of the other loci. Both TM23 and TM24 had private alleles in almost all the clusters, whereas loci TM3, TM10, TM12, and TM15 did not have private alleles in any of the clusters. The inbreeding coefficient, FIS, was high, indicating an excess of homozygotes, as revealed by both GenAlEx and FSTAT in most loci. For locus TM13, the FIS was 1, whereas excess heterozygotes were detected only in loci TM7, TM23, and TM24.

Relatedness

Hamilton’s (1971) relatedness, relat, was high at 0.317 ± 0.05. The ML Relate uncovered one parent-offspring (PO) and 38 full-sibs (FS) relationships within clusters (see Supplementary Table S2 online). All relationships were observed within the states except for Clusters E and F. Individual TK064 from Terengganu in Cluster E had an FS relationship with two cluster members from Selangor (TK011 and TK013). Similarly, TK017 from Pahang in Cluster F had an FS relationship with two cluster members from Selangor (TK010 and TK060).

Identification of important clusters for breeding plans

The gain/loss analysis revealed that when Clusters B and D were removed, their contribution to Nei’s (1987) gene diversity at all three levels (within, between, and across the clusters) decreased, making them more important for population diversity from a nuclear gene perspective (Table 6). Cluster D revealed a decline of 0.003% for within-cluster and 0.1% for total loss of diversity, whereas a greater magnitude of contribution was reported in Cluster B (0.1% for between-cluster, 3.1% and 3.2% for internal and total diversities). Cluster E revealed to be important at the within-cluster level following Cluster B (−1.231 and − 3.066, respectively).

mtDNA

Genetic diversity

The concatenated data revealed 32 parsimony variable sites across 1302 bp. Only the H1 and H4 haplotypes were detected in the newly acquired samples (five individuals each). The H1 lineage was distributed across clusters C, D and F (Fig. 2c) in the STRUCTURE analysis. Cluster E comprised exclusively individuals of the H4 lineage. The Sarawak individuals (haplotype H2) formed Cluster A solely, whereas the Peninsular Malaysian individuals with this lineage clustered with individuals TK030 (haplotype H5) and TK066 (haplotype H1) in Cluster B. Only Cluster B displayed mtDNA diversity (π = 0.00538 ± 0.00627, h = 0.8 ± 0.02688).

Population differentiation

AMOVA was affected by the removal of the haplotype H4 in the analysis (Table 5). When Cluster E (H4 haplotype) was excluded from the AMOVA analyses, the PhiPT was not significant, whereas its inclusion resulted in a structured population (PhiPT = 0.9, p = 0.001).

Sex-biased dispersal/philopatry

As the numbers of female T. schlegelii were overwhelmingly more than males in the sample set, the findings of the AMOVA analysis (all adult females) are similar to the AMOVA above. A significant population divergence, with more than 90% of the variance observed between clusters (PhiPT = 0.91, p value = 0.001) was seen when the H4 lineage was included, suggesting female philopatry (Table 5). Meanwhile, excluding individuals of this lineage (Cluster E) showed nonsignificant results (PhiPT = 0.29, p value = 0.074), suggesting dispersal. The microsatellite data showed insignificant nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test results with inconsistent assignment index distributions.

Discussion

The within-clusters genetic diversity of both microsatellite and mtDNA data was low. The trend of low genetic diversity levels has been reported in other crocodilians as well17,25,51–53. Several reasons had been proposed such as a “signature” of nuclear diversity caused by a prehistorical population bottleneck or a selection process that removed much of the variation from the crocodilian genome51. However, features such as longevity, generation overlap, late maturation, low metabolic rates and low mutation rates during DNA replication have also been suggested for this “signature”17,21. The explanations above could also explain the low nuclear gene diversity observed in T. schlegelii. However, heterozygosity can also be lowered when a population is structured, inbreeding, or sampling closely related individuals. This was supported by the positive FIS, the nonsignificant RIS and Hamilton’s (1989) relatedness. This was further supported by the clustering of a known maternal parent, TK008, and all her offspring together into Cluster D. Additionally, the PO and FS relationships were observed only within cluster members.

Although previous study detected a high mtDNA diversity at regional scale48, diversity within the clusters identified with STRUCTURE was only seen in Cluster B. The inferences for variation in Cluster B are limited because of incomplete and unverified information on the origins of the sampled individuals although studies on other crocodilians attributed this trend to metapopulations and the introduction of new individuals22,54. In the case of West African and Nile crocodiles, mtDNA diversity was attributed to historical gene flow and connectivity22. Historical connectivity could also be the case in T. schlegelii, as it has been suggested for the distribution of haplotypes between regions previously48.

In this study, microsatellite markers were more sensitive than mtDNA (STRUCTURE and AMOVA results). This reflected in the differentiation of the four haplotypes into six clusters and the separation of the H2 lineage into Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysian clusters. This study also revealed a more fine-scaled population structure within the Peninsular Malaysian samples compared to the previous study using crocodilian microsatellite markers49. Nonspecific microsatellite markers have been reported to not yield satisfactory population and individual-level data as well in other crocodilians19,20,23. More importantly, STRUCTURE analysis grouped all individual with H4 lineage into Cluster E. The among-population variation increased in the significantly differentiated AMOVA (RST) when this cluster was considered. These findings add support to the previous study48 of a possible management/conservation unit for T. schlegelii from the nuclear gene perspective. However, in the absence of any morphological variations and background information, more diagnostic approaches are needed to help in future conservation efforts for T. schlegelii.

As mentioned above, the relatedness of members within clusters (FIS, RIS, PO and FS relationships) following the K = 6 is further supported by the single maternal lineage in all clusters except Cluster B and single state as likely origin. However, FS relationships were also detected on both sides of the Titiwangsa Ranges (within Clusters E and F) involving more than one state. This could indicate contemporary gene flow. Due to limited knowledge of confirmed origins, the contemporary gene flow could not be address. However, in other crocodilians, contemporary gene flow in the presence of geographical barriers and terrestrial movements have been documented21,55. For T. schlegelii, only historical gene flow has been reported with the widespread occurrence of H1 and H2 from the mtDNA data so far48.

Even with limited field and background information of our samples, the combination of microsatellite and mtDNA data were able to indicate movements of females likely associated with breeding instincts. The mating season reported for this species is from April to June in Malaysia56 but at the Sungai Dusun Wildlife Conservation Centre (SDWCC), Selangor, nesting consistently occurs from March to June. Meanwhile, June to August is reported nesting months in Indonesia57. When the mtDNA AMOVA (excluding the H4 haplotype) was considered with the microsatellite data, the possibility of female dispersal was supported with capture information. Individuals TK060 and TK064 were captured during mating months, suggesting that movement was related to mating instincts, possibly in search of males or nesting sites. Female TK060 was captured at the SDWCC. The centre initiated a breeding programme circa 2010, and the capture of this adult female adjacent to the male enclosure in April could suggest dispersal in search of mating partners. Female TK064 was captured in June after becoming entangled in fishing equipment in Terengganu, where local fishermen are familiar only with the presence of saltwater crocodiles. However, intraindividual variability due to phenotypic plasticity and ontogenetic shifts during the lifetime of an individual has also been attributed to the movement of crocodilians21. Additionally, natal philopatry is better documented than dispersal for crocodilians.

The possibility of natal philopatry was supported by the mtDNA AMOVA (including the H4 lineage) with the microsatellite data. Individual TK058 was captured in August in Sungkai River, Perak and relocated into captivity to avoid human-crocodile conflict. In the early 2000 s, another female (TK014 or TK016) was caught in this river, suggesting potential philopatry. So far, homing instincts in T. schlegelii was recorded in a young individual (< 1.5 m) that returned to its capture site after 17 days58. However, information on the movement of adult T. schlegelii for breeding purposes is lacking, as previous studies have documented only nest characteristics, breeding periods, and mating acoustics57,59. Similar trend of demographically fragmented population suggestive of natal philopatry has been reported in other crocodilians20–22,60, while other crocodilian studies have attributed population differentiation trends to ecological preferences9,61,62 or distance17,19,51,64.

Therefore, without comprehensive genetic analysis and behavioural studies, understanding the population structure and breeding behaviour of T. schlegelii remains limited. To better understand the FS relationships within clusters and the spatial connectivity of T. schlegelii, whether movements are due to dispersal, philopatry, or other factors, further investigations with larger samples and detailed geographical data are needed. It should also be noted that the inferences in this study are based on small number of samples and markers. In addition to detecting neutral genetic variation, recent studies have indicated that short repeat tandems could be used as adaptive markers to detect functional diversity as well64. In crocodilians, spatially proximate populations have been differentiated based on ecology even though microsatellites and mtDNA markers were analysed assuming the neutral theory9,62,63. However, the neutral theory has also been challenged when it was found that measures like nucleotide diversity and fixation probabilities were impacted by fluctuating selection65. Instead, the Maximum Genetic Diversity (MGD) theory offers a new perspective on molecular evolution, emphasizing the interplay between genetic diversity, epigenetic complexity, and environmental factors with consideration that most genetic variants are functional and quasi-neutral rather than truly neutral66.

Nevertheless, the data from this study were able to identify important clusters for future breeding plans. Incorporating Cluster B into the breeding plan would not only maintain nuclear gene diversity but also prevent the loss of mtDNA diversity. Compared with the other clusters, Cluster B contributed to Nei’s (1987) gene diversity at all levels, contributed positively to the internal and total population allelic diversity, had the highest number of effective private alleles, with the highest observed and expected heterozygosity. It is also the only cluster with mtDNA diversity. The molecular coancestry approach has proven to be useful in recovering native genetic material in other taxa67. However, allelic richness is also crucial for long-term evolutionary adaptability68, and both gene and allelic diversities should complement each other for conservation purposes69. Preserving high internal diversity (GDW) is essential for maintaining high heterozygosity as a short-term strategy, whereas maintaining allelic richness and breed differentiation is vital for long-term strategies70. Although maximising gene diversity usually also maximises allelic richness71, this is not a rule69, as observed in the case of T. schlegelii. Cluster E was important for allelic diversity at all levels. Additionally, it contributed to the GDW (−1.232) after Cluster B and would maintain the H4 lineage, further supporting the importance of this cluster. Selecting Cluster F, which reduced Petit et al.68 diversities at all levels, would favour inbred populations, as Nei’s (1987) gene diversity would also be reduced to the greatest magnitude at both the internal and total population levels.

In the current captive situation, there is movement or exchange between breeding centres and zoos in Peninsular Malaysia. Owing to the endangered status of this species, breeding programs should be initiated in as many holding facilities as possible. Except for Cluster F, all the other clusters presented some level of contribution (gene and allelic diversities) at the within-cluster, between-clusters or total population level (Table 6). Given the availability of microsatellite and mtDNA data from this study, the holding facilities could test individual molecular coancestry coefficients for proper assignment of males to females.

A review of ex situ conservation and captive breeding studies in vertebrates, found that long-term captive breeding led to a loss of expected heterozygosity and a decreased number of alleles72. Additionally, to minimise the loss of genetic diversity, a minimum of 15 unrelated founders and a captive population size of 100 individuals were needed for a self-sustaining captive population that resembled wild populations. These estimates of founders were also found to be able to retain 90% of the natural genetic diversity, which would then be suitable for future reintroduction programmes72. As the number of captive T. schlegelii individuals continue to increase over time due to habitat destruction, the approach of starting with a minimum of 15 unrelated individuals as founders should be considered.

Available data from both captive and wild populations of other crocodilians suggest that multiple matings are common. Polyandry and bipaternity have proven to increase the genetic diversity and reduce inbreeding in a single clutch24,73–75. With most clusters in this study showing significant inbreeding, this approach should be given serious consideration when planning captive breeding programs for T. schlegelii. The only successful breeding of captive-held T. schlegelii was in Zoo Negara, where a female (TK008) and three males (TK007, TK009 and TK010) shared an enclosure. With improvements in diet and enclosure enrichment, the female laid 19 eggs, of which eleven were fertile and produced seven hatchlings56. The inbreeding coefficient, FIS, for Cluster D, which comprised an adult male (TK007) with the maternal parent (TK008) and her five surviving hatchlings, was nonsignificant (Table 4). In addition, the gain/loss analyses (Table 6) also identified this cluster as an important contributor of the Nei’s (1987) gene diversity at all levels, albeit at a much smaller magnitude than in Cluster B. The combination of genetic data and molecular coancestry analyses, together with the role of multiple paternity to increase genetic variability in crocodilians, suggest that this approach would be beneficial for captive breeding plans of this species. The mtDNA sequences are available in GenBank database (accession numbers OR149072-OR149081 and OR487880-OR487889) at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/.

Conclusion

In our data, while diversity was low for the microsatellite data, mtDNA diversity within clusters was also low except in Cluster B. The combination of both markers was able to identify important clusters for breeding programs for this species. The microsatellite findings supported the previous mtDNA study in which the H4 haplotype formed an exclusive cluster of its own, in addition to increasing the among cluster variation and RST in the AMOVA analyses. The main limitation of this study is the assignment of known geographical regions of the samples in Peninsular Malaysia, with only three individuals from confirmed locations. The low representation of males in the current dataset also limits the inferences that this study could make.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Sampling was carried out in zoological and wildlife centres in Malaysia with precise GPS coordinates available for only three individuals. Blood drawn from the caudal vein by venipuncture76 after physical restraint was stored in 10% EDTA at −20 °C.

Ethics statement

The Department of Wildlife and National Parks, Peninsular Malaysia (PERHILITAN) approved the study and subsequently issued the special permits to carry out research and sample collection from the endangered T. schlegelii in Peninsular Malaysia from 2016–2021(Special Permit Nos. B-00004-16-16 and B-00973-16-19, respectively). We confirm that all experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the special permits which complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. Additionally, the blood sampling was carried out together with the personnel of PERHILITAN.

DNA extraction

Total DNA was isolated from whole blood using the phenol‒chloroform method previously described77 and quantified using a spectrophotometer. DNA integrity was assessed via gel electrophoresis by running 1 µL of the eluted DNA and 1 µL of a 1 kb ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a 1% agarose gel premixed with GelRed (Biotium, Fremont, USA). The gel was visualised via a UV bioimaging system (UVP, Upland, USA).

Microsatellite

The PCR conditions for the previously described 18 microsatellite markers50 were 0.5 U of Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 2 mM MgCl2, each primer at 0.5 µM, 0.2 mM dNTP mixture (Invitrogen), and 50–150 ng of DNA in a final volume of 25 µL. The PCR cycles consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. The optimised annealing temperature, elongation time for the PCR cycles, polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE; 30% bis-acrylamide) concentration, and runtime for each primer set are listed in Table 1. PAGE was run at a constant voltage of 70 V, with a 50 bp DNA ladder (New England Biolabs Inc. (NEB), USA) placed in the far left and right lanes and one in the middle lane of the gel. The gels were visualised with UV Bioimaging system (UVP, Upland, USA).

Band sizes were determined with GelJ v.2 software78 and GelQuest (Sequentix, Germany). For GelJ, band size similarity was confirmed via the DICE method, whereas tolerance and similarity percentages were left at default settings. In GelQuest, the lanes of the gel images were extracted and transformed into a trace data curve, with base sizes as the chosen parameter. Both GelJ and GelQuest normalise each gel with the three lanes of the DNA ladder separately and subsequently across all gels for each marker.

Since most individuals in the dataset were identified only by state, followed by individuals of unknown origin and only three individuals with known locations, the assignment of these individuals to their respective subpopulations was conducted via the program STRUCTURE v.2.3.479. The analysis employed the admixture model with all other parameters set to default values. The burn-in was set at 1,000,000, the MCMC reps at 1,000,000, the number of clusters (K) set from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 10, and the number of iterations was set to 10. The analysis was performed using all loci without assigning any populations to the dataset. The CLUMPAK server (CLUMPAK servertau.ac.il) was used for graphical presentation of the STRUCTURE results and the best K.

A check for scoring errors such as large allele drop-out, stuttering, and null alleles was performed via MICRO-CHECKER v.2.2.380 at 95% confidence levels with 10,000 randomisations. Subsequent downstream analyses followed the clusters generated by STRUCTURE. The LD analysis was conducted in Genepop v.4.7.581 with Markov chain parameters set at 10,000 dememorisations, 100 batches with 10,000 iterations per batch.

Deviations from HWE were investigated via a permutation test with 10,000 iterations at the locus (f) and overall sample level (F) as implemented in the program FSTAT v.2.9.482. This program corrects for unequal and small sample sizes, allowing for unbiased comparisons between clusters/populations of various sizes. The allelic richness, inbreeding coefficient (FIS), and observed and expected heterozygosity were calculated at both the locus and sample levels through Bonferroni randomisations with 10,000 permutations. The proportion of randomisations yielding a larger p value than the adjusted nominal 5% and 1% significance was used to accept the null hypothesis.

The number of alleles (NA), number of effective alleles (NE), number of private alleles (PA), observed and expected heterozygosity (HO and HE) and mean FIS (heterozygote deficit) were documented via GenAlEx v.6.51b283,84. The PIC was analysed via Molkin v.3.085.

Population differentiation

The FSTAT program was used to perform F-statistics analysis, including FIT, FST (ϴ), and FIS, with the same randomisation method as before. The AMOVA via the RST statistic in GenAlEx. The six clusters identified by STRUCTURE were considered as populations and the analyses was carried out twice: (1) including and (2) excluding the individuals with the H4 lineage. This is because previous study48 had indicated that this lineage could be genetically distinct.

Relatedness and kinship

Relatedness was estimated in FSTAT using Hamilton’s (1971) relatedness, relat, which is equivalent to Queller and Goodnight’s (1989) estimator. It is the average relatedness of individuals within a cluster compared to the whole dataset. Randomisation was carried out with 10,000 permutations. Relatedness was also carried out by the maximum likelihood estimates in ML-Relate86. Four characterisations of relationships, namely, parent-offspring (PO), full-siblings (FS), half-siblings (HS) and unrelated (U), were performed at the 95% confidence level with 1,000 permutations. The relationships categorised as PO or FS were accepted as kinship.

Identification of important clusters for breeding plans

The Molkin program was used to analyse the molecular coancestry, weighted based on the PIC value71. The identification of important breeding clusters was carried out at the gene and allelic levels with the same program. At the gene level, maintenance of the maximum overall Nei’s (1987) gene diversity (GD) was carried out by removing a cluster. A positive or increased contribution to overall diversity indicates that it would be least preferred for genetic conservation. At the allelic level, diversity was assessed using the method of Petit et al.68, which was performed within and between clusters and the total contribution of the cluster after rarefaction of alleles per locus (k = four copies). A positive contribution is considered beneficial for conservation.

mtDNA

The mtDNA control region, CR, and ND 6-tRNAglu-cyt b fragments were amplified and sequenced following previously described protocols48. For the control region, overlapping sequences from the two primer sets (Set I and Set II, from the 3’ end of cytochrome b to the third conserved sequence block of CR) into a single contig were carried out in DNA Dragon v.1.5.1 (Sequentix) software with default settings. Only the ten new samples were sequenced, as the sequences of the other individuals in a previous study are available in GenBank48.

Genetic diversity

As the two gene fragments were analysed as concatenated data in the previous study48, DnaSP v.687 was used to concatenate the sequences in this study. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was then conducted using all the newly generated sequences and the sequences available in GenBank (accession numbers HM593977 to HM594032, HM594033 to HM594088) with ClustalW in MEGA X 10.0.588. The haplotype, h, and nucleotide, π, diversities were calculated in DnaSP.

Population differentiation

GenAlEx was used to perform AMOVA analyses by treating clusters as populations. The six clusters identified by STRUCTURE were considered as populations and the analyses was carried out twice: (1) including and (2) excluding the individuals with the H4 lineage.

Sex-biased dispersal

For microsatellite data, the mean corrected assignment index (mAIc) and significance testing with a nonparametric Mann‒Whitney U test was performed in GenAlEx. The same software was used for mtDNA AMOVA analyses using adult females and treating clusters as populations in GenAlEx. This was repeated by excluding the most divergent mtDNA haplotype, H4. Owing to the low representation of males in each cluster, AMOVA analysis of males could not be conducted.
