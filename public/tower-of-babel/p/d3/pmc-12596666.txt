Investigating the corrosion inhibition mechanism of Eucalyptus citriodora leaf extract on Q235 carbon steel
Authors: Yangyang Feng, Bingjie Zhou, Min Jie Wang, Ke Xu, Yaqi Cheng, Ziyan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn response to the corrosion protection requirements for Q235 carbon steel in acidic environments, this study systematically evaluated the anti-corrosion performance of Eucalyptus citriodora leaf extract (ECLE) on Q235 carbon steel in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution for the first time. The primarily water-soluble constituents were extracted using an eco-friendly ethanol-based method. Subsequently, electrochemical experiments demonstrated that ECLE achieved a corrosion inhibition efficiency of 91.2% at 298 K. Meanwhile, surface characterizations further confirmed that the addition of ECLE significantly reduced the corrosion extent of Q235 steel. Furthermore, quantum calculations further demonstrated that the synergistic adsorption mechanism of ECLE on the carbon steel surface involved the coordination of deprotonated carboxyl groups (R-COO-) with iron ions, the mixed adsorption of hydrogen-bonded interfacial structure, and the physical adsorption of alkyl chains. This work demonstrates an efficient, cost-effective, and sustainable approach for industrial acid cleaning processes, contributing to green corrosion protection technologies.

Introduction

Q235 carbon steel, renowned for its exceptional mechanical properties, ductility, and thermal characteristics, finds widespread application across diverse industrial sectors, particularly serving as a primary structural material in oil and gas pipeline systems.1,2 However, the inherent chemical reactivity of elemental iron makes Q235 carbon steel highly susceptible to oxidative corrosion in corrosive environment during extended periods of service.3,4 Dilute sulfuric acid pickling effectively removes surface oxides from Q235 carbon steel.5 However, in continuous-flow pickling systems commonly employed for pipelines, the aggressive nature of acidic media inevitably accelerates the corrosion of carbon steel.6,7,8 Therefore, the development of effective corrosion mitigation strategies to prevent corrosion of carbon steel in acidic solutions is paramount. Among various corrosion protection methods, organic corrosion inhibitors are widely recognized as one of the most effective approaches, as they function by forming coordinate bonds between iron ions and heteroatoms, thereby significantly reducing the corrosion rate of carbon steel in acidic environment.9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 For instance, benzimidazole derivatives and Schiff base molecules exhibit excellent corrosion resistance for carbon steel in acidic media.17,18,19 However, many traditional organic inhibitors face challenges such as limited water solubility, complicated synthesis procedures, high production costs, and potential environmental risks. With increasing global emphasis on environmental protection and the implementation of China’s “dual carbon” policy,20 there is a growing demand for safer and more eco-friendly alternatives.

Plant extracts have attracted considerable attention due to their advantages, including cost-effectiveness, biodegradability, and renewability. Organic plant extracts contain heteroatoms and aromatic rings, which act as primary adsorption sites for effective inhibitors.21,22 Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of various plant extracts in protecting carbon steel in acidic environments.23,24,25 For example, Zhao et al.26 reported that betel nut shell extract achieved a remarkable 93.1% corrosion inhibition efficiency for Q235 carbon steel in acidic solution. These findings highlight the potential of plant extracts as sustainable alternatives to traditional inhibitors. However, existing studies face several imitations, including the use of environmentally unfriendly extraction solvents, poor water solubility, low corrosion inhibition efficiency and a lack of comprehensive mechanistic understanding. Therefore, it is necessary to further study resource-rich and economically efficient plant-based extracts as corrosion inhibitors for carbon steel in sulfuric acid solution. Eucalyptus citriodora, native to coastal Queensland, Australia, has been widely introduced to the mountainous and hilly regions of South and Southwest China. As a renewable resource, its leaves can be harvested up to three times annually, even on marginal land, producing between 4.5 and 10 tons of fresh leaves per hectare, indicating strong potential for sustainable utilization. ECLE, known for its rich composition of terpenoids, organic acids, and polysaccharides, is considered a promising candidate for corrosion inhibition.27 Despite its potential, a systematic investigation of ECLE’s anti-corrosion property for Q235 steel remains unexplored.

Based on this background, the corrosion inhibition performance of ECLE as a sustainable corrosion inhibitor for Q235 carbon steel in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution was systematically investigated. Water-soluble organic compounds were extracted from Eucalyptus citriodora leaves using an eco-friendly ethanol solution. Electrochemical analyses, including polarization curve and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), demonstrated that the corrosion inhibition efficiency of ECLE reached up to 91.2% at 298 K. The surface morphology of the samples was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). The surface elemental composition and corrosion products of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Moreover, contact angle (CA) measurements were conducted to evaluate the hydrophobic characteristics of the protective film formed on the metal surface. Experimental results indicated that ECLE adsorbed on the carbon steel surface to form a protective film, significantly reducing the corrosion rate at the tested temperature range. Quantum chemical calculations provided theoretical insights into the high inhibition efficiency of ECLE. The findings suggested that ECLE inhibited corrosion through physicochemical synergistic adsorption, forming a protective barrier on the Q235 steel surface. This study not only provided a scientific foundation for the high-value utilization of Eucalyptus citriodora resources but also contributed to the molecular design of corrosion inhibitors applicable to acid cleaning processes in industrial pipelines.

Therefore, the anti-corrosion effect of ECLE on the cathodic corrosion reaction was more significant than that on the anodic reaction. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy analysis

EIS provided valuable insights into the corrosion inhibition mechanism of ECLE. As shown in Figures 3A–3C, the Nyquist plots exhibited a semicircular pattern composed of a high-frequency capacitive loop and a low-frequency inductive loop. The capacitive arc was attributed to the charge transfer process, while the inductive arc was associated with the diffusion of ECLE molecules or corrosive species at the working electrode surface. A concentration-dependent increase in the diameter of the capacitive loop was observed, indicating enhanced corrosion protection with increasing ECLE concentration. This trend indicated the formation of a protective adsorption layer that impeded charge transfer by increasing the energy barrier for corrosion reactions, thereby mitigating the corrosion process. Moreover, the consistent semicircular shape across all tested concentrations implied that ECLE adsorption primarily involved simple surface coverage without significant changes in the adsorption mechanism. It was crucial to note that the flattened appearance of all semicircles was due to roughness and inhomogeneity of the electrode surfaces.33 In addition, at the same concentration of ECLE, the radius of the capacitive loop reached its maximum at 298 K (Figure 3A) and progressively decreased at 303 K (Figure 3B) and further at 308 K (Figure 3C). This observation was attributed to the increased test temperature, which intensified the thermal motion of corrosive species (H+ and SO42−), thereby reducing the thermodynamic stability of the ECLE adsorption layer.Figure 3Nyquist impedance and bode plots for Q235 carbon steel samplesNyquist impedance (A–C) and bode plots (D–F) for Q235 carbon steel in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution containing different concentration of ECLE at different test temperatures.

After the addition of ECLE, the phase angle peaks and impedance modulus exhibited a gradual increasing trend, suggesting improved corrosion resistance, as depicted in Figures 3D–3F. With the increase of ECLE concentration, the maximum phase angle value also increased (Table S2), indicating a corresponding decrease in corrosion rate. Furthermore, the maximum impedance modulus increased by an order of magnitude compared to the blank, indicating that the ECLE adsorbed on the carbon steel surface and formed an effective protective film, preventing direct contact between corrosive substances and carbon steel surface. Additionally, the maximum impedance modulus at 298 K was higher than that observed at 303 K and 308 K, which was consistent with accelerated corrosion kinetics at elevated temperatures.

Adsorption isotherm

Adsorption isotherms were employed to investigate the adsorption mechanism of ECLE on the surface of Q235 carbon steel. The Langmuir adsorption model showed an excellent agreement with experimental data, as evidenced by the high correlation coefficients (R2) of 0.996 at 298 K, 0.997 at 303 K, and 0.998 at 308 K. These results confirmed that ECLE on Q235 steel was consistent with Langmuir adsorption model.

The Langmuir isotherm equation is expressed as:(Equation 5)θ1-θ=Kads×CWhere Kads is the equilibrium adsorption constant, θ is the surface coverage, and C is the inhibitor concentration.

The relationship between Kads and energy of adsorption (ΔGads0) is shown in the following formula:(Equation 6)ΔGads0=-RTln(1×106Kads)where R is the universal gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature.

As depicted in Figures 4A–4C, the Langmuir adsorption isotherms for ECLE at different temperatures (298 K, 303 K, and 308 K) exhibited nearly parallel linear slopes, confirming a monolayer adsorption mechanism. The higher value of Kads indicated that ECLE could strongly adsorb on the carbon surface. According to Table S4, the Kads values followed the temperature-dependent order: 298K > 303 K > 308 K, indicating that elevated temperatures reduced the adsorption capacity of the inhibitor on the steel surface. The calculated values of Gibbs free energy were −23.60 kJ/mol (298 K), −23.70 kJ/mol (303 K), and −23.94 kJ/mol (308 K), as illustrated in Figure 4D, suggesting that the adsorption of ECLE on the carbon steel involved both physisorption and chemisorption.34Figure 4Langmuir adsorption behavior of ECLE on the surface of Q235 carbon steelLangmuir adsorption isotherms of ECLE on carbon steel surface at 298 K (A), 303 K (B), and 308 K (C), along with the corresponding free energy values at different temperatures (D).

SEM analysis

The surface morphology of Q235 carbon steel was examined using SEM to evaluate the corrosion inhibition effect of ECLE. Figures 5A–5C illustrated that the blank exhibited severe corrosion with deep pits and roughness, primarily due to attack by corrosive ions (H+ and SO42−). Furthermore, temperature-dependent analysis revealed a progressive deterioration of surface morphology, where more pronounced corrosion features were observed at 303 K (Figure 5B) compared to 298 K (Figure 5A), and further exacerbation occurred at 308 K (Figure 5C). This temperature-dependent behavior confirms the accelerated corrosion kinetics in acidic media at elevated temperatures. In contrast, when the carbon steel specimens were immersed in H2SO4 solution containing 700 mg/L ECLE, their surfaces showed significantly reduced corrosion damage, as shown in Figure 5D-F. Notably, the carbon steel displayed a smoother surface at 298 K (Figure 5B) compared to those at higher temperatures, suggesting that the anti-corrosion performance of ECLE decreased as the temperature increased.Figure 5SEM morphology of Q235 carbon steel samplesSEM images of Q235 carbon steel after 4 h immersion in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution: in the absence of ECLE at 298 K (A), 303 K (B), and 308 K (C); and in the presence of ECLE at 298 K (D), 303 K (E), and 308 K (F). AFM analysis

The surface morphology and roughness of carbon steel samples in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution were studied by AFM. As depicted in Figures 6A–6C, the surface of the blank exhibited a rough morphology characterized by deep and large pits resulting from the attack of corrosive ions (H+ and SO42−). The average roughness values were measured as 76.5 nm (298 K), 86.8 nm (303 K) and 108.9 nm (308 K), respectively. In contrast, carbon steel samples immersed in H2SO4 solution containing 700 mg/L ECLE showed significantly reduced surface roughness in Figures 6D–6F, with values of 34.9 nm at (298 K), 46.6 nm (303 K), and 54.2 nm (308 K). This phenomenon indicated that ECLE formed a protective adsorption layer on the surface of carbon steel, effectively shielding the steel from corrosive ions. CA analysis

The contact angle measurement was employed to evaluate the wettability of the Q235 carbon steel surface. According to the literature, a water contact angle less than 90° indicates a hydrophilic surface, whereas an angle greater than 90° suggests a hydrophobic surface.35 As illustrated in Figures 7A–7C, the contact angles for the blank samples were measured as 55.6° (298 K), 45.6° (303 K), and 38.7° (308 K) at different test temperatures. In contrast, the surfaces of ECLE-modified samples displayed increased contact angles, increasing to 106.6° (298 K), 93.5° (303 K), and 80.8° (308 K), as shown in Figures 7D and 7E. This increase in contact angle indicated the formation of a hydrophobic protective layer, thereby preventing corrosive ions from interacting with the carbon steel surface.36Figure 7Contact angle of Q235 carbon steel samplesContact angle diagrams of Q235 carbon steel after 4 h immersion in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution: in the absence of ECLE at 298 K (A), 303 K (B), and 308 K(C); in the presence of 500 mg/L PCRE at 298 K (D), 303 K (E), and 308 K (F).

XRD analysis

The crystalline phases of corrosion products formed on the carbon steel surface were analyzed using XRD. Figures 8A–8C displays the diffraction pattern of Q235 steel in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 without ECLE, showing characteristic peaks at 44.6° and 65.5°, which correspond to metallic iron. Additionally, diffraction peaks observed at 35.5°, 59.9°, and 71.7° were indicative of the presence of Fe3O4 and FeOOH phases.37,38 In contrast, when carbon steel specimens were immersed in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution containing ECLE, a significant reduction in the peak intensities of iron oxides was observed relative to the blank in Figures 8D–8F, indicating that ECLE effectively slowed down the corrosion of carbon steel. Furthermore, temperature-dependent analysis revealed that the peak intensities of iron oxides were lower at 298 K compared to those at higher temperatures (303 K and 308 K), suggesting a decrease in the corrosion inhibition efficiency of ECLE with increasing temperature. XPS analysis

The surface elemental composition and chemical states of Q235 steel were analyzed using XPS. As illustrated in Figure S4, the introduction of ECLE resulted in a significantly enhancement in the characteristic peak intensities of C 1s and O 1s, while the Fe 2p signal was correspondingly reduced. This observation provided direct evidence for the formation of an organic protective film on the surface of the carbon steel. Figure 9 presented the high-resolution C 1s, O 1s, and Fe 2p XPS spectra. In Figure 9A, the C 1s spectrum of the blank sample surface exhibited three characteristic peaks at 284.8 eV (C-C), 286.3 eV (C-O), and 288.6 eV (O-C=O), which were attributed to adventitious carbon contamination. After the introduction of ECLE, the binding energies of all carbon-related peaks exhibited slight variations, measuring 284.87 eV, 286.39 eV, and 288.3 eV, respectively. The observed negative shifted in the carboxyl peak suggests that deprotonation occurred, leading to the formation of carboxylate groups (R-COO-). Concurrently, the enhancement of the C-C signal at 284.87 eV implied the co-adsorption of hydrocarbon chains. The O 1s spectrum in Figure 9B was deconvolved into three distinct peaks. The first peak at 530.05 eV was attributed to the Fe–O bond, the other peak at 531.5 eV corresponded to the O–H bond, possibly originating from hydrous iron oxides (i.e., FeOOH). The third peak at 532.55 eV was associated with the C=O group.39 Notably, in the presence of ECLE, the Fe–O peak exhibited a positive shift to 530.2 eV. The O–H peak shifted to 531.86 eV with a considerable increase in intensity, whereas the C=O peak stabilizes at 533.55 eV. The Fe 2p spectrum was deconvoluted into Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 peaks, along with their corresponding satellite peaks in Figure 9C. After adding PCRE, the 2p3/2 peak binding energy of FeOOH shifted from 712.1 eV to 712.55 eV, while its 2p1/2 peak exhibited a concurrent positive shift (724.05→724.1 eV).40 This was attributed to the formation of covalent coordination bonds between carboxylate ions and iron ions, which resulted in a reduction of the electron density around Fe3+. In contrast, the 2p3/2 peak binding energy of Fe3O4 shifted negatively from 709.5 eV to 709.29 eV, and a similar negative shift was observed for the 2p1/2 peak (726.2→726.1 eV), suggesting that certain Fe3+ ions acquire electrons and are reduced to Fe2+.Figure 9XPS spectra of Q235 carbon steel samplesThe XPS spectra of Q235 carbon steel immersed in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 solution for 8 h without (A–C) and with (D–F) 700 mg/L ECLE at 298 K.

Theoretical calculation

To further explore the synergistic adsorption mechanism of ECLE, quantum chemical calculations provided a theoretical perspective. The primary components of gallic acid, quercetin, and ellagic acid in ECLE were optimized using Gaussian. The molecular frontier orbitals including the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), and electrostatic potential (ESP) maps were shown in Figure 10.Figure 10Molecular frontier orbitals and electrostatic potential maps

Molecular frontier orbitals and electrostatic potential maps

From the perspective of quantum chemical parameters, quercetin exhibited the highest HOMO energy among the three compounds, indicating a strong electron-donating capacity.41,42 Its smallest HOMO-LUMO energy gap (ΔE) indicated an enhanced adsorption tendency,43,44 while its largest dipole moment (μ) promoted favorable molecular orientation and dense interfacial coverage.45,46 Therefore, quercetin was determined to be the primary corrosion-inhibiting constituent in ECLE.

The HOMO electron density distribution typically indicated the molecule’s active sites, which readily interact with nucleophiles. As shown in Figure 10 the electron clouds of gallic acid, quercetin, and ellagic acid were concentrated on hydroxyl, carbonyl, and aromatic rings. This demonstrated that these active adhesion sites could donate electrons and form coordination bonds with vacant metal orbitals. Furthermore, the ESP maps exhibited that nucleophilic regions (in red) concentrated around oxygen atoms and O-heterocyclic rings, facilitating the formation of coordination bonds between inhibitor molecules and carbon steel surface.

Discussion

The proposed corrosion inhibition mechanism of ECLE on Q235 carbon steel was shown in Figure 11. In sulfuric acid solution, H+ ions diffused toward the carbon steel surface, leading to significant corrosion. However, the addition of ECLE effectively inhibited the corrosion of Q235 carbon steel. The calculated Gibbs free energy indicated that ECLE was likely to experience both physical and chemical adsorption on the surface of carbon steel. Specifically, the significant negative shift in the C 1s binding energy of the carboxyl group (288.6 → 288.3 eV) confirmed the deprotonation of oxygen atoms, leading to the formation of R–COO- species. The presence of a strong nucleophilic region around the oxygen atom (represented as red in the ESP diagram), combined with a positive binding energy shift of Fe 2p3/2 to 712.55 eV, suggested that the R-COO- species coordinated with iron ions. Furthermore, the positive shift in the O-H peak binding energy observed in the O 1s spectrum (531.5 → 531.86 eV) suggested the polarization of the O-H bond. The strongly electrophilic region surrounding the hydroxyl group (represented as blue in the ESP diagram), along with a positive binding energy shift of Fe 2p3/2 to 712.55 eV, suggested that the formation of a hydrogen-bonded interfacial structure via mixed adsorption: R-COO-···+H-OH-Fe3+. Moreover, the enhanced C-C signal at 284.87 eV observed in XPS spectrum, without a significant shift in binding energy, indicated that alkyl chains increased interfacial coverage attributed to physical adsorption. This synergistic adsorption mechanism facilitated the formation of a stable protective film on the carbon steel surface, effectively inhibiting the corrosion process.Figure 11Corrosion inhibition mechanism diagram of ECLE

The systematic investigation established ECLE as a high-performance green corrosion inhibitor for Q235 carbon steel in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4. This work provided a mechanistic basis for plant-derived inhibitor design, demonstrating that electron-rich functional groups, and stable conjugated frameworks are critical for barrier film formation. The findings bridge molecular quantum chemistry with industrial corrosion protection, offering an eco-friendly paradigm to replace toxic inhibitors in acid pickling applications.

Limitations of the study

Although ECLE achieved 91.2% inhibition efficiency in controlled 0.5 mol/L H2SO4, its performance in industrial acid cleaning requires rigorous validation. Critical operational variables, including acid concentration fluctuations, contaminant ions, and thermal gradients, may alter corrosion inhibition efficacy. Quantifying efficiency retention under such realistic conditions remains essential prior to scale-up.

Method details

Preparation of ECLE

Firstly, Eucalyptus citriodora leaves were washed with deionized water to remove surface impurities, dried in an oven for 12 h, and ground into a fine powder. Subsequently, 200 g of the powder were extracted with 95% ethanol via reflux, followed by filtration and concentration. Finally, the concentrated solution was dissolved in water, separated by centrifugation, and dried in oven to yield 13.5 g of brown product.

Electrochemical experiments

Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation with a three-electrode system. The Q235 steel electrode (1 cm × 1 cm) served as the working electrode, a platinum electrode was employed as the counter electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) was used as the reference electrode. Initially, the Q235 steel electrode was immersed in 0.5 mol/L H2SO4 for 1200 s to reach a stable open circuit potential (EOCP). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed over a frequency range of 0.01 Hz–1 × 105 Hz with a disturbance amplitude of ±5 mV. Finally, polarization curve measurements were recorded in the potential range from −750 mV to −200 mV at a scan rate of 1 mV/s.

Surface characterizations

Comprehensive surface characterization was conducted employing multiple analytical techniques: Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010) assessed morphological changes, atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon) quantified surface roughness topography, and contact angle measurements (CA, Biolin PD-200) evaluated wettability alterations. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Bruker Tensor 37) was conducted to analyze the organic functional groups present in the sample. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab SE) was employed to identify corrosion product phases. Furthermore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha) was utilized to investigate elemental composition and chemical states.

Theoretical calculation research

The electrostatic potential, dipole moment, and frontier orbital of pulsatilla saponin D, pulchinenoside A, and pulchinenoside B4 were studied by density functional theory, whose structure were optimized by the B3LYP functional and def2-SVP basis set with Grimme’s DFT-D3(BJ) empirical dispersion correction by using Gaussian 16 C.02 quantum chemical package. Moreover, vibrational frequency was performed at the same level to guarantee that there is no imaginary frequency in that molecule, i.e., it locates on the minima of the potential energy surface. ESP, highest occupied molecular orbital, and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital were calculated by Multiwfn 3.8 (dev), whose input file was extracted from Gaussian formatting checkpoint files from Gaussian, and plotted by GaussView 6.0.16. All above calculations have considered water as implicit solvent by polarizable continuum model using the integral equation formalism variant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

No statistical analysis or significance testing was performed. The reproducibility of experimental outcomes was confirmed through triplicate independent measurements. Corrosion inhibition efficiency (η%) exhibited a relative standard deviation (RSD) of ≤3% across replicates. Surface characterization demonstrated consistent results, with AFM-measured average roughness (Ra) showing ≤5% RSD across three randomly selected locations, and contact angle measurements maintaining ≤5% RSD.
