Whole-exome sequencing-centered genetic evaluation for early-onset obesity in Chinese children: a retrospective single-center cohort
Authors: Si Chen, Jing Zhang, Liyuan Ge, Zhonghui Li, Liuxu Wang, Xinran Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenetic causes of early-onset obesity remain undercharacterized in East Asian children. This study evaluated a whole-exome sequencing (WES)-centered diagnostic workflow in Chinese children with obesity onset before 5 years.MethodsConsecutive children with body mass index above the 95th percentile and obesity onset before 5 years who completed structured inpatient assessment at a single center between February 2022 and December 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. Phenotyping included clinical, biochemical, oral glucose tolerance, cortisol rhythm, and liver assessments. Genetic testing combined WES, mitochondrial DNA analysis, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) for obesity-related imprinting loci, WES-based copy-number variant and runs-of-homozygosity analyses, and Sanger validation. Variants were interpreted according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) criteria.ResultsAmong 23 children, clinically relevant genetic findings were identified in 6 (26.1%): melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) c.831T>A (p.C277*), maternal uniparental disomy of 15q11–13, and four phenotype-correlated variants of uncertain significance in UCP3, BBS1, NCOA1, and SH2B1. The definitive/likely diagnostic yield, restricted to pathogenic or confirmed imprinting findings, was 8.7% (2/23). Findings involved leptin-MC4R signaling, BBSome function, fatty-acid oxidation, and chromosomal imprinting. Genetically positive children showed numerically higher alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), but differences were not statistically significant. BBS1 p.T374S was relatively enriched in East Asian reference data.ConclusionsA WES-centered integrated workflow detected heterogeneous genetic mechanisms in Chinese children with early-onset obesity, but variant of uncertain significance (VUS)-associated findings should be distinguished from confirmed diagnoses. Orthogonal methylation/MLPA and runs of homozygosity (ROH) analyses were necessary for imprinting diagnosis. Larger multicenter studies and functional validation are needed.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A whole-exome sequencing (WES)-centered integrated genetic workflow identified clinically relevant findings in 6 of 23 Chinese children with obesity onset before 5 years.

• Definitive/likely molecular diagnoses were established in 2 of 23 children, whereas the remaining positive findings were variants of uncertain significance with phenotype correlation.

What is known and what is new?

• WES can improve the evaluation of early-onset obesity, but East Asian pediatric data remain limited.

• This retrospective cohort demonstrates that WES, when integrated with methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), WES-derived runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis, and targeted confirmatory testing, can characterize heterogeneous genetic mechanisms in Chinese children. Genetically positive cases showed a non-significant exploratory trend toward higher liver enzyme levels.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Diagnostic yield should be reported separately for definitive/likely diagnoses and variant of uncertain significance (VUS)-associated findings to allow accurate counseling and fair inter-study comparisons.

• Genetic evaluation should be prioritized in carefully phenotyped children with obesity onset before age 5 years, especially when syndromic features, hyperphagia, family history, or unexplained metabolic complications are present; larger East Asian cohorts are needed before broader claims are made.

Introduction

Background

Childhood obesity has emerged as one of the foremost pediatric public health concerns of this century. In China, national surveillance data indicate that the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity among school-aged children rose from 15.5% in 2010 to approximately 29.4% in 2022, and projections suggest that the number of affected children will continue to grow in the coming decade (1-3). The country now carries the largest absolute burden of childhood obesity globally (2,3). While most cases reflect a multifactorial interplay between genetic susceptibility, dietary patterns, physical inactivity, and environmental change (4), a subset—particularly those presenting at a very young age—may be driven by rare, highly penetrant single-gene mutations or chromosomal imprinting abnormalities (5,6).

The distinction between polygenic and monogenic or syndromic obesity carries substantial clinical implications. Monogenic obesity, most commonly caused by variants in genes of the leptin-melanocortin pathway such as melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R), LEP, LEPR, POMC, and PCSK1, typically presents with severe, early-onset hyperphagia and progressive weight gain beginning in infancy or early childhood (5,7). Syndromic forms, including Prader-Willi syndrome, Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and rarer entities, are characterized by additional features including intellectual disability, dysmorphic signs, or multi-organ involvement. Recognition of these genetic etiologies has direct clinical consequences: it allows accurate family counseling, guides phenotype-directed surveillance, and increasingly opens the door to mechanism-based pharmacotherapy—most notably the MC4R agonist setmelanotide, now approved for POMC, LEPR, and PCSK1 deficiency as well as Bardet-Biedl syndrome in children aged 2 years and older (8).

The frequency of monogenic and syndromic obesity among children with early-onset severe obesity is difficult to estimate precisely, given wide variability in case ascertainment and diagnostic methods across published series. Reported definitive diagnostic yields from whole-exome sequencing (WES)-based or targeted-panel studies in pediatric cohorts range broadly, from around 5% to over 30%, reflecting differences in enrollment criteria, degree of phenotypic enrichment, and reporting thresholds for variant classification (6,9-11). Studies from the Middle East, Europe, and North America have contributed substantially to our understanding of the genetic spectrum, but East Asian (EAS) pediatric data—particularly from mainland China—remain comparatively sparse (5,12).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Obesity beginning before 5 years of age is a clinically important trigger for genetic investigation. Current international guidelines recommend considering genetic evaluation when early-onset severe obesity is accompanied by hyperphagia, syndromic features, consanguinity, or a family history of severe obesity (6). However, candidate gene frequencies, pathogenic variant spectra, and population-specific interpretation frameworks derived largely from European and North American cohorts may not translate directly to EAS children. Existing Chinese studies have identified rare variants in the leptin-melanocortin pathway but have generally examined targeted gene panels rather than comprehensive, integrated genomic workflows (12). Furthermore, the relative contribution of imprinting disorders such as Prader-Willi syndrome to the early-onset obesity phenotype in Chinese children has received limited attention, and the appropriate orthogonal assays needed to detect such disorders alongside WES have not been systematically characterized in this population.

There is also a growing recognition that the clinical phenotype accompanying genetic obesity—including metabolic and inflammatory laboratory markers—may help identify children who warrant prioritized genetic workup. Elevated liver enzymes, white blood cell (WBC) count, and markers of insulin resistance have each been associated with obesity-related metabolic dysfunction in children, but their potential utility as biomarkers for the presence of a genetic etiology has not been formally evaluated in EAS cohorts (13,14).

Objective

We performed a retrospective review of a WES-centered integrated genetic workflow applied to Chinese children with obesity onset before 5 years of age at Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Center Hospital, School of Medicine, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China. Our specific aims were: (I) to describe the clinically relevant genetic findings identified and distinguish definitive/likely molecular diagnoses from variants of uncertain significance (VUS) with phenotype correlation; (II) to compare selected clinical and laboratory characteristics between genetically positive and negative children; and (III) to explore EAS population-frequency context for the identified variants. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0407/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a retrospective single-center observational cohort study conducted at Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Center Hospital, School of Medicine, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China. Medical records were reviewed for consecutive children with body mass index (BMI) above the 95th percentile and obesity onset before 5 years of age who underwent structured inpatient evaluation between February 2022 and December 2023. Obesity was defined according to the Expert Consensus on Diagnosis, Assessment, and Management of Obesity in Chinese Children (15).

Inclusion criteria were BMI above the 95th percentile and obesity onset before 5 years of age. Exclusion criteria were a previously established genetic syndrome, attenuated growth velocity, major neurodevelopmental abnormality, severe hyperphagia with an already known etiology, or prior central nervous system injury. Children were not required to be younger than 5 years at the time of evaluation; the age criterion referred to age of obesity onset. Severe hyperphagia was assessed clinically from the structured feeding and developmental history obtained at admission rather than with a validated appetite questionnaire. The exclusion criterion applied only to children in whom severe hyperphagia already had an established cause (for example, a previously diagnosed syndromic or hypothalamic disorder); children with isolated or otherwise unexplained increased appetite remained eligible. Detailed quantitative appetite phenotyping was therefore not performed, which is consistent with the limitation noted below.

Thirty-five consecutive eligible children were identified. Twenty-three completed the planned inpatient evaluation and constituted the analytic cohort; 12 children whose guardians declined the full inpatient workup were excluded. Clinical assessment included physical examination, routine laboratory testing, oral glucose tolerance testing, and cortisol rhythm assessment. Sex, parental consanguinity, family history of obesity, and the parent-reported age at obesity onset were recorded from the medical record. Hepatic involvement was evaluated by serum aminotransferases together with abdominal ultrasonography; hepatic steatosis was documented when ultrasonography demonstrated increased hepatic echogenicity consistent with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Systematic MASLD characterization (for example, standardized body-composition analysis, transient elastography, or liver biopsy) was not uniformly performed. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting insulin (µU/L) × fasting glucose (mmol/L)/22.5 (16).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Center Hospital, School of Medicine, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China [approval number: 2023(21)-2], and written informed consent was obtained from all guardians of the children.

Genetic evaluation

All 23 participants underwent a WES-centered integrated genetic evaluation comprising WES, mitochondrial DNA sequencing, and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) targeting obesity-related imprinting loci including 15q11-13. WES was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with a mean coverage depth of at least 200×, and mitochondrial DNA sequencing was performed at a depth of at least 5,000×.

Single-nucleotide variant (SNV)/indel calling used GATK HaplotypeCaller with downstream annotation by ANNOVAR. Copy-number variant (CNV) analysis from WES data employed ExomeDepth with read-depth normalization. Potential uniparental disomy (UPD) was assessed by ROH analysis of WES variant data.

In case P6, a diagnosis of Prader-Willi syndrome due to maternal UPD 15 was established through a combined workflow. WES first excluded pathogenic SNV/indel and CNV findings in 15q11-q13, and runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis revealed a single large absence of heterozygosity (AOH)/ROH on chromosome 15, supporting chromosome 15 isodisomy. MS-MLPA demonstrated a Prader-Willi-pattern maternal-only methylation abnormality at SNRPN with normal copy number, indicating a non-deletion methylation abnormality. Parent-of-origin was then confirmed by trio short tandem repeat (STR) analysis, which showed only maternal alleles and no paternal contribution at informative chromosome 15 markers, establishing maternal UPD rather than a deletion or isolated epimutation. Candidate SNVs were validated by Sanger sequencing. Additional targeted Sanger analysis of POMC, LEPR, SRC1, SH2B1, and PCSK1 p.N221D was performed in selected cases when clinically indicated.

Variant interpretation and population-frequency assessment

Variant pathogenicity was classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines (17). Two reporting levels were used: (I) clinically relevant findings, encompassing pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, confirmed chromosomal or imprinting diagnoses, and VUS with phenotypic correlation considered potentially relevant to the presenting phenotype; and (II) definitive/likely molecular diagnoses, restricted to pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or confirmed imprinting/chromosomal findings. VUS were kept within the clinically relevant tier rather than discarded because each was rare [minor allele frequency (MAF) typically below 0.01%], sat in a biologically plausible candidate gene for the leptin-MC4R or related energy-homeostasis pathways, and matched the proband’s phenotype. They were excluded from the definitive/likely diagnostic yield and were not treated as confirmed diagnoses; the genotype-phenotype correlations are tentative and hypothesis-generating, pending functional validation and family segregation analysis. This two-tier scheme was used specifically to keep VUS from inflating the reported diagnostic rate. Identified genes were grouped into four biological mechanisms: leptin-MC4R signaling, BBSome function, fatty-acid oxidation, and chromosomal imprinting. Population allele frequencies were obtained from gnomAD version 2.1.1 (18), and EAS frequencies were compared with global frequencies for each identified SNV.

Statistical analysis

Participants were classified as genetically positive (n=6) or genetically negative (n=17) based on the presence or absence of a clinically relevant genetic finding. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Because several variables violated normality assumptions and the sample was small, between-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared by Fisher’s exact test. To account for 11 prespecified clinical comparisons, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P<0.0045 was applied; findings with P<0.05 above this threshold were considered nominally significant. All analyses were performed in SPSS Statistics 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Because only six children had a clinically relevant finding and these were distributed across four mechanistic categories (one to three cases each), formal statistical stratification by genetic subtype was not feasible; differences in clinical and metabolic phenotype across mechanisms are therefore described qualitatively at the case level rather than compared statistically.

Three findings involved the leptin-MC4R pathway. The MC4R nonsense variant c.831T>A (p.C277*) was classified as pathogenic. This variant was carried by proband P2, a girl evaluated at 9 years of age whose phenotype was consistent with MC4R-related monogenic obesity: early-onset obesity with parent-reported onset at 3 years of age (BMI 21.6 kg/m2), acanthosis nigricans, and insulin resistance (fasting insulin 13.8 mU/L; HOMA-IR 2.72), without dysmorphic or syndromic features. Serum aminotransferases were within the normal range (ALT 23.7 U/L, AST 23.3 U/L) and the lipid profile was largely unremarkable (total cholesterol 3.93 mmol/L, triglycerides 1.51 mmol/L, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 2.21 mmol/L). There was a strong maternal family history of obesity (mother, younger sister, and maternal grandmother), and the variant was inherited in the heterozygous state from the proband’s father. Severe hyperphagia, an exclusion criterion in this cohort, was not present, and standardized quantitative appetite phenotyping was not performed. SH2B1 c.1019G>A (p.R340Q) and NCOA1 c.2936C>T (p.T979M) were classified as VUS because available clinical and computational evidence supported relevance but was insufficient for pathogenic or likely pathogenic classification.

A homozygous BBS1 missense variant, c.1121C>G (p.T374S), was identified in a boy with severe obesity and hepatic steatosis. Despite the homozygous state and phenotype correlation, limited functional evidence necessitated retention as VUS. Notably, p.T374S is relatively common in EAS reference populations, with a gnomAD EAS allele frequency about 1.5-fold above the global figure (Figure 2). It may therefore be a benign or hypomorphic polymorphism rather than a fully penetrant disease-causing allele, and the homozygous state may partly reflect this higher background frequency. Confirming or excluding pathogenicity will require functional study of BBSome assembly and ciliary function, family segregation, and adequately powered EAS case-control data; until then its contribution to the proband phenotype remains unproven. The heterozygous UCP3 c.208C>T (p.R70W) variant was classified as VUS with phenotypic correlation; although previously reported in a type 2 diabetes family, the absence of functional validation and limited gene-disease evidence for UCP3 in monogenic obesity precluded a higher classification (19). One child had maternal UPD of 15q11-13 with Prader-Willi syndrome-like features; MLPA established the imprinting abnormality and WES-derived ROH provided corroborating evidence. This case illustrates the value of the integrated, multi-technology approach: the 15q11-13 imprinting defect accounted for 1 of the 6 clinically relevant findings and 1 of the 2 definitive diagnoses, and would have been missed by short-read WES alone, which does not reliably detect UPD or methylation abnormalities. ROH analysis identified a single large homozygous region on chromosome 15 (chr15:22,765,664–101,981,189, GRCh37/hg19; ~79.2 Mb), and methylation-specific MLPA showed a maternal (Prader-Willi-pattern) SNRPN hypermethylation with normal copy number; maternal origin was confirmed by microsatellite (STR) segregation in the trio, the proband carrying a single maternal allele with no paternal allele at any informative chromosome-15 locus.

The distribution of findings across biological mechanisms is shown in Figure 3. When the children with clinically relevant findings were grouped by the underlying biological mechanism, the accompanying metabolic features were heterogeneous and did not follow a single pathway-specific pattern; the subgroups were too small for formal statistical comparison, and these observations are descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. The most pronounced hepatic involvement was seen in the child with imprinting-related disease (P6: ALT 182.6 U/L, AST 90.7 U/L, BMI 28.9 kg/m2, HOMA-IR 3.51) and in the child with the BBSome-related BBS1 variant (P1: ALT 163.2 U/L, AST 140.6 U/L, BMI 23.4 kg/m2), both with hepatic steatosis; notably, however, the BBS1 case had a low HOMA-IR (0.38), dissociating the hepatic phenotype from insulin resistance. Within the leptin-melanocortin axis the picture was mixed: the child with the SH2B1 variant had elevated aminotransferases with steatosis and the highest-range insulin resistance (P4: ALT 74.0 U/L, AST 42.0 U/L, BMI 22.2 kg/m2, HOMA-IR 3.43), whereas the children with the MC4R and NCOA1 variants had normal aminotransferases without steatosis (P2: ALT 23.7 U/L, AST 23.3 U/L, HOMA-IR 2.72; P3: ALT 24.1 U/L, AST 21.6 U/L, HOMA-IR 2.07). The child with the UCP3 fatty-acid-oxidation variant had the mildest biochemical profile (P5: ALT 17.0 U/L, AST 19.8 U/L, BMI 23.0 kg/m2, HOMA-IR 2.82, no steatosis). EAS population-frequency comparison

Rare variant frequencies are compared between gnomAD EAS and global datasets in Figure 2. Among the five identified SNVs, BBS1 p.T374S showed relative enrichment in the EAS dataset (EAS MAF 1.21×10–4 vs. global MAF 0.83×10–4; EAS/global ratio 1.46). The remaining variants were less frequent in the EAS subset than globally: MC4R p.C277* (ratio 0.44), NCOA1 p.T979M (ratio 0.54), SH2B1 p.R340Q (ratio 0.76), and UCP3 p.R70W (ratio 0.51). All five SNVs remained rare in both datasets. The maternal UPD of 15q11-13 represents a chromosomal imprinting mechanism and was not included in the allele-frequency comparison.

Discussion

Key findings

In this retrospective single-center cohort of Chinese children with obesity onset before 5 years of age, a WES-centered integrated evaluation identified clinically relevant findings in 26.1% of the analytic cohort and definitive/likely molecular diagnoses in 8.7%. Positive findings spanned four biological mechanisms, underscoring the genetic heterogeneity underlying apparently uniform early-onset obesity presentations.

Strengths and limitations

A central strength of this workflow is its integrative rather than purely WES-based design. WES efficiently interrogated SNV/indels and supported CNV/ROH analysis; however, the 15q11-13 maternal UPD diagnosis required orthogonal MLPA confirmation of the imprinting abnormality. In practice, a clinically useful genomic workflow for early-onset obesity in Chinese children may need complementary assays when the phenotype raises suspicion of Prader-Willi syndrome or another imprinting disorder—information not routinely captured in studies reporting WES alone.

The study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The sample was small and derived from a single tertiary center, limiting both precision of yield estimates and generalizability. Twelve of 35 eligible children (34%) did not complete the full inpatient evaluation because their guardians declined one or more components of the protocolized workup. This attrition rate is substantial and may have introduced selection bias in either direction: toward more clinically complex cases if severely affected families were more motivated to pursue comprehensive evaluation, or toward less complex cases if the burden of inpatient testing deterred families of less severely affected children. Because the 12 excluded children were not systematically characterized, we cannot empirically assess the magnitude or direction of this bias. For these reasons, the absolute proportions reported here - including the 26.1% clinically relevant finding rate and the 8.7% definitive or likely-pathogenic diagnostic yield - should be regarded as provisional, exploratory point estimates with wide and largely unquantified confidence limits, rather than as precise population frequencies. They require confirmation in larger, prospectively and consecutively recruited multicenter cohorts before being generalized to Chinese children with early-onset obesity.

The pronounced group size imbalance (6 genetically positive vs. 17 genetically negative) further reduced statistical power for between-group inference; non-significant comparisons should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of no true difference. For ALT and AST, large standardized effect sizes (Hedges’ g approximately 1.0) despite non-significant P values suggest that real between-group differences may exist but require larger samples to be detected reliably. Four of six positive cases remained at the VUS level without functional confirmation, meaning the clinically relevant yield overstates the proportion with actionable diagnoses. Because four of the six findings are variants of uncertain significance, the mechanistic and genotype-phenotype attributions made for these cases—including the assignment of individual children to specific biological pathways and the inference that their metabolic features are caused by the identified variant—should be regarded as tentative and hypothesis-generating. Such variants may ultimately be reclassified as benign or as only weakly contributory, and their causal role cannot be established without family segregation data and functional validation. Detailed appetite phenotyping, parental anthropometrics, birth history, and other potentially informative covariates were not systematically captured in the retrospective review. In particular, MASLD was evaluated only on the basis of ultrasonographically detected hepatic steatosis together with routine aminotransferase measurement; systematic quantification of liver fat, hepatic elastography, detailed body-composition analysis, and histological confirmation were not performed, so the hepatic and metabolic phenotype reported here may be incompletely characterized. Finally, the population-frequency analysis was based on gnomAD v2.1.1, which contains a relatively limited EAS representation; larger reference datasets are needed to validate population-specific frequency patterns.

Comparison with similar research

The clinically relevant finding rate of 26.1% falls within the broad range reported across genetic studies of pediatric early-onset obesity (9-11). Direct comparison is, however, confounded by marked differences in case selection, phenotypic enrichment, sequencing platform, and—critically—how diagnostic yield is defined. A recent Belgian real-world cohort using targeted panel testing reported positive findings in approximately 3.1% of 223 patients, with higher rates among those with early-onset phenotypes (10). A Qatari cohort of 243 children screened with a 52-gene targeted panel identified likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants in 14.8% (9). A large German exome-wide study reported monogenic obesity diagnosed in 5.8% of 521 patients, with higher rates in children and syndromic cases (11). The 8.7 % definitive/likely yield in the present study is broadly comparable to these pediatric-enriched series; the higher overall clinically relevant rate (26.1%) reflects inclusion of VUS with phenotype correlation and should not be compared directly to series reporting only pathogenic/likely pathogenic findings. Beyond these methodological factors, the apparent differences in yield across the Belgian, Qatari, German, and present Chinese cohorts are difficult to attribute to ethnicity alone, because they are confounded by several non-genetic factors: the stringency and nature of phenotypic ascertainment (the degree of early-onset, syndromic, or severity enrichment), the breadth of the genetic test applied (targeted panels of a few dozen genes versus exome-wide analysis), the variant-classification framework used, and differences in sample size and statistical precision. Genuine population-genetic contributions—such as differing allele frequencies of obesity-associated variants, founder effects, and rates of parental consanguinity, which are high in some Middle Eastern populations and can inflate the apparent yield of recessive disease—may also contribute, but cannot be disentangled from these design differences in the absence of harmonized, ancestry-matched studies. The present observation that BBS1 p.T374S is comparatively common in EAS reference populations illustrates how population-specific allele frequencies can influence both which variants are detected and how confidently they can be interpreted.

Explanations of findings

Clustering of findings within the leptin-MC4R axis is biologically expected and consistent with the central role of this pathway in hypothalamic energy homeostasis (5,20). Nevertheless, two of three leptin-MC4R pathway findings in this cohort remained VUS, and the results should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than as immediate indications for pathway-directed pharmacotherapy. The most immediate clinical value of the two definitive diagnoses in this cohort lies in etiologic clarification, cascade family counseling, and phenotype-directed surveillance. The VUS-associated findings, including UCP3 c.208C>T (p.R70W), should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and not as definitive molecular diagnoses. The BBS1 finding supports continued surveillance for extra-weight manifestations of Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and the 15q11-13 maternal UPD case has direct implications for Prader-Willi syndrome-directed management. These disorder-specific consequences justify comprehensive genetic workup even when classical syndromic features are not fully expressed at the time of presentation. The clinical relevance of precise genetic diagnosis is further underscored by the expanding therapeutic landscape: setmelanotide, an MC4R agonist, is now approved for children as young as 2 years with POMC, LEPR, PCSK1 deficiency, or Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and phase 3 trial data in the 2–5-year age group support its benefit as an early intervention (8).

Liver enzyme levels showed a consistent directional tendency toward elevation in the genetically positive group, with large standardized effect sizes for both ALT (Hedges’ g=0.96) and AST (g=1.07); WBC and 4 pm cortisol showed similar directional trends of smaller effect size, whereas monocyte count was comparable between groups. None of these comparisons reached statistical significance after multiple-comparison correction, a result that is consistent with, rather than contradictory to, the presence of a true biological signal given the small positive group (n=6) and the resulting low detection power. The magnitude of the median ALT difference (approximately 2-fold), its concordance with case-level phenotyping (hepatic steatosis in the BBS1 proband, steatosis and insulin resistance in the SH2B1 proband), and the consistency of effect direction across four inflammatory and metabolic markers together suggest a biologically plausible signal that merits prospective investigation in larger cohort. These findings are consistent with the broader literature linking obesity-related inflammation, adipose tissue macrophage polarization, and metabolic liver disease (21-24), and may reflect more severe metabolic dysregulation in children with underlying genetic vulnerability. Whether elevated liver enzymes provide clinically useful guidance for genetic referral warrants prospective evaluation in larger cohorts. When the clinically relevant findings were considered by biological mechanism, the metabolic phenotype was heterogeneous and did not follow a simple pathway-dependent gradient. The most marked hepatic involvement was seen in the imprinting-related case and in the BBSome-related BBS1 case, yet these two children diverged sharply in insulin resistance, the BBS1 case having a notably low HOMA-IR despite high aminotransferases; and within the leptin-melanocortin axis only the SH2B1 case showed elevated aminotransferases, whereas the MC4R and NCOA1 cases had normal values, and the fatty-acid-oxidation (UCP3) case had the mildest biochemical profile. Any apparent mechanism-related differences are therefore inconsistent and must be interpreted with caution, because each subgroup comprised only one or two children and was far too small for statistical comparison; the pattern is descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and would need to be confirmed in adequately powered, mechanism-stratified cohorts. The relative enrichment of BBS1 p.T374S in the EAS gnomAD dataset compared with the global dataset suggests that population frequency context can materially influence prior probability and variant interpretation. However, conclusions must remain tentative given the small cohort size, the limited EAS representation in gnomAD v2.1.1, and the absence of functional validation. The present study contributes an EAS-oriented reference point rather than a definitive population framework; larger multicenter EAS sequencing efforts will be required to characterize population-specific pathogenic variant spectra and inform ethnicity-adjusted interpretation criteria.

Implications and actions needed

Based on this retrospective experience, we suggest that genetic evaluation using a WES-centered integrated approach be prioritized in Chinese children presenting with obesity onset before age 5 years, particularly when accompanied by hyperphagia, syndromic features, a family history of severe obesity, or unexplained metabolic complications such as elevated liver enzymes. The workflow described here—combining WES with MLPA for imprinting loci and ROH analysis—captured a range of genetic mechanisms that would not have been fully characterized by WES alone. Reporting of diagnostic yield should distinguish definitive/likely diagnoses from VUS-associated findings to support accurate counseling and facilitate valid cross-study comparisons.

Conclusions

In this retrospective single-center cohort, a WES-centered integrated workflow identified clinically relevant genetic findings in 6 of 23 Chinese children with early-onset obesity, yielding definitive/likely molecular diagnoses in 2 of 23 cases. The identified findings, including four VUS-associated findings, mapped to four biological mechanisms/categories and included an imprinting disorder detectable only through orthogonal assays. Elevated liver enzymes, although not reaching statistical significance in this small sample, showed the largest effect-size signal (Hedges’ g approximately 1.0 for both ALT and AST) among the clinical variables examined and represent the most promising candidate biomarker for prospective evaluation in larger cohorts. These results support considering genetic evaluation in carefully phenotyped Chinese children with obesity onset before age 5 years—particularly those with syndromic features, hyperphagia, family history, or unexplained metabolic complications. Larger multicenter prospective studies and functional validation of VUS are needed to establish robust population-specific diagnostic-yield estimates and guide broader clinical application of genetic testing in this setting.
