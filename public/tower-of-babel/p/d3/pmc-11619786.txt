The intestinal microbiome, but not clinical aspects of inflammatory bowel disease, is impacted by lactose malabsorption compared to lactose digestion in children
Authors: Alexandra Cohen, Jennifer Li, James Butcher, Ruth Singleton, Pauline Barbeau, Alain Stintzi, David R Mack
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDietary exclusion of lactose from patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) persists with speculation that deleterious effects are mediated through intestinal microbes.ObjectivesTo compare IBD characteristics and changes in the intestinal microbiome (IM) at diagnosis in children with and without lactose malabsorption (LM).MethodsA cross-sectional cohort of children (8–17 y of age) diagnosed with Crohn’s disease [n = 149 (63%)] or ulcerative colitis (n = 86) that had undergone lactose breath hydrogen testing was evaluated. The IM of mucosal luminal aspirates was profiled at the time of diagnosis using 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid gene amplicon sequencing of the V6 hypervariable region.ResultsOf the 235 children, 61 (26%) had LM. Microbial characterization yielded differences in bacterial differential abundance between children who could and could not absorb lactose, which varied by intestinal site and between subtypes of IBD. There were no differences in the ages [13.2 ± 3.0 y (mean ± standard deviation) compared with 12.7 ± 3.4 y; P = 0.25], sex (P = 0.88), extent of disease involvement or severity of disease at presentation (P = 0.74) when comparing those that could or could not absorb lactose nor was there a difference in the need for initiation of biological agents (P = 0.43) during 2 y of follow-up.ConclusionsLM does not affect the clinical presentation or outcomes of children with IBD. However, this study establishes that a single nonabsorbed fermentable food product can alter the IM in both a regional and disease-specific manner. As we continue to learn more about the pathophysiology of IBD and the role of the IM in disease onset and progression, it would be of benefit to examine the impact of other potential fermentable nutrients and their products on IBD outcomes.

Introduction

Patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) have had alterations to their intestinal microbiome (IM) described [1,2]. Thus, there is interest in identifying whether specific microbes are involved in IBD pathogenesis and whether IM restoration will have beneficial clinical effects for either of the 2 main subtypes of IBD, namely Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). One way to change the IM is using antibiotics; however, in CD, antibiotics do not provide a meaningful clinical benefit [3], are associated with a worsened clinical presentation at the time of diagnosis [1], and are even associated with an increased risk of its developing [4]. Another approach to induce changes in the IM is through one’s diet [5]. Different dietary approaches are advocated to improve outcomes in patients with IBD, such as the use of exclusive enteral nutrition, the CD exclusion diet, the specific carbohydrate diet, the Mediterranean diet, and the IBD anti-inflammatory diet. A Mediterranean-like diet decreases subclinical inflammation in CD, an effect thought to be partially mediated by the IM, although no single food item is identified to be responsible for this effect [6]. However, the effects of these proposed diets on inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein may be minimal [7]. A common theme in dietary interventions is to reduce or eliminate specific nutrients such as lactose (e.g., the specific carbohydrate diet and the IBD anti-inflammatory diet). Although lactose intake is associated with UC relapse [8], other disease aspects of IBD relating to the presence or absence of lactose and its resultant effects on the IM in IBD are unclear.

Lactose represents a common carbohydrate in diets due to its presence in dairy products and is often recommended to growing children as a source of calcium to support bone growth. Lactose malabsorption (LM) can be due to several potential causes, including congenital lactase deficiency, primary lactase nonpersistence, or secondary to various intestinal pathologies [9,10]. Nonabsorbed lactose will transit into regions of the bowel harboring large numbers of microbes, such as the terminal ileum (TI) and colon, where it undergoes microbial fermentation, and resultant products may include molecules not generated by human cells, such as hydrogen gas. Following its production, hydrogen gas passes from the intestinal lumen to the lungs, resulting in an increase in exhaled breath, which can be measured in clinical practice to diagnose LM [11]. Thus, dietary LM provides the potential to study the effect of a single food component, namely lactose, on the IM. To assess the impact of this single nutrient on IBD clinical features and IM composition, we evaluated the IM composition and clinical characteristics of children diagnosed with either CD or UC, stratified by LM.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional cohort of 235 children diagnosed with IBD [CD: n = 149 (63%); UC: n = 86 (37%)] between 2008 and 2022 who had undergone lactose breath hydrogen testing (LBHT) and had 2 y follow-up were included (Figure 1). Some of the participants (n = 185) had previously been prospectively enrolled in a Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario Review Ethics Board-approved microbiome study (#09/37X). All participants were IBD treatment naïve, and participants of the microbiome study had mucosal luminal interface (MLI) aspirate sampling (see below) performed during their diagnostic colonoscopy for suspected IBD. Exclusion criteria for microbiome study recruitment and collection of MLI aspirates for the cohort included 1) presence of diabetes mellitus, 2) presence of documented or suspected infectious diarrhea within the previous 2 mo, and 3) use of antibiotics or probiotics within the past 4 wk. Diagnosis of CD or UC was made following thorough clinical, endoscopic, histologic, and radiologic evaluations according to standardized criteria [12] to reduce observer bias of disease sub-type, location, and severity. Disease location was described using the Montreal modification to the Paris classification system [13]. For CD, the clinical severity was reported using the Pediatric CD activity index [14], and for UC, the Pediatric UC activity index [15] was used. Clinical data was managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) and hosted at the CHEO Research Institute. REDCap is a secure, web-based application designed to support data capture for research studies [16]. The dietary intake of participants (n = 74) was collected using a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [17] administered prior to LBHT and before contemplation of this study to reduce recall bias. The choice of therapy for patients was based on published guidelines [18,19] to minimize variation in care with the final choices for medical therapy made by the treating physician, patient, and family.FIGURE 1Participant cohort flowchart. LBHT

LBHT was performed by a trained registered practical nurse in children diagnosed with IBD and who initially had symptoms of LM (e.g., diarrhea, pain, and bloating). To mitigate potential confounding ascertainment bias factors on LBHT results that may be associated with active disease, LBHT was conducted after the initiation of IBD induction treatments once patients had shown clinical improvement and finished the induction therapy. Testing began at 08:00 using a standardized protocol [11] and according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the testing machine (Quintron Instrument Company) and using Quintron breath sample collectors. Lactose (1 g/kg body weight) was prepared as a 20% solution to a maximum dose of 25 g lactose per patient in the hospital pharmacy and was sent to the testing room on the day of LBHT. The evening prior to undergoing LBHT, patients were instructed to ingest foods with low potential for hydrogen gas production (i.e., a meal consisting of chicken and rice) and then to fast overnight. Additional requirements included: no antibiotics within the preceding 4 wk, no colonic cleansing in the preceding 2 wk, and no cigarette smoking or exercise for ≥2 h before LBHT [11]. If baseline breath hydrogen samples were above 15 parts per million (ppm), LBHT was not performed and was re-scheduled. Expiratory breath samples were collected at 30-min intervals for 3 h, and hydrogen measurements were made immediately following their collection. A rise of ≥20 ppm of hydrogen above baseline was considered a positive test indicating LM, whereas a rise of <20 ppm indicated lactose digestion [normal lactose absorption (non-LM)]. Carbon dioxide levels were also measured to confirm that end-expiratory breath was collected and was used to adjust hydrogen values accordingly. There were no subjects that had a rise in methane distinct from a rise in hydrogen.

MLI aspirate collection

MLI aspirates (40–80 mL) were collected from participants during their diagnostic endoscopy, with sampling either being performed or supervised by a single physician to reduce sampling bias. In brief, a colonoscopy was performed following a 1-d standard colon clean-out preparation [20], with first aspirating and discarding any existing fluid and luminal debris. Sterile water was flushed through the colonoscope onto the mucosa of the site of interest [i.e., descending colon (DC), ascending colon (AC), and lastly from the TI] and then aspirated through the colonoscope into a sterile container, as described previously [2,21]. The MLI samples were immediately put on ice in the endoscopy room, transported to the laboratory, and aliquot within 30 min for storage at –80°C until further processing.

Amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA hypervariable region 6

The 16S rRNA hypervariable region 6 (V6-16S) gene amplicon libraries were constructed and sequenced as previously described [2,22]. MLI aspirate samples were processed in batches as we obtained sufficient samples for constructing sequencing libraries. Samples were randomly assigned within each sequencing batch without considering their IBD phenotype. Briefly, MLI metagenomic DNA was extracted from 2 mL aspirate using a FastPrep-24 homogenizer and Fast-DNA Spin Kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions with an additional round of lysis followed by a 5 min incubation on ice. MLI metagenomic DNA was eluted in sterile nuclease-free water, quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and stored at –20°C until library construction.

Two sequential polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were used for V6-16S library construction to first amplify the targeted V6-16S rRNA hypervariable gene region and then to incorporate Illumina paired-end sequencing adapters. The PCRs consisted of 50 ng of metagenomic DNA, 0.5 μmol of each primer, and a Phusion flash high-fidelity PCR master mix. The cycling conditions were an initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s, 10 cycles of 5 s at 98°C, 15 s at 61°C, and 15 s 72°C (with an annealing temperature stepdown of 1°C per cycle), 15 cycles at an annealing temperature of 51°C, and a final extension of 5 min at 72°C. The second PCR used 10 μL of the initial amplicon, the same concentration of primers, and Phusion with cycling consisting of an initial denaturation of 1 min at 98°C, 15 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 30 s at 65°C, 30 s at 72°C, and a final elongation of 5 min at 72°C. PCR amplicons were purified using a MilliPore MultiScreen PCR96 plate. DNA concentrations were again quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and samples were pooled in equimolar amounts. The final library was gel purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq to generate 100 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatic and statistical analyses

Clinical characteristics

Cochrane’s χ2 test was used in most clinical analyses with a few exceptions: Fisher’s exact test was used for 2 x 2 analyses with cells that contained <5 counts and the Freeman-Halton test was used for R x C (>2 x 2) analyses with cells that contained <5 counts. Ages were presented as mean age in years (± SD), and statistical significance was assessed using a 2-sample t-test. Mutually exclusive variables (sex, severity, and location of disease at diagnosis) are presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences in nutritional intake as measured by the FFQ were assessed using Wilcox tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate corrections. Analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4. P values of <0.05 were considered significant.

IM

Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed using cutadapt and subsequently processed into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using dada2. Demultiplexed reads were deposited to the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive under accession PRJNA1062375. Briefly, demultiplexed reads from each HiSeq lane were filtered to remove primer sequences, read pairs with >1 expected error, denoized using pooling, and the pairs merged using the default parameters. Samples from the different analyses were subsequently merged; chimera was filtered and had their taxonomy assigned against the high quality ribosomal RNA SILVA 132 database [23] using the ribosomal database project naïve Bayesian classifier algorithm. Phylogenetic distances were calculated by inserting the ASVs into the SILVA 132 backbone tree using the phylogenetic placement SEPP technique [24]. Samples with <150,000 reads were discarded from the analysis, and all samples were rarefied to 150,000 using phyloseq. Phyloseq was used to assess alpha (α) and beta (β) diversities, and differences in β diversities for LM and non-LM at each region were assessed using permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), as implemented in the vegan R package [25]. Taxa at each sampling site were identified as being differentially abundant between LM and non-LM participants using the differential gene expression analysis software program DESeq2 [26] with absolute log2 fold changes >1 and adjusted P values <0.01 considered to be significant.

Ethics approval

Aspirate samples and data collection from participants were approved by the hospital review ethics board with informed consent/assent obtained from parents and/or participants, as appropriate.

Results

Study population

Among the participants, 26.0% (n = 61) tested positive for LM, whereas 74% (n = 174) did not (Figure 1). There were no differences in age, sex, or clinical severity of disease between participants with LM and non-LM at the time of diagnosis (Table 1). In comparing LM and non-LM (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1), there were no differences in the intestinal sites of CD or UC involvement. Most participants with UC had extensive involvement of the colon (83%), and most participants with either CD or UC had moderate or severe clinical disease (82%) at the time of diagnosis (Supplemental Table 1). Furthermore, during 2 y of follow-up, the progression of treatments to monoclonal antibody therapy for CD or UC for disease control was similar between children with LM and non-LM (Table 1). Mucosal luminal aspirate IM characterization

As shown in Figure 1, a total of 185 participants (CD = 118; UC = 67) underwent MLI sampling. This contributed to 460 MLI samples collected at the TI, AC, and DC sites (Supplemental Table 2). Consequently, most children had their microbial composition characterized from ≥1 sampled intestinal site. Among the 3 geographical sites where aspirates were performed, the AC site had the greatest number of samples collected with IM characterization (Supplemental Table 2). Similar to the larger cohort, in the sub-cohort of 185 participants with MLI characterization, there were no differences in age at diagnosis, sex, clinical severity at diagnosis, and time from diagnosis to the start of monoclonal antibody therapy (Supplemental Table 3) between the LM and non-LM groups. In addition, 74 participants (LM = 19; non-LM = 55) completed an FFQ prior to MLI sample collection or within 3 d afterward (Figure 1). There was no difference in the intake of any of the nutrients measured by the FFQ (including lactose) between the LM and non-LM groups, nor were there any participants actively avoiding lactose ingestion at the time of their IBD diagnosis.

In the CD with LM group, significant changes in ≥87, 71, and 36 ASVs at the TI, AC, and DC, respectively, were detected. The most marked differences of enrichment in participants with CD and LM were in ASVs belonging to the genera Blautia, Oscillibacter, and Bacteroides in the TI, AC, and DC, respectively, with Blautia and Oscillibacter being more abundant in multiple biogeographical locations in CD.

Participants with UC and LM showed significant changes in 58, 91, and 62 ASVs at the TI, AC, and DC, respectively. For UC, increased relative enrichments in the ASVs were from the genera Bacteroides and Lachnoclostridium at the TI, Parabacteroides and Epulopiscium at the AC, and Anaerostipes at the DC site. Interestingly, ASVs from the genus Bacteroides showed variable abundance patterns, with ASVs from this genus being both increased and decreased in abundance in the LM group compared to the non-LM group, depending on the region of the intestinal tract.

Discussion

There is considerable interest in studying perturbations of the IM as it relates to the pathogenesis of IBD [27,28]. As the prevalence of IBD mirrors the frequency of a population’s dairy food consumption and patients with IBD report reduced symptomatology, there has been speculation that LM may be a factor in IBD development or disease progression [29,30]. However, we found there was no difference in clinical presentation or in the 2-y outcomes of children diagnosed with IBD, regardless of their ability to digest lactose.

The observed frequency of LM in our population was 26%, similar to the 29% prevalence rate for a Chicago population of children with IBD [31] and the estimated lactase nonpersistence frequency in Ontario [9]. Importantly, participants who could digest lactose and participants with LM both reported lactose intake at the time of their diagnosis and taken together; this suggests that the presence of nonabsorbed lactose reaching the lower gastrointestinal tract does not impact the initial presentation of IBD or consequent outcomes.

Site-specific MLI samples allowed for the assessment of IM differences in regions with anticipated higher concentrations of lactose (i.e., TI and AC), and although there were no clinical differences in the LM compared with non-LM participants with IBD, we were able to identify differences in IM composition. These differences, however, did not translate into discernable variations in overall microbial α- (Figure 2) or β-diversities (Figure 3, Supplemental Figures 1–3). This finding is not overtly surprising, as we have previously reported a lack of differences in the α- or β-diversity of the IM between children with and without CD, also using MLI samples collected from the AC at the time of diagnosis [2]. Nevertheless, our investigation identified specific taxa exhibiting differential abundance in children with IBD diagnosed with LM compared with no LM at all 3 biogeographical regions assessed, with these differences also being dependent on IBD sub-type. This suggests that the microbiotas of patients, either with CD or UC, are composed of members who are differentially equipped to exploit the presence of fermentable products. Furthermore, our findings imply that both the microbes affected by the presence of lactose as well as the metabolic byproducts produced through lactose fermentation do not play a key role in the presentation of IBD.

Differential abundance analyses conducted on participants with CD unveiled regional variations, with the majority of ASV changes being a lower relative abundance. For example, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which has garnered interest in relation to IBD pathogenesis, has been found by us and others to be increased at the genus level in samples from children with CD compared to children without CD, with some differences being strain-related [2,27]. In the present study, children with CD and non-LM showed decreases in specific Faecalibacterium ASVs. Identifying these specific strains and understanding their functional capabilities would be key to unraveling the consequences of these differences. We also observed increases in ASVs belonging to the families known to be capable of utilizing lactose (e.g., Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroidaceae, and Ruminococcaceae, Supplemental Table 4), in our participants with LM [32]. Consistent with our observations is a report that the relative abundance of members from the Lachnospiraceae family was found to be higher in individuals with LM who consume a high dose of lactose [32]. It is also noteworthy that not all members within given genera (e.g., Bacteroides) had the same pattern of differential abundance in the LM and non-LM groups (Figure 4). This could be due to each species differing in their ability/propensity for lactose metabolism and, thus, not all being able to exploit this resource. Our 16S amplicon rRNA gene amplicon approach cannot identify the specific bacterial taxa that can actively ferment lactose compared with those that cross-feed on byproducts of lactose fermentation.

Although sampling from the bowel following a clean-out cannot fully represent conditions without a clean-out [33], using regional MLI samples is a strength of this study. It allowed us to investigate the IM composition regionally and assess the tightly adherent MLI communities [21]. Another strength is the collection of dietary lactose intake data prior to LBHT. The absence of significant differences in lactose intake between the LM and non-LM groups rules out lactose avoidance as a potential confounding variable in characterizing the IM. However, dietary intake was not monitored throughout the 2-y follow-up period, and therefore, we lack information on whether changes in dietary habits during this period might have influenced the observed lack of differences in the progression to monoclonal antibodies and disease complication rates. Future research could benefit from incorporating more comprehensive measures to directly assess the functions of each microbial member (e.g., shotgun metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, and/or metabolomics) to differentiate between microbes actively fermenting lactose and those that are cross-feeders.

In summary, our study demonstrates that individuals with CD or UC and who have LM exhibit similar clinical presentation at diagnosis and have a similar natural history as those who can digest lactose. In addition, this study demonstrates that a single nonabsorbed fermentable food product that passes through the intestinal tract to the TI and colon is associated with a different IM composition in both a regional and disease-specific manner. Although the cohort evaluated was young, we anticipate that these findings are applicable across various age groups, suggesting there is minimal rationale for patients with IBD who are lactose tolerant to avoid lactose. As we continue to learn more about the pathophysiology of IBD and the role of the IM in disease onset and progression, it would be of benefit to examine the effects of other fermentable nutrients on IBD outcomes.
