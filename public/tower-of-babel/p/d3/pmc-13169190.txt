Association between prescribed parenteral energy doses and the composite of clinical adverse events in adult patients after gastrointestinal cancer surgery: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yoshikuni Kawaguchi, Kenta Murotani, Nahoki Hayashi, Satoru Kamoshita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to compare the impact of prescribed parenteral energy on clinical outcomes in patients who exclusively received parenteral nutrition after gastrointestinal cancer surgery.MethodsWe identified patients who exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days after surgery using a medical claims database. Based on the mean parenteral energy doses during postoperative days 1–7, patients were divided into restricted‐energy (5–10 kcal/kg/day), low‐energy (10–20 kcal/kg/day), and standard‐energy (20–30 kcal/kg/day) groups. The primary outcome was a composite of clinical adverse events, and secondary outcomes included in‐hospital mortality, postoperative complications, declines in activities of daily living, hospital readmission, and length of hospital stay (LOS). Multivariable logistic and multiple regression analyses were employed using the restricted‐energy group as the reference.ResultsOf 18,294 eligible patients, 6727, 9760, and 1807 were classified into the restricted‐, low‐, and standard‐energy groups, respectively. The adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) for the composite outcome was 1.062 (0.972–1.161; P = 0.18) in the low‐energy group and 1.093 (0.942–1.267; P = 0.24) in the standard‐energy group. For in‐hospital mortality, the adjusted odds ratio was 1.060 (1.057–1.062; P < 0.001) in the low‐energy group and 1.281 (1.275–1.287; P < 0.001) in the standard‐energy group. The adjusted regression coefficient (95% confidence interval) for LOS was 2.0 (0.7–3.3; P = 0.004) in the low‐energy group and 3.2 (1.0–5.5; P = 0.005) in the standard‐energy group. No significant differences were observed in other outcomes.ConclusionEarly postoperative higher energy doses were associated with increased in‐hospital mortality and LOS.Keywords: clinical outcome, early parenteral nutrition, energy restriction, gastrointestinal cancer surgery, real‐world data

INTRODUCTION

Nutritional management during the acute phase is crucial for clinical outcomes. 1 , 2 Recently, hypocaloric feeding, which limits energy intake after intensive care unit (ICU) admission, has been recommended for critically ill patients. 3 In contrast, perioperative nutritional guidelines recommend an energy intake of 20–30 kcal/kg/day without energy restriction. 4 , 5 , 6 Because the early postoperative period is considered an acute phase, energy restriction might similarly improve clinical outcomes. 7

A multicenter randomized trial showed that ICU patients who initiated parenteral nutrition within 48 h targeting 20–30 kcal/kg/day had more infectious complications than those who started after day 8. 8 Another multicenter randomized trial showed that patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support for shock under restricted nutrition (6 kcal/kg/day) within 24 h had shorter ICU stays and fewer complications than those receiving standard nutrition (25 kcal/kg/day). 9 Conversely, a multicenter randomized trial in patients undergoing major gastrointestinal surgery found that initiating parenteral nutrition on postoperative day 3 at 25–30 kcal/kg/day reduced infectious complications. 10 These inconsistent findings suggest that the impact of early postoperative energy restriction on clinical outcomes remains unclear. 7

Patients who exclusively receive parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days after gastrointestinal cancer surgery generally have greater disease severity, making energy intake more likely to influence outcomes. Real‐world data indicate that only 20%–30% of these patients achieved ≥20 kcal/kg/day by postoperative day 7, 11 and prescribed energy doses have declined over 12 years, 12 reflecting a trend toward energy restriction. However, no study has specifically evaluated the impact of energy restriction in this population. Understanding the impact of energy restriction could offer new insights into early postoperative nutritional management. We hypothesized that higher energy doses would be associated with an increase in the incidence of the composite of clinical adverse events compared to early postoperative energy restriction in patients after gastrointestinal cancer surgery. Utilizing a Japanese national medical claims database, we conducted a retrospective cohort study in patients undergoing gastrointestinal cancer surgery who exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days.

METHODS

Study design and data source

Utilizing the Japanese national medical claims database (Medical Data Vision Co. Ltd.), we conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients who underwent gastrointestinal cancer surgery between 2011 and 2022 and exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days. The database contains data from approximately 42 million patients across 475 hospitals, representing 27% of acute care hospitals in Japan as of December 2022. It includes admission and discharge dates, patient characteristics at hospital admission, in‐hospital medical treatments, prescribed medications, hospitalization costs, and discharge outcomes. Diagnoses were identified using International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes, and medical treatments and medications were identified using Japan‐specific medical claims codes.

Ethics statements

This study was approved by the Shiba Palace Clinic Ethics Review Committee (Approval No. 153969_rn‐36564) and registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trial Registry (UMIN000053645). The database was fully anonymized, with no identifiable patient or hospital information; therefore, an opt‐out procedure was not implemented. This article complies with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology statement 13 and the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data statement. 14

Study patients

Adult patients who underwent gastrointestinal cancer surgery under general anesthesia between January 2011 and December 2022, and exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days because of an inability to tolerate oral intake or tube feeding, were included. Patients were identified when the surgical site based on Japan‐specific medical claims codes corresponded to the gastrointestinal cancer type defined by ICD‐10 codes. Oral intake and tube feeding were identified using Japan‐specific claims codes, and prescribed parenteral energy doses were calculated from product names and prescribed amounts. The ICD‐10 and Japan‐specific codes used for patient identification are shown in Table S1.

We excluded patients with missing or implausible values suggestive of input error (height <100 or ≥200 cm; weight <10 or ≥200 kg), those who died or were discharged within 7 days after surgery, those with implausible prescribed energy doses (mean energy doses during postoperative days 1–7 <5 kcal/kg/day), and those exceeding the upper limit of prescribed parenteral energy doses recommended by the Japanese clinical nutrition guideline 4 (mean energy doses during postoperative days 1–7 ≥30 kcal/kg/day). Postoperative day 1 was defined as the day after surgery.

Exposure and comparator

The exposures of interest were low prescribed energy (10–20 kcal/kg/day) and standard prescribed energy (20–30 kcal/kg/day), defined as the mean prescribed parenteral energy doses during postoperative days 1–7. The comparator was restricted prescribed energy (5–10 kcal/kg/day) during the same period.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was a composite of clinical adverse events, defined as any occurrence of in‐hospital mortality, postoperative complications, declines in activities of daily living (ADL), or hospital readmission. The secondary outcomes included in‐hospital mortality, postoperative complications, declines in ADL, hospital readmission, and length of hospital stay (LOS). Postoperative complications comprised wound dehiscence, wound infection, urinary tract infection, intravenous catheter infection, or pneumonia, identified using ICD‐10 codes (Table S1). A decline in ADL was defined as a decrease in the Barthel Index score 15 at discharge compared with admission. Hospital readmission was defined as an unplanned repeat hospitalization to the same hospital within 30 days of discharge from the index hospitalization. Declines in ADL, hospital readmission, and LOS were assessed only in patients discharged alive.

Variables

Data on patient characteristics, preoperative medical treatments, surgery‐day information, and prescribed parenteral nutrition doses during postoperative days 1–7 were identified from the database. The ICD‐10 and Japan‐specific medical claims codes used are shown in Table S1.

Patient characteristics and preoperative medical treatments

Patient characteristics at hospital admission included age, sex, height, body weight, hospital bed capacity, admission type and year, disease diagnosis (using ICD‐10), ADL (assessed using the Barthel Index), consciousness level (using the Japan Coma Scale 16 ), smoking history, and cancer stage (using the Tumor–Node–Metastasis [TNM] classification 17 ).

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and body weight obtained at hospital admission. Hospital bed capacity was extracted into the categories of <200, 200–500, or ≥500 beds. Admission type was extracted from the categories of emergency or elective. Admission year was categorized as 2011–2013, 2014–2016, 2017–2019, or 2020–2022. The Charlson Comorbidity Index 18 , 19 was calculated from ICD‐10 diagnoses. Barthel Index scores ranged from 0 (requiring full assistance) to 100 (requiring no assistance) and were categorized as 0, 5–40, 45–60, 65–95, or 100. Japan Coma Scale scores were recorded as 0 (alert), a 1‐digit code (not fully alert but awake without any stimuli), a 2‐digit code (arousable with stimulation), or a 3‐digit code (unarousable). 16 Low BMI was defined as a BMI < 18.5 in patients <70 years or a BMI < 20 in those ≥70 years, according to the Asian Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria. 20

Preoperative medical treatments from hospital admission to the day before surgery included oral management 21 (support for oral intake functions, including swallowing and/or chewing), tube feeding, parenteral nutrition (intravenous solutions containing amino acids, glucose, lipids, electrolytes, vitamins, and trace elements 22 ), duration of prescribed parenteral nutrition, and cancer chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy within 60 days before surgery.

Surgery‐day information

Surgery‐day variables included surgical site (esophagus, stomach, colon, rectum, liver, gallbladder/bile duct, pancreas, or multiple organs), surgical method (laparoscopic or open), crystalloids, colloids (dextrans and/or hydroxyethyl starches), albumin, blood transfusion (red blood cells, platelets, and/or fresh frozen plasma), vasopressors (ephedrine, phenylephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, norepinephrine, and/or epinephrine), central venous catheter insertion, and ICU admission.

Crystalloids were categorized as ≤5000, >5000 to ≤10,000, or >10,000 mL, and colloids, albumin, and blood products as 0, >0 to ≤500, or >500 mL.

Prescribed parenteral nutrition doses during postoperative days 1–7

The mean prescribed doses of energy (kcal/kg/day), amino acids (g/kg/day), lipids (g/kg/day), and dextrose (g/kg/day) were calculated using prescribed product names and amounts and adjusted for ideal body weight (22 × [height, m²]). Ideal body weight was used to avoid overestimation in underweight Japanese patients. 23 The calculations of energy, lipid, and dextrose doses included not only parenteral nutrition products but also medications in 5% dextrose in water or propofol, an anesthetic agent containing an injectable lipid emulsion (ILE) as a solvent. The mean prescribed parenteral energy and amino acid doses were calculated for each postoperative day from days 1 to 7, and their trends were illustrated. Moreover, the postoperative and total durations of parenteral nutrition were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data management and statistical analyses were performed independently by A2 Healthcare Corporation (Tokyo, Japan) using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc.).

Continuous variables were reported as medians (50%), the first quartile (Q1, 25%), and the third quartile (Q3, 75%), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were treated as a separate category, not imputed, and categorized as “Not available.” For comparison, the Kruskal–Wallis test (for continuous variables) or the Chi‐squared test (for categorical variables) was employed, with a significance level of 5% (two‐sided). Confidence intervals (CIs) were set at 95%.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis for categorical outcomes and multiple regression analysis for continuous outcomes were performed to evaluate the impact of prescribed parenteral energy on clinical outcomes, adjusting for all variables (age, sex, BMI [continuous variable], hospital bed capacity, admission type and year, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Barthel Index, Japan Coma Scale, smoking history, low BMI [according to the Asian GLIM criteria], TNM classification, preoperative oral management, preoperative artificial nutrition, preoperative cancer therapy, surgical site, surgical method, prescribed infusions, vasopressors, central venous catheter insertion, ICU admission, and mean prescribed doses of amino acids and lipids during postoperative days 1–7) (Tables 1, 2, 3). Using the restricted‐energy group as the reference, adjusted odds ratios and regression coefficients with 95% CIs were calculated for the low‐ and standard‐energy groups. For comparison, the t‐test (for continuous variables) or the Wald test (for categorical variables) was employed. Before the multivariable analysis, variance inflation factors were calculated to assess multicollinearity among covariates. Alpha allocation for multiplicity was not performed because this study was an observational study using a large medical claims database.

To confirm robustness, the mean prescribed nutrition doses during postoperative days 1–7 were adjusted for actual body weight, and the same analyses as in the main analysis were performed.

Subgroup analysis

To examine interactions, patients were divided into subgroups according to the presence or absence of lipids. Each outcome was used as the dependent variable, and either the group alone or the group with interaction terms was used as the independent variable. Multivariable analysis was then performed, adjusting for all variables listed in Tables 1, 2, 3, except for the mean prescribed energy, lipid, and dextrose doses during postoperative days 1–7.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics, preoperative medical treatments, and surgery‐day information

Of 20,700 patients, 18,294 met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 6727 (36.8%), 9760 (53.4%), and 1807 (9.9%) were classified into the restricted‐, low‐, and standard‐energy groups, respectively (Figure 1).

Study flow chart and disposition of 18,294 adult patients in Japan who exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days after gastrointestinal cancer surgery. aPostoperative day 1 is defined as the next day after surgery.

Patients aged ≥70 years accounted for 60.9% (n = 4096), 60.7% (n = 5923), and 65.6% (n = 1185) of the restricted‐, low‐, and standard‐energy groups, respectively. In the restricted‐energy group, 5311 patients (79.0%) were male, compared to 7297 (74.8%) in the low‐energy group and 1147 (63.5%) in the standard‐energy group. A BMI ≥ 30 was observed in 233 patients (3.5%) in the restricted‐energy group, 308 (3.2%) in the low‐energy group, and 47 (2.6%) in the standard‐energy group. Low BMI was reported in 1265 patients (18.8%) in the restricted‐energy group, 2047 (21.0%) in the low‐energy group, and 434 (24.0%) in the standard‐energy group (Tables 1 and S2). On the day of surgery, 2935 patients (43.6%) in the restricted‐energy group were admitted to the ICU, compared to 4636 (47.5%) in the low‐energy group and 932 (51.6%) in the standard‐energy group (Table 2). The median (Q1, Q3) of the mean prescribed energy doses during postoperative days 1–7 was 7.4 (6.2, 8.7) kcal/kg/day in the restricted‐energy group, 13.7 (11.8, 16.0) kcal/kg/day in the low‐energy group, and 22.8 (21.3, 25.1) kcal/kg/day in the standard‐energy group (Table 3). Trends in prescribed energy and amino acid doses from postoperative days 1 to 7 are shown in Figure S1. Moreover, the preoperative, postoperative, and total durations of prescribed parenteral nutrition are shown in Table S3.

Outcomes

Outcomes are summarized in Table S4. The composite of clinical adverse events occurred in 2910 patients (43.3%), 4378 (44.9%), and 853 (47.2%) of the restricted‐, low‐, and standard‐energy groups, respectively. Table 4 shows adjusted odds ratios (95% CI) and adjusted regression coefficients (95% CI). Using the restricted‐energy group as the reference, adjusted odds ratios (95% CI) for the composite outcome were 1.06 (0.97–1.16; P = 0.18) in the low‐energy group and 1.09 (0.94–1.27; P = 0.24) in the standard‐energy group, showing no significant differences.

DISCUSSION

We investigated the impact of prescribed parenteral energy on clinical outcomes in patients who exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days after gastrointestinal cancer surgery using a large medical claims database. A major strength is that the multivariable analysis was adjusted for 33 potential confounders, including parenteral amino acid doses. Using the restricted‐energy group (5–10 kcal/kg/day) as the reference, higher energy groups (10–20 and 20–30 kcal/kg/day) were associated with increased in‐hospital mortality and prolonged hospital stays.

In contrast, a previous multicenter randomized trial in patients undergoing major gastrointestinal surgery found that starting parenteral nutrition on postoperative day 3 at 25–30 kcal/kg/day reduced infectious complications compared to starting on postoperative day 8. 10 Nonetheless, that study excluded critically ill patients (with severe malnutrition, liver dysfunction, and renal dysfunction) and included those receiving tube feeding within 24 h after surgery. This differs from our broader real‐world population, which included more critically ill patients unable to receive oral intake or tube feeding for ≥7 days, indicating that the patient group benefiting from early postoperative energy restriction requires further study.

Our results align with multicenter randomized ICU trials showing that energy restriction during the first 7 days after ICU admission reduced complications and ICU length of stay. 8 , 9 These studies suggested that early adequate energy intake might suppress autophagy, increasing complications and delaying recovery. Autophagy contributes to cell repair, homeostasis, and stress adaptation, 24 and is activated by energy restriction. 8 , 25 In our study, this mechanism may have contributed to improved clinical outcomes.

Reduced oxidative stress may be another mechanism underlying improved clinical outcomes. Surgical stress early after gastrointestinal cancer surgery increases inflammation and oxidative stress, 26 which can damage DNA and proteins and increase complications. 26 , 27 Energy restriction suppresses mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production, reducing oxidative damage and potentially improving clinical outcomes. 27 , 28

In this study, the median of the mean prescribed lipid doses during postoperative days 1–7 was 0.0 g/kg/day in all groups, indicating that ≥50% of patients received no lipid during the first 7 days after surgery. Large‐scale real‐world studies in Japan show that ILE is rarely prescribed after gastrointestinal cancer surgery 12 , 13 or for other conditions. 29 , 30 , 31 One possible reason for the limited use of ILE in Japan is that only soybean oil ILE is commercially available. 31 Soybean oil ILE may be associated with inflammation and immunosuppression, 32 so its use may be avoided during the acute phase in clinical practice. In our subgroup analysis, no association was observed between prescribed energy doses and postoperative complications in the non‐ILE group, whereas higher prescribed energy doses were associated with fewer complications in the ILE group. An observational study of approximately 20,000 ICU patients receiving mechanical ventilation also showed that soybean oil ILE was associated with reduced in‐hospital mortality. 23 The 2022 American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition guideline recommends either mixed oil or 100% soybean oil ILE for patients receiving parenteral nutrition. 33 Taken together, soybean oil ILE may be effective for patients exclusively receiving parenteral nutrition after gastrointestinal cancer surgery.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the data were not collected for research purposes, which may result in input errors and missing data, potentially reducing diagnostic accuracy; however, a validation study confirmed the validity of these classifications. 34 Second, although we adjusted for clinically important confounders from preoperative to postoperative day 7, unmeasured or unknown confounders may remain. Third, discarded nutritional infusions were not recorded, so prescribed energy doses may be overestimated compared with actual energy intake. Fourth, low BMI was used as a malnutrition indicator based on the Asian Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria, 20 but data on weight loss, muscle mass, food intake decline, and disease‐related stress or inflammation were unavailable, potentially underestimating malnutrition. Fifth, this study focused on patients exclusively receiving parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days, so the findings may not apply to those able to receive oral or tube feeding early postoperatively. Finally, this study population included 11,204 patients (61.2%) aged ≥70 years and 588 (3.2%) with BMI ≥ 30, differing from European and US populations, 35 , 36 which may limit generalizability.

CONCLUSION

This study investigated the impact of prescribed parenteral energy on clinical outcomes in patients who exclusively received parenteral nutrition for ≥7 days after gastrointestinal cancer surgery. Early postoperative higher energy doses were associated with increased in‐hospital mortality and LOS. Given the retrospective cohort design and study limitations, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Prospective studies are warranted to confirm these associations.
