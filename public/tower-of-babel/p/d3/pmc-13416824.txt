RATIONALE-303: Long-Term Outcomes of Tislelizumab in Previously Treated Advanced/Metastatic NSCLC
Authors: Pedro de Marchi, Yun Fan, Zhiyong Ma, Yan Wang, Yuanyuan Bao, Wenjuan Zheng, Kirsha Naicker, Caicun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRATIONALE-303 (NCT03358875) investigated the efficacy and safety of tislelizumab versus docetaxel in pretreated patients with advanced NSCLC. We present long-term outcomes with an additional 30-month follow-up after the final analysis.MethodsIn this open-label, randomized, phase 3 trial, patients aged 18 years or older with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC that progressed on prior platinum-based chemotherapy were randomized (2:1) to tislelizumab or docetaxel. The dual primary end points were overall survival (OS) in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population and OS in patients with programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor cell expression 25% or greater. Secondary end points included progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), duration of response (DoR), and safety.ResultsA total of 805 patients were randomized (ITT: tislelizumab, n = 535, docetaxel, n = 270; PD-L1 ≥ 25%: tislelizumab, n = 227, docetaxel, n = 115). Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events were less frequent with tislelizumab than with docetaxel (16.1% versus 66.3%). Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 The most prevalent type is NSCLC, which accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases.2 Treatment options for patients with advanced NSCLC that has progressed after first-line chemotherapy have historically been limited, resulting in poor long-term survival rates.3,4 However, the treatment landscape has been revolutionized by the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors. These innovative therapies have demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in terms of overall survival (OS), durable responses, and favorable safety profiles compared with traditional chemotherapy, establishing themselves as a new standard of care in the post–first-line setting.5 For example, several randomized phase 3 trials have revealed that patients with previously treated advanced NSCLC experienced improved OS with anti–PD-1/anti–PD-L1 antibodies (tislelizumab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab) compared with docetaxel.6, 7, 8, 9

Tislelizumab, an anti–PD-1 antibody, has gained regulatory approvals across several countries and regions, including by the European Commission and the Chinese National Medical Products Administration, for multiple indications, including NSCLC and SCLC. Among these approvals are both monotherapy and combination therapy regimens, covering first-line treatment with chemotherapy and second-/third-line monotherapy for locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC, perioperative treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy for resectable NSCLC, and first-line treatment for extensive-stage SCLC in combination with chemotherapy.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

In the interim and final analyses of RATIONALE-303 (NCT03358875), patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC that progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy experienced significantly improved OS compared with docetaxel. RATIONALE-303 met its dual primary end points of OS in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population and OS in patients with PD-L1 tumor cell expression of 25% or greater.6 Here, we report long-term efficacy and safety outcomes with an additional 30 months of follow-up after the final analysis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

RATIONALE-303 is a global, open-label, randomized phase 3 clinical trial that was conducted in 10 countries. The study design has been described in full.6 The trial was performed according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was approved by the relevant institutional review board or independent ethics committee for each study site.

Eligible patients were randomized (2:1) using an interactive web-response technology system to receive either tislelizumab 200 mg or docetaxel 75 mg/m2 intravenously every 3 weeks until disease progression per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. Randomization was stratified by histology (squamous versus nonsquamous), line of therapy (second versus third), and PD-L1 tumor cell (TC) expression (<25% versus ≥25%). At the investigator’s discretion, patients could continue tislelizumab treatment beyond disease progression under protocol-defined conditions. Crossover from docetaxel to tislelizumab was not permitted per protocol.

An independent data monitoring committee reviewed the efficacy and safety data. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans for this research. The trial was designed by clinical experts and the sponsor, based on scientific rationale and unmet medical needs in the treatment of NSCLC.

Patients

Eligible patients were adults (aged ≥18 y) with histologically confirmed locally advanced or metastatic squamous or nonsquamous NSCLC that progressed during or after platinum-based chemotherapy (no more than two previous lines of systemic chemotherapy for advanced or metastatic disease), had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1, and had a life expectancy of at least 12 weeks. Patients who had previously received docetaxel treatment for metastatic disease or ICI therapies (anti–PD-1, anti–PD-L1, and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4) and those with known EGFR mutations or ALK translocations were ineligible. For patients with nonsquamous NSCLC, documentation of wild-type EGFR determined by a tissue-based test was required to enter the study.

End Points

The dual primary end points were OS in the ITT population, which included all randomized patients, and OS in a subset of patients with PD-L1 TC expression 25% or greater. Secondary efficacy end points included investigator-assessed progression-free survival (PFS), investigator-assessed objective response rate (ORR), investigator-assessed duration of response (DoR), health-related quality of life (data not reported herein), and safety and tolerability.

Assessments

PD-L1 TC expression was evaluated using the VENTANA (SP263) Assay (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). Tumor response was assessed by the study investigators using RECIST version 1.1. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to the first objectively documented tumor progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a complete or partial response. DoR was defined as the time from the first documented objective response to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Any-grade and grade more than or equal to 3 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious TEAEs, adverse events (AEs) leading to discontinuation, and AEs leading to death were monitored and classified based on the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 24.0; they were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.03 and recorded up to 30 days after the last study treatment or initiation of new anticancer therapy, whichever occurred first. Immune-mediated AEs (imAEs) were determined by a predefined programmatic algorithmic approach and based on a list of preferred terms, without manual medical adjudication, and monitored for up to 90 days after the last dose of tislelizumab, regardless of whether the patient started a new anticancer therapy.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical methodology for the study has been reported in full.6 Efficacy end points were assessed in all randomized patients (ITT population). The safety population comprised all randomized patients who received at least one dose of the study drug. Categorical variables were summarized by the number (percentage) of patients, whereas continuous variables were reported using descriptive statistics. Time-to-event end points were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methodology, with the Brookmeyer–Crowley method used to estimate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for median PFS, OS, and DoR. Hazard ratios (HRs) and associated 95% CIs were calculated using an unstratified and a stratified (by histology: squamous versus nonsquamous; lines of therapy: second versus third; and PD-L1 TC expression: ≥25% versus <25%) Cox proportional hazards model. As the dual primary end points were met in the final analysis, no formal significance testing was performed at the closeout analysis. Therefore, the p values presented are descriptive. Missing data were not imputed for any analyses.

Results

Patients

Between November 2017 and April 2020, 805 eligible patients were randomly assigned to the tislelizumab (n = 535) and docetaxel (n = 270) treatment arms (Fig. 1). A total of 13 patients (one in the tislelizumab arm and 12 in the docetaxel arm) who did not receive treatment were included in the ITT population but excluded from the safety population. As of the data cutoff date for study closeout (January 18, 2024), the median OS follow-up was 46.5 months (95% CI: 45.8–47.1) in the tislelizumab arm and 41.0 months (95% CI: 39.3–44.0) in the docetaxel arm. Patient distributions were generally balanced between the two arms and were representative of the target population with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC (Supplementary Table 1). In the ITT population, the median age was 61.0 years; 643 patients (79.9%) were Asian, and 343 patients (42.6%) had tumors with PD-L1 TC expression 25% or greater. At the data cutoff date (January 18, 2024), only one patient (0.2%) in the tislelizumab arm remained on study treatment, and all patients in the docetaxel arm had discontinued treatment (Fig. 1).

Efficacy

A total of 631 and 256 deaths had occurred in the ITT and PD-L1 25% or greater populations, respectively. In the ITT population, a clinically meaningful improvement in OS was observed with tislelizumab compared with docetaxel. The median OS was 16.9 months (95% CI: 15.2–19.1) and 11.9 months (95% CI: 9.6–13.5) in the tislelizumab and docetaxel arms, respectively (stratified HR = 0.67 [95% CI: 0.57–0.80]; nominal p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). In the tislelizumab and docetaxel arms, 12-month OS rates were 62.1% (95% CI: 57.9–66.1) and 49.7% (95% CI: 43.5–55.7), 24-month OS rates were 37.3% (95% CI: 33.2–41.5) and 23.9% (95% CI: 18.8–29.3), 36-month OS rates were 26.0% (95% CI: 22.3–29.9) and 14.7% (95% CI: 10.6–19.4), and 48-month OS rates were 20.1% (95% CI: 16.5–23.9) and 11.1% (95% CI: 7.5–15.6), respectively. Among the 47 patients in the tislelizumab arm who remained at risk for disease progression or death at 36 months, a higher proportion had locally advanced disease compared with the overall population in the tislelizumab arm (34.0% versus 15.7%), PD-L1 TC 25% or greater expression (63.8% versus 42.4%), and lower rates of bone (12.8% versus 31.0%) and liver (10.6% versus 13.6%) metastases (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Tislelizumab demonstrated a clinically meaningful improvement in OS compared with docetaxel in the PD-L1 25% or greater population. Patients treated with tislelizumab achieved a median OS of 19.3 months (95% CI: 16.5–22.6), and those receiving docetaxel had a median OS of 11.5 months (95% CI: 8.2–13.5). The stratified HR was 0.52 (95% CI: 0.40–0.68; nominal p < 0.0001), favoring tislelizumab (Fig. 2B). In the tislelizumab and docetaxel arms, 12-month OS rates were 67.4% (95% CI: 60.8–73.1) and 48.3% (95% CI: 38.5–57.4), 24-month OS rates were 42.7% (95% CI: 36.1–49.1) and 22.4% (95% CI: 15.0–30.8), 36-month OS rates were 32.5% (95% CI: 26.4–38.7) and 14.3% (95% CI: 8.3–21.8), and 48-month OS rates were 26.1% (95% CI: 20.3–32.2) and 9.9% (95% CI: 5.0–16.8), respectively.

Patients in the tislelizumab arm consistently experienced an OS benefit versus those in the docetaxel arm across most prespecified subgroups (Fig. 2C). Subgroup analysis demonstrated the effectiveness of tislelizumab across both squamous (HR = 0.59 [95% CI: 0.46–0.74]) and nonsquamous (HR = 0.74 [95% CI: 0.59–0.93]) histologic subtypes, different PD-L1 TC expression levels (HR = 0.60 [95% CI: 0.48–0.74] for PD-L1 ≥ 1%; HR = 0.81 [95% CI: 0.62–1.06] for PD-L1 < 1%), and lines of therapy (HR = 0.64 [95% CI: 0.53–0.89] for second line; HR = 0.93 [95% CI: 0.61–1.42] for third line). Notably, tislelizumab also proved effective regardless of liver metastasis status at baseline, with benefit observed both in patients with liver metastases (HR = 0.48 [95% CI: 0.30–0.78]) and in those without (HR = 0.69 [95% CI: 0.58–0.82]).

In the ITT population, a clinically meaningful improvement in PFS was observed with tislelizumab compared with docetaxel. The median PFS was 4.2 months (95% CI: 3.9–5.5) in the tislelizumab arm versus 2.6 months (95% CI: 2.2–3.8) in the docetaxel arm (stratified HR = 0.64 [95% CI: 0.54–0.76]; nominal p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). In the tislelizumab and docetaxel arms, 12-month PFS rates were 24.0% (95% CI: 20.4–5.5) and 6.5% (95% CI: 3.6–10.6), 24-month PFS rates were 12.8% (95% CI: 10.0–15.9) and 2.7% (95% CI: 1.0–5.8), and 36-month PFS rates were 10.6% (95% CI: 8.0–13.5) and 2.2% (95% CI: 0.7–5.1), respectively.

In addition, a higher ORR was observed in the tislelizumab arm (22.6% [95% CI: 19.1–26.4]) than in the docetaxel arm (7.8% [95% CI: 4.9–11.6]) (Table 1). The median DoR was 13.5 months (95% CI: 8.5–19.4) in the tislelizumab arm versus 6.1 months (95% CI: 2.3–7.2) in the docetaxel arm (Table 1). Similarly, in the PD-L1 25% or greater population, the ORR in the tislelizumab arm was higher than that in the docetaxel arm (37.4% [95% CI: 31.1–44.1] versus 7.8% [95% CI: 3.6–14.3]).

Safety

At the data cutoff, with an additional 30 months of follow-up since the final analysis, the median duration of exposure was 24.0 (range: 1–275) weeks in the tislelizumab arm and 9.1 (range: 1–210) weeks in the docetaxel arm. The median number of treatment cycles was 8.0 (range: 1–88) in the tislelizumab arm versus 3.0 (range: 1–70) in the docetaxel arm. Overall, with more than 2.5 times longer exposure to treatment, patients in the tislelizumab arm generally experienced a lower rate of TEAEs than those in the docetaxel arm (Table 2).

Almost all patients in both arms experienced at least one TEAE (97.0% in the tislelizumab arm versus 98.4% in the docetaxel arm) (Table 3). The three most frequently reported TEAEs in the tislelizumab arm were anemia (29.6%), cough (21.5%), and increased alanine aminotransferase level (21.2%). Patients in the docetaxel arm primarily experienced alopecia (49.2%), anemia (45.3%), and decreased neutrophil count (36.8%). The incidence of grade 3 or higher TEAEs was lower in the tislelizumab arm (43.6%) than in the docetaxel arm (74.8%). Serious TEAEs were reported in 36.0% of patients in the tislelizumab arm and in 32.6% of those in the docetaxel arm. The incidence of TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation was similar between the arms (12.5% for tislelizumab versus 13.2% for docetaxel). TEAEs leading to death occurred in 6.6% of patients in the tislelizumab arm and in 4.7% of those in the docetaxel arm (Table 2).

Treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) were observed in 75.7% of patients receiving tislelizumab and 93.8% of those receiving docetaxel (Supplementary Table 3). The three most frequently reported TRAEs (>10%) in the tislelizumab arm were increased alanine aminotransferase level (17.4%), increased aspartate aminotransferase level (16.1%), and hypothyroidism (12.0%). In contrast, patients in the docetaxel arm experienced a different adverse event profile, with higher frequencies of specific TRAEs, including alopecia (48.1%), anemia (39.1%), and decreased neutrophil count (36.0%). Grade 3 or higher TRAEs were less frequent in the tislelizumab arm (16.1%) than in the docetaxel arm (66.3%). Although most events in both arms were grade 1 or 2, there was a higher incidence of severe hematotoxicity in the docetaxel arm.

Serious TRAEs were less common in the tislelizumab arm than in the docetaxel arm (14.2% versus 22.9% for all grades; 10.5% versus 21.7% for grade ≥3). The incidence of TRAEs leading to treatment discontinuation was 7.1% for tislelizumab versus 10.1% for docetaxel. TRAEs leading to death occurred in 1.5% of patients in the tislelizumab arm and in 1.6% of patients in the docetaxel arm.

imAEs occurred in 34.8% of patients receiving tislelizumab, with most being grades 1 to 2 in severity. Grade 3 or higher imAEs occurred in 6.7% of patients in the tislelizumab arm and in 0.4% of patients in the docetaxel arm (Supplementary Table 4). Grade 5 imAEs were reported in one patient (0.2%) in the tislelizumab arm (pneumonitis); no grade 5 imAEs were reported in the docetaxel arm.

Discussion

The updated efficacy results from the RATIONALE-303 trial at study closeout (January 18, 2024), with an additional 30 months of follow-up after the final analysis (data cutoff: July 15, 2021; median study follow-up was 16.0 mo for tislelizumab and 10.7 mo for docetaxel), demonstrate that tislelizumab continues to provide clinically meaningful, sustained, and consistent survival benefits compared with docetaxel in previously treated patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC, regardless of PD-L1 TC expression. In the ITT population, tislelizumab maintained an OS improvement versus docetaxel (HR = 0.67), consistent with the final analysis results (HR = 0.66; nominal p < 0.0001).6 The OS benefit was even more pronounced in patients with PD-L1 TC expression 25% or greater (HR = 0.52; nominal p < 0.0001). Long-term survival rates at 48 months favored tislelizumab in both the ITT population and the PD-L1 25% or greater population. These results demonstrate the durable efficacy of tislelizumab in this patient population. Importantly, the OS improvement with tislelizumab was observed in most prespecified subgroups, including different PD-L1 TC expression levels, histologic subtypes, lines of therapy, and presence or absence of liver metastases. The apparent OS benefit in PD-L1–negative patients (TC < 1%; HR = 0.81 [95% CI: 0.62–1.06]) may reflect PD-L1 expression heterogeneity, the unique Fcγ receptor binding profile of tislelizumab, and other immune mechanisms, including tumor mutational burden. Subgroup analysis demonstrated the effectiveness of tislelizumab across both squamous (HR = 0.59) and nonsquamous (HR = 0.74) histologic subtypes. Tislelizumab also proved effective regardless of liver metastasis status at baseline, with benefit observed in patients with liver metastases (HR = 0.48) and those without (HR = 0.69). These results underscore the broad efficacy of tislelizumab across key NSCLC subgroups, suggesting that it may benefit a broad range of patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC. Secondary end points (PFS, ORR, and DoR) also favored tislelizumab, further supporting its efficacy. Tislelizumab demonstrated superior PFS (HR = 0.64; nominal p < 0.0001), a higher ORR, and longer DoR compared with docetaxel. Patients in the tislelizumab arm who remained progression free at 36 months had a less extensive disease burden, a more favorable baseline performance status, and higher PD-L1 TC expression, though this exploratory finding should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. These improvements across multiple efficacy end points reinforce the overall clinical benefit of tislelizumab in this patient population.

The efficacy results of RATIONALE-303 are consistent with and comparable to results observed in similar trials of other PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in previously treated NSCLC. However, it is important to note that cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in study designs and patient populations. The CheckMate 017 and CheckMate 057 trials of nivolumab versus docetaxel revealed 48-month OS rates of 14.2% versus 4.6%, respectively (OS HR = 0.68), in previously treated patients with NSCLC.8 In addition, the KEYNOTE-010 trial of pembrolizumab versus docetaxel demonstrated 48-month OS rates of 17.5% versus 8.8%, respectively (OS HR = 0.70), in PD-L1–positive NSCLC (PD-L1 tumor proportion score ≥ 1%).7 Similarly, the OAK trial of atezolizumab versus docetaxel reported 48-month OS rates of 15.5% versus 8.7%, respectively (OS HR = 0.78).9 Importantly, all of these trials also provided evidence of the potential for long-term survival in patients treated with ICIs after prior treatment; 5-year survival rates reached 15%,7,8 compared with approximately 5% historically observed with chemotherapy.3 This long-term benefit has been particularly pronounced in patients with high PD-L1 TC expression, although improvements have been observed across all PD-L1 TC expression levels.

With an additional 30 months of follow-up compared with the final analysis, the safety profile of tislelizumab remained favorable and consistent with previous reports, identifying no new safety signals.16 Despite longer treatment exposure (median 24.0 versus 9.1 wk), tislelizumab demonstrated lower rates of grade 3 or higher TRAEs (16.1% versus 66.3%) compared with docetaxel. As anticipated, imAEs were more prevalent in the tislelizumab arm. The incidence of imAEs of any grade associated with tislelizumab treatment was 34.8%. Although most of these events were of low severity, pneumonitis emerged as the most frequent grade 3 or higher event.

Our findings continue to validate tislelizumab monotherapy as a treatment option for patients with previously treated locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC. The favorable safety profile, combined with the demonstrated long-term efficacy, suggests that tislelizumab offers a well-tolerated treatment option with durable clinical benefits and better quality of life. With regulatory approvals across multiple regions, tislelizumab represents a valuable addition to the NSCLC treatment landscape, particularly for patients who have progressed after first-line chemotherapy and in regions with limited access to ICIs in the first-line setting.

Limitations

The RATIONALE-303 trial had limitations to consider. Although the trial was conducted globally, the study population was predominantly Asian (79.9%). Population pharmacokinetic analyses across multiple studies suggest comparable tislelizumab exposure between Asian and non-Asian patients, indicating likely consistency in the efficacy and safety profile of tislelizumab across various racial groups.17 Some subgroup analyses were limited by small sample sizes, which may have resulted in wide CIs and potentially inconsistent results. These exploratory subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential imbalances in baseline characteristics. As RATIONALE-303 was an open-label trial, there was potential for bias in the assessment of subjective end points. Furthermore, although this limitation was mitigated for the primary OS end point (an objective measure), it could affect the evaluation of other end points, such as PFS and safety.

Conclusions

The RATIONALE-303 trial continued to demonstrate positive outcomes after an additional 30 months of follow-up subsequent to the final analysis. In both the ITT and PD-L1 25% or greater populations, patients with previously treated advanced or metastatic NSCLC experienced a clinically meaningful improvement in OS with tislelizumab compared with docetaxel. Moreover, patients treated with tislelizumab exhibited more durable responses and delayed disease progression. Tislelizumab treatment also maintained a favorable safety profile compared with docetaxel. These long-term efficacy and safety results further reinforce that tislelizumab is an effective and tolerable treatment option with long-term benefit for patients with previously treated advanced or metastatic NSCLC. Tislelizumab represents a valuable treatment option for the second-line setting and beyond, addressing an important need in the NSCLC treatment landscape.
