Integrating machine learning with histopathological, immunohistochemical, and PET parameters in the study of invasive breast carcinoma, no special type
Authors: Berkay Çağdaş, Hüseyin Şan, Kardelen Çağdaş, Ahmet Kılıçaslan, Nedim C M Gülaldı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the relationship between histopathological (HP) features, immunohistochemical (IHC) markers, 18F- FDG PET/CT parameters, and machine learning algorithms in patients diagnosed with invasive breast carcinoma, no special type.Methods384 patients were included in the study. 18F- FDG PET/CT images after diagnosis of invasive breast carcinoma, before treatment, were analyzed for the PET metabolic parameters retrospectively. PET/CT images of patients who met the inclusion criteria and were retrospectively analysed. Metabolic parameters including SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV (Metabolic Tumour Volume), and TLG (Total Lesion Glycolysis) were calculated, considering SUVmax at 42% of the threshold value in the primary lesion for the PET/CT imaging. None of the parameters fit into the normal distribution. Therefore, Mann-Whitney-U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman correlation tests were applied. The machine learning algorithms were also used to describe any connection between the HP and IHC properties of the tumors.ResultsSignificant correlations were observed between PET-derived parameters (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV, and TLG) and HP/IHC characteristics, including Nottingham grade, molecular subtypes, and Ki-67 proliferation index. Machine learning models (XGBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost) demonstrated moderate success in identifying high-risk pathological and phenotypic features, with SUVmean identified as the most influential predictor via SHAP analysis. The coefficient of variation revealed low to moderate variability across most performance metrics, with CV values predominantly between 0.01 and 0.09, indicating stable and reliable model outputs.ConclusionThese findings suggest that integrating 18 F-FDG PET/CT metabolic parameters and machine learning algorithms can enhance non-invasive risk stratification and guide personalized treatment strategies in breast cancer management.

Introduction

IBC, NST, exhibits marked biological heterogeneity that directly influences prognosis and treatment response. While HP assessment and IHC profiling remain essential for tumor classification and therapeutic decision-making, these approaches are limited to localized tissue sampling and may not fully capture whole-tumor biology. IHC analysis complements HP by identifying key molecular markers, including ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 proliferation index, which guide targeted therapies and predict patient outcomes [1].

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) enables non-invasive, whole-body assessment of tumor metabolism. Quantitative PET parameters—including standardized uptake values, metabolic tumor volume, and total lesion glycolysis—have shown potential as imaging biomarkers of tumor aggressiveness. PET-derived parameters, such as SUV, MTV, and TLG, are increasingly used to quantify tumor biology, predict treatment response, and monitor disease progression [2]. However, their associations with established HP and IHC features in IBC, NST remain incompletely defined, with prior studies yielding heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting results [3]. Higher 18F-FDG uptake (e.g., SUVmax) is associated with aggressive breast carcinoma subtypes, such as triple-negative and HER2-enriched tumors, while lower uptake is observed in less aggressive luminal A subtypes. Understanding these associations can provide insights into tumor biology and treatment response.

A more integrated evaluation of microscopic tumor characteristics and macroscopic metabolic behavior may improve risk stratification and prognostic modeling. In this context, ML offers a promising framework for extracting clinically relevant patterns from multiparametric imaging data, yet its application to predicting pathological and phenotypic features in breast cancer using PET-derived metrics remains limited. In IBC, integrating PET parameters with ML may enable the identification of high-risk pathological and phenotypic features, facilitating personalized treatment approaches.

This study investigates the relationship between HP features (tumor differentiation and mitotic activity), IHC markers, and PET-derived metabolic parameters in patients with IBC, NST. Additionally, we explore the role of ML in identifying high-risk pathological and phenotypic characteristics using PET parameters. The novelty of this study lies in the application of machine learning algorithms for risk stratification in a large real-world cohort of 384 patients, incorporating rigorous molecular subtype classification that includes the Ki-67 proliferation index, thus extending beyond previous studies by providing a more comprehensive and clinically applicable analysis.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Scientific and Ethical Review Board for Medical Research at our hospital (10 July 2024, TABED 2-24-343). A total of 1270 female patients who underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging for BC staging between 2019 and 2024 were screened. Patients without available histopathology data, those who received treatment before PET imaging, those with widespread metastatic lesions at the time of diagnosis, and those diagnosed with other than IBC, NST, were excluded from the study. A total of 384 patients diagnosed with IBC, NST, with known Nottingham scoring, histological grade, Ki-67 proliferation index, hormone receptors, and HER2 expression status at the time of diagnosis without treatment before PET imaging, met the inclusion criteria and were retrospectively analysed. Nottingham Grade classification is determined separately based on nuclear pleomorphism, tubular formation, and mitotic count, and these parameters are recorded separately. Patients were also categorized into three groups based on their Nottingham grade score: poorly differentiated, moderately differentiated, and well-differentiated. Metabolic parameters, including SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV, and TLG, were extracted using segmentation of SUVmax at 42% of the threshold value in the primary lesion for the PET imaging. Furthermore, the analysis included ratios obtained from these parameters (SUVmax/SUVmean, SUVpeak/SUVmean, MTV/SUVmean, and TLG/SUVmax). None of the parameters fit into the normal distribution. Therefore, Mann-Whitney-U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman correlation tests were applied. Additionally, this study explored the use of machine learning to distinguish high-risk pathological and phenotypic features based on PET parameters. High-risk tumor groups were identified using a Nottingham score of 8 and 9, a tumor grade of 3, and classifications of pleomorphism, mitosis, and tubules at level 3. Additional criteria included an N stage of 1, a Ki-67 proliferation index exceeding 14%, negativity for estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors, HER2 positivity, and phenotypic classifications that do not fall within luminal A and B categories. Nottingham 8–9, grade 3, pleomorphism/mitosis/tubule 3, N1, Ki-67 > 14%, ER/PR negativity, HER2 + denote the highest-risk category within each classification system.

To address the class imbalance between high-risk and low-risk tumor groups in the dataset, SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) and Borderline-SMOTE methods were implemented to improve model performance and facilitate more effective learning of the minority class. The utilized machine learning algorithms comprised XGBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, and CatBoost. A 10 × 10 nested cross-validation scheme (10-fold inner, 10-fold outer) was used: inner loops for hyperparameter tuning, outer loops for unbiased performance estimation. Hence, the models underwent training and testing with this approach to enhance generalizability and mitigate the risk of overfitting. Given the strong class imbalance for most high-risk targets, SMOTE or Borderline-SMOTE was applied within the training folds to reduce bias towards the majority class.

The evaluation of model performances was conducted thoroughly through various metrics, including accuracy, precision/recall, F1 score, ROC AUC, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV). The coefficient of variation for the performance metrics was computed to evaluate model stability and consistency comprehensively.

Additionally, analyses using SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) were performed to determine the PET parameters that had the greatest impact on the decision-making processes of the models. The SHAP results were refined through weighting with ROC AUC values, leading to a Weighted SHAP Value scaled from 0 to 1 to more accurately represent model performance.

All analyses and model development processes were performed using Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, 2023) within Jupyter Notebook and the R environment, utilizing relevant machine learning and statistical libraries including scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, scipy, xgboost, lightgbm, catboost, imblearn, shap, and irr [4–7].

Model stability across nested cross-validation folds was assessed by summarizing performance metrics as mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV). We did not use intraclass correlation coefficients, because we aimed to describe the variability of metrics rather than the agreement between raters.

The results from this study provide insights into the relationship between BC molecular subtypes and 18 F FDG PET/CT parameters, which can inform personalized treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Results

The study included 84 patients with Nottingham Grade 1, 212 with Grade 2, and 88 with Grade 3 tumors. The mean age was 56 years (range: 34–94). Nottingham scores were distributed as follows: 18 cases (score 4), 66 (score 5), 122 (score 6), 90 (score 7), 61 (score 8), and 27 (score 9). ER positivity was observed in 329 cases, PR positivity in 334, and HER2 positivity in 100. For most high-risk targets, the positive class was relatively infrequent (e.g. Nottingham 8–9: n = 88/384; ER negativity: n = 55/384). This substantial class imbalance partly explains the discrepancy between overall accuracy and the lower F1/recall values for high-risk prediction.

In the Kruskal-Wallis analysis among Nottingham Score groups, significant differences were found in SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG values (p < 0.05). When the parameters of nuclear pleomorphism, tubule formation ratio, and maximum mitosis per 2 square millimeters used in Nottingham scoring were examined separately, significant differences were found in SUVmax, SUVmean, and SUVpeak parameters between groups with low, moderate, and high nuclear pleomorphism (p < 0.05). Significant differences were also found in SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG values between groups based on tubule formation ratio score and maximum mitosis per 2 square millimeters score (p < 0.05). In the analysis based on histological grade, significant differences were observed in SUVmax, SUVmean, and SUVpeak values between groups (p < 0.05).

Significant differences were found in SUVmax (11.21 vs. 8.25), SUVmean (6.92 vs. 5), SUVpeak (8.33 vs. 6.17), and TLG (103 vs. 57) values between cases with HER2 amplification and those without, with higher values observed in cases with HER2 amplification (p < 0.05).

Cases showing ER receptor expression had lower SUVmax (8.16 vs. 14.89), SUVmean (4.99 vs. 8.87), and SUVpeak (6.1 vs. 10.96) values compared to cases not showing expression (p < 0.05). Similarly, cases showing PR receptor expression had lower SUVmax (8.1 vs. 14.97), SUVmean (4.95 vs. 8.96), and SUVpeak (6.05 vs. 11.09) values compared to cases not showing expression (p < 0.05).

For the Nottingham score (8 and 9), the models achieved an average F1 score of 0.46, a ROC AUC of 0.69, and an accuracy of approximately 74%. Similar trends were observed in the tumor grade group (3), with an F1 score of 0.46, ROC AUC of 0.67, and accuracy near 75%. Precision/recall were balanced at about 0.45 and 0.51, while specificity and NPV remained elevated, exceeding 0.81 and 0.85, respectively. The low CV values for these metrics indicate consistent model performance.

Pleomorphism (3) and tubule (3) groups showed slightly higher F1 scores (0.60 and 0.67, respectively) but lower ROC AUC values (~ 0.59 and 0.60). Accuracy ranged from 57% to 59%, with precision/recall suggesting moderate predictive power. Specificity and NPV were lower compared to Nottingham and grade groups, indicating challenges in accurately identifying negative cases for these features.

The mitosis (3) group exhibited lower F1 scores (~ 0.26) despite a relatively high ROC AUC (~ 0.74) and accuracy (~ 86%). Precision/recall were low (~ 0.30 and 0.28), indicating difficulty in correctly classifying positive cases. However, specificity and NPV were high (> 0.91), demonstrating reliable identification of negative cases.

Regarding receptor status, ER negativity was associated with a low F1 score (0.22) and ROC AUC (0.56), with moderate accuracy (~ 73%). PR negativity showed slightly better performance, with an F1 score of approximately 0.23, ROC AUC around 0.64, and accuracy near 77%. The Ki-67 proliferation index (> 14%) demonstrated a higher F1 score (0.74) and ROC AUC (0.67), but moderate accuracy (~ 64%). N stage (1) showed an F1 score of about 0.65, ROC AUC of 0.74, and accuracy close to 67% (Fig. 5).

F1 score, ROC AUC and key PET features across high-risk classes

For ER and PR negativity and HER2 positivity, ROC AUC values remained in the 0.56–0.64 range with F1 scores around 0.22–0.30, underscoring that PET-derived metrics alone are insufficient to robustly predict these immunohistochemical phenotypes. Overall accuracy ranged from 73% to 85%; however, this was accompanied by modest precision (18–66%) and recall (23–54%) for the high-risk class, resulting in F1 scores of 0.28–0.63. These findings indicate that, despite capturing some signal in the imaging features, the models still miss a substantial proportion of high-risk tumors and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than clinically actionable tools at this stage.

The coefficient of variation revealed low to moderate variability across most performance metrics, with CV values predominantly between 0.01 and 0.09, indicating stable and reliable model outputs. Some targets, including mitosis, HER2 positivity, and ER status, exhibited higher variability in precision/recall, suggesting areas for further model refinement.

Table 8 provides a detailed summary of the SHAP-based feature importance results for the high-risk class across different machine learning models. The SHAP analysis consistently identified SUVmean as the most influential PET parameter across various tumor characteristics, with SUVpeak and the SUVmax/SUVmean ratio following closely behind (Fig. 4). Weighted SHAP values, modified by ROC AUC, further confirmed the strong and consistent impact of these features on the models’ predictive performance. SUVmean was identified as a significant feature across all assessed algorithms (XGBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, and CatBoost) and consistently ranked among the top predictors for at least ten different target variables, such as Nottingham score, Grade group, Mitosis group, ER negativity, PR negativity, and Ki-67 proliferation index. Similarly, SUVpeak consistently ranked among the top three features across approximately eight to ten target variables, demonstrating notable significance concerning PR positivity, Nottingham score, and tumor phenotype classifications. The SUVmax/SUVmean ratio was identified as a significant contributor in seven to nine target variables, particularly within the Grade group, Ki-67, Mitosis group, and Pleomorphism group, highlighting its importance in reflecting intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity. The clinical relevance of SUVpeak lies in its reproducibility as a measure of metabolic activity, while the SUVmax/SUVmean ratio reflects intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity associated with tumor aggressiveness. MTV and TLG provide volumetric metabolic information that complements point-based SUV measurements.

Discussion

Nottingham Grading System evaluates three key features of the tumor: tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count. These classify breast carcinomas into three grades: Grade 1 (well-differentiated, score 3–5), Grade 2 (moderately differentiated, score 6–7), and Grade 3 (poorly differentiated, score 8–9). This grading provides important prognostic information, as higher-grade tumors are generally more aggressive and have worse clinical outcomes [5].

The major subtypes of BC which include luminal A (ER+/PR+, HER2-), luminal B (ER+/PR+, HER2+/-), HER2-enriched (ER-/PR-, HER2+), and triple-negative (ER-/PR-, HER2-), are associated with varying tumor behavior, clinical outcomes, and response to targeted therapies. Understanding the distinctive features of each subtype, such as their proliferative capacity, angiogenesis, and metabolic activity, is crucial for guiding personalized treatment strategies and improving patient management.

In this study, it was observed that increasing Nottingham scores and histological grades in the 364 evaluated cases were associated with corresponding increases in PET metabolic parameters. Similar relations were found for nuclear pleomorphism, tubule formation ratio, and maximum mitosis per 2 square millimeters, which constitute the Nottingham score. Consequently, a relationship between HP differentiation parameters of IBC, NST and PET metabolic parameters was observed. Among cases without or with inaccessible HP diagnosis, those with higher SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG values may potentially have worse HP differentiation parameters.

When PET metabolic parameters were compared with IHC parameters, it was noted that cases showing ER and PR expression had lower SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG values; whereas cases with HER2 amplification had higher values for these parameters. Similarly, a positive correlation was demonstrated between the Ki-67 proliferation index and SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG metabolic parameters. These proven relationships between HP, IHC differentiation parameters and PETmetabolic parameters indicate that the metabolic data observed in PET imaging performed for breast carcinoma staging can provide clinicians with insights into both HP and IHC differentiation parameters of the followed IBC, NST.

In parallel with the findings of the present study, several studies in the literature have demonstrated significant differences between metabolic parameters and HP grade, showing that SUV parameters tend to increase with higher Nottingham grades [8, 9]. However, some studies have reported no significant association between Nottingham grade and SUV parameters [10].

Although some researchers focus on a specific type of receptor expression and the usefulness of ML methods, and others focus on radiomics features of the disease, our study differs from them in correlating a wide range of PET parameters with different receptor and mitosis status with ML models [11–13]. Gil-Rendo et al. demonstrated a significant difference in SUVmax values between Nottingham grades (Grade III vs. I–II) in their analysis of 275 female BC patients. In this study, beyond this significant difference, a progressive increase in metabolic parameters including SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG was observed with increasing Nottingham grade (p < 0.05) [8].

In IBC, NST, several studies have reported significant differences in metabolic parameters across molecular subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-positive, and Triple-Negative) and hormone receptor status, consistent with the findings of this study [8, 9, 14–16]. The highest SUVmax values were most frequently observed in the triple-negative group in the literature. These findings further support the growing body of evidence indicating that triple-negative tumors tend to exhibit higher metabolic activity on PET, highlighting its potential as non-invasive biomarkers for tumor aggressiveness and subtype differentiation.

In 2015, Groheux et al. investigated the relationship between metabolic parameters, including SUVmax, SUVmean, TLG, and MTV, and IHC markers in a cohort of 171 patients with Stage II and III IBC. Their study demonstrated higher SUVmax and SUVmean values in hormone receptor-negative tumors, while no significant association was found between HER2 status between metabolic parameters. Notably, Groheux et al. analyzed metabolic differences among ER-positive, PR-positive, and triple-negative groups without stratifying the cases into molecular subtypes. Furthermore, their study included patients with invasive lobular carcinoma. In contrast, our study focused exclusively on IBC, NST and classified the cases into molecular subtypes in accordance with current clinical guidelines. Importantly, Ki-67 index was incorporated into the molecular subtype classification, allowing for a more accurate and clinically relevant stratification of the patient cohort [14].

Consistent with this study, several investigations have reported significant associations between HER2 status and metabolic parameters, with higher SUVmax values observed in HER2-positive tumors [3, 9, 14]. These findings suggest a potential link between HER2 overexpression and increased tumor glycolytic activity. However, other studies have failed to demonstrate such an association, indicating that the metabolic behavior of HER2-positive tumors may be influenced by additional molecular or microenvironmental factors and the number of patients with confirmed HER2 amplification [17–19]. The variability in HER2-SUV associations across studies may be attributed to differences in sample sizes, methodological approaches in defining HER2 positivity, and tumor microenvironmental factors that influence glucose metabolism. Our large cohort study provides robust evidence supporting the association between HER2 amplification and increased metabolic activity.

The Ki-67 proliferation index is an indirect prognostic marker that reflects cellular mitotic activity. In the literature, cutoff values such as 14% or 20% have been commonly used to stratify patients, and significant differences between the resulting groups have been demonstrated [3, 17]. Unlike some prior studies, the present research identified a moderate correlation between the Ki-67 proliferation index and metabolic parameters (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV and TLG), in line with the findings of Arslan et al. (p < 0.05). This association suggests that increased cellular proliferation, as indicated by Ki-67, may be linked to higher tumor glycolytic activity, thereby supporting the potential utility of 18F FDG PET/CT as a non-invasive surrogate marker for tumor aggressiveness [16]. MTV/TLG were calculated using a 42% SUVmax threshold. There is no consensus on which threshold should be used to delineate textural features. Especially in low contrast lesions adaptive threshold method can result in inconsistent values and also lead to non-reproducible models [20]. The fixed threshold method can overcome this problem with high contrast lesions, as in our patients, and also has the advantage of reproducibility between the operators because of automated selection of the lesion boundaries. Despite a high interreader agreement, fixed threshold method, because of its deterministic and binary natüre, sometimes cannot reliably define MTV [21]. However, if the metabolic activity within the tumor is high enough at the edges, it can be reliably used for heterogeneity estimations. 42% threshold is the most commonly used threshold method for solid tumors such as lymphoma, and we tried to make it comparable with most of the studies done with PET/CT in the literature. We wanted to avoid manual segmentation to help the results be more reproducible.

Additionally, this study demonstrates that FDG PET/CT imaging parameters serve as an effective means for classifying high-risk pathological and phenotypic tumor characteristics in IBC through the application of machine learning models. The models demonstrated adequate accuracy and stability, especially regarding essential tumor characteristics like the Nottingham score, tumor grade, Ki-67 proliferation index, and N stage, suggesting that PET parameters convey significant insights into tumor biology.

The low sensitivity and specificity values of the models regarding certain pathological features, including mitotic count, hormone receptor negativity, and HER2 positivity, underscore the need for model enhancements and the acquisition of additional data in these domains. This situation indicates that PET parameters might have constraints in detecting specific tumor biomarkers or that current models may inadequately reflect these intricate biological characteristics.

The clinical implications of these findings are considerable. The timely and precise identification of high-risk tumors in IBC is essential for effective treatment planning in patients. Traditional pathological evaluations necessitate invasive biopsies; however, the integration of machine learning with non-invasive imaging techniques like 18F- FDG PET/CT facilitates risk classification for patients through less invasive approaches. This enhances patient comfort and expedites treatment decision-making [22–24].

The analysis of PET parameters through machine learning enhances the representation of tumor heterogeneity and biological aggressiveness [24]. This approach allows for the integration of anatomical, functional, and metabolic information into treatment strategies. The identification of high-risk tumors facilitates the selection of more aggressive treatment strategies or the enhancement of clinical follow-up protocols [23, 25]. These studies collectively demonstrate that machine learning applied to 18F FDG PET/CT metabolic and radiomic parameters is increasingly being used not only to predict pathological response but also to classify molecular subtypes and predict recurrence risk in IBC.

The study’s results advance the personalized medicine approach, establishing a foundation for the creation of treatment and monitoring plans tailored to patients’ biological characteristics. Future studies involving larger and more diverse patient populations may improve the generalizability of models, while the integration of PET/CT imaging with machine learning can be more effectively implemented in clinical practice.

A positive correlation between the Ki-67 proliferation index and SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and TLG metabolic parameters of the 18F-FDG PET/CT scan can provide clinicians with insights into both HP and IHC differentiation parameters of the IBC, NST. Conventional non-parametric tests confirmed strong associations between PET parameters and histopathological/immunohistochemical features at the group level. The machine-learning analysis extends these findings by demonstrating that, even when using only simple metabolic PET metrics, moderate discrimination of several high-risk features (e.g. Ki-67 > 14%, Nottingham 8–9, N1) is achievable, and by highlighting SUVmean and SUVpeak as consistently informative predictors in SHAP analysis. Nevertheless, the modest F1 scores and sensitivities indicate that these models should currently be viewed as proof-of-concept tools rather than ready-to-use clinical classifiers.

Beyond SUVmean, our SHAP analysis identified SUVpeak and the SUVmax/SUVmean ratio as consistently important predictors. SUVpeak represents a more robust measure of tumor metabolism that is less susceptible to noise compared to single-voxel SUVmax. The SUVmax/SUVmean ratio reflects intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity, which has been associated with tumor aggressiveness and treatment resistance. MTV and TLG, representing volumetric and total metabolic burden respectively, showed particular importance for N stage prediction and certain molecular subtype classifications, consistent with their established prognostic value in breast cancer.

In addition, this study illustrates that integrating 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging parameters with machine learning techniques offers substantial potential for the non-invasive, objective, and reproducible classification of high-risk tumors in BC. This method represents a significant advancement in enhancing patient outcomes through improved accuracy in treatment decision-making. Compared to conventional statistical approaches, machine learning models offer several advantages: they can capture complex nonlinear relationships between multiple PET parameters simultaneously, provide feature importance rankings through SHAP analysis, and enable more nuanced risk stratification by integrating multiple parameters into unified predictive frameworks.

This study has certain limitations in concluding powerful results. First, the retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, for several high-risk target outcomes, the number of positive cases was relatively small, resulting in class imbalance. This may have influenced model training and performance metrics, particularly for sensitivity-specificity trade-offs, despite the use of appropriate cross-validation strategies. Third, the analysis was restricted to conventional metabolic PET parameters. More advanced imaging features, such as PET radiomics, as well as complementary data sources, including other imaging modalities, genomic, or molecular information, were not incorporated. Consequently, the predictive performance of the models may be underestimated compared with multimodal approaches. Fourth, external validation was not available. Model evaluation relied solely on nested cross-validation for internal validation, which, while reducing optimism bias, does not fully substitute for independent external testing. Therefore, the robustness and transportability of the proposed models across different populations and imaging settings remain to be established. Finally, the machine-learning models were developed for clinical decision support rather than the replacement of clinical judgment. Some molecular and proliferation targets, such as mitosis, HER2 positivity, and ER status, exhibited higher variability in precision/recall, suggesting areas for further model refinement in the machine learning algorithm.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant correlations between 18F-FDG PET/CT parameters, HP/IHC characteristics, and molecular subtypes in IBC, NST. ML models leveraging PET parameters offer promise for non-invasive identification of high-risk features, with SUVmean as a key predictor. These findings support the integration of PET/CT and ML in IBC management to enhance risk stratification and personalize treatment. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate and refine these models for clinical application. Future research directions should include multicenter validation studies to confirm generalizability across diverse populations, exploration of additional radiomic features that may enhance predictive accuracy, and refinement of ML models through deep learning approaches. Prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the clinical utility of these models in guiding treatment decisions.
