Tourniquet application during ipsilateral shoulder surgery in a hemodialysis patient - A case report of proximal tourniquet technique (proximal tourniquet technique in a hemodialysis patient during shoulder surgery)
Authors: Dong Ju Shin, Jae Hwi Han
Published: 2025

Introduction

Surgical management of fractures in patients undergoing hemodialysis presents unique challenges due to bone metabolism changes, bleeding tendencies, and vascular access considerations [1]. The presence of an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) for hemodialysis complicates hemostasis during upper extremity surgeries, especially in cases requiring extensive soft tissue dissection [2]. While tourniquets are commonly used to control bleeding in orthopedic surgeries, their application in hemodialysis patients with an ipsilateral AVF remains controversial due to concerns about compromising vascular access [3].

In this case report, we describe an innovative approach using a “proximal tourniquet” technique to control intraoperative bleeding during shoulder surgery in a hemodialysis patient. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria [4].

Case report

A 57-year-old woman presented to our emergency department with left shoulder pain after falling from a height. Physical examination revealed sensory deficits in the left shoulder and upper arm, along with wrist drop and an inability to perform a thumb-up gesture, indicating a brachial plexus injury. Notably, no significant circulatory abnormalities were observed in her hand. Plain radiographs revealed an anterior dislocation of the left shoulder accompanied by a fracture of the greater tuberosity of the humerus (Fig. 1). Closed reduction was performed, and post-reduction radiographs confirmed proper alignment of both the dislocated joint and the greater tuberosity fracture (Fig. 2). Follow-up radiographs two days later indicated displacement of the fracture (Fig. 3), which progressed over the subsequent week, prompting consideration of surgical intervention. The patient had a history of hypertension, angina, hypothyroidism and chronic kidney disease, and she had been receiving anticoagulant therapy along with regular hemodialysis for the past several years (Fig. 4). On examination of the affected upper extremity, an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) was present, with signs of venous hypertension, including significant shunt dilation and engorgement of subcutaneous veins in the shoulder. To minimize bleeding tendencies, an alternative to heparin was utilized during dialysis. Hemoglobin levels were 10.8 g/dL before surgery and 9.6 g/dL after surgery. Coagulation test showed INR was 0.96, PT was 11.1 s, aPTT was 28.9 s and platelet count was 222 109/L. The patient was receiving clopidogrel 75 mg daily, which was discontinued prior to surgery and replaced with a minimized heparin dialysis protocol. Surgery was performed under general anesthesia, and blood pressure during surgery was maintained at 79–144 mmHg.

Surgery was conducted via a deltopectoral approach in the beach-chair position. This approach was selected because it utilizes an internervous interval, which minimizes the risk of axillary nerve injury while preserving the deltoid, and it is a technique with which the surgeon was most experienced for addressing this type of injury. Despite the absence of apparent major vascular injuries, diffuse bleeding from the surgical field hindered adequate visualization. Attempts to control bleeding using Bovie electrocautery and gauze packing were ineffective, leading to a near-abort of the procedure.

We hypothesized that increased venous blood and rapid return from the AVF contributed to the bleeding. To mitigate this, we performed gravity exsanguination and applied an Esmarch tourniquet proximal to the AVF around mid-upper arm to block the venous return (Fig. 5). Gravity exsanguination was performed by elevating the affected limb to approximately 60° for 2 min before bandage application. An Esmarch elastic band was chosen instead of a pneumatic cuff because in this clinical setting a sterilized pneumatic tourniquet was not available, and based on the surgeon's long-standing experience using Esmarch bandages in patients with relatively short proximal humeri, this method was considered safe and effective.Fig. 5Intraoperative photographs showing the application of a proximal tourniquet (Esmarch band) at the mid–upper arm. Because an Esmarch elastic band was used instead of a pneumatic tourniquet, direct pressure parameters (mmHg) and inflation/deflation cycles could not be specified. According to standard orthopedic tourniquet guidelines, such as those from the Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN), safe practice emphasizes limiting tourniquet time, monitoring limb perfusion, and confirming vascular patency after release [5]. In line with these recommendations, we restricted the application to 60 min, continuously monitored limb color, capillary refill, and oxygen saturation, and confirmed AVF thrill and bruit both before and after bandage release.

Estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 250 mL, and no blood transfusion was required. Although there is no standardized surgical field visibility score in orthopedic surgery, we adopted the 4-point surgical field visibility scale commonly used in other surgical fields. On this scale, visibility improved from Grade 1 (poor, profuse bleeding that prevented continuation of the procedure) before proximal tourniquet application to Grade 3 (good, sufficiently clear field with intermittent suction) during fixation.

This significantly decreased bleeding tendencies in the surgical field, allowing for successful fracture reduction and subsequent internal fixation using a metal plate, autogenous bone grafting, and rotator cuff suture augmentation (Fig. 6). Postoperative follow-up demonstrated gradual recovery of the brachial plexus injury. Discussion

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a global prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) at approximately 11 %–13 %, with fracture incidence rates in CKD patients more than four times higher than in the general population [1]. Hemodialysis patients often present with metabolic bone diseases, with various studies addressing outcomes related to hip fractures in this group [1]. However, there is a dearth of research focused on upper extremity injuries, particularly concerning the unique anatomical and functional implications of the area surrounding the arteriovenous fistula (AVF) [2,3,[7], [8], [9]].

Historically, discussions around surgical treatments for distal radius fractures or conditions like carpal tunnel syndrome, which occur distal to the AVF, have emerged [1,3,7]. Notably, several studies support this issue regarding the feasibility and effectiveness of using a tourniquet for hemostasis during such procedures [1,7].

Since Recchetti et al. first reported ipsilateral shoulder hemiarthroplasty in a hemodialysis patient in 2010, subsequent studies have compared outcomes of shoulder arthroplasty in CKD patients [2]. Schwartz et al. noted that the number of CKD patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty doubled from 2007 to 2015, yet the mortality rate in dialysis patients was 32 times higher than in non-CKD patients, highlighting significant surgical risks. Their findings also indicated a 70 % higher incidence of postoperative hemorrhage and a threefold increase in transfusion requirements for CKD patients [9]. In trauma cases like fractures, where surgical timing is critical, these risks could be heightened. While joint replacement surgeries typically follow standardized protocols, trauma surgery often necessitates more intricate adjustments based on fracture patterns, soft tissue conditions, and the degree of displacement, emphasizing the need for a clear surgical field.

Managing bleeding tendencies in hemodialysis patients poses unique challenges due to elevated blood flow, increased venous pressure from AVFs, uremic platelet dysfunction, and the anticoagulant medications commonly used to mitigate cardiovascular risks [1,10]. Venous hypertension can manifest in all upper extremity access configurations due to arterialization of veins, and distended venous collaterals in the shoulder may indicate venous hypertension [11,12].

There are number of existing strategies for controlling intraoperative bleeding in hemodialysis patients. These include meticulous electrocoagulation, use of topical hemostatic agents, adjusting anticoagulant protocols before and after surgery, and scheduling operations with minimal or heparin-free dialysis immediately after hemodialysis. In orthopedic procedures in the distal AVF, some authors have reported avoiding hemostasis and relying solely on careful hemostasis, while others have described the safe use of hemostasis without adverse effects. Vascular surgeons also use inflatable hemostasis at the time of AVF formation or recapitalization for preventive hemostasis. Compared with these approaches, the proximal tourniquet technique presented in this study immediately and effectively reduced venous hypertension at the surgical site, facilitating exposure and fixation without impairing AVF function. However, unlike conventional methods, this technique requires careful monitoring during surgery and should be considered selectively [1,3,7,13].

Previous reports have described the use of proximal tourniquets in distal upper-extremity procedures. Im et al. reported no complications in 11 patients with ipsilateral distal radius fractures treated with a proximal tourniquet, demonstrating feasibility in distal surgery [7]. However, shoulder surgery presents greater hemostatic challenges because venous engorgement in the proximal arm and shoulder is often more pronounced. Schwartz et al. analyzed national data and found that dialysis-dependent CKD patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty had a 70 % higher risk of postoperative hemorrhage, a threefold greater transfusion requirement, and up to 32-fold higher in-hospital mortality compared with non-CKD patients [9]. Our case suggests that proximal tourniquet application may mitigate these elevated bleeding risks in carefully selected patients.

Applying a proximal tourniquet above the AVF transiently reduces shunt flow by increasing venous outflow resistance in the draining segment. This blunts the arterial–venous pressure gradient across the anastomosis, alleviates venous hypertension, and decreases collateral engorgement, thereby improving surgical field visibility [9]. Gravity exsanguination before application further reduces venous volume. Importantly, this maneuver aims for partial venous outflow reduction rather than complete arterial inflow occlusion; thus, we limited the duration to 60 min, monitored limb perfusion, and confirmed AVF patency after bandage release.

Our use of a tourniquet applied above the AVF during shoulder surgery is a novel approach not previously documented in the literature. However, vascular surgeons have recognized the use of an inflatable tourniquet on the upper arm for preventive hemostasis during surgical reinterventions for the treatment of complications or ligation of hemodialysis vascular access as a safe and effective method [13]. In the field of orthopedics, Im et al. reported no complications in 11 patients with ipsilateral distal radius fractures treated with a tourniquet positioned proximal to the AVF, demonstrating the clinical safety of this procedure [7]. The effectiveness observed in our case underscores the potential benefits of this technique in managing surgical bleeding. By occluding venous flow from the AVF, we achieve a clearer surgical field, facilitating successful fracture repair.

Although the proximal tourniquet technique is effective in this case, several potential risks, such as thrombosis or stenosis of the arteriovenous fistula, distal ischemia, nerve compression, and skin damage, should be recognized. In hemodialysis patients, venous hypertension and blood clotting abnormalities further increase these risks. To reduce these risks, we avoid cuffing directly above the AVF anastomosis, limited the use of the hemostasis to 60 min, and confirmed the opening of AVF through tremors and noise before and after the procedure [11,13]. These approaches are consistent with existing vascular access guidelines, such as the KDOQI Recommendation, which emphasizes the preservation of AVF function by avoiding long-term ischemia or mechanical trauma [14].

While our case shows that proximal tourniquet application can be safe and effective, this technique should be reserved for selected patients. It may be considered in hemodialysis patients with ipsilateral AVF when conventional hemostatic measures are insufficient. However, it should be avoided in patients with central venous stenosis, inadequate collateral circulation, or severe peripheral arterial disease, as proximal compression in these settings could exacerbate ischemia or compromise fistula function [11,12].

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of proximal tourniquet application during shoulder surgery in a patient with an ipsilateral AVF. Because of this novelty, direct comparison with similar reports is not possible; previous studies have only addressed distal upper-extremity procedures or vascular access surgeries [3,7,13].

In addition, objective intraoperative data—including estimated blood loss, hemoglobin change, and the exact 60-minute duration of Esmarch band application—have been provided in the Case Presentation. These strengthen the reproducibility of the report and highlight the effectiveness of this approach in reducing venous congestion and improving surgical visibility [1,10].

Nevertheless, the limitations of our study should be acknowledged. This report describes only a single patient, the assessment of surgical field visibility remains partly subjective, and the follow-up period was relatively short. Despite these limitations, our experience demonstrates effective bleeding control and preserved AVF function. Future studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up are required to validate the safety and efficacy of this technique, ideally incorporating objective measures of surgical field visibility and standardized vascular assessments.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that the application of proximal hemostasis during shoulder surgery in hemodialysis patients with ipsilateral AVF can provide effective bleeding control. Importantly, AVF patency is maintained, and the patient achieved satisfactory bone union and functional recovery. Although it is a single case, the experience of this study shows that if strict preventive measures are taken, this method can be used as an optional adjuvant therapy when conventional hemostasis methods are not sufficient. Further studies are needed to confirm the broader safety and applicability of this method.
