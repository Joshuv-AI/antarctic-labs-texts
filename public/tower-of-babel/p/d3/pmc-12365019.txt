Knowledge-guided self-learning control strategy for mixed vehicle platoons with delays
Authors: Jingyao Wang, Huinian Wang, Jian Song, Xingyu Chen, Jinghua Guo, Keqiang Li, Xunrui Li, Bowen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles will coexist for several decades, how to efficiently manage the mixed traffic, while enhancing road throughput, fuel consumption and traffic stability becomes a challenge. This is due to the randomness and heterogeneity of traditional vehicles interspersed among autonomous vehicles. Moreover, communication delays arising from the shared wireless communication network substantially degrade the performance of platooning control for connected autonomous vehicles. To address these challenging problems, this paper proposes a knowledge-guided self-learning mixed platoon control strategy. Firstly, the proposed strategy extracts key features of the continuous and aggregated behavior of traditional vehicles, such as desired time-varying time gap and standstill spacing, by integrating knowledge from the kinematic wave model and Newell’s car-following model. This helps autonomous vehicles predict traditional vehicles’ trajectories. Secondly, to tackle delayed current state information, the study incorporates previous control instructions into the state representation of the soft actor-critic algorithm. Simulations show the proposed strategy outperforms existing methods in traffic stability, passenger comfort, energy consumption cost and traffic oscillation dampening, with a zero collision rate in vehicle merging and diverging scenarios. The framework provides a generalizable and scalable solution for the development and adoption of connected autonomous vehicle systems.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

The rapid growth of autonomous vehicles is placing significant strain on global traffic management systems, due to the complexities of mixed-traffic environments and the unpredictable nature of vehicle behavior1,2. Research suggests that by 2050, fully autonomous vehicles, classified as SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) Level 5 Autonomous Driving, may account for between 10% and 71% of total vehicle travel, and comprise 90% of vehicle sales3,4. This suggests that autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles will coexist for several decades5–7. The effectiveness of future traffic and safety control systems will hinge on their ability to manage this mixed traffic efficiently, while also addressing traffic congestion, improving safety performance8,9, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and energy consumption10,11. Unlike autonomous vehicles, which operate based on predefined algorithms and sensor inputs, traditional vehicles are controlled by human drivers whose behaviors can be highly unpredictable. Autonomous vehicles adhere to strict control protocols, allowing for precise and consistent actions. In contrast, human-driven vehicles introduce variability and complexity into traffic systems due to their tendency to deviate from optimal driving patterns12–15. This difference in behavior is a significant challenge for traffic management systems, as it affects the stability and efficiency of traffic flow. For instance, as shown in Fig. 1b, sudden braking or acceleration by a human-driven vehicle can cause traffic oscillations, impacting the performance of platoons and increasing the risk of collisions16–18.

To address the challenges of mixed traffic, platooning control techniques have emerged as a promising solution19. Platooning involves the coordination of multiple vehicles to travel closely together at a consistent speed, reducing the impact of individual vehicle actions on traffic flow. The primary objective of platooning control is to ensure that all vehicles maintain the same velocity while keeping a safe inter-vehicular distance. However, implementing effective platooning control in mixed traffic environments is extremely complex. The integration of autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles requires sophisticated control algorithms that can accommodate the unpredictable behavior of human-driven vehicles and the communication delays inherent in the shared wireless communication system.

A significant challenge in designing platooning controllers is accounting for the unpredictable behavior of individual traditional vehicles interspersed among connected autonomous vehicles. Some related studies estimate the behavior of traditional vehicles using statistical methods20,21, but these methods are limited to traffic monitoring purposes because of the lack of an explicit model. The study referenced in22 analyzes the interactions between classical vehicles and autonomous vehicles with the car-following experiment data, and highlights the importance of randomness and heterogeneity of human-driving behaviors when designing mixed-traffic platooning algorithms, but it does not incorporate controller design. In the following, we limit ourselves to the literature on platooning control strategies for vehicle platoons only, which are most relevant to the current work.

Based on methodological differences, state-of-the-art algorithms for mixed vehicle platooning control can be divided into those based on linear and nonlinear control23–31, optimization control15,16,32–36, and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) based control37–40.

Researchers have designed the platooning controller based on the linear and nonlinear control theory, such as the H∞ control and sliding mode control23–31. Based on the differences in control objectives, the above controllers can be divided into predictive control-based controller23–28 and robust controller29–31. Predictive control optimizes control inputs by forecasting the future states of the system to achieve specific performance metrics, such as minimizing prediction errors. This approach prioritizes achieving high system performance through prediction and optimization. In contrast, robust control emphasizes maintaining system stability and reliability under uncertainties. For instance, we designed some kinds of robust controllers to guarantee the stability of a vehicle platoon in the presence of time delays and external disturbances in our paper29–31. While effective in simple scenarios, their model-dependent nature can lead to significant performance issues in real and complex scenarios. Thus, effectively managing mixed traffic with high variability remains a challenging task.

Optimization control methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), have been used to compute real-time vehicle control commands15,16,32–36. However, MPC usually requires convex problems, and its complex solving formulas result in a large computational load and poor real-time performance. To address this issue, references34–36 propose optimal longitudinal control algorithms for connected autonomous vehicles in mixed platoons. These algorithms aim to minimize the deviations of vehicle headway and velocity in the presence of wireless communication delays, but they are model-dependent and require the drivers’ behavior to be homogeneous.

DRL offers significant advantages, such as simplifying the need for complex model construction and enabling real-time operation. While prior research has extensively applied DRL to enhance the safety, energy efficiency, and driving comfort of vehicle platoons41–46, these methods, primarily designed for platoons consisting solely of connected autonomous vehicles, are not optimized for the current mixed traffic where autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles share the road. In other words, the exploration of DRL in mixed platoons is still in first stages. Utilizing typical DRL algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)37, and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)38, researchers have made strides in optimizing the car-following behavior and safety of mixed platoons, and have also experimented with adaptive control at signalized intersections39. Specifically, the PPO algorithm is selected to control the longitudinal motions of a leading autonomous vehicle to minimize the fuel consumption of the leading autonomous vehicle and the following platoon composed of traditional vehicles37. The DDPG control strategy is applied to drive the following connected autonomous vehicles to track a leading classical vehicle with the classical vehicle following empirical data38. A random selection algorithm is proposed in ref. 39 to implement the self-learning algorithm for a mixed platoon consisting of a leading autonomous vehicle and its following traditional vehicles. However, these studies cannot guarantee the safety and robust stability of the mixed platoon in the presence of the communication delay. To address the issue of communication delay, an intelligent control algorithm based on DDPG is designed in ref. 40 to control an autonomous vehicle following the preceding vehicle platoon consisting of multiple traditional vehicles. The primary distinctions between40 and our work are two-fold: first, the assumption that the desired inter-vehicular distance and velocity information of each traditional vehicle are available has been relied upon in ref. 40. This assumption underlying the algorithm in ref. 40 limits its application in practice, since accurately measuring these information of human-driven vehicles is challenging due to their limited communication capability. Second, whereas the communication delay considered in ref. 40 is assumed to be constant, while the proposed algorithm in our study is designed to handle varying delays, making it more adaptable to real-world conditions. Therefore, developing a controller capable of managing mixed traffic conditions, where both autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles share the road, while simultaneously considering the random actions of human-driven vehicles and communication delays, remains an open problem.

This paper aims to fill this gap by presenting a knowledge-guided self-learning framework. This framework not only accounts for the unpredictable behavior of traditional vehicles but also adapts to varying traffic conditions and random communication delays, ensuring robust performance across different scenarios. The proposed framework is built on the vehicle-road-cloud system, as presented in Fig. 2, where the cloud platform and connected autonomous vehicles exchange real-time data through the vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication network, facilitated by the roadside unites, and the cloud controller generates the control command for connected autonomous vehicles47. The details of this framework will be elaborated in the following section.

Compared with the results in the published literature, the principal contributions of this research are outlined as follows:

To address the limitation of current work that does not incorporate controller design into the analysis of interactions between classical vehicles and autonomous vehicles, we propose a generic knowledge-guided self-learning platooning control framework. This framework is designed to be robust regardless of the number of human-driven vehicles and random communication delays, while also meeting the real-time requirements of future transportation systems.

Aiming at the problem that current research relies on the known behavior of each human-driven vehicle, which are challenging to predict due to the randomness inherent in traditional vehicles, this paper first characterizes the continuous and aggregated classical vehicles insert among connected autonomous vehicles as a single entity. This characterization helps in predicting the aggregated behavior of traditional vehicles and their impact on the overall traffic dynamics. Then estimates the aggregated behavior of classical vehicles, such as desired time-varying time gaps and desired standstill spacing information, by incorporating the kinematic wave model and the Newell’s car-following model, which presents the linear relationship between standstill spacing and the kinematic wave velocity-with the prior traffic information. Moreover, by integrating prior knowledge such as the kinematic wave model and Newell’s car-following model into the learning process, we can guide the learning more effectively and improve efficiency.

Considering the causality of the system–where the current state is influenced by its previous states and previous inputs–we incorporate the previous control input into the state representation of the SAC method. This design guides the training process and leverages the high computational performance and storage capacity of the cloud server. In addition, a switching DRL-based model mechanism is designed to mitigate performance degradation caused by random communication delays, addressing an issue not fully resolved by existing literature.

Results in Results section demonstrate that the proposed knowledge-guided self-learning control framework is effective across a wide range of traffic conditions, including varying communication delays, platoon scales, and human drivers’ behavior parameters. When compared with existing traditional algorithms and state-of-the-art DRL algorithms, the proposed knowledge-guided self-learning algorithm exhibits superior performance in terms of traffic stability, passenger comfort, energy consumption cost, and traffic oscillation dampening. In addition, it shows stronger robustness against the lane-changing behavior of traditional vehicles.

Results

Knowledge-guided self-learning control framework

The knowledge-guided self-learning mixed platoon control framework, as depicted in Fig. 2, firstly decomposes the mixed platoon into distinct sub-platoons in the “CV-HVs-CV" (i.e., leading connected autonomous vehicles followed by traditional vehicles and then connected autonomous vehicles) formation (shown in Fig. 1c). Secondly, incorporates the aggregated model of classical vehicles as a knowledge-guided module, which is designed to predict the desired time-varying time gaps and desired standstill space of aggregated human-driven vehicles. Thirdly, consider the previous control instructions in the state representation of the SAC, and design a model switching mechanism to compensate for the performance degradation caused by random communication delays. Finally, the intelligent soft actor-critic (SAC) algorithm is used as the data-driven module of the system to maximize the car-following and comfort rewards for the connected autonomous vehicles and verify the effectiveness of the control framework in various scenarios.

The advantages of using cloud computing are twofold: it provides high-performance computing resources that support real-time decision-making for complex control algorithms, and it offers elastic storage space capable of storing multiple neural network models, enabling rapid loading and access.

Our mixed platoon control framework implements an end-to-end control strategy, as it maps the information gathered from roadside units and the V2X wireless communication network directly to control commands, as detailed in Fig. 2. Specifically, the cloud serves as the central processing unit, handling computational tasks such as data processing and control command generation. This high-performance capability ensures that complex control algorithms can be executed in real-time, providing dynamic and responsive control strategies. In parallel, roadside units are responsible for collecting macroscopic traffic information (such as traffic wave speed and jam density) from the current road segment47. Through V2X communication, as well as vehicle-mounted radar sensors, connected autonomous vehicles interact both with each other, the cloud and roadside units, facilitating real-time data exchange. Upon gathering vehicle and traffic data, roadside units forward this information to the cloud’s self-learning controller. The controller then computes the appropriate longitudinal control commands for each autonomous vehicle, tailoring them to the specific conditions of the road and traffic. These commands are relayed back to the respective autonomous vehicles via the V2X network. The elastic storage space provided by the cloud allows for the storage of multiple neural network models, enabling the controller to switch between different models based on real-time conditions, thereby optimizing performance.

Results in Fig. 3 show that, under the guidance of the cloud controller, the connected vehicle driving strategy integrated with the intelligent driver model (CVDS-IDM) strategies in ref. 48, DDPG control in ref. 40, and the proposed strategy enables platoons consisting of connected autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles to closely track an aggressive leading vehicle with minimal traffic oscillation and a comfortable driving experience. Moreover, the proposed strategy outperforms PPO control in ref. 37, DDPG control in ref. 40, actor-critic (AC) control in ref. 44, and the CVDS-IDM strategies in ref. 48 in terms of traffic stability, passenger comfort, energy consumption cost, and traffic oscillation dampening performance. These results highlight the feasibility of our self-learning method, demonstrating its applicability across various traffic system conditions.

Comparison results between different control algorithms

To demonstrate the practicability and effectiveness of the proposed control framework, this section presents numerical simulation examples. These examples are designed to simulate the dynamic behavior of the mixed platoon comprising seven vehicles, as depicted in Fig. 3a, where the leading vehicle, as well as the 2-nd and 6-th following vehicles, are designated as connected autonomous vehicles, while the remaining vehicles are classified as traditional vehicles. The leading vehicle serves as the tracking target. Guided by the NGSIM data49, it follows a low-velocity aggressive driving style characterized by low velocity and significant volatility in acceleration and deceleration. This driving style is representative of the behavior exhibited by most human drivers in real-world scenarios22,43,50. Besides the leading vehicle, all autonomous vehicles are controlled by the proposed strategy. And traditional vehicles are governed by the optimal velocity model used in refs. 36,44, which is one of the most popular models to capture the car-following behaviors of classical vehicles. For the sake of consistency and reproducibility, the default experimental settings for this study are outlined in Supplementary Table 2, offering a foundation for the simulation parameters used.

The initial velocity of all vehicles is set to 11m/s, and each vehicle maintains a specified distance from the preceding vehicle. The communication delay is set to a constant value of 20 ms by default. Due to the limited availability of related research, we compare the proposed knowledge-guided self-learning SAC strategy with several existing methods in the mixed platoon context, including PPO37, DDPG40, AC44, and CVDS-IDM strategies48. This comparison aims to further demonstrate the superiority of the proposed strategy. The signal phases and timing of the intersection are considered in ref. 37, and the impacts of traffic signals are incorporated into the reward function design to optimize the trajectories of connected autonomous vehicles. It requires exact desired inter-vehicular distance and velocity information for human-driven vehicles in ref. 40. A cooperative multi-agent actor-critic scheme is developed to optimize connected autonomous vehicles platoon management policies via the AC architecture in ref. 44. The IDM with estimation errors is utilized to model traditional vehicles and the CVDS-IDM is develpoed to model connected automomous vehicles48. It incorporates human factors such as estimation errors and driver compliance, which are critical for the success of connected autonomous vehicles. The assumptions underlying these methods differ from those of this paper. To address the problem considered in this paper, we have extended PPO37, DDPG40, AC44, and CVDS-IDM strategies48 to fit the proposed framework and have made appropriate modifications to them. The switching DRL-based model mechanism

Influenced by the fluctuation of network conditions and environmental interference, the delays at various stages, denoted as ϵi, are not constant but exhibit randomness, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. This randomness in communication delays significantly impacts the performance of control models. For instance, a comparison between Fig. 6b and e shows that the variation in the acceleration amplitude across different delays for the 6-th vehicle is larger than that for the 2-nd vehicle. This indicates that the control performance has been affected by the random communication delay.

To effectively address this issue, this paper proposes a switching mechanism for the control model. Specifically, by utilizing timestamp measurement techniques, the receiving end’s timestamp TR and the sending end’s timestamp TT are obtained in real-time. The time difference TR − TT is then computed to achieve real-time detection of delays.

In light of the stability of small perturbations as considered in robust control theory, we have divided the distribution range of delays into multiple smaller intervals. Correspondingly, we have designed a set of models to accommodate these intervals, as drawn in Supplementary Fig. 1. Under identical state dimensions, we prioritize the selection of a model that closely matches the detected delay value. This strategy ensures that the control model maintains optimal performance within environments characterized by stochastic delays, thereby achieving robustness against random disturbances.

A total of 100 simulation experiments were conducted under random delays, and the experimental results are shown in Fig. 8. From Fig. 8, it can be observed that under random delays, all evaluation metrics exhibit only minor fluctuations, and the median evaluation metrics of the 6-th connected autonomous vehicle are superior to those of the preceding traditional vehicles (i.e., vehicles with number 3–5). This demonstrates the robustness of the proposed method to random delays.

In the decision-making graphs for the CVDS-IDM, DDPG, and PPO, as depicted in Fig. 10b, c, e and h, noticeable curve intersections occur at multiple points. These intersections indicate collisions between vehicles, resulting in dramatic changes in their relative positions. For example, the position trajectory of the 6-th connected autonomous vehicle rapidly ascends over time in Fig. 10b, surpassing other vehicles, and its path crosses with others. This suggests that the 6-th connected autonomous vehicle engaged in inappropriate acceleration or path selection, leading to collisions. In contrast, the decision-making process of the SAC algorithm demonstrates clear smoothness, with no instances of curve intersections. In Fig. 10g–l, all vehicle trajectories remain stable and gradually increase, maintaining consistent relative positions. This indicates that the SAC algorithm effectively avoids collisions and ensures appropriate spacing between vehicles. The reasons for the above performance differences are attributed to the traditional method (CVDS-IDM) and the multiple DRL methods themselves. Since all of these methods have been modified and trained to accommodate the random behavior of autonomous vehicles, the specific reasons are as follows:

CVDS-IDM relies on preset rules and cooperative communication mechanisms, with its parameter adjustments depending on fixed thresholds. When sudden disturbances occur, such as the merging of a traditional vehicle, these models need to rely on real-time sensor data for recalculations. However, due to the constraints of the rule-based framework, they struggle to quickly respond to the nonlinear behaviors of the human-driven vehicle, which can lead to delayed decision-making or sub-optimal adjustments.

Discussion

The key challenge in the development of the platooning controller for mixed traffic stems from the challenge of considering the randomness of human driving behaviors and the random time delay in the actual communication environment. We address these challenges with three aspects of our knowledge-guided self-learning SAC framework. First, this study introduces an approach by integrating the cloud to be the controller, thereby maximizing the utilization of traffic information, which holds significant practical application value. This is different from previous studies on mixed platoons, which have often overlooked the potential of cloud computing and roadside units, leading to sub-optimal use of communication resources15,16,36. Secondly, we embed the kinematic wave model and Newell’s car-following model as a knowledge-guided module into the SAC algorithm to estimate the overall behavior of traditional vehicles. This integration aims to mitigate the interference caused by the randomness inherent in each human-driven vehicle and enhance the overall stability of the platoon. Third, to address the inherent random delays in wireless networks, we integrated previous control instructions, which are induced by the delay, into the state representation and design a model switching mechanism. Lastly, the number of classical vehicles in training is variable, and the extended scenarios have been studied, enriching the empirical data and broadening the model’s application scenarios.

The performance of the proposed control strategy is assessed by benchmarking it against other DRL-based control37,40,44 and CVDS-IDM control48 methods. The evaluation criteria include traffic stability, driving comfort, energy consumption cost, traffic oscillation dampening performance, and robustness against disturbances caused by the lane-changing behavior of a human-driven vehicle. The results suggest that the proposed control strategy significantly outperforms other strategies in every aspect and can greatly suppress the traffic oscillations, demonstrating its robustness and resilience against disturbances.

This research demonstrates the potential of employing the proposed data-driven strategy, in conjunction with a switching model approach, to manage random communication delays effectively. In addition, the generalizability of the proposed strategy has been confirmed through extensive testing with the NGSIM datasets and a variety of customized traffic scenarios, further validating its practical applicability in real-world conditions.

Future research could focus on applying the proposed framework to real-world vehicle platoons, further enhancing the robustness and adaptability of the control strategy.

Mixed platoon scenario

Regulating the longitudinal behavior of connected autonomous vehicles in a mixed platoon is essential to dampen traffic oscillations caused by the randomness and heterogeneity of traditional vehicles’ behavior. To improve ride comfort and mitigate traffic oscillations, the first step in our study is to decompose the mixed platoon into sub-platoons with the car-following structure of “CV-HVs-CV". This structure is practical for real-world scenarios where connected autonomous vehicles and traditional vehicles coexist, and serves as a versatile unit for mixed platoons with various vehicle combinations, as depicted in Fig. 11. In this structure, the leading autonomous vehicle (indexed by i − n) is guided by the real-world data, while the following connected autonomous vehicle (indexed by i) is the vehicle that needs to be controlled. Neural network structure

The actor network comprises an input layer with z + 3 units, which receives the state information, including Δd~i(k¯),Δv~i(k¯),ui(1),ui(2),…,ui(z+1). Following the input layer, there are two hidden layers with 128 and 64 units, respectively. The output layer of the actor network is designed to produce the mean and standard deviation of the action distribution, which adheres to a Gaussian distribution, thereby enabling the network to select actions stochastically. The critic network shares a similar structure to the actor network but has a distinct input layer consisting of z + 4 neurons. This layer receives both the action ui(k¯) and the state si(k¯). The primary role of the critic’s output layer is to output the Q value, which serves as a critical evaluation metric for the quality or ‘superiority’ of the action taken in a given state. This comparison allows the network to learn the value of actions in specific states and to guide the actor network in selecting better actions over time.

SAC algorithm

The SAC algorithm is an off-policy DRL algorithm that distinctively incorporates the principle of maximum entropy53. This innovative approach results in a more dispersed action output, counteracting the tendency for actions to become overly concentrated. Compared with other DRL algorithms, these attributes provide SAC with a significant advantage in terms of adaptability and performance. Data preparation

In this study, we utilize the Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) data from the California I-80 project for our tests49. This dataset is chosen due to the frequent traffic congestion and oscillation observed during peak hours, which are critical for our analysis. Specifically, we focus on the vehicle trajectory data captured between 4: 00 pm and 4: 15 pm, a period characterized by relatively fewer lane change maneuvers, thus providing a stable dataset for our experiments. To enhance the quality of the data for analysis, the trajectories are reconstructed using a low-pass filter. This filtering process is essential for smoothing out the noise and interference present in the original data.
