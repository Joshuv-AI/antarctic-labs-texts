Optimising PET imaging for dosimetry in SIRT: insights from phantom and simulation studies on the Discovery MI scanner
Authors: Farzam Sayah, Benoît Presles, Hussein Harb, Tien-Phong Pham, Jean-Louis Alberini, Romain Popoff, Jean-Marc Vrigneaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to optimise the acquisition and reconstruction parameters for imaging on a digital PET scanner (Discovery MI 4-ring) under high (3 GBq), intermediate (1 GBq), and low (200 MBq) activity conditions. First, quantitative linearity was evaluated. Then, NEMA IEC body phantom acquisitions were reconstructed using various scan durations and Q.Clear reconstruction parameters. Next, optimal protocols were identified by minimising the discrepancies between absorbed dose maps derived from images (using the Local Deposition Model (LDM)) and those obtained from Monte Carlo (MC) simulations. The images reconstructed with these optimal protocols were then used to evaluate effective spatial resolution and to compare the accuracy of LDM with that of the Dose Voxel Kernel (DVK) approach.ResultsQuantitative linearity analysis revealed an underestimation of phantom activity at high activity (up to − 15.7% at 3.6 GBq) and an overestimation at lower levels (up to 52.1% at 208 MBq). At high activity, the most accurate results were produced by 15- and 20-minute acquisitions with intermediate beta values (2000–5000). In contrast, intermediate and low activity levels required longer acquisition times and higher beta values (> 6000). Effective spatial resolution ranged from 7 mm at high activity to 24.9 mm at low activity. In terms of absorbed dose accuracy, the mean absorbed dose was underestimated in all spheres of the phantom. However, the error in the mean absorbed dose could be kept within 10% in the background by applying non-linearity corrections. Using MC as reference, LDM achieved greater accuracy in terms of mean absorbed dose and Dose Volume Histogram (DVH) agreement in the spheres, while DVK performed better in the background.ConclusionsThis study enabled the optimisation of scan duration and beta parameter values for Q.Clear reconstructions for dosimetric purposes. Although accurate dosimetry remains challenging in PET imaging due to quantitative nonlinearity and limited spatial resolution, an error of less than 10% on the mean absorbed dose can be achieved in large structures if model-specific nonlinearities are corrected for.Keywords: PET, 3D dosimetry, SIRT, Optimisation

Background

Selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) is a treatment option for unresectable primary or secondary liver cancer [1, 2]. The normal liver parenchyma’s blood supply is mainly provided through the portal vein, while hepatic tumours mostly draw their blood supply from the hepatic artery [3]. SIRT exploits this difference in the vascularisation network by injecting radioactive microspheres into specific branches of the hepatic artery to locally target the tumour while leaving the healthy liver untouched.

Until now, microspheres used for liver implantation have either been associated with Yttrium-90 () or Holmium-166 (). Although is beginning to gain interest [4], is still the most used radionuclide in SIRT. is a pure emitter with a half-life of 64.2 hours. Its energy spectrum shows a maximum energy of 2.28 MeV and a mean energy of 0.94 MeV. This translates to a maximum range of 11 mm and a mean range of approximately 4 mm in soft tissue [5].

Personalised pre-therapy dosimetry has proven effective in patient management and has become a cornerstone of SIRT [6]. Recent international guidelines state that post-treatment imaging must be performed to visually inspect the microsphere distribution, in order to detect any technical failure during the treatment procedure that results in the presence of microspheres outside the targeted area [3, 5, 7]. It is also recommended to complement the visual review with quantitative image-based dosimetry to assess agreement between planned and delivered dose, and to improve the understanding of the absorbed dose–effect relationship [3, 7]. Post-treatment imaging can either be performed by SPECT/CT (Bremsstrahlung) or PET/CT imaging [8]. Despite its low branching ratio of internal pair production (), PET has proven to be a suitable modality for imaging on a variety of scanners, particularly when quantification is required [9–12]. Using PET allows us to take advantage of a scanner already calibrated at 511 keV in Bq/mL. However, quantification of should be considered carefully as -PET imaging has been shown to be prone to non-linearity effects as a function of activity [12, 13]. In addition, due to the low branching ratio, -PET leads to noisy imaging that requires extended scan times with short-axial field-of-view (SFOV) PET scanners in the clinical setting.

Early studies focused on the possibility of quantifying using conventional (non-digital) PET scanners [14–16]. Accurate quantification of seemed feasible without any effect of detector saturation on modern scanners equipped with LSO or LYSO crystals [17–19], with a minimum detectable concentration of approximately 1 MBq/mL [9, 20]. However, high random-to-true coincidence ratios present challenges for random correction at low counts, resulting in positive bias in iterative reconstruction [12, 20, 21]. Moreover, the accuracy of scatter correction is also compromised at low concentrations of [14, 22].

One way to improve the quantification of is to use more powerful reconstruction algorithms that can handle noise, such as the block sequential regularised expectation maximisation (BSREM) algorithm [23]. On General Electric (GE) PET systems, this algorithm is available under the name Q.Clear and is modulated by a user-defined parameter β that controls the smoothness of the image. In the case of conventional PET scanners, the impact of the beta factor for has been extensively studied by the group of McGowan [24, 25], and by Hou et al. [26]. All of these studies highlighted the fact that the beta value needs to be tailored to the imaging task (e.g. β = 4000 for visual analysis and 1000 for quantification of activity according to the McGowan group and β = 5000 for accurate dose distribution estimation according to Hou et al. ). Both studies also found different optimal beta values for the same imaging goals, although similar GE systems (D710 vs. D690) were investigated. More recently, Calatayud-Jordán et al. [27] reported similar findings to the McGowan group on a digital GE Signa PET/MR scanner, proposing a β value of 4000 for optimal image quality and values of 1000–2000 for quantification. The optimisation of imaging appears to depend heavily on acquisition parameters (e.g. scan time and activity) and image metrics used for analysis.

Once -PET quantification is considered reliable, image-based dosimetry seems to be fairly straightforward using methods such as the local deposition method (LDM) or dose voxel kernel (DVK). However, the low-count statistics of PET imaging still lead to non-uniform activity maps in homogeneous areas, which in turn generate non-uniform dose distributions [13, 28]. The reconstruction parameters optimised for accurate activity quantification may not be the same as those for dose distribution [26].

The purpose of this study was two-fold. The first goal was to optimise the acquisition and reconstruction parameters for on a digital PET scanner (Discovery MI 4-rings, GE ) for dosimetry purposes. Unlike conventional optimisation methods, which rely on image quality metrics such as the recovery coefficient (RC) and background variability (BV), dose-based optimisation aims to minimise the difference between an absorbed dose map derived from an image and a reference absorbed dose map [29, 30]. As preliminary requirement, the linearity of quantification was evaluated. Then, the impact of the beta factor from the Q.Clear reconstruction algorithm was investigated as a function of scan times and activity present in the phantom. Specifically, image-based dose maps obtained at different activity levels were compared with reference dose maps generated from Monte Carlo (MC) simulations (Geant4 Application for Tomographic Emission, GATE, version 9.0) [31]. The second objective was to compare the performance of LDM and DVK dosimetry with MC dosimetry as a reference, to establish the most suitable method for dosimetry using PET imaging with a Discovery MI scanner. In theory, DVK mitigates the incorrect assumption of local dose deposition (LDM) by taking into account the contribution of neighbouring voxels. In SPECT imaging, the inaccurate assumption of local deposition is somewhat compensated by prominent partial volume effects (PVE) when calculating dose in soft tissue [32, 33]. Digital PET imaging, on the other hand, allows for theoretical improvements in spatial resolution and, as a result, reduced PVE. Nevertheless, there is still no consensus on the optimal image-based dosimetry approach for PET imaging which may depend on the spatial resolution of the imaging system [7, 34].

Materials and methods

Phantom acquisitions and reconstructions

In this study, the National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA) IEC body phantom was used. It was filled with 3.64 GBq of -citrate, with a sphere-to-background ratio of 8:1. This corresponded to concentrations of 2.87 MBq/mL in the spheres and 0.36 MBq/mL in the background at the time of the first scan. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was added to the solution before filling the phantom to prevent adherence to the phantom walls. The dose calibrator (MEDI-405, Veenstra Instruments, now Comecer Netherlands) used includes -citrate vial and syringe calibration factors, traceable to the French national metrology laboratory for ionising radiations (Laboratoire National Henri Becquerel, Gif-sur-Yvette, France). The expanded uncertainties (k=2) for vial and syringe calibrations are 5.1% and 5.5%, respectively.

The phantom was imaged on a digital PET/CT scanner (Discovery MI 4-ring model, GE HealthCare, Chicago, Il, USA) for 12 consecutive days covering an activity ranging from 3597 to 207 MBq. A single bed position was used throughout all acquisitions. To assess the feasibility of reducing acquisition times while maintaining clinical utility, three different scan times were considered: 20, 15, and 10 minutes. The 10- and 15-minute frames were obtained by rebinning the original 20-minute list-mode data. As the minimum recommended acquisition time for modern SFOV PET scanners is estimated to be around 15 minutes [5, 17, 25], scan times shorter than 10 minutes were not investigated. Scan times longer than 20 minutes were excluded, as they are impractical in a clinical context requiring at least two bed positions to fully image the patient’s lungs and liver.

Data were reconstructed using the Q.Clear algorithm with beta values ranging from 300 to 10,000. Time-of-flight (TOF) information and point spread function (PSF) correction were used for all reconstructed images. Furthermore, no post-filtering was applied and all images were reconstructed on a 256 × 256 matrix using a voxel size of for a FOV (transverse × axial). A standard-dose CT scan was used for attenuation correction. The scan had the following settings: 140 kVp, automatic tube current modulation, a pitch of 0.984, a slice thickness of 3.75 mm and a reconstruction matrix of 512 x 512.

To automate the image segmentation of the different regions of the NEMA IEC phantom, an in-house tool based on the Insight Toolkit (ITK) library was used. This tool uses the coordinates of the centre of the spheres along with the theoretical dimensions of the phantom to segment the different regions according to the given voxel size, while trying to keep the volume of the different structures as close as possible to their theoretical values [35]. The segmentation produces a 3D labelled image of the NEMA IEC phantom that can be used as a mask with each region (spheres, lung insert and background) identified by a unique label (voxel value). The background dose was quantified using 48 3D spheres scattered throughout the background of the phantom and positioned sufficiently away from the hot spheres to avoid any influence from partial volume effects close to the sphere boundaries. Figure 1 illustrates an example of the labelled image. To account for variability in phantom positioning throughout the different acquisition days, an individual mask was generated for each day.

Quantitative linearity

To characterise the quantitative linearity of the PET scanner as a function of activity, we compared the activity measured in the reconstructed image with the true activity present in the phantom over the full activity range. Quantified activity was extracted using a volume of interest (VOI) covering the entire phantom (). In addition, to test for reconstruction inaccuracies that could result in misplaced counts outside the phantom, we also quantified the total activity within the entire PET image ().

The PET images used for this evaluation correspond to the 20 minutes acquisitions reconstructed with the current clinical protocol (OSEM algorithm with two iterations and 17 subsets, TOF information and PSF correction). Post-filtering was applied with a 6.4 mm cut-off frequency in the transverse direction and a standard 3-point filter in the axial direction.

Monte Carlo reference dosimetry

Reference dose maps were generated using MC simulations (GATE, version 9.0), with the NEMA IEC body model and physics parameters identical to the ones described by Pham et al. [35]. A GATE simulation of the NEMA model was conducted with a background-to-sphere ratio of 8:1, matching the conditions of the clinical acquisitions [35]. The reference dose map was generated using the Dose Actor, with a voxel size of mm3. To maintain an uncertainty below 1%, the simulation was performed with a total of decays. Each dose map was then scaled according to the theoretical cumulative activity at the various acquisition time points.

Image-based dosimetry

Local deposition method

LDM considers the deposition of the absorbed dose to be localised within the same voxel as the decay [34]. The absorbed dose in each voxel is determined by multiplying the cumulative activity (Bq.s) or concentration (Bq.ml−1) within the voxel by a constant factor, commonly referred to as , converting the image into an absorbed dose map in Gray (Gy). is the ratio between the mean energy released per decay and the mass of the voxel. In this study, the mean energy released per decay of was considered to be 0.935 MeV. The mass of a voxel was calculated using its volume and density (1 g/cm3 for water). The corresponding was Gy.Bq−1.s−1 if applied to a cumulative activity image or Gy.mL.Bq−1 if applied to a concentration image.

Dose Voxel Kernel convolution

Image-based dose maps were also computed using the DVK method. In this method, the absorbed dose in the target voxel is calculated by convolving the cumulative activity map with an absorbed dose kernel [36, 37].

The absorbed dose kernel was generated using the GATE Dose Actor with radioactive decays [35]. The size of the generated kernel was carefully chosen so that it extends far enough from the source while ensuring that the uncertainty remains below 2% at the edges of the dose kernel. This resulted in a kernel size of: voxels ().

PET protocol optimisation

Given the need for an acquisition protocol that can be easily translated into clinical routine, three acquisition days were selected from a total of 12. This selection aims to simplify the optimisation process while ensuring that different activity ranges were included in the analysis. In our institution, -PET imaging is usually carried out the day after administration, which means that some decay has occurred by the time the scan is taken. Patient-administered activities can vary based on clinical goals, lesion size, and pre-treatment dosimetry. For each of the acquisition days listed above, the data was reconstructed using the different beta values and scan times mentioned earlier. Rather than optimising the acquisition and reconstruction parameters separately, they were analysed together to determine the optimal PET protocol (acquisition and reconstruction) as a whole.

A dose-based comparison was performed by converting PET images into dose maps using LDM dosimetry, which were subsequently compared to reference MC dose maps. This method, adapted from a previous work [35], is summarised below. Dose volume histograms (DHVs) were generated for the background region and all hot spheres, but the analysis focused on the smallest visible sphere , which varied depending on the activity level, and on the largest sphere . The smallest visible sphere was selected via visual inspection of the PET images. To quantify the difference between LDM- and MC-derived DVHs, the root mean square error (RMSE) was used. As RMSE values for background spheres () were typically much lower than those for hot spheres (), each RMSE was normalised by the respective reference mean absorbed dose, extracted from the MC dose map, to obtain normalised-RMSE for background () and hot sphere () regions. The optimal PET protocol was identified by minimising the Euclidean norm . The notation used for optimal protocols is , where X is either the diameter of the smallest visible sphere or the diameter of the largest sphere (X=37 mm). For each activity range, the three best protocols were ranked to evaluate whether longer acquisition times could be replaced with shorter ones, aiming to improve patient comfort while maintaining comparable dosimetric accuracy.

Comparison of image-based voxelised dosimetry methods

The images corresponding to the optimal PET protocols identified in the previous section were used to generate dose maps using DVK dosimetry. Along with the previously generated LDM dose maps, DVK dose maps were compared to the reference MC dose map using the same metrics as before. In addition, the mean absorbed dose error was calculated.

To assess the influence of non-linearity effects on absorbed dose calculations, the mean absorbed dose error and the nRMSE were also computed for rescaled versions of the optimal protocols, in which the corresponding images were scaled to align the total image activity with the theoretical activity at the time of acquisition.

Reconstructed spatial resolution with -PET

To assess PET spatial resolution in the context of imaging, we used a matched-filter approach as explained by Prieto et al. [38]. Sphere RC from the optimised protocols were compared with those of a pristine NEMA model blurred using Gaussian kernels of different full width at half maximum (FWHM) values : 2–30 mm with a step of 0.1 mm. The -PET spatial resolution was determined by minimising the RMSE between the RC values of the PET reconstructed image and those of the blurred distributions of the model. The FWHM of the Gaussian filter resulting in the minimal RMSE value corresponds to the most probable estimate of the spatial resolution for the reconstructed image. The term effective spatial resolution is used to describe this metric.

The pristine NEMA model is an activity map generated using GATE with a sphere-to-background ratio of 8:1, identical to the phantom acquisitions. It has the same voxel spacing as the corresponding PET images but is not affected by other factors that degrade spatial resolution in realistic PET acquisitions. To avoid bias in RC calculation due to non-linearity effects, the PET images were scaled to match the true phantom activities. Quantitative linearity

The quantified image activity in the two VOIs ( and ) and the relative errors were plotted as a function of the theoretical activity during phantom decay (Fig. 3). Above 2.5 GBq, the quantified activity within the phantom considerably underestimated the theoretical value, with relative errors ranging from − 12.9% at 2.8 GBq to − 15.7% at 3.6 GBq. For the total image VOI, the underestimation was less pronounced, ranging from − 7.7 to − 11.2% between 2.8 and 3.6 GBq. Between 750 and 2.5 GBq, the quantified phantom activity was considered accurate, with errors remaining within ± 10 %. Below 750 MBq, the quantified activity within the phantom was overestimated with errors ranging from +15.8 % at around 590 MBq to +52.1 % at 208 MBq. For high activity levels (Table 1), lower beta values (2000–3000) were most suitable for the smallest sphere (10 mm), whereas higher values (4000–6000) yielded better performance for the largest sphere (37 mm). The highest-ranked protocols corresponded to 15- and 20-minute acquisitions, respectively, but the small differences in Euclidean norm between top-ranked protocols indicate that multiple parameter combinations performed similarly well. At intermediate activity levels (Table 2), a 20-minute acquisition was consistently retained, with suitable beta ranges of 5000–7000 for the smallest visible sphere (17 mm) and 8000–10,000 for the largest sphere. For low activity levels (Table 3), the smallest visible sphere was the 28 mm sphere (sphere 5). Due to the high image noise, the optimal protocols were the longest acquisition time (20 minutes) and the highest beta value (10,000).

Discussion

Quantitative linearity

For almost a decade, it has been known that the quantitative linearity of PET for depends on the scanner model and the corrections implemented in the reconstruction, as demonstrated by the Quest phantom study [12].

Using our digital PET scanner (Discovery MI, 4 rings), we observed a linear relationship between the measured and expected activities when considering either the entire image () or the phantom VOI (). However, noticeable deviations from the identity line (>10%) occurred at high activities (> 2.5 GBq) and low activities (< 1.0 GBq). While a full description of this effect was beyond the scope of this study, these non-linearities are likely due to the challenges of - PET imaging, which have been investigated in detail by several groups [14, 20–22]. Specifically, the low coincidence rate (inherent to the low branching ratio) and the high random fraction (random divided by prompt coincidences), caused by the intrinsic radioactivity of the LYSO crystal and, above all, the Bremsstrahlung radiation, could lead to inaccuracies in the random and scatter corrections typically applied in PET reconstruction. It has been suggested that a key limitation arises from the non-negativity constraint of the maximum likelihood expectation maximisation (MLEM)-based reconstruction algorithm, which can generate a significant quantity of negative sinograms when random fractions are high [20, 22]. Truncating these negative values at zero introduces a positive, noisy bias to the background if a dedicated reconstruction strategy is not used [21]. For instance, in our study, the random fractions for a 20 minutes acquisition were 0.85, 0.90 and 0.94 in the high, intermediate, and low activity ranges, respectively. These values are higher than those of a standard EARL acquisition of the NEMA IEC phantom with fluorodeoxyglucose -FDG (0.18). Moreover, in the low activity range, noise makes scatter correction even more challenging [22]. Overall, these non-linearity effects introduce a systematic error into the quantified activity of (especially at low activities encountered in practice) and thus into the resulting absorbed dose map. According to the literature, these effects appear to be more pronounced for GE scanners than for Siemens scanners [13, 18, 22]. Finally, it should be emphasised that the activities we measured were traceable to a metrological reference, and that further discrepancies may arise when measuring microspheres with calibrated activities supplied by manufacturers [39, 40].

Optimal PET protocols

In this study, we aimed to optimise PET protocol (acquisition and reconstruction) for SIRT, with a specific focus on Q.Clear. Q.Clear is a Bayesian penalised likelihood reconstruction algorithm that has been shown to be superior to conventional iterative reconstruction algorithms in clinical settings, particularly with -FDG [41]. This superiority is mainly due to the ability of its penalty function to suppress noise in a self-controlled manner while maintaining resolution at full convergence [23]. In the commercial GE software, the penalty function relies on a single user input variable (the beta value) to control the noise suppression. The known reported behaviour of the beta value is that increasing the beta value results in smoother images with reduced contrast and blurred edges [42]. The choice of this parameter depends on the objectives of the imaging task. On non-digital GE scanners, McGowan et al. concluded that a beta value of 4000 was optimal for confident diagnostic reading of PET imaging [24], whereas a beta value of 1000 (and a scan time of 15 min) was better for quantification [25]. Using the same GE PET scanner, Hou et al. [26] concluded that beta values of 2500, 300–1000 and 4000–5000 were optimal for image quality, quantification and dose distribution with , respectively. On a digital PET/MR scanner, Calatayud-Jordán et al. [27] reported an optimal beta value of 4000 for image quality and proposed values between 1000 and 2000 for quantification. The fact that the requirements for quantification and dose distribution are different may seem counter-intuitive at first, but it has actually been known for a long time [43]. We have also observed this for SPECT in the pre-treatment dosimetry for SIRT [35]: accurate absolute quantification generally requires no smoothing and high convergence of conventional iterative algorithms, resulting in noise amplification. However, noise has a detrimental effect on the shape of the DVH, stretching both ends (at low and high absorbed dose levels) and flattening the whole shape with respect to the reference DVH [13, 26]. As our study focused on dose distribution accuracy, our results are consistent with those of Hou et al. [26]. Under high activity conditions, beta values between 4000 and 6000 provided the most accurate DVH estimates for structures less affected by PVE (37-mm sphere). Structures heavily affected by PVE (10-mm sphere) required lower beta values of 2000–3000 (less smoothing). Low beta values (< 1000) resulted in highly noisy images with heterogeneous dose distributions, whereas high beta values (> 6000) produced over-smoothed images, leading to an underestimation of the absorbed dose over the spheres. This over-smoothing tends to produce a better DVH agreement in the intermediate and low activity conditions, as it effectively reduces image noise and thus dose distribution heterogeneity with respect to MC DVH.

Regarding acquisition duration, there was no substantial difference when scanning for 15 or 20 minutes at high activity levels with our SFOV digital PET. However, our DVH-based optimisation method required an acquisition time of 20 minutes for intermediate and low activity scans. Similar findings were reported by Scott et al. [25] on a non-digital SFOV PET, where scan times greater than 15 minutes did not result in noticeable improvements in image metrics under both high activity (3 GBq) and intermediate activity (1 GBq) conditions. Similarly, Calatayud-Jordán et al. [26] noted that 20-minute scans offered the best image quality on a digital GE PET/MR scanner at activity ranges close to our high and intermediate conditions, although suggesting 15-minute scans could also be considered in a clinical setting for improved patient comfort. Recent studies on large axial field-of-view (LAFOV) PET/CT scanners suggest that their increased sensitivity enables acquisition time to be substantially reduced, to as little as five minutes, without compromising image quality [44]. In addition, DVHs tend to exhibit a shorter high-dose tail, likely reflecting reduced image noise [45]. These systems enable the liver and lungs to be imaged in a single scan, reducing the overall acquisition time and improving patient comfort. However, quantifying small structures remains problematic [46].

Dosimetric accuracy with PET

The dosimetric analysis of the optimised protocols using mean absorbed dose revealed a consistent underestimation of the absorbed dose in spheres across all activity ranges. These values are higher than those obtained using the same methodology for protocol optimisation in99mTc-SPECT pre-treatment dosimetry, as described in [35]. The challenges of PET imaging should not be underestimated. Firstly, as highlighted above, the low signal-to-noise ratio of scans greatly impairs image quality. Secondly, non-linearity effects discussed above appear to substantially affect our quantitative accuracy, particularly at low activity levels. However, the low activity range of this study corresponds to a sphere concentration that is well below the minimum detectable concentration (1 MBq/mL) usually considered for detectability and quantitative accuracy within 10% in PET imaging [9, 20]. To counteract these effects, some authors have applied corrections directly to the mean absorbed dose measured with PET imaging [13]. To evaluate the impact of these non-linearities on quantitative accuracy, we scaled our images prior to performing the dose calculation. However, for small volumes, errors with imaging remain substantial: all spheres of the IEC NEMA phantom are significantly affected by PVE effects, as also demonstrated in modern SFOV PET systems from each manufacturer [9, 24, 30]. The effective reconstructed spatial resolution of is rather poor in the clinical context (greater than 7 mm), i.e. no better than that of SPECT. In background regions less prone to PVE effects, correcting non-linearity effects should nevertheless ensure that mean absorbed doses can be recovered with an error margin of 10% (Tables 4, 6 and 8). This is important in the context of SIRT, where most volumes of interest (lesions, non-tumoral liver and whole liver) are rather large.

Examining the optimisation strategies, it was found that the accuracy of the mean absorbed dose and the DVH agreement were better with protocols optimised for the smallest visible sphere, regardless of the activity level. Targeting smaller spheres resulted indeed in lower beta values, producing less blurred images with improved effective spatial resolution and mitigating the previously discussed PVE. Conversely, in the background region, protocols optimised for the largest sphere produced better DVH agreement as higher beta values reduced image noise. Overall, the optimised protocols for the smallest visible spheres perform better than the clinical OSEM-based protocol for large spheres and the background. In these cases, they provide a better match with the reference DVHs obtained via Monte Carlo simulation, while maintaining the same level of accuracy for mean absorbed doses (within 10%). At the same time, image quality is also improved with Q.Clear. For SIRT, this demonstrates that reliable voxel-based dosimetry and good visual quality can be achieved on the Discovery MI scanner by selecting the appropriate activity range and lesion volume for a given treatment.

When the LDM and DVK methods were compared in spheres, it was found that LDM dosimetry demonstrated superior agreement with MC dosimetry with respect to the mean absorbed dose and DVH shapes, particularly in smaller spheres (). Pasciak et al. [34] demonstrated that LDM provided more accurate dosimetry for imaging systems if the FWHM of their point spread function was larger than the FWHM of the S-value dose kernel. In this case, the inherent blurring due to the limited spatial resolution is sufficient to simulate beta energy transport [7]. Considering the range of effective spatial resolutions achieved with in this study, LDM remains the most suitable method for imaging small structures in the context of SIRT with PET. Equivalent mean absorbed dose and DVH agreement were found in the background regions for LDM and DVK. However, as observed in [30], LDM was found to be more sensitive to image noise and exhibited a slight tendency to amplify the maximum dose. This could negatively impact large structures of interest in SIRT (background regions), as demonstrated by the lower DVH agreement when using reconstructions with lower beta values.

Conclusion

This study investigated the optimisation of PET imaging on a Discovery MI scanner for post-treatment SIRT dosimetry as well as the performance of LDM and DVK dosimetry methods on the optimised protocols. The findings revealed that achieving accurate dosimetry with PET imaging remains challenging due to limitations in quantitative linearity and effective spatial resolution. Of the evaluated approaches, the LDM method appears suitable for calculating the mean absorbed dose and if adequate corrections are implemented to compensate for quantitative errors caused by model-specific nonlinearity, it can ensure agreement within 10%. DVK performed better in terms of DVH agreement in large structures due to its greater ability to handle noise.
