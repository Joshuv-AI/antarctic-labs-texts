Serum cytokine levels towards precision medicine in Still’s disease: a subanalysis of a randomized controlled trial of tocilizumab
Authors: Koji Suzuki, Hideto Kameda, Kei Ikeda, Tomonori Ishii, Kosaku Murakami, Hyota Takamatsu, Yoshiya Tanaka, Takayuki Abe, Tsutomu Takeuchi, Yuko Kaneko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo predict the efficacy of IL-6 inhibition in patients with Still’s disease by analysing inflammatory cytokine profiles before and during an IL-6 receptor inhibitor therapy.MethodsThis is a subanalysis of the 52-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, in patients with Still’s disease. Multiple serum cytokine levels were measured regularly, and their pattern and profiles were analysed based on the response to tocilizumab.ResultsA total of 26 patients (13 in the tocilizumab group and 13 in the placebo group) were enrolled. Before tocilizumab treatment, IL-6 levels were correlated with DAS with 28 joints (r = 0.67, P < 0.01), and IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-18 levels tended to be correlated with systemic feature score (r = 0.33, P = 0.09; r = 0.38, P = 0.05; r = 0.40, P = 0.04, respectively). IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels were significantly elevated after tocilizumab initiation, while the other cytokines showed no significant difference compared with placebo. Baseline levels of IFN-γ and IL-1β were significantly higher in non-responders compared with responders (28.49 vs 5.65 pg/ml, P = 0.03; 0.26 vs 0.04 pg/ml, P = 0.048), and IFN-γ and IL-18 levels at week 52 remained high in non-responders (15.56 vs 7.03 pg/ml, P = 0.02; 5924 vs 392 pg/ml, P = 0.02).ConclusionsThe effect of tocilizumab is limited to the IL-6 signalling pathway in Still’s disease. Patients who did not respond to tocilizumab exhibited distinct cytokine profiles that pivot the IFN-γ axis. These findings highlight the role of IL-6 inhibition in Still’s disease and shed light on its personalized treatment strategies.Clinical trial registration numberUMIN Clinical Trials Registry; UMIN000012987.

Introduction

Still’s disease is a systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by spiking fever, sore throat, skin rash, arthralgia and neutrophilic leucocytosis [1, 2]. Although the exact pathophysiology of Still’s disease remains unclear, proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-18, TNFα and IFN-γ (IFN-γ), are involved in a complex cytokine loop [3]. These cytokines are derived from various immune cells and associated with disease activity and systemic and local symptoms [3, 4].

Glucocorticoids are the first-line treatment in Still’s disease, suppressing immune cells and proinflammatory cytokines [5]. However, long-term use of glucocorticoids causes various side effects, and a number of patients experience relapse along with glucocorticoid dose tapering [6]. Recently, biological drugs targeting specific cytokine signals, such as IL-6 and IL-1, have shown efficacy in controlling disease activity of Still’s disease, preventing disease flares, and reducing glucocorticoid doses [7–9]. However, the effectiveness of these drugs is still partial with ACR 50 response rates of 50–60% [10, 11], and prediction of response or classification of patients prior to initiation of a targeted drug has yet to be established. Here, we elucidated the profiles of inflammatory cytokine levels during the use of tocilizumab, an anti-IL-6 receptor inhibitor, and the distinct cytokine patterns for non-response to tocilizumab, from the subanalysis of the randomized, controlled trial of tocilizumab in Still’s disease [10].

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This subanalysis was based on the randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase III trial of tocilizumab for Still’s disease, conducted in Japan and registered with UMIN (trial registration number: UMIN000012987) [10]. The detailed protocol was published previously [10]. Briefly, patients diagnosed with Still’s disease according to the Yamaguchi criteria [1] who had shown inadequate response to more than 2 weeks of glucocorticoids (at least 0.5 mg/kg of prednisolone) were enrolled. Patients were randomized to receive either intravenous tocilizumab at a dose of 8 mg/kg or placebo every 2 weeks for 4 weeks (part 1, the initial glucocorticoid dose continued) and 8 weeks (part 2, glucocorticoid dose tapering allowed). After the initial placebo-controlled 12-week period, all patients received intravenous tocilizumab until week 52 (part 3). The trial enrolled 26 patients (13 in the tocilizumab group and 13 in the placebo group).

In the current subanalysis, we included all 26 patients for baseline cytokine analysis and 25 patients with cytokine levels after tocilizumab initiation available (13 in the tocilizumab group and 12 in the placebo group) for cytokine analysis during tocilizumab treatment (Fig. 1).

Patient flowchart. *The patient withdrew from the study prior to the measurement of cytokine levels following the initiation of tocilizumab and was therefore excluded from the analysis of cytokine levels after tocilizumab treatment

Definition of response to tocilizumab

Baseline disease activity of arthritis and systemic features were evaluated according to DAS in 28 joints using CRP (DAS28-CRP) [12] and systemic feature score (SFS) [10]. The response to tocilizumab was assessed using ACR coreset [13] and SFS [14]. Since the primary and major secondary end point of the trial was the ACR50 response and changes in SFS [10], we employed the achievement of ACR50 response, absence of clinical points on the SFS or clinically inactive disease (CID) [2] at week 52 with biweekly intravenous tocilizumab as a response in the current subanalysis.

Measurements of serum cytokines

We measured following serum cytokine levels at weeks 0, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 28 and 52; IL-1β, IL-6, IL-6 receptor, IL-8, IL-12, IL-18, GM-CSF, TNFα and IFN-γ using electrochemiluminescence assay. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, TNFα and IFN-γ were quantified using the V-PLEX Custom Human Cytokine Proinflammatory Panel 1 (Meso Scale Discovery: MSD, USA). GM-CSF was measured using the V-PLEX Custom Human Cytokine Panel 1 (MSD, USA). IL-6 receptor levels were measured using the Human IL-6R Ultra-Sensitive Kit (MSD, USA), and IL-18 was measured using the U-PLEX Human IL-18 Assay (MSD, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Parametric continuous variables are presented as the mean with standard deviation (SD), while non-parametric continuous variables are presented as the median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data are reported as percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test and that between three groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with additional pairwise comparisons conducted using the Steel-Dwass test to identify the source of differences. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to analyse the correlations between two continuous variables. Variables that were significant in the previous univariate analysis or clinically important were used as covariates in the multiple logistic regression models. We conducted principal component analysis (PCA) to generate principal component scores and cluster analysis using Ward’s method. Patients who withdrew the study (two in each of the tocilizumab and placebo groups) were classified as non-responders, using non-responder imputation. Last observation carried forward was used to impute missing values. Sensitivity analysis was conducted in cases who completed the study (Fig. 1). All statistical analyses were performed with JMP Software 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant, and P < 0.10 as a tendency.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by Certified Review Board of Keio (CRB3180017) with approval number of D13-02/UMIN000012987 and by an independent Ethics Committee or Institutional Review Board at each site and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part.

The analysis regarding cytokine levels at baseline included 26 patients, with 13 patients in each of the tocilizumab and placebo groups (Fig. 1). Baseline clinical characteristics and cytokine levels in the two groups are presented in Table 1. All patients exhibited both arthritis and systemic symptoms, and no significant difference in baseline characteristics or cytokine levels was observed between the two groups, although the disease activity and glucocorticoid dose tended to be higher in the placebo group. No patients developed macrophage activation syndrome during the study.

Correlation of Still’s disease symptoms and cytokine levels

We analysed the correlation between cytokine levels and disease activity of Still’s disease, symptoms and laboratory tests at baseline (Table 2). Pearson’s correlation showed a significant positive correlation between IL-6 levels and DAS28-CRP (r = 0.67, P< 0.01). IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-18 levels tended to correlate with SFS (r = 0.33, P = 0.09; r = 0.38, P = 0.05; r = 0.40, P = 0.04, respectively). Regarding individual symptoms, GM-CSF was lower in patients with rash (0.0 pg/ml vs 0.10 pg/ml, P = 0.08), while IL-6 and IL-18 levels were higher in patients with fever (8.11 pg/ml vs 3.01 pg/ml, P = 0.04; 73 395 pg/ml vs 25 573 pg/ml, P = 0.05, respectively). CRP levels were significantly correlated with IL-6, and ferritin levels were correlated with IL-1β, IL-18, GM-CSF, TNFα, and IFN-γ (r = 0.68, P< 0.001; r = 0.44, P = 0.03; r = 0.54, P = 0.004; r = 0.61, P = 0.001; r = 0.73, P< 0.001, respectively). We also performed cluster analysis between each cytokine (Supplementary Fig. S1A, available at Rheumatology online). IL-1β, GM-CSF, TNFα and IFN-γ formed a cluster and correlated with ferritin, while IL-6, IL-6 receptor, IL-8, IL-12 and IL-18 formed another cluster. When other variables including DAS28, SFS, CRP and ferritin levels were added in the cluster analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1B, available at Rheumatology online), IL-6 became clustered with CRP, DAS28 and SFS.

Time-course changes in cytokine levels during tocilizumab or placebo treatment

Figure 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online, show the time-course changes in cytokine levels. For this analysis, one patient in the placebo group was excluded due to withdrawal from the study and no cytokine data available after tocilizumab initiation (Fig. 1). At week 4, the tocilizumab group showed higher IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels compared with the placebo group (10.70 pg/ml vs 1.00 pg/ml, P = 0.03; 110 807 pg/ml vs 25 737 pg/ml, P< 0.001, respectively), while the other cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-18 and IFN-γ, showed no significant differences between the tocilizumab and placebo groups (Fig. 2A). IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels were stabilized by week 12, and the difference between the tocilizumab and placebo groups was no longer observed after the placebo group started tocilizumab. In contrast, the trajectories of the other cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-18 and IFN-γ, showed no significant differences between the tocilizumab and placebo groups throughout the study duration (Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online).

Characteristics of non-responders to tocilizumab before tocilizumab initiation

We compared the baseline characteristics, including cytokine levels, between tocilizumab non-responders and responders. Response was defined as the achievement of ACR50, absence of clinical SFS, or both. As cytokine levels at baseline (Table 1) and the final proportion of responders were not different between the tocilizumab and placebo-to-tocilizumab groups (ACR50 response: 53.9% vs 83.3%, P = 0.20; absence of SFS: 46.2% vs 58.3%, P = 0.70; both: 30.1% vs 50.0%, P = 0.43), the two groups were combined for this analysis. Of the 25 patients, 10 patients (40%) achieved both ACR50 and absence of clinical SFS at week 52. As shown in Table 3, compared with the responder patients, the non-responder patients demonstrated a higher proportion of females (93.3% vs 50%, P = 0.02) and significantly elevated levels of IFN-γ and IL-1β at baseline (28.5 pg/ml vs 5.6 pg/ml, P = 0.03; 0.26 pg/ml vs 0.04 pg/ml, P = 0.048, respectively). When the achievement of ACR50 (n = 14 [56%]), absence of clinical SFS (n = 13 [52%]), and additionally CID (n = 7 [28%]) were analysed individually, IL-1β levels and IFN-γ levels at baseline were significantly elevated in the ACR50 non-responders and CID non-responders. No significant difference was observed as for absence of clinical SFS (Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online).

Predictive model for tocilizumab response based on baseline cytokines

Given that baseline IL-1β and IFN-γ levels were significantly higher in the tocilizumab non-responders based on the achievement of both ACR50 and absence of clinical SFS at week 52, we depicted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to determine their predictive values. The ROC curves identified IL-1β > 0.07 pg/ml and IFN-γ > 7.80 pg/ml at baseline as significant thresholds for distinguishing non-responders from responders, with area under the curves of 0.74 and 0.76, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B, available at Rheumatology online). When we divided patients into four groups according to the high/low IL-1β and IFN-γ levels, 85% of patients with both high levels were non-responders, whereas all patients with both low were responders (Fig. 2B). We performed multiple logistic regression analysis to identify the characteristics of non-responders to tocilizumab, incorporating age, sex and the IL-1β and IFN-γ levels as covariates. The analysis revealed a high odds ratio for female sex and elevated IL-1β and IFN-γ levels, although these were not statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size (Supplementary Table S2, available at Rheumatology online).

Cytokine profiles of non-responders after tocilizumab treatment

We then analysed cytokine levels at week 52 based on the response to tocilizumab. Compared with the tocilizumab responders who achieved both ACR50 and absence of clinical SFS at week 52, the non-responders exhibited significantly higher levels of IL-18 and IFN-γ (5924 pg/ml vs 392 pg/ml, P = 0.03; 15.6 pg/ml vs 7.03 pg/ml, P = 0.02, respectively, Table 3). This tendency was maintained when ACR50 response, absence of clinical SFS and CID were analysed individually. Additionally, ACR50 non-responders showed elevated levels of TNFα at week 52 (Supplementary Table S3, available at Rheumatology online). A sensitivity analysis performed using only complete cases (n = 22) demonstrated similar results (Supplementary Table S4, available at Rheumatology online).

The relationship between cytokine patterns and tocilizumab response

We analysed the relationship between cytokine patterns and tocilizumab response using cluster analysis and PCA. To identify the characteristics of tocilizumab non-responders, we included IL-18 and IFN-γ levels at week 52 as well as all baseline cytokine levels, as these cytokines at week 52 were indicative of the characteristics of non-responder as described above. Cluster analysis classified the patients into four subgroups (Fig. 3A). The characteristics of the four clusters are shown in Supplementary Table S5, available at Rheumatology online. Figure 3B shows differences in cytokine levels among the clusters. Cluster 2 showed significantly low levels of baseline IL-18 and IL-6 receptor compared with the other clusters. Clusters 1 and 3 showed largely similar baseline cytokines but were different in terms of IL-18 and IFN-γ levels at week 52 (IL-18 at week 52: 70 041 pg/ml vs 3502 pg/ml, P = 0.02; IFN-γ at week 52: 72.51 pg/ml vs 7.30 pg/ml, P = 0.08). Although Cluster 4 included only one patient, the high levels of multiple cytokines of the patient was remarkable (Fig. 3B). Achievements of both ACR50 and absence of clinical SFS were 54.5%, 57.1%, 0%, and 0% in Clusters 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively (Fig. 3C). By PCA, these cytokines were statistically integrated into principal components (PCs), with two PCs explaining 50.2% of the total variance in the data (Fig. 3D). The first principal component predominantly consisted of baseline IL-1β, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and TNFα, while the second principal component was mainly characterized by IL-6, IL-6 receptor, and IL-18 levels at baseline and IL-18 and IFN-γ levels at week 52 (Fig. 3E).

Discussion

In this exploratory analysis of the randomized, controlled trial of tocilizumab in Still’s disease, we demonstrated that IL-6 levels correlated with arthritis, while IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-18 levels correlated with systemic signs and symptoms. After tocilizumab treatment, IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels were significantly elevated, while the other cytokine levels showed no significant differences between tocilizumab and placebo administration, suggesting that the effect of tocilizumab in Still’s disease is attributed to the suppression of IL-6 signalling pathway. Patients who did not respond to tocilizumab showed higher IL-1β and IFN-γ levels prior to tocilizumab initiation, as well as higher IL-18 and IFN-γ levels after tocilizumab treatment, indicating the persistent elevation of IL-18 and IFN-γ levels play an important role in the refractoriness to tocilizumab.

Elevation of proinflammatory cytokines is a hallmark of Still’s disease, with previous studies demonstrating that IL-6, IL-8, IL-18, TNF-α and IFN-γ levels were elevated in patients with Still’s disease with high disease activity [4, 15, 16]. We further revealed that IL-6 was correlated with DAS28 and SFS, and IL-1β and IL-18 were correlated with SFS. This finding of distinct cytokine subsets in specific symptoms aligns with previous studies that reported IL-6 were elevated in cases with arthritis and IL-18 were elevated in those without arthritis [17–19]. Furthermore, we identified that ferritin levels were correlated with serum IL-1β, IL-18, GM-CSF, TNF-α and IFN-γ, suggesting ferritin levels are a surrogate for an activation loop of macrophages.

Our study revealed that IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels increased significantly after tocilizumab treatment compared with placebo. These changes in IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels after inhibition of IL-6 receptors are well known in rheumatoid arthritis [20, 21], but our study was the first to report in Still’s disease, especially in comparison with placebo. Moreover, we measured other proinflammatory cytokines related to the pathophysiology of Still’s disease, such as IL-1β, IL-18 and GM-CSF and demonstrated that changes in those cytokines other than IL-6 and IL-6 receptor levels after tocilizumab treatment were similar to placebo administration. These findings suggest that the effect of tocilizumab on the cytokine cascade in Still’s disease is limited to the IL-6 signalling pathway and that the concomitant use of glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressive drugs at the initiation of tocilizumab may be important in active patients with multiple cytokine elevations. In patients who do not respond to the suppression of IL-6 signalling, inhibition of other treatment targets is also required.

The characteristic cytokine profiles of Still’s disease refractory to tocilizumab treatment remain poorly understood. Previous studies regarding rheumatoid arthritis reported that high baseline IL-6 or IL-1β levels may be characteristics of poor response to tocilizumab [20, 22]. In the context of Still’s disease, however, there have been only two conflicting reports. Tang et al. demonstrated that baseline IL-18 levels, but not IL-6 levels, predicted a poor response to tocilizumab in Still’s disease [23], whereas Oka et al. reported the opposite [24]. Furthermore, no studies have focused on cytokine levels after tocilizumab treatment, except for a few sporadic cases reports [25, 26]. Our study, comparing baseline and post-treatment cytokine levels between tocilizumab responders and non-responders, revealed that non-responders showed significantly higher baseline IL-1β and IFN-γ levels and elevated post-treatment IL-18 and IFN-γ levels. Our cluster analysis also revealed that, in addition to elevated levels of several inflammatory cytokines at baseline, persistently elevated IL-18 and IFN-γ levels were a distinct feature of non-responders. This indicates the need to target these cytokines with alternative biological agents or possibly with tocilizumab with concurrent immunosuppressive therapy that can inhibit multiple cytokines.

Regarding cluster analysis, a previous study classified patients with Still’s disease into four clusters: Cluster 1: juvenile/transitional, Cluster 2: uncomplicated, Cluster 3: hyperferritinemic and Cluster 4: catastrophic, based on clinical characteristics and laboratory data [27]. While it is challenging to correlate these clusters directly with our cytokine-based clusters, Clusters 2 and 4 in our study appear to align with Clusters 2 and 4 in the previous study, respectively, which were stratified based on clinical course and laboratory data. Further studies with larger cohorts, including cytokine level data, are necessary to refine patient clustering and develop more targeted treatment strategies.

Our study and previous studies suggest that IL-18 plays an important role in the pathogenesis of Still’s disease [3]. IL-18 is a proinflammatory cytokine belonging to the IL-1 family and has a potent ability to induce IFN-γ production [28], which, as shown in our study, has also plays significant role in Still’s disease. The biological activity of IL-18 is regulated by IL-18 binding protein (IL-18BP), which inhibits its interaction with receptors and the subsequent production of IFN-γ [29]. In Still’s disease, both IL-18 overexpression and insufficient IL-18BP lead to the pathogenic activation of IL-18 [30]. Although there is not yet sufficient evidence to establish the efficacy of IL-18 inhibition in Still’s disease [31, 32], our findings suggest that IL-18 inhibitors may be a potentially viable treatment option for patients who do not respond to tocilizumab. In addition, IL-1 inhibitors such as anakinra or canakinumab, which are approved for the treatment of Still’s disease in Europe and the United States [33], are known to reduce IL-18 levels [34], supporting their promising efficacy in patients who do not respond to tocilizumab. In contrast, non-responders to IL-1 inhibitors exhibit significantly lower baseline IL-18 levels and insufficient reductions in IL-6 levels after treatment [34], suggesting that tocilizumab may be effective in these cases. Further studies are needed to develop precise personalized treatment strategies for Still’s disease.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small due to the rarity of Still’s disease. However, the nature of a randomized, controlled trial with regular measurement of cytokines made the results reliable. The small sample size also limits the robustness and generalizability of the cluster analysis, highlighting the need for future studies with larger cohorts to validate our findings. Second, patient enrolment was regulated by strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, which may have limited the generalizability of the results. Third, the duration of treatment with tocilizumab was not necessarily uniform between patients who responded to tocilizumab and those who did not. However, this limitation was not relevant because cytokine levels and treatment efficacy reached a plateau within 12 weeks after tocilizumab initiation. Fourth, we did not distinguish IL-18 complexed with IL-18BP and unbound free IL-18 [30], which limits the functional implications of IL-18 in Still’s disease. Despite these limitations, we believe our study demonstrates the novel findings of cytokine characteristics in Still’s disease treated with an IL-6 receptor inhibition.

In conclusion, the effect of tocilizumab on cytokines outside of the IL-6 signalling pathway is limited, and the elevation of IL-1β, IL-18 and IFN-γ may play an important role in the pathogenesis of Still’s disease refractory to IL-6 inhibition. These findings provide deep implications towards future personalized treatment strategies.
