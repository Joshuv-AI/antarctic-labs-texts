DHX15 affects AML1‐ETO9a splicing together with HNRNPL, RBM33 in AML1‐ETO‐positive acute myeloid leukaemia
Authors: Qiao Liu, Xuechun Wang, Jiqiang Fan, Yi Zheng, Luya Ye, Wenhan Chang, Xianglei Chen, Yuanhua Cai, Xiaolin Lai, Yang Li, Lili Pan, Xiaofan Li, RenJang Lin, Shaoyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe AML1‐ETO (AE) fusion gene, resulting from t(8;21)(q22;q22), represents a prevalent subtype of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Retention of exon 9a between exon 8 and 9 of the ETO (RUNX1 Partner Transcriptional Co‐Repressor 1, also name RUNX1T1) gene generates the oncogenic AML1‐ETO9a (AE9a) splice variant. Here, we uncover the potential involvement of RNA (ribonucleic acid) helicase DHX15 (DEAH‐box helicase 15) in AE9a splicing and AML progression. Clinically, higher expressed DHX15 is associated with increased AE9a/AE abundance and poor outcomes in AE‐positive (AE+) AML patients. DHX15 knockdown impaired cell proliferation, induced S/G2 cell‐cycle arrest in vitro and prolonged overall survival in AE+ leukaemia‐bearing mice in vivo. In addition, DHX15 knockdown reduced AE and AE9a expression, and RNA immunoprecipitation revealed DHX15 bound to AE and AE9a transcripts and RNA pull‐down assay showed a selective interaction of HNRNPL and RBM33 with AE9a transcripts. Three‐dimensional structure prediction suggests a sophisticated regulatory complex formation involving DHX15, HNRNPL (heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L) and RBM33 (RNA binding motif protein 33). Further, HNRNPL and RBM33 gene expression positively correlated with AE9a levels in AE+ AML samples. Downregulation of HNRNPL and RBM33 reduced AE9a expression in Kasumi‐1 cells. Collectively, our findings support an association between DHX15, HNRNPL, RBM33 and AE9a splicing and suggest their contribution to leukemogenic progression in AE+ AML.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) is a haematopoietic malignant clonal proliferative disease driven by genetic abnormalities or acquired somatic mutations in leukaemia cells. 1 Chromosome translocation‐related proto‐oncogene activation is one of the most critical genetic abnormalities that provides proliferation advantages and differentiation/maturation disorders. 2

T(8;21) translocation generates AML1‐ETO (AE) fusion gene, 3 , 4 which retains the Runt domain of AML1 and four NHR (nervy homology region) domains of ETO. Due to the loss of activation domains in AML1, AE lacks transcription activation ability and is associated with a favourable outcome according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines. 5 , 6

AML1‐ETO9a (AE9a) is an alternative spliced isoform of AE from additional exon 9a (155 bp) retention between exon 8 and 9 of the ETO gene. 7 Exon9a provides a stop codon, resulting in a carboxy‐terminal truncated isoform AE9a, which lacks NHR3 and 4 domains of the ETO protein. 8 Unlike AE, AE9a alone mediates leukaemia development in mouse models and induces poor outcomes in AE‐positive (AE+) AML patients. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Genomic analysis showed AE cases harboured recurrent somatic mutations, including a DEAD‐box (Aspartic acid ‐ Glutamic acid ‐ Alanine ‐ Aspartic acid seqence) RNA helicase DHX15 p.R222G mutation. 15 This missense mutation may not affect the ATPase (Adenosine Triphosphatase) or RNA‐binding ability of DHX15. However, knockdown (KD) of DHX15, which mimics a complete loss of function, leads to abnormal expression of splicing genes and increased alternative splicing events, 15 highlighting DHX15's importance in AE splicing in AML.

DHX15 is known to participate in spliceosome disassembly by interacting with G‐patch domain‐enriched proteins. 16 , 17 Recent studies show DHX15 also monitors early spliceosome assembly and ensures the quality control of U2 snRNP's (small nuclear ribonucleoprotein) engagement with an intron. 18 This inspired us to explore the potential relationship between DHX15 and AE alternative splicing in AE+ AML.

A previous study demonstrated that DHX15 is highly expressed in AML and associated with shorter overall survival (OS) and relapse‐free survival. 19 Here, we found DHX15 also predicts poor prognosis in AE+ AML patients, and DHX15 downregulation inhibited tumour growth and prolonged OS in AE+ cell‐derived xenograft (CDX) models in vivo. DHX15 expression positively correlated with AE and AE9a in both clinical and in vitro experiments. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) demonstrated associations between DHX15 and AE, AE9a transcripts, while RNA pull‐down identified selective interactions of HNRNPL and RBM33 with AE9a transcripts. Furthermore, predicted three‐dimensional structural analyses suggested potential interactions among DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33. Expression levels of DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 were positively related to AE9a in AE+ AML clinical samples. Collectively, we propose a potential association between DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 with AE9a splicing in AE+ AML.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient samples

AML1‐ETO negative (AE−) AML samples were collected from Fujian Union Hospital (n = 114, 2011–2018). The AE+ AML samples came from Fujian Union Hospital (n = 85) and other hospitals in China (n = 84, years 2016–2017, Wuhan Kindstar Diagnostics Co., Ltd). This study was approved by Fujian Medical University Union Hospital Ethics Committee.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

RNA was extracted with the E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit I (OMEGA, USA) for the clinical samples and TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) for the cell lines. cDNA (complementary DNA) was synthesized with Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific™, USA). RT‐qPCR (Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction) used QuantStudioTM Real‐Time PCR System (ABI, QuantStudio 6 Flex, USA) and FastStart Universal SYBR Green master (Roche, Switzerland). ΔCT (Delta Cycle Threshold) (Ct gene‐Ct GAPDH (glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase)) and 2−(∆Ct experiment group−∆Ct control group) were used for calculating relative gene expression and fold change. Primers are listed in Table S1.

Cell lines

Kasumi‐1 cells (ATCC [American Type Culture Collection], USA, RRID:CVCL_0589) were cultured in RPMI (Roswell Park Memorial Institute)‐1640 medium (SH30809.01B, HyClone, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099‐141‐FBS, Gibco). SKNO‐1 cells (A GM‐CSF dependent cell line with an t(8, 21) chromosome translocation) (gift of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, RRID:CVCL_2196) were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium with 10% FBS and 10 mg/mL of GM‐CSF (Granulocyte‐Macrophage Colony‐Stimulating Factor) (PeproTech 300‐03, USA). HEK293T cells (ATCC, RRID:CVCL_0063) were grown in DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium) (SH30022.01B, HyClone) with 10% FBS. Cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. All cells were authenticated by STR (short tandem repeat) profiling.

RNA interference and restoration

The sequences and methods of DHX15‐shRNA (short hairpin RNA) and DHX15 overexpression were described in a previous study. 19 Materials for HNRNPL and RBM33 KD are described in the Supporting Information.

Western blot

The immunoblotting was performed as described previously. 20 Antibodies used include DHX15 (12265‐1‐AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), AML1 (4336T, CST: Cell Signaling Technology), P53 (9282S, CST), p‐p53 (9286S, CST), p21 (2947S, CST), p‐AKT (4060S, CST), AKT (RAC‐alpha serine/threonine‐protein kinase) (4685S, CST), c‐myc (5605S, CST), Cyclin D1 (2978S, CST), Cyclin E2 (4132S, CST), Cyclin A2 (4656T, CST), ATM (Ataxia‐Telangiectasia Mutated, a serine/threonine protein kinase) (ab32420, Abcam, UK), p‐CDC2 (Cyclin‐dependent kinase 1) (4539T, CST), MYT1 (4282T, CST), CHK2 (Checkpoint kinase 2) (ab32148, Abcam), GAPDH (5147S, CST).

Cell cycle and proliferation

Cell cycle analysis followed BD_FlowCytometry_DNA_Staining_Protocol (BD Biosciences, USA, Cat. no. 550825). Cell proliferation used cell counting kit (CCK8, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were analysed 48 h post DHX15‐shRNA and DHX15 overexpression lentivirus infection.

CDX model and in vivo imaging system assay

NCG mice (NOD (Non‐obese diabetic)/ShiLtJGpt‐Prkdc em26Cd52 Il2rg em26Cd22/Gpt: a severely immunodeficient laboratory model that lack functional T, B, and natural killer cells) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China, Strain no. T001475). Luciferase reporters (pLVX‐Puro‐Luc, Miaolingbio, Wuhan, China, P18849) were packaged with pMD2.G (a common plasmid used as an envelope vector for lentivirus and retrovirus packaging) and psPAX2 (a second‐generation lentiviral packaging plasmid) plasmids and infected Kasumi‐1 cells, followed by puromycin screening. Hypodermically injected 1.2 × 106 cells into 6‐week‐old female NCG mice to establish xenograft models, monitored the tumour size every 3 days and detected bioluminescence intensity every 7 days by in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer). The signal data were saved and the total flux in photons per second (photons/s) was calculated and used to determine significance. Record the OS as tumour volumes reached 1.5 cm3. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Medical University approved all animal procedures.

RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq)

Samples were collected 48 h post‐virus infection. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Germany) and submitted for mRNA library prep and sequencing (Novogene, Beijing, China). High/low expression genes were identified with fold change ≥2 and p value ≤0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA v4.3.2. (UC San Diego and BROAD Institute).

RNA immunoprecipitation

RIP used Magna RIP™ Kit (Millipore, #17‐701, Germany)—details in Supporting Information.

RNA pull‐down assay

RNA pull‐down used Pierce™ Magnetic RNA‐Protein pull‐down kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #20164, USA)—details in Supporting Information.

3D structure prediction

The DHX15‐RMB33‐hnRNPL complex 3D structure was predicted by ColabFold 21 and viewed by ChimeraX.

Statistics

Unpaired t‐test compared DHX15 expression in AML samples. One‐way/two‐way anova (analysis of variance) with unpaired t‐test analysed gene expressions (RT‐qPCR), OD (Optical density) value for CCK‐8 assay, cell cycle, xenograft tumour and weight growth analysis. OS of AML patients and cell line xenografts was assessed with Kaplan–Meier, Wilcoxon and log‐rank tests respectively. Scatter plot was used for gene correlation analysis with the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD); p‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses used GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA, RRID:SCR_002798).

RESULTS

DHX15 correlates with the poor progress in AE + AML

Highly expressed DHX15 predicts poor prognosis in AML, 19 and has been proven to be involved in alternative splicing events in AE+ AML within in vitro studies. 15 To explore the effect of DHX15 on AE+ AML, we analysed its expression in 169 AE+ and 114 AE− AML samples. The results show DHX15 is lower expressed in AE+ AML than in AE− AML (p < 0.001) (Figure 1A). Further, 28 clinical features available AE+ AML samples (Table S3) were analysed and divided into two groups by median DHX15 expression, revealing that the higher DHX15 expression correlated with shorter OS in AE+ AML (high vs. low, median 10 vs. 20 months, p = 0.0275) (Figure 1B).

To investigate the role of DHX15 in AE+ AML prognosis, we performed DHX15 KD in AE+ Kasumi‐1 and SKNO‐1 cells. DHX15‐KD inhibited cell growth and was reversed upon DHX15 rescue (Figure 1C,D; Figure S1A). In Kasumi‐1 CDX models, DHX15‐KD mitigated tumour generation and growth (Figure 1E,F) and prolonged OS (NC vs. KD: 26 vs. 23 days, p = 0.003; Figure 1G). These results indicate that higher DHX15 expression contributes to poor progress in AE+ AML.

DHX15 KD induced S/G2 cell cycle arrest in AE + AML cells

To understand how DHX15 affects the progression and proliferation of AE+ AML cells, GSEA was performed on RNA‐seq data from Kasumi‐1 and SKNO‐1. We observed a pronounced suppression of cell cycle‐related pathways such as E2F (E2 promoter‐binding factor) and G2M (G2/M phase of cell cycle) checkpoint pathways 22 in Kasumi‐1 cells (Figure 2A,B). A subsequent cell cycle assay verified an arrest in the S/G2 phase in both Kaumi‐1 and SKNO‐1 cells (Figure 2C). Consistently, DHX15‐KD in Kasumi‐1 cells downregulated cell cycle proteins ATM, p‐ATM, AKT, P53, MYC (MYC proto‐oncogene, bHLH transcription factor), cyclinA2, and p‐WEEL (G2 Checkpoint Kinase), and upregulated p‐AKT, p‐p53, p21 and CDK2 (Cyclin‐dependent kinase 2) proteins (Figure 2D). Restoring DHX15 expression rescued all affected proteins to untreated levels (Figure 2E). These findings strongly suggest that DHX15 influences AE+ AML cell proliferation via cell‐cycle regulation.

DHX15 regulates the AE9a transcript expression level

While AE+ AML fusion generally has a favourable prognosis per NCCN guidelines, this is also observed in our cohort (45 AE+ vs. 68 AE− AML patients, median survival 26 vs. 18 months; Figure S2A). We sought to explore the potential relationship between DHX15 and AE9a expression beyond the previously observed effects on cell‐cycle regulation. Given the reported involvement of DHX15 in alternative splicing 15 and the association of AE9a with poor outcomes in AE+ AML, 13 , 14 we explored whether DHX15 expression was associated with AE9a transcript abundance.

First, we detected DHX15, AE and AE9a transcript levels in AE+ AML clinical samples. AE9a is co‐expressed with AE (Figure S2B–F) and shows a positive correlation (p < 0.0001) (Figure S2B), parallel with previous studies. 8 , 10 , 14 The AE9a/AE abundance ranged from 0% to 100% (average 45.8%) (Figure S2F). Moreover, higher DHX15 mRNA levels positively correlated with both AE9a (p < 0.0001) and AE (p = 0.0098) (Figure 3A,B), but not with the AE9a/AE ratio (Figure S3A) in AE+ AML cases.

Next, DHX15‐KD in Kasumi‐1 and SKNO‐1 cells significantly downregulated both AE and AE9a expression (Figure 3C,G). Notably, subsequent DHX15 rescue assay showed a more pronounced recovery of AE9a over AE at the mRNA level (Figure 3E,H). However, only AE, not AE9a protein, showed downregulation (Figure 3D) and recovery (Figure 3F) in Kasumi‐1 cells, potentially due to the low abundance of AE9a protein (Figure S3C). Despite this, we conclude a close relationship between DHX15 and AE9a transcripts.

HNRNPL and RBM33 are highly correlated with AE9a transcripts

To investigate the potential relationship between DHX15 and AE transcript splicing, we performed the GSEA in DHX15‐NC/KD Kasumi‐1 and SKNO‐1 cells. The DHX15‐KD group showed significant downregulation of the splicing‐related pathways 23 and AE target gene sets 24 , 25 (Figure 4A,B; Figure S4A). The RIP assay in Kasumi‐1 cells detected both AE and AE9a mRNA in DHX15‐precipitated complex (Figure 4C), indicating potential DHX15 binding to AE pre‐mRNA. Subsequently, we performed RNA pull‐down using in vitro transcripts of AE, AE9a‐short (non‐natural) and AE9a (full‐length AE mRNA with exon9a) (Figure 4D,E upper), followed by LC–MS/MS (Liquid Chromatography Tandem Mass Spectrometry) (Data S1). Intriguingly, DHX15 was not detected in the RNA‐binding protein complexes from these transcripts by WB (Western Blot) (Figure 4E bottom) or MS (Figure 4F; Figure S4B), suggesting the interaction of DHX15 is weak or indirect.

To identify factors potentially associated with AE9a formation, we compared proteins enriched in AE versus AE9a RNA pull‐down assays. Twenty‐one proteins were identified exclusively in the AE9a‐associated group, including HNRNPL, RBM14, RBM33, SF3A2 (splicing factor 3a subunit 2) and SRSF4 (Serine and Arginine‐Rich Splicing Factor 4). Conversely, cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPL10A, RPL23A, RPL24, RPL3, RPL31, RPL1, RPS13 and RPS14) preferentially associated with AE (Figure 4F; Figure S4B).

Among the proteins enriched in the AE9a group, UCSC (University of California, Santa Cruz) genome browser analysis (https://genome.soe.ucsc.edu/) predicted potential interactions between DHX15 and HNRNPL, RBM33, SF3A2 and SRSF4. We therefore assessed their expression with AE/AE9a transcripts in 69 available AE+ AML patients. AE9a expression strongly positively correlated with RBM33 and HNRNPL, while negatively with SRSF4 (Figure 5A). Interestingly, a bidirectional relationship was observed between AE and HNRNPL, RBM33 and SF3A2: lower AE expression (−delta Ct <−10) correlated positively, whereas higher AE expression (−delta Ct >−10) showed the opposite trend (Figure 5B). This suggests HNRNPL and RBM33 potentially promote both AE and AE9a production, but skew towards AE9a at higher expression levels, suggesting a unique role of HNRNPL and RBM33 in AE9a expression.

3D structure prediction of DHX15–HNRNPL–RBM33 complex

Correlation analyses indicate a multifaceted regulatory process of AE9a formation. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) and University of Alabama at Birmingham (UALCAN) showed that higher expression of HNRNPL and RBM33 also predicts poor prognosis in AML (Figure S5A–D). In addition, HNRNPL and RBM33 KD lead to AE9a and AE downregulation (Figure 5C,C'), while AE9a/AE ratio decreased slightly under RBM33 KD but not significantly altered under HNRNPL KD (Figure S5E,F). To note, DHX15 depletion causes downregulation of HNRNPL (Figure S5G).

To further reveal the potential relationships among DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33, we examined their expression patterns across our 69 available clinical samples (Figure 6A), the TCGA AML cohort (Figure S6A) and 1517 DepMap (a dependency map portal) oncology cell lines (Figure S6B). In all three datasets, the three genes showed consistently positive correlations.

DHX15 (Alphafold ID: AF‐O43143), RBM33 (Alphafold ID: AF‐Q96EV2) and HNRNPL (Alphafold ID: AF‐P14866) protein structures were downloaded from Alphafold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). In parallel, protein–interaction predictions from UCSC and ColabFold 21 suggested a potential spatial association among DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 proteins (Figure 6B). Prior studies indicate that G‐patch motifs can bind and activate DEAH (Aspartate‐Glutamate‐Alanine‐Histidine amino acid sequence motif) helicases. 26 Our structural predictions showed that the G‐patch of RBM33, an unstructured sequence with a short N‐terminal α‐helix, stretches across the RecA2 (RecA‐like domain 2) domain of DHX15 (Figure 6C). Additionally, the RNA recognition motif (RRM) domain of HNRNPL and RBM33 was predicted to localize near the Ratchet and OB (Oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide‐binding fold) domain of DHX15 (Figure 6D). While requiring experimental validation, these models provide a potential structural framework for understanding the observed associations among DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 and may guide future mechanistic studies.

DISCUSSION

AE‐positive AML accounts for ~15% of all AML cases and represents a prevalent chromosomal abnormality. 27 Previous genomic analyses implicated DHX15 in alternative splicing dysregulation in AE+ AML. Our work further defines the expression patterns and functional relationships of DHX15, AE and AE9a in AE+ AML, uncovering an unrecognized role for DHX15 in generating oncogenic AE9a splice variants.

Across clinical samples and cell lines, higher DHX15 expression predicts poor prognosis in AE+ AML. Clinically, DHX15 levels positively correlate with both AE and AE9a transcripts. In vitro, DHX15‐KD reduces AE and AE9a expression, whereas DHX15 restoration rescues AE9a mRNA levels. In vivo, DHX15‐KD inhibits tumour growth and prolongs survival in leukaemia‐bearing mice. These findings support a role for DHX15 in regulating AE9a expression and associated cellular phenotypes. However, whether enforced AE9a expression can rescue the phenotypic defects induced by DHX15 depletion remains to be determined. Therefore, additional studies are required to establish a direct causal relationship between DHX15‐mediated regulation and AE9a‐dependent leukemogenesis.

DHX15 is traditionally acknowledged for spliceosome disassembly involvement. 28 Recent studies expanded its functions to early spliceosome assembly and splicing quality control. 29 Loss of DHX15 disrupts RNA splicing, including exon skipping and intron retention. 30 Given its positive correlation with AE9a, we investigated its contribution to AE9a formation. RIP assay showed DHX15 associated with AE and AE9a transcripts, although RNA pull‐down precipitation with MS detection did not consistently detect DHX15, suggesting that the interaction may be indirect, transient or context‐dependent. Notably, MS identified HNRNPL and RBM33 as proteins preferentially enriched in AE9a‐containing transcripts and both correlated positively with AE9a in AE+ AML samples, supporting their potential involvement in AE9a abundance.

Consistent with the MS findings, HNRNPL, RBM33, SRSF4 and SF3A2—splicing regulators implicated in alternative splicing and AML progression 31 , 32 , 33 —were uniquely associated with AE9a transcript. Among them, HNRNPL and RBM33 showed strong positive correlations with AE9a and variable associations with AE, suggesting a possible link to AE9a‐related splicing patterns, although their functional contributions remain undefined.

HNRNPL contains four RRM domains 34 and mediates RNA binding and protein interaction. 35 RBM33, comprising a G‐patch and an RRM domain, has been reported to bind ALKBH5 and regulate RNA m6A demethylation. 36 Despite these features, their biological functions remain incompletely elucidated. Our data reveal DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 exhibit AE9a‐binding activity and show strong expression correlations with AE9a, raising the possibility that they participate in a shared RNA‐processing network influencing AE9a expression. Structural modelling using ColabFold predicted spatial proximity among DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33, with interfaces involving the RBM33 G‐patch and DHX15 AecA2 domain and the RRM domains of RBM33 and HNRNPL near the DHX15 Ratchet and OB domain. A limitation of the current study is the lack of direct biochemical validation of the proposed DHX15–HNRNPL–RBM33 complex in AE+ AML cells. Our co‐IP (co‐immunoprecipitation) attempts were hindered by low endogenous RBM33 expression in Kasumi‐1 cells and technical challenges in expressing this large protein. Future work using optimized tagging strategies and proteomic approaches will be required to confirm the proposed interaction network.

Beyond RNA splicing, DHX15 emerges pivotal in cancer development and diverse signalling pathways, reported essential in intestinal, 37 hepatoma, 38 colorectal cancer 39 and leukaemia 30 and implicated in Wnt (Wingless‐related integration site) 37 and NF‐kB (nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells) pathways. 19 Notably, we pioneeringly reveal DHX15 significantly influences the AE+ AML cell cycle regulation in vitro. DHX15‐KD induced S/G2 phase arrest and downregulated key cell cycle proteins. Previous studies show AE regulates cell cycle through p53 40 and p21 41 activation, facilitating G1/S transition. 42 While DHX15 suppression upregulates p21 and p‐p53 with decreased cyclin A expression, causing S/G2 phase arrest in this study, suggesting DHX15 may regulate cell cycle independently of AE.

Overall, while our GSEA, expression analyses and RNA‐binding studies support an association between DHX15, HNRNPL, RBM33 and altered AE9a splicing patterns in AE+ AML, they do not exclude alternative explanations, including transcriptional co‐regulation, shared upstream regulatory programmes or indirect downstream effects. Therefore, further mechanistic studies will be required to definitively establish direct molecular interactions and causal splicing regulation.

In conclusion, this study provides insights into the association between DHX15 and AE9a splicing patterns and highlights a potential RNA‐processing network involving DHX15, HNRNPL and RBM33 in AE+ AML. Given the clinical relevance of AE9a in AML progression and prognosis, DHX15‐associated pathways may represent a potential area for future therapeutic exploration, pending further mechanistic validation.
