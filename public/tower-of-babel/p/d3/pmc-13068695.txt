CardiaTics: An explainable AI integrated heart disease diagnosis model with feature engineering and stacked ensemble approach
Authors: Partho Ghose, Hasan Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart disease is a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality. Accurate and prompt diagnoses are crucial for its effective prevention and management. Integrating multiple machine learning algorithms, this research introduces a stacked ensemble machine learning model, called CardiaTics (stands for Cardiac DiagnosTics), toward improving heart disease detection. We detect outliers and remove them as a first-step to ensure data quality and maintain integrity. Ten distinct machine learning algorithms are then individually applied, culminating in the creation of a stacked ensemble model. We use feature engineering to refine the model further applying three well-known techniques –Pearson correlation, Chi-Square Test (Chi-2), and Recursive Feature Elimination. The implementation of these techniques on the benchmark dataset results in an optimized feature set. Experimental results show that CardiaTics delivers 89.3% accuracy on raw data, and significantly improves its accuracy after feature selection to 93.3%, outperforming the individual classifiers. However, can human professionals rely on algorithms for prediction when the underlying process is not fully understood? To address concerns regarding interpretability, trust, and transparency in black-box predictions, we propose utilizing SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Explain Like I’m 5 (ELI5) in the second phase to elucidate feature importance in our model. The SHAP summary plots of CardiaTics reveal that the positive and negative contributors to heart disease are comparable, thereby enhancing the model’s interpretability and reliability and helping refine the decision-making process.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) pose a significant global health threat. American Heart Association reports it claims about 17.9 million lives globally each year [1]. In the United States alone, a stroke-related fatalities occurs every three minutes and is considered as one of the leading causes of death [2, 3]. High rates of stroke-related fatalities are also observed in regions such as Central, Southeast, and East Asia, Oceania, and sub-Saharan Africa [4]. Even in the cases of non-fatal strokes, serious heart complication often ensue post stroke requiring significant care regimes [5].

Traditional diagnostics of heart diseases rely on archaic protocols involving patient medical history, physical examinations, and presenting symptoms [6] that are plagued with potential inaccuracies, delays, and increased costs due to human errors [7]. Recent emergence of computational methods have shown promise in addressing these shortcomings potentially reducing moralities and healthcare costs. These computational models are also helping in early detection and in improving patient outcomes [8]. In particular, heart disease prediction using machine learning (ML) has emerged as a leading area of research and investigation [9]. Unlike conventional methods, comprehensive ML based methods leverage a multitude of health variables to predict patients’ likelihood of having CVD [7].

Among these ML approaches, an approach called ensembling [10] has gained prominence in the area of heart disease diagnoses and predictions. Recently, several ensembling adaptations were also shown to have been instrumental in improved and effective prediction toward CVD identification [11, 12]. This technique combine multiple models for enhanced performance. In this paper, our goal is to propose a novel adaptation of the ensembling model combined with effective feature selection techniques for cardiac disease prediction and diagnosis [11].

Though ML based diagnoses approaches are powerful and promising, they also have wide ranging significant limitations. Among them, feature selection and engineering have attracted significant attention from the research community due to the roles they play in the prediction models, ensembing in particular.

In the context of heart disease prediction [13, 14], various studies have examined the impact of specific features, including ST depression, ST slope, maximum heart rate achieved, and cholesterol levels [15, 16]. These investigations provide valuable insights into the significance of these parameters for cardiovascular health assessments.

Feature engineering is crucial for enhancing the performance of machine learning models, particularly when dealing with complex medical datasets. Previous research [17, 18] has highlighted the importance of selecting and transforming relevant features to achieve accurate disease prediction. Techniques such as dimensionality reduction [19, 20], feature scaling, and identification of critical biomarkers [21–23] significantly contribute to the optimization of diagnostic models.

Like most other methods, feature selection play a critical role in refining model inputs in ensembling. Existing methods [8, 24] struggle to identify and utilize the most relevant features for heart disease prediction. For instances, Pearson correlation and Chi-Square Test may overlook subtle interactions and fail to adapt to the dynamic nature of cardiovascular dynamics [8]. Therefore a compensatory assembly of multiple feature selection technique to help select the most significant features that potentially contribute to accurate predictions of heart diseases deserves due attention.

In this paper, we employ three feature selection strategies such as Pearson correlation, Chi-Square Test (Chi-2), and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE)) to select the most effective features so that the model can focus only on the relevant features to learn. We apply three classifiers – LR, RF and Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM) to observe the feature contribution of these methods. Later, we combine those features which are used by all six methods for prediction and contribute with high scores. Finally, we incorporate model interpretability tools, SHapley Additive exPlanations, more commonly known as SHAP values, to support explainability toward improving our understanding of the intricacies of heart disease dynamics. This strategic approach seeks to overcome the challenges associated with the “black-box" nature of some ML models and aims to provide more reliable and interpretable predictions for improved clinical decision-making. The proposed CVD prediction system CardiaTics makes several interesting contributions as summarized below. Related research

Though ML-based diagnostic approaches are powerful and promising, they also have wide-ranging significant limitations. Among them, feature selection and engineering have attracted significant attention from the research community due to the roles they play in the prediction models, ensembling in particular.

Stacked ensemble learning models have emerged as a powerful medical diagnosis technique because they enhance predictive performance using diverse algorithms. Stacking [25] combines strengths from multiple base models, compensating for individual weaknesses and improving overall accuracy. Previous health research [24, 26], including heart disease diagnosis, attests to the effectiveness of ensemble learning and showcases enhanced sensitivity and specificity. In the heart disease prediction spectrum, studies [12, 27] have individually examined various base classifiers such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Gradient Boosting Machines (GDM) [8, 9]. They highlight the pivotal role of selecting and combining base models toward significantly influencing the stacked ensemble’s predictive capabilities [28].

One of the voting ensemble methods [29] incorporates multiple machine learning techniques, yielding an accuracy of 90%, albeit with a poor overall performance. One of the hybrid intelligent machine-learning-based predictive approaches for heart disease detection [10], on the other hand, utilized data from Cleveland Clinic’s heart disease cases and integrated seven well-known classifiers with three feature selection algorithms to achieve 89% accuracy using Logistic Regression (LR). Another intelligent healthcare monitoring system for heart disease prediction used a deep learning-based ensemble method to achieve promising results [30].

The literature highlights the critical importance of selecting and combining base models to enhance the predictive capabilities of stacked ensemble models [28]. A thorough understanding of the strengths and limitations of individual classifiers is essential for building a robust stacked ensemble.

Abdar et al. [31] made a significant contribution by introducing a novel machine learning technique for detecting coronary artery disease. They used an N2Genetic optimizer, derived from Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), for data processing and feature selection, achieving an accuracy of 93.08% with the N2Genetic-nuSVM approach. This method, however, relied solely on GA and PSO techniques, which are known to be time-consuming due to the elimination of redundant features.

Rahma et al. [29] proposed a voting ensemble method that incorporated multiple machine learning techniques, achieving an accuracy of 90%. However, their study lacked any discussion of feature engineering techniques, and the overall performance was relatively poor.

Shah et al. [10] introduced a hybrid intelligent machine-learning-based predictive approach for heart disease detection. Using data from Cleveland heart disease cases, their system integrated seven well-known classifiers with three feature selection algorithms, achieving the highest accuracy of 89% with logistic regression.

Ali et al. [30] presented an intelligent healthcare monitoring system for heart disease prediction using a deep learning-based ensemble method, achieving promising results. Despite these advances, there is still a need for improved performance evaluation of these diagnostic models. Many existing models offer only limited performance measurements and do not adequately address the contribution of each feature to the detection of heart disease.

In line with these efforts, several recent studies also have introduced advanced ensemble-based frameworks for heart disease prediction. MU Rehman et al. [32] proposed a hybrid machine learning approach combining Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) with an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to enhance the prediction of coronary heart disease. Their framework incorporated mutual information (MI) for feature selection and applied the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to handle class imbalance in the dataset. They achieved a prediction accuracy of 97% using the PSO-ANN model, outperforming conventional classifiers like Logistic Regression and Random Forest, which reached up to 95.8% accuracy on the NHANES dataset. However, the approach had notable limitations, including high computational cost due to the iterative nature of PSO and a lack of external validation, which raises concerns about the model’s generalizability to unseen clinical datasets. Similarly, M Darshan Teja and G Mokesh Rayalu [33] presented a hybrid voting ensemble combining LR, RF, and MLP, which achieved 93.1% accuracy, although their manual hyperparameter tuning and lack of external validation limited scalability. M.T. Ashfaq [34]introduced a deep learning model, FCW-Net, for heart disease prediction, combining ProWSyn oversampling to address class imbalance and PCA for dimensionality reduction. The model achieved strong results with an AUC-ROC of 0.9622, accuracy of 92.37%, and recall of 97.21%. To enhance transparency, SHAP analysis was used to interpret feature contributions. However, the model’s complexity increases computational cost, and despite SHAP, it remains partially black-box, limiting full clinical interpretability. In [35], the authors proposed a stacked ensemble model for heart disease detection, incorporating outlier removal and feature selection to enhance performance. The model achieved 93.3% accuracy. While SHAP was used for global interpretability, the study lacked local explainability, limiting insights into individual predictions.

Model building

We now focus on the process of model construction, beginning with the cross-validation and baseline model selection for the proposed framework. Through systematic evaluation and comparison, we identify the most effective models to serve as the foundation for further refinement. Finally, we introduce the proposed stacked ensemble model that combines the strengths of each of its component models for an improved predictive accuracy and robustness compared individual models alone.

Proposed model

Stacking, also known as super learning, represents a category of algorithms that involves training a second-level "meta learner" to optimize the combination of diverse base learners [38]. Unlike bagging and boosting, stacking aims to combine robust and varied sets of learners. We selected stacking rather than bagging/boosting because (i) we combine heterogeneous learners whose errors are complementary, and (ii) a meta-learner can learn nonlinear interactions among base predictions. Bagging is most effective when aggregating many instances of the same high-variance learner, while boosting focuses on sequential error correction; both are valuable baselines, but stacking is better suited to fusing diverse model families.Fig. 3Procedure of proposed ensemble learning

Procedure of proposed ensemble learning

Based on the cross-validation accuracy performance of different baseline models, we select the top ten performing models for the base level (level 0) of the stacked ensemble. This ensemble is designed to outperform individual ML models. In our study, we designate Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) as our super learner or generalizer. The workflow of the 10-fold stacking model is presented in Fig. 3. The process proceeds as follows.

The training set is divided into ten initial segments, nine of which serve as training data for individual classifiers, and any part of each fold acts as the test data. The fundamental classifiers include RF, MLP, K-Nenarest Neighbors (KNN), Extra Tree Classifier (ETC), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Singular Value Decomposition (SGD), Adaboost Classifier (ABC), Classification And Regression Tree (CART), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). Each classifier is trained and evaluated over the randomly selected segment in each of the ten folds. Consequently, each classifier generates ten sets of test results, and their union constitutes the entire training set.

To identify the optimal ensemble configuration, we employ a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) as the meta-learner to integrate the predictions of the base classifiers. MLP was chosen due to its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships among the outputs of heterogeneous base learners, which enhances its capacity to learn intricate decision boundaries at the meta-level [39]. Compared to linear models such as logistic regression or simpler learners like decision trees, MLP offers superior representational power, enabling it to exploit subtle inter-model dependencies. Furthermore, its scalability and robustness to overfitting, when combined with appropriate regularization and early stopping, make it a suitable choice for aggregating diverse model outputs in high-dimensional decision spaces [39].

This ensemble technique takes advantage of the collective wisdom of multiple base learners, each trained using k-fold (in our case \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=10$$\end{document}) cross-validation. In this framework, the ensemble’s prediction, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{f}_{CardiaTics}(x)$$\end{document}, is computed by combining the predictions of individual base learners through a weighted averaging scheme as expressed in Eq. 7.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{f}_{CardiaTics}(x) = MLP\left( \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{1}{k} \sum _{j=1}^{k} w_{i,j} \times \hat{y}_{i,j} \right) \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, N represents the number of base learners, k signifies the number of folds in cross-validation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{i,j}$$\end{document} denotes the prediction of the i-th base learner on the j-th fold. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{ij}$$\end{document} denotes the weight of the connection between neuron i in the input layer and neuron j in the output layer. The MLP meta learner synthesizes these weighted predictions to yield the final ensemble output. By leveraging the strengths of diverse base learners and incorporating the MLP’s capacity for nonlinear modeling, this stack ensemble methodology excels at tackling complex predictive tasks, offering superior generalization performance and robustness in real-world scenarios.

XAI

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a vital area of research aimed at making complex machine-learning models more transparent, interpretable, and trustworthy. The core objective of XAI is to bridge the gap between highly accurate but opaque "black-box" models and the growing demand for accountability, especially in critical domains, such as healthcare. By providing human-understandable insights into how models arrive at their predictions, XAI enables domain experts and stakeholders to make informed decisions, validate model behavior, and build trust in AI-driven systems.

The importance of explainability has grown considerably in recent years, not only from a scientific and ethical standpoint but also due to evolving legal and regulatory frameworks. Unlike a decade ago, when automated algorithms were often deployed without questioning their internal decision logic, current applications increasingly require transparency by design. This shift is particularly evident in the European Union, where new regulations mandate the traceability and interpretability of AI systems, especially those used in high-stakes areas, such as medicine, finance, and law. These regulatory developments reflect a broader global trend toward the responsible and auditable deployment of AI technologies.

The need for explainable AI is even more pronounced in healthcare. Although AI holds immense potential to enhance diagnostics, treatment planning, and patient outcomes, its adoption in clinical practice remains limited [40]. Although the lack of labeled medical data is often cited as a barrier, a recent work by Pagallo et al. highlights that regulatory uncertainty and legal constraints are equally significant factors hindering the integration of AI into healthcare workflows. Without clear frameworks that balance innovation with ethical oversight, the healthcare sector risks underutilizing AI and incurring opportunity costs in terms of efficiency, accuracy, and patient-care outcomes [41].

In response to these challenges, a wide array of XAI techniques have been developed, making it one of the most dynamic and rapidly evolving in AI research. These techniques are generally categorized into global and local [42]. Global methods aim to explain the overall logic and behavior of a model, helping users understand how different input features influence predictions. By contrast, local methods focus on individual predictions and offer explanations tailored to specific instances. Both approaches are essential to ensure transparency and trust, particularly when AI systems are used in sensitive and regulated environments.

However, the proliferation of XAI methods presents a new challenge for practitioners: how to choose the most suitable technique for their specific use case. As XAI matures, there is growing recognition of the gap between theoretical advancements and real-world implementation. To address this, Retzlaff et al. [43] proposed a comprehensive decision-making framework to guide data scientists and engineers in selecting between ante-hoc (inherently interpretable) and post hoc (retrospectively interpretable) approaches. Their work provides actionable design guidelines that align the XAI method selection with application requirements, model complexity, and end-user needs [43]. This framework is particularly valuable for practitioners working in domains such as healthcare, where explainability is not only desirable, but also often mandated by policy [44].

Above all, as AI continues to permeate decision-critical sectors, explainability is no longer optional; it is a prerequisite. Understanding both the technical spectrum of XAI methods and the broader legal and ethical contexts is essential for deploying AI responsibly and effectively.

Various interpretability techniques are available, each with distinct advantages and limitations based on the type of model for which they are designed. Local interpretable model-agnostic explanation (LIME) is a widely used method that generates explanations for individual predictions by approximating a model’s decision boundary with a simpler, interpretable surrogate model. Although effective, LIME can lead to unstable explanations owing to its reliance on local approximations [45]. This can become problematic in clinical environments, where consistent and repeatable results are essential for making informed decisions. Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) is another popular method, particularly in image-based applications, such as medical imaging. It visualizes the important regions in an image that contribute to a model’s decision. However, Grad-CAM is limited to convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which makes it unsuitable for models beyond CNNs, such as those used in medical AI applications that combine different neural architectures [45]. Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) works by propagating relevance scores through a network, offering insights into the features that influence model predictions. While it has been effective in certain medical imaging tasks, LRP is also architecture-dependent, meaning that it is not as flexible across diverse model types.

In contrast, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and ELI5 offer a more versatile and robust approach, especially for the various models we work with in medical AI. SHAP provides model-agnostic explanations by fairly distributing feature importance and ensuring consistency and transparency across both local and global interpretability [45]. This is crucial for healthcare applications, where understanding the contribution of each feature is important not only for accuracy but also for patient safety and clinical decision-making. ELI5, a complement to SHAP, provides a simplified and intuitive interface for visualizing feature contributions, making it accessible to clinicians and nontechnical users. Together, these two methods overcome the limitations of LIME, Grad-CAM, and LRP, as they are adaptable to different model types and provide a higher level of consistency and transparency. This makes SHAP and ELI5 particularly suitable for our study, where explanations that are reliable, interpretable, and comprehensible in a medical context are required. These methods perfectly align with the needs of clinical AI applications, where transparency and trust in the system are paramount for practical implementation and widespread adoption [45].

Local interpretability using SHAP

While global explainability underscores the intricate details of AI systems, local explainability, on the other hand, reveals how predictions alter with changes in specific features. This fosters trust in individual predictions and the system as a whole. Moreover, features that are significant on a global scale might not carry the same weight locally and vice versa. For local interpretation, SHAP is a commonly used tool for local interpretation, offering a force plot that visually represents the impact of each feature on the prediction model for an individual.

In Figure 9, local feature importance is illustrated using SHAP values, offering insights into individual predictions. The visualization highlights higher feature values in red (heart disease) and lower values in blue (no disease), with varying magnitudes. In this figure, a local explanation is provided for predictions generated by a black box model. The visualization associates features such as major vessel, maximum heart rate achieved, and st_depression with the positive class, whereas thalassemia defect is correlated with the negative class. These features interact with each other, ultimately determining the highlighted prediction value for the specific observation.

Partial Dependence Plot (PDP): A PDP illustrates how changes in a single feature influence the model’s predicted outcome while averaging out the effects of all other features, thereby revealing the direction and strength of that feature’s contribution to the prediction.Fig. 11SHAP partial dependence plot for st_slope_upsloping, showing a negative contribution to heart disease risk when the feature is present.Fig. 12SHAP partial dependence plot for st_slope_flat, indicating a positive contribution to heart disease risk when the feature is present.

SHAP partial dependence plot for st_slope_upsloping, showing a negative contribution to heart disease risk when the feature is present.

SHAP partial dependence plot for st_slope_flat, indicating a positive contribution to heart disease risk when the feature is present.

Figure 11 and Figure 12 present SHAP-based partial dependence plots for ST-segment slope features, illustrating their marginal effects on heart disease risk prediction. The PDP shows (Figure 11) a negative contribution to the predicted heart disease risk when the upsloping ST segment is present. As the feature transitions from absence (0) to presence (1), the SHAP value decreases, indicating that an upsloping ST slope reduces the model’s predicted risk. This behavior is clinically consistent, as an upsloping ST segment is generally associated with a lower likelihood of ischemic abnormalities. In contrast, the PDP for flat ST slope exhibits (Figure 12) a positive contribution to the predicted risk. Moving from 0 to 1 result in an increase in SHAP values, suggesting that the presence of a flat ST segment elevates the model’s predicted probability of heart disease. This aligns with clinical understanding, where a flat ST segment is often considered a pathological indicator.

SHAP provides individualized and model-agnostic feature attributions, enabling clinicians to understand not only which features influence a model’s predictions but also how they do so for each patient. This level of transparency supports clinical decision-making by identifying key risk factors, uncovering unexpected model behavior, and enhancing trust in AI-assisted diagnostics. Moreover, SHAP visualizations can guide targeted interventions by highlighting the most influential variables specific to a patient’s profile.

Limitations and future plans

Although CardiaTics demonstrates strong predictive performance and interpretability on the benchmark dataset, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, an experimental evaluation was conducted on a UCI-based heart disease dataset comprising a limited number of samples and features. Although this dataset is widely used for benchmarking and allows for fair comparison with existing studies, it may not fully capture the variability present in real-world clinical environments. Consequently, the generalizability of the proposed framework to external institutional datasets requires further validation. Second, CardiaTics employs a stacked ensemble architecture that integrates multiple heterogeneous base and meta learners. Although this design improves predictive robustness and performance, it introduces additional computational overhead compared to single-model approaches. However, this trade-off is considered acceptable given the clinical context, where reliability and interpretability are often prioritized over marginal increases in computational costs.

In terms of future work, the framework can be extended by evaluating CardiaTics using external and multi-institutional datasets to assess its robustness across diverse populations and healthcare settings. Model calibration, retraining, and fine-tuning strategies may be explored to address potential domain shifts arising from differences in data acquisition protocols or patient demographics. Furthermore, future studies may investigate domain adaptation techniques and incremental learning to support broader deployment scenarios. We also plan to develop a cloud-based CardiaTics application based on the proposed framework. This platform will enable the collection of diverse real-world clinical data from practitioners, facilitating continuous model refinement and validation. By incorporating newly acquired data, the system aims to improve predictive performance, robustness, and generalizability, ultimately supporting more accurate heart disease assessments and improving patient outcomes.

Implementation challenges and advantages

The CardiaTics image analysis system is hosted on the University of Idaho’s high-performance computing infrastructure at http://dblab.nkn.uidaho.edu/cardiatics/ and operates in full compliance with HIPAA regulations. All images stored in the system are stripped of patient identifiers and are submitted voluntarily for research purposes only. Each submission undergoes a manual compliance review and is treated as a research contribution. Image ownership remains with the depositor, and the data is not publicly accessible or shared with other users. Internally, images are assigned unique identifiers specific to CardiaTics that are not linked to patient information, ensuring privacy. These images are used exclusively to improve the CardiaTics system and are never employed for commercial purposes.

CardiaTics is also evaluating a HIPAA-compliant image sharing protocol currently being tested as part of the PerHL (Personal Health Library) initiative [47, 48]. This protocol is designed to facilitate secure collaboration between clinicians and researchers, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and promoting knowledge sharing. Importantly, CardiaTics is not a repository for medical records and cannot be connected to health information systems. Its focus is strictly on cardiological image analysis using advanced machine learning models not available through existing platforms. The system emphasizes ethical sharing of analytical tools – such as ML models – rather than data or images themselves.

Conclusion

In this paper, introduced a stacked machine learning ensemble classifier, called CardiaTics, that used several machine learning techniques to provide a more precise and reliable model for detecting the presence of a heart disease. We choose the top 10 models for level 0, or base level, of the stacked ensemble based on how well each baseline model performed in cross-validation accuracy. Then we fed the test data from each of the core classifiers into an MLP algorithm to select the optimal ensemble of the group of classifiers. We also used Pearson correlation, Chi-2, and RFE techniques for feature selection using feature engineering. The stacked classifier model outperformed each individual classifier, with an accuracy of 89.3% on the raw data and 93.3% after feature selection. The results underscore the potential of this stacked model as a valuable tool for healthcare practitioners in improving their diagnostic capabilities. Furthermore, the interpretability of the model’s decision-making process provides valuable insights, aiding clinicians an understanding of the factors contributing to each prediction.

As we move forward, it is important to continue refining and expanding the model by incorporating additional data sources to enrich medical knowledge, while exploring domain adaptation strategies to address potential distribution shifts in future work. We have plans to develop a CardiaTics cloud application based on this approach so that more novel data could be collected from practitioners so that the model can be improved, and play a role in understanding heart disease through improved predictions for more impactful patient outcomes.
