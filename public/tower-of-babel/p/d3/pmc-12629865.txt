Advances in radiopharmaceuticals for cancer radiotheranostics: CCK2R targeting as a paradigm for translational innovation
Authors: Jing Li, Xuejun Wen, Rebeka Rita Reszegi, Hemavarshini Kamalrhaj, Wolfgang J Parak, Xiaoyuan Chen, Jingjing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiopharmaceuticals are reshaping the landscape of cancer therapy, offering a unique theranostic advantage that is becoming increasingly central to precision medicine. By labeling the same molecular scaffold with different radionuclides, these agents enable seamless integration of diagnostic imaging and targeted therapy. Clinical breakthroughs with somatostatin receptor subtype 2 (SSTR2)‐ and prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA)‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals have significantly enhanced both tumor visualization and therapeutic efficacy, establishing new benchmarks in oncology. Ongoing research is exploring novel molecular targets such as cholecystokinin‐2 receptor (CCK2R), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), and C‐X‐C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4). In parallel, there is growing interest in utilizing alternative radionuclides, including alpha‐particle emitters and Auger electron emitters, beyond the commonly used beta‐emitters, to improve therapeutic outcomes. Simultaneously, advances in ligand and linker design are being leveraged to optimize in vivo pharmacokinetics and tissue distribution. Among the emerging targets, CCK2R has attracted notable attention due to its overexpression in multiple malignancies. Research efforts have focused on improving ligand stability, receptor‐binding affinity, and tumor retention, while also exploring strategies to enhance CCK2R expression on cancer cells. This review offers a comprehensive overview of the current landscape in cancer radiotheranostics, exploring the role of CCK2R in cancer biology and summarizing the latest advancements in the development of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals. Using these advancements as a case study, we systematically examine key aspects of next‐generation radiopharmaceutical design, from target selection and ligand engineering to pharmacokinetic optimization and clinical translation, providing a multidimensional framework for future innovation in cancer radiotheranostics.Keywords: cancer theranostics, CCK2R, molecular targets, radiopharmaceuticals, radiotheranostics

BACKGROUND

Radiopharmaceuticals are rapidly reshaping the oncology landscape, driven by both clinical success and growing scientific interest, and are emerging as an increasingly integral component of cancer care [1, 2, 3, 4]. The approvals of [177Lu]Lu‐DOTATATE (Lutathera®) for somatostatin receptor‐expressing neuroendocrine tumors [5] and [177Lu]Lu‐PSMA‐617 (PluvictoTM) for prostate cancer [6] represent a major step forward in the field. Both agents demonstrated not only significant therapeutic efficacy but also favorable safety profiles, drawing attention from clinicians and researchers. Their diagnostic imaging counterparts, [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐TATE (Netspot) and [68Ga]Ga‐PSMA‐11 (Locametz) [7, 8, 9, 10], assist in guiding treatment by supporting patient selection and monitoring. These diagnostic‐therapeutic pairs reflect the principle of theranostics by using the same molecular framework for both imaging and treatment. This enables a seamless transition from identifying tumors to delivering targeted treatment. Their strategy relies mostly on targeting receptors that are overexpressed on cancer cells, ensuring selective uptake into malignant tissue. Once internalized, the radiopharmaceutical releases its radioactive payload, allowing direct irradiation. In contrast to external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), which delivers high‐dose‐rate radiation from outside the body, the internal mechanism of radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) leverages heterogeneous targeting and a low absorbed dose rate. This tends to reduce the biologically effective dose to surrounding healthy tissues and limit systemic toxicity, compared to EBRT. Additionally, by integrating positron emission tomography (PET) or single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging modalities, radiopharmaceuticals allow non‐invasive, whole‐body assessment of tumor burden. This “see what you treat and treat what you see” concept offers a powerful tool in personalized cancer care [11].

The field continues to expand, with ongoing research focused on finding new cancer targets, improving radionuclide technology, and enhancing synthetic chemistry. Among the most promising are G‐protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), which are highly accessible on the cell surface and readily internalized. In addition to the clinically validated somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) [8], other GPCRs such as gastrin‐releasing peptide receptor (GRPR) [12, 13], C‐X‐C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) [14, 15], neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1) [16], and cholecystokinin‐2 receptor (CCK2R) [17] are gaining attention for their potential in targeted imaging and therapy. Additionally, targets such as prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) [7], fibroblast activation protein (FAP) [18, 19, 20], urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) [21], Nectin‐4 [22], and σ‐receptors [23] and integrins [24] demonstrate similar potential due to their selective overexpression in diverse malignancies. This specificity improves targeting, minimizing off‐target effects. FAP, highly overexpressed in cancer‐associated fibroblasts, has emerged as a promising pan‐cancer target for both diagnosis and therapy [25]. [68Ga]Ga‐FAPI‐46 [26], [68Ga]Ga/[177Lu]Lu‐FAP‐2286 [27], [177Lu]Lu‐LNC1004 [28, 29] as the representative FAP targeted radioligands had been developed and are currently undergoing clinical evaluation. In prostate cancer, beyond the widely explored PSMA target, the GRPR‐targeted radioligand [68Ga]Ga/[177Lu]Lu‐NeoB has entered phase III clinical trials [30]. CXCR4‐targeted radiotheranostics such as [68Ga]Ga‐pentixafor [31] (phase III) and [177Lu]Lu‐pentixather [32] (phase I/II) are being investigated for imaging and treating hematological malignancies, particularly multiple myeloma. [177Lu]Lu‐3BP‐227, targeting neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1), is under phase I/II clinical evaluation for metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma [33]. For the CCK2R, radioligands including [68Ga]Ga‐MGS5 [34, 35] and [68Ga]Ga‐CCK‐66 [36] have been assessed in clinical trials for imaging medullary thyroid carcinoma. Their therapeutic counterparts, [177Lu]Lu‐CCK‐66 [36] and [177Lu]Lu‐MGS5 [37], have also been developed for targeted radionuclide therapy. Emerging molecular targets, including human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2) [38], trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (Trop2) [39], CLDN18.2 [40], Delta‐like ligand 3 (DLL3) [41], and glypican‐3 (GPC3) [42], are also being actively explored due to their high tumor specificity and potential to expand the therapeutic landscape in precision oncology.

Radiotheranostics relies on radionuclides that emit either imaging signals (γ for SPECT or β+ for PET) or therapeutic radiation (β−, α‐particles, or Auger electrons). For diagnostic imaging, commonly used radionuclides include 18F, 68Ga, and 99mTc. Several radiotracers based on these isotopes, such as [18F]F‐FDG [43], [18F]F‐DCFPyL [44], [68Ga]Ga‐PSMA‐11 [45], [99mTc]Tc‐tetrofosmin [46], have received FDA approval for routine clinical use. Among particles for therapeutic, β‐emitters like yttrium‐90 (90Y), lutetium‐177 (177Lu), iodine‐131 (131I) are most commonly used due to their clinical validation and reliable production. Representative approved radiotherapeutics include [90Y]Y‐resin microsphere [47], [177Lu]Lu‐PSMA‐617 [48], [177Lu]Lu‐DOTATATE [49], [131I]NaI [50], each demonstrating substantial clinical utility. Alpha emitter such as actinium‐225 (225Ac), astatine‐211 (211At), bismuth‐213 (213Bi), and lead‐212 (212Pb) deliver high‐energy radiation over short distances to damage tumor tissues. This property makes them particularly effective for eradicating isolated tumor cells and small‐volume lesions, including micrometastases. Notably, clinical studies have also demonstrated that alpha emitters can be effective in treating larger tumors (e.g., >1 cm), likely through mechanisms beyond direct irradiation [51, 52]. Alpha particle irradiation can induce non‐targeted effects, such as bystander [53, 54, 55] and abscopal effects [56, 57], wherein non‐irradiated neighboring cells exhibit biological damage through intercellular communication and immune activation. These effects may significantly enhance the therapeutic outcome and extend the impact of alpha therapy beyond its short physical range.

Radioligands including [225Ac]Ac‐PSMA‐617 [58], [225Ac]Ac‐LNC1011 [59], [212Pb]Pb‐DOTAM‐GRPR1 [60] have demonstrated potent therapeutic efficacy in preclinical and early clinical settings. Nevertheless, challenges related to their production, supply chain logistics, and imaging compatibility currently limit their widespread clinical implementation [61, 62, 63]. Another promising approach involves radionuclides that emit Auger electrons. However, achieving a therapeutic effect mediated by Auger electrons requires the delivery of high intracellular activity and precise subcellular localization, ideally to the nucleus. While 161Tb emits both beta particles and Auger electrons, its therapeutic advantage over 177Lu is primarily attributed to additional conversion electrons rather than to Auger emission alone [13, 64]. Similarly, 111In emits only ∼7‐8 Auger electrons per decay, which may be insufficient for effective therapy, and its use has been limited by suboptimal radiobiological efficacy. These limitations may explain why Auger‐based therapies have not yet translated into widespread clinical success. Nevertheless, the selection of suboptimal Auger‐emitting radionuclides has likely contributed to the lack of effective Auger‐based treatments currently available in clinical practice. However, several Auger emitters with more favorable decay characteristics are under active investigation, including 197mHg, 119Sb, 103Pd, 103Ru, 165Er, 195mPt and 193mPt [65]. 201Tl also shows potential, although it poses significant chelation challenges [66]. Beyond the widely adopted 68Ga/177Lu pair, isotope combinations from the same element, including 152Tb/149Tb, 64Cu/67Cu, 203Pb/212Pb, offer unified chemistry for both imaging and therapy, expanding the versatility of radiotheranostics.

Advances in synthetic chemistry are improving radiopharmaceutical designs, enhancing binding affinity and stability to better target tumors. Techniques like ligand cyclization and multimerization enhance binding affinity and stability of the agents, enabling them to better target tumor cells and remain stable until it reaches the tumor site. Additionally, incorporating albumin‐binding moieties or lipophilic linkers also improves prolong circulation time and tumor targeting.

The urgent need for novel molecular targets and improved radiopharmaceutical designs stems from challenges such as tumor heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance. Among emerging targets, the CCK2R is particularly promising due to its high expression in certain tumors and limited presence in normal tissues—a profile that is especially relevant in malignancies such as medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [17, 67]. In this review, we highlight the development of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals as a case study to illustrate a structured approach to radiopharmaceutical innovation (Figure 1). The progression of these compounds is analyzed from two perspectives—peptide analogs and non‐peptide antagonists—tracing their progression from molecular innovations to clinical applications. The review examines the physiological and pathological mechanisms underlying CCK2R expression and explores strategies to overcome challenges, providing a cohesive framework from innovation to clinical translation. As advances in molecular biology, radionuclide engineering, and chemical innovation, radiopharmaceuticals are poised to play an increasingly central role in precision oncology, offering tailored and highly effective solutions for cancer diagnosis and therapy.

CCK2R AS A PROMISING TARGET

Gastrin and cholecystokinin are peptide hormones involved significantly in digestion. Gastrin is secreted and produced by G‐cells localized mostly in the antrum, and CCK is mainly generated by intestinal endocrine cells in the duodenum. The most abundant form of gastrin in the human body is gastrin‐17, also called gastrin‐I (GnI) or mini gastrin (MG), which contains 17 amino acid fragments [pGlu‐Gly‐Pro‐Trp‐Leu‐(Glu)5‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2]. The five consecutive glutamic acid sequences enhance the selectivity of gastrin for CCK2R. Another form of gastrin, is gastrin‐34, showcasing 34 amino acid fragments (H‐Gln‐Leu‐Gly‐Pro‐Gln‐Gly‐Pro‐Pro‐His‐Leu‐Val‐Ala‐Asp‐Pro‐Ser‐Lys‐Lys‐Gln‐Gly‐Pro‐Trp‐Leu‐Glu‐Glu‐Glu‐Glu‐Glu‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2), with identical C‐terminal fragments to GnI [68]. CCK also exists in different forms in the gut, mostly CCK‐33 can be detected, CCK‐22, CCK‐58 and CCK8 are in a lower amount present. CCK‐8 (Asp‐Tyr(SO3H)‐Met‐Gly‐Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2) and the other CCK peptides, also share the same C‐terminal end (Gly‐Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2) as gastrin‐17 and ‐34 [69].

Gastrin, secreted into the blood, directly contributes to acid production in the digestion process. Through binding to paretal cells and activating enterochromaffin‐like cells, gastrin preserves an acidic environment for effective nutrient breakdown and microbial clearance. Beyond its role in stimulating acid generation, gastrin promotes gastric motility and the growth of the gastric mucosa, thus assisting in the maintenance of gastrointestinal health. CCK is also involved in digestion and nutrient absorption, releasing digestive enzymes and indirectly bile.

The effects of gastrin and CCK are primarily activated via binding to GPCRs on the cell membrane to induce different signaling pathways. These GPCRs in this context can be categorized into two subtypes: CCK1R and CCK2R [70]. Their distinct anatomical distributions demonstrate system‐specific functional specialization. CCK1R is localized in hepatobiliary system, gastrointestinal smooth muscle layers and peripheral autonomic nervous plexuses. On the other hand, CCK2R is mostly expressed in the gastric parietal cells and the intestinal mucosal enterochromaffin‐like cells. Both receptor subtypes exhibit distinct binding preferences, with gastrin showing strong selectivity for CCK2R, while the sulfated tyrosine (Tyr‐SO3H) moiety at the C‐terminal of CCK preferentially binds to CCK1R.

Upon activation by gastrin or CCK, CCK1R and CCK2R trigger intracellular signaling pathways that regulate key biological processes. These receptors primarily couple with Gq proteins, leading to the activation of phospholipase C (PLC), the generation of diacylglycerol (DAG), and the activation of protein kinase C (PKC). Subsequently, downstream effectors such as extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK), c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK), p38 MAPK, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways are engaged, controlling cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis. Additionally, CCK2R‐specific activation can stimulate Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) signaling and SRC/EGFR transactivation, further promoting tumor growth and progression. While these pathways are essential for normal physiology, their dysregulation in cancer underscores the potential oncogenic role of CCK2R, further supporting its relevance as a molecular target [69].

Importantly, CCK2R is significantly overexpressed in several tumor types, most notably in MTC where it exhibits the highest receptor density, followed by astrocytomas and small cell lung cancers [71, 72, 73]. Furthermore, it was found that also meningiomas, endometrial and ovarian adenocarcinomas, mammary carcinomas, colon and gastric cancers express CCK2R. This overexpression often correlates with aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcomes, supporting the significance of CCK2R as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

Moreover, the ability of gastrin‐based peptides to bind selectively to CCK2R has driven the development of various radiolabeled peptide ligands over the past decades for cancer imaging and targeted radionuclide therapy [74]. In 2002, Béhé et al. [68] highlighted the importance of peptide platforms targeting the CCK2R/gastrin receptor for the staging and therapy of MTC through nuclear medicine. Their research focused on the development of radioligands targeting CCK2R, particularly those featuring C‐terminal binding sequences, associated with CCK receptors. Preclinical models revealed that peptide derivatives from the gastrin family exhibited superior binding affinity and selectivity for CCK2R, leading to promising biodistribution profiles and therapeutic efficacy, especially in MTC. Subsequent clinical studies further confirmed high patient‐based sensitivity for tumor detection.

Based on the specific and high‐level expression of CCK2R in malignancies, two major strategies have been developed to exploit this target using radiolabeled peptides: agonist‐ and antagonist‐based approaches (Figure 2). Agonists, which mimic natural ligands such as gastrin, activate receptor‐mediated internalization and intracellular signaling. Antagonists bind to CCK2R without activating downstream pathways and typically do not internalize. However, they may offer additional advantages, such as recognizing a broader spectrum of receptor conformations, improved in vivo stability, and prolonged residence time at the cell membrane. Notably, recent studies suggest that antagonists may be particularly well suited for delivering alpha or Auger‐emitting radionuclides to the cell membrane [65, 75]. Evidence indicates that irradiation of the plasma membrane—especially within cholesterol‐rich lipid rafts—can induce cytotoxic effects through oxidative stress, apoptotic signaling, and other non‐targeted mechanisms. These findings highlight the therapeutic relevance of membrane‐localized irradiation and support the further investigation of CCK2R antagonists in RPT strategies that rely on high linear energy transfer (LET) emitters. The choice between agonists and antagonists should therefore consider not only pharmacokinetics and receptor biology, but also the specific radiobiological mechanisms engaged by the chosen radionuclide.

The overexpression of CCK2R is frequently linked with aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcomes, underscoring its role in tumor progression. Early research demonstrated that CCK2R‐binding ligands can selectively accumulate in tumor tissues, providing a strong rationale for their use in the development of radiopharmaceuticals. By conjugating these ligands to radioactive isotopes via appropriate chelators, they can serve dual purposes depending on the physical properties of the chosen radionuclide. For diagnostic imaging, ligands are typically labeled with positron‐emitting isotopes (e.g., 68Ga for PET), allowing for non‐invasive visualization of CCK2R‐positive tumors. For therapeutic applications, the same ligands can be labeled with beta‐ or alpha‐emitting radionuclides (e.g., 177Lu or 225Ac), delivering cytotoxic radiation specifically to cancer cells while sparing healthy tissues [76, 77] (Figure 2). Such flexibility has enabled the development of a range of CCK2R‐targeted agents, among which agonist‐based compounds have been most extensively explored and will be discussed in detail in the following section.

CCK2R‐TARGETING AGONISTS IN CANCER RADIOTHERANOSTICS

Early radioligand development exploited endogenous ligand analogs, including MG and CCK‐8, both of which exhibit strong receptor affinity and share a conserved C‐terminal motif critical for binding CCK2R [78, 79, 80]. These structural features provided the foundation for the development of CCK2R‐targeted radioligands with agonistic properties. Building on these natural ligands, extensive synthetic modifications have been employed to refine receptor selectivity, improve pharmacokinetics, and enhance overall in vivo performance.

Sequence optimization strategies for gastrin‐derived CCK2R ligands

N‐terminal optimization

Several gastrin‐derived radiolabeled peptides have been investigated [81, 82, 83], including [DGlu1]MG (MG0), which shows high receptor‐specific tumor uptake but also significant renal accumulation. This elevated renal retention is primarily attributed to the strong negative charge of the N‐terminal penta‐Glu sequence, which enhances reabsorption within the renal proximal tubules [84]. The resulting high kidney doses raise concerns regarding potential nephrotoxicity. To address this issue, a modified analog, [DGlu1, desGlu2‐6]MG (MG11), lacking the penta‐Glu sequence and conjugated to the macrocyclic chelator 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid (DOTA) (Figure 3A), was developed as an initial strategy. Although this modification significantly reduced renal uptake, its low enzymatic stability appeared to impair tumor uptake [83].

Modifications to minigastrin‐based peptides have primarily focused on optimizing their amino acid sequence to improve metabolic stability and biodistribution characteristics. These modifications facilitated the clinical translation of analogs such as DOTA‐(DGlu)6‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2 (DOTA‐CP04) (Figure 3B) and DOTA‐(DGlu)6‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐Nle‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2 (DOTA‐PP‐F11N) (Figure 3C). The latter incorporates the substitution of the oxidation‐prone methionine (Met) with norleucine (Nle) [85, 86]. A key strategy has involved replacing the penta‐Glu with DGlu and introducing a sixth DGlu residue. This modification significantly improved the metabolic stability of the peptides by leveraging the resistance of D‐amino acids to enzymatic degradation in the cellular environment. Although these advancements improved stability and biodistribution, significant challenges remain, particularly in achieving an optimal balance between renal clearance and tumor targeting efficacy.

To investigate how N‐terminal modifications influence both receptor affinity and metabolic stability in vivo, various substitutions were introduced at the N‐terminal residues of MG analogs, these included proline [87], triazole linkages [88], alanine [89], and glycine [90]. Notably, these alterations did not cause significant loss of CCK2R affinity. Meanwhile, Rahimi et al. [91] introduced a minigastrin‐based analog with N‐terminal D‐Glu‐to‐D‐Ala substitutions to reduce overall negative charge and mitigate renal reabsorption. These findings demonstrate a high tolerance for substitutions without the loss of function. The pharmacophore of MG analogs is defined by the conserved C‐terminal sequence Trp‐Met‐Asp‐Phe, while the N‐terminal residues (e.g., DGlu, Ala, Tyr, and Gly) mainly act as spacers for the DOTA chelator.

C‐terminal optimization

The above‐mentioned modifications have primarily targeted the N‐terminal region of minigastrin analogs [79, 92]. However, these linear peptides exhibited limited enzymatic stability in vivo. When tested in humans, radiolabeled minigastrin analogs undergo rapid proteolytic degradation at the C‐terminal sequence that interacts with the receptor by various proteases [86]. To overcome this limitation, optimization shifted toward modifying the C‐terminal sequence. Key strategies included substituting Met with Nle, replacing phenylalanine (Phe) with 1‐naphthylalanine (1‐Nal), and introducing an N‐methyl group at the amide bond linking tryptophan (Trp) and Nle. These modifications enhanced ligand affinity, selectivity, and bioavailability for CCK2R‐targeting peptides. Building on these improvements, the DOTA‐MGS5 was designed, with the sequence DOTA‐D‐Glu‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐(N‐Me)Nle‐Asp‐1Nal‐NH2 (Figure 3D) [93, 94].

MGS5 addressed enzymatic instability by methylating the Trp‐Nle peptide bond, which enhanced structural rigidity and enzymatic resistance without compromising receptor affinity. In vivo evaluation employing A431‐CCK2R and A431‐mock xenograft mouse models revealed tumor uptake exceeding 20%IA/g and tumor‐to‐kidney ratio ranging from 4:1 to 6:1. These findings marked a substantial improvement in biodistribution for the peptide agent. Imaging with [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 demonstrated a notably high tumor‐to‐background contrast [93, 94], underscoring its strong selectivity for CCK2R‐positive tumors in vivo.

Building on the advancements of MGS5, further structural modifications have been explored to enhance enzymatic stability, receptor affinity, and tumor‐targeting efficiency. Key modifications included the replacement of 1‐Nal with 2‐naphthylalanine (2‐Nal) to refine receptor interactions. Additionally, the introduction of N‐methylation at the Asp‐1Nal peptide bond increased resistance to enzymatic degradation and substituting D‐Glu with D‐lysine (D‐Lys) was explored to investigate the influence of molecular charge on biodistribution. Preclinical studies in BALB/c nude mice bearing CCK2R/A431‐mock xenografts showed that [111In]In‐DOTA‐[(N‐Me)1Nal8]MGS5 (Figure 3E) exhibited improved stability while retaining strong receptor affinity, as evidenced by low‐nanomolar IC50 values. Notably, its tumor uptake reached 48.1 ± 9.2%IA/g, nearly twice that of the original MGS5. This derivative also showed a significantly improved tumor‐to‐kidney ratio (7.55 ± 0.48) and reduced stomach uptake (4.2 ± 0.5%IA/g vs. 8.2 ± 2.4%IA/g for MGS5), minimizing off‐target accumulation. Furthermore, tumor retention was influenced by the choice of radiometal, influenced tumor retention. Specifically, [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐[(N‐Me)1Nal8]MGS5 demonstrated particularly high tumor uptake (35.1 ± 6.3%IA/g) compared to the other two MGS5 molecules. These refinements highlight the potential of optimized MGS5 analogs to further improve the precision and efficacy of CCK2R‐targeted diagnostics and therapeutics [95].

Meanwhile, DOTA‐MGS8 (DOTA‐D‐Glu‐Pro‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐(N‐Me)Nle‐Asp‐1Nal‐NH2, Figure 3F) was designed to improve enzymatic stability and tumor affinity by introducing a proline (Pro) substitution at position 2 within the N‐terminal domain. This modification, together with the existing C‐terminal replacements involving N‐MeNle and 1‐Nal, reinforces the peptide backbone against enzymatic degradation while maintaining high receptor affinity. Preclinical studies of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS8 demonstrated strong receptor‐targeted internalization (∼47% at 2 h) and high tumor uptake (∼27%IA/g at 1 h post‐injection) in A431‐CCK2R xenografted BALB/c nude mice. Compared to DOTA‐MGS5, DOTA‐MGS8 exhibits higher tumor activity levels, thereby enhancing the tumor‐to‐background ratio [87, 96, 97].

Linker‐based optimization

Although animal studies and early clinical data indicate that [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 exhibits favorable in vivo performance, its molecular design still includes two key degradation sites, Tyr‐Gly and Gly‐Trp. As previously mentioned, Holzleitner et al. [90] demonstrated that the N‐terminal amino acid sequence in MG analogs plays a negligible role in CCK2R binding. Based on this, they further simplified the structure by substituting DOTA‐MGS5 with a polyethylene glycol (PEG) spacer. While this alteration maintained both receptor affinity and lipophilicity, it unexpectedly led to reduced compound stability.

Günther et al. [98] introduced γ‐D‐glutamic acid (γ‐Glu) between the DOTA chelator and the triethylene glycol (PEG3) linker in the design of DOTA‐CCK‐66 (DOTA‐γ‐Glu‐PEG3‐Trp‐(N‐Me)Nle‐Asp‐1Nal‐NH2, Figure 3G). Preclinical evaluation (Figure 4) demonstrated superior in vivo stability for [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 compared to earlier analogs, as evidenced by higher proportion of intact peptide detected in urine (77.8% vs. 23.7% for [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐MGS5) [98]. In AR42J tumor‐bearing mice, another CCK‐66 analog, [67Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 exhibited high tumor uptake (19.4 ± 3.5%ID/g at 1 h, while off‐target accumulation in key organs such as the liver (0.31 ± 0.02%ID/g), pancreas (0.23 ± 0.07%ID/g), and kidneys (2.51 ± 0.49%ID/g) remained low (Figure 4E‐F) [98]. To further evaluate therapeutic capability, Holzleitner et al. [61] conducted additional studies examining the radiobiological effects of alpha‐particles (Figure 4G‐J). Targeted alpha therapy with [225Ac]Ac‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 in AR42J murine models induced a 4.5‐fold survival increase (54 ± 6 days vs. 12 ± 3 days controls), surpassing the 3‐fold gain (37 ± 3 days) from [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 beta therapy. Importantly, no significant hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity was observed, supporting the feasibility of targeted alpha therapy with this analog.

Triazolo‐peptidomimetic substitution for enhanced stability and tumor retention

Peptidomimetic substitution has become a promising approach to improve the stability and tumor retention of CCK2R‐targeting radiopharmaceuticals. One such approach involves the systematic replacement of enzymatically labile peptide bonds in DOTA‐[Nle15]MG11 and DOTA‐PP‐F11N with metabolically stable 1,4‐disubstituted 1,2,3‐triazole bioisosteres. This modification aims to prolong peptide half‐life, effectively addressing the challenge of rapid in vivo degradation. Building on PP‐F11N, Grob et al. [99] introduced amide‐to‐triazole substitutions at key positions, including D‐Glu10‐Ala11, Tyr12‐Gly13 and Trp14‐Nle15, to enhance metabolic stability. Preclinical evaluation in A431‐CCK2R tumor‐bearing mice revealed that minigastrin analogs of PP‐F11N, modified by triazole insertion between D‐Glu10‐Ala11 and Tyr12‐Gly13, exhibited significantly increased cellular uptake and affinity toward CCK2R in vitro. In vivo, these analogs demonstrated up to 1.9‐fold increase in tumor uptake compared to unmodified PP‐F11N at 24 h post‐injection, along with an improved tumor‐to‐kidney ratio, indicating superior retention at the target site [99]. Additionally, SPECT/CT imaging confirmed prolonged tumor residence, suggesting a potential advantage for therapeutic applications. However, the stabilization came at the cost of a significant reduction in CCK2R affinity. As a result, these modifications did not lead to a notable improvement in tumor accumulation [88, 99].

Foldamer‐based structural engineering for enhanced CCK2R targeting

Beyond conventional sequence optimization, foldamer‐based peptide engineering presents a promising strategy for enhancing CCK2R‐targeting radiopharmaceuticals. By introducing structural constraints, this approach can improve receptor binding, metabolic stability, and tumor retention. Alicia Corlett et al. [100] developed a series of β‐hairpin‐stabilized CCK2R‐targeting peptides using a combination of N‐methylation, aromatic substitutions, and backbone cyclization. Their approach aimed to mimic the biologically active conformation of gastrin while enhancing receptor affinity and in vivo stability. The lead candidates, [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐[Sar9, NMe‐Nle11, NMe‐Nal13]CP04, demonstrated superior tumor‐targeting properties compared to conventional minigastrin derivatives. In A431‐CCK2R tumor‐bearing mice, this compound exhibited a six‐fold increase in tumor uptake compared to [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐CP04, reaching 31.1 ± 5.3%ID/g at 1 h post‐injection. The improved metabolic stability of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐[Sar9, NMe‐Nle11, NMe‐Nal13]CP04 likely contributes to its increased renal uptake. Despite this, renal retention remains significantly lower than that observed with DGlu‐MG0 analogs [85]. Notably, its higher plasma protein binding of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐[Sar9, NMe‐Nle11, NMe‐Nal13]CP04 (82%) may influence biodistribution and clearance rates, indicating the need for further optimization to balance prolonged circulation and tumor targeting.

Radiohybrid (rhCCK) strategy: Dual‐labeling for theranostic applications

The development of 18F‐labeled CCK2R‐targeting radiopharmaceuticals has garnered significant interest due to the favorable physical and PET imaging properties of fluorine‐18. These include its optimal physical half‐life (109.8 min vs. 67.7 min for 68Ga), high positron yield, and widespread availability from cyclotrons. However, initial attempts to directly radiofluorinate minigastrin analogs via nucleophilic substitution on an aromatic scaffold resulted in poor in vivo stability and rapid defluorination, ultimately limiting their translation potential [101].

To address this, Günther et al. [102] applied the radiohybrid (rh) concept—previously successful in PSMA‐targeted agent—to CCK2R ligands. They introduced a silicon‐based fluoride acceptor (SiFA) moiety into the D‐glutamate chain of DOTA‐PP‐F11N, enabling 18F‐labeling via isotopic exchange. At 1 h post‐injection, [18F]F‐[natLu]Lu‐DOTA‐rhCCK‐18 demonstrated high tumor accumulation, as evaluated by micro‐PET/CT in AR42J tumor‐bearing mice, making it a promising candidate for PET imaging due to both its pharmacokinetics and the physical and PET imaging properties of fluorine‐18. [19F]F‐[177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐D‐Dap(pSiFA)‐(D‐γ‐Glu)8‐Ala‐Tyr‐Gly‐Trp‐Nle‐Asp‐Phe‐NH2 ([19F]F‐[177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐rhCCK‐18) exhibited a 13‐fold higher tumor uptake at 24 h post‐injection (25.4 ± 4.7%IA/g) compared to [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐PP‐F11N (1.9 ± 0.8%IA/g). However, it also demonstrated increased renal retention, which is likely attributable to the presence of negatively charged residues near the SiFA moiety.

To reduce kidney retention, Holzleitner et al. [103] modified DOTA‐D‐Dap(p‐SiFA)‐D‐γ‐Glu‐PEG7‐D‐γ‐Glu‐ PEG3‐Trp‐(N‐Me)Nle‐Asp‐1‐Nal‐NH2 (DOTA‐rhCCK‐70) and DOTA‐D‐Dap(SiFAlin)‐D‐γ‐Glu‐PEG4‐D‐γ‐Glu‐PEG3‐Trp‐(N‐Me)Nle‐Asp‐1‐Nal‐NH2 (DOTA‐rhCCK‐91) by replacing negatively charged DGlu residues with hydrophilic PEG linkers. In AR42J tumor‐bearing mice, [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐rhCCK‐70 and [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐rhCCK‐91 showed tumor uptake of 12.0 ± 0.8%IA/g and 7.5 ± 1.0%IA/g at 24 h post‐injection, respectively, compared to 25.4 ± 4.7%IA/g for [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐rhCCK‐18. Despite reduced tumor uptake, kidney retention decreased notably from 134 ± 18%IA/g to 8.4 ± 0.8%IA/g and 6.6 ± 0.5%IA/g, resulting in improved tumor‐to‐kidney ratios from 0.19 ± 0.01 to 1.44 ± 0.14 and 1.14 ± 0.12, respectively. Although these modifications effectively reduced kidney retention, the resulting tumor‐to‐background ratios remained lower than those achieved with DOTA‐rhCCK‐18 [102, 103]. Future refinements should focus on fine‐tuning linker chemistry and charge distribution to maximize receptor binding while minimizing off‐target accumulation. Additionally, exploring alternative SiFA modifications with lower lipophilicity may further reduce kidney retention.

CCK2R‐TARGETING ANTAGONISTS IN CANCER RADIOTHERANOSTICS

CCK2R agonists can induce side effects in patients due to receptor activation or lead to unintended accumulation of radiopharmaceuticals in physiologically CCK2R‐expressing tissues, such as the gastric mucosa [81, 82, 83, 104]. Recent studies have shown that non‐internalizing receptor antagonists hold great potential in nuclear medicine due to their ability to bind to more accessible receptor sites [105, 106, 107]. Unlike peptide agonists, antagonists bind to CCK2R without triggering receptor activation, which is expected to reduce side effects such as gastrointestinal discomfort, transient cardiovascular changes, and systemic receptor activation. Additionally, antagonists are proposed to offer more accessible binding sites, potentially leading to higher tumor uptake, though this remains to be further investigated.

CCK2R antagonists include benzodiazepine derivatives such as devazepide, netazepide (YF476), L‐365,260, CI‐988, YM022, nastorazepide (Z‐360). They are substituted imidazoles like Gastrazole; and benzamide derivatives such as JNJ‐26070109 [108, 109, 110, 111, 112]. Among these CCK2R antagonists, Z‐360 is particularly notable. Studies have shown that Z‐360 plays a role in various gastrointestinal pathological processes and has been proposed as a therapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer [113, 114]. The free carboxyl group of Z‐360 allows for the attachment of functional moieties, without compromising its affinity for CCK2R, making it a promising strategy for targeted applications. Z‐360 has been already utilized as a carrier scaffold in the development of fluorescent or radiolabeled ligands, including CRL‐LS288 [115] and CRL‐3 [116].

Preclinical evaluations of Z‐360‐based radiopharmaceuticals labeled with 111In, 68Ga, and 177Lu have demonstrated selective targeting of CCK2R‐positive tumors with favorable pharmacokinetics. To facilitate radiolabeling, macrocyclic chelators such as DOTA, DOTAGA (1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1‐(glutaric acid)‐4,7,10‐triacetic acid) and NODAGA (1,4,7‐triazacyclononane‐1‐glutaric acid‐4,7‐acetic acid) were conjugated to the pendant carboxyl group of Z‐360. Among these, the DOTAGA‐Z360 demonstrated high and sustained tumor uptake in a twin xenograft model comprising HEK293‐CCK2i4svR tumors and receptor‐negative HEK293 tumors (Figure 5A). Both [67Ga]Ga‐DOTAGA‐Z360 and [177Lu]Lu‐DOTAGA‐Z360 demonstrated high and sustained tumor uptake (21.75 ± 4.90%IA/g at 1 h and 20.39 ± 3.96%IA/g at 4 h for [67Ga]Ga‐DOTAGA‐Z360; 20.88 ± 1.20%IA/g at 4 h for [177Lu]Lu‐DOTAGA‐Z360), with the tumor‐to‐kidney ratio of [177Lu]Lu‐DOTAGA‐Z360 increasing over time (2.4 at 4 h, 3.8 at 24 h, and 13.8 at 96 h p.i.), supporting its theranostic potential (Figure 5B). A direct comparison between CCK2R antagonists and peptide agonists, such as [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N, revealed that while PP‐F11N achieves higher initial tumor uptake (∼25%IA/g at 1 h), Z‐360‐based radioligands exhibit slower washout kinetics, enabling prolonged tumor localization [117]. An important advantage of Z‐360 antagonists over peptide‐based agonists is lower physiological uptake in the stomach. [177Lu]Lu‐DOTAGA‐Z360 achieved a tumor‐to‐kidney ratio of 2.4 at 4 h post‐injection, improving to 3.8 at 24 h and 13.8 at 96 h, reflecting favorable clearance. However, moderate renal accumulation remains a challenge for Z‐360 derivatives. To address this hydrophilic PEG linkers have been introduced, leading to improved clearance kinetics and reduced renal accumulation [118, 119].

Despite promising tumor selectivity and retention, further optimizations are required to refine the pharmacokinetic profile of Z‐360‐based radioligands. Current efforts are focused on modulating linker structures, optimizing chelation strategies, and enhancing hydrophilicity to improve biodistribution and minimize renal retention. Additionally, combining Z‐360 antagonists with tumor microenvironment‐targeting strategies may further enhance therapeutic efficacy and selectivity. With ongoing structural refinements, non‐peptidic CCK2R antagonists have the potential to fully exploit their therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities.

Beyond their favorable pharmacokinetics, CCK2R antagonists also hold promise for use with high‐LET radionuclides such as alpha emitters or Auger electron emitters. Because these agents typically localize at the cell membrane without internalization, they may effectively deliver high absorbed doses directly to membrane domains. This could trigger lipid raft‐mediated responses, including oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling, which have been implicated as key mechanisms of cytotoxicity in non‐internalizing targeted radiotherapies [65, 75].

mTORC1 INHIBITION: ENHANCING CCK2R EXPRESSION FOR IMPROVED TUMOR TARGETING

Mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 inhibition has been shown to induce adaptive responses that upregulate CCK2R expression in tumor cells. Pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 with rapamycin and its analogs leads to a reduction in global protein synthesis while selectively enhancing the expression of stress‐responsive and nutrient‐sensitive genes, including CCK2R. Mechanistically, inhibition of mTORC1 alters the translational landscape by regulating key downstream effectors, notably 4E‐binding protein 1 (4E‐BP1) and ribosomal S6 kinase 1 (S6K1), which are critical for controlling protein synthesis and cellular growth. The dephosphorylation of 4E‐BP1 following mTORC1 inhibition reduces cap‐dependent translation, favoring alternative translational programs that promote receptor upregulation. Additionally, decreased S6K1 activity alters ribosomal output and shifts cellular homeostasis toward a stress‐adaptive state that enhances CCK2R transcription [120, 121].

Preclinical studies have confirmed the beneficial effects of mTORC1 inhibition on CCK2R‐targeted radioligand uptake. In vitro experiments demonstrated that treatment with mTORC1 inhibitors significantly increased CCK2R mRNA and protein levels in tumor cells, leading to higher binding affinity and internalization of radiolabeled peptides. Biodistribution studies in an A431‐CCK2R tumor mouse model demonstrated that pre‐treatment with the clinical approved mTORC1 inhibitor Everolimus (RAD001) significantly increased tumor uptake of [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N. SPECT/CT imaging and quantification demonstrated a 3‐fold increase in [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N tumor uptake (mean ratio = 3.11, p < 0.001) in RAD001‐treated mice (Figure 5C). Notably, this enhancement did not affect the uptake in normal tissues expressing CCK2R, including the gastrointestinal tract [120]. In therapeutic efficacy studies, RAD001 pretreatment not only amplified radiotracer uptake but also led to significant tumor volume reduction (p < 0.05) without compromising systemic tolerance, as evidenced by stable body weights (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that mTORC1 inhibition can be leveraged as an adjunctive strategy to enhance the therapeutic index of CCK2R‐targeting radiopharmaceuticals. However, prolonged inhibition may induce compensatory feedback mechanisms that alter receptor turnover or affect tumor microenvironmental factors influencing ligand biodistribution. Therefore, optimizing dosing regimens and evaluating combination strategies are essential for maximizing the therapeutic benefits of mTORC1‐targeted modulation in PRRT applications.

CLINICAL TESTING AND TRANSLATION

The clinical translation of CCK2R‐targeting radiopharmaceuticals is progressing steadily, although currently lack regulatory endorsement for routine clinical application. Research has been driven by the potential of these radiotracers to improve the diagnosis and treatment of MTC, NETs, SCLC, and GIST. Several investigational agents have entered early‐ to mid‐stage clinical trials, evaluating their efficacy in diagnosis and PRRT. Despite promising results, major challenges remain in reducing renal toxicity, improving in vivo stability, enhancing tumor uptake, and demonstrating long‐term efficacy and safety. Ongoing clinical trials, summarized in Table 1, aim to address these issues by exploring structural modifications, nephroprotective strategies, and combination therapies.

A multicenter Phase I clinical trial (GRAN‐T‐MTC) was conducted to investigate the theranostic potential of CP04. The study employed a two‐step protocol to assess safety and efficacy, administering [111In]In‐CP04 at two peptide amounts (10 µg and 50 µg), with the higher dose intended for therapeutic applications. To assess strategies for nephroprotection, Gelofusine was co‐administered as a means of reducing renal radiation burden. [111In]In‐CP04 exhibited strong uptake in MTC lesions, especially in cervical and mediastinal regions, with metastases also detected in the liver and other organs in advanced cases. Patient‐level analysis demonstrated an 81% detection rate. Compared to conventional imaging, lesion‐based analysis showed that [111In]In‐CP04 revealed additional lesions in six patients, highlighting its improved diagnostic sensitivity. The compound was generally well‐tolerated, with no serious adverse events observed during the study. Pharmacokinetics analysis showed rapid clearance from blood and almost exclusive renal elimination. The clinical test results of [111In]In‐CP04 support their potential as theranostic agents for MTC [122].

In parallel, the clinical evaluation of [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N has also been conducted to explore its potential as a theranostic agent for MTC. The Lumed Phase 0a study enrolled patients with advanced MTC to investigate the compound's safety, biodistribution, and dosimetric characteristics. Using a randomized crossover design, each participant received two administrations of approximately 1 GBq [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N: one with and one without co‐infusion of succinylated gelatin (SG), a nephroprotective agent. The compound exhibited specific tumor localization, primarily in the cervical and mediastinal regions, with additional uptake detected in the kidneys and gastric wall. Tumor‐absorbed doses were lower than those observed with somatostatin receptor‐targeted PRRT, with a median value of 0.88 Gy/GBq. The stomach emerged as the dose‐limiting organ, receiving a median absorbed dose of 0.42 Gy/GBq. The median tumor‐to‐kidney dose ratio showed a modest improvement with SG co‐infusion, rising from 11.6 to 13.0, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. The safety profile was favorable, with only mild (Grade 1) adverse effects such as hypotension, flushing, and hypokalaemia. However, a key limitation of this study was the relatively low tumor radiation dose, raising concerns about the stability of the radiopharmaceutical and its ability to achieve therapeutic efficacy [81].

To address this limitation, a subsequent Phase 0b trial explored the impact of neutral endopeptidase (NEP) inhibition of enhancing the therapeutic performance of [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N. Given NEP's role in degrading circulating peptides, patients received pretreatment with Entresto® (a sacubitril/valsartan combination) 2 h prior to radiopharmaceutical administration. This approach yielded a 2.6‐fold increase in tumor‐absorbed dose, indicating a marked improvement in tumor uptake. However, this increase was accompanied by higher radiation doses to non‐target organs, with the stomach, kidneys, and bone marrow experiencing 2.1‐, 2.4‐, and 1.4‐fold increases, respectively. Despite these elevations, the tumor‐to‐organ absorbed dose ratios remained unchanged, indicating that while more radiation was delivered to tumors, off‐target exposure also increased proportionally. The rationale behind the improved tumor radiation dose with Entresto® lies in NEP's role in degrading circulating peptides, limiting the bioavailability of [177Lu]Lu‐PP‐F11N. By inhibiting NEP, Entresto® prolongs the radiopharmaceutical's circulation time, increasing its tumor uptake before degradation occurs. This finding underscores the potential of enzyme inhibition as a strategy to optimize therapy efficacy. However, given that off‐target radiation exposure also increased, further refinement is needed to balance tumor targeting with organ protection, particularly in the stomach, which remains the dose‐limiting organ. As with CP04, these results emphasize the importance of optimizing both the molecular design of the radiotracer and the treatment protocol to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing toxicity. Future clinical trials should focus on refining the dosing and scheduling of Entresto® pretreatment, exploring alternative nephroprotective strategies, and integrating receptor imaging to tailor therapy to individual patients [123].

Building upon the progress of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals demonstrated by CP04 and PP‐F11N, the clinical evaluation of MGS5 has further explored its potential as a theranostic agent (Figure 6A). The introduction of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 for PET/CT imaging has shown exceptional sensitivity in detecting MTC or SCLC. In a preliminary clinical study, six patients with advanced MTC underwent PET/CT imaging with [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5, which successfully identified 87 malignant lesions, including local recurrences, lymph node metastases, liver metastases, and bone lesions. Notably, radiotracer accumulation was higher at 2 h post‐injection than at 1 h, with a mean SUVmax increase from 6.0 to 7.2 and a mean SUVmean increase from 3.6 to 4.4, demonstrating improved lesion contrast over time. The biodistribution analysis of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 revealed favorable tumor‐to‐background contrast, with particularly high uptake in liver and bone lesions, which are common metastatic sites in MTC. Importantly, physiological uptake in normal tissues such as the blood pool, lungs, and pancreas were low, enhancing imaging contrast. [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 PET/CT has also been applied in patients with SCLC, where it detected all lesions identified by [18F]FDG PET/CT and revealed additional sites of uptake not observed on [68Ga]Ga‐DOTATOC scans, including subpleural, adrenal, gastric, and pelvic bone metastases (Figure 6B). These observations suggest a potential role for CCK2R‐targeted imaging in improving detection and staging in SCLC. One of the major advantages of [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐MGS5 over previous CCK2R‐targeted agents is its improved in vivo stability and enhanced tumor retention, addressing a key limitation observed in earlier minigastrin analogs. This increased stability results in prolonged tumor uptake while minimizing degradation‐related loss of activity, thereby improving both diagnostic accuracy and potential therapeutic utility [124].

For therapeutic applications, the first clinical dosimetry study of [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐MGS5 (1.5 GBq administered activity) in a patient with small cell lung cancer demonstrated a mean absorbed tumor dose of 12.5 ± 10.2 Gy/GBq. The kidneys, stomach and bone marrow exhibited absorbed doses of 0.28, 0.42 and 0.022 Gy/GBq, respectively, maintaining a favorable tumor‐to‐organ ratio. Despite these promising results, challenges remain in balancing tumor uptake with off‐target radiation exposure. Additionally, comparative studies are required to evaluate the effectiveness of [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐MGS5 against established radiotherapeutic agents such as [177Lu]Lu‐DOTATATE, which has been widely used for somatostatin receptor‐targeted PRRT. Future research should focus on optimizing treatment protocols, exploring combination strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy, and refining imaging‐based patient selection criteria [126].

[68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 has been introduced as a PET/CT tracer with promising pharmacokinetics and specificity for CCK2R. PET/CT scans performed at multiple time points (10 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h post‐injection) demonstrated rapid blood clearance of the tracer. Importantly, two of the six patients showed significant CCK2R‐expressing tumor uptake, with lesions detected in the lymph nodes, liver, and bones, supporting the feasibility of this agent for MTC imaging [127].

In addition to MTC, [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 has also shown clinical promise in SCLC. Among the patients evaluated, one SCLC case with extensive disease burden following multiple systemic therapies showed only limited tracer accumulation on [68Ga]Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT. In contrast, imaging with [68Ga]Ga‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 in the same patient revealed substantially enhanced uptake across several tumor sites, including pulmonary, nodal, hepatic, and osseous lesions. This differential uptake profile led to the decision to perform individualized dosimetry using [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐CCK‐66, illustrating the potential of CCK2R‐targeted imaging to guide therapeutic planning in advanced‐stage SCLC (Figure 6C) [125]. For therapeutic applications, the feasibility [177Lu]Lu‐DOTA‐CCK‐66 for PRRT is supported by its favorable pharmacokinetics, which demonstrate specific tumor uptake while minimizing retention in non‐target organs. However, as with other CCK2R‐targeted therapies, stomach radiation exposure remains a key challenge, given the physiological expression of CCK2R in gastric tissue. Future studies are needed to refine dosing strategies, implement nephroprotective protocols, and explore ways to reduce stomach radiation while preserving tumor uptake.

The versatility of CCK2R‐targeting PRRT extends beyond MTC and SCLC, to other cancers, including GIST, and stromal ovarian cancer. These cancers, which are often difficult to manage with conventional treatments, have exhibited promising responses to radiolabeled minigastrin analogs, making them strong candidates for expanded clinical application.

BROADER TRENDS AND PERSPECTIVES IN RADIOPHARMACEUTICALS

CCK2R as a case study in theranostic innovation

The development of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals exemplifies the key challenges and strategies in modern theranostic innovation. Early efforts have focused on optimizing ligand design, evolving from minigastrin‐based peptide agonists to novel antagonists such as Z‐360 derivatives. Further modifications such as albumin‐binding motifs, covalent serum‐binding groups, and enzymatically cleavable linkers (e.g., substrates sensitive to neprilysin or glutamyl endopeptidase) may enhance systemic circulation while minimizing off‐target accumulation.

Among these agents, non‐internalizing CCK2R antagonists, such as Z‐360 derivatives, present unique advantages for high‐linear energy transfer (LET) radionuclide therapy. Due to their membrane‐localized receptor binding and lack of internalization, these ligands are well‐positioned to deliver alpha or Auger radiation directly to the cell surface. This spatially confined energy deposition may trigger lipid raft‐mediated cytotoxic responses and induce non‐targeted effects such as oxidative stress and bystander killing—mechanisms increasingly recognized as critical contributors to the efficacy of non‐internalizing radiotherapies.

Several strategies may further enhance the therapeutic potential of CCK2R‐targeted agents. These include combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors to amplify antitumor immune activation, especially in immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. In parallel, the development of heterodimeric ligands co‐targeting CCK2R and complementary tumor‐associated markers may help overcome tumor heterogeneity, broaden patient eligibility, and improve treatment specificity.

Refinement of clinically validated targets

For clinically validated targets, intra‐ and inter‐tumoral heterogeneity has emerged as a key limitation to the therapeutic efficacy. To address this, next‐generation ligands are being developed with the goal of improving binding affinity, metabolic stability, and tumor retention.

Strategies such as the incorporation of albumin‐binding moieties or reversible plasma protein interactions are under investigation to enhance systemic exposure and maximize tumor accumulation. Covalent modification approaches, including conjugation to electrophilic groups that form stable serum protein adducts, offer a non‐affinity‐based method to prolong circulation time. Alternative ligand scaffolds and allosteric modulators are also being explored to overcome resistance and insufficient expression in certain tumor types. Dual‐targeting strategies have shown promise in enhancing tumor uptake and specificity, particularly when combined with pharmacokinetic‐optimizing modifications.

Efforts to mitigate renal toxicity and enhance therapeutic indices remain a priority. Co‐administration of renal protective agents like Gelofusine® and innovative linker designs will further reduce off‐target accumulation [128]. Enzymatically cleavable linkers, including those sensitive to neprilysin (e.g., MVK) [129, 130, 131] or glutamyl endopeptidase (e.g., brush border membrane (BBM) enzyme substrates) [132], enable selective degradation in the kidney, reducing tubular reabsorption and off‐target radiation.

Furthermore, companion imaging and biomarker‐driven patient selection are increasingly recognized as essential tools to guide treatment decisions and improve clinical outcomes.

Identification and translation of novel targets

To broaden the therapeutic landscape, exploration of new molecular targets that are selectively overexpressed in cancer tissues but absent in normal organs is critical. These targets must demonstrate not only high tumor specificity but also favorable pharmacokinetics and radiolabeling feasibility. Emerging candidates, such as FAP, integrin, CXCR4, GRPR, uPAR, NTSR1, Nectin‐4, σ‐receptor, CCK2R, CAIX, are actively being evaluated for these purposes. However, turning these targets into viable therapeutics requires overcoming limitations in metabolic stability, tumor penetration, and radiolabeling feasibility. Strategies such as ligand cyclization, multimerization, and the use of engineered antibodies or binding proteins are under evaluation to enhance drug‐like properties. Computational modeling and AI‐assisted ligand design are also being employed to accelerate lead optimization and predict in vivo performance. Preclinical validation in humanized mouse models and patient‐derived xenografts will be increasingly important to assess efficacy and safety profiles early in development.

Emerging ligands and radionuclides

The choice of ligand platform, including peptides, small molecules, antibodies, and engineered fragments, profoundly influences biodistribution, clearance, and isotope compatibility. Peptides offer rapid tumor penetration and fast clearance, aligning with short‐lived isotopes. In contrast, antibodies or nanoparticle‐based constructs may be preferred for longer‐circulating isotopes or sustained exposure applications. Nanotechnology‐based delivery systems, such as liposomes, micelles, and albumin‐based nanoparticles, may enhance circulation time, reduce off‐target toxicity, and enable controlled release for both classes of emitters.

Parallel to ligand selection, interest is growing in novel isotope pairs beyond the 68Ga/177Lu standard, such as 152Tb/149Tb, 64Cu/67Cu, 203Pb/212Pb, which offer theranostic capability with matched chemistry for both imaging and therapy. Alpha emitters are gaining attention currently, making them ideal for eliminating micrometastases and resistant tumor clones. In contrast, beta emitters remain the standard of radiotherapy for bulk tumor burden.

Integration with immune modulation and combination therapies

There is growing interest in combining radiopharmaceuticals with immunotherapies to exploit potential synergistic effects. Notably, studies combining PD‐L1 blockade with external radiation have shown promising results, providing a strong rationale for similar strategies with targeted radionuclide therapies [133, 134, 135]. For example, pairing CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals with checkpoint inhibitors could potentiate antitumor immunity, particularly in immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. Combination therapies that integrate radiopharmaceuticals with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or external beam radiation are being explored to maximize efficacy through complementary mechanisms.

Large‐scale clinical trials and commercialization

Large‐scale clinical trials regarding radiopharmaceuticals, such as PSMA, SSTR2, FAP‐targeting radioligands, are carrying out to expand different indications and regions for further commercialization. In addition, for other new targets, including GRPR and CXCR4, the large‐scale phase III clinical trials are underway for preparation of commercialization. Comprehensive multicenter clinical trials are critical for validating the efficacy and safety of new agents across diverse populations. Concurrently, addressing scalability and cost‐effectiveness in the production of radiopharmaceuticals will ensure their global accessibility. Collaboration with regulatory bodies will streamline the approval process.

CONCLUSION

Notable progress has been made in the design, optimization, and preclinical evaluation of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals, highlighting their potential as precision tools for both diagnostic imaging and targeted radionuclide therapy. Advances in ligand engineering, through backbone modification, stabilization via peptidomimetic substitution, and strategic introduction of bioisosteres, have contributed to improved metabolic stability, tumor uptake, and receptor selectivity. Moreover, the integration of innovative radiolabeling strategies, including the use of next‐generation chelators and silicon‐based motifs, has further expanded the versatility and translational potential of these agents.

Despite these advancements, key challenges remain unresolved. The balance between achieving prolonged tumor retention and minimizing off‐target accumulation, requires further refinement. Additionally, variability in receptor expression, tumor biology, and patient‐specific pharmacokinetics underscores the need for a deeper understanding of how molecular design influences in vivo behavior. While CCK2R agonists have demonstrated high tumor uptake, their physiological activity and rapid degradation have spurred interest in antagonist‐based and structurally constrained ligands as potentially more stable and safer alternatives.

Taken together, these findings underscore the critical importance of continued structure‐activity relationship studies, systematic preclinical evaluation, and rational design approaches to guide the development of next‐generation CCK2R‐targeting agents. Such efforts will be essential to bridge the gap between preclinical promise and clinical translation, enabling the development of radiopharmaceuticals that are both effective and clinically feasible for the personalized management of CCK2R‐expressing malignancies.

The evolution of CCK2R‐targeted radiopharmaceuticals exemplifies how strategic innovation in target selection, ligand architecture, and radionuclide application can drive the advancement of cancer radiotheranostics. Moving forward, the field will benefit from the integration of emerging molecular targets, customized ligand scaffolds, next‐generation radionuclides, and synergistic treatment strategies, ultimately facilitating the development of clinically translatable agents capable of delivering durable, personalized therapeutic responses while addressing cancer heterogeneity, resistance mechanisms, and interpatient variability.
