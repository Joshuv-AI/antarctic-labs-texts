Design, synthesis, and anticancer activity of phenoxyacetohydrazide derivatives in burkitt lymphoma
Authors: Yasser Hussein Issa Mohammed, Saad Alghamdi, Hibah Ali Almasmoum, Ahmed Hassen Shntaif, Nida Suhail, Bodour S Rajab, Sadeq K Alhag, Mariah N Hafiz, Mohammed M Jawad, Ahmed M Senan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurkitt lymphoma (BL) is an aggressive non- Hodgkin lymphoma mainly driven by c- MYC translocations, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and high mortality. Although treatments have improved, issues like toxicity, resistance, and poor outcomes after recurrence remain major challenges. In this study, a series of phenoxyacetohydrazide derivatives were synthesized and characterized using spectroscopic techniques. Their anticancer activity was tested against Burkitt lymphoma cell lines. The compounds were evaluated for cytotoxicity at various concentrations, and the most effective ones were selected for further analysis. Structural modifications produced candidates that were screened for in-vitro cytotoxicity against BL cells (Blc), human lung adenocarcinoma (A549), and normal fibroblasts (NIH- 3 T 3) using trypan blue and MTT assays to determine IC50 values. Several compounds exhibited significant activity against cancer cells with lower toxicity toward normal cells. Lead compounds were then tested in a murine BL ascites model over 30 days, with survival rates and ascitic volume monitored. Compounds 12 d and 12 f were identified as the most active, killing Blc cells with IC 50 values of 11. 8 ± 0. 0.2 µM and 12. 3 ± 0. 1 µM, respectively, while showing minimal toxicity toward NIH- 3 T 3 cells (IC 50 > 40 µM). In vivo, 12 d and 12 f significantly increased median survival times and reduced malignant ascites by 67–72%, indicating strong suppression of tumor growth and related symptoms. Additionally, molecular docking was performed to predict the binding modes of the synthesized ligands with the Pygo- BCL 9 Wnt signaling complex (PDB ID: 2 vp 7: A), involved in decoding methylated histone H 3 tail, and Bruton’ s Tyrosine Kinase (BTK) kinase domain (PDB ID: 3 pj 2). The docking results showed promising inhibitory activity of compounds 12 d and 12 f against these target proteins, suggesting their potential as anti- cancer agents. Overall, the findings demonstrate that chemical structure can be optimized for potency and tolerability, potentially overcoming challenges associated with conventional therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52968-9.Keywords: Burkitt lymphoma, Hydrazide derivatives, Anti-tumor activity, Cytotoxicity, IC50 values, Murine model, Ascites, Structural modificationsSubject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the major health problems worldwide, with almost 10 million deaths annually reported by the World Health Organization1. Among malignant hematological disorders, Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is distinct for its brisk tumor growth and extreme aggressiveness2,3. Originally described among pediatricians in Africa, non-Hodgkin lymphoma caused by this variant is mainly the result of translocations involving the c-MYC oncogene4 and is responsible for inducing uncontrolled cell division and heavy tumor burden. Though the advances in combination chemotherapy regimens and the addition of rituximab led to better patient outcomes, challenges like toxicity, drug resistance5, and limited efficacy on relapse indicate an urgent need for novel targeted and less toxic therapeutic modalities6,7. While seeking novel chemotherapeutic agents8, special attention has been given to heterocyclic scaffolds and hydrazide derivatives due to the immense pharmacological potential of these molecules, including antimicrobial9, anti-inflammatory10, antioxidant, and anticancer properties11. In Hydrazide-derived molecules, the (–NH– and –C = O) groups are excellent hydrogen bond donors (HBD) and acceptors (HBA). This allows hydrazide derivatives to form strong, specific interactions with the active sites of various enzymes and receptors, leading to an increase in their binding affinity to biological targets. They have emerged as promising candidates during anticancer lead molecule discovery studies12–14. Of special interest are the strategic modifications that optimise lipophilicity, steric compatibility, and electronic characteristics to enhance selectivity and efficacy against tumor cells, which have emerged as promising candidates during anticancer lead molecule discovery studies12–14. Of special interest is the strategic modification that optimises lipophilicity, steric compatibility, and electronic properties to greatly enhance its selectivity and efficacy against tumor cells15. The current study is founded on this rationale and reports a series of novel 2-(4-benzoyl-2-chloro-6-fluorophenoxy)-N’-(2-phenoxyacetoxy)acetohydrazide derivatives (12a–f). The hydrazide derivatives were synthesized via a stepwise coupling strategy, in which specific electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents were introduced systematically into the benzoyl and phenoxyacetoxy regions at each step. This design was intended to enable a preliminary structure–activity relationship (SAR) assessment by tuning key determinants of cellular activity, including electronic effects (which can alter hydrogen-bonding and polar interactions), lipophilicity/permeability, and steric profile. Specifically, incorporation of a methyl group was expected to increase local hydrophobicity and potentially improve membrane penetration. In contrast, bulky and/or halogenated phenoxy substituents were introduced to probe how steric and electronic modulation could enhance complementarity with tumor-associated molecular interactions and influence potency/selectivity16. Based on this rationale, we anticipated that changes in substituent electronic and steric properties would translate into measurable differences in cytotoxicity across the series17. To evaluate these hypotheses, the compounds were examined using an integrated workflow combining in vitro cytotoxicity and mechanism-linked assays, supported by in vivo/ex vivo and in silico analyses where applicable18. The library was screened against Burkitt lymphoma (BLc) cells19, A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells20, and NIH-3T3 normal fibroblasts to compare potency and selectivity, revealing differential activities among the derivatives rather than uniform effects across the series21. In vivo studies will utilise the murine Burkitt lymphoma ascites model22, an extremely aggressive lymphoma model that systemically mimics the malignancy’s pathophysiology. Notably, the survival analysis showed a substantial increase in lifespan compared with untreated controls, though without histological verification of liver or spleen toxicity. The ex vivo verification is to be performed using the chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay23,24, an established model for angiogenesis and a non-tumour construct that spans the in vitro/in vivo divide. These studies independently confirmed the anti-angiogenic and angiopreventive properties for the leading analogues, highlighting the potential to inhibit one of the major processes underlying tumour progression and metastasis. In silico docking experiments lent further rigor to the mechanistic validity of the compounds25. Docking simulations at two cancer-related protein targets Pygo-BCL9 Wnt signalling complex (2vp7:A)26, the central hub in the canonical Wnt signalling pathway27, and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK, 3pj2), a critical component in the B-cell receptor pathways, exhibited efficacious binding interactions for these derivatives28. These in silico results not only elucidate probable molecular mechanisms but also validate the strategy design logic behind the chemical series. When taken collectively, this multifaceted strategy allows for a panoramic analysis of anticancer activity, from molecular transactions to organismal viability. Through the employment of in vitro cell-based assays, in vivo murine lymphoma models, ex vivo measurements of CAM angiogenesis, and in silico docking models, the study ensures that observations are not left to the limitations of a single experimental arena but stringently reproduced under various domains of increasing biological complexity. This study, therefore identifies compounds 12d and 12f as lead compounds, characterized by strong cytotoxicity, tumor-selective targeting, inhibition of angiogenesis, and a survival advantage in Burkitt lymphoma model systems. Beyond their pro tanto relevance, the findings shed light on the general principle that synergistic structural alterations comprising hydrophobic, bulk substituents in combination with electron-altering groups can yield enhanced antitumor activity in phenoxyacetohydrazide templates.

Materials and methods

Chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemical Co. TLC was performed on aluminium-backed silica plates and visualized by UV-light. Melting points were determined on a Thomas Hoover capillary melting point apparatus with a digital thermometer. IR spectra were recorded on an FT-IR Shimadzu 8300 spectrometer, 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz NMR spectrometer in DMSO-d6 and the chemical shifts were recorded in parts per million downfield from tetramethylsilane. Mass spectra were obtained with a VG70-70 H spectrometer. Elemental analysis was done by Perkin Elmer 2400, and the results were within 0.4% of the calculated value. The purity of the product was preliminarily evaluated by thin-layer chromatography and melting-point determination.

Strategy of synthesis

A series of 2-(4-benzoyl-2-chloro-6-fluorophenoxy)-N′-(2-phenoxyacetoxy)acetohydrazide derivatives (12a–f) was synthesized as follows. 2-Chloro-6-fluorophenol (1) was acylated with substituted benzoyl chlorides (2a–b) under basic conditions (10% NaOH) to afford aryl benzoates (3a–b), which undergo Fries rearrangement to give the corresponding hydroxybenzophenones (4a–b). O-Alkylation of 4a–b with ethyl bromoacetate (5) in dry acetone/K₂CO₃ furnished ethyl phenoxyacetates (6a–b), and subsequent treatment with 99% hydrazine hydrate provided the hydrazides (7a–b). In a parallel sequence, substituted phenols (8a–c) were converted to ethyl phenoxyacetates (10a–c) via Williamson etherification with ethyl chloroacetate (9) in dry acetone/K₂CO₃, followed by alkaline hydrolysis to deliver the corresponding phenoxyacetic acids (11a–c). Final coupling of acids 11a–c with hydrazides 7a–b using TBTU as the activating agent and 2,6-lutidine as base in dichloromethane afforded the target products 12a–f. Reactions were monitored by TLC, and products were purified by silica gel chromatography and recrystallization to give the title compounds in good to excellent yields (Fig. 1).

General procedure for the synthesis of benzoic acid phenyl ester (3a–b)

Intermediates 3a–b were prepared according to the literature procedure28. Substituted benzoic acid phenyl esters 3a-b were synthesised by dissolving phenol 1 in dichloromethane (DCM), to which triethylamine (TEA, 0.45 mol) was added. The reaction mixture was then cooled to 0 ℃. Subsequently, a solution of substituted benzoyl chloride 2a-b (0.23 mol) in DCM was slowly added to the mixture, which was stirred for 5 h. The completion of the reaction was monitored by TLC using a solvent mixture of n-hexane: ethyl acetate (5:2). The reaction mixture was then diluted with DCM (100 mL), washed with 10% sodium hydroxide solution (3 × 40 mL), followed by water (3 × 30 mL). The organic layer was dried over sodium sulphate, and the solid obtained after evaporating the solvent was recrystallised from ethanol to yield compounds 3a-b.

General procedure for the synthesis of 4-hydroxy benzophenone (4a-b)

Compounds 4a–b were prepared according to the reported method29. Substituted 4-hydroxybenzophenones (4a–b) were synthesised via a Fries rearrangement. Compounds 3a–b (0.065 mol) were mixed thoroughly with anhydrous aluminium chloride (0.128 mol, 1.97 equiv.), and the reaction mixture was heated at 152 °C for 3 h. Reaction progress was monitored by TLC using n-hexane/ethyl acetate (5:2, v/v) as the eluent. After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and carefully quenched with ice water and 7 N HCl. The resulting mixture was stirred for 2 h, and the precipitated solid was collected by filtration and recrystallised from methanol to afford the desired compounds 4a–b.

General procedure for the synthesis of (4-benzoyl-phenoxy)- acetic acid ethyl esters (6a-b)

The reaction was carried out using the same key intermediates 4a–b as reported previously28. To a solution of compounds 4a–b (0.036 mol) in dry acetone (72 mL), potassium carbonate (0.078 mol) and ethyl bromoacetate (0.057 mol) were added, and the reaction mixture was heated at 60 ℃ for 4–6 h. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC using n-hexane/ethyl acetate (4:2, v/v) as the eluent. After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The residue was triturated with cold water to remove inorganic salts and extracted with diethyl ether (3 × 50 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with 10% aq. NaOH (3 × 50 mL), followed by water (3 × 30 mL), dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated to dryness to afford the crude product. Recrystallisation from ethanol gave the desired compounds 6a–b.

General procedure for the synthesis of 2-(4-benzoyl-phenoxy)acetohydrazides (7a-b)

A white solid was separated out, which was quenched with water (50 mL), filtered and washed with water (50 mL). The solid was dried under vacuum, and the product was recrystallized from ethanol to obtain compounds 7a-b with good yield30.

General procedure for the synthesis of N’-(2-(4-benzoyl-2-fluoro-6-methylphenoxy)acetoxy)−2-phenoxyacetohydrazide derivatives (12a–f)

2-(4-Benzoylphenoxy) acetohydrazides 7a–b (0.0010 mol) in dry DCM (20 mL) was stirred at 25–30 Cº, and then lutidine (0.0015 mol) was added, followed by the addition of phenoxyacetic acid (11a-c) (0.0010 mol). The reaction mixture was stirred at the same temperature for 30 min, then cooled to 0–5 ℃ and TBTU (0.0015 mol) was added and the temperature was maintained to 5 Cº over a period of 30 min. The reaction mass was stirred overnight and monitored by TLC using chloroform: methanol (7:1) as the mobile phase. The solvent was evaporated at reduced pressure, quenched by the addition of crushed ice and the solid obtained was filtered, dried and recrystallized from ethanol to afford compounds 12a-f in good yield24.

Cell culture

Burkitt lymphoma cells (Blc), human lung adenocarcinoma cells (A549), and normal fibroblast cells (NIH-3T3) were purchased from the National Center for Cell Sciences (NCCS), Pune, India. The cancer cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified eagles’ medium (DMEM) supplemented with 2 mM l-glutamine and balanced salt solution (BSS) adjusted to contain 1.5 g/L Na2CO3, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM l-glutamine, 1.5 g/L glucose, 10 mM (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1- piperazineethane sulfonic acid) (HEPES) and 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO, USA). Penicillin and streptomycin (100 IU/100µg) were adjusted to (1 mL/L). The cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified CO2 incubator32.

Preparation of test concentrations for experimental analysis

Fresh stock solutions for each compound and the positive control were created just before the beginning of each experiment. Each compound was dissolved to formulate a stock solution at a concentration of 8 mg/mL in 100% Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO). Working solutions of the test chemicals were subsequently prepared through serial dilutions using the appropriate culture media. All compounds were assessed at the specified concentrations, which ranged from 10 to 200 µg/mL.

The cytotoxic properties of novel derivatives of 2-(4-benzoyl-2-chloro-6-fluorophenoxy)-N’-(2 phenoxyacetoxy)acetohydrazide (12a-f) were evaluated against Burkitt lymphoma cells (Blc), human lung adenocarcinoma cell line (A549), and normal fib (NIH-3T3) with trypan blue dye exclusion assay. Acute toxicity screening and determination of working dose

The acute toxicity of the synthesized compounds was assessed using a fixed-dose screening design in healthy male Swiss albino mice (27–30 g). As per OECD guideline (TG 423), animals were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3 per group). The test compounds were dissolved in DMSO and administered intraperitoneally at doses of 100, 200, 500, and 1000 mg/kg body weight. Following administration, animals were observed for behavioral, neurological, and autonomic responses for the first 4 h, with additional assessments at 30-minute intervals for the subsequent 4 h. Mortality was recorded at 24 h post-treatment. Based on the observed mortality, the maximum tolerable dose (the highest dose with no mortality) and the minimum lethal dose (the lowest dose causing mortality) were identified. A working therapeutic dose of 25 mg/kg was empirically selected for subsequent in vivo antitumor efficacy studies, ensuring a safety margin below the observed toxic range37.

Docking study

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-tumor activity of the synthesized hydrazide derivatives (12a-f), in silico molecular docking was performed against two key cancer-related protein targets: Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK, PDB ID: 3pj2) and the Pygo-BCL9 complex (PDB ID: 2vp7:A). BTK is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase involved in B-cell receptor signalling and survival of malignant B-cells, making it a critical therapeutic target in lymphomas, including Burkitt lymphoma. Meanwhile, the Pygo-BCL9 complex participates in the Wnt/β-catenin signalling cascade, which governs oncogenic transcriptional activation and tumor proliferation. The dual docking approach thus allowed for mechanistic evaluation of both B-cell survival and Wnt-mediated tumor progression pathways. The docking simulations were carried out using a commonly used molecular docking software package. To validate the docking protocol, the co-crystallized ligand was extracted and re-docked into the 3pj2 and 2vp7:A active sites using the same docking settings. The docked poses with the highest binding affinity (highest negative energy) and having less than 2.0 Å in positional root-mean square deviation (RMSD) were considered ideal. After validation, Each ligand molecule was docked inside the active binding sites of both protein targets. The docking process involved pose detection, binding mode analysis, and scoring function calculation. However, the final docking poses were then chosen using binding affinity scores, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions. Additionally, the analysis or visualization of docking simulations was done using 3D Ribbon Model, 2D interaction diagrams for analysis related to hydrogen bonding, and active site orientation.

Results

Structure activity relationship (SAR)

The structure design of the derivatives of phenoxyacetohydrazide 12a-f has optimised their anti-tumor activity, particularly in murine Beyer Burkit lymphoma cells. The substitution patterns present in the structure, namely, both the benzoyl and phenoxyacetoxy, display various correlations for activity analysis. The substituents brought on the 4-position in the benzoyl structure include chlorine and methyl. The corresponding 4-chlorobenzoyl compounds (12a, 12c, 12e) had moderate levels of activity. The chlorine substitution pattern tends to exhibit electron withdrawal, thereby raising polarity. However, such structures functioned in a less active manner, perhaps because of reduced lipophilic interaction. The analogous 4-methylbenzoyl derivatives (12b, 12 d, 12f) were found to display greater activity. The electron-releasing properties introduced by the methyl group tend to display greater hydrophobic properties, thereby showing improved lipophilic interaction properties. The difference clearly implies that the introduction of the methyl group causes greater activity than chlorine substitution in the structure. The N’-phenoxyacetoxy moiety further modulated cytotoxic potential through variations in steric bulk and electronic effects of chloro-methylphenoxy group on 12a, 12b compounds, these derivatives were among the least potent, implying that the smaller substituents fail to provide sufficient steric or electronic complementarity to tumor cell binding sites. Also, diisopropylphenoxy group on 12c, 12 d compounds, the bulky diisopropyl substituents significantly enhanced activity in combination with the 4-methylbenzoyl moiety. In particular, compound 12d emerged as the most potent derivative in the series. The steric bulk of the diisopropyl group likely increases hydrophobic contacts and enhances affinity for lipophilic regions of cellular proteins. The synergy between the electron-donating methyl group on the benzoyl ring and the bulky diisopropyl substituents produced an optimal balance of lipophilicity and steric fit. Moreover, 3,4-chlorophenoxy group on 12e, 12f compound, the introduction of a chlorine atom at the phenoxy ring yielded favorable electron-withdrawing effects. Among these, compound 12f bearing both a 4-methylbenzoyl and 4-chlorophenoxy group exhibited potent cytotoxicity, second only to 12d. This highlights the beneficial role of combining hydrophobic (methyl) and electron-withdrawing (chloro) substituents, which together enhance binding affinity and cytotoxic efficacy. To further explain how the observed biological activity relates to the structural features of the synthesized compounds, the SAR trends can be examined by first considering the phenoxy substituents. For the 3,4-chlorophenoxy group (compounds 12e, 12f), compound 12f demonstrated excellent cytotoxic potency, close to that of 12d. Its activity results from the combined effects of a methylbenzoyl group (hydrophobic donor) and a 4-chlorophenoxy moiety (electron-withdrawing stabilizer), which together confer favorable physicochemical and biological properties. Moving to the diisopropylphenoxy group (compounds 12c, 12d), compound 12d showed the most potent activity, attributed to the dual effect of methylbenzoyl substitution (hydrophobic enhancement) and diisopropylphenoxy substitution (bulk and lipophilic interactions). These features likely improve both membrane permeability and stabilization of binding interactions within tumor-associated protein sites. In contrast, smaller phenoxy substituents (as seen in compounds 12a and 12b) imparted lower potency, underscoring the importance of steric bulk for optimal activity. Turning to the benzoyl ring substitution, methylbenzoyl substitution consistently increased activity over chlorobenzoyl substitution. Accordingly, the most favored pair involved coupling 4-methylbenzoyl with diisopropylphenoxy (12d), while the combination of 4-methylbenzoyl with 4-chlorophenoxy (12f) also yielded outstanding potency. Together, these SAR trends clearly identify 12d and 12f as lead candidates with outstanding cytotoxic potential. The results underscore the critical balance required among hydrophobicity, steric influence, and electronic contributions when optimizing cytotoxicity. SAR profiling recommends that modifications with bulky lipophilic groups in concert with electron donors may lead to even more effective derivatives for treating Burkitt lymphoma. The results point to a mechanistically inspired guideline for rational design of next-generation agents for treating Burkitt lymphoma and angiogenesis-related cancers (Table 1).

Determination of IC50 values for derivatives 12a-f and lead compound selection

A series of novel hydrazide derivatives (12a-f), synthesized by incorporating phenoxy acetic acid moieties into the 2-(4-benzoyl-2-chloro-6-fluorophenoxy)-N’-(2-phenoxyacetoxy)acetohydrazide scaffold, were evaluated for antiproliferative activity against Blc, A549, and NIH-3T3 cell lines using both trypan blue exclusion and MTT assays. Among these, compounds 12d and 12f demonstrated notable antineoplastic effects. For Blc cells, compound 12d exhibited IC50 values of 11.8 ± 0.2 µM (trypan blue) and 12.1 ± 0.1 µM (MTT), while compound 12f showed IC50s of 12.3 ± 0.1 µM and 12.8 ± 0.3 µM, respectively. Against A549 cells, 12d yielded IC50s of 10.1 ± 0.1 µM (trypan blue) and 9.9 ± 3.1 µM (MTT), whereas 12f showed 11.8 ± 0.5 µM and 10.9 ± 0.3 µM. Importantly, both compounds exhibited minimal cytotoxicity toward NIH-3T3 normal fibroblasts, with IC50 values exceeding 40 µM in both assays as shown in Table 2. Docking visualization and binding mode interpretation

3D visualization revealed that the hydrazide linker (-CONH-NH-CO-) of both compounds acts as a flexible bridge anchoring aromatic moieties into dual subpackets within the enzyme cleft. In BTK, the hydrazide carbonyl oxygen formed hydrogen bonds with Lys430 and Glu475, while the phenoxy oxygen engaged in dipolar interactions with Cys481, mirroring key pharmacophoric interactions observed in known inhibitors. In Pygo-BCL9, the ligands inserted their benzoyl rings into a hydrophobic groove bordered by Tyr345, Asp352, and Arg341, residues essential for histone H3 tail recognition. The electron-rich oxygen atoms of the acetoxy groups facilitated hydrogen bonding that reinforced anchoring within the binding cavity.

The predicted interactions demonstrate that compounds 12d and 12f not only fit optimally within both protein targets but also engage essential catalytic residues, suggesting potential dual inhibitory effects on Wnt and BTK pathways. This dual modulation could explain the pronounced in vitro cytotoxic and anti-angiogenic activity of these molecules, as both pathways are central to lymphoma growth and neovascularization.

Structure-binding relationship insights

The docking results align closely with SAR observations derived from biological assays, Hydrophobic methylbenzoyl substitution enhanced van der Waals and π–π interactions within hydrophobic regions of both proteins, Bulky diisopropylphenoxy in 12d optimized cavity occupation, reducing entropic penalties and increasing stability.

Also, 4-Chlorophenoxy in 12f provided favorable electrostatic complementarity with charged residues such as Lys430 and Asp352. Together, these findings suggest that the combined lipophilic bulk and polar functional groups of compounds 12d and 12f confer optimal physicochemical properties for dual-target inhibition. Their superior docking profiles further substantiate their selection as lead molecules in this chemical series.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that derivatives of 2-(4-Benzoyl-2-chloro-6-fluorophenoxy)-N’-(2-phenoxyacetoxy)acetohydrazide represent a promising class of anti-lymphoma agents, with compounds 12d and 12f emerging as the most potent lead candidates. These compounds exhibited sub-micromolar cytotoxic activity against Burkitt lymphoma and A549 cells, while showing substantially lower activity toward NIH-3T3 fibroblasts, suggesting a degree of tumor selectivity. Their antitumor potential was further supported by in vivo findings, in which both compounds reduced ascites volume, decreased microvessel density, and significantly prolonged median survival relative to the untreated control group. In addition, ex vivo CAM assay results confirmed their anti-angiogenic activity through significant inhibition of VEGF-induced microvessel formation. Molecular docking studies further indicated strong binding affinity of 12d and 12f toward BTK and the Pygo-BCL9/Wnt protein complex, supporting a possible dual-target mechanism involving suppression of both B-cell survival signaling and oncogenic transcriptional regulation.

Importantly, these findings also provide a strong basis for future development and highlight the potential clinical relevance of these compounds. In particular, 12d and 12f may serve as valuable lead scaffolds for the development of new targeted therapies for Burkitt lymphoma, especially in view of their combined cytotoxic and anti-angiogenic properties. Nevertheless, additional studies are required before clinical translation can be considered. Future work should focus on lead optimization to improve oral bioavailability, metabolic stability, and overall pharmacokinetic properties, while maintaining potency and selectivity. Moreover, the antitumor efficacy of these compounds should be validated in larger and more statistically powered in vivo studies, accompanied by comprehensive safety profiling, including hematological assessment, hepatic and renal function analysis, and histopathological evaluation. Further mechanistic studies are also warranted to confirm dual inhibition of BTK and Pygo-BCL9/Wnt signalling in biological systems. Finally, evaluation of compounds 12d and 12f in combination with standard Burkitt lymphoma therapies would be particularly important to assess possible synergistic effects, reduction of therapeutic resistance, and broader translational potential. Taken together, the present findings establish 12d and 12f as promising candidates for further preclinical investigation and support their continued development as next-generation anti-lymphoma agents.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

While cytotoxicity was evaluated against NIH-3T3 normal fibroblast cells and selectivity indices were calculated, additional normal cell lines (e.g., HEK-293, primary human fibroblasts) were not tested. Pharmacokinetic parameters such as bioavailability, half-life, and tissue distribution were not assessed. A formal LD₅₀ was not calculated; instead, a fixed-dose screening design was used to identify the maximum tolerable dose and minimum lethal dose. Tumor burden monitoring relied primarily on body weight changes, with ascitic fluid volume measured only at the endpoint (day 30), and quantitative tumor cell counts were not performed. Survival was not monitored until natural death, as all animals were euthanized on day 30; therefore, median survival time and increase in life span could not be reported. Angiogenesis assessment was qualitative rather than quantitative. Finally, the initial characterization of compounds 3a–3d lacked HPLC purity data and high-resolution mass spectrometry confirmation. Future studies will address these limitations.
