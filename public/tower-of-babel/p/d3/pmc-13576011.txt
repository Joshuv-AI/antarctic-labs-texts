Strengthening the Physician Workforce: An Expert Panel Discussion
Authors: Ted O’Connell, Lupe Alonzo-Diaz, Theresa Azevedo-Rousso, Julie Byerley, Alexa McKinley Abel, Michael B Rothberg, Kevin Smith
Published: 2026

Introduction

The United States faces a growing physician workforce crisis driven by rising population needs, persistent geographic maldistribution, financial and structural barriers to training, and escalating burnout across specialties. In this expert panel discussion, leaders in medical education, health policy, rural health, and clinical practice explore the multifaceted roots of the shortage and its impact on access, quality, and community well-being. Their insights highlight longstanding constraints in graduate medical education (GME), inequities in pathways into medicine, challenges unique to rural and underserved regions, and the evolving pressures reshaping the physician career experience. Together, the panelists outline both the urgency of the moment and the opportunities for innovative, system-level reforms to strengthen and sustain the future physician workforce.

Broader Implications of the Physician Workforce Shortage

TED O'CONNELL

Great. Thank you, all. We have convened a well-rounded panel, as we can all see. I am looking forward to this discussion. Our first question to guide the discussion is, what currently are, or soon will be, the broader impacts of the shortage of physicians in the United States, especially with regard to caring for our aging population?

JULIE BYERLEY

I'm sure everyone has their own ideas, but one of the things that I think is important to note about access to health care is that it is not simply about early death. Although it is certainly true and important that when people don't get the health care that they need, they could potentially experience early death, the truth is that people who don't get the health care that they need live less well.

That means that our communities are not as healthy as they could be. It means that the grandparent who does not receive health care can't participate as actively in the care of their grandchildren, which ends up hurting their adult children who struggle to find childcare and so forth. It means that our communities are full of individuals who could otherwise be contributing to the workforce or addressing the local social needs, but instead they are suffering with problems that could have been managed with appropriate health care.

These scenarios lead to worsening mental health as well. Lack of access to care does not mean only that more people will die of cancer, for instance, although that will probably happen; it also means that fewer people will be able to contribute to the goodness of life and to the goodness of the communities around them. All of this is unfortunate, expensive, and preventable.

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

To build on what Dr Byerley said, we all recognize that the concentration and density of primary care providers is correlated with reduced mortality. This is particularly true in rural areas. There are community and economic impacts too. Most physicians are employed by hospitals, so when considering rural areas where there is a greater worry about hospital closures, it is noteworthy that some of the concern about closures stems from workforce challenges. When there is limited access to care through a clinic or hospital, it also follows that there is reduced access to well-compensated work for members of the rural community. This impedes economic stimulation and stagnates development. We are understandably very focused on the health outcomes in these communities, but it’s also prudent to reflect on what a lack of health care access in rural settings could mean for a community’s health in a broader sense.

KEVIN SMITH

I agree with what has been said so far, particularly with Alexa’s notion of the overall systemic impact that affects already-threatened communities and stretched health care access. There is reduced access, which means in turn that the touchpoints that do exist become overutilized and overtaxed. This equates to longer wait times and delays for patients. Maybe people don't actually get to those spaces because they're so impacted. The system that appears to be available as health care is now overburdened, and health care becomes reserved for situations that are much more acute. Chronic management is not as widely used, and perhaps patients are visiting emergency departments for treatment, which in turn continues to tax whatever form of a system is in place, thereby exacerbating the challenges.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

This is a complex concept that starts off with this idea of a shortage of physicians. The term “physicians” is also quite broad, given that there are several different kinds of physicians. Given that, I am not sure exactly how to predict how many physicians the country will need 10 or 20 years from now because the technology is changing so quickly. It is fairly well anticipated that some of the tasks physicians perform will be replaced. For instance, will we have a shortage of radiologists or surgeons going forward? Will some of their work outputs be replaced by robots? Will some of the tasks that are performed in the primary care setting be done by artificial intelligence, and how does that idea correlate to the physician shortage we are discussing? There is also a rapidly growing workforce of advanced practice providers who are able to do many of the things that physicians do, sometimes without supervision. In most states, pharmacists are able to prescribe certain medications. I don't know how these changes will affect the physician shortage, and it is difficult to speculate because the workplace is changing so quickly.

The impacts will be dependent on where the shortage is and the type of shortage. I am hearing this panel mention preventive care, which of course falls under primary care, but there could be shortages of other types of physicians as well, for instance, among those who replace aortic valves, just to name one possibility.

KEVIN SMITH

The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has projected that the most significant impact will be within primary care. 1 We do not necessarily anticipate a shortage of specialists, which is also concerning because of the potential negative impact on preventive care.

LUPE ALONZO-DIAZ

I would like to look at the word “shortage” with a bit more scrutiny. The word is applicable to both pure numbers (such as patient-to-physician ratios, for example) and geographic maldistribution, as is especially true in rural communities. Perhaps the number of certain types of physicians is sufficient, but the community concentrations are not balanced.

I would also like to examine the shortage in relation to demographics and whether the demographics of the physician community and workforce are representative of patient demographics. Does the physician workforce represent the communities served? This aspect is just as important as the discussion around specialties and location, for instance.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

I agree. If we are talking only about the shortage of primary care physicians, this is not a new thing. We have had this shortage for 70 years. 2 In fact, if you watch the original episode of Marcus Welby, MD, it comments on the decline of the general practitioner, lamenting how everybody was going into specialties and the harms that could cause. Marcus Welby was the last general practitioner. You can see from the show how valuable he is, but his “specialty” gets no respect. I don't know that the primary care shortage is going to get worse. It might and certainly could, but there are also ways that that might be alleviated.

JULIE BYERLEY

Dr Rothberg raises some interesting points that we also need to consider in our educational pathways for physicians and other health professionals. We need to think differently about the workforce needs. I would argue that we will still need people to tend to the health needs of the community, but that we have to be nimble and think differently about how we prepare those people to serve others as our future evolves. We can be a whole lot more innovative to help people get the skillsets they need, to use the technologies that become available, and to meet the needs of the people more efficiently.

What Led to the US Physician Shortage?

TED O'CONNELL

Thank you, all. Let’s talk a bit about how we got here. What factors in the American health care system have led us to the current physician shortages?

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

That one’s easy. You just follow the money. Physicians are intelligent, and they follow incentives. And our incentives are set up to discourage people from going into primary care and to encourage them to go into specialties. If we want more primary care doctors, all we have to do is change the way that we pay people.

LUPE ALONZO-DIAZ

If I can add to that, it is a simple supply and demand model—supply in terms of both numbers and retirements. On the demand side, our population is aging, growing, and diversifying. Therefore, the complexity of who and what we need, and when we need it, is also shifting. I would also add that the high cost of medical education and training, particularly in underserved communities, is something that needs to be considered.

There was a report from AAMC a couple of years ago with troubling data showing an increase in the number of medical school graduates that were graduating with zero debt. On the contrary, there was also an increase in the number of individuals from particular communities that were graduating with debt loads exceeding $300,000. 3 This tells us that there is significant disparity among students attending medical school, reinforcing what we know about communities that do not have the same level of affluence or access to assets and resources. In addition to some of the structural changes that we need to talk about in terms of addressing the physician shortage, there’s also a narrative change. Do we see ourselves as physicians? Does the community seek mentorship and see others that look like them or feel familiar? Are they finding others with similar experiences to theirs?

THERESA AZEVEDO-ROUSSO

Although we expanded the front door to medicine, we narrowed the exit ramp. We grew medical schools in this country by 40% according to the AAMC, yet we did not simultaneously expand residency programs at the same rate. That mismatch is primarily due to financial constraints, as Dr Rothberg mentioned. The AAMC has repeatedly stated that although medical school enrollment in the United States has increased by 30% to 40% since 2002, the number of federally funded residency positions has increased by only about 1% per year because federal funding caps have restricted growth. This mismatch means that expanding medical schools alone cannot resolve the physician shortage. 4

Medicare funding for residency training was capped in 1997, and the redistribution of residency positions has not kept pace with the need to expand them. This redistribution has also not been equitable in areas experiencing the most significant physician shortages. Even with the development of 3-year accelerated medical education pathways that move students through training more quickly, the total number of residency positions has not increased. As a result, we now have a larger number of medical school graduates competing for the same fixed number of residency positions. To address this growing imbalance, we must widen the exit ramp for medical school graduates by expanding residency opportunities.

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

From a rural perspective, there are significant structural barriers and changes that have made rural America a less attractive place to live generally. This then makes all of the challenges we are discussing even more acute because there have been lower birth rates and higher outmigration over the last decade in these areas. People are less inclined today to stay living in these types of areas, and it is less attractive to recruit physicians to these areas for several reasons, which can include decreased access to opportunities for family members and less available housing. The difficulties of getting someone to stay in or move to a rural area have exacerbated physician shortages in rural areas.

JULIE BYERLEY

Building on what both Lupe and Alexa mentioned about geographic disparities, there are a few evidence-based aspects that I believe are crucial for creating a workforce in a geographic area of need.

First, we need to admit students into medical schools and training programs who grew up in the area of need. It is significantly less likely that someone will move from an urban area to a rural area for work. Our best chance of having a sustainable rural workforce is by cultivating and keeping clinicians and physicians who grew up rural.

Second, we need to educate these students in community-engaged ways so they grow roots in the setting of need and build relationships with people who are living, working, and thriving in those areas. Community engagement through curriculum is important.

Third, we must put these students in cohorts of like-minded individuals where service to their communities by staying local is celebrated. If we want people going into primary care in rural Pennsylvania, we should celebrate a family medicine match in rural Pennsylvania even more than we celebrate those competitive matches in orthopedic surgery. If rural primary care is what success looks like, we build a cohort in which that celebration is shared. And to Dr Rothberg’s point, we should also financially incentivize that outcome.

KEVIN SMITH

I agree with everything being said. The high cost of medical training is a major deterrent. When I was a younger, aspiring medical student, there was a general sense among the public that there was an aspect of glamour and glitz associated with being a physician. But times have changed, and that perception has since been overshadowed by concerns that are very real to this newer generation, like the risk of burnout. Burnout is a major deterrent to pursuing and continuing a career in providing health care. As a result, some of today’s brightest students are considering other career paths.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

A career as a physician remains extremely well compensated. If you look at the top 10 highest-paid professions, 9 are physicians. 5 The number of people who want to go to medical school still outnumbers the number of people who can go to medical school. Acceptance rates remain low, and schools remain selective. Medical schools are still turning away a lot of applicants who are willing to put in the effort and take on the debt. But the fact remains we do not have residency slots for them, even with the 40% expansion. So, now, the real limitation is residency, and we have opened up all these medical schools and replaced foreign graduates who used to fill a large portion of our residencies with American graduates.

We have a reservoir of foreign physicians who would love to practice in the United States, including in rural areas, on J-1 visas. If we place conditions around these foreign physicians, such as the only way they can stay in the United States is by practicing in a rural area, most of them will agree, because they really want to be here. If we take that incentive away, they will be less likely to go into those rural areas and will prefer to practice instead in urban areas, just like everybody else.

Some states are now going to license foreign physicians without requiring them to finish residencies in the United States. To me, this remains a concern, but it will certainly ease the physician shortage.

Private Equity in Medicine and the Evolution of the Physician Career

TED O'CONNELL

Thank you, everyone. I’d like to shift gears and talk about your thoughts on the evolution of the physician career experience within private equity models of care.

KEVIN SMITH

For the sake of full transparency, I have not practiced under that model, but I have heard the concerns of several colleagues who have, which I will reference for the purpose of this conversation. The autonomy that is presumed to be associated with a career in medicine seems to be lacking within that model. Private equity models of care also seem to involve a bait-and-switch experience in which a very rosy situation is initially presented. It sounds great, and it is great for some initial part of the relationship, but later those contracts change along with changes in a physician’s practice and income.

On the other hand, private equity models can present some benefits and security—for instance, providing malpractice insurance, which an independent practice would otherwise be paying for—and operational support.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

Private practice in the United States has become incredibly difficult, especially factoring in the high cost of malpractice premiums, the burdens of the electronic health record, and the cost of running a practice. These costs have become so high that now the majority of physicians here do not work in private practices. They work for hospitals or other organizations as expensive salaried employees. They have given up that autonomy because they could not otherwise afford to practice at all.

Now, the people who employ them are trying to figure out how to get a higher return on their investment with regard to these very expensive employees. That often equates to working conditions that the physicians do not enjoy. Private equity wants even better returns than nonprofits do, and the financial models certainly differ, so they end up squeezing the physicians more by creating ways for them to be more efficient.

When it comes down to it, there are some areas where additional efficiency simply cannot be squeezed out. The most limiting factor is time. Private equity does not know how to run a practice better than physicians do (ie, they cannot make them provide the same quality of care in less time). Their one advantage is scale. With scale, they can at least buy the electronic health record for a lower per-physician price. In addition, they can also centralize billing and do other administrative tasks on behalf of the physicians for cost savings. But, from the physician perspective, private equity will not provide a pleasant work experience simply given that the physician is an expensive employee. One way to make them more “efficient” is to have them see more patients in the same amount of time (eg, 4 patients per hour instead of 3). But then the quality suffers, which makes physicians and patients unhappy.

Entering and Exiting the Profession

TED O'CONNELL

Great, thanks, everyone. My next question harkens back to our earlier discussion about how we got here, and drills down more deeply. What are some of the contributing factors that led to the physician shortage in the United States? More specifically, what have been some of the challenges for new entrants to the field, and what are some of the reasons for physicians leaving the workforce?

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

This topic has been on my mind. Specifically, physicians may be leaving rural areas to pursue practice elsewhere because they don't feel supported in their rural communities, not only by means of geographic isolation, but also because they may not have a sufficiently large care team. They may not feel comfortable without having specialists nearby to refer to or lean on for consultation. Rural physicians may simply experience a general lack of support for these reasons, which takes them out of the rural geographic workforce.

I would be remiss if I did not mention aging out of the profession. Across the country, physicians are retiring, and it is happening at a potentially higher rate in rural communities where there is more of an aging population. Often, when a physician retires in these areas, there is nobody coming up behind them to take their place.

THERESA AZEVEDO-ROUSSO

I believe the regulatory burden and the extensive documentation requirements have also contributed to the challenges we face today, particularly in primary care, where physicians spend an extraordinary amount of time in documentation. In addition, there are far fewer faculty members of color in leadership roles. The underrepresentation of faculty of color in academic medicine is concerning as we work to increase the pipeline of physicians entering medicine. 5

I often ask residents and fellows graduating about their future work plans, and it is quite interesting that a large percentage want to work part-time, often citing physician burnout and lifestyle preferences. We must consider how the next generation wants things to change. We would be quite mistaken to speak about physician burnout and not expect the next generation to make different lifestyle changes. The full-time equivalent staff replacement levels of the graduates are not 1:1. We see that about 30% of the new workforce will want to work less time. With that in mind, the predicted physician workforce gap will actually continue to widen. 6

JULIE BYERLEY

In addition to those good points that Theresa made, I always think about what we're doing in the educational pathway of becoming a physician. Are we making promises, or are we hurting people along the way if the job that is promised does not match what their job truly ends up being?

Previously, Dr Rothberg reminded us that there are many more qualified individuals applying to medical schools in the United States than we are able to admit. Because it is so competitive, applicants can have a checkbox mentality where students do “all the right things” to look good on an application. This makes them become very self-focused throughout their educational journey. These students are plagued by questions such as, “Have I published enough papers? Did I do enough shadowing hours? Am I going to a prestigious-enough undergraduate institution to get into medical school?” By this funnel, we create learners who are ready to elbow everybody else out of the way. Then the promise of medicine is not fulfilled in their eyes.

Our admissions process rewards individual success, while what we need in medicine is collaborative practice. What we reward in the physician workforce now is being a good member of a group who practices in an evidence-based manner and delivers value. It is more focused on following existing algorithms than on creating your own. I think along the way in our pathways, we should be more honest about what the end result for doctoring looks like in American medicine. We would set our learners up to be less disappointed when they begin their careers.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

I think this connects back to what Theresa said before. We could do everything perfectly at every step of the way. We could recruit the perfect people and train them in the perfect way, and yet that still would not yield a greater number of physicians, because the number of physicians is set by the number of residency slots. The fact remains that nobody can become a practicing physician without completing a residency, and we cannot create new residency slots. So, based on that, in some ways this feels like a manufactured shortage.

By contrast, in other professions, if they need more people, they train more people. We as physicians do not. We need a new way of doing business whereby we can expand residency slots outside of Medicare, in which the employers of future physicians would pay for the residency. I do not personally have the solution for how to expand the training opportunities, but if we want more physicians, we need to train more physicians.

LUPE ALONZO-DIAZ

I agree with everything we are discussing. We have talked about institutional barriers, whether they be cost, or slots, or some deviation of that. In addition, I would say that another institutional barrier is our narrow approach to how we select a competitive candidate or a good physician. Socioeconomic disadvantages come into play in a meaningful way because, if a high school student does not have a mentor, or counselor, or access to basic health care, these are all barriers.

For example, several of the medical students we work with have noted a change in what classifies someone as a competitive candidate. Now, it is no longer 200 hours of shadowing or an externship, but rather a gap year that makes a medical school candidate stand out. Well, that sounds wonderful; however, if a student is from a community that is rural, underserved, or underresourced, it is possible that they have never heard of an externship, or frankly, they are wondering about the economics of how to even take a whole year off without pay. This makes it challenging to think about long-term levels of effort vs return on investment. Those conversations start at the kitchen table.

However, if the level of effort vs return on investment appears to be really challenging on top of institutional barriers, such as students feeling pressure to take a gap year, we have to consider a different version of holistic admissions. Each of these pieces are critically important when you consider the various barriers to entry.

KEVIN SMITH

We are getting into some interesting territory here. I agree with Dr Rothberg that we may have manufactured this challenge. But I want to harken back to what Lupe mentioned regarding the different types of shortages. If we're going to matriculate a known number of providers before we fix our GME challenge, how do we then make the shortage of diverse providers within those numbers less pronounced, especially when we know that racial concordance in care of any kind improves outcomes? 7 So, I agree that the upstream barriers and access exposure also contribute to that particular shortage in this discussion. I think you all saw the 2025 entering class data from AAMC. 8 The number of Black men has essentially not changed since 1978, and in 2024, the number of Black applicants increased. These data show that there is not a lack of interest from people wanting to become physicians. So, what is that? Is that the candidate? Is that the evaluator? The point is, we need to shine a floodlight on this reality, and we need to create those upstream opportunities to mitigate whatever the challenge is.

I also appreciate Lupe’s thoughts. At Morehouse School of Medicine, we are creating a pathway from community college to medical school. I am extremely excited about this. The reason I love this approach is 2-fold.

First, if we believe that the shortage is going to be most pronounced in primary care, we do know that if a medical student’s education has involved community college at any time, the student will be more likely to go into primary care. Second, many of the students that we are engaging at the community college level may never have thought they could become a physician, physician assistant, or nurse, for instance. Through this program, we are giving some exposure to those students and creating pathways for access to open some gates.

I’d like to share one last thought about why physicians leave and that is the impact legislation is having on how we practice. I’m thinking now as an OB-GYN, and there are other specialties where this will resonate. Our practice is now being restricted by laws in certain ways.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

Physicians are following the money. We can do as much as we can to try to get people excited about primary care or rural medicine, but the vast majority of people are going to follow the money.

I disagree with Dr Byerley that it’s about celebrating the primary care physician who goes to work in a rural area vs the one who goes to become an urban orthopedist. Pay the primary care doctor twice what you pay the orthopedist, and those celebrations will follow. I guarantee it.

That is really where we are. We have developed a pay system, and people have responded to it. As for me personally, the reason why I am a primary care physician is because I trained at a time when HMOs were taking over, and there was a very short window during the late 1980s and early 1990s when all of a sudden primary care physicians were the gatekeepers to all the other specialties. At that time, there was a real fear among the other specialties that they were going to be unable to get patients. When I was a resident, primary care was reasonably competitive. While I was a resident, my hospital was unable to fill the radiology program. We actually had a lot of preliminary residents who did their internship at my hospital who later went on to other specialties. One year, we had 3 people who were supposed to do anesthesia at a very prominent Boston hospital, and all 3 of them decided that they wanted to do primary care instead. That would not happen today.

So, it is all about how much we are paying and how we reimburse, and if we don't change that, nothing that we change—whether it is about trying to get people excited and whether they went to community colleges or where they live—will change where the majority of physicians choose to practice. Sure, in certain individual instances it could make a difference, but on the whole it will not. The problem will not be solved in that manner.

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

I want to mention something about challenges for potential new entrants. This is somewhat of a projection, but I think most people agree that the new federal student loan limits that are going to go into effect this year as a result of HR-1 will disincentivize certain students from pursuing postsecondary education. The Department of Education is currently pursuing rulemaking to define things, and ultimately, medical students will be able to borrow more than nursing students, but they will remain limited. Graduates from professional programs will now soon be capped at borrowing up to $200,000 in federal student loans. Quite simply, this is not enough to cover medical school in many cases. This is also going to put medical school out of reach for students from underserved and rural backgrounds.

Have We Moved the Needle on the Physician Shortage?

TED O'CONNELL

Thanks, everyone. Let’s move on and discuss how or whether we have moved the needle in the past decade on the physician shortage, specifically regarding the rural vs urban divide and specialties vs primary care. What practice areas are most impacted?

JULIE BYERLEY

Speaking as a pediatrician, the pediatric workforce is challenged more now than it was before. I am sure financial factors drive that significantly. There is a different set of challenges within pediatrics than there is for adult care, in that the subspecialty areas are experiencing shortages to a greater extent than primary care pediatrics. This is a relatively new problem compared with the primary care shortage for adults, as Dr Rothberg described, which has endured for much longer.

Additionally, pediatric subspecialty care often has inherent financial disincentive compared to general pediatric care. In many cases, as with the so-called cognitive specialties or the nonprocedural specialties, the more training a clinician has, the lower their take-home pay actually becomes. This is true, for example, with pediatric infectious disease physicians or pediatric rheumatologists. The same is true in some of the specialties like radiology. A pediatric fellowship might lead to a lower income than stopping at the end of a radiology residency. As a pediatrician, of course, I believe that this is one of the many reflections of our nation’s lack of investment in children. 9,10

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

A promising solution that we have seen is the Rural Residency Planning and Development program at the Health Resources and Services Administration, launched in fiscal year 2019. 11 Although it is relatively small and still a pilot, it provides start-up funding for rural residency programs. It has started 62 new rural residency programs and created 752 approved new residency positions in rural areas. 11 Although these slots still need to be filled by people who want to participate in these residency programs, there is research indicating that rural training or exposure to rural settings during training increases likelihood of practicing in rural areas, 12 even more so than coming from a rural background. Of course, if someone both comes from a rural area and trains in a rural area, the likelihood of that person practicing in a rural area increases further, but rural training is associated with a 5-fold increase in rural practice. 13,14 Being able to provide that seed funding, essentially, to a hospital that wants to start a rural program has been meaningful. Again, it is a fairly new and small program, but the outcomes are there. I think it simply becomes a matter of getting interested graduates into those residency programs.

LUPE ALONZO-DIAZ

We at the California Medical Association are proud of taking this conversation to voters as well. As a note, in 2016, Proposition 56 was passed. That was the cigarette tax, and it provided funding both for Medicaid reimbursement as well as for GME. In 2024, voters also passed Proposition 35, which added additional funding. That was the managed care tax that would have added additional funding for Medicaid reimbursement. So, in the last 8 years, between Prop 56 and Prop 35, almost $340 million has been invested directly into GME programs, and it has supported more than 1000 residents and more than 325 residency programs. Finding these kinds of opportunities is certainly part of the long-term solution. The other thing I'll say is that there has been some discussion among policymakers interested in looking at the long-term viability and financial sustainability for GME programs. So, whether we are looking at GME, Medicaid, or other points, there are challenges across the entire ecosystem.

KEVIN SMITH

I am joining Alexa’s glass-half-full club here. We have done quite a bit of work recently at Morehouse School of Medicine with rural expansion, in some ways taking advantage of federal incentives such as have been described, and in some other ways, we have not yet discussed during this panel. This includes partnering with private hospitals here in southern Georgia. Additionally, we are standing up regional medical campuses in Seattle, Bakersfield, and Chattanooga by partnering with large organizations like CommonSpirit Health, a large, nonprofit Catholic health system formed in Chicago in 2019 with a focus on providing care to vulnerable populations and advancing health equity. As part of those partnerships, we are building GME programs with a focus on primary care. So, I do feel like there’s some hope, to Theresa’s point, to expand that GME exit.

How Specialties Experience the Shortage

TED O'CONNELL

Great. Thank you, all. Some of the responses touched on this a little bit, but do physician shortages in various specialties require the same general approach to mitigation? Or are there differences in approaches and needs by various specialties?

THERESA AZEVEDO-ROUSSO

The approach for workforce development needs to change, and I will use dermatology as an example. Dermatology remains one of the most competitive specialties, with limited program expansion compared with larger fields such as internal medicine, family medicine, psychiatry, and emergency medicine, which continue to add positions at higher rates due to workforce shortages and federal funding priorities. 12 It is very difficult to initiate a dermatology program in the United States, yet the need for dermatology, especially with the aging population, has continued to increase. We need to look at health needs and outcomes, align with workforce development, and include GME as part of the health strategy for all.

JULIE BYERLEY

I agree with Theresa. The different organizing entities need to do what they can. For example, we need innovative pathways that lead to credentialing. The boards are responsible for that in the different disciplines, but the accrediting bodies need to do their part with the expectations for resident and medical student training. They can also encourage innovation in these educational pathways and provide more precise training within those programs.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

We could also be a lot more creative in terms of letting other allied health care professionals do things that physicians do. Take dermatology again, for example. There are high walls around what dermatologists practice. As a result, dermatologists perform a lot of work that does not require such significant training. Injecting varicose veins or Botox does not require years of training. When I think about it, I could say the same about many of the procedures that physicians do. For example, there are gastroenterologists who spend their whole day doing colonoscopies. Performing a colonoscopy does not require 4 years of university education, then 4 years of medical school, 3 years of internal medicine residency, and then maybe 3 years of a gastroenterology fellowship, plus maybe a special endoscopy fellowship. When this is all tallied up, we are looking at something like 14 years of training or more to allow someone to perform a colonoscopy. I think you could probably teach people to perform competent colonoscopies in about 6 months. And the physicians performing colonoscopies are very highly compensated, whereas if the training barrier could be reduced to 6 months, the cost would also decrease, and along with it, the interest in performing these all day. So, as physicians, we have created these barriers that are essentially protectionist, and they keep salaries in the United States high, which is the reason that physicians from all over the world want to come here.

Recruiting and Retaining Physicians

TED O'CONNELL

This has been a very interesting conversation. Let us now look toward the future. Where do we go from here in terms of recruitment and retention strategies or expanding investments in GME to widen that off-ramp as Theresa talked about? Does anyone have any thoughts about extending the health care workforce with advanced practice provider roles?

JULIE BYERLEY

It is my opinion that we need to use this opportunity for disruptive change in health care delivery to really think about how we can ensure competence in our physician workforce, and the workforce of the other health professionals we partner with, to care for people and populations. We need to think differently about needs and means of achieving competence. We also need to allow for innovation all along the way.

LUPE ALONZO-DIAZ

I think everything we have talked about is a part of how we look toward the future. I love what Dr Smith shared with respect to the community college pathway. We also must think through where we can streamline education, whether medical school or GME. Narrative change matters as well. The kitchen table conversations matter and whether individuals and families can see themselves as viable within this pathway as an opportunity. Scholarships and loan repayments are important. Part of the long-term approach will require addressing the financial cost associated with medical education and training. However, scholarships and loan repayments, particularly tied to underresourced communities, are going to be critically important. Also important are increasing funding, creating more positions, and ensuring proper reimbursement. Reimbursement of primary care is an area that deserves additional attention.

ALEXA MCKINLEY ABEL

I echo what everyone else has said. All these potential solutions need to be taken seriously. With specific regard to our rural communities, there are 2 more promising solutions. First, providing rural training opportunities for residents is crucial, whether that involves a full rural residency program or simply rotating in a rural track program. Secondly, of course, simply increasing the number of slots for residencies remains critical generally because we have been locked in at these levels for years, with a couple one-off slot creations. Like Dr Rothberg, I too emphasize the need to look outside of Medicare. Congress is not willing to pass legislation with a price tag that a meaningful number of Medicare GME slots would require. But even if the slots should become available, I really believe we need to get rural students interested. So those pathways or pipelines grow programs within education. I think getting K–12 students interested and being able to upskill them to eventually become a physician is also important.

THERESA AZEVEDO-ROUSSO

A key part of the solution involves focusing on workforce outcomes. We could offer financial incentives to support physicians who train and remain in rural communities as well as to encourage training programs that produce clinicians for these areas. The necessary data already exist. The opportunity lies in how we measure and use those data to align incentives, including directing financial support to training sites that successfully contribute to strengthening the local workforce.

MICHAEL B ROTHBERG

As I mentioned before, we really do not know what the impact of artificial intelligence is going to be on the physician shortage. It could be that it will turn us from having a physician shortage to having a physician surplus. This is certainly how it is playing out with coders right now. If you had asked 10 years ago, the conversation would have been that there would be a shortage of software engineers, and that we would have to hire people to perform these functions. Now suddenly, on the contrary, tech companies are laying people off en masse because the coders can write a lot more code with the support of artificial intelligence. We have not yet seen physicians have a similar capacity to do more work in the same amount of time, but we also have not yet seen physicians have access to the kind of tools that we are about to have via artificial intelligence. So, I think that we will have to see what happens over the next couple of years. It is going to be interesting.

TED O'CONNELL

This has been an inspiring conversation about the physician workforce in the United States. I thank each of you for spending the time with The Permanente Journal and sharing your brilliant thoughts. This was such a great discussion. Thank you.
