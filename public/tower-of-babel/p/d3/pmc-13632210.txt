Value of multiparametric MRI as a potential marker to differentiate membranous nephropathy from IgA nephropathy
Authors: Rongchao Shi, Hao Wang, Min Li, KaiXin Li, Huai Yang, JinXia Zhu, Bernd Kuehn, Marcel Dominik Nickel, Dawei Yang, Weikang Guo, Hui Xu, Zhenghan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMembranous nephropathy (MN) and IgA nephropathy (IgAN) exhibit similar clinical symptoms but differ substantially in treatment strategies and prognoses, highlighting the need for a non-invasive and precise diagnostic method. Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) has shown great potential for the non-invasive assessment and quantification of renal function. This study aims to explore the potential value of mpMRI in distinguishing between MN and IgAN.MethodsCortical renal blood flow (cRBF), cortical true diffusion coefficient (cD), cortical pseudo-diffusion coefficient (cD*), cortical perfusion fraction (cf), cortical R2* (cR2*), cortical T1 mapping (cT1), cortical mean diffusivity (cMD), and cortical mean kurtosis (cMK) were prospectively measured in 58 patients with MN, 47 patients with IgAN, and 75 healthy controls. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare MRI parameters among the three groups. The correlations between laboratory data and MRI parameters were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. Logistic regression analysis was performed to construct diagnostic models. The diagnostic performances of models for differentiating MN and IgAN were assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves with area under the curve (AUC).ResultsCompared with the IgAN group, patients with MN were older and had higher cRBF, cf, and cT1 values (all P < 0.050), but cMK value was significantly lower than that in IgAN (P = 0.009). In the MN group, a significant correlation was observed between eGFR and cf. (r = 0.61, P < 0.001), whereas in the IgAN group, eGFR showed a significant correlation with cRBF (r = 0.51, P < 0.001). Age, eGFR, 24 h urinary protein, and cMK demonstrated the ability to distinguish between MN and IgAN in both univariable and multivariate logistic regression analysis and were used to construct diagnostic models. Among the multiparametric models, the combination model incorporating age, eGFR, 24 h urinary protein, and cMK exhibited the highest AUC of 0.92 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.87–0.97), with a sensitivity of 0.91 (0.81–0.96) and a specificity of 0.81 (0.67–0.90).ConclusionAn integrated model combining mpMRI and clinical variables provides a non-invasive method for differentiating MN from IgAN with high diagnostic accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02516-8.Keywords: Membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Kidney diseases represent a significant global public health challenge, with approximately 10% of the worldwide population affected by chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2]. Epidemiological investigations have shown that membranous nephropathy (MN) and IgA nephropathy (IgAN) are the predominant types of primary glomerular disease in adults [3]. Both diseases present with similar clinical symptoms, such as proteinuria, hematuria, and renal dysfunction, etc., and there is some overlap in pathological manifestations. However, their treatment strategies and prognoses differ substantially. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [4], patients with MN are advised to receive steroids combined with immunosuppressants (recommendation level: 1B). In contrast, steroids should be considered for high-risk IgAN (recommendation level: 2B), while the use of immunosuppressants remains controversial. Accurate differentiation between these two diseases is critical for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes.

Currently, renal tissue biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing both diseases. However, the invasive nature of biopsy may lead to complications such as bleeding and infection. And some patients cannot tolerate the procedure due to renal dysfunction or coagulation disorders [5]. Meanwhile, the non-invasive diagnostic methods for MN and IgAN still have prominent limitations. Ultrasound and imaging radiomics methods have been used to distinguish between these two diseases, but they exhibit suboptimal diagnostic efficacy and cannot dynamically reflect disease-specific pathophysiological processes, including renal functional changes in microstructure, blood perfusion, and tissue oxygenation [6, 7]. Therefore, there is an urgent and important clinical need to develop a non-invasive and precise diagnostic method.

In recent years, non-contrast multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) technology has demonstrated considerable potential for non-invasively assessing and quantifying functional and pathophysiological changes in CKD [8–10]. It may provide comprehensive information on renal artery blood flow (RBF), microcirculation perfusion, renal tissue oxygenation, renal microstructure (renal fibrosis), and renal macrostructure (renal length etc.). Each included MRI modality targets distinct renal pathophysiological characteristics, providing synergistic information to support differential diagnosis of glomerular diseases.

Renal perfusion, both total RBF and local microcirculation, represents a major factor for the oxygen supply of renal tissues. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is an attractive non-invasive approach for RBF measurement and can quantify RBF. ASL has been proven to be a reproducible and accurate technique for evaluating tissue blood perfusion [11]. Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) can simultaneously assess tissue microcirculation perfusion and water molecule diffusion [12]. Its parameters include true diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f). The D value represents pure molecular diffusion by minimizing the influence of blood flow on tissue diffusion. The D* value reflects the renal vascular blood flow and tubular fluid velocity. The f value is the ratio of vascular and tubular fluid volume to the total fluid within the tissue [13].

Insufficient renal perfusion promotes renal hypoxia and tissue damage. Renal tissue hypoxia is an important factor accelerating the progression of CKD. Blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) MRI uses the magnetism of deoxyhemoglobin to dynamically assess tissue oxygenation status [14]. The cortical relaxation rate R2* derived from BOLD MRI can quantitatively reflect the degree of renal tissue hypoxia [15], which is an important factor accelerating CKD progression [16].

Hypoxic damage of renal tissue triggers inflammatory and fibrotic responses, leading to structural changes in the tissue. Renal fibrosis represents the common pathway and outcome of nearly all forms of CKD. Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) and T1 mapping hold promise for the non-invasive evaluation of changes in renal tissue microstructure. As a non-Gaussian diffusion model, DKI quantifies the complexity of renal tissue structure via mean diffusivity (MD) and mean kurtosis (MK) parameters, capturing subtle structural abnormalities beyond conventional Gaussian diffusion detection [17]. T1 mapping is sensitive to alterations in the physicochemical microenvironment of renal tissue and can quantitatively assess the severity of renal fibrosis [18]. Collectively, the combination of ASL, IVIM, BOLD MRI, DKI, and T1 mapping enables systematic evaluation of the entire pathological cascade of glomerular diseases, covering perfusion, oxygenation, inflammation, and fibrosis. This multi-dimensional information makes integrated mpMRI far more comprehensive than single-sequence imaging for distinguishing the subtle pathophysiological differences between MN and IgAN.

In this kidney mpMRI study, we explored the value of mpMRI in MN and IgAN, analyzing the differences in mpMRI characteristics of both diseases and assessing the correlation between MRI parameters and conventional biomarkers of kidney damage. Furthermore, we combined quantitative mpMRI features with clinical indicators to construct a differential diagnostic model for distinguishing MN from IgAN, providing a non-invasive and precise diagnostic method for two common primary glomerular diseases.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

We consecutively recruited 58 patients with MN and 47 patients with IgAN diagnosed by renal biopsy from the Department of Nephrology of our hospital. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adults with a diagnosis of CKD based on the KDIGO 2021 guidelines [19]. (2) all patients who had undergone renal biopsy and whose pathological results confirmed MN or IgAN. (3) patients undergoing clinical assessments, laboratory examinations, and abdominal MRI. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-MN and non-IgAN patients; (2) no abdominal MRI examination; (3) heavy motion artifacts on images.

In addition, 75 healthy volunteers were recruited as the healthy control (HC) group, without kidney diseases. Details of the study participants are presented in Fig. 1.

Demographic data and laboratory tests

For all patients, we assessed their clinical presentation during hospitalization through medical records review, and recorded sex, age, history of hypertension and diabetes. Laboratory examinations were obtained within one week before or after the MRI examination, including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hemoglobin, urea, serum creatinine (Scr), uric acid, and 24 h urinary protein. The eGFR was calculated based on the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration’s equation [20].

MRI protocols

The MRI examinations were performed on a 3 T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthineers AG, Forchheim, Germany) using an 18-channel body array coil. All participants underwent standard MRI safety screening prior to scanning. To improve image quality and reduce motion-related artifacts, breathing training was provided to all participants before the scanning.

T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) was acquired using the half-fourier-acquired single-shot turbo spin echo with the following parameters: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE): 800 ms/91 ms; matrix: 320 × 320; slice-thickness: 5.0 mm; field-of-view (FOV): 360 × 360 mm.

T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) was performed using volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) with the following parameters: TR/TE: 3.97 ms/2.49 ms; matrix: 260 × 320; slice-thickness: 3.0 mm; FOV: 325 × 400 mm.

R2* imaging using the VIBE Dixon had the following parameters: TR/TE: 9.05 ms/1.05 ms; matrix: 160 × 111; slice-thickness: 3 mm; FOV: 393 × 450 mm. R2* maps were automatically generated via pixel-by-pixel fitting.

IVIM was performed using respiratory-triggered spin-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) with monopolar diffusion gradients. The specific parameters are as follows: TR/TE: 2000 ms/55 ms; matrix: 128 × 58; slice-thickness: 4 mm; FOV: 200 × 400 mm; b values: 0, 20, 40, 50, 70, 100, 150, 800 s/mm2. The parameters D, D*, and f were calculated using software Body-DiffusionLab (BoDilab, Chengdu ZhongYing Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) [21].

DKI was obtained using respiratory-triggered spin EPI with monopolar diffusion gradients with the following parameters: TR/TE: 2000 ms/55 ms; matrix: 134 × 68; slice-thickness: 4 mm; FOV: 200 × 400 mm; b values: 0, 200, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000 s/mm2, with 3 diffusion directions at each non-zero b value and b0 images. DKI metrics were calculated using the standard kurtosis model implemented in software, as for IVIM [21].

T1 mapping was acquired using the Look-Locker sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE: 3.00 ms/1.32 ms; matrix: 192 × 110; slice-thickness: 6 mm; FOV: 276 × 384 mm. T1 maps were generated inline immediately after acquisition.

Renal perfusion imaging was performed using a prototype, free-breathing, three-dimensional turbo gradient spin echo sequence with the pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling scheme. The specific parameters are as follows: TR/ TE: 5590 ms/19.28 ms; matrix: 32 × 64; slice-thickness: 5.0 mm; FOV: 150 × 300 mm. Quantitative RBF maps derived from pseudo-continuous ASL were generated inline based on a formula that has been used in previous studies [22, 23].

T1WI and R2* images were acquired in the axial plane; all other sequences were acquired coronally. The total scan time for the full sequence was 22 min 54 s. The detailed MRI protocols were shown in Supplemental material S1.

Imaging analysis

All image quality assessment and region of interest (ROI) delineations were independently performed by two radiologists with 20 years (H.X.) and 4 years (RC.S.) of experience in abdominal imaging, who were blinded to the clinical information, laboratory results, pathological results and patient group allocation. Image quality was rated according to a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = non-diagnostic, 2 = poor, 3 = moderate, 4 = good, 5 = excellent) [24, 25]. Two radiologists delineated the bilateral renal ROI at the largest level through the renal hilum on every key sequence, using the T2WI anatomical images as a reference. One cortical ROI (70–110 voxels) covering the entire renal cortex for each kidney was drawn, avoiding vascular structures, hemorrhage, or cysts [26, 27]. The placement of cortical ROI was shown in Fig. 2. The MRI parameter values were obtained by averaging the values of the right and left kidneys, because eGFR represents the overall function of both kidneys.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10.0 were used for statistical analysis. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to evaluate the interobserver reproducibility of the MRI parameters. The normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were compared using Student’s t‑test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed, shown as median and quartiles. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare MRI parameters among HC, MN, and IgAN. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to determine the association between MRI parameters and clinical data. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to filter variables. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed on all included independent variables in the final model to assess the stability of the model estimates. The indicators included variance inflation factor (VIF), tolerance, condition index and variance proportions. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses with the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the diagnostic performance, and the AUCs were compared by DeLong’s test. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. The detailed Statistical analysis is shown in Supplemental material S2.

Results

Clinical characteristics

In total, 58 patients with MN, 47 patients with IgAN and 75 healthy individuals were included in this study. Patients with MN were significantly older than those with IgAN and the HC group (all P < 0.050). The median age was the same between the IgAN and HC groups (P > 0.999). There was no significant difference in sex (χ2 = 4.70, P = 0.095) among the three groups. The MN group had relatively preserved or supranormal eGFR and higher 24 h urinary protein levels than those of IgAN group (P = 0.004 and P < 0.001, respectively). Compared to the IgAN group, the MN group had lower levels of urea and Scr (P = 0.034 and P < 0.001, respectively). The demographic and clinical data of the study population are shown in Table 1.

In univariate analysis, age, sex, eGFR, hemoglobin, urea, Scr, uric acid, 24 h urinary protein, cRBF, cD, cD*, cf, cR2*, cT1, cMD, and cMK were included. Variates with P < 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression. Diagnostic efficacy in differentiating MN and IgAN

Table 4 and Fig. 5 showed the diagnostic efficacy of the ROC curves in differentiating MN and IgAN. AUC values ranged from 0.66 to 0.92, with 24 h urinary protein exhibiting a high AUC of 0.80 (0.71–0.88) and a specificity of 0.76 (0.63–0.85) in single-parameter model. In multiparametric models, the combination model of age, eGFR, 24 h urinary protein, and cMK exhibited the highest AUC of 0.92 (0.87–0.97), with higher sensitivity of 0.91 (0.81–0.96) and specificity of 0.81 (0.67–0.90). The AUC values of multiparametric models outperformed single-parameter models, such as combination model of age, eGFR, 24 h urinary protein, and cMK versus single age model (Z = 5.00, P < 0.001), single 24 h urinary protein model (Z = 3.36, P < 0.001), single eGFR model (Z = 4.80, P < 0.001), and single 1/cMK model (Z = 4.70, P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the utility of mpMRI, including RBF, IVIM, R2*, T1 mapping, and DKI for non-invasively distinguishing MN from IgAN. Our main findings demonstrated that the integrated model combining clinical data (age, eGFR, and 24 h urinary protein) with MRI parameter (cMK) achieved satisfactory diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.92). Furthermore, the correlations between MRI parameters and laboratory data exhibited distinct patterns between the two diseases, reflecting their differing underlying pathophysiologies. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate clinical data and a comprehensive set of functional MRI parameters for the differential diagnosis of these two common glomerulopathies.

Glomerulopathy can be classified according to demographic characteristics (such as age and sex) and clinical manifestations (such as eGFR and urinary protein) [28]. In this study, MN primarily affected middle and elderly individuals, manifesting as preserved or supranormal eGFR and heavy proteinuria. In contrast, IgAN occurred frequently in young and middle-aged people. The clinical features were characterized by asymptomatic haematuria with urinary protein that generally did not exceed 2.0 g/24 h. These baseline clinical features were consistent with previous literature [6, 28], validating the representativeness of the enrolled cohort and laying a reliable foundation for subsequent imaging pathophysiological analysis.

The different MRI parameters reflected the distinct histopathological states of MN and IgAN. Renal perfusion and circulation play a crucial role in the delivery of nutrients and oxygen to renal tissue. Decreased renal perfusion (lower cRBF and cf values) was observed exclusively in patients with IgAN, while patients with MN exhibited perfusion status comparable to healthy controls. These findings align with their distinct pathological features. MN primarily involves the deposited immune complexes outside the glomerular basement membrane, and the glomerular capillary loops were normal or had little influence on blood perfusion [7, 29, 30]. In contrast, IgAN features predominant mesangial IgA deposition, frequently accompanied by mesangial hypercellularity and matrix expansion [6, 31], leading to compression of blood vessels and impaired microvascular perfusion [32]. The cf results were consistent with those of cRBF. We hypothesize that the mechanisms underlying these changes are similar to those of cRBF, which reflect damage to renal function. These results were similar to the findings of previous studies in CKD [22, 26]. There were no statistically significant differences in D and D* values among the three groups. The insensitivity is consistent with previous studies, which fail to capture early and subtle renal functional changes [33–35]. Further large-cohort studies are needed to determine the clinical utility of D and D*.

When RBF and microcirculation perfusion decrease, the renal tissue oxygenation state will deteriorate. An increase in the R2∗value corresponds to a decrease in oxyhemoglobin, a decrease in partial pressure of oxygen, and tissue hypoxia. Compared with HC, increased cR2* values were detected in both disease groups, which was consistent with previous research [15]. However, there was no statistical difference between MN and IgAN. It is possible that the two groups of patients at different disease stages have compensatory mechanisms, resulting in a similar degree of overall chronic hypoxia in renal tissue. Lang et al. showed that R2* value was not sensitive to IgAN [36].

Renal tissue hypoxia serves as a key driver of chronic kidney disease progression, while renal fibrosis constitutes the final common pathway and outcome across nearly all forms of CKD. Elevated cT1 values in both MN and IgAN groups confirmed widespread renal parenchymal injury in patients with glomerulopathy [23, 37], yet cT1 failed to differentiate the two diseases, likely owing to similar degrees of interstitial damage and renal function impairment in the enrolled patients [38, 39]. In comparison, cMK emerged as the most robust differential biomarker in this study. MD reflects the average magnitude of water diffusion, which can be influenced by opposing factors such as cellular swelling (decreasing MD) and interstitial edema (increasing MD). In contrast, MK is sensitive to the deviation of water diffusion from Gaussian behavior, which is driven by the spatial complexity of barriers. The heterogeneous and segmental lesions (mesangial proliferation, focal sclerosis) in IgAN create a more complex microenvironment compared with the uniform and diffuse thickening of the basement membrane in MN, resulting in higher cMK levels in IgAN [27]. This inherent pathological difference fundamentally supports the superior diagnostic efficacy of cMK, highlighting its unique value in reflecting microstructural disorder in glomerular diseases.

As the disease progresses and fibrosis worsens, the kidney volume and length decrease, which is more pronounced in patients with severe renal impairment. There were no statistically significant differences in renal length among the three groups. The small sample size may explain this finding, especially in patients with moderate-to-severe, which also suggests that renal macrostructure or morphology may have limited value in the early stages of disease.

Notably, mpMRI-clinical correlation signatures further distinguished the two diseases and revealed their distinct injury mechanisms. In MN, cRBF, cD, cf, and cMD positively correlated with eGFR, while cT1 and cMK negatively correlated, reflecting a chronic fibrotic process driven by podocyte injury. In IgAN, cRBF correlated with eGFR/Scr and cMD with hemoglobin, indicating that reduced renal perfusion due to mesangial proliferation is the core mechanism. These findings demonstrate that mpMRI serves as a non-invasive imaging biomarker complementary to conventional laboratory measures for assessing renal microstructural changes and functional impairment.

ROC analysis confirmed that integrating clinical variables with mpMRI parameters, particularly cMK, yielded superior diagnostic performance compared with single-parameter models. This multi-model strategy substantially improves the accuracy of non-invasive differentiation between MN and IgAN. This integrated approach has the potential to serve as a non-invasive complementary tool for renal biopsy rather than a replacement.

The clinical implications of our findings are noteworthy. MpMRI serves as a valuable non-invasive complementary tool to differentiate MN from IgAN, particularly in cases where biopsy is contraindicated, declined invasive procedures by the patient, or requires repeated assessments during follow-up.

However, our study had several limitations. First, the single-center design and small sample size may limit generalizability. Second, external validation is lacking. The stability and universality of the multi-modal model across different populations and MRI equipment remain to be verified. Third, manual ROI delineation inevitably introduces subjective bias, despite being performed by experienced radiologists. Finally, due to the limited sample size, subgroup analysis based on histopathological grading was not feasible. Future multi-center, large-sample, prospective studies with automated segmentation technology and pathological analysis are warranted to refine and validate the clinical value of this diagnostic model.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that mpMRI (cMK), particularly when combined with key clinical variables, can effectively discriminate MN from IgAN. An integrated imaging‑clinical model has the potential to serve as a non-invasive biomarker for differentiating main primary glomerular diseases. #
