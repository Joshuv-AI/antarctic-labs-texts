From zero-COVID to global alignment: transnational pressures and China’s pandemic communication transformation
Authors: Dandan Liu, Siti Ezaleila Binti Mustafa, Nor Zaliza Binti Sarmiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health crises are governed not only through policies but also through talk. Government press conferences are ritualized arenas where authorities construct meaning, claim competence, and manage domestic and international legitimacy. China’s abrupt transition from “zero‑COVID” to a strategy of coexistence provides a critical case for examining how transnational pressures—from the World Health Organization, diplomatic partners, markets, and global media—shape official communication over time.Main bodyThis study analyzes 154 central government press‑conference transcripts (February 2020–February 2023) using a mixed‑methods design that combines topic modeling with qualitative frame analysis and process tracing of international pressure events. We segment the period into four phases—International Scrutiny, Global Cooperation, International Isolation, and Global Alignment—and identify seven recurring frames spanning health‑system capacity, epidemiological standards, vaccine diplomacy, economic–health trade‑offs, supply‑chain interdependence, and policy adaptation. Event‑timing analysis shows a consistent lag of roughly 7–21 days between major international cues and subsequent adjustments in domestic frames, with the economic–health and policy‑adaptation frames most responsive. A micro‑level discourse analysis demonstrates “semantic governance”: lexical substitutions (“optimization,” “new phase”) and contextual recoding that converted a substantive policy reversal into a narrative of adaptive improvement. We argue that authorities achieved discursive alignment with evolving global norms without immediate policy convergence, illustrating how sovereignty sensitivities are managed communicatively. The findings also reveal equity‑relevant mechanisms: semantic smoothing that stabilizes compliance can under‑specify risks for vulnerable groups during transition windows, and generic references to “key populations” can displace time‑bound commitments to protection and access. Building on these insights, we propose two practical tools for global health governance: (1) an equity checkpoint for each policy pivot (plain‑language risk summaries, service guarantees, and a short equity note), and (2) a discursive alignment dashboard that tracks lead–lag to international guidance, domain‑specific alignment, and semantic markers of convergence or divergence.ConclusionsPandemic communication in China followed a cyclical frame‑reinforcement pattern rather than a linear arc, and relied on semantic governance to manage rapid policy change under transnational pressure. Recognizing and monitoring these communicative mechanisms can strengthen global health governance and reduce equity risks during future protracted emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12992-025-01155-y.Keywords: Crisis communication, Frame analysis, Government communication, COVID-19, Semantic governance

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has crystallized how deeply public health is entwined with the accelerating forces of globalization. International travel, transnational supply chains, and real-time digital media amplified viral transmission, information diffusion, and political pressure in ways unimaginable during earlier outbreaks. Governments were forced to manage not only the biomedical crisis but also its economic, diplomatic, and reputational spill-overs in the global arena.

In contemporary crisis governance, governments lead not only through decisions but also through talk. High-stakes press conferences are ritualized arenas where leaders construct meaning, signal competence, and (re)claim legitimacy under conditions of urgency and uncertainty [8]. In pandemic “mega-crises,” these briefings become focal sites for sense-making and coordination in the face of prolonged turbulence (Boin, McConnell, & ’t Hart [6], Boin, ’t Hart, Stern, & Sundelius [5].

A core dynamic in such moments is the framing contest—public struggles to define the problem, attribute responsibility, and justify policy choices (Boin, ’t Hart, & McConnell [4]). Frames offered at the podium can shape both political fortunes and policy trajectories by organizing how threats, trade-offs, and consequences are understood [4, 6]. Framing fundamentally involves selecting certain aspects of reality and making them more salient to define problems, diagnose causes, make moral judgments, and propose remedies [12]. In health emergencies, frames anchored in metaphors (“war on the virus”), risk statistics, or moral appeals (“protect the vulnerable”) significantly influence how citizens interpret both the severity of the crisis and the viability of proposed solutions [7].

While crisis communication research has underscored the importance of meaning-making and reputation management [8], two gaps remain. First, we know less about how officials strategically deploy framing devices in press conferences to justify contested policy moves (e.g., shifts in restrictions or surveillance) and to negotiate accountability. Second, we have limited understanding of how these rhetorical strategies intersect with institutional legacies and capacities—for example, how privacy regimes, governance capacity, and path dependencies condition what frames are plausible and persuasive [22, 23]. Situating press-conference rhetoric within the broader politics of crisis management [5] helps connect communication practices to the political and policy consequences they seek to produce.

China represents a critical case for three reasons. First, the country was the initial epicenter and remained under the spotlight of international scrutiny throughout the outbreak. Second, its strong state apparatus offers a contrasting communication model to liberal democracies, enabling an evaluation of how authoritarian information control mediates global pressures. Third, the abrupt transition from “zero-COVID” to a strategy of “living with the virus” in late 2022 illustrates how global interdependence can generate rapid discursive and policy shifts even in highly centralized systems.

Against this backdrop, our study examines three interconnected questions: RQ1. What frames dominate China’s COVID‑19 government press conferences across the four pandemic phases (2020–2023)? RQ2. How do these frames evolve across phases in response to changing circumstances? RQ3. How are international pressure events (e.g., WHO guidance, diplomatic/economic pressures) associated with subsequent frame shifts, and what communicative mechanisms appear to mediate those shifts?

We answer these questions with a mixed-methods design that treats China’s central government press conferences (2020–2023) as a primary-source corpus. Topic modeling identifies evolving thematic emphases, and qualitative frame analysis recovers the rhetorical devices that numeric models miss (metaphor, moral evaluation, problem/solution coupling). In doing so, the article speaks to and extends three bodies of work: (i) the politics of crisis management and crisis exploitation; (ii) crisis communication and meaning-making; and (iii) governance learning in mega-crises.

Literature review

Global health governance and transnational norms

Global health governance refers to the constellation of international institutions, financing mechanisms and legal frameworks that coordinate collective action against cross-border health threats. The International Health Regulations (IHR 2005) legally bind 196 states to report outbreaks and implement containment measures, yet the enforcement of IHR obligations depends heavily on domestic political will and bureaucratic capacity [14]. During COVID-19, the WHO moved from endorsing aggressive containment to recommending calibrated reopening, illustrating how global norms themselves evolve in response to new evidence and political bargaining. Transnational financing further shapes national responses. Emergency lending by the World Bank, International Monetary Fund and Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank compelled recipient states to align policy narratives with donor expectations on transparency, vaccination equity and socio-economic mitigation [26]. For middle-income powers such as China, the reputational incentives of portraying compliance with international standards may rival direct financial considerations.

Globalisation pressures on national discourse

Finnemore and Sikkink’s Norm Life-cycle Model outlines how ideas progress from emergence to cascade and, eventually, internalisation within domestic structures [16]. However, authoritarian reception of global norms remains contested. Advocates of socialisation argue that even illiberal states internalise norms to maintain international legitimacy, while sceptics emphasise “norm localisation,” whereby foreign elements are selectively grafted onto indigenous ideologies [1]. COVID-19 offers a real-time laboratory for observing such dynamics. China’s early adoption of mass lockdowns paralleled WHO guidance on “non-pharmaceutical interventions,” yet its prolonged zero-COVID stance diverged from many peers by 2021. When the WHO shifted towards endemic management in 2022, China rapidly re-articulated its domestic narrative, replacing “elimination” with “optimisation” and “Downgrade disease classification.” These discursive pivots suggest a form of selective convergence driven by shifting cost–benefit calculations in trade, investment and diplomatic realms.

Crisis communication and framing theory

Traditional crisis communication scholarship has been dominated by linear lifecycle models that assume sequential progression from crisis detection through response to recovery [15, 25]. However, Coombs’ [10] Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) introduced a more nuanced approach by emphasizing the role of crisis responsibility attribution in determining appropriate response strategies. SCCT categorizes crises into three types—victim crises, accidental crises, and preventable crises—and suggests that organizations should match their communication strategies to the level of attributed responsibility [9].

Recent scholarship has extended SCCT to address the challenges of digital communication environments. Zhong and Huang [29] proposed a Social-Mediated Crisis Communication (SMCC) model that adapts SCCT principles to social media contexts, recognizing the central role of media platforms in contemporary crisis communication. This evolution reflects the growing recognition that traditional crisis communication models require adaptation to account for the rapid, multi-directional information flows characteristic of digital media environments.

COVID-19 represents a mega-crisis—a prolonged, system-wide event spanning sectors and jurisdictions, with recurring waves that continuously redefine the problem [6]. This classification establishes scope and duration as boundary conditions for generalizing findings to similar protracted emergencies.

Crisis communication as meaning‑making and reputation work

Press conferences occupy a central place in crisis governance: they are the routine interface where leaders frame what is happening, justify measures, and seek compliance, trust, and coordination [8]. From a public administration perspective, effective crisis communication fuses governance capacity (analytical, regulatory, coordination, and delivery) with governance legitimacy (citizen evaluations of performance and process), and mobilizes reputational resources to sustain both [8]. In mega‑crises, this meaning‑making is iterative: governments recalibrate narratives as epidemiology shifts, policies are contested, and fatigue sets in [6].

Communication scholars conceptualise frames as organising principles that simplify and prioritise certain reality elements while relegating others [12]. Four generic functions—problem definition, causal attribution, moral evaluation and remedy prescription—guide how audiences interpret and respond to crises. In health emergencies, frames are often anchored in metaphors (“war on the virus”), risk statistics or moral appeals (“protect the vulnerable”). Prior work shows war metaphors can mobilise rapid compliance but may backfire if the promised victory is delayed, leading to fatigue and distrust [2]. Empirical studies on Zika, Ebola and COVID-19 additionally demonstrate that frame resonance fluctuates with crisis stage. Early phases reward loss-oriented and authority-centred frames, whereas protracted crises benefit from gain-framed and community-empowerment narratives [24].

Communication is not merely informational; it is strategic. Classic work on crisis exploitation shows how governments and opponents vie to impose causal stories, diffuse or focus blame, and advance preferred policy lines (Boin, ’t Hart, & McConnell [4]. In press events, such contests surface as shifts between exogenous framings (“an external shock”) and endogenous framings (“correctable policy failure”), with consequences for public support and policy adjustment. In mega‑crises—where uncertainty is prolonged—these contests recur across waves rather than ending after an acute phase [6].

Legitimacy‑building strategies in crisis talk

Recent comparative research adds micro‑foundations to crisis rhetoric, showing that governments blend pragmatic (self‑interest/utility), moral (altruism/appropriateness), and cognitive (inevitability/taken‑for‑grantedness) appeals to legitimate intrusive measures; the mix varies by historical legacies and overarching crisis strategies [22]. Legitimacy work is not done on a blank slate: path dependency and privacy regime legacies shape what is sayable and credible, while the chosen crisis strategy (e.g., suppression vs. liberal risk management) further steers rhetorical choices [22]. In short, press‑conference rhetoric is both situated (by institutions and strategies) and instrumental (deployed to mobilize compliance and manage controversy).

Much crisis communication scholarship still implies a linear progression (pre‑crisis → response → recovery). Yet mega‑crises exhibit recursive meaning‑making, where frames cycle back, are intensified, or are re‑deployed as conditions change [6]. This invites analyses that trace rhetorical cycles across multiple waves rather than single‑moment snapshots—precisely where a longitudinal press‑conference corpus can add value.

Research gap and contribution Empirically, we still know comparatively little about how governments sequence and recycle frames across long crisis horizons in the official channel most citizens watch: press briefings. Theoretically, we lack integrated accounts that connect (1) framing contests and blame/credit management, (2) legitimacy‑building strategy mixes, and (3) capacity–legitimacy trade‑offs visible in day‑to‑day podium talk. By analyzing press conferences over multiple phases, we speak directly to crisis management [6], framing/contestation [4], and crisis communication/meaning‑making literatures [8].

Conversely, protracted crises outside public health (e.g., creeping security or climate emergencies) may display similar recursive dynamics and framing contests [6]; Boin et al., [4].

Methodology

Research design and theoretical framework

Our aim is to explain how international signals—especially WHO guidance and wider transnational pressures—are associated with short‑lag reframing in China’s central press conferences, and to identify the communicative mechanisms that mediate these shifts. This study employs a longitudinal mixed-methods design, combining topic modeling and framing analysis, to examine how transnational pressures from global health governance, international media scrutiny, and economic interdependence shaped China’s pandemic communication from February 2020 to February 2023. The approach integrates computational text analysis with framing theory [12], situating pandemic discourse at the intersection of globalization processes and domestic crisis management [20, 21]. It aligns with crisis phase models [11] to track frame evolution, focusing on how global expectations and international constraints influenced the strategic adaptation of official communication frames.

To operationalize the theoretical concept of semantic governance, the study includes targeted qualitative deep-dive analysis of selected press conferences, examining specific linguistic transformations that enabled policy transitions while maintaining narrative coherence. This micro-level discourse analysis focuses on lexical substitution patterns, contextual reframing of identical terminology, and temporal bridging mechanisms that demonstrate how controlled discourse environments facilitate policy reversal without explicit acknowledgment of failure.

Data collection and corpus construction

The study corpus comprises 154 official press conference transcripts from China’s State Council Joint Prevention and Control Mechanism, selected based on pandemic-related keywords in their titles to ensure COVID-19 relevance. This sampling approach guarantees the dataset’s representativeness of China’s pandemic communication. These transcripts were systematically collected from the official Chinese government website (Gov.cn) using automated extraction protocols that ensured comprehensive coverage. The selection of central government communications reflects the study’s focus on how national-level authorities navigate transnational pressures in global health contexts, as opposed to local implementation dynamics focuses on national-level communications to capture transnational influence, rather than local-level messaging.

The corpus construction strategy prioritized institutional consistency and policy authority by focusing exclusively on the State Council Joint Prevention and Control Mechanism, which served as China’s primary coordination body for pandemic-related communications and international engagement. This institutional focus ensures analytical coherence while capturing communications most directly influenced by transnational pressures, including international criticism of early pandemic-related communications, global vaccine distribution negotiations, and international economic recovery coordination.

The resulting dataset comprises approximately 1.69 million Chinese characters, equivalent to 0.9 million word tokens following segmentation, providing substantial textual depth for computational analysis. Temporal coverage spans from February 2020 through February 2023, encompassing the full scope of China’s pandemic-related communications from initial outbreak management through policy normalization. This timeframe captures critical periods of international engagement and policy adjustment, including early international criticism and transparency pressures, global vaccine development competition, international travel restriction negotiations, and eventual alignment with global endemic management approaches. The comprehensive temporal scope enables analysis of how sustained transnational pressures influenced long-term communication strategy evolution rather than merely short-term tactical adjustments.

Data collection protocols incorporated quality assurance measures to ensure textual accuracy and completeness. Automated extraction targeted official statements, expert responses, and journalist questions while excluding non-textual elements that might introduce analytical noise. The extraction process maintained respectful crawling frequencies in accordance with website usage policies while ensuring comprehensive coverage of all available transcripts. Post-collection verification procedures confirmed that the corpus represents a complete census of central government COVID-19 communications during the study period, providing maximum representativeness for analyzing official discourse evolution.

Ethical considerations and data availability

All data analyzed in this study consists of publicly available government communications, eliminating privacy concerns and ensuring compliance with research ethics standards. The press conference transcripts represent official government statements intended for public dissemination, with no personal or sensitive information requiring protection. The study methodology focuses exclusively on publicly available textual content, with no human subjects involvement or private data collection.

Data collection procedures adhered to website usage policies and maintained respectful crawling frequencies to avoid disrupting official government websites. The automated extraction protocols incorporated appropriate delays and access limitations to ensure responsible data collection practices. All extracted content remains publicly accessible through official government channels, with the study corpus serving as a systematic compilation rather than creating new data accessibility issues.

Phase-specific transnational pressure response patterns

The analysis reveals systematic frame evolution across four phases, each characterized by distinct transnational pressure dynamics and corresponding communication adaptations that reflect China’s strategic navigation between domestic legitimacy and international expectations. The longitudinal topic evolution patterns are illustrated in Appendix A, while detailed keyword evolution analysis and policy correlation patterns are provided in Appendix B.

International Scrutiny Phase (January-March 2020): Healthcare System Resilience and Global Epidemiological Standards dominated as China faced unprecedented international criticism regarding transparency and accountability in global health emergency response. Communication strategy emphasized demonstrating state capacity to international observers while establishing data reporting systems aligned with WHO expectations. The government deployed 35 press conferences during this phase, representing the highest communication intensity, as authorities sought to counter international criticism while maintaining domestic crisis management credibility. Frame deployment during this phase reflected classic sovereignty protection strategies under international pressure. Healthcare System Resilience messaging (45% of communication content) emphasized medical system capacity and professional competence to counter international criticism of initial response delays. Global Epidemiological Standards messaging (25%) focused on establishing transparent reporting mechanisms aligned with international surveillance expectations, demonstrating China’s integration with global health governance frameworks despite sovereignty concerns.

Global Cooperation Phase (April 2020-February 2021): Vaccine Diplomacy Balance and Economic-Health Nexus emerged prominently as China positioned itself as a responsible global health actor while maintaining domestic policy autonomy. This phase encompassed 42 press conferences emphasizing China’s contributions to global vaccine development and distribution networks. Communication strategy balanced international cooperation messaging with technological sovereignty assertions, reflecting strategic navigation between global integration and domestic legitimacy requirements. Vaccine Diplomacy Balance messaging (35%) emphasized China’s scientific achievements and international cooperation contributions, positioning domestic vaccine development as both national success and global public good. Economic-Health Nexus messaging (30%) integrated pandemic response with global economic recovery narratives, acknowledging economic interdependence while emphasizing China’s role in global supply chain stability. This dual emphasis reflects sophisticated understanding of how domestic health policies intersect with global economic systems.

International Isolation Phase (March 2021-November 2022): Adaptive Policy Convergence and Healthcare System Resilience resurged as zero-COVID policies faced mounting international criticism and economic pressure. This phase featured 53 press conferences, the highest absolute number, as authorities struggled to justify policy divergence from global approaches while managing increasing international isolation pressures. Communication during this phase revealed tensions between domestic policy commitments and international pressure for convergence. Healthcare System Resilience messaging (40%) emphasized system capacity and policy effectiveness to justify continued zero-COVID approaches despite international criticism. Adaptive Policy Convergence messaging (25%) introduced flexibility language (“scientific precision,” “dynamic adjustment”) that provided linguistic foundation for subsequent policy transitions while maintaining surface policy consistency. The emergence of “optimization” terminology during late 2022 represents sophisticated semantic governance, enabling policy flexibility without explicit acknowledgment of international pressure influence. This linguistic strategy demonstrates how authoritarian communication systems can prepare for policy transitions through gradual semantic adjustment rather than direct policy reversal announcements.

Global Alignment Phase (December 2022-February 2023): Transnational Health Governance and Economic-Health Nexus gained prominence as China rapidly converged with international endemic management approaches. This phase encompassed 24 press conferences emphasizing policy “optimization” and alignment with global health governance recommendations. Communication strategy reframed policy changes as adaptive evolution rather than external capitulation, demonstrating sophisticated crisis termination under transnational pressure. Transnational Health Governance messaging (35%) emphasized China’s integration with global health governance frameworks and alignment with WHO recommendations for endemic management. Economic-Health Nexus messaging (40%) prioritized economic recovery and international integration, reflecting recognition that continued policy divergence threatened economic interdependence relationships essential for development objectives.

The analysis reveals distinct alignment patterns across pandemic phases (see Appendix D): high alignment during the International Scrutiny Phase (emergency containment protocols), moderate alignment during Global Cooperation (vaccine equity initiatives), low alignment during International Isolation (endemic transition advocacy), and renewed high alignment during Global Alignment Phase (sustainable endemic management).

Key empirical findings from Table 4 reveal two critical patterns: (1) Communication frame adjustments consistently followed major international pressure events within 7–21 days (mean lag = 14 days), demonstrating systematic responsiveness; (2) Economic-Health Nexus and Adaptive Policy Convergence frames showed the highest sensitivity to international signals, accounting for 68% of observed adjustments, while Healthcare System Resilience frames demonstrated greater inertia.

The systematic lag structure observed in our analysis—with communication adjustments consistently occurring within 7–21 days of international pressure events—demonstrates the direct temporal relationship between transnational pressures and domestic frame evolution. Economic-Health Nexus and Adaptive Policy Convergence frames emerged as the most responsive to international signals, reflecting China’s strategic prioritization of economic integration and policy flexibility when facing external pressures.

Semantic governance: Micro‑level discourse patterns

This targeted qualitative analysis examines specific linguistic transformations that enabled policy transitions while maintaining narrative coherence, demonstrating how controlled discourse environments facilitate policy reversal without explicit acknowledgment of failure. The analysis focuses on two critical semantic shifts that exemplify the transnational pressure to frame shift mechanism through micro-level discursive adaptation.

Zero-COVID to optimization semantic bridge (November-December 2022)

The transition from zero-COVID enforcement to policy relaxation in late 2022 demonstrates sophisticated semantic engineering. This linguistic transformation involved strategic policy continuity framing, where the term “优化” (optimization) appeared 47 times in December 2022 press conferences compared to only 3 times in November, while “清零” (zero-COVID) usage declined from 23 to 7 instances. This semantic substitution enabled presentation of policy change as enhancement rather than abandonment.

The discourse also employed temporal recontextualization, with phrases like “新阶段” (new phase) and “新形势” (new situation) increasing by 340% in December communications. This created temporal discontinuity that justified policy shifts as adaptive responses to changed circumstances rather than policy corrections.

Furthermore, references to “科学精准” (scientific precision) maintained consistent frequency (15–18 instances per month), but the semantic context shifted from justifying strict measures to supporting flexible approaches, demonstrating how identical terminology can serve opposing policy objectives.

Vaccination discourse evolution (April 2021-December 2022)

Vaccine communication reveals systematic semantic adaptation to international pressure. Early vaccine discourse emphasized sovereignty protection through terms like “自主研发” (independent development) and “中国方案” (Chinese solution), which appeared 2.3 times more frequently during international vaccine diplomacy tensions in July-September 2021, effectively countering international criticism.

From October 2022, however, the discourse increasingly incorporated “国际合作” (international cooperation) and “全球健康” (global health), representing a 45% increase in usage as China prepared for policy alignment with global approaches. This shift demonstrates the strategic integration of international cooperation rhetoric in response to evolving transnational pressures.

These micro-level discursive patterns collectively demonstrate that semantic governance operates through lexical substitution without semantic disruption, contextual reframing of identical terminology, proportional adjustment of competing discourse elements, and temporal bridging that enables policy transitions while maintaining narrative legitimacy. Each linguistic transformation directly corresponds to specific international pressure events, providing empirical evidence for the transnational pressure to frame shift mechanism that drives China’s pandemic communication adaptation.

Discussion

Theoretical implications, scope, and propositions

This study makes two theoretical contributions that reconceptualize crisis communication dynamics in extended emergencies. First, the empirical analysis reveals a cyclical frame reinforcement model that challenges and modifies the linear progression assumptions of traditional crisis lifecycle models. Traditional crisis lifecycle models, exemplified by Fink’s [15] four-stage model and Mitroff’s [25] five-stage framework, assume linear progression from prodromal detection through acute response to chronic recovery and eventual resolution. These models treat crisis communication as a problem-solving sequence where each phase builds upon the previous one, ultimately culminating in resolution and organizational learning [17].

However, recent scholarship on “mega-crises” has challenged these linear assumptions. Boin et al. [6] argue that prolonged, multi-wave emergencies exhibit different dynamics than contained incidents, requiring theoretical frameworks that account for recursive rather than linear temporal patterns. The Chinese COVID-19 as a “mega-crisis” reveals a recursive pattern where frames undergo strategic cyclical intensification and redeployment. The empirical evidence demonstrates that crisis communication in extended emergencies operates through what can be termed “spiral progression”—a dynamic where previously deployed frames are not abandoned but rather stored as strategic resources for future reactivation.

This cyclical mechanism—where “retired” frames are strategically reactivated, modified, and redeployed for new circumstances—represents a theoretical advancement that requires crisis communication models to account for recursive rather than linear temporal dynamics. The theoretical implications extend beyond simple model modification to challenge core assumptions about crisis narrative development. Where traditional models assume progressive frame abandonment as crises evolve, the cyclical reinforcement model reveals that effective extended crisis communication operates through iterative adaptation cycles that treat previous frames as renewable strategic assets. This finding necessitates theoretical frameworks that model recursive intensification as a core feature of protracted crisis management, altering how scholars conceptualize the relationship between time, narrative evolution, and communication effectiveness in extended emergencies.

This framework focuses on the recurrence of external threat conditions (e.g., virus variants) that compel a return to high-alert operations. By contrast, the cyclical frame reinforcement model proposed in this study foregrounds the deliberate, strategic redeployment of previously shelved frames rather than the involuntary re-entry into operational phases. The cyclical frame reinforcement model explains how communicators actively revive earlier discursive packages—often in altered form—to maintain legitimacy or mobilize compliance even when the material threat profile has changed. This distinction highlights the unique contribution of the present research: it shifts analytical focus from organizational process loops to rhetorical strategy cycles, revealing how narrative tools themselves become resources that can be stored, re-activated, and reconfigured across protracted emergencies.

Our findings resonate with recent research on pandemic learning and governance adaptation. Lund-Tønnesen and Christensen [23] identify similar recursive patterns in how governments recalibrate communication strategies based on shifting epidemiological and political conditions. The cyclical frame reinforcement observed in China’s case extends their framework by demonstrating how authoritarian systems leverage discursive resources across multiple crisis phases.

Second, the analysis identifies semantic governance as a distinctive mechanism of authoritarian crisis communication that extends framing theory into the domain of regime legitimacy maintenance. Unlike democratic contexts where policy reversals typically require explicit acknowledgment and justification due to pluralistic discourse environments, the Chinese case demonstrates how controlled information ecosystems enable sophisticated linguistic engineering to preserve governmental credibility during major policy transitions. The transition from “zero-COVID” to “coexistence” messaging exemplifies semantic governance in action: rather than acknowledging policy failure, authorities employed strategic euphemism (“optimization”), temporal reframing (“new phase”), and victory narratives (“decisive success”) to transform policy reversal into adaptive evolution. This linguistic management represents a form of soft power governance where semantic control becomes a mechanism for exercising authority over public perception, enabling regimes to navigate policy contradictions without appearing to falter.

The theoretical significance lies in demonstrating how authoritarian communication systems can leverage discourse monopoly to achieve frame transformation impossible in competitive information environments. This insight expands framing theory beyond its traditional focus on competitive discourse dynamics [7] to encompass how controlled information systems generate unique opportunities for strategic narrative management that preserve legitimacy while enabling policy adaptation.

The analysis reveals that “semantic governance” in authoritarian contexts transcends mere administrative control over information flows, constituting instead a systematic discursive capacity for meaning construction [13]. Through the strategic establishment of acceptable semantic boundaries, the manipulation of keyword emotional orientations, and the systematic “addition, deletion, and substitution” of core vocabulary across different crisis phases, governmental actors achieve the reallocation of public emotions and attribution logic without substantially altering underlying policy objectives. This monopolization of symbolic resources enables regimes to rewrite potential policy failures—such as the termination of zero-COVID strategies—as strategic upgrades, thereby achieving what might be termed “continuity through policy discontinuity” [28]. In contrast to democratic contexts that rely on public deliberation to modify narratives, semantic governance demonstrates an alternative pathway for legitimacy maintenance through discursive recoding rather than discursive competition [18].

Semantic governance contributes two distinctive insights to crisis communication scholarship. First, the narrative resilience mechanism operates through gradual semantic adjustments—such as the progression from “zero-COVID” to “optimization” to “Class downgrade”—enabling authorities to absorb external shocks while preserving overarching narrative frameworks [5]. This demonstrates superior narrative flexibility compared to simple media censorship, revealing sophisticated discursive elasticity that maintains interpretive coherence despite policy volatility [12]. Second, semantic governance functions as a legitimacy transformation bridge, connecting performance-based legitimacy with performative legitimacy through the intermediary mechanism of “narrative legitimacy” [27].

This enables regimes to maintain psychological dominance over public perception through semantic recoding when performance indicators falter, suggesting that traditional crisis lifecycle models require supplementation with a pervasive “semantic adaptation” component [11]. Such theoretical expansion explains how authoritarian systems accomplish crisis narrative self-repair and reproduction through controlled discourse systems in the absence of institutionalized accountability mechanisms.

Theoretical positioning: dialogue with international crisis communication scholarship

The empirical patterns we document invite theoretical positioning within broader crisis‑communication scholarship. They aim to enrich existing models by highlighting possible cyclicality and semantic adjustments in extended emergencies. Firstly, the evidence of recursive frame deployment invites a reconsideration of widely accepted crisis stages models beyond Chinese or authoritarian contexts.

While cyclicality was observed in China’s crisis narrative, future research should examine whether elements of frame recycling appear in protracted crises elsewhere. The concept of semantic governance underscores how political context can fundamentally alter crisis communication mechanisms. In democratic settings, crisis communication effectiveness is often tied to managing a contested information environment, as stakeholders publicly debate narratives [7]. The Chinese case suggests that in controlled media environments, narrative flexibility can substitute for public debate. Scholars should explore to what extent semantic governance tactics—like euphemisms or strategic re-framings—are present or viable in hybrid regimes or even democracies under certain conditions (e.g., wartime censorship or emergency laws). Additionally, by focusing on how global pressures mediate domestic communication (and vice versa), this study aligns with calls for a more internationalized crisis communication theory [19].

The findings support the notion that crisis communication strategies must be analyzed within a multi-level governance context, acknowledging that global interdependence and transnational norms increasingly shape national narratives. This Chinese case thus contributes a concrete example of how global forces (WHO guidelines, international opinion, etc.) can be internalized and linguistically managed within state narratives. Finally, the research highlights an interplay between performance legitimacy and narrative legitimacy. It suggests crisis communication scholars should incorporate insights from comparative politics, recognizing that the goals and methods of crisis framing may differ across regimes not only in degree but in kind. By engaging with interdisciplinary perspectives, the refined theoretical constructs proposed here—cyclical frame reinforcement and semantic governance—can be tested and elaborated in varied socio-political contexts, advancing a more globally attuned crisis communication scholarship.

Globalization and health equity implications

Our findings show that transnational cues shape not only when governments reframe but also whose risks are foregrounded when reframing occurs. In a pandemic mega-crisis [6], China’s podium rhetoric recycled and repurposed frames as WHO guidance, markets, and diplomacy evolved. Narrating rapid policy changes as “optimization” rather than reversal preserved communicative continuity, which can stabilize compliance, yet it also risks under-specifying transition risks for clinically and socioeconomically vulnerable groups (e.g., surge capacity, targeted antivirals, occupational protections). This is the classic capacity–legitimacy trade-off in crisis talk: meaning-making that sustains credibility may simultaneously obscure distributional consequences [5, 8].

Two equity mechanisms emerge from our evidence. First, semantic smoothing—euphemism (“optimization”), temporal reframing (“new phase”), and selective metrics—reduces perceived discontinuity but can delay actionable guidance for priority populations during pivot windows. Second, priority-group diffusion: generic references to “key populations” crowd out explicit, time-bound commitments (e.g., booster outreach timelines, triage guarantees). These mechanisms align with scholarship on crisis exploitation and framing contests—where rhetorical choices redistribute blame/credit and shape policy trajectories [4].

Cross-national work also shows that privacy regimes and crisis strategies condition legitimation of intrusive tools and their distributive effects. Lund-Tønnesen [22] demonstrates that Germany, Norway, and the UK justified digital surveillance through distinct legitimacy mixes rooted in privacy institutions and strategic orientations. Our authoritarian case adds a complementary pathway: semantic governance can legitimate equally intrusive or abrupt shifts without open contestation—raising different equity risks (opaque targeting, uneven relief).

Equity hinges on learning that surfaces and corrects such blind spots. Building on Lund-Tønnesen and Christensen’s distinction between internal (capacity-facing) and external (audience-facing) learning [23], our results suggest strong external learning (fast rhetorical accommodation to global cues) but thinner internal learning on equity disclosure during transitions. For mega-crises, we recommend three equity checkpoints embedded in press briefings at each policy change: (i) a plain-language risk summary for high-risk groups; (ii) time-bound service guarantees (antivirals, ICU access, disability accommodations); and (iii) a one-paragraph equity note explaining how the change was assessed against distributional criteria. These communicative guardrails align global norms with equity-first implementation, limiting the costs of semantic smoothing.

Transnational communication dynamics and global health governance

Our longitudinal design points to a recurring short lag between international signals and domestic rhetorical adjustment in this case. With that caveat, we sketch two governance dynamics that may merit further study (see Table 4). In mega-crises, meaning-making is recursive rather than linear [6]; our contribution is to show how those recursions are timed by transnational cues and implemented through podium discourse—linking global norms to national decision cycles. This clarifies two governance dynamics.

First, discursive alignment without immediate policy convergence: governments may mirror WHO lexicon and frames while sustaining divergence in implementation, especially under sovereignty-sensitive conditions [8]. Second, selective globalization in talk: technical cooperation often appears emphasized while autonomy claims are reinforced, preserving room for delayed or partial uptake—an instance of strategic framing consistent with crisis exploitation logics [4, 5].

Comparative insights suggest these dynamics are institutionally contingent. In European democracies, differences in privacy regimes and crisis strategies yielded distinct legitimation scripts for intrusive tools [22]. In information-controlled environments, semantic governance can perform a similar function to contested legitimation: it orchestrates narrative transitions that absorb global pressure while maintaining autonomy. For assessment, we propose a light-weight discursive alignment dashboard to complement standard IHR/WHO monitoring: (i) event-study lead/lag between WHO advisories and official briefings; (ii) domain-coded alignment (surveillance, PHSM, borders, vaccination, therapeutics, data transparency; see Table Appendix D); and (iii) semantic indicators (pivot lexemes, narrative rationales). These indicators do not prove causality, but they make visible when and how global governance travels through national communication—and when it stalls. They also create a platform for learning that narrows the gap between external rhetorical accommodation and internal capacity adjustments [5, 6, 23].

Conclusion

This longitudinal frame analysis offers an empirical account of communication patterns in China’s COVID‑19 press conferences (2020–2023). Using computational and qualitative tools, we describe how frame salience appeared to shift across four phases and in proximity to international cues.

The study also points to how government public health messaging served a critical function in legitimizing stringent interventions, sustaining vaccine uptake, and gradually guiding citizens toward post-crisis normalcy. Communication strategies frequently integrated moral appeals, scientific justifications, and national metaphors—approaches that may have supported compliance, vaccine uptake, and a shift toward post‑crisis normalcy. This multi-frame approach underscores the complexity of large-scale, prolonged crisis communication, where no single narrative can continuously suffice.
