Direct and transdentinal antibacterial activity of synthetic and biomimetic agents against different Streptococcus and Lactobacillus strains
Authors: Heba El-Deeb, Maha Omran, Enas Mobarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the direct and transdentinal antibacterial activity of some synthetic and biomimetic agents against different cariogenic strains.Materials and MethodsThe antibacterial activity of synthetic and biomimetic agents, including chlorhexidine digluconate (CHX) (positive control), sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), sodium fluoride (NaF), nisin, Biorepair, UltraDex, and distilled water (negative control) against different cariogenic species, including four Streptococcus strains (S); S. mutans, S. sobrinus, S. mitis, and S. sanguinis, and three Lactobacillus strains (L); L. acidophilus, L. gasseri, and L. casei were evaluated using direct and transdentinal diffusion tests. For the transdentinal test, 0.2mm human dentin discs were used, and their permeability was standardized using a modified fluid transport apparatus. Six replicates were made to determine the inhibition zone for each tested agent against each tested bacterial strain. Data were statistically analyzed using a multivariate analysis of variance using a general linear model.ResultsCHX exhibited the highest antibacterial activity against all strains. Nisin showed moderate efficacy, particularly against S. mutans. NaOCl and Biorepair showed similar effects (p=1.000), as did NaF and UltraDex, which were least effective against Streptococcus. Biorepair showed notable transdentinal activity against S. sanguinis and L. acidophilus.ConclusionChlorhexidine remains the reference standard for cavity disinfection. Nisin demonstrated promising efficacy against the tested strains. Biorepair could be successfully recommended in caries management. S. mutans' marked resistance defines it as a primary target for antimicrobial intervention. Dentin, as a biological barrier, hinders the effectiveness of the tested antibacterial agents and should be considered in treatment planning.

Introduction

The medical model of treating dental caries, which aims to preserve tooth structures, involves disinfection, remineralization, and/or sealing of the carious lesion. This model of treatment supports the use of antibacterials in non-cavitated and, currently, in cavitated lesions, particularly with the evolution of the selective caries removal approach. Nevertheless, for disinfection, the applied antibacterial agent should have a penetration power through the existing dentin thickness to be efficient (1, 2). It should also be non-toxic to the pulp cells and have broad-spectrum activity against anaerobes and facultative anaerobes.

Chlorhexidine (CHX) is considered the gold standard broad-spectrum antibacterial agent (3). It is a cationic bis-biguanide biocide; recommended to eradicate bacteria left in the dentinal tubules after mechanical caries debridement (4). It shows considerable antimicrobial activity against oral pathogens, such as Streptococcus mutans, Staphylococcus aureus, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Enterococcus faecalis, and Candida albicans (5). Furthermore, sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl); has been considered as a powerful cavity disinfectant against anaerobic and facultative bacteria (6). Likewise, sodium fluoride (NaF) mouthwash was proposed to maintain a balance between demineralization and remineralization of dental hard tissues in addition to its antimicrobial effect (7, 8).

Recently, nisin, an antimicrobial peptide of the lantibiotic family, was introduced in the dental field as a steady step in oral health management and disease control (9). It has been used as a food preservative because of its high antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, and to a lesser extent, Gram-negative bacteria (10), and its non-toxic effect (11). Yet, further research is still required to optimize its role in the anticaries program.

Concurrently, researchers have developed biomimetic approaches to mitigate the side effects of synthetic antibacterial agents. Novel nano-hydroxyapatite (HA) formulations, one of the most biocompatible and bioactive materials, have gained wide acceptance in dentistry. Being a natural component of tooth and bone, it has the ability to be incorporated into the tooth structure (12). As claimed by the manufacturers, these aforementioned formulations would have the potential for remineralization and control of microbial biofilm. However, researches that evaluate the transdentinal efficacy of such recently introduced synthetic and biomimetic formulations are scarce. Hence, this study aimed to provide a preliminary assessment of the impacts of synthetic and biomimetic antibacterials against cariogenic pathogens using direct and transdentinal diffusion tests.

The null hypotheses were twofold, firstly the tested synthetic and biomimetic agents have no statistically significant difference in their direct/transdentinal antibacterial activity against each bacterial species and secondly, the different bacterial species were not statistically different in their direct/transdentinal response to each tested synthetic and biomimetic agent.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed by the King Abdulaziz University Faculty of Dentistry Research Ethics Committee and approved with the number 124128.

Study materials

Details of the tested agents and their preparations are listed in Table 1, while tested bacterial strains and their codes are presented in Table 2. All solutions were passed through a hydrophilic cellulose acetate membrane filter (0.45 μm Sterlitech Corporation, USA). To ensure blindness and minimize bias during testing, solutions were coded by a person not involved in the study. Positive and negative (distilled water) control solutions were coded using the same marker color, which differed from that used for the other tested solutions. The tested solutions were wrapped in aluminum foil to protect them from light and stored in a cool, dark, and dry place at 4 oC.

Direct agar diffusion test

The media used for Streptococcus strains was brain heart infusion (BHI) agar and broth (Oxoid microbiology products, Nepean, Canada), whereas that used for Lactobacillus species was Mitis Regosa Salivaris (MRS) agar and broth (Biolife, Milano, Italy). The pH of the media was maintained at 7.2-7.4. Petri dishes of 150 mm diameter were used. A sterile 30 mL of BHI or MRS agar was poured into each plate and left to solidify at room temperature for 2 hours. All standard bacterial strains were grown at 37 °C for 24 hours, using an anaerobic jar and gas pack (Oxoid, Hampshire, England). The inoculum was prepared by suspending 3 to 5 well-isolated colonies into 4 mL of sterile normal saline. The turbidity of bacterial suspensions was adjusted to a McFarland standard of 0.5, which is equivalent to approximately 1.5×108 colony-forming units per mL (CFU/mL).

Streptococcus or Lactobacillus strains' suspensions were transferred by a sterile swab to the surface of BHI and MRS agar plates, respectively. To obtain uniform growth, the swab was streaked over the surface of the media three times, rotating the plate through an angle of 60° after each application to be finally passed close to the edge of the agar surface. The inoculum was left to dry for approximately 3 minutes at room temperature with the lid closed.

Sterile filter paper discs (9 mm diameter) were immersed in the tested agents for 1 minute. Four equidistant paper discs (2 different agents in addition to positive and negative controls) were placed on each plate. The plates were left at room temperature for 10 minutes to allow diffusion of the tested agents through the agar; afterwards, they were placed in a jar containing an anaerobic gas pack (Oxoid, Hampshire, England) and incubated at 37 oC for 24 hours. Thereafter, the plates were removed and the inhibition zones around each disc were measured (in millimeters) using a digital caliper (Proficraft, Mebschieber, Germany) by double blind observers. The reliability between observers was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A value of 0.997 was obtained, reflecting excellent agreement between observers. Measurements of the inhibition zones were taken from three locations, and the mean was calculated (1). Data were recorded for each strain and each tested agent. Six replicates were made to determine the antibacterial effect of each tested agent against each tested bacterial strain.

Dentin disc preparation

Sound human third molars, extracted for periodontal or malocclusion problems, from young patients (15-25 years), were collected following the regulation established by the Faculty of Dentistry's Ethics Committee. The collected teeth were thoroughly washed under running water, scaled using a hand scaler (Prima-Dent International, Frankfurt, Germany) to remove attached periodontal tissues, plaque, and calculus, and inspected with a magnifying lens 6X (Bausch and Lomb, Co., Rochester, NY, USA) to detect cracks or caries. Teeth with any defects were discarded. Selected teeth were stored in phosphate buffer saline containing 0.2% sodium azide at 4°C pending use within one week. Figure 1 represents the disc prepared for the measurement of dentine permeability.

Each tooth was attached with its occlusal surface placed on the center of a circle drawn on a glass slab (Figure 1A) to help in its complete central embedding in a chemically curing polyester resin (Polyester #2121, Eternal Chemical; Hsien, Taiwan) (Figure 1B). Occlusal enamel was trimmed to expose midcoronal flat dentin surface without enamel remnants or pulpal projection. Two dentin discs (0.2±0.001 mm) were cut transversely from the middle third of each tooth using a low-speed diamond-saw sectioning machine (Buehler Isomet Low Speed Saw, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) under water coolant. Dentin discs of average diameter (10±0.02 mm) were used (Figure 1C). The thickness and diameter of the discs were checked using a precise digital caliper (Proficraft, Mebschieber, Germany). The occlusal surface of each disc was wet polished with 600-grit SiC paper to create a standard surface roughness and smear layer. Citric acid 50% was applied for 30 seconds onto the pulpal side of the dentin disc to remove the smear layer (1, 2).

Measuring the permeability of the dentin discs

To allow standardized allocation of dentin discs per group, dentin permeability was determined for each disc. Dentin permeability was measured using a modified fluid transport apparatus (Figure 1F) (13). The apparatus comprises a split Teflon chamber that consists of female and male parts attached by screwing action. A pair of identical rubber "O" rings, with an outside diameter of 10 mm to snugly fit within the female part and a central hole (5 mm in diameter) to limit the tested surface area of each dentin disc (Figure 1D), and to seal the chamber. The female portion was connected to one end of the metallic three-way extension of the transport apparatus from one side, and the male portion was connected to a graduated glass pipette (3 mm internal diameter) from the other side (Figure 1E). The metallic three-way extension was further connected to a sphygmomanometer gauge and an inflation bulb. All parts of the assembly were filled with distilled water.

Each disc was placed in the split chamber device between two rubbers "O" rings with the pulpal side of the discs facing the pump side (inlet) and the occlusal side facing the pipette side of the chamber (outlet). The location of the air bubble was determined and marked with a permanent ultra-fine point marker on the pipette for each specimen. The steps taken to measure the permeability of dentin discs are illustrated in Figure 1. Hydraulic conductance (Lp) by filtration was calculated according to the following formula: Lp = Jv/A.ΔP.t, where (Lp) is the hydraulic conductance (μL cm−2 min−1 cm H2O−1); (Jv) is the volume of fluid in microliter per millimeter; (A) is dentin surface area (cm2); (ΔP) is the hydrostatic pressure in cm H2O; and (t) is the time required for fluid movement (min). Dentin discs of comparable hydraulic conductance measurements (±0.0001µl cm-2 min-1) were used in the study.

A rubber "O" ring was fixed to the occlusal side of each dentin disc to limit the area of testing at the dentin disc and to prevent seepage of the tested agents. Discs were randomly packed and sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C for 15 minutes).

Transdentinal diffusion test

After sterilization, dentin discs were randomly distributed into seven groups according to the tested bacterial strains (66 discs per group). Subsequently, they were divided into seven subgroups according to the tested agents (18 discs for each positive and negative control and 6 discs for each other tested agent). Sterile discs were placed at equidistant points onto the surface of inoculated agar plates (as described before), with their pulpal side facing the agar. A 75 µL of each tested agent was dispensed into each disc using a micropipette (Scilogex, MicroPette Plus Autoclavable Pipettor Single channel, China). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours in a jar containing an anaerobic gas pack (Oxoid, Hampshire, England). Inhibition zones were measured in millimeters as stated above. Six replicates for each of the tested agents were performed.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically described in terms of mean ± standard deviation (±SD). Normal assumption was performed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test within each test, type of bacteria, and antibacterial agent. To test whether there is any significant effect or interaction between the type of test, type of bacteria, and type of antibacterial agent on the inhibition zone (in mm), a multivariate analysis of variance using a general linear model was performed. This model was chosen because the data generally followed the normal assumption in each subgroup. The inhibition zone was designated as a dependent factor, and each of the diffusion test, type of bacteria, and antibacterial agent was entered as independent variables. The model included the effect of each independent variable alone as well as the two-way and three-way interactions among the independent variables. Power analysis of each component of the model was also performed. Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) release 22 for Microsoft Windows was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

The three-way ANOVA test is presented in Table 3. There was a statistically significant difference between the tested antibacterial agents, the bacterial strains, the type of diffusion test, and their interactions (p< 0.001).

Table 4 shows Bonferroni post-hoc pairwise comparisons between each antibacterial agent. Notably, distilled water (negative control) exhibited no inhibition zones. CHX (positive control) exhibited significantly larger inhibition zones compared to all other agents (p < .001), with the greatest differences observed against NaF, UltraDex, and NaOCl. NaOCl showed no significant difference when compared to Biorepair (p = 1.000), while NaF and UltraDex were also statistically similar (p = 1.000). All other pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p < .001).

A one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests showed significant differences in inhibition zones between several bacteria (Table 5). Streptococcus strains generally showed lower inhibition, with no significant difference between S. mutans, S. sobrinus, and S. sanguinis (p > .05). However, S. mitis differed significantly from other Streptococcus strains (p < .001). L. acidophilus exhibited significantly higher inhibition zones than all Streptococcus strains and other Lactobacillus strains (all p < .001).

Figure 2 and Figure 3 show bar charts for the means (in mm) and standard deviations of the inhibition zones of the tested antibacterial agents applied directly and transdentinally on the tested Streptococcus and Lactobacillus strains, respectively.

All antibacterial agents demonstrated decreased inhibition zone values when applied transdentinally. CHX consistently exhibited the highest antibacterial activity across all tested bacterial strains, particularly under direct application, with peak inhibition observed against S. sobrinus and L. acidophilus. Nisin demonstrated moderate direct antibacterial activity, particularly against S. mutans, though its transdentinal efficacy remained limited. Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) showed moderate direct efficacy, notably against S. mitis and L. acidophilus, but its effectiveness declined sharply under transdentinal conditions. In contrast, sodium fluoride (NaF), Biorepair, and UltraDex displayed low antibacterial performance in both application modes. An exception was Biorepair, which showed comparatively higher transdentinal inhibition against S. sanguinis and L. acidophilus.

Discussion

Based on the results of this study, the two anticipated null hypotheses were rejected. There was a statistically significant antibacterial effect of synthetic/biomimetic agents when applied directly and transdentinally. There was also a statistically significant difference in the responses of the bacterial strains to the tested synthetic and biomimetic agents in both direct and transdentinal applications.

Streptococcus mutans strains, one of the primary causative microorganisms of dental caries, had significant serological (14) and genetic heterogeneity (15). They are classified into eight serotypes according to their carbohydrate antigens and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) (14, 16). S. mutans, mostly isolated from human, hold the c, e, or f antigens, and specifically, those holding the c antigen account for approximately 70% of the tooth decay. On the other hand, those holding the d, g, and h carbohydrate antigens, known as S.sobrinus, are the second strain isolated from tooth decay, mainly associated with smooth surface decay (16).

S. sobrinus differed from S. mutans in that it had higher glucosyl transferase activity, responsible for glucan synthesis. Nevertheless, they lack adhesin-mediated attachment mechanisms, allowing S. mutans to dominate in terms of virulence among microorganisms present in dental plaque(17). One of the tested strains was S. mitis, the initial colonizer that selectively adhered to the acquired enamel pellicle through specific ionic (18), hydrophobic, and lectin-like interactions (19).

Before establishing the role of S. mutans in dental caries, Lactobacilli had been accused for several years of being the main etiological factor of dental caries (20). Over 150 species of Lactobacilli, especially the tested strains, had been isolated from the oral cavity and the carious lesions (21). They are the secondary opportunistic lesion invaders (22). Once a carious lesion is established, it creates a favorable low-pH niche for the colonization of the Lactobacilli, which are well-adapted to both producing and surviving in a highly acidic environment (23). In some cases, they even compete and exclude bacteria that created the retentive niche (24). Researchers focused on their ability to bind to collagen type I exposed in dentin caries. This binding ability helps to sustain them in dentin carious lesions, conversely to their cariogenic patterns of S. mutans (25).

Thus, to emphasize the efficacy of the therapeutic agents, they have to target the major cariogenic pathogens. Likewise, the role of these agents does not only aim to inhibit the superficially attached microorganisms but also to penetrate deeply into the tooth tissue to eliminate the resident ones. From this perspective, the use of dentin discs for antibacterial diffusion testing is considered a valuable in vitro approach to simulate clinical conditions. The present study also revealed a difference between the direct and indirect results, which was in agreement with Schmalz et al. (1), Ergucu et al. (2), and Lessa et al. (4).

The possible explanation for this difference could be attributed to two reasons. Dentin disc, even at minimal thickness, acts as a natural physical barrier that limits the passage of minute molecules, which may hinder the diffusion of the tested agents. The second possible reason was that the tested agents might adhere to or react with the structural components of the dentin disc.

Regarding the results of the tested agents, in general, they proved antibacterial efficacy against all tested bacterial strains in the following descending order; CHX, nisin, and NaOCl. Biorepair demonstrated notable antibacterial efficacy against Streptococcus strains; but exhibited low activity against Lactobacillus strains. Conversely, the inverse was for the NaF. UltraDex displayed limited antibacterial potential against all tested strains, with a few enhancements against the lactobacillus strains when tested directly.

Chlorhexidine is a large dicationic molecule (1,6-di(4-chlorophenyl-diguanido) hexane, with nitrogen atoms carrying the positive charge on either side of the hexamethylene bridge. These positive charges were adsorbed onto the negatively charged surfaces, as phosphoproteins, bacterial cell walls, tooth surfaces, mucosal membranes, and pellicles (3). The antibacterial action of CHX relies upon the strong and specific adsorption between the cationic molecule and the phosphate-containing compounds present at the bacterial cell membrane. This reaction altered the integrity of the bacterial cell membrane, which allowed the CHX molecules to bind to the phospholipids of the inner cell membrane. This increased the permeability of the inner membrane, leading to the leakage of low-molecular-weight components, such as potassium ions. At this level, the action of CHX is reversible and so-called bacteriostatic as it causes minor changes in the permeability of the bacterial cell membrane. At higher CHX concentration levels, the bactericidal effect is expected. Where progressive damage to the cell membrane, which increased permeability and leakage of low-molecular-weight cytoplasmic components, coagulation, cytoplasmic precipitation by the formation of phosphate complexes, such as adenosine triphosphate and nucleic acids (3).

The decrease in the transdentinal diffusion test results of CHX, compared to its direct diffusion test results, was reported by Schmaltz et al.(2) and Lessa et al. (4). This decrease may be attributed to the substantivity of CHX. As it binds to the dentin disc, and is released over a long time, creating an oral reservoir and bacteriostatic milieu (3). Although this property didn't influence its overwhelming results (2, 4). Furthermore, Kim et al. (26) reported that CHX exhibits binding affinity to both hydroxyapatite crystallites and dentin collagen. Binding to hydroxyapatite occurs through electrostatic interactions with the phosphate groups, while binding to collagen involves a combination of electrostatic force to the negatively charged carboxyl groups and hydrogen bonding with collagen peptides. These binding mechanisms maintained the stability of the CHX-dentin complex (26).

One of the new and remarkable findings in this study was the antibacterial efficacy of the oldest known antibiotic bacteriocin, nisin. It is a linear cationic ribosomal synthesis polypeptide with 34 amino acid residues and post-translationally modified peptide, one lanthionine, four methyllanthionine rings {two consisting of N-terminal B-methyllanthionine rings (A, B, and C) linked to two C-terminal rings (D and E) by a ﬂexible hinge region}and unusual residues as dehydroalanine and dehydrobutyrine (11, 27).

In addition to its high effectiveness against Gram-positive bacteria, it also demonstrated antibacterial efficacy against S. mutans, S. sanguinis, and L. acidophilus. These results were corroborated by findings from the present study as well as previous investigations (28, 29).

Nisin's antibacterial effect proceeds through two mechanisms of action: micromolar and nanomolar ranges. At the micromolar range, nisin binds to the anionic lipids on the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, causing its disorder with a low affinity of permeation. At the nanomolar range, the N-terminal region of nisin forms a pyrophosphate that interacts with Lipid II in the target cell membrane by specific recognition, forming a Lipid II-dependent pathway, restricting access to lipid II for peptidoglycan synthesis, resulting in membrane insertion and pores formation. This, in turn, allows efflux of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), amino acids, pre-accumulated rubidium, or the collapse of vital ion gradients, leading to cell death (11).

Being odorless, colorless, tasteless, with low toxicity, classified as GARS (Generally Regarded as Safe), stable, and active at low pH, nisin has recently gained attention to be used in the dental field, producing a new era of peptidomimetics. A recent study proved the ability of nisin to modify oral-disease-associated biofilms and support the formation of healthy biofilms (30). Previous studies proved its therapeutic effectiveness when used in experimental mouthwash and as an additive to the dentin bonding agent (31, 32).

Results of the present study revealed a decrease in the antibacterial potential of nisin when used in the transdentinal (indirect) diffusion test. A suggested reason might be that a reaction between the cationic nisin and the anionic dentin collagen proteins might occur, which could deplete some of the nisin molecules before their complete diffusion to the bacterial strains. Further investigations are needed to identify the interaction between the nisin molecules and the dentin. The findings of this study demonstrated that nisin had an antibacterial potential against caries-producing microorganisms through the dentin.

Concerning the results of sodium hypochlorite, it showed a potent direct antibacterial activity against S. mutans, S. mitis, L. gasseri, and to a lesser extent S. sobrinus. These results were confirmed by previous researchers (1, 6). These studies elucidated the antibacterial action of NaOCl. The chlorine content of NaOCl reacts with amino groups of the bacterial cell wall, forming chloramines, which in turn have a potent oxidative power, altering cellular metabolism by the inactivation of bacterial enzymes (1). When tested indirectly through the dentin disc, the antibacterial efficacy of NaOCl decreased despite applying the recommended concentration for cavity disinfection (1). Subsequently, this result contradicted the findings of Ergucu et al.(1). NaOCl is known as a dentin-deproteinizing agent; it forms superoxide radicals that transform the long peptide chains of the dentin collagen type I and chondroitin sulfates into fragments of chlorinated protein terminal groups, which are easily broken down (1).

Therefore, the possible explanation for the decreased efficacy is that the NaOCl molecules might react with dentin, thus, the remaining molecules might not be enough to diffuse through the agar or kill the microorganisms. Previous studies reported that the bactericidal effect of NaOCl was directly proportional to its concentration. Hebling et al. (33) stated that 1% NaOCl was insufficient to kill microorganisms inside the dentinal tubules. Also, Muratovska et al. (34) affirmed that even 5.25% NaOCl showed weak antibacterial efficacy and small inhibition zones against the S. mutans strain. Consequently, the concentration tested in the present study (1% NaOCl), although recommended for dentin disinfection in daily dental practice (35), might not be adequate to kill the tested odontopathogens. Nonetheless, the use of NaOCl for cavity disinfection might be reconsidered, due to its relative toxicity and its affection on the dentin/restoration bond strength (36).

In the present study, sodium fluoride showed lower antibacterial efficacy compared to the CHX, nisin, and Biorepair, particularly against Streptococcus strains. However, this antibacterial efficacy was slightly augmented when the material was applied to Lactobacillus strains. Previous researches confirmed the findings of the present study. Yang et al. (37) showed that NaF had little to no effect on S. mutans biofilms grown on hydroxyapatite discs. Also, Randall et al. (38) demonstrated that NaF at various concentrations failed to produce an inhibition zone when tested for its antimicrobial activity against S. mutans. Moreover, Evans et al. (39) demonstrated that a commercial NaF mouth rinse exhibited higher antibacterial activity against Lactobacillus strains than against Streptococcus strains. The known mode of action of NaF occurs intracellularly in the bacterial cell cytoplasm. At low pH, NaF forms hydrogen fluoride (HF), which greatly diffuses through the bacterial cell membrane, dissociates into H+ and F-, and accumulates in the cytoplasm (40). It inhibits the bacterial enolase glycolytic enzyme, the membrane-bound ATPase, and glucose transport. This, in turn, inhibits polysaccharide production and causes cell wall lysis (41).

The possible explanation for the present findings of the low efficacy of NaF compared to the other antibacterial agents could be attributed to the following reasons. First, the requirement of a low pH for the NaF to favor its dissociation and penetration into the bacterial cell membrane. Second, some Streptococcus and Lactobacillus strains proved tolerance to NaF through a mutational change in their genotype and phenotype, as reflected in the variability of NaF results with the Lactobacillus strains (41, 42).

A novel finding in the present study was the results of the biomimetic zinc-containing hydroxyapatite nanoclusters mouthwash (Biorepair). Its antibacterial efficacy surprisingly surpassed NaF and was equivalent to or surpassed that of the deproteinizing NaOCl. The antibacterial efficacy of this product was attributed to its constituent elements. Foremost, one of the added components in therapeutic agents was zinc oxide, due to its potent antimicrobial properties. The latter content of Biorepair in aqueous suspension was able to increase the level of hydroxyl radicals, which additionally contributed to the antibacterial activity. Researchers revealed that the abrasiveness of ZnO nanoparticles might alter the cell wall and membrane of bacterial cells (43). Moreover, some researchers suggested that the presence of surface reactivity between the ZnO nanoparticles and the bacteria might cause direct bacterial death (43). Secondly, the incorporation of sugar alcohol and xylitol in Biorepair, being highly consumable by Streptococcus strains but non-fermentable, might influence its antibacterial activity (44).

Previous studies reported the bacteriostatic action of Biorepair against S. mutans and S. sobrinus, confirming the results of the present study. The authors explained that these strains convert xylitol into xylitol-5-phosphate, resulting in the formation of intracellular vacuoles that degrade the cell membrane (44). Additionally, they highlighted the role of nanohydroxyapatite microclusters in bacterial inhibition. Electrostatic interaction between hydroxyapatite particles and bacterial cells blocks the specific bacterial adhesins and even causes agglutination of the bacteria (45). It is worth mentioning that there is no published data on the antibacterial efficacy of this biomimetic product against most types of the tested strains. The remarkable decrease in the antibacterial efficacy of the Biorepair suspension, when tested indirectly through dentin, could be attributed to the deposition of the biomimetic hydroxyapatite nanocrystals onto the dentin surface. Accordingly, the remaining amount allowed for passage through the dentin disc might not be effective against the tested microorganisms. Still, further investigations are required to guarantee the role of these biomimetic products as antimicrobials and as an adjunct treatment in the partial caries removal technique.

Notably, there is scant literature addressing the use of UltraDex as a mouthwash (12). UltraDex was used as a remineralizing agent as it combines nanohydroxyapatite with fluoride. Additionally, the present study was the first to evaluate the antibacterial potential of UltraDex against caries-induced pathogens. However, UltraDex showed a very low antibacterial activity; this finding might be attributed to the interaction between nanohydroxyapatite and fluoride (in the aqueous formulation of UltraDex), which might reduce the bioavailable fluoride concentration (46, 47).

A key limitation of this study is that it does not fully capture the complexity of the oral biofilm. Consequently, further research under clinically relevant conditions is necessary to assess the interaction between antibacterial agents and the biofilm matrix. Streptococcus mutans exhibited greater resistance to antibacterial agents than Lactobacillus species, emphasizing the need for strain-specific therapeutic approaches. The findings of this study support the development of peptidomimetic and biomimetic agents as targeted, biocompatible alternatives to conventional antimicrobial therapies. The demonstrated efficacy of nisin underscores its potential application in dentistry, particularly as a component of varnishes or restorative materials such as high-viscosity glass ionomers. Similarly, the favorable transdentinal activity of Biorepair reinforces its potential as a biomimetic alternative.

Conclusion

Chlorhexidine remains the reference standard for cavity disinfection. Nisin demonstrated promising efficacy against the tested strains. Biorepair could be successfully recommended in caries management. S. mutans' marked resistance defines it as a primary target for antimicrobial intervention. Dentin, as a biological barrier, hinders the effectiveness of the tested antibacterial agents and should be considered in treatment planning.
