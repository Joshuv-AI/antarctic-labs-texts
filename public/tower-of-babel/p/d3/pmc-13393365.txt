Asymmetric Structure Design of Polymer Gel Electrolyte with a Functional Metal–Organic Framework Layer for Stable Lithium–Metal Batteries
Authors: Qi Cao, Taifan Yang, Weiguo Liu, Wei Tang, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ionogel electrolytes (IGEs) containing ionic liquids (ILs) as the unique liquid component can guarantee high ionic conductivity, a wide electrochemical window, and high thermal stability simultaneously for developing stable and safe Li metal batteries. Li+ transport in IGEs depends on the content of IL; however, increasing the IL content improves ionic conductivity but sharply decreases mechanical properties and leads to low Li+ transference, limiting the ability to resist Li dendrites. Thus, IGEs must strike a balance between ionic conductivity and mechanical properties. Herein, we adopt a new strategy to suppress Li dendrites by introducing a functional metal–organic framework (MOF) layer onto IGE. The IGE provides an asymmetric SSE (ASSE) with good interfacial contact, whereas the MOF layer regulates uniform dissolution/deposition of lithium and inhibits Li dendrite growth. Thereby, the ASSE not only demonstrates excellent ion-transport properties at 30 °C with an ionic conductivity of 1.1 × 10–4 S cm–1, but also enables the Li symmetric battery to cycle for 900 h stably. Besides, the full cell (LiFePO4 cathode) maintains 94.1% capacity after 200 cycles at 0.5C.

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries have been the dominant energy storage technology for decades, but their energy density limitations have accelerated the exploration of next-generation battery systems. − Lithium metal batteries (LMBs), featuring a lithium metal anode with an ultrahigh theoretical capacity (3860 mAh g–1) and a low reduction potential (−3.04 V vs SHE), are considered a promising solution to meet the surging demand for high-energy-density batteries. − However, the commercialization of LMBs is severely hindered by challenges such as uncontrolled lithium dendrite growth, unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), and safety issues. , Traditional liquid electrolytes exacerbate these problems due to their flammability and electrochemical reactivity. In this way, solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) have emerged as a viable alternative to address these issues with excellent mechanical strength, electrochemical, and thermal stability, which can effectively curtail side reactions with lithium metal electrodes and suppress lithium dendrite growth. − Among the numerous solid electrolytes, quasi-solid-state electrolytes, typically composed of a polymer matrix and plasticizer solvent, integrate the advantages of both liquid electrolytes and SSEs and achieve a combination of high ionic conductivity and high interfacial stability, along with high mechanical strength. , As the core component, ionic liquids (ILs) are regarded as ideal ionic solvents due to their high ionic conductivity, nonvolatility, and superior thermal stability. − Recently, there has been growing attention on ionogel electrolytes (IGEs) based on ILs. For example, Dong et al. fabricated flexible and highly conductive IGEs via a simple casting method. By incorporating the common IL [EMIM]­[FSI], they not only induced an anion-dominated solvation structure but also formed abundant Li+ transport channels through the interaction between the IL and the electron-rich groups on PVDF-HFP chains. Zhang et al. introduced a novel quasi-solid electrolyte composed of high-concentration IL and high-molecular-weight PEO. The unique interconnection and local arrangement of ion clusters between the IL and the polymer provided rich pathways for ion transport, resulting in an ionic conductivity of 5.6 × 10–4 S cm–1 at room temperature and significantly enhanced oxidation stability of PEO (5.1 V). However, the aforementioned works all introduced large amounts of ILs, leading to a significant decrease in the mechanical strength of the IGEs and inducing lithium dendrite growth. Thus, IGEs must strike a balance between ionic conductivity and mechanical properties, and it is crucial to develop an IGE with high ionic conductivity and good mechanical properties.

Herein, we report a bilayer asymmetric electrolyte architecture comprising a functionalized metal–organic framework (MOF) layer and a flexible ionogel layer based on a high-dielectric-constant PVDF-HFP polymer matrix. The MIL-101­(Fe) in the functional MOF layer possesses abundant and ordered pores, which could guide the uniform lithium deposition. Furthermore, its octahedral structure offers sufficient mechanical stability to suppress dendrite growth, while the numerous active metal sites effectively restrict the movement of anions, thereby enhancing the electrolyte’s Li+ transference number. On the other hand, the flexible ionogel layer ensures good interfacial contact with the electrode. The strong interaction between IL and PVDF-HFP polymer chains endows the ionogel with self-healing properties, which can further reduce the risk of dendrite penetration. As a consequence, the employment of asymmetric SSE (ASSE) gives rise to a dendrite-free Li anode with reinforced SEI, which is embodied in the highly reversible Li plating/stripping cycle for over 900 h at 0.5 mA cm–2. Moreover, the assembled Li/ASSE/LFP full cell demonstrates good cycling stability at room temperature, with a capacity retention of 94.1% over 200 cycles at 0.5C.

Experimental Section

Materials Synthesis

Synthesis of MIL-101­(Fe), MIL-101-HAc, MIL-101-HCl, and MIL101-HNO3

The original MIL-101 (Fe) was prepared with a simple solvothermal method previously reported with some modification. Initially, 1.351 g (0.005 mol) of FeCl3·6H2O and 0.415 g (0.0025 mol) of H2BDC were weighed and ultrasonicated in 30 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide for 30 min until complete dissolution, yielding a clear, transparent orange solution. This solution was then transferred to a sealed 50 mL autoclave and maintained at 110 °C for 20 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the resulting orange paste was centrifuged, washed three times with DMF to eliminate impurities, and subjected to multiple solvent exchanges in a hot methanol solution. Finally, the product was vacuum-dried at 120 °C overnight to obtain activated MIL-101­(Fe) crystals. Similarly, to prepare samples with different acid-induced defects (MIL-101-HAc, MIL-101-HCl, and MIL-101-HNO3), the same procedure was followed, with the exception of adding 375 μL of HAc, HCl, or HNO3 as modulators to the mixture before the heating reaction.

Synthesis of MIL-101:NO3

After sufficient activation, MIL-101­(Fe), MIL-101-HAc, MIL-101-HCl, and MIL-101-HNO3 are dispersed in a 2 mol/L methanol solution of lithium nitrate. The mixture is continuously stirred for 24 h to ensure adequate exchange of nitrate ions. After the ion exchange, the product is separated by centrifugation and transferred to a fresh lithium nitrate methanol solution. This procedure is repeated three times. Finally, the product is washed three times with methanol and dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 120 °C.

Preparation of the IL Electrolyte

IL electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.0015 mol of lithium bis­(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)­imide (LiTFSI) in 1 g of 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis­(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)­imide ([EMIM]­[TFSI]). After thorough stirring to achieve homogeneity and transparency, the electrolyte was transferred to a vacuum oven for drying overnight at 120 °C prior to subsequent use.

Preparation of the Asymmetric Bilayer Electrolyte

An asymmetric bilayer IGE membrane was fabricated via a straightforward layer-by-layer doctor-blading method. Initially, the IGE precursor slurry was uniformly cast onto a glass substrate using a 1000 μm doctor blade. To prevent rapid solvent evaporation from adversely affecting the membrane’s planar morphology, the gel layer was slowly dried in a sealed environment at room temperature overnight and then placed in a 60 °C vacuum oven for complete solvent removal. Subsequently, the MOF slurry was evenly coated onto the fully dried gel layer with a 250 μm doctor blade and dried in a 60 °C vacuum oven for 24 h. Finally, the bilayer electrolyte membrane was cut into circular pieces of diameter 19 mm using a precision slicer and transferred to a glovebox for storage.

Materials Characterizations

The morphologies and the element distribution of the samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, MAIA3 LMH) and the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings. The crystal structures were recorded by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured by a BSD-PM2 system (Beishide Instrument Technology) using a static volumetric method at 77 K. The thermostability of the electrolyte was detected by an infrared thermal imager (FOTRIC Fluke Ti480). The intrinsic analysis of chemical properties was conducted through Fourier transform infrared (FTIR, Nicolet iS50) spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Nexsa). Notably, the samples of Li deposition after the cycles were prepared in an Ar-filled glovebox and transferred into an SEM instrument immediately to avoid exposure to air.

To test the properties of ion transport, SS||SS symmetric cells and Li||Li symmetric cells in CR2032 type were assembled with different separators to estimate ionic conductivity (σ) and Li+ transference number (t Li +), respectively.

The SS||SS blocking cells were constructed with two stainless steel electrodes and 80 μL of LiTFSI as the electrolyte, tested by a Princeton electrochemical workstation with a frequency range from 10–1 to 105 Hz. The ionic conductivity was calculated from the following equation 1 σ=d/(Rb×S) where R b represents the bulk resistance, and d and S are the thickness and effective area of the separators, respectively.

The Li+ transference number was obtained by testing a Li||Li cell with chronoamperometry at a small polarization potential of 10 mV for 1800 s to reach steady state. The t Li + was calculated based on the following equation 2 tLi+=Is(ΔV−I0R0)/I0(ΔV−IsRs) where I 0, I s, R 0, and R s refer to the initial state current, steady-state current, initial state resistance, and steady-state resistance, respectively. ΔV is the polarized voltage applied across the cell.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were carried out in a PerkinElmer DSC 6000 equipment in the temperature range between 25 and 200 °C at 10 °C.min–1 under a nitrogen atmosphere. The degree of crystallinity (X c) was evaluated from the DSC thermograms using the following equation 3 Xc=ΔHmΔHm0×100 where ΔH m represents the heat of fusion of the polymer electrolyte membrane, and ΔH m 0 is the standard heat of fusion for completely crystalline PVDF-HFP.

Electrochemical Measurements

All electrochemical performances of the separators were evaluated by using CR2032-type coin cells assembled in an Air-filled glovebox with oxygen and moisture content less than 1 ppm. The applied electrolyte consists of 1.0 M LiTFSI in DME and DOL (v/v = 1:1). The cycling and rate performances were performed on a Neware battery test system at 25 °C. The EIS measurements of the coin cell were carried out on a Princeton electrochemical workstation over a frequency range from 10–1 to 105 Hz. To evaluate the stability of SSEs during lithium deposition and stripping, Li||Li symmetric cells were employed. Meanwhile, LFP||Li full-cell systems were used to assess the performance of solid-state Li–metal batteries during charge–discharge and cycle tests. For the full cells, the LFP cathode areal loading was 2.5 mg cm–2, and the lithium metal negative/positive capacity ratio (N/P ratio) was 300, calculated from the Li-foil thickness and the cathode areal capacity. It is worthy to note that thinner lithium foil should be used for practical application. The ASSE was assembled with the MOF layer facing the lithium–metal anode and the ionogel layer facing the LFP cathode. Specifically, 8 μL of IL electrolyte was added to the anode side, and 1.0 M LiTFSI DME/DOL (v/v = 1:1) ether electrolyte without additives was added to the cathode side to improve the interfacial contact between the ionogel layer and the porous LFP cathode. Therefore, the tested Li/ASSE/LFP cells should be regarded as a quasi-solid-state/semisolid-state lithium–metal battery configuration with limited liquid electrolyte confined at the electrode/electrolyte interfaces, rather than a completely dry all-solid-state battery.

Results and Discussion

The synthetic strategy was depicted in Figure a. It is possible to adjust the active centers, pore size, and conductivity of MOF by accurately modulating the electron property, pore environment, topology, and structure of MOF at the atomic or molecular level. − Four types of defective MIL-101­(Fe) were generated by adjusting the content of modulators. HAC, HCl, and HNO3 were used as modulators to tune the MIL101­(Fe) crystal growth in the process of synthesis, which could terminate the framework. Meanwhile, modulators could take part in the framework formation as a replacement of H2BDC on account of possessing a similar coordination mode. Therefore, defects were produced in the frameworks, which resulted in coordinatively unsaturated Fe sites as well as expanded pore sizes by means of combining adjoining pores. − The crystal structure of the defective MIL-101­(Fe) was characterized by XRD. As shown in Figure b, among the four samples, only the PXRD pattern of MIL-101-HNO3 completely matches the simulated curve from single-crystal data. Its main characteristic peaks are at 5.2°, 5.9°, 8.5°, 9.1°, and 16.5°, with no other impurity peaks. In contrast, the pristine MIL-101 and MIL-101-HAc and MIL-101-HCl samples exhibit peak absence and deviation due to the generation of impurity phases or local crystal-structure distortion. In addition, the SEM of the material morphology further supports the above conclusions. As shown in Figure S1, the four MIL-101­(Fe) samples prepared under different conditions all display a similar octahedral morphology with a particle size of approximately 1–2 μm. However, abnormal crystal morphologies distinct from the octahedral are observed in the MIL-101­(Fe), MIL-101-HAc, and MIL-101-HCl samples. In contrast, the MIL-101 sample treated with HNO3 as a modulator exhibits a uniform distribution of regular octahedral morphologies without significant abnormal crystals. This indicates that the HNO3 modulator can effectively control the growth of the MIL-101 framework, yielding high-phase-purity MIL-101­(Fe). − HNO3, as an inorganic acid modulator, can regulate the nucleation and coordination process of MIL-101­(Fe), thereby promoting the formation of a more phase-pure and uniform octahedral framework. Such a well-defined structure is beneficial for maintaining continuous porous channels and exposing accessible Fe-based Lewis acidic sites. These sites can interact with anions and promote lithium-salt dissociation, while the ordered pores provide confined pathways for selective Li+ migration. In contrast, impurity phases or distorted crystal structures in other MIL-101­(Fe) samples may interrupt ion-transport pathways and reduce the effective utilization of active Fe sites.

As shown in Figure S2, the ion-exchange procedure was further employed to introduce nitrate into the MIL-101-HNO3 framework. The as-prepared MIL-101:NO3 was characterized by XRD, as shown in Figure c. The XRD pattern of the MIL-101:NO3 is closely matched with that of MIL-101-HNO3, confirming that the crystal structure of MIL-101-HNO3 remains intact after loading LiNO3. As shown by the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of MIL-101:NO3 (Figures e and S3), the specific surface area and pore volume of MIL-101:NO3 are substantially reduced from 1759.15 m2 g–1 and 1.62 cm3 g–1 (MIL-101-HNO3) to 562.48 m2 g–1 and 0.47 cm3 g–1, respectively (Table S1), after incorporation of LiNO3. The reduced porosity verifies the hypothesis that LiNO3 is mainly encapsulated into the pores of MIL-101-HNO3, rather than precipitate alongside the MOF particles. Uniform distribution of N and Fe elements in MIL-101:NO3 is revealed by EDS elemental mapping (Figure g,h). Furthermore, XPS was employed to provide evidence for the incorporation of NO3 – into the MOF framework. As shown in Figure S17, the N 1s spectrum of the MIL-101:NO3 sample exhibits a prominent nitrogen peak. In contrast, the pristine MIL-101-HNO3 sample displays a negligible N 1s signal. This distinct compositional difference confirms that the NO3 – have been successfully encapsulated into the MOF pores following the ion-exchange procedure.

The XRD test results confirmed that the MOF membrane preparation process did not damage the basic structure of MIL-101­(Fe) (Figure a). The surface SEM image of the Fe-MIL-101-integrated SPE membrane reveals a smooth and dense surface (Figures b and S4). Additionally, the EDS mappings (Figure c) confirm the uniform distribution of Fe-MIL-101 throughout the membrane.

A series of SPEs were fabricated by adjusting the amount of Fe-MIL-101 added, resulting in four different compositions: SPE-0, SPE-5, SPE-10, and SPE-20 with 0, 5, 10, and 20% (by weight) of Fe-MIL-101, respectively. Figures d and S5 present the variations in the current and impedance of Li∥Li cells with different SPEs under polarization. The calculated t Li + of different SPEs, based on the Bruce–Vincent equation, − are shown in Figure e, and SPE-10 shows the highest t Li + of 0.33. To gain insights into the modulation of ion transport by MIL-101:NO3 doping, Raman spectroscopy was employed to analyze the ion-pairing of TFSI– in different electrolytes. Figures f and S6 show that the peak at 740 cm–1, arising from the S–N–S stretching vibration of free TFSI–, splits into two peaks near 740 cm–1 and 750 cm–1, corresponding to free and bound anions. , Fitting the peak areas allows calculation of the TFSI– percentages in these two states. In SPE–0, the bound-anion peak area is only 6%. Adding 10 wt % MIL-101 shows the highest value of 29%. The enhanced Li+ transference number of the MOF-containing SPE is attributed to the synergistic effects of Lewis acid–base interactions between the Fe active sites and TFSI– anions, together with the pore confinement effect of the MIL-101­(Fe) framework. The Fe sites can partially immobilize anions and promote Li-salt dissociation, while the porous channels preferentially restrict the transport of bulky anions and facilitate more selective Li+ migration.

The ionic conductivity of SPEs without FeMIL-101 (SPE-0) and with different Fe-MIL-101 concentrations (SPE-5, SPE-10, and SPE-20) was measured by EIS. At 30 °C, SPE-10 shows the highest conductivity of 1.45 × 10–4 S cm–1, whereas SPE-0 exhibits the lowest Li+ conductivity of 6.23 × 10–5 S cm–1. This significant improvement can be attributed to the enhanced availability of free Li+ in the system as a result of interaction between Fe3+ metal centers and the polymer chain, as well as the immobilization of anions by the positive −CH2– segments of the PVDF block. It is worth noting that the ionic conductivity of SPE-10 is higher than that of SPE-20, indicating that conductivity does not continuously increase with the Fe-MIL-101 concentration. After a certain concentration (SPE-10), Fe-MIL-101 might tend to agglomerate, leading to decreased ionic conductivity. Besides, Figures h and S7 present the DSC profiles of SPE-0 and SPE-10, scanned from 30 to 200 °C. The DSC profile of SPE-0 exhibits a melting point (T m) at 141.8 °C, whereas the DSC profile of SPE-10 shows a T m at around 133.2 °C. The lowered T m of SPE-10 suggests that the addition of Fe-MIL-101 enlarges the amorphous region within the PVDF-HFP polymer matrix and enhances the Li+ transport properties. ,

Subsequently, the morphology and elemental distribution of the ionogel layer were characterized. As shown in Figure a–d, the ionogel layer exhibits a smooth surface and compact structure without any pinholes, and the corresponding EDS mappings of F, N, and S elements confirm the uniform distribution of [EMIM]­[TFSI]. However, when the IL content was further increased to 60%, an increase in spherulites on the polymer surface was observed (Figure S8). This is attributed to the excessive IL acting as a nucleating agent, increasing the number of crystallization nuclei and thereby generating smaller spherulites with well-defined interfaces. , The XRD patterns of pristine PVDF-HFP and IL-PVDF-HFP are presented in Figure e. The characteristic peaks of PVDF-HFP) at 17.9°, 20.2°, 26.4°, and 38.7° confirm the presence of α-phase PVDF. After crosslinking with [EMIM]­[TFSI], the peak intensities of IL-PVDF-HFP are obviously reduced, indicating decreased crystallinity and a higher degree of amorphous phase.

PVDF-HFP comprises various crystalline phases, including the α, β, γ, and δ phases. The electroactive β phase, highly polar, interacts with lithium ions to regulate ion transport within the electrolyte (Figure S9). FTIR spectroscopy was used to evaluate the impact of different IL contents on the phase composition of PVDF-HFP. Figure f shows the ATR spectra of pristine PVDF-HFP and PVDF-HFP films composited with varying IL contents within the 650–1600 cm–1 range. The spectra reveal primary absorption bands at 760 and 796 cm–1, attributed to the stretching vibrations of CF2 and CH2 groups in the α phase. , Upon incorporation of [EMIM]­[TFSI], a significant increase in the absorption band at 840 cm–1, corresponding to the CH2 group in the electroactive β phase, was observed. Using eq , the β phase content was quantified, and it peaked at 67.1% when the IL content was 40%, indicating that IL incorporation alters the polymer chain arrangement, promoting a shift toward the highly polar β phase.

The ionic conductivity of the ionogel layer at 30 °C was assessed via EIS (Figure S10). As IL content increases, the ionic conductivity of the composite membrane rises, reaching 4.19 × 10–4 S cm–1 with 60 wt % IL. This improvement is attributed to more mobile ions and enhanced polymer amorphousness. DSC results in Figure S11 indicate that IL-doped ionogel polymers exhibit thermal behavior similar to pristine PVDF-HFP but with lower melting temperatures and enthalpies, suggesting that IL’s electrostatic interaction with the polymer matrix hinders crystallization. ,

To thoroughly examine the impact of ILs on the mechanical properties of the ionogel layer, mechanical tests were conducted using a stress–strain mechanical tester, with the resulting stress–strain curves presented in Figure g. As the IL content increases, the maximum strain of the ionogel membrane first increases and then declines. Specifically, when the IL content is 40%, the composite membrane attains its maximum strain and optimal ratio, indicating that a moderate amount of IL plasticizes PVDF-HFP, enhancing the composite membrane’s drawability. Conversely, when the IL content reaches 60%, the maximum strain decreases significantly, revealing that an excessive IL impairs the polymer matrix’s cross-linking and reinforcement.

Based on the above composition screening, SPE-10 and the ionogel containing 40 wt % IL were selected to construct the ASSE. For the MOF-containing layer, SPE-10 provides the best balance between anion immobilization and Li+ transport, showing the highest Li+ transference number and ionic conductivity among the tested SPEs. For the ionogel layer, although ionic conductivity increases with increasing IL content, the 40 wt % IL composition exhibits a more balanced combination of β-phase formation, mechanical flexibility, and structural integrity, whereas excessive IL weakens the polymer network. Therefore, the final asymmetric design combines a 10 wt % MIL-101:NO3-containing MOF layer for Li+ selectivity and anion regulation with a 40 wt % IL ionogel layer for interfacial compliance and mechanical robustness.

To validate the self-healing capability of the IL/PVDF-HFP cross-linked network, visible incisions were artificially created on the ionogel membrane using a scalpel. Subsequently, a small amount of IL electrolyte was deposited onto the membrane surface. Throughout the healing process, periodic observations were conducted by using an optical microscope. As depicted in Figure h,i, after a 2 h interval, the incisions on the composite gel membrane had substantially healed. This indicates the gel membrane’s effective response to damage and its capacity for autonomous repair. After an extended period of 4 h, the incisions nearly vanished completely. This occurred as the polymer backbone and [EMIM]­[TFSI] within the gel membrane were drawn together and recross-linked through ionic dipole interactions.

As illustrated in Figure a, the asymmetric bilayer electrolyte membrane (ASSE) is prepared layer by layer via the doctor blade coating method. Owing to the strong interlayer adhesion and excellent mechanical flexibility, the bilayer electrolyte membrane maintains structural integrity without damage after being repeatedly twisted and folded by external forces (Figure S12). As shown in Figures c and S13, the ionogel layer has a flat and smooth surface, enabling good interface contact with the cathode material. The MOF layer’s surface is relatively dense, with uniformly distributed MIL-101 particles that facilitate a uniform Li+ flux. The cross-sectional scan clearly reveals the layered ASSE structure. The MOF layer is tightly and evenly attached to the gel layer, with a thickness of just 14.3 μm and no obvious interlayer gaps. Fe-element mapping shows MIL-101 is evenly distributed on one side of the ASSE. Also, due to IL addition in the gel layer, the F-element content differs between layers.

The activation energy (E a) was calculated according to an Arrhenius plot (Figure S14), which reflects the energy barrier ions overcome within the electrolyte. Reported E a values represent the mean ± standard deviation derived from three independent, replicate measurements under identical experimental conditions. The ASSE shows an E a of 0.20 eV, lower than the 0.22 eV of the IGE layer. This indicates that the MIL-101 composite functional layer ASSE offers more ion-transport channels, enabling efficient ion conduction. In addition, as shown in Figure S15, the t Li + values of the MOF layer, ionogel layer, and ASSE are 0.33, 0.15, and 0.30, respectively. In the ionogel layer, the presence of a large amount of [EMIM]+ and TFSI– results in a low contribution of Li+ migration to total ionic conductivity. In contrast, the MOF layer with abundant pore size and active sites promotes effective Li+ conduction while restricting TFSI– migration, thereby endowing ASSE with a higher t Li + value.

In addition, the electrochemical and thermal stability of electrolyte materials are crucial for practical applications. As shown in Figure e, the ionogel layer’s electrochemical stability plateau is 5.0 V, higher than that of the MOF layer (4.5 V). To evaluate the thermal stability of the electrolyte membrane, thermogravimetric analysis of the MOF and ionogel membranes was conducted in a nitrogen atmosphere. Figure f shows that at 200 °C, all samples retain 90% of their initial weight due to the high thermal decomposition temperatures of MIL-101 and [EMIM]­[TFSI], indicating the electrolyte’s superior thermal stability and enhanced battery safety. For the ASSE membrane, a minor and gradual weight loss of approximately 3–6% is observed from room temperature up to 150–200 °C. This slight mass decay is ascribed to the volatilization of trace residual solvents trapped within the porous MIL-101 framework and the polymer matrix.

To evaluate the stability of ion transport and the interfacial stability of ASSE, tests on Li+ transport kinetics and interfacial polarization were conducted using Li/SPEs/Li symmetric cells. Figures a and S18 show the time–voltage curves of symmetric Li cells based on ASSE, MOF layer, ionogel, and homogeneous blend of MOF and ionogel layer at a current density of 0.5 mA cm–2 and a deposition capacity of 1 mAh cm–2. The ASSE demonstrated stable cycle performance with a low overpotential (34 mV) over 900 h. This was attributed to its high ionic conductivity, Li+ selectivity, and effective suppression of Li dendrite growth. In contrast, the ionogel-based cell showed high polarization overpotential (58 mV) and failed after 160 h due to its low Li+ selectivity and ionic conductivity. The MOF layer experienced a polarization surge at around 440 h due to poor interfacial contact. Meanwhile, the homogeneous blend membrane exhibited severe voltage fluctuations after approximately 240 h due to uncontrolled dendrite growth, which demonstrated the significant advantages of the double-layer structure in terms of electrochemical performance. At a higher current density of 1 mA cm–2, the ASSE-based cell maintained stable Li deposition/stripping for 700 h with voltage polarization below 120 mV (Figure b).

To intuitively evaluate the effectiveness of ASSE in achieving uniform lithium deposition, Li symmetric batteries after 100 cycles were disassembled, and the Li deposition morphology on the surface was observed by SEM. Figures c and S16 show the SEM images of the Li anode surfaces from lithium symmetric batteries based on the MOF layer, ionogel layers, and ASSE. After 100 cycles at 0.5 mA cm–2 and 1 mAh cm–2, the Li metal surface with ASSE maintained a dense and smooth morphology. In contrast, the anodes with only MOF or ionogel layers exhibited loose, porous structures and severe dendrite formation, leading to increased overpotential for Li deposition/stripping. These results support the improved ability of ASSE to regulate Li deposition. Such behavior is plausibly related to the combined effects of ion-flux homogenization and the mechanical robustness of the functional MOF layer, although the present data do not allow a strict separation of these two contributions. Besides, the lithium symmetric batteries with ASSE underwent impedance testing before and after cycling to explore the interfacial evolution during the Li deposition/stripping process. The Nyquist plots, divided into a high-frequency semicircle and a low-frequency line via an equivalent circuit model, corresponded to the total bulk and interfacial resistance and the capacitive behavior at the electrode–electrolyte interface. As shown in Figure d, after 900 h of cycling, the interfacial charge-transfer resistance of the symmetric battery with ASSE decreased from 288.5 Ω to 155.5 Ω. Similar results were reported in other MOF-based solid–electrolyte studies. This reduction suggests a progressive stabilization of the electrode/electrolyte interface during Li plating/stripping, which may be associated with improved interfacial compatibility.

In order to analyze the SEI film induced by the ASSE membrane and verify the effectiveness of the MIL-101:NO3-doped functional MOF layer in regulating the composition of the Li metal interface, XPS tests were carried out on the Li anode facing the MOF layer side after cycling. As shown in Figure e, the presence of Li3N and LiN x O y in the N 1s spectrum indicates that the NO3 – in the MIL-101:NO3 framework participates in SEI construction. This N-rich SEI composition, with its high ionic conductivity, effectively promotes Li+ transport at the interface. Additionally, in the F 1s spectrum, distinct peaks at 688.5 and 685 eV reveal the presence of LiF and –CF3 groups. After sputter-etching the anode surface with Ar+, the intensity of LiF increased at a depth of 20 nm, indicating its enrichment in the inner SEI film. Previous AFM-based studies have shown that Li3N-rich or LiNO3-derived inorganic-rich SEI layers usually exhibit much higher Young’s modulus than conventional organic-rich SEI layers. For instance, a Li3N-rich SEI was reported to show an average Young’s modulus of 6.85 GPa, compared with only 0.32 GPa for untreated Li, while an inorganic-rich SEI derived from ES + LiNO3 additives exhibited a Young’s modulus of 9.02 GPa. , Therefore, the Li3N/LiNxOy-containing SEI formed in our ASSE system is expected to provide enhanced mechanical robustness, which may contribute to suppressing dendrite growth together with improved Li+ transport at the interface. Additionally, LiF, a beneficial SEI component, has low electronic conductivity and a wide electrochemical window (∼6.4 V vs Li/Li+). Its low conductivity and wide electrochemical window enhance the passivation of the SEI, while its high mechanical modulus can suppress Li dendrite growth. Moreover, when LiF forms composites with other substances (e.g., Li2CO3 and Li2O) at the nanoscale, the Li+ transport rate at the interface could significantly increase. − In contrast, as shown in Figures S19 and S20, while beneficial LiF and C–F3 species are detected in the F 1s spectra across all three samples, a significant difference emerges in the N 1s spectra. Compared to the MOF layer and the ASSE, the SEI derived from the ionogel layer exhibits a drastically reduced nitrogen content, where the trace N elements originate solely from the decomposition of the IL. This comparative analysis unambiguously confirms that the functional MIL-101:NO3 layer serves as the primary nitrogen source, inducing a nitrogen-rich and highly conductive SEI that continuously facilitates uniform Li+ transport at the interface.

Based on the above research, the practicality of electrolyte materials in quasi-solid-state Li/SPEs/LFP full cells was further explored. Initially, cyclic performance tests were conducted on full cells using three types of polymer electrolyte membranes at different charge–discharge rates of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5C. As indicated in Figure a,b, the rate performance and charge–discharge curves at different rates show that ASSE has lower polarization and higher discharge specific capacity. The Li/ASSE/LFP cell delivers average discharge specific capacities of 151, 148, and 130 mAh g–1 at 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5C, respectively. These values are higher than the 148, 146, and 121 mAh g–1 for the MOF-layer electrolyte and the 144, 140, and 105 mAh g–1 for the ionogel-layer electrolyte. Moreover, ASSE exhibits a more stable discharge specific capacity across rates and can still recover to 150 mAh g–1 when the rate returns to 0.1C. This indicates that ASSE can recover quickly from polarization with its high electrochemical stability and ionic selectivity. As shown in Figure c,d, the Li/ASSE/LFP battery exhibits outstanding cycling stability. At a charge–discharge rate of 0.2C, the full cell with ASSE delivers a discharge specific capacity of 144.5 mAh g–1 and presents a capacity retention rate of 98.4% after 100 cycles with a Coulombic efficiency of 99.5%. Furthermore, at a higher charge–discharge rate of 0.5C, the ASSE full cell delivered an initial discharge capacity of 126.7 mAh g–1, with a capacity retention of 94.1% over 200 cycles. This is attributed to the high stability of ASSE toward the Li anode and its effective suppression of Li dendrite growth.

Based on the results above, we proposed the mechanistic model for LMBs using ASSE during electrochemical cycles (Figure e). On the one hand, the abundant and ordered porous structure of MIL-101 (Fe) can guide uniform Li deposition. Its octahedral structure with mechanical stability can effectively suppress dendrite growth. Also, the numerous active metal sites can bind with anions and restrict their movement, thus boosting the electrolyte’s Li+ transference number. The NO3 – introduced by ion exchange helps form a beneficial SEI layer, further stabilizing the Li metal anode. On the other hand, the flexible ionogel layer ensures good interfacial contact with the electrode. The strong interaction between IL and PVDF-HFP polymer chains gives it unique self-healing properties, which can further prevent dendrite penetration.

Conclusion

In summary, asymmetric bilayer-structural IGE composed of buffer layers and a reinforcement layer was fabricated for safe and highly stable solid-state LMBs. Benefiting from the ion selectivity and high modulus of MIL-101­(Fe), the functional MOF layer can not only transport ions selectively but also suppress the growth of lithium dendrites. Ionogel layers can greatly enhance the contact between IGE and electrodes. As a result, the ASSE showed a high ionic conductivity of 1.1 × 10–4 S cm–1 at 30 °C, a wide electrochemical window (up to 4.75 V vs Li/Li+), and high thermal stability (quality retention of ∼90% at 200 °C). The assembled Li metal battery demonstrated high electrochemical stability without apparent dendrite formation (94.1% retention after 200 cycles at 0.5C). The functional MIL-101:NO3 with open pores and metal sites can enrich the material pool for the design of other SSEs and promote the scalable production and practical application of IGE. Moreover, the structure design of the asymmetric bilayer-structural IGE provides a new avenue for IGE structure to realize a safe, stable Li–metal battery, revealing great prospects in the future.
