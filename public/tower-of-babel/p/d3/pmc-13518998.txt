Genome-wide identification and expression profiling of the bHLH transcription factor family under salt stress and hormonal treatments in cowpea (Vigna unguiculata)
Authors: Adhimoolam Karthikeyan, Gunasekaran Ariharasutharsan, Murugesan Tamilzharasi, Manoharan Akilan, Jeeva Kasiviswanathan Lekshmi, Muthaiyan Pandiyan, Natesan Senthil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) gene family, a major class of transcription factors in plants, plays critical role in growth, development, and responses to environmental stresses. Despite its importance, knowledge of bHLH gene functions in cowpea remains limited. Here, we conducted a comprehensive genome-wide in silico analysis of the cowpea bHLH family. A total of 60 VubHLH genes were identified and found to be non-uniformly distributed across the 11 chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis grouped 60 VubHLH and 127 AtbHLH genes into six distinct subgroups. Gene structural characteristics were conserved within subgroups but diversed among subgroups. Synteny analysis indicated that segmental duplication, rather than tandem duplication, was the primary driver of VubHLH family expansion. Promoter analysis revealed diverse cis-acting elements associated with plant growth, developmental processes, phytohormone regulation, and stress responses. Tissue-specific expression suggested functional diversification of VubHLH genes during evolution. qRT-PCR analysis showed that VubHLH1.1 and VubHLH11.1 genes were significantly upregulated under ABA, MeJA, and salt treatments, whereas VubHLH3.7, VubHLH5.1, and VubHLH6.3 were downregulated. Collectively, these results provide valuable insights into the evolutionary relationships and functional characteristics of the bHLH gene family and lay a foundation for future studies of bHLH gene function and improving the cowpea salt tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61580-w.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH), Cowpea, Genome-wide analysis, Transcription factorSubject terms: Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor family is a large and diverse group of proteins distributed across the plant kingdom. Members of the bHLH superfamily contain two functionally distinct, highly conserved domains that together comprise a region of approximately 60 amino acids1. The basic region mediates DNA binding, whereas the HLH motif facilitates protein-protein interactions2,3. bHLH transcription factors form homodimers or heterodimers with other proteins, including MYB, bZIP, and WRKY, thereby expanding their biological functions. In these complexes, bHLH proteins bind specific DNA sequences to regulate target gene transcription. The target genes regulated by bHLH transcription factors vary depending on the biological process or environmental stimulus involved4. The first plant bHLH gene was discovered in maize, and many bHLH proteins have since been characterized across diverse plant species. The number of bHLH genes identified includes 162 in Arabidopsis5, 167 in rice6, 152 in tomato7, 155 in common bean8, 261 in peanut9, 124 in potato10, 159 in wheat11, 85 in chickpea12, 141 in barley13, 183 in sunflower14, 122 in foxtail millet15, and 122 in grass pea16.

There is mounting evidence that bHLH genes play essential regulatory roles in diverse plant developmental and physiological processes, including cell differentiation, organogenesis, hormone signaling17,18, growth and development19,20, responses to biotic and abiotic stresses14,21–25, and anthocyanin biosynthesis26,27. Several bHLH transcription factors are involved in plant’s responses to abiotic stress. For instance, OsbHLH148 regulates the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway and OsJAZ protein activity, and its overexpression in rice improves drought tolerance28.

Liu et al.22 demonstrated that AtbHLH122 acts as a positive regulator that enhances salt stress tolerance through overexpression and knockout analyses. PebHLH35 from Populus euphratica enhances drought tolerance in Arabidopsis by modulating stomatal formation, photosynthetic activity, and plant growth29. Zhai, et al.30 isolated and characterized bHLH39 from wheat; overexpression of TabHLH39 in Arabidopsis improved tolerance to drought, salt, and freezing stress at the seedling stage. Similarly, overexpression of VvbHLH1 in grape enhanced flavonoid accumulation and increased salt and drought tolerance in Arabidopsis31. Ion transport is a key mechanism underlying salt tolerance, and the Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter NHX is regulated by several bHLH transcription factors. Krishnamurthy et al.32 reported that AtMYC2 and AtbHLH122 function as upstream regulators of the ABA-dependent Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers AtNHX1 and AtNHX6, both of which contribute to salt tolerance in Arabidopsis. OrbHLH001 from wild rice enhances salt tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis, whereas in cultivated rice, it maintains ionic balance and induces OsAKT1 expression under salt stress33,34. Additionally, in cucumber, overexpression of CsbHLH041 enhanced tolerance to NaCl and ABA in both transgenic Arabidopsis and cucumber35. Yu et al.36 showed that ZmbHLH55 positively regulates salt tolerance in maize by modulating ascorbic acid biosynthesis and the expression of genes involved in the GDP-mannose pathway.

Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), commonly referred to as black-eyed pea or southern pea, is a widely cultivated crop that contributes to sustainable agriculture and food security. Most Vigna species, including cowpea, are cultivated in semiarid regions due to their ability to thrive under challenging environmental conditions, including water deficit. Cowpea is a vital source of protein, minerals, essential amino acids, vitamins, and dietary fiber, making it a valuable component of balanced diets, particularly in regions with limited access to animal protein37,38. Despite these advantages, salinity can significantly reduce cowpea yield. Limited rainfall in semiarid regions intensifies salinity by reducing the effective leaching of salt ions from the soil. Salinity primarily affects plant growth and development, with the germination and seedling stages being particularly vulnerable. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms by which plants withstand salt stress and developing salt-tolerant varieties are essential for improving cowpea production. The evidence suggests that bHLH proteins play crucial roles in regulating plant responses to salt stress; however, the characterization and functional analysis of bHLH genes in cowpea remain limited. The availability of a high-quality genome sequence39 facilitates the genome-wide identification of bHLH genes in cowpea and subsequent analyses. In light of these developments, this study aims to conduct a comprehensive genome-wide analysis and characterization of bHLH transcription factors in cowpea to elucidate their roles in salt stress responses.

Materials and methods

Identification of the bHLH gene family in cowpea

The nucleotide and protein sequences of cowpea bHLH genes were obtained from the Plant Transcription Factor Database (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/; accessed August 2024). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile of the bHLH domain (PF00010) was retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org; accessed August 2024). The HMM profile was used as a query in HMMER 3.3 with an E-value threshold of 1e −5 to identify candidate bHLH proteins in the cowpea genome. The resulting sequences were manually curated to remove redundant entries. To validate the presence of the bHLH domain, candidate protein sequences were analysed using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi; accessed August 2024) and the SMART database (http://smart.embl.de/; accessed August 2024). Sequences with truncated or incomplete bHLH domains were excluded from further analysis. All the bHLH protein sequences from Arabidopsis were also retrieved from TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) for comparative analysis. Physicochemical properties of the identified proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight, theoretical pI, grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), instability index, and aliphatic index were calculated using ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/; accessed August 2024). Subcellular localization of VubHLH proteins was predicted using BUSCA (http://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/; accessed August 2024).

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

The identified bHLH proteins were aligned using ClustalW (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/clustalw/; accessed August 2024) with default parameters. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (N-J) method in MEGA 1140, based on the aligned sequences. Tree robustness was assessed using the bootstrap method with 1000 replicates. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and edited using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/; accessed August 2024). A combined dataset of VubHLH and AtbHLH protein sequences was used for comparative phylogenetic analysis. Conserved amino acid sequences were compared and annotated using GenDoc, and motif logos were generated using WebLogo (http://weblogo.threeplusone.com; accessed August 2024).

Gene structure, conserved motifs, and chromosomal distribution

The cowpea genome annotation file was used to extract exon–intron organization and splicing phase information for the predicted VubHLH genes. Gene structures were visualized using Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/; accessed August 2024). Conserved motifs in bHLH protein sequences were identified using the Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME) Suite (http://meme-suite.org/; accessed August 2024), with parameters set to a maximum of 20 motifs and a minimum of two sites per motif. The chromosomal distribution of cowpea bHLH genes was analysed based on their physical positions. Genes were mapped to chromosomes using annotation data, including chromosome numbers and genome coordinates. The distribution of bHLH genes across cowpea chromosomes was visualized using TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools/releases; accessed August 2024).

Synteny analysis and cis-regulatory element prediction for promoters

Inter-chromosomal synteny relationships between cowpea and Arabidopsis, based on VubHLH and AtbHLH proteins and their homologous gene pairs, were visualized using the Circos module implemented in TBtools. Synteny analysis of bHLH genes was conducted in cowpea, rice, and soybean. Genome sequences and annotation files for these species were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/; accessed August 2024). These data were analysed using Multiple Collinearity Scan Toolkit (MCScanX) to identify synteny relationship across species. Homologous gene pairs between cowpea and rice, and between cowpea and soybean were identified using the Dual Synteny Plot function in MCScanX41. For cis-regulatory element analysis, 2000 bp upstream sequences from transcription start sites of all bHLH genes were retrieved from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/; accessed August 2024) and analysed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/; accessed August 2024) to identify potential cis-acting elements. Identified elements were mapped to their respective positions within the 2000 bp upstream regions of the bHLH genes.

miRNA target prediction and regulatory network analysis

To predict miRNA target sites in VubHLH genes, cDNA sequences were used as targets. V.unguiculata miRNA sequences were retrieved from miRBase database (http://www.mirbase.org/; accessed August 2024) and used to query the CDS using the psRNATarget server with default parameters. Predicted miRNA- target interactions were visualized using Sankey plot generated in R. Protein–protein interaction networks of VubHLH proteins were predicted using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/; accessed August 2024), using Arabidopsis thaliana as the reference species.

Investigation of tissue-specific expression profiles

RNA-seq data from leaf, stem, root, and flower tissues were retrieved from the NCBI BioProject (PRJNA389300) and used to assess VubHLH genes expression across tissues in V. unguiculata. Expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM), using TBtools. Mean FPKM values for each tissue are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Plant materials and salt stress treatment

Cowpea cv. CO (CP) 7 seeds were obtained from the National Pulses Research Centre, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Vamban, India and were used in this study. Seeds were placed between layers of moistened sterile paper and incubated in Petri dishes at 25 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod for three days. The germinated seedlings were transferred to hydroponic containers containing one-fifth-strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution. After 10 days, the seedlings were treated with 100 µM abscisic acid (ABA), 200 µM methyl jasmonate (MeJA), or 200 mM sodium chloride (NaCl). Leaf and root samples from the ABA- and MeJA- and NaCl-treated plants were collected at 4, 8, and 12 h post-treatment and used for qRT-PCR analysis. Untreated plants served as controls.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from various cowpea tissues using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed and then converted to cDNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), and the resulting cDNA was used as a template for qRT-PCR. Gene-specific primer pairs were designed using Primer Premier 6.0, and qRT-PCR was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Each 20 µL qRT-PCR reaction contained 2.5 µL of first-strand cDNA, 1.0 µL of gene-specific primers (10 µM), 10 µL of AccuPower® 2× GreenStar™ SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad), and 6.5 µL of nuclease-free water. Each reaction included three independent biological replicates. For normalization, Actin42 was used as the internal reference gene and the relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. All primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Results

Primary characteristics of bHLH family members in cowpea

In the cowpea genome, 60 bHLH genes were identified and were designated as VubHLH1.1–1.7, 2.1–2.8, 3.1–3.12, 4.1–4.2, 5.1–5.3, 6.1–6.5, 7.1–7.5, 8.1–8.2, 9.1–9.4, 10.1–10.9, and 11.1–11.3 after confirmation of the presence of the bHLH domain and based on their chromosomal positions. The protein sequences varied in length from 165 amino acids (VubHLH3.4) to 666 amino acids (VubHLH1.4). Theoretical isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 4.78 (VubHLH3.8) to 9.44 (VubHLH3.9 and VubHLH9.4), reflecting the electrochemical diversity of the family. Molecular weights ranged from 18.19 kDa (VubHLH3.4) to 74.10 kDa (VubHLH7.4). All VubHLH proteins exhibited negative GRAVY values, indicating that they are predominantly hydrophilic. The instability index ranged from 34.42 (VubHLH10.3) to 77.28 (VubHLH11.3), with VubHLH10.3 predicted to be the most stable protein. Subcellular localization analysis indicated that 55 VubHLH proteins localize to the nucleus, one was localized to the chloroplast outer membrane, and four were localized to the chloroplast stroma. Detailed characteristics of the VubHLH genes are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Phylogenetic analysis, multiple sequence alignment and DNA-binding activity

Evolutionary relationships within the cowpea bHLH family were inferred using a phylogenetic analysis of 60 VubHLH (V. unguiculata) and 127 AtbHLH (A. thaliana) protein sequences (Fig. 1). The 60 VubHLH proteins were classified into six distinct subgroups based on their phylogenetic topology, consistent with the established bHLH nomenclature in A. thaliana. Subgroups 1 and 2 contained the fewest members. Subgroup 1 contained VubHLH3.10 and AtbHLH5.2, whereas subgroup 2 contained VubHLH1.4 and AtbHLH1.8, 3.2, 2.17, 1.6, 1.20, 4.28, and 4.27. Subgroup 6 contained the largest number of members, including 25 VubHLH and 36 AtbHLH proteins. Sequence alignment of the VubHLH domains revealed four highly conserved segments: two α-helices, a loop, and a basic region (Fig. 2a). Sixteen conserved amino acid residues showed consensus frequencies exceeded 50% (Fig. 2b). Notably, Arg31, Leu42, Ala62, and Leu76 were highly conserved, with consensus rates above 75%. Arg31 and Leu42 were located in the basic and first helical regions, respectively, whereas Ala62 and Leu76 resided in the second helical region. Based on classification criteria used for A. thaliana, the DNA-binding potential of the VubHLH basic region was assessed (Supplementary Table S3). A total of 43 VubHLH proteins were classified as E-box-binding proteins and eight as non-E-box-binding. Among the E-box-binding proteins, 42 were G-box-binding, whereas one was classified as a non-G-box-binding. Additionally, 9 of the 60 VubHLH proteins were classified as non-DNA-binding.

VubHLH genes structure and conserved motifs

Gene structure was determined by analysing exon–intron organization (Fig. 3a). VubHLH genes showed a variable number of exons and introns, ranging from 1 to 11 exons. Seven genes contained a single exon, whereas the remainder contained two or more exons. The most frequent exon number was four (12 genes), followed by five exons (nine genes). Subgroup I exhibited a higher number of exons, ranging from five in VubHLH2.1 to 11 in VubHLH1.4. In contrast, Subgroup IX had the lowest average exon number (3.16 exons), with six genes containing a single exon. VubHLH1.4 contained the highest number of introns (10). Most genes contained two (six genes), three (12 genes), four (eight genes), or five introns (six genes). Twenty conserved motifs were identified within the VubHLH proteins (Fig. 3b). Motif 2 was present in most proteins, whereas motif 1 was found in all except VubHLH2.1, 9.3, 3.3, 10.2, 7.1, 5.1, 11.1, and 6.5. Motifs 1 and 2 co-occurred in most proteins (53 out of 60), and were located in close spatial proximity, suggesting functional relevance. VubHLH10.3, VubHLH6.3, VubHLH3.7, and VubHLH2.6 each contained eight conserved motifs, the highest number. VubHLH9.3 and VubHLH3.3 each contained a single motif (motif 2), whereas VubHLH10.6, VubHLH1.6, VubHLH2.8, and VubHLH1.5 each contained two motifs (motifs 1 and 2).

Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis of VubHLH genes

VubHLH genes were unevenly distributed across the 11 chromosomes of cowpea (Fig. 4). Chromosome 3 (Chr3) contained the largest number of VubHLH genes (12), while chromosomes 4 (Chr4) and 8 (Chr8) each contained two genes. The remaining chromosomes contained 3–9 genes. Among the 60 VubHLH genes, 15 segmental duplication gene pairs were identified, while no tandem duplications were detected (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 4). Ka/Ks ratios were calculated for these gene pairs to assess evolutionary selection pressure (Supplementary Table 4). Twelve gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks ratios greater than one, indicating positive selection, whereas three pairs—VubHLH2.4–VubHLH3.5, VubHLH3.1–VubHLH4.1, and VubHLH6.2–VubHLH7.5—had Ka/Ks ratios below one, indicating purifying selection. Inter-genomic synteny analysis identified 16 collinear gene pairs between cowpea and rice (VubHLH–OsbHLH) and 146 orthologous gene pairs between cowpea and soybean (VubHLH–GmbHLH) (Fig. 6; Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Cis-elements and miRNA targets analysis

To elucidate the functional roles of VubHLH genes, a putative cis-acting elements within the 2000 bp upstream promoter regions were analysed (Fig. 7). The identified elements were categorized into three functional groups: five associated with phytohormone responses, 14 with plant growth and development, and 10 with stress responses. The highest number of cis-acting elements was associated with plant growth, development, and phytohormone regulation. The stress-response group included ABA-responsive, light-responsive, and anaerobic induction elements, each present in different subsets of VubHLH genes. miRNA target prediction for VubHLH genes provided insight into the interaction among plant development, stress adaptation, and post-transcriptional regulation. A total of eight miRNAs were predicted to target 14 VubHLH genes. Four genes—VubHLH7.3, VubHLH3.2, VubHLH10.3, and VubHLH9.4—contained two binding sites for specific miRNAs. Among these, vun-MIR164 exhibited the highest number of targets, with seven binding sites across six VubHLH genes. vun-MIR482 and vun-MIR156a targeted three and two genes, respectively, each with three binding sites. Most other miRNAs were predicted to target one or two genes (Fig. 8). These miRNA-binding sites may play important roles in regulating plant development and responses to abiotic stress.

Regulatory network between VubHLH

Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed based on the homologs of VubHLH proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 9). Most VubHLH proteins were predicted to interact with multiple family members. VubHLH1.3 exhibited the highest connectivity, interacting with seven proteins, followed by VubHLH1.1, which interacted with six. FAMA, homologous to VubHLH2.2, was predicted to interact with SCRM, which is homologous to VubHLH1.2, VubHLH3.2, and VubHLH9.4; both are involved in stomatal development. PIF1 (VubHLH1.5) and PIF3 (VubHLH1.6) were predicted to interact and are known to regulate seed germination in darkness and participate in phytochrome signaling. BIM1 (VubHLH3.10) was predicted to interact with MYC2 (VubHLH3.7), BEE1 (VubHLH4.2), and BHLH94 (VubHLH7.5), which are all associated with brassinosteroid signaling.

Expression profiling of VubHLHs in different tissues

We analysed the expression levels of VubHLH genes using transcriptomic datasets from leaf, stem, root, and flower tissues (Fig. 10). Log2-transformed FPKM values were used for analysis, revealing tissue-specific expression patterns. Of the 60 identified VubHLH genes, 19 showed uniformly low expression levels across all four tissues. Seventeen genes showed moderate expression, whereas 24 were highly expressed, particularly in the roots. Many genes also showed elevated expression in the stem, while fewer showed tissue-specific expression in the stems and flowers. Notably, VubHLH2.6, VubHLH9.1, VubHLH5.1, VubHLH10.3, VubHLH7.5, VubHLH3.7, VubHLH6.3, VubHLH10.4, and VubHLH3.12 showed higher expression in the roots than in other tissues. The genes VubHLH3.5, VubHLH3.9, and VubHLH9.2 were highly expressed specifically in stems and flowers. Although most homologous genes exhibited similar expression profiles across tissues, several genes showed distinct tissue-specific expression patterns. These results suggest that VubHLH genes play diverse roles in regulating plant growth, development, and stress responses in cowpea.

VubHLHs response to hormone and salt stress

qRT-PCR analysis of 15 selected VubHLH genes revealed that VubHLH genes showed comparable expression trends in roots and leaves. VubHLH1.1 was strongly induced in both leaves and roots by ABA and MeJA, with peak expression generally observed between 4 and 8 h. Likewise, VubHLH7.3, VubHLH10.4, and VubHLH11.1 exhibited consistent upregulation in both tissues, although transcript accumulation was generally stronger and more sustained in roots than in leaves.

VubHLH5.1 and VubHLH6.3 were consistently repressed by both hormones in leaves and roots, whereas VubHLH3.7 showed only minor transcriptional variation in either tissue (Supplementary Figs. 1–4). VubHLH7.1 expression peaked at 12 h after ABA treatment but was suppressed within 12 h of MeJA exposure in leaves and roots. Similarly, VubHLH10.3 peaked at 4 h and was downregulated at later time points under ABA in leaves and roots, whereas under MeJA it was downregulated at 4 and 8 h but highly expressed at 12 h. Under salt stress, several VubHLH genes exhibited distinct temporal expression patterns. VubHLH1.1, VubHLH 3.9, VubHLH10.3, and VubHLH11.1 showed induced expression at different time points. VubHLH2.8, VubHLH3.7, VubHLH3.12, VubHLH5.1, VubHLH6.3, and VubHLH7.1, were significantly downregulated in response to salt stress. VubHLH7.3, VubHLH9.1 and VubHLH10.4, was significantly upregulated at 4 h, followed by a sharp decline (Supplementary Figs. 5–6). Overall, these results highlight roles of VubHLH genes in mediating hormonal and salt stress responses in cowpea.

Discussion

In this study, we identified 60 VubHLH genes, providing insights into their evolutionary relationships, structural features, and potential functional roles in cowpea. Phylogenetic analysis grouped VubHLH genes into six subgroups alongside Arabidopsis thaliana bHLHs, suggesting that major lineage diversification preceded species divergence43. Structural analysis revealed strong conservation of key residues, particularly Arg31, Leu42, Ala62, and Leu76, highlights significant selective pressure to maintain DNA-binding capacity and protein dimerization stability44–46. These conserved features, along with overall sequence similarity, support a common ancestry and functional conservation across the species47,48. Notably, variation in DNA-binding properties, including G-box /non-G-box recognition and the presence of non-DNA-binding members, highlights the functional diversification with in the VubHLH family19,49. The exon-intron organization of VubHLH genes exhibited substantial variability, ranging from 1 to 11, and introns from 0 to 10, indicating dynamic evolutionary processes such as exon gain/loss and insertion/deletion events. This variability reveals the structural diversity, evolutionary history, and functional divergence characteristic of transcription factor gene families50. The presence of both single- and multi-exon genes suggests distinct evolutionary origins, with intronless genes likely arising from retrotransposition and multi-exon genes shaped by exon shuffling and domain rearrangement51. The absence of introns in several VubHLH members (i.e.,VubHLH3.7 and VubHLH10.4) may enhance transcriptional efficiency and responsiveness to environmental stress52,53. In contrast, VubHLH1.4 contains ten introns, showed the most complex exon–intron architecture. Such structural complexity may implies potential alternative splicing and functional diversification, supporting their diverse roles in plant development and environmental adaptation54,55. Conserved Motif 2 was identified in most VubHLHs, consistent with findings in Solanum lycopersicum, Zea mays, Hordeum vulgare, and Ginkgo biloba13,56,57, highlighting evolutionary conservation of bHLH transcription factors. Such conserved motifs often correspond to functional domains involved in DNA binding, protein–protein interactions, and transcriptional regulation58,59. The widespread presence of motif 2 suggests its crucial role in maintaining structural integrity and shared functional roles, particularly in transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Despite sequence diversity within the VubHLH family, this motif may facilitate protein–protein interactions essential for complex formation and downstream gene regulation. Functional analysis of motif 2-containing genes may reveal links to specific biological pathways. Its evolutionary conservation suggests strong selective pressure to maintain this motif due to its critical regulatory role60. Moreover, segmental duplication analysis identified 15 duplicated gene pairs, suggesting that large-scale genomic events, such as chromosomal rearrangements or whole-genome duplication, contributed to family expansion61. Tandem duplications arising through unequal crossing over drive rapid functional diversification and expansion of plant gene families during evolution. The absence of tandem duplications in VubHLH genes indicates a distinct evolutionary pattern of gene family expansion in cowpea, likely driven by specific selective pressures and functional constraints that limit redundancy and diversification process62,63. Inter-genomic synteny analysis between cowpea and the model species revealed evolutionary conservation of bHLH genes. Sixteen collinear gene pairs identified between cowpea and rice indicate conserved genomic organization and potential functional similarities. A substantially higher number of orthologous pairs with soybean (146 gene pairs) suggests lineage-specific expansion closer evolutionary relatedness among cowpea and soybean64. These conserved relationships provide a basis for understanding common regulatory mechanisms and biological functions, highlighting evolutionary dynamics of the bHLH genes and supporting their roles in plant development and adaptation35,65,66.

Investigating VubHLH genes is important for understanding plant growth, development, and stress responses. Promoter analysis revealed abundant growth-, hormone-, and stress-responsive cis-elements. The enrichment of auxin-, gibberellin-, and ABA-responsive elements suggests that VubHLH genes play important roles in hormone-mediated signaling and stress adaptation pathways67,68. These regulatory elements support that VubHLH genes integrate developmental and hormonal signals, thereby contributing to plant adaptation under changing environmental conditions. The presence of stress-responsive elements linked to ABA, light, and anaerobic conditions, further emphasizes VubHLH genes involvement in stress tolerance mechanisms69,70. In addition, protein–protein interaction analysis demonstrated that VubHLH proteins function in complex regulatory networks. Key members such as VubHLH1.3 and VubHLH1.1 exhibited multiple interactions, associated with seven and six other VubHLH proteins, indicating central roles in transcriptional regulation. Moreover, interactions with proteins such as FAMA and SCRM in stomatal development71, PIF1 with PIF3 in seed germination and light signaling, and BIM1 with MYC2, BEE1, and BHLH94 in brassinosteroid signaling further support their involvement in diverse biological processes72.

Four genes (VubHLH7.3, VubHLH3.2, VubHLH10.3, and VubHLH9.4) possess dual miRNA target sites, indicating regulatory specificity and complexity of post-transcriptional regulation73,74. vun-MIR164 targets six genes through seven distinct sites, suggesting a multifaceted regulatory role. Other miRNAs, such as vun-MIR482 and vun-MIR156a, showed distinct targeting patterns, highlighting miRNAs role in fine-tuning transcriptional regulation in plants75. Furthermore, tissue-specific expression analysis showed VubHLH genes exhibit diverse expression patterns, suggesting functional divergence; most genes were highly expressed in stem and root tissues, while some were specific to stem and flower. In particular, VubHLH2.6, VubHLH9.1, VubHLH5.1, VubHLH10.3, VubHLH7.5, VubHLH3.7, VubHLH6.3, VubHLH10.4, and VubHLH3.12 exhibited high expression in the root, while VubHLH3.5, VubHLH3.9, and VubHLH9.2 were uniquely and strongly expressed in the stem and flower. Although many homologous gene pairs showed similar expression, while others showed tissue-specific expression, indicating diverse roles in plant development and environmental adaptation in cowpea.

We selected 15 bHLH genes for ABA-, MeJA-, and salt-stress response analyses based on three criteria: the presence of complete bHLH domains and stress-responsive cis-regulatory elements, sequence homology with previously characterized stress-responsive bHLH genes from Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, and their predicted roles in ABA signaling, ROS regulation, and ion homeostasis. ABA is an important hormone regulating plant response to dehydration and salinity by activating transcription factors to regulate stress-responsive genes76 and influencing seed development and germination77. MeJA enhances salt tolerance by promoting plant growth, boosting antioxidant defenses, and reducing excessive sodium accumulation under various stress condition78,79. Our expression studies via qRT-PCR analysis showed that VubHLH genes exhibited broadly similar expression patterns in both roots and leaves; In particular, VubHLH1.1, VubHLH10.3, VubHLH11.1, VubHLH3.7, VubHLH3.12, VubHLH5.1, VubHLH6.3, and VubHLH7.1 exhibited remarkable differential responses to ABA, MeJA, and salt stress. These findings suggest that VubHLH gene expression is tightly regulated by environmental and phytohormone signals, enabling cowpea adaptation and response to changing conditions and salt stress. However, functional validation through knockdown and overexpression is needed to clarify VubHLH roles in salinity tolerance. In summary, this study comprehensively characterized the cowpea bHLH gene family, including physicochemical properties, structural features, chromosomal distribution, and stress-responsive expression patterns. These findings provide a foundation for future functional analyses and offer potential targets for improving salinity tolerance in cowpea.
