Characteristics of non-suicidal self-injury and its associations with thyroid and sex hormones in adolescents with major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder
Authors: Na Zhang, Yuanyuan Dai, Lin Zhao, Chengcong Wu, Zhihai Zhou, Cheng Yang, Hongxian Shen, Kan Zhang, Shucai Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is prevalent among adolescents with mood disorders and represents an important clinical risk factor for future suicidal behaviors. Although thyroid and sex hormones have been implicated in emotional and behavioral dysregulation, whether their associations with NSSI differ between major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) remains unclear. This study examined NSSI characteristics and their associations with thyroid and sex hormones in adolescents with MDD and BD.MethodsThis retrospective study included 471 adolescents diagnosed with MDD or BD. Demographic characteristics and hormone levels were compared within each diagnostic group. Sex-stratified binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with NSSI. Additionally, adolescents with NSSI were compared between the MDD and BD groups.ResultsThe prevalence of NSSI was 58.3% in adolescents with BD and 66.0% in those with MDD. Females showed significantly higher rates of NSSI than males in BD (p < 0.001) and MDD (p < 0.001) groups. Among adolescents with BD, those with NSSI had an earlier age at onset (p = 0.007) and lower testosterone levels (p < 0.001) than those without NSSI. Sex-stratified logistic regression showed that lower thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (OR = 0.789, 95% CI:0.633–0.983) and earlier age at onset (OR = 0.801, 95% CI: 0.648–0.990) were significantly associated with NSSI in female patients, whereas higher testosterone levels were associated with NSSI in male patients (OR = 1.226, 95% CI: 1.012–1.484). In adolescents with MDD, those with NSSI showed lower testosterone (p = 0.040) and TSH levels (p = 0.040) and an earlier age at onset (p < 0.001) than those without NSSI; In sex-stratified logistic regression analyses, earlier age at onset remained significantly associated with NSSI in both male (OR = 0.577, 95% CI: 0.356–0.935) and female patients (OR = 0.744, 95% CI: 0.623–0.888), whereas none of the hormone indices were independently associated with NSSI in the sex-stratified analyses (p > 0.05). Further comparisons showed that, among adolescents with NSSI, there were no demographic or hormonal differences between the BD and MDD groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionsTestosterone and TSH showed sex-specific associations with NSSI in adolescents with BD, whereas earlier age at onset was more consistently associated with NSSI in adolescents with MDD. These findings highlight the importance of considering both diagnostic subtype and sex when assessing NSSI risk in adolescents. However, given the limitations of the present study, further validation is required.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Bipolar disorder, Testosterone, Thyroid hormones, Non-suicidal self-injury

Background

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the deliberate, repetitive destruction of one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent, such as cutting, scratching, or hitting oneself [1]. In recent years, NSSI has attracted increasing global attention, particularly among adolescents. A meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of NSSI was 17.2% among adolescents, 13.4% among young adults, and 5.5% among adults, highlighting adolescence as a high-risk developmental period for NSSI [2]. Furthermore, individuals who initiated self-injury at or before the age of 12 exhibited more lifetime self-injurious than those who began after the age of 17, suggesting that early-onset NSSI may be associated with more persistent and severe clinical outcomes [3]. Currently, the lifetime prevalence among adolescents is approximately 22.0%, with a recurrence rate of up to 20.3% [4]. In China, prevalence is higher, reaching 24.7%; children and adolescents show higher rates than university students [5], highlighting the need for prompt identification and intervention.

Previous studies have shown that NSSI is closely associated with emotional dysregulation, impaired impulse control, and other psychiatric disorders, and is linked to an increased risk of subsequent suicidal behaviors [6]. Increasing evidence suggests that NSSI is a transdiagnostic symptom that is particularly common among patients with mood disorders, with prevalence rates reaching up to 66% [7]. Compared with individuals without NSSI, those with NSSI often exhibit more severe anxiety and depressive symptoms. Although psychosocial factors such as childhood trauma, school bullying, and family dysfunction have been widely associated with NSSI [8], these factors cannot fully explain its underlying mechanisms. Therefore, increasing attention has been directed toward the biological mechanisms underlying NSSI in recent years.

Against this background, adolescence represents a critical developmental period characterized by rapid maturation of the neuroendocrine system, during which hormonal fluctuations may contribute to the occurrence of NSSI by influencing emotional regulation, impulse control, and stress responses. Recent neurobiological and endocrine studies further suggest that the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis and hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, both of which are closely involved in emotional regulation, may play important roles in the development of NSSI [9]. Given the substantial differences between major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) in illness course, emotional regulation patterns, and potential neuroendocrine mechanisms, different mood disorder subtypes may exhibit distinct patterns of NSSI and related biological characteristics.

Thyroid hormones, the major end products of the HPT axis, are closely involved in emotional regulation, neurotransmitter metabolism, and neurodevelopment [10]. Hormones such as thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and free thyroxine (FT4) have been implicated in mood disorders and NSSI [11]. Previous studies have suggested that altered thyroid function may be associated with emotional dysregulation and self-harm-related behaviors. For example, FT4 levels were reported to be significantly lower in individuals with self-harm or suicidal behaviors, whereas findings regarding TSH and FT3 have remained inconsistent [12, 13]. Other studies have reported elevated TSH levels in patients with mood disorders [14]. This suggests that thyroid function may influence self-injurious impulses through complex mechanisms. However, heterogeneity in study samples, including medication use and comorbidities, has limited the consistency of findings.

Puberty is a critical period for rapid brain remodeling, during which emotional regulation and impulse control are not yet fully mature, thereby increasing vulnerability to impulsive behaviors such as self-injury [15]. Testosterone levels rise significantly in puberty and are considered an important factor influencing central nervous system development and behavioral changes in adolescents [16]. As a major hormone of the HPG axis, testosterone is closely linked to risk-taking behaviors, impulse control, and emotional regulation [17, 18]. Testosterone can cross the blood–brain barrier and modulate neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine, GABA, and serotonin. These actions influence emotional and behavioral responses [19]. Some studies have reported that adolescents with depression who engage in self-injury may have higher testosterone levels than their non-injurious peers [11]. However, findings regarding testosterone and self-injurious behaviors have remained inconsistent [20].

Previous studies have preliminarily examined abnormalities in thyroid and sex hormones in patients with mood disorders and their potential associations with NSSI. However, whether these associations differ between MDD and BD and across sexes remains unclear. Given the distinct neuroendocrine characteristics and clinical manifestations of MDD and BD [21], further investigation of the relationships between thyroid hormones, sex hormones, and NSSI in mood disorders is warranted. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the characteristics of NSSI and their associations with thyroid and sex hormones in adolescents with MDD and BD. We hypothesized that the associations between hormone levels and NSSI would differ by diagnosis and sex.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study included adolescent inpatients with mood disorders. These patients were hospitalized at the Fourth People’s Hospital of Wuhu, Anhui Province, China, between January 2020 and December 2021.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

A diagnosis of MDD or BD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), confirmed jointly by experienced attending psychiatrists and senior physicians. For participants with BD, only those who were hospitalized during an acute depressive episode were eligible for inclusion;

A diagnosis of MDD or BD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), confirmed jointly by experienced attending psychiatrists and senior physicians. For participants with BD, only those who were hospitalized during an acute depressive episode were eligible for inclusion;

Age between 13 and 19 years;

Age between 13 and 19 years;

Complete, reliable medical records.

Complete, reliable medical records.

The exclusion criteria were as follows:

Comorbid psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia spectrum disorders or pervasive developmental disorders) or serious neurological conditions;

Comorbid psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia spectrum disorders or pervasive developmental disorders) or serious neurological conditions;

History of or current endocrine, andrological, or gynecological disorders; thyroid dysfunction; metabolic syndrome; gout; hypertension; long-term liver or kidney problems; asthma; or any other major physical illness;

History of or current endocrine, andrological, or gynecological disorders; thyroid dysfunction; metabolic syndrome; gout; hypertension; long-term liver or kidney problems; asthma; or any other major physical illness;

Pregnancy or lactation at the time of hospitalization;

Pregnancy or lactation at the time of hospitalization;

Recent (within three months) use of medications that could affect hormones, such as hormone medicines, oral contraceptives, or any kind of corticosteroids;

Recent (within three months) use of medications that could affect hormones, such as hormone medicines, oral contraceptives, or any kind of corticosteroids;

Substance or alcohol abuse or dependence, confirmed by urine screening;

Substance or alcohol abuse or dependence, confirmed by urine screening;

Evident intellectual disability;

Evident intellectual disability;

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhu Fourth People’s Hospital (approval number: [2021]-KY-06). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent. All patient data were anonymized during analysis.

Clinical data collection

All demographic and clinical data were obtained from the electronic medical record system and standardized assessment forms of Wuhu Fourth People’s Hospital. Two trained researchers independently extracted and entered the data, which were cross-checked by a third researcher for accuracy and completeness. Demographic variables included sex, age, place of residence, only-child status (defined as having no siblings), and family structure. Clinical variables included somatic symptoms and positive psychotic symptoms. NSSI was independently assessed by two psychiatrists using medical history, examination records, and psychiatric evaluations. In the retrospective medical records, self-injurious behaviors were classified as NSSI when clinical interviews and psychiatric assessments documented that the behaviors were performed without explicit suicidal intent and were not considered suicide attempts by the treating psychiatrists. Consensus between the two psychiatrists was required for a DSM-5 diagnosis of NSSI.

Hormone assays

Serum levels of thyroid and sex hormones were measured using fasting venous blood samples collected between 6:30 and 7:00 a.m. on the morning following hospital admission. All samples were delivered to the laboratory within 30 min of collection and analyzed immediately. Sex hormone levels were measured using the Atellica Solution analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) with a chemiluminescent immunoassay. Thyroid function indices were assessed with the Roche Cobas e601 electrochemiluminescence immunoassay system (Shanghai Lanwei Clinical Testing Co., Ltd.). All assays were performed by laboratory physicians who had completed standardized training and followed established procedures. The reference ranges were 2.76–6.45 pmol/L for FT3, 11.20–23.81 pmol/L for FT4, 0.50–7.30 mIU/L for TSH, and 1.81–8.07 ng/mL for testosterone.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-samples t tests, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range and analyzed using nonparametric tests. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. To ensure the robustness of the regression models, multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values less than 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity [22]. To examine the predictive effects of hormone levels on NSSI risk while controlling for sex-related confounding, sex-stratified binary logistic regression analyses were conducted within each diagnostic group, with hormone variables entered using the enter method. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between thyroid hormones, sex hormones, and NSSI in adolescents with MDD and BD. To our knowledge, research focusing on the endocrine correlates of NSSI in these clinical populations remains limited. In the present sample, the prevalence of NSSI was 58.3% among adolescents with BD and 66.0% among those with MDD. In both diagnostic groups, female adolescents showed a significantly higher prevalence of NSSI than males. Further analyses indicated that, among female adolescents with BD, TSH levels and age at onset were significantly associated with NSSI risk, whereas among male patients, testosterone levels were significantly associated with increased NSSI risk. In contrast, among adolescents with MDD, age at onset significantly associated NSSI risk in both males and females. These findings suggest that the occurrence of NSSI may be jointly influenced by illness characteristics, diagnostic subtype, and sex-related endocrine mechanisms.

Factors associated with NSSI in adolescents with BD

Among adolescents with BD, this study found that NSSI was significantly associated with lower levels of testosterone, suggesting that reduced testosterone may increase vulnerability to NSSI. Building on this, sex-stratified logistic regression analyses further showed that, among female patients, TSH levels and age at onset were significantly associated with NSSI, whereas in male patients, testosterone levels were significantly associated with NSSI. These findings suggest that the neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying NSSI in adolescents with BD may be sex-specific.

Previous studies have reported fluctuations in testosterone levels among adolescents with BD during depressive episodes and remission, with marked sex differences in the direction of these changes [23, 24]. Marked endocrine fluctuations during puberty may further heighten emotional reactivity and behavioral impulsivity in male adolescents, increasing susceptibility to NSSI. A community-based study found that male adolescents with higher plasma testosterone levels were more prone to irritability and exhibited stronger behavioral responses to provocation and threat [25]. The present study further demonstrated that higher testosterone levels were associated with increased NSSI risk among male adolescents with BD. Moreover, previous studies have reported associations between higher testosterone levels and self-injurious behaviors and impulsive aggression in male adolescents [26]. One possible neurobiological explanation is that elevated testosterone may enhance limbic reactivity to emotional stimuli while weakening prefrontal inhibitory control over impulsive behaviors, which may impair emotion regulation and impulse control [27]. In addition, P-glycoprotein (P-gp), a major efflux transporter at the blood–brain barrier, may influence testosterone transport within the brain [28]. Genetic variants associated with impaired P-gp function have been linked to self-injurious and suicidal behaviors [29, 30]. These findings may partly explain the association between altered testosterone signaling and NSSI. Interestingly, the testosterone–NSSI association was not fully consistent between the overall comparisons and the sex-stratified regression analyses, possibly due to sex-related confounding effects. Because female patients accounted for a larger proportion of the NSSI group and baseline testosterone levels differ substantially between sexes, the overall analyses may have been influenced by differences in sex distribution. After sex stratification, the regression model more accurately captured the association between testosterone and NSSI within male patients, underscoring the importance of sex-stratified analyses when examining sex hormone–related indicators in adolescent mood disorders. However, given the relatively small sample of male adolescents with BD in the present study, these findings should be replicated in larger cohorts.

In addition to sex hormones, thyroid hormones also appear to play an important role in adolescents with BD. Thyroid hormones are involved in multiple central nervous system processes, including neuronal differentiation, synaptogenesis, neuroplasticity, and stress regulation. Even mild thyroid dysfunction may be associated with emotional instability and increased impulsive behaviors [31, 32]. In the present study, lower TSH levels were significantly associated with NSSI risk only among female adolescents with BD, whereas FT3 and FT4 did not show independent effects in the regression models. These findings suggest that TSH, as an indicator of central HPT axis feedback regulation, may better reflect emotional and behavioral dysregulation in female BD patients than absolute peripheral thyroid hormone levels. Previous studies have similarly reported reduced TSH levels in drug-naïve patients with BD, whereas findings regarding FT3 and FT4 have remained inconsistent [33, 34]. Moreover, symptoms related to hypothyroidism, including depressed mood, cognitive impairment, and psychomotor retardation, partially overlap with bipolar depressive features [35, 36], suggesting that subtle HPT axis alterations may contribute to emotional and behavioral dysregulation in BD even in the absence of clinically diagnosed thyroid disease.

The association between TSH and NSSI was observed primarily in female patients. One possible explanation is that estrogen may influence deiodinase activity, modulate TSH feedback sensitivity, and increase thyroid hormone–binding protein expression, thereby rendering females more sensitive to fluctuations in thyroid function [37, 38]. Özerdem et al. also reported that female patients with BD were more likely to exhibit TSH abnormalities [39], further suggesting sex-specific characteristics of HPT axis dysfunction. However, given the cross-sectional design and the lack of differentiation among specific mood states, future longitudinal studies with repeated hormone assessments are needed to clarify the relationship between HPT axis alterations and NSSI.

In addition, the present study found that an earlier age at onset was significantly associated with increased NSSI risk among female adolescents with BD. Previous studies have shown that early-onset BD is often linked to more severe emotional dysregulation, higher impulsivity, and a more complex clinical course [40, 41]. Onset during early adolescence may therefore reflect a period of heightened neurodevelopmental vulnerability, during which disruptions in the maturation of cognitive and affective control systems could increase susceptibility to maladaptive coping behaviors such as NSSI. Accordingly, age at onset may serve as an indicator of underlying vulnerability in affect regulation and behavioral control, rather than simply marking illness severity.

The present findings suggest potential sex-specific patterns in endocrine correlates of NSSI among adolescents with BD. Male patients may be more closely associated with alterations in the HPG axis, whereas female patients appear more affected by variations in the HPT axis. These differential endocrine profiles may reflect distinct biological pathways linking stress regulation systems to NSSI vulnerability in adolescents with BD.

Factors associated with NSSI in adolescents with MDD

In adolescents with MDD, we observed that those engaging in NSSI showed significantly lower testosterone and TSH levels compared with those without NSSI. However, in sex-stratified logistic regression analyses, none of the hormone variables showed independent associations with NSSI. By contrast, an earlier age at onset was significantly associated with increased NSSI risk in both male and female patients. These findings suggest that, in adolescents with MDD, early age at onset may represent a more consistent correlate of NSSI than current hormone levels.

Previous studies have indicated that patients with depression often exhibit HPA axis dysfunction and heightened inflammatory responses. These factors may modulate the risk of NSSI and obscure the independent predictive effects of testosterone and thyroid hormones [42]. Moreover, sex hormone levels in depressed populations are influenced by many physiological and environmental factors. These include age, disease status, medication use, and psychological stress [43]. Testosterone shows considerable variability among adolescents with MDD, which may limit its association with NSSI. In addition, hormone levels display pronounced diurnal and cyclical fluctuations. Single measurements, therefore, are insufficient to reflect long-term status. There may also be lagged effects between hormonal changes and NSSI behaviors. Cross-sectional or single-time-point designs are thus inadequate to capture their dynamic associations.

Previous studies have shown that early-onset depressive disorder is associated with more severe emotional dysregulation, higher suicide risk, and poorer psychosocial functioning [44, 45]. Early onset may therefore reflect a more vulnerable developmental profile rather than simply longer illness duration. Previous studies have suggested that adolescents with early-onset depression are more likely to experience persistent emotional dysregulation and maladaptive coping behaviors, which may increase vulnerability to NSSI [46]. In early-onset cases, prolonged exposure to depressive symptoms may further reinforce maladaptive coping patterns, including NSSI.

Comparison between BD and MDD groups with NSSI

Among adolescents with NSSI, there were no significant differences between the BD and MDD groups in hormone levels or demographic characteristics. These findings suggest that demographic variables and single-time-point hormone measures were insufficient to distinguish BD from MDD after the onset of NSSI. This pattern suggests that thyroid and sex hormones may serve better as biological marker of risk during the vulnerability stage, rather than as a marker for diagnostic differentiation after NSSI has begun. Clinically, these findings underscore the importance of considering both diagnostic subtype and sex when identifying adolescents at high risk for NSSI. In particular, assessment and ongoing monitoring of thyroid function may be especially relevant in female adolescents with BD.

The present findings suggest that NSSI in adolescents with mood disorders is likely influenced by a combination of diagnostic subtype, sex, and early illness onset. Adolescents with BD appear to show more pronounced endocrine-related characteristics, whereas in MDD, early age at onset may represent a more consistent correlate of NSSI risk. These results provide preliminary insight into the heterogeneous biological and developmental pathways underlying adolescent NSSI and underscore the importance of comprehensive clinical assessment in youths with early-onset or high-risk presentations. At the same time, caution is warranted in the interpretation of hormonal markers in clinical settings, as they should not be used in isolation for risk evaluation. Future research should focus on longitudinal designs and integrate dynamic biological and clinical indicators to improve the identification of NSSI risk and support individualized intervention strategies.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged:

First, the single-center retrospective cross-sectional design focusing on hospitalized adolescents may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, as inpatient samples are likely to represent more severe cases than community populations. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes any inference about temporal or causal relationships between hormonal alterations and NSSI. Third, hormone levels were measured using a single fasting morning blood sample. Given the known diurnal and cyclical variation of both thyroid and sex hormones, such measurements may not fully capture longer-term endocrine status. Fourth, all patients with BD included in the present study were experiencing an acute depressive episode. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with BD in manic, hypomanic, mixed, or euthymic states, as neuroendocrine characteristics may differ across mood states [47]. Fifth, potential confounders, including psychotropic medication use, body mass index, sleep patterns, and menstrual cycle phase, were not systematically controlled, which may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, clinically relevant severity indicators were not included, limiting the ability to account for illness severity in the analyses. Future studies should adopt multicenter prospective designs with repeated hormone measurements and more detailed clinical characterization to better clarify the dynamic relationship between endocrine function and NSSI in adolescents with mood disorders.

Conclusions

The present findings suggest that endocrine correlates of NSSI in adolescents with mood disorders may vary according to both diagnostic subtype and sex. In BD, NSSI appears to be more closely associated with hormonal alterations, whereas in MDD, early age at onset may be more consistently associated with NSSI. These results point to the potential heterogeneity in biological and developmental pathways underlying NSSI in adolescent mood disorders and suggest that clinical evaluation should integrate both biological and developmental indicators. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the study limitations and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.
