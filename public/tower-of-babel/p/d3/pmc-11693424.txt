Fruquintinib in refractory metastatic colorectal cancer: a multicenter real-world study
Authors: D. Xu, S. Zeng, W. Qiu, G. Wang, Z. Qin, Y. Liu, S. Zhou, Z. Zhang, W. Chang, Q. Feng, J. Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFruquintinib has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration for refractory metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). In clinical practice, fruquintinib is sometimes used in combination with other drugs, but its efficacy and safety are still unknown. In this study, we present a comprehensive analysis of the real-world treatment modalities involving fruquintinib in late-line settings for mCRC across six centers in China.Patients and methodsPatients with refractory mCRC who received fruquintinib treatment in six centers in China between 1 January 2021 and 31 June 2022 were included in this study. Patients were categorized into two cohorts: the monotherapy group (treated solely with fruquintinib) and the combined group (received fruquintinib combined with chemotherapy and/or anti-programmed cell death protein 1 antibodies). Demographic, clinical, survival, and safety data were retrospectively analyzed. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06202417.ResultsA total of 520 patients were included in this study. The median follow-up time was 9.7 months. The disease control rate was 64.8%. The median progression-free survival was 5.0 months and the median overall survival was 11.4 months. Of them, 387 (74.4%) were treated with fruquintinib alone, while 133 (25.6%) were administered fruquintinib plus chemotherapy and/or anti-programmed cell death protein 1 antibodies, respectively. Adverse events were reported by 91.3% (457/520) of patients. The rate of grade 3 or 4 toxicity was 42.4% (237/520). No treatment-related death occurred.ConclusionFruquintinib, either as a standalone treatment or in combination with other medications, demonstrates substantial efficacy and favorable tolerability in refractory mCRC patients.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) stands as one of the most prevalent digestive tract tumors worldwide, approximately one-fifth of them diagnosed at stage IV.1,2 Patients diagnosed at this advanced stage face a challenging prognosis, making it a persistent challenge and focus in treatment and research. Significant progress has been made in recent years in the pharmacological treatment of metastatic CRC (mCRC). The management for patients who have exhausted standard treatment options is challenging due to their poor condition and the significant variability in treatment regimens. This is not only a challenge in the treatment of CRC, but also the key to improving prognosis. For patients progressing to the third line and beyond, however, the efficacy of drug therapies often encounters limitations. Currently available options in this setting encompass fruquintinib,3,4 regorafenib,5,6 TAS-102,7 among others. Despite these advancements, the pursuit of effective treatment strategies for advanced CRC remains an ongoing challenge.

Fruquintinib, as an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), effectively suppresses vascular endothelial growth factor/vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGF/VEGFR) signaling and inhibits cell proliferation.8 Two pivotal randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the FRESCO and the FRESCO2 studies, underscore the potential of fruquintinib as a valuable therapeutic option in later lines of treatment.3,4 Real-world studies further substantiate the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib in third line and subsequent treatments.9 TKIs are frequently used in combination with other drugs in late-line treatment. Fruquintinib, as a novel TKI, lacks large-sample studies supporting its use in combination with other drugs. Preclinical models have indicated potential advantages through the combination of fruquintinib with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibition.10,11 Several retrospective studies have demonstrated the potential effectiveness of combining fruquintinib with PD-1 inhibitors.12, 13, 14, 15 Phase Ib/II studies have delved into the synergy between fruquintinib and sintilimab, revealing promising outcomes.16 In addition, some studies have started to explore the potential of fruquintinib in combination with chemotherapy.17,18 Previous high-quality studies, however, have all been on the use of fruquintinib as a single agent. Reports on the combination of fruquintinib with other drugs have only been from small-sample, single-arm, retrospective cohort studies, and evidence regarding the amalgamation of fruquintinib with chemotherapy is lacking.

Here, we present comprehensive data on fruquintinib gathered from six CRC centers in China. The aim of this study is to demonstrate the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib, both as monotherapy and in combination with other drugs, in real-world practice for refractory mCRC patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study (clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06202417), was carried out across six comprehensive cancer centers in China. Demographic, clinical, and survival data for all patients were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records.

Patients diagnosed with refractory mCRC and administered fruquintinib from 1 January 2021 to 31 June 31 2022 were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were: (i) histology-confirmed mCRC; (ii) disease progression on standard therapy with at least two lines of chemotherapy, including fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan with or without biologics such as bevacizumab and cetuximab; (iii) fruquintinib administered as salvage treatment; (iv) available clinical data. Exclusion criteria were: (i) lack of follow-up data; (ii) fruquintinib administration as second-line treatment; (iii) fruquintinib administration stopped after fewer than two cycles.

Patients were grouped into two study cohorts according to the treatment received: (i) fruquintinib alone (monotherapy group); (ii) fruquintinib in combination with chemotherapy and/or anti-PD-1 antibodies (combined group).

Treatment assessment adhered to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumor (RECIST) criteria.19 The study received approval from the ethics committee of each participating institute, conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Multidisciplinary team and follow-up

Weekly multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions were conducted, featuring physicians from various specialties including colorectal surgery, liver surgery, oncology, radiation oncology, intervention treatment, medical imaging, and thoracic surgery departments. Following the deliberation of each patient with colorectal liver metastasis, a consensus was reached on the treatment plan or the necessity for additional diagnostic work-up.20

The general practice for surveillance included physical examination, serum carcinoembryonic antigen testing, computed tomography of the chest, abdomen with intravenous contrast, and MRI of abdomen, pelvis with intravenous contrast. These were carried out every 2-4 cycles of fruquintinib administration.

Variables

The variables examined included sex, age, ECOG-PS (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status), primary tumor site, metastatic tumor site, resection, treatment line, previous use of biologics, and initial and final dosages of fruquintinib. RAS/BRAF mutations were detected with KRAS/NRAS/BRAF Mutations Detection Kit, and mismatch repair (MMR) status was determined by immunohistochemistry.

Outcomes

Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR) and adverse events (AEs) were assessed in each cohort. PFS was calculated from the date of fruquintinib administration to the first observation of disease progression or death. OS was defined as the time from fruquintinib administration to death. DCR was determined as the proportion of patients who achieved complete response, partial response or stable disease assessed at least 6 weeks after drug administration. AEs included any grade symptomatic or hematological events, and were evaluated using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE V5.0).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using either the student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the distribution. Categorical variables underwent comparison through the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan–Meier curve analysis was employed to assess PFS and OS, treating death and disease progression as events in the analysis. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox regression models and evaluated through the Wald test. Factors associated with PFS and OS were assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.1.1 (R Project).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 558 consecutive patients diagnosed with refractory mCRC and treated with fruquintinib from 1 January 2021 to 31 June 2022, were initially collected. Exclusions were made for those with insufficient follow-up data (n = 21), those receiving fruquintinib as a second-line treatment (n = 9), and those discontinuing fruquintinib after fewer than two cycles (n = 5). Consequently, a final cohort of 520 patients meeting the eligibility criteria underwent further analysis (Figure 1).Figure 1Flowchart of the patient queue.

Of the 520 patients, 387 (74.4%) received fruquintinib alone (monotherapy group), while 133 (25.6%) were administered fruquintinib in combination with chemotherapy or/and anti-PD-1 antibodies (combined group), respectively (Figure 1). Compared with patients receiving monotherapy, those in the combined group were more likely to have multiple metastases, and had undergone a greater number of lines of treatment, and those patients appeared to be younger with better ECOG-PS. The baseline data of each cohort are shown in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702.

Efficacy of different treatment regimens

In the 520 patients, no patients had a complete response, 11 patients achieved partial response, and 326 patients maintained stable disease. Disease control was achieved in 337 (64.8%) of the 520 patients. Fruquintinib exhibited a median PFS (mPFS) of 5.0 months (95% CI 4.33-5.82 months) and a median OS (mOS) of 11.4 months (95% CI 10.8-22.9 months). A total of 387 patients who received fruquintinib alone had an mPFS of 4.23 months (95% CI 3.97-5.33 months) and an mOS of 11.0 months (95% CI 9.77-12.7 months). Within the combined group, 71 (53.4%) patients received fruquintinib plus anti-PD-1 antibodies; these cases demonstrated an mOS of 15.7 months (95% CI 11.03-26.5 months) and an mPFS of 6.13 months (95% CI 4.80-7.93 months). As for 56 (42.1%) patients who had fruquintinib combined with chemotherapy, an mOS of 11.6 months (95% CI 9.47-23.4 months) and an mPFS of 5.8 months (95% CI 4.67-9.23 months) were reached. It is noteworthy that among patients in the combined group, six (1.2%) patients received fruquintinib in combination with both chemotherapy and immunotherapy; although the mOS has not yet been reached, these cases demonstrated an mPFS of 8.8 months [95% CI 8.80 months-not available (NA)] (Figure 2C and D, Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702). The medication regimens for all patients in the combination group are detailed in Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702.

Univariable and multivariable COX regression analyses showed that RAS mutation, BRAF mutation, and combination therapy were independent prognostic factors of PFS (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702), and prior anti-VEGF and combination therapy were independent prognostic factors of OS (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702).

The incidence of any AEs in the monotherapy group and the combined group were 86.2% and 87.9%, respectively. The rate of grade ≥3 AEs in the two groups were 42.1% and 56%, respectively. The number of patients in the two groups who had dose reduction due to AEs was 67 (17.3%) and 32 (24.0%), respectively.

Within the combined group, the incidences of any AEs and grade ≥3 AEs in patients receiving fruquintinib combined with immunotherapy were 93.0% and 54.9%, respectively. For patients receiving fruquintinib combined with chemotherapy, the incidence of any AEs and grade 3 or higher AEs were 92.9% and 58.9%, respectively. In six patients who received a treatment regimen of fruquintinib combined with chemotherapy and anti-PD-1 antibodies, the incidence of any AEs was 100%, and the rate of grade ≥3 AEs was 66.7% (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702).

Treatment line, dosage, and sequential treatment sequence

The majority of patients included in the study [397 (76.3%)] received fruquintinib treatment as third-line therapy; an mOS of 11.6 months (95% CI 10.8-13.3 months) was observed, alongside an mPFS of 5.2 months (95% CI 4.33-6.07 months). A total of 123 (23.7%) patients receiving fruquintinib as a later-line regimen experienced an mOS of 11.0 months (95% CI 8.23-13.5 months) and an mPFS of 4.47 months (95% CI 3.53-5.87 months) (Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure S2A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702).Figure 3Kaplan–Meier survival curves of different treatment patterns. A total of 267 (51.3%) patients had a starting dose of <5 mg, and 253 (48.7%) patients began treatment at 5 mg. Ultimately, 325 patients (62.5%) had a final dose of 5 mg or <5 mg, while 195 patients (37.5%) reached final doses of 5 mg. For patients with a starting dose of <5 mg, mOS and mPFS were found to be 11.5 months (95% CI 10.8-13.3 months) and 5.17 months (95% CI 4.23-6.57 months), respectively. As for patients with a starting dose of 5 mg, the mOS was recorded at 11.3 months (95% CI 10.2-13.3 months), while mPFS was noted at 4.6 months (95% CI 4.10-5.73 months) (Figure 3B, Supplementary Figure S2B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702). In terms of final doses, the analysis revealed an mOS of 11.3 months (95% CI 10.67-13.5 months) and an mPFS of 5.2 months (95% CI 4.23-6.07 months) for patients with a final dose of <5 mg. In patients with a final dose of 5 mg, mOS was recorded at 11.4 months (95% CI 9.83-14.4 months), while mPFS was noted at 4.9 months (95% CI 4.17-5.87 months) (Figure 3C, Supplementary Figure S2C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702).

For patients receiving fruquintinib monotherapy, we retrospectively collected data on their frontline and subsequent treatment regimens. Among them, 111 patients received regorafenib either in the front-line or subsequent-line treatment, with 52 patients switching to regorafenib after progression on fruquintinib (R-F), and 59 patients receiving fruquintinib after progression on regorafenib (F-R). For patients who received F-R and R-F, median values for OS were 15.7 months (95% CI 11.0 months-NA) and 11.1 months (95% CI 8.8 months-NA), respectively (Figure 3D).

Discussion

This multicenter study offers a real-world depiction of the application of fruquintinib in the treatment of refractory mCRC. For the first time, this study reported on the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib alone and its combination with chemotherapy and/or anti-PD-1 antibodies. Moreover, the data are derived from real-world clinical practice, utilizing an MDT approach for a comprehensive and detailed analysis.

Two pivotal RCTs, the FRESCO and FRESCO-2 studies, have substantiated the efficacy of fruquintinib in the treatment of mCRC, further reinforcing its role in salvage therapy for mCRC.3,4 The China Food and Drug Administration and the United States Food and Drug Administration approved fruquintinib in refractory mCRC in 2018 and 2023, respectively. In our study, patients receiving fruquintinib monotherapy showed an mPFS of 4.23 months and mOS of 11.0 months. Comparing these results to the RCT studies (FRESCO: mPFS 3.71 months, mOS 9.3 months; FRESCO-2: mPFS 3.7 months, mOS 7.4 months), our patients exhibited better prognoses. This discrepancy may be attributed to our patients having undergone fewer lines of treatment with a better ECOG-PS. In our study, patients in the combined group had undergone a greater number lines of treatment with multiple metastases. This suggests these patients may represent a group that is more challenging to treat with a poorer prognosis. For these more challenging patients, receiving combination therapy can still yield survival benefits. Preclinical studies suggest that fruquintinib has the potential to improve the immune microenvironment when used in conjunction with PD-1 inhibitors,10,11 and this has been corroborated by some retrospective studies.12,15 The PFS in our combined immunotherapy group aligns with findings from recently published phase Ib/II studies.16 Previous studies have validated the viability of integrating anti-angiogenic therapy with chemotherapy in mCRC, encompassing large molecular monoclonal antibodies like bevacizumab21 and other small-molecule TKIs such as regorafenib.22 Given that fruquintinib primarily exerts its antitumor effects through anti-angiogenesis, exploring combination chemotherapy might be another worthwhile direction owing to its distinct mechanism of action.8 Reports on the combination of fruquintinib with chemotherapy, however, are still lacking. We firstly reported the efficacy of chemotherapy combined with fruquintinib in a real-world setting. The chemotherapy regimens used in combination with fruquintinib varied in our study (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103702). Further studies are needed to determine the specific combinations offering the most benefit.

Adverse reactions are a prominent concern in clinical practice regarding drug therapy. The incidence of AEs in our study was lower than in RCT studies, and this difference may be attributed to: (i) patients had undergone fewer lines of treatment in our study, and (ii) less than half of the patients received the 5 mg in our study. Interestingly, in addition to AEs unique to fruquintinib, such as hand-foot syndrome, hypertension, and proteinuria, it is worth noting that vomiting and leukopenia were more commonly detected in the combination chemotherapy group, and hypothyroidism was more frequently observed in patients receiving fruquintinib plus anti-PD-1 antibodies.

While our study provides insights into everyday clinical practices, it is crucial to acknowledge certain limitations. Firstly, due to the retrospective and non-randomized nature, we may be unable to eliminate omitted variables and compare the efficacy of different fruquintinib treatment regimens. Secondly, data collection relies on actual medical records, introducing the risk of incomplete or inaccurate information compared with prospective studies. Thirdly, the data were obtained solely in six large colorectal centers in China, restricting the ability to assess the use of fruquintinib in smaller medical centers and among non-Chinese populations. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the study results.

In conclusion, our study presents the use of fruquintinib in patients with refractory mCRC at six major colorectal centers in China. The data suggest that fruquintinib, whether used as monotherapy or in combination with other drugs, exhibits significant efficacy and good tolerability. Future rigorously controlled studies are needed to assess the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib in combination with other drugs.
