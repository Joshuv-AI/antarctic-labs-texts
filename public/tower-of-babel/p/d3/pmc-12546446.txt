Anti-CD38 and Regression of Chronic Active Antibody-Mediated Rejection After Kidney Transplantation - Myth or Reality?
Authors: Marie Matignon, Philippe Grimbert, Anissa Moktefi, Caroline Pilon
Published: 2025

Chronic active antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) remains a serious concern following kidney transplantation, because it is one of the leading causes of allograft loss.1 The histological definition was simplified in 2022 by excluding new-onset arterial intimal fibrosis and other causes.1,2 Currently, there are no approved therapies that are effective.1

A monoclonal anti-CD38 antibody that destroys plasma cells, immune cells specialized in producing anti–human leukocyte antigen antibodies, represents a real hope for controlling alloimmune responses after solid organ transplantation. First, its effectiveness has been demonstrated in case reports of kidney transplant recipients with acute and chronic ABMR, and in desensitization before transplantation.3 Natural killer (NK) cells are implicated in the pathophysiological sequence of acute and chronic ABMR and are involved in several effector mechanisms of the alloimmune response that lead to allograft injury. They also express CD38 highly.4

Last year, 2 cohort studies reported on the use of anti-CD38 in desensitization before kidney transplantation (i.v. isatuximab and daratumumab).5,6 Despite an early significant decrease in anti–human leukocyte antigen antibodies, the effect was transient and anti–human leukocyte antigen antibodies returned to pretreatment levels 6 months after treatment. In both studies, the number of CD38-positive plasma cells decreased and returned to baseline after 12 months whereas the number of NK cells remained lower than baseline after 12 months. A responsive profile involving a prolonged decrease in anti–human leukocyte antigen antibodies could be identified and predicted based on the pretreatment level of CD38-positive cells.7

A randomized phase 2 case-control study analyzed the effects of another anti-CD38 (felzartamab) over 24 weeks in 11 late donor specific antibodies-positive ABMR cases after kidney transplantation (> 6 months).3 Up to 70% of ABMR cases were chronic. Sustained regression of microcirculation acute inflammation as well as NK cells and transcripts has been reported with a significantly higher decrease in the estimated glomerular filtration rate in the control group at week 52.3,8 In each study, the timing and dosage of the anti-CD38 injections differed.

In this issue of KI Reports, Lye et al. present a retrospective, open-label study of the first and the largest cohort of 16 kidney transplant recipients with late chronic active ABMR, who were treated with subcutaneous anti-CD38 (daratumumab) for 10 months.9 They also reported successful follow-up with cell-free DNA, with a perfect correlation between rejection and remission. Up to 80% of patients showed regression of microvascular inflammation and 56% experienced a complete remission of microvascular inflammation with a score < 1. Complete resolution was confirmed by molecular histology in 8 out of 9 kidney transplant recipients. None of them showed signs of chronic histological changes. There were no serious adverse events associated with subcutaneous daratumumab during the follow-up period. Only mild adverse events were reported, occurring only after the first injection and not resulting in any major adverse event, especially severe infections.

In addition to the high number of patients, this study presented multiple benefits. First, it added data on the efficacy of the anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody in treating microvascular inflammation in ABMR following kidney transplantation. The molecular probe for ABMR and the molecular resolution of almost all microvascular inflammation increase the level of evidence. In addition, it increases the value of cell-free DNA as a noninvasive biomarker for monitoring acute ABMR microvascular lesions as an alternative to sequential biopsy particularly after treatment cessation. Second, no kidney transplant recipient developed chronic lesions during the 10-month follow-up period, suggesting that anti-CD38 could delay the development of chronic lesions probably by neutralizing NK cells as described in the felzartamab study.S1 The total regression of chronic glomerulopathy merits further exploration and confirmation; because if confirmed, anti-CD38 would be the first treatment described as improving chronic glomerulopathy. Finally, this is the first study to use a subcutaneous form of the anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody which has significantly fewer adverse events than in i.v. treatment without loss of efficacy. This permits home-based treatment of ABMR after kidney transplantation, improving quality of life after kidney transplantation and reducing treatment costs.

The timing and dose of the injection differed from those previously described with a higher dose compared with the i.v. dose (1800 mg per injection or 25 mg/kg compared with the usual 16 mg/kg usually) and fewer injections.

However, based on the data presented, we cannot conclude that all 16 recipients presented with chronic ABMR alone. We suspect that acute ABMR without chronic changes as defined in the Banff 2019 classification was included. It would be interesting to reclassify the biopsies using the updated Banff 2024 classification in order to revise the conclusion on the efficacy of anti-CD38 in chronic ABMR in 2025. Better understanding of how plasma and NK cells evolve after treatment could improve our comprehension of the anti-CD38 effect in chronic ABMR, especially if regression of chronic glomerulopathy is confirmed. One final concern is the necessity for a longer follow-up to draw further conclusions about sustained responses.

In conclusion (Figure 1), this study provided favorable data on the use of anti-CD38 in chronic ABMR after kidney transplantation occurring > 6 months after transplantation, as well as on the efficacy of cell-free DNA in monitoring microvascular inflammation as a noninvasive biomarker and its potential to inform the reinjection of anti-CD38 because of its transient effect. It seems that the early use of anti-CD38 in ABMR after kidney transplantation could significantly improve the prognosis of ABMR, decreasing the risk of chronic ABMR lesions and limiting acute inflammation.1 Subcutaneous treatment could considerably simplify ABMR treatment. Further prospective studies are required to analyze the regression of chronic glomerulopathy and the effects of different injection timing and duration.
