Microfluidics-based cell recognition through optimizing suspended cell staining techniques and artificial intelligence
Authors: Areen K. Al-Bashir, Lamis R. Bany Issa, Haneen Ababneh, Farah Alshami, Esra’a Al-shawwa, Ala’a Al-Rashdan, Ruba E. Khnouf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeBlood staining is necessary to differentiate between its various cells. It is typically performed on a microscopic slide that contains a blood film. However, in some applications, there is a need to stain blood in suspension.MethodsThree techniques were optimized (Romanowsky stains, Hematoxylin stain, and our proposed modified Romanowsky stain) for cell staining before injection into a microfluidic device. Images of stained cells in the microfluidic device were captured under an optical microscope. The effectiveness of staining techniques in cell differentiation was evaluated using color-based k-means clustering. Then, four pre-trained models (Alexnet, VGG16, Resnet, and Densnet) were applied to extract features and classify RBCs, WBCs, and CTCs.ResultsOnly images showing clear cell borders were retained. The models achieved high testing accuracy, reaching 100%, 98%, 98%, and 95.34%, respectively.ConclusionsThis high accuracy was achieved without the use of fluorescence labeling or expensive materials, thereby preserving the cells alive. Based on these results, the proposed staining technique was the most effective in differentiating between WBCs, RBCs, and CTCs, where all WBCs and RBCs were classified correctly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00678-1.

Introduction

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) are cells of malignant tumors that slough off and are carried away by either the circulation or the lymphatic system [1, 2]. These cells can metastasize to new tumors in distant organs [3]. Assessing the existence and nature of CTCs is considered beneficial in cancer detection, management, and treatment [4–6]. Detecting CTCs is a substantial engineering challenge, because it is an exceedingly rare count in comparison with other blood cells [7], as it does not have any unique surface markers [8].

Manual CTC detection is tedious, time-consuming, and subjective. Therefore, other techniques such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) [9], automated single-cell sorting using dual-beam optical trapping [10], magnetic-activated cell separation (MACS) [11], and disposable microfabricated fluorescence-activated cell sorting (µFACS) [12] have been used for efficient and fast cell purification.

Among these, microfluidics is a promising technique for automated CTC detection. It gives a lot of advantages, such as satisfactory performance, small reagent volumes, good sensitivity, enhanced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), small footprints, automated loading, separation, and analysis, besides saving time and cost compared to other techniques [13]. The most widely used microfluidic device techniques for CTCs isolation are antibody-based microfluidic devices [14–19]. In these techniques, the cells are normally labeled with fluorescent dye using expensive antibodies, then optical signals are identified and analyzed to classify biological samples. Fluorescent dye may damage cells and reduce the recovery rate; furthermore, most cancer cells do not have antibodies that can identify them. These factors have generated the need for nonspecific label-free CTC sorting techniques, such as size-based separation, dielectrophoresis, and pinched flow-based isolation [20–22].

Size-based separation techniques of blood samples have the benefit of size variations between CTCs (4 to 30 μm)[16] and hematological cells (6–10 μm simplicity) [23]. However, in some carcinomas, some CTCs are smaller than the leukocytes, reducing the device’s isolation performance [24, 25]. Dielectrophoresis (DEP) takes advantage of variations in dielectric properties [26]. However, such variations are small, resulting in less purity and efficiency [27]. Cross-over frequency is also an essential factor in this process, where decreasing this frequency limits higher separation performance [28–30].

Pinched flow-based cell separation subject cells to less stress, enhancing viability. But it takes greater processing time and can often contribute to cell clogging in the pinching area [31].

The aforementioned drawbacks increased the need for developing a high-performance system with decreased size and cost while keeping sufficient detection sensitivity, isolation throughput, and efficient sorting without the need for labeling or fixing [32].

Several studies proposed image-based on-chip cell sorters integrated with optical imaging microscope for CTC recognition, which has many advantages, specifically in detecting cells that have complex morphologies [33].

Yasuda et al. [34] automatically classified microscopic images of yeast cells in a microfluidic channel using a computer-based image processing technique. The algorithm extracted cell geometrical features, then fed them into a machine learning classifier. System performance was investigated under different focusing and illumination conditions. The results indicate that the morphological characteristics of yeast cells can be automatically classified with accuracy affected by image noise and exposure levels. Another study by Yang et al. [35], extended the previous work to automatically sort various yeast cells based on their fluorescent tag. The device was employed for single-cell imaging in-flow, while Artificial neural networks were used for the unsupervised characterization of the yeast cells. The system achieved a classification accuracy of single-cell images of 88% [36]. Another study used a label-free microfluidic droplet-sorting system that evolved by utilizing image recognition of cells in droplets. The results demonstrated the ability of the system to isolate single target cells from a mixture of cells without any staining, with high accuracy [37]. The variation of the morphometric parameters’ distribution of cells and CTC clusters, in blood over time, has been applied to the unlabeled isolation of CTCs. Both bright-field and fluorescent images were obtained and analyzed for real-time cell sorting. The results show that detecting a rise in irregular cells, such as cell clusters in the blood, has the advantage of mapping cell size distribution [38]. For high-throughput microscopy-based label-free in a microfluidic chip, a Real-time Moving Object Detector based on deep learning was introduced in [39]. It uses a high-speed camera and a microscope to capture all single-cell images of cells in flow and identify them in real-time. The experiments demonstrate that it has quick and dependable accuracy.

Many studies have begun implementing AI methods for CTC detection in microscopic optical images to enhance cell detection accuracy, achieve high throughput, and save resources. Study [40] developed a CNN model to detect ALL cells and achieved an accuracy of 96.6%. In [41], The authors proposed two models using transfer learning of pretrained AlexNet with and without fine-tuning to detect ALL cells and achieved 99.7% and 100% accuracy, respectively, on 2820 images. In [42], the authors used CNN to classify different leukemia types and achieved an accuracy of 81.74%. Another study [43] uses 4 different CNN-based algorithms to detect and classify four types of leukemia. All models used gave acceptable accuracies, reaching 99.8%, 99.7%, and 94% for training, validation, and testing sets, respectively. In this study, we propose and optimize a new technique to stain cells in suspension, then propose an automated technique to isolate and sort CTCs based on their morphological features extracted from optical microscopic images using artificial intelligence techniques.

Microfluidic device fabrication

The microfluidic device was fabricated using traditional soft lithography techniques. Design and fabrication details can be found in the supplementary information.

Blood sample and CTCs mixture preparation

Blood samples were collected from healthy donors via the Autolet Mini device in a 2 ml Vacuette tube (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria), which contains ethylenediamine-tetraacetic acid (K3E K3EDTA) to prevent cell coagulation. The collected cells were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (OXOID LIMITED, BASINGSTOKE, HAMPSHIRE, ENGLAND) by adding 1 ml of PBS to 6 µl of blood. Cell clumps were broken up with PBS and careful pipetting. This diluted blood sample was added to the CTC solution. The CTC solution is composed of cancer cells of breast cancer (MDA-MB-231) suspended in 1mL of PBS. It has the same osmolarity and ion concentration as human tissues; thus, blood cells will not shrivel or explode.

Staining cells in suspension and optimization

Staining methods used in this study were derived from the Romanowsky staining method. The suspended cells were stained by trying different stains to ensure cell differentiation. The selected staining technique was optimized after several experiments with different stain concentrations and incubation times. Below are three different staining methods used in this work.

Romanowsky stain

Almost all Romanowsky stains include Methylene blue as a basic component and Eosin Y as an acidic component. Methylene blue and Eosin dye are mixed in an alcohol solution. In this stain, the diluted cell sample was stained using Wright stain (Sigma Aldrich), a type of Romanowsky stain. Initially, the Wright stain did not work well; thus, diluted Wright with distilled water (1:1) was used to enhance the results, see Table 1.

Hematoxylin stain (colorimetric staining)

Our first trial used the exact colorimetric staining method described in [44]. Then, to enhance the staining in suspension results, some modifications to the method were added in trials 2 through 4. Accordingly, the modified steps can be summarized as follows: (1) Adding 50 µl of whole blood to an Eppendorf tube. (2) Adding 1500 µl of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min. (3) Centrifuge at 95 *100 rpm for 3 min. (4) Wash the mixture with 1000 µl of PBS and centrifuge. (5) Incubate with 1000 µl of hematoxylin Mayer’s solution for 15 min while shaking. (6) Centrifuge at 95 *100 rpm for 3 min. (7) Wash with 1000 µl of PBS and centrifuge (this step was repeated 3 times) (8) Incubate with 1000 µl of eosin for 15 min while shaking. (9) Centrifuge at 95 *100 rpm for 3 min and wash with 1000 µl of PBS. (10) Finally, resuspend with 500 µl of PBS. In trial 2, only steps 1 through 6 were performed. While on the third trial, steps 1 through 10 were performed. The effect of increasing the incubation time was tested in the fourth trial. These trials are summarized in Table 2 below.

The proposed Eosin & Methylene blue staining method

Here, the suspended cells were incubated with Eosin for 10 min, then incubated with Methylene blue for another 10 min. Eosin Y solution (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) was used to stain the RBCs and the cytoplasm of the WBCs, and Methylene blue solution (AZ Chem for chemicals, Pretoria, South Africa) was used to stain the nucleus of the WBCs at a dilution ratio of 1:1. This is an additional step to enable the cell to move freely in the microchannel. The blood sample was prepared with 6 µL blood + 250 mL PBS + 1 ml of CTC solution (CTCs spiked in specialized media or PBS. Different concentrations of stains were used to stain the cells; these quantities were adjusted based on the results. Eosin was adjusted based on the clarity of the RBCs, and Methylene blue was adjusted based on the clarity of the WBCs. Sample focusing

Once the device and the sample were ready for use, the blood sample containing CTCs was pumped into the microfluidic device. The single stained cell’s appearance in the captured image was enhanced by sample focusing through hydrodynamic focusing, as shown in Fig. 1. An upright microscope (Nikon Eclipse E200, Japan) coupled with a digitized camera with a color CMOS sensor, was used to capture images of the cross-section profile of the sample flow within the microchannel. (see supplementary for more information).

Image capturing

Starting with a 10x objective lens to see how the cells flow, to determine the region of interest (ROI), and to capture the stream through the channel. Then the 40x objective lens was used for cell image capturing. The frames were then exported using MATLAB 2021a, and the ROI was cropped for dataset creation. The images were captured by a camera (VisiCam 5 Plus, China) mounted on the upright microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell staining methods assessments

The captured images of the three staining techniques were assessed to evaluate their ability to differentiate between cells. Images were converted to the LAB color space, and then cells in each image were clustered using color-based k-means clustering. When the differentiation was not accomplished, the staining method was excluded or modified to enhance differentiation between the cells. This differentiation was accomplished between the background, WBCs, and RBCs, but it was not between WBCs and CTCs due to the similarity between them in most cases.

Cell recognition

A dataset of all differentiable images was created, see Table 4. The proposed algorithm was published in [43] is adopted to detect and differentiate between RBCs, WBCs, and CTCs.

Image preparation

The collected dataset included different cell images of the 3 classes (RBC of 90 cells, WBC of 85 cells, and CTC of 84 cells). These images were first split into two groups, 80% of the images for training and validation, and 20% of the images were preserved for testing. The training dataset was augmented and preprocessed before entering the model, as shown in Fig. 2. The image augmentation process was performed on the training dataset only with wide ranges (rotation, translation, scaling, reflection, and shear), as shown in Fig. 3. Image preprocessing includes contrast enhancement, image resizing to a fixed size that matches the model requirements, and image normalization.

Staining results

Assessment of the staining methods was based on the differentiability of RBCs and WBCs in the captured images. If cells in the image were not differentiable under the microscope, then the image would be neglected; otherwise, the image is used for AI evaluation techniques. The results from each staining method are shown below.

Romanowsky stains

The differentiation between RBCs and WBCs was achieved using the Romanowsky stain in suspension, but the stain was unstable. Looking at the images in Fig. 5, when the stain (700 µl) was used as is, the WBCs and RBCs overstained, but when utilizing only 400 µl Wright stain and diluted it with water, all cells were transparent. Increasing the stain amount to 700 µl while keeping the dilution with water, the WBCs stained well, but the RBCs did not. K-mean clustering method was only applied on the third trial, it showed complete differentiability of the WBCs, and only the borders of the RBCs because of their similarity with the background. This confirms that the RBCs are approximately transparent, see Fig. 6.

Increasing incubation time in hematoxylin increases the number of stained cells but lowers the image contrast, as shown in trial 4, see Fig. 7. CTCs identification using AI results

Only images from trial 1 of the proposed method were used. Each image was manually labeled with the true label. CTCs were identified separately to prevent confusion between them and WBCs. The 5-fold cross-validation method was used in splitting the data to ensure that each part of the data was used for training and validation. Some samples were preserved unseen for testing purposes. Four pretrained models (Alexnet, VGG 16, Resnet, and Densnet) were used as described in [43]. Accuracy and loss curves, confusion matrix, Precision, Recall, and f1-score for all models were calculated.

Figure 12 shows that the accuracy curve of each training and validation set increases with training progress for the four pretrained models to reach 100% and 99.63% for training and validation, respectively in Alexnet, 100% and 99.23% for training and validation, respectively in VGG16, 100% and 96.24% for training and validation, respectively in Resnet, and 100% and 94.98% for training and validation, respectively in Densnet. The loss of both groups is approximately zero for Alxnet and VGG16, and a little higher for Resnet and Densnet. The training and validation curves fit well, as confirmed by the testing group’s detailed results in the confusion matrix. Variation on cases correctly classified depending on the classifier, the testing accuracy of the Alexnet 100%, VGG16 is 98%, ResNet 98%, and Densnet 96% as shown in Fig. 13. Discussion

Based on the results presented, the 3 methods used have been successfully applied. However, the results from each were varied. Romanowsky stains (i.e., Wright) are particularly unstable when diluted with an aqueous buffer and cause precipitation [45, 46]. Thus, it did not serve our goal to detect suspended cells in the buffer or solution. The second staining method used was hematoxylin, which, to our knowledge, is the only method that was found in the literature for staining in suspension. Its results were not stable; besides, there was some overstaining and cell damage, especially for the cancer cells under repeated centrifugation. Nevertheless, there were no clear cells (neither RBCs nor WBCs) along with cell lysis resulting from the repeated centrifugation. Some modification was performed on the staining process, but the results showed the same problems. Therefore, modified staining techniques based on the Romanowsky stain components technique were proposed. Different methylene blue and eosin concentrations and different incubation times were used. The results showed that the technique enabled differentiation between cells and backgrounds. Much clearer results when using eosin of 200 µl, methylene blue of 1000 µl, and more than 15 min of incubation.

The sample flow in the microfluidic device was controlled to ensure single-cell passage through it. This was achieved through the process of designing and fabricating the device and applying hydrodynamic focusing. Thus, each cell is passed separately inside the microfluidic device, which allows single-cell imaging.

The collected dataset of suspended blood cells and CTCs is new and unique. It is composed of blood cells and CTCs in suspension in real shapes without labeling or fixation, which ensures the viability of the cells and allows further analysis. The available datasets in the literature were either in fixed microscopic images [47] or fluorescent-labeled images [38] and [48]. The two challenges in suspended cell imaging are the undesirable air bubbles that appear as the cell passes through the channels and maintaining a low flow rate of cell passage to ensure a single pass. To minimize the effect of these two challenges, a bubble-free sample was introduced, tube cleaning was ensured, and system stability was maintained while performing the experiments.

The dilution factors used in microfluidics, however, varied considerably from the aforementioned number depending on the type of cells being counted, for example, platelets required much higher dilution factors than WBCs [49]. The dilution factor also depended on the device’s design and on the method used for counting the cells, for example, impedance measurements required higher dilutions than image-based measurements, since it is easier to differentiate clusters visually than from an impedance signal [50].

The 0.6% (1:166) dilution used is slightly more concentrated than the typically used 1:200 dilution value usually used in microfluidic systems [51], it has provided sufficient results as can be seen under the microscope. This dilution, paired with the blood cells being under continuous shear due to the laminar flow taking place in the microfluidic devices, prevents the overlap of cells and the undercounting that dilution is meant to avoid. On the other hand, increasing the dilution factor –which was not necessary since platelets were not of interest in this study- would result in a substantial increase in sample volume, increasing sample processing time, a factor that we are trying to reduce. Using the clustering method may enable cell recognition but not classification. Thus, AI techniques were used to classify the cells with high accuracy. 4 pre-trained models (Alexnet, VGG16, Resnet, and Densnet) were applied to automatically extract cell features and classify RBC, WBC, and CTC.

The used models, Alexnet, VGG16, Resnet, and Densnet, achieved high testing accuracy, reaching 100%, 98%, 98%, and 95.34%, respectively. This high accuracy was achieved without fluorescence labeling or expensive materials to preserve the cells alive, like in [38] and [48]. To our knowledge, two studies did not use fluorescence labeling; one was designed for yeast cells [36], and the other one [39]ignored the WBCs detection, which is a critical issue, and indirectly depended on the particle size. The work published in [36] presented a similar idea of the proposed system, but their work was applied to yeast cells, which have different features from blood cells and CTCs. They achieved an accuracy of 88% on a few-shot classification of single-cell images. The work presented in [39] was applied to fluorescent-free labeling of blood and cancer cell images. The work relied on the size differences for cell differentiation, and their system was initially applied to beads with different diameters before being applied to blood with cancer cells. The system differentiated RBCs and cancer cells, but not WBCs. This system does not use hydrodynamic focusing; it tracks all available cells in the frame at the same time, creating a chance of cells overlapping.

The AlexNet model achieved 100% accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. This can be due to the combined impact of data preparation, class separability, and architectural compatibility. The three classes’ significant visual distinctiveness is a major contributing factor. Class-specific patterns emerged after using the proposed staining procedure, improved the contrast, and highlighted discriminative regions. Thus, the classification problem became naturally more separable, which successfully converted delicate raw information into distinct visual cues.

Nevertheless, AlexNet is particularly sensitive to texture contrasts, edge responses, and color distributions [52]. AlexNet’s early convolutional filters, which are known to specialize in capturing low-level color and edge-based data, automatically matched the colored images [53, 54]. Consequently, the network demonstrated significant class confidence in extracting highly informative representations. Consequently, deeper architectures (i.e.like VGG, ResNet, and DenseNet) yielded slightly more conservative performance estimates, while AlexNet was able to fully utilize the improved visual separability brought about by the coloring procedure.

Overall, the proposed model’s performance shows that data preparation, dataset properties, and its architecture all work well together. While highlighting the significance of dataset size in subsequent extensions of this work, the results show that the suggested coloring strategy successfully reduced the visual representation space, allowing AlexNet to achieve optimal classification under the specified experimental conditions.

This work suggests an integrated system to classify blood cells and CTCs using microfluidics with optimized cell staining and AI techniques. Cell staining optimization was done by modifying the Romanowsky technique for unfixed cells, which has never been used before on blood cells. Slightly similar work was done on two different cancer cell lines [44]. This optimization makes the system a innovative addition to microfluidic science. In addition to the unique optimized method of staining in suspension, the dataset of suspended cells became available to other future works. Our classification system achieved high performance (100% accuracy, precision, and sensitivity) without expensive materials to preserve the cells’ viability. Presently used suspension cell identification techniques via imaging depend on the use of costly labels that usually include antibodies and fluorescent tags. The system has successfully identified excessively rare cells from blood samples without the use of fluorescence labeling or expensive antibodies, which would further reduce operating costs and ensure an increase in viable cells and recovery rate, enabling the use of the detected cells in additional analysis. In the future, the dataset will increase to get the most reliable system, and the software of the system will be integrated with the hardware part to enable the microfluidic channels to sort each type of cell from its outlet based on the decision that will be taken using the built neural network.
