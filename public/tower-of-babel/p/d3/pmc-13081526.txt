Elevated platelet-activating factor levels predict complete brachial plexus injury: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Ziyang Zhang, Guoxu Ma, Tingting Dang, Yonglu Huang, Fan Gong, Jianke Wu, Xiaoliang Li, Jian Gao, Hanlin Zhang, Yi Ding, Tianyu Zhou, Bowen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObjective biochemical markers for stratifying brachial plexus injury (BPI) severity remain lacking. Platelet-activating factor (PAF), an inflammatory mediator implicated in neurological damage, has not been evaluated in traumatic BPI. We investigated whether serum PAF levels predict complete injury and correlate with nerve conduction impairment.MethodsThis retrospective case-control study analyzed 102 traumatic BPI patients (43 complete, 59 incomplete) and 51 matched controls enrolled between January 2020 and December 2023. Serum PAF and nerve conduction velocities (NCV) of median, radial, and ulnar nerves were measured. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling, and ROC analysis were performed.ResultsBPI patients exhibited elevated PAF versus controls (171.8±26.1 vs. 147.7±12.1 pg/mL, P<0.001) and reduced mean NCV (19.8±2.6 vs. 64.0±6.1 m/s, P<0.001).PAF correlated inversely with NCV (ρ=-0.50, P<0.001). Complete injuries showed higher PAF (189.3±23.5 vs. 158.9±19.8 pg/mL, P<0.001) and lower NCV (all P<0.001). PAF (OR=1.151 per pg/mL, 95% CI: 1.033–1.284, P=0.011) and mean NCV (OR=0.012, P=0.014) independently predicted complete injury. RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear threshold at 165 pg/mL (OR=5.247, 95% CI: 2.808-9.803). PAF achieved good discrimination (AUC=0.840, sensitivity 84%, specificity 68%), though inferior to NCV (AUC=0.982). A multivariable model combining PAF, NCV, and injury-to-detection interval reached near-perfect discrimination (AUC=0.997). ConclusionsSerum PAF independently predicts complete BPI with a nonlinear threshold at 165 pg/mL. While electrophysiological assessment remains the gold standard, PAF demonstrates potential as a rapid, minimally invasive screening tool in acute settings where immediate nerve conduction studies are unavailable, particularly for triaging patients requiring urgent intervention. Introduction

Brachial plexus injury (BPI) is a common type of peripheral nerve injury, often caused by traffic accidents, birth injuries, sports trauma, or iatrogenic factors. It severely affects the upper limb motor and sensory functions of patients, leading to shoulder abduction, elbow flexion and extension, and fine hand movement disorders. It can also cause intractable neuropathic pain, significantly impacting the quality of life. Approximately 30% of severe cases result in permanent disability [1]. Depending on the severity of the injury, BPI can be classified into neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis [2–4]. The most severe neurotmesis indicates complete disruption of the nerve structure, including all supporting structures within the nerve, and such injuries are almost impossible to heal spontaneously, requiring surgical intervention.

Currently, research on BPI mainly focuses on surgical treatments, such as nerve grafting and transfer, and rehabilitation interventions. However, molecular mechanism studies are relatively limited. Clinical assessment of BPI severity mainly relies on physical examination, imaging (e.g., MRI, ultrasound), and neurophysiological testing. However, these methods are susceptible to the timing of examination, technical factors, and patient cooperation, and there is still a lack of objective and sensitive biochemical markers for auxiliary diagnosis and prognostic evaluation.

Platelet-activating factor (PAF) is a potent phospholipid inflammatory mediator produced by platelets, endothelial cells, and nerve cells, among others. It is involved in inflammatory responses, thrombus formation, and nerve injury repair processes [5]. Recent studies have shown that PAF is abnormally expressed in various neurological diseases and is closely related to disease progression [6, 7]. In acute central nervous system injuries, such as stroke and spinal cord injury, PAF levels are significantly elevated, exacerbating secondary nerve injury by promoting platelet aggregation, intensifying inflammatory responses, and causing microcirculatory disturbances [8, 9]. In chronic neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, PAF may promote neuroinflammation by activating microglia [10]. In peripheral neuropathies, such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy, PAF has been shown to be associated with oxidative stress and microvascular lesions [11]. Animal experiments have demonstrated that the activation of the PAF pathway can inhibit the expression of neurotrophic factors, thereby suppressing the repair of rat facial nerve crush injuries [12].

However, the changes in PAF in BPI and its relationship with injury severity and nerve function recovery remain unclear. This study focuses on serum PAF levels in BPI patients and aims to explore their association with injury severity and nerve conduction velocity, including identification of clinically relevant thresholds for risk stratification, providing a new molecular marker for clinical diagnosis and prognosis.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective case-control study was conducted at The People’s Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region between July 2022 and January 2025, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. (2025)-LL-073) prior to data analysis. The study involved retrospective analysis of clinical data and biological samples that were collected during routine clinical care. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data.

Brachial plexus injury (BPI) patients (n = 102) met the following criteria: (1) Diagnosed with traumatic BPI based on clinical examination (upper limb motor and/or sensory dysfunction) and confirmed by electromyography (EMG) [13]; (2) Clear history of acute trauma (motor vehicle accidents, falls, sports injuries, industrial accidents) with blood sample collection within 90 days post-injury; (3) Unilateral BPI with stable vital signs at the time of evaluation; (4) Age ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Non-traumatic BPI (e.g., obstetric injury, radiation-induced neuropathy, tumor infiltration, inflammatory neuritis); (2) Pre-existing peripheral neuropathy (e.g., diabetic neuropathy, hereditary neuropathy); (3) Concurrent conditions affecting PAF metabolism (e.g., sepsis, hematological malignancies, chronic inflammatory diseases);

(4) Bilateral BPI or unstable systemic condition requiring intensive care.

Age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 51) were recruited from individuals undergoing routine health examinations at the hospital physical examination center during the same period. Control participants had no history of neurological disorders, trauma within the past year, or chronic inflammatory conditions.

Blood sample collection and PAF measurement

Venous blood (5 mL) was collected from all participants in the morning (07:00–09:00 AM) after overnight fasting (≥ 8 h). For BPI patients, blood was collected on the morning following hospital admission prior to any surgical intervention. No patients underwent emergency surgery before blood collection, as all surgical interventions occurred at least 6 h post-admission. For healthy controls, blood samples were collected during their scheduled health examination appointments.

All samples were transported to the central laboratory within 30 min and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C in the institutional biobank with no freeze-thaw cycles. All samples from both groups were analyzed in a single batch to eliminate inter-assay variability.

Plasma PAF levels were quantified using a Human PAF ELISA Kit (Elabscience, Cat. No. E-EL-H2348, Houston, TX, USA) with sensitivity of 9.38 pg/mL and detection range of 15.63-1,000 pg/mL (intra-assay CV < 5%, inter-assay CV < 10%). Samples were analyzed in duplicate; means were used for analysis. Laboratory personnel were blinded to clinical data. Standard curves exhibited R² >0.99; samples with duplicate CV > 10% were re-assayed.

Nerve conduction studies

Motor nerve conduction studies (NCS) were performed using a KEYPOINT 9033A07 EMG system (Dantec, Denmark) following American Association of Neuromuscular & Electrodiagnostic Medicine (AANEM) guidelines [14]. A single blinded neurophysiologist measured motor nerve conduction velocity (NCV) for three nerves (median, radial, ulnar) using surface electrodes:

Stimulation parameters: 0.2 ms duration, supramaximal intensity (10–20% above maximal compound muscle action potential [CMAP] amplitude). Skin temperature was maintained at 32–34 °C [15]. NCV was calculated as: Distance (mm) / Latency difference (ms). Normal reference range: ≥50 m/s for all nerves [1].

Mean NCV was calculated as the arithmetic average of the three nerves, justified by high intercorrelation (Pearson r = 0.72–0.85, all P < 0.001) and similar associations with injury severity (all P < 0.001).

Injury severity classification

BPI severity was classified by EMG examination within 90 days post-injury, performed independently by two blinded neurophysiologists with discrepancies resolved by consensus [16]:

Complete injury (n = 43): A large number of spontaneous potentials (fibrillations and positive sharp waves) were detected in the muscles innervated by the corresponding nerve roots and their branches, with no voluntary motor unit potentials and CMAP amplitude < 0.5 mV or < 10% of the contralateral side upon electrical stimulation; SNAPs were absent.

Incomplete injury (n = 59): Spontaneous potentials were present, with detectable voluntary motor unit potentials and CMAP amplitude ≥ 0.5 mV or ≥ 10% of the contralateral side, but with motor NCV < 80% of normal; partial sensory preservation was confirmed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD; skewed data as median [IQR]. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests (continuous variables) or chi-square tests (categorical variables). PAF-NCV relationships were assessed by Spearman’s correlation and visualized using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS, span = 0.5). Outliers were identified by box plot analysis (1.5× IQR criterion) and sensitivity analyses were performed.

Multivariable logistic regression modeled injury severity (complete vs. incomplete) using PAF, mean NCV, and potential confounders. Nine candidate confounders were systematically screened ( injury-to-detection interval, age, surgical intervention, mechanism of injury, concomitant fracture, brain/spinal cord injury, vascular injury, thoracic injury, muscle injury) by testing associations with: (1) exposure (PAF) using Spearman correlation (continuous variables) or t-test/ANOVA (categorical variables), and (2) outcome (injury severity) using univariate logistic regression. Variables showing association with either at P < 0.10 were retained as covariates. Sample size: 43 events, 2 primary predictors, events-per-variable ratio = 21.5 [17]. Model fit was assessed by Hosmer-Lemeshow test; multicollinearity by variance inflation factors (VIF < 2.0).

Dose-response relationship analysis: Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with 4 knots (placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of PAF distribution) modeled the potentially nonlinear association between PAF levels and complete injury risk, adjusted for mean NCV and injury-to-detection interval. The reference level was set at the 10th percentile of PAF. Nonlinearity was formally tested using the likelihood ratio test comparing the RCS model to a linear logistic model. RCS curves with 95% confidence intervals were generated using the `rms` package in R.

ROC analysis evaluated diagnostic performance of PAF alone, mean NCV alone, and the multivariable model. Optimal cut-offs were determined by Youden’s Index (single predictors) or predicted probability ≥ 0.5 (multivariable model). AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test [18]. Bootstrap internal validation (1,000 resamples) quantified model optimism, with optimism-corrected AUC calculated as apparent AUC minus mean optimism [19].

Results

PAF elevation in BPI patients

To establish the pathological relevance of PAF in traumatic BPI, we compared serum PAF levels between BPI patients (n = 102) and healthy controls (n = 51). The groups were well-matched for age (41 ± 6 vs. 41 ± 5 years, P = 0.80) and sex (77% vs. 71% male, P = 0.47). BPI patients exhibited significantly elevated PAF levels compared to controls (171.8 ± 26.1 vs. 147.7 ± 12.1 pg/mL, P < 0.001), representing a 16.3% increase above the normal reference range (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). Nerve conduction velocities (NCV) were profoundly reduced in BPI patients (mean NCV: 19.8 ± 2.6 m/s) compared to controls (64.0 ± 6.1 m/s, P < 0.001), reflecting severe neurophysiological impairment.

Box plot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) identified 6 outliers using the 1.5× interquartile range criterion: 5 PAF outliers (1 control, 4 BPI) and 1 mean nerve conduction velocity outlier (control only). Clinical characteristics stratified by injury severity

Among 102 BPI patients, 43 (42%) were classified as complete injuries based on electrophysiological criteria. Complete injury patients had significantly higher PAF (189.3 ± 23.5 vs. 158.9 ± 19.8 pg/mL, P < 0.001) and lower NCV across all three nerves (median: 15.1 ± 2.8 m/s; radial: 17.9 ± 3.5 m/s; ulnar: 18.9 ± 2.4 m/s) compared to incomplete injuries (median: 18.4 ± 3.6 m/s; radial: 23.0 ± 2.0 m/s; ulnar: 23.5 ± 1.9 m/s; all P < 0.001) (Table 2).

The two groups were comparable in demographics, trauma mechanism, and concomitant injuries (all P > 0.05). Notably, trauma mechanism (motor vehicle accidents, falls, etc.) was not associated with injury severity (Fisher’s exact P = 0.99). Blood samples were collected at a median of 7 days (IQR: 3–14 days; range: 1–87 days) post-injury. The distribution was right-skewed, with 68% of samples collected within 14 days and 89% within 30 days (Supplementary Fig. 2). Complete injury patients were tested slightly earlier (6.8 ± 6.6 vs. 11.1 ± 11.2 days, P = 0.018), likely reflecting more urgent clinical presentation; this variable was adjusted in multivariable analysis (Table 2).

Systematic screening of potential confounders

To address potential confounding effects, we systematically examined five clinically relevant variables: injury-to-detection interval, age, surgical intervention, mechanism of injury and concomitant injuries. The screening results are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3.

Injury-to-detection interval met confounding criteria (associated with severity: OR = 0.945 per day, 95% CI: 0.897–0.996, P = 0.036; marginally associated with PAF: Spearman ρ=-0.178, P = 0.07). The remaining variables showed no significant associations with PAF or severity: age (PAF: ρ = 0.03, P = 0.76; severity: OR = 1.003, P = 0.93), surgical intervention (PAF: t = 0.46, P = 0.65; severity: OR = 1.18, P = 0.72), trauma mechanism (PAF: F = 0.67, P = 0.67; severity: Fisher’s P = 1.00), and concomitant injuries (PAF: t = 0.32, P = 0.75; severity: OR = 1.175, P = 0.74). Injury-to-detection interval was therefore adjusted in multivariable models.

Multivariable analysis: independent prediction of complete injury

To construct a parsimonious multivariable model while maintaining adequate statistical power, mean NCV (averaged across median, radial, and ulnar nerves) was used as a composite measure to optimize the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio. Multivariable logistic regression was performed adjusting for injury-to-detection interval as a potential confounder. The model included two primary predictors (PAF and mean NCV), yielding an EPV of 21.5 (43 events / 2 predictors) or 14.3 when including the confounder (43 events / 3 variables), both exceeding the recommended threshold of ≥ 10 for logistic regression stability [17].

Both PAF and mean NCV were independent predictors of complete injury (Table 3): PAF (OR = 1.151 per pg/mL, 95% CI: 1.033–1.284, P = 0.011) and mean NCV (OR = 0.012 per m/s, 95% CI: 0.000-0.404, P = 0.014). Injury-to-detection interval was not significantly associated with severity after adjustment (OR = 0.745, 95% CI: 0.501–1.109, P = 0.15).

Stratified correlation: PAF as a categorical classifier

Stratified correlation analysis revealed distinct patterns by injury severity (Supplementary Fig. 4). Overall, PAF showed a moderate negative correlation with mean NCV (Spearman’s ρ=-0.50, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 4A). However, the correlation weakened substantially within each severity group: complete injury group (n = 43, ρ=-0.13, P = 0.42, Supplementary Fig. 4B); incomplete injury group (n = 59, ρ=-0.05, P = 0.72, Supplementary Fig. 4C). PAF values clustered into two zones: incomplete injuries predominantly < 165 pg/mL; complete injuries predominantly > 180 pg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Individual nerve analysis (median, radial, ulnar) revealed similarly weak correlations (ρ=-0.27 to -0.47, P < 0.05;Supplementary Fig. 4D).

Dose-response relationship: threshold effect at 165 pg/mL

Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a nonlinear dose-response relationship with a threshold effect at 165 pg/mL (Fig. 1). Below this threshold, OR increased progressively from 1.000 (142.5 pg/mL) to 5.247 (165 pg/mL). The 165–190 pg/mL range exhibited the steepest slope (OR 5.247→26.967, approximately 1.9-fold per 10 pg/mL increase), while risk continued rising steeply above 190 pg/mL (OR 26.967→49.634 at 200 pg/mL). At the ROC-derived cutoff (165.1 pg/mL), PAF was associated with 5.247-fold increased odds of complete injury (95% CI: 2.808–9.803, P < 0.001) compared to the reference (142.5 pg/mL). The nonlinear relationship was statistically significant (P < 0.001), indicating threshold-based risk acceleration beyond 165 pg/mL.

Diagnostic performance

ROC curve analysis within the BPI cohort (Fig. 2; Table 4) evaluated diagnostic utility for classifying injury severity. PAF alone achieved good discrimination (AUC = 0.840, 95% CI: 0.765–0.914) with 84% sensitivity and 68% specificity at the 165 pg/mL cut-off—a threshold validated by the RCS analysis as the inflection point of maximum risk acceleration (section "Dose-response relationship: threshold effect at 165 pg/mL"). As expected, mean NCV demonstrated superior performance (AUC = 0.982, 95% CI: 0.961-1.000; sensitivity 100%, specificity 88%). A multivariable model combining PAF, NCV, and injury-to-detection interval achieved near-perfect discrimination (AUC = 0.997), significantly outperforming PAF alone (P < 0.001, DeLong test) but not NCV alone (P = 0.13). Bootstrap internal validation with 1,000 resamples demonstrated excellent model stability with minimal optimism (mean optimism = 0.0085 ± 0.0135; optimism-corrected AUC = 0.989, 95% CI: 0.953-1.000; Supplementary Fig. 5 and Table 4).

Discussion

This retrospective study provides the first systematic evidence that serum PAF is elevated in traumatic brachial plexus injury (BPI) and independently predicts complete injury. Through systematic confounder screening, Bootstrap internal validation (optimism-corrected AUC = 0.989), and restricted cubic spline analysis capturing nonlinear thresholds, our principal findings include: (1) PAF levels were significantly higher in BPI patients versus matched controls (171.8 ± 26.1 vs. 147.7 ± 12.1 pg/mL, P < 0.001); (2) PAF demonstrated a validated nonlinear threshold at 165 pg/mL, beyond which complete injury risk increased 5.3-fold (95% CI: 2.8–9.8); (3) PAF correlated inversely with nerve conduction velocities (mean NCV: ρ=-0.50, P < 0.001); (4) after adjusting for NCV and injury-to-detection interval, PAF independently predicted complete injury (OR = 1.151 per pg/mL, 95% CI: 1.033–1.284, P = 0.011).

Pathophysiological mechanisms and specificity

PAF is a ubiquitous inflammatory mediator released during tissue injury [20], raising a critical question: does PAF elevation reflect nerve-specific pathology or systemic trauma response? Our findings suggest PAF may associate with neural injury severity through three converging lines of evidence:

First, nerve-specific correlations: PAF correlated inversely with individual nerve conduction velocities (median nerve ρ=-0.27, radial nerve ρ=-0.38, ulnar nerve ρ=-0.47; Supplementary Fig. 4D), suggesting PAF levels may reflect the degree of neural dysfunction, albeit with weak-to-moderate correlation strength. Second, multivariable adjustment: in multivariable analysis, injury mechanism, surgical intervention, and concomitant injuries showed no independent association with injury severity (adjusted P > 0.05), while PAF retained independent predictive value (P = 0.011). This pattern suggests PAF elevation more likely reflects secondary inflammatory pathological processes following neural injury rather than trauma type differences, surgical effects, or systemic trauma burden. Third, dose-response pattern: restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear threshold effect with accelerated risk beyond 165 pg/mL. This threshold phenomenon aligns with dose-dependent regulatory mechanisms of inflammatory mediators [21], where low-dose stimuli require amplification mechanisms while high-dose stimuli directly activate cascade reactions.

At the molecular level, PAF may contribute to secondary BPI injury through multiple mechanisms [20, 22–24]: (1) inflammatory cell activation—PAF activates platelets and leukocytes, promoting release of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) that create an inflammatory microenvironment inhibiting nerve regeneration [22]; (2) microcirculatory dysfunction—PAF increases vascular permeability, causing endoneurial edema and ischemia that exacerbate conduction block [23]; (3) regeneration inhibition—PAF may activate glial cells and alter the nerve regeneration microenvironment, impairing axonal regrowth [24]. These mechanisms collectively explain the differential PAF elevation between complete and incomplete injuries (189.3 vs. 158.9 pg/mL, P < 0.001), though whether PAF elevation represents a specific response to local neural injury or systemic inflammation manifesting in neural tissue remains unresolved.

Importantly, we do not claim PAF is a nerve-specific biomarker. PAF distributions overlap partially between complete and incomplete injuries, suggesting other factors influence PAF levels. As an inflammatory-axis biomarker, PAF is complementary to emerging structural markers: peripherin, a type-III intermediate filament showing promise for traumatic axonal injury detection in preclinical models [25], and phosphatidylserine (not yet available as a blood biomarker). Future studies integrating PAF (inflammatory axis) with neurofilament light chain and peripherin (structural axis) into multi-biomarker panels may enhance diagnostic precision beyond single markers [21, 26]. Multicenter validation incorporating polytrauma controls without nerve injury is needed to establish PAF’s specificity for neural injury versus systemic inflammation. Our findings suggest that in clinical contexts of suspected nerve injury, PAF provides incremental prognostic information beyond clinical examination alone.

Diagnostic performance and clinical applications

While nerve conduction studies achieve near-perfect diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.982), PAF testing offers practical advantages: simple venipuncture, ease of operation, and no requirement for specialized electrophysiological personnel. Although adding PAF to NCV-based multivariable models reached AUC = 0.997, given NCV’s already exceptional accuracy, PAF’s primary value lies in providing rapid triage tools rather than replacing NCV. The 165 pg/mL threshold identified by restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated 84% sensitivity and 68% specificity—acceptable metrics for screening tools, with high sensitivity minimizing false negatives.

We emphasize that PAF cannot substitute comprehensive electrophysiological assessment [27–29], which remains the gold standard for injury classification and surgical planning. Rather, PAF represents a complementary adjunctive tool that may accelerate triage decisions in acute settings. This positioning is supported by stratified correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4): overall PAF-NCV correlation was strong (ρ=-0.50, P < 0.001), but attenuated substantially within severity strata (complete injury: ρ=-0.13, P = 0.42; incomplete injury: ρ=-0.05, P = 0.72), with PAF values clustering into distinct ranges (incomplete injury < 165 pg/mL, complete injury > 180 pg/mL). This pattern suggests PAF functions more effectively as a categorical marker distinguishing severity groups than as a continuous predictor of individual nerve function. Specifically, within the same severity group, PAF shows weak correlation with subtle NCV variations, yet effectively discriminates between complete and incomplete injury categories—a characteristic rendering PAF most suitable for triage applications rather than granular functional assessment.

Based on these properties, we propose three evidence-based clinical applications: (1) emergency triage in resource-limited settings—in hospitals without immediate access to nerve conduction studies (e.g., community hospitals, emergency departments), PAF > 165 pg/mL may identify high-risk patients requiring urgent referral or expedited electrophysiological evaluation; (2) preoperative risk stratification—elevated PAF may reflect a more severe neuroinflammatory microenvironment; future studies should explore PAF’s relationship with postoperative functional recovery to evaluate its prognostic value; (3) clinical trial enrichment—PAF could serve as an inclusion criterion for neuroprotective trials targeting inflammatory pathways (e.g., PAF receptor antagonists [30]), enriching subgroups most likely to benefit by selecting patients with active inflammation (high PAF), a strategy proven to enhance trial efficiency and reduce costs in Alzheimer’s disease and other neurological conditions [31].

Study limitations

Three key limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our single-center cross-sectional design measured PAF at a single timepoint before surgery, precluding assessment of PAF kinetics and optimal sampling windows despite standardized collection (median 7 days post-injury; 68% within 14 days) and covariate adjustment for injury-to-detection interval (adjusted OR = 0.745, 95% CI: 0.501–1.109, P = 0.15). Longitudinal studies are needed to characterize temporal trajectories. Additionally, diagnostic performance estimates derived from our case-control framework may overestimate discrimination compared to consecutive patient cohorts in routine clinical practice with more heterogeneous disease spectra; external validation in prospective diagnostic cohorts is essential to confirm generalizability despite minimal optimism in Bootstrap internal validation (optimism-corrected AUC = 0.989). Second, we measured circulating PAF but not local expression in injured nerve tissue or PTAFR activation status. While nerve-specific correlation patterns argue against purely systemic spillover, histological studies will further clarify PAF’s neurogenic origin. Third, residual confounding risk persists despite adjusting for injury mechanism, surgical intervention, and concomitant injuries, as quantitative trauma severity scores (e.g., ISS) and systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, IL-6) were unavailable. However, PAF’s independent predictive value after controlling for major confounders (P = 0.011) suggests residual confounding is unlikely to fully explain these associations.

Conclusion

This retrospective study provides the first systematic evidence that serum PAF independently predicts complete BPI. PAF discriminated complete from incomplete injuries with an AUC of 0.840 (sensitivity 84%, specificity 68%), with a nonlinear threshold at 165 pg/mL conferring 5.3-fold increased risk of complete injury (95% CI: 2.8–9.8). Critically, PAF retains independent predictive value after adjusting for NCV and injury-to-detection interval (adjusted OR = 1.151 per pg/mL, 95% CI: 1.033–1.284, P = 0.011; concomitant injuries and surgical intervention showed no association with PAF or injury severity and were excluded from multivariable analysis), suggesting PAF elevation may preferentially reflect neural injury-associated inflammation rather than systemic trauma burden, though definitive differentiation requires validation in cohorts with polytrauma controls without nerve injury. Although electrophysiological assessment remains the gold standard for BPI classification, PAF provides a rapid, minimally invasive adjunctive tool for emergency triage when nerve conduction studies are unavailable—particularly valuable for identifying high-risk patients (PAF > 165 pg/mL) requiring urgent specialist referral.
