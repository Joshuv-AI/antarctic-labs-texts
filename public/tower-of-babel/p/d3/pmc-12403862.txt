Social prescribing for informal carers: a pre-post study
Authors: Christina Aggar, Alessandra K. Teunisse, Michelle Bissett, Rosanne Freak-Poli, JR Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInformal carers often experience compromised mental and physical wellbeing due to their caring responsibilities. While social prescribing shows promise in supporting various populations, evidence for its effectiveness in supporting carers is limited. This study assessed the impact of a social prescribing program on health-related quality of life and other wellbeing measures among Australian adult carers.MethodsAnalysis of de-identified data was conducted using pre- and post-intervention measures from two social prescribing programs in New South Wales, Australia (December 2021-August 2024). Participants were adults who self-presented or were referred by their GP for either mental ill-health or chronic disease management. Of 821 eligible participants, 93 (11.3%) were identified as carers based on their social prescriptions and case note review. The intervention involved link workers co-designing individualised plans with participants over 12 weeks, providing referrals to community services and supports. Outcomes included quality of life, mental wellbeing, self-reported health, psychological distress, and healthcare utilisation. Statistical analysis included paired t-tests for pre-post comparisons.ResultsCarers received significantly more social prescriptions than non-carers (mean difference = 2.05, p = .002), particularly in mental and emotional wellbeing (54%) and physical wellbeing (45%) domains. Following the intervention, carers showed significant improvements in quality of life (p < .001), mental wellbeing (p < .001), self-reported health (p < .001), and psychological distress (p < .001). GP visits reduced significantly (p = .002), while hospital visits showed non-significant reductions. Link workers primarily engaged with carers via phone (98%), followed by internet (80%) and face-to-face (67%) contact.ConclusionsSocial prescribing appears to be an effective intervention for supporting carers’ wellbeing and reducing healthcare utilisation. The high uptake of phone-based support suggests potential for scalable remote delivery. Future research with larger samples is needed to examine program benefits across diverse demographic groups and varying intensities of caring responsibilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-02978-9.

Social prescribing for informal carers: a pre-post study

Three million Australians are carers and nearly half of them are primary carers who also live with a disability [1]. This substantial workforce provides AUD$77.9 billion worth of unpaid care annually [2], highlighting both their economic contribution and the scale of informal caregiving in Australia. Despite this significant contribution, many carers do not receive adequate support as they do not identify as carers, are unable to complete the necessary paperwork, or are ineligible for funding [3]. Carers Australia, the national peak body for carers define carers as “people who provide unpaid care and support to family members and friends who have a disability, mental illness, chronic condition, terminal illness, an alcohol or other drug issue or who are frail aged” [4]. The majority of carers are female, with many caring for parents in later life and children with special needs [2].

The impact of caregiving on carers’ time, wellbeing and social connection is significant. Carers spend an average of 72.84 h per week on caregiving duties [5], with those who are female, older, spousal-carers, or primary carers particularly vulnerable to compromised mental and physical wellbeing [6]. Phillips et al. [6] highlighted that female carers particularly face higher levels of psychological distress, spouses tend to prioritise caregiving over their own wellbeing, and older family carers tend to struggle with the physical demands of care and accessing formal support. However, factors such as social support and community engagement may offer some protection to carer’s mental and physical wellbeing, suggesting that interventions targeting social connections and community resources could be beneficial [6]. The caregiving role can significantly diminish social circles, with carers’ own health conditions potentially limiting their ability to socialise or create new connections [6]. The intensity of the caring commitment often leads to social isolation, with more than half of carers experiencing loneliness compared to the Australian population norm of 7% [5]. This loneliness often stems from social isolation, as the intensity of caring responsibilities can limit opportunities for social interaction and community engagement [7]. These factors collectively underscore the critical need to focus on carers’ wellbeing and develop targeted interventions to support this vulnerable group.

The mechanisms through which caring impacts health and wellbeing, include social isolation due to caring responsibilities, chronic stress from managing complex care needs, neglect of personal health while prioritising the care recipient, and financial strain from reduced work capacity. Social prescribing may address these mechanisms of carer burden by: [1] connecting carers to peer support networks to reduce isolation; [2] linking them to practical services (respite care, financial counselling) to reduce stress; [3] facilitating access to health and wellbeing activities that carers can prioritise for themselves; and [4] providing advocacy support to navigate complex service systems. This theoretical alignment between carer burden mechanisms and social prescribing intervention targets provides strong rationale for examining social prescribing effectiveness in this population.

Social prescribing enables trusted professionals, such as GPs, to refer individuals to a link worker (a trained professional who acts as a bridge between healthcare and community services) who connects them with community-based, non-clinical supports to address social needs and enhance health and wellbeing [8]. Given the impact of caregiving on carers’ health and well-being, social prescribing may be of benefit as systematic reviews of other population has demonstrated improvements in wellbeing [9, 10], mental wellbeing [11–13], social isolation [14], and quality of life [12]. A key feature of social prescribing is offering a range of co-designed programs or activities that address the social determinants of health such as housing stability, financial security, social connections, and access to community resources—through targeted referrals and practical support [15, 16]. This flexible, person-centred approach allows tailored support to meet carers’ specific needs and interests, promoting holistic health and wellbeing by addressing both external social determinants (such as access to services and social connections) and supporting individual capacity to navigate these systems effectively [15]. In practice, social prescribing takes a holistic approach by conducting comprehensive assessments that explore physical, mental, social, and practical needs simultaneously, then developing coordinated responses that may include referrals to multiple services, practical support to overcome access barriers, and ongoing follow-up to ensure interventions meet evolving needs.

While research specifically focused on social prescribing for carers is limited, some studies suggest promising outcomes. A systematic review by Letrondo et al. [17] found that arts and culture interventions, such as museum visits and music activities, may benefit carers, showing improvements in overall mood and reduced carer strain. Giebel et al. [18] reported improved mental wellbeing for both care recipients and their carers using the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (SWEMWBS), although their sample size was small (14 people with dementia and 11 carers). A review on loneliness in carers by Velloze et al. [19] indicated that community activity group interventions provided valuable social contact and emotional support. While these studies observed decreases in loneliness, many were small-scale and did not always achieve statistical significance, despite positive qualitative results. Vlachantoni et al.’s [20] study found that carers not engaged in social participation had significantly poorer mental health and wellbeing scores, with the qualitative findings supporting this, showing that social participation improved carers’ coping, reduced burden, and strengthened community. Similarly, Mohanty et al. [21] found that informal carers suffer from worse general and mental health than non-carers, but their social connectedness (defined as both the quantity and quality of social relationships and sense of belonging within social networks) was demonstrated to be important in counteracting these negative health effects, consistent with the stress buffering hypothesis of social networks [22]. While these studies suggest potential benefits for social prescribing to improve carers’ quality of life and wellbeing, robust evidence specifically demonstrating its effectiveness for this population is currently lacking, underscoring the need for further research in this area.

Given that carers often neglect their own health needs while prioritising caregiving responsibilities, examining both wellbeing outcomes and healthcare utilisation patterns is important for understanding intervention effects. The aim of this study was to assess the impact of a social prescribing program on health-related quality of life, general wellbeing, mental wellbeing, subjective health, psychological distress, and healthcare utilisation among Australian adults who are carers. A secondary exploratory analysis was conducted to: determine if carers receive more social prescriptions or spend more time with the link worker than non-carers; examine the social prescription types allocated to carers; and examine link worker engagement modalities for carers. We defined intervention intensity as the number of social prescriptions received and duration of engagement, as these factors may influence intervention effectiveness and provide insights for program planning.

Methods

Design

An analysis of de-identified (personally identifiable information removed with participant codes used for linkage) pre-post intervention data was undertaken. Pre-intervention data were collected at program commencement following initial assessment, and post-intervention data were collected at program completion after up to 12 weeks of intervention. All participants signed informed consent on participation. The study was approved by the relevant institutional Human Research Ethics Committee (Project ID: removed for review).

Participants

This study evaluated a sub-group of carers involved in a larger social prescribing program. Participants from the larger study were eligible for the social prescribing programs (and subsequently this research analysis) if they were adults aged 18 or over and met program-specific criteria. Participants required: [1] moderate or severe mental illness or psychological distress as assessed by their referring GP using standardised screening tools or clinical judgment; [2] at least one additional complexity factor including significant life stressors, co-occurring physical health conditions, recent or risk of hospitalisation, moderate to severe functional impairment, limited support networks, or difficulty accessing services; [3] a chronic condition requiring ongoing management. Exclusion criteria included current acute inpatient treatment or significant cognitive impairment that would prevent engagement with the intervention. Participants learned about the programs through GP referrals or self-referral pathways advertised in healthcare settings.

Social prescribing programs

The social prescribing programs were delivered by a not-for-profit primary health and social care organisation in New South Wales, Australia. A link worker model consisting of qualified social workers who served as intermediaries between healthcare services and community resources was utilised. The programs commenced in 2021 and remain ongoing, accepting 50–100 participants per year. Referral pathways included GP referrals and self-referrals, with no cost to participants. The programs addressed a wide range of issues including social isolation, housing instability, financial stress, health management, and access to community services. Interventions were individually tailored but commonly included referrals to support groups, practical assistance services, educational programs, social activities, and advocacy support. Data for this analysis were collected from December 1, 2021, to August 9, 2024.

Link workers contacted participants within 24 h of receiving a referral to introduce the program and conduct an initial assessment. This early contact ensured that people with urgent issues like housing or food security could be connected sooner through priority pathways. Assessments were conducted through home visits or telephone consultations, involving review of GP referral forms, verification of demographic information, and risk assessment. Participants were then connected with a link worker, typically within 4 weeks. As part of routine program evaluation (not research-specific assessment), link workers administered a comprehensive battery of surveys to participants, either in their homes or via telephone recording their responses. This approach ensured consistency in data collection while accommodating participants with varying literacy levels or visual impairments. Link workers also explored participants’ needs across various domains including housing stability, financial security, social connections, physical health management, mental health support, transportation, education/employment, and family/caring responsibilities to inform the development of tailored social prescriptions.

Participants, working with their link workers, co-designed their individualised plan through a three step approach: (i) discussing their social, emotional, or practical needs and goals; (ii) exploring potential services available as solutions to support their needs; and (iii) developing formal referrals for suitable services, which included written referral letters, direct telephone introductions to services, assistance with application processes, and provision of contact information and appointment scheduling support. This included direct support to access government services as well as linking to services in the voluntary, not-for-profit, and community sectors. The intention was that link workers would continue to support participants for up to 12 weeks through regular contact (phone, email, face-to-face), progress monitoring, problem-solving barriers to service access, advocacy with service providers, additional referrals as needs evolved, and practical assistance such as completing forms or attending appointments when necessary. At the conclusion of the program, the link worker assisted the participant in completing the post-intervention assessment. It is important to note that this study represents a secondary analysis of routinely collected program data. The assessment measures were administered as part of standard program evaluation, with participants consenting to have their de-identified data used for research purposes. No additional research-specific procedures were implemented.

Carer status

In both programs, the social prescription that a participant received was categorised in one of thirty domains by the link workers. Carer status was defined as participants with social prescriptions in the “caring for children”, or “caring for others” domains or if they had received a referral to specific carer organisations. Then, the standardised case notes maintained by link workers for each participant were systematically reviewed (by co-author AT) to determine the person’s carer status. While we acknowledge that different caring relationships (spousal, parent, adult child) may have distinct support needs, our analysis grouped all non-child caregiving as ‘caring for others’ due to: [1] the social prescribing program’s categorisation system, which did not differentiate between types of adult caregiving relationships; [2] the primary focus on intervention delivery rather than relationship-specific analysis; and [3] insufficient sample sizes within caring subgroups to enable meaningful statistical comparison.

Process measures

Social Prescriptions

Each social prescription represented a single referral to a specific service, activity, or intervention (e.g., support group, housing assistance, community garden, exercise program). Participants could receive multiple prescriptions across seven categories: Caring, Physical Wellbeing, Mental and Emotional Wellbeing, Social Wellbeing, Activities of Daily Living, Education and Economic Participation, and Accommodation and Environment. Each prescription was assigned to its primary category based on main intended outcome, as determined by the referring link worker.

Mental health diagnosis Referral forms from the GPs included a primary mental health diagnosis and, where applicable, additional mental health or physical health diagnoses.

Link worker engagement

Each engagement a participant had with a link worker was recorded in terms of frequency and duration across modalities: face-to-face, phone, video calls (including online platforms), or internet contact (text-based communications including email, messaging, and online portals).

Outcome measures

At pre- and post-intervention, five domains of outcomes were measured by link workers through structured interviews, as well as healthcare provider utilisation.

Quality of life

The EQ-5D-5 L [23] was administered verbally by link workers, with response options read aloud and responses recorded. While this differs from the standard self-completion tick-box format, verbal administration was necessary to accommodate our diverse participant population and ensure consistent data collection across home visits and telephone consultations. Participants rated themselves on their levels of mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (No problems) to 5 (Extreme problems). Scores are indexed from 0 (worst possible health) to 1 (full health) [24]. The EQ-5D-5 L has demonstrated good reliability and validity across diverse populations [25].

General wellbeing

Single-item Measure Yourself Concerns and Wellbeing sub-scale [26] was used to measure general wellbeing. Participants rated their wellbeing concerns on a scale of 0 (Not bothering me at all) to 6 (Bothers me greatly). This scale was then reversed-scored such that higher scores represented greater wellbeing. This single-item measure has demonstrated sensitivity to change in social prescribing populations and is widely used in this field [26].

Mental wellbeing

Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (SWEMWBS) [27] with 7 items rated 1–5. Scores range 7–35, with higher scores indicating better wellbeing. The SWEMWBS has shown good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84–0.89) and construct validity in both clinical and general population samples [27].

Self-reported health

The EuroQol health thermometer [23] was used to measure self-reported health. Participants were asked to rate their health today on a visual analogue scale from 0 (worst health you can imagine) to 100 (best health you can imagine). The EQ visual analogue scale is a validated component of the EuroQol measure and has demonstrated sensitivity to changes in health outcomes in social prescribing studies [28].

Psychological distress

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) is a 10-item measure assessing participants’ experiences over the past 30 days [29]. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘none of the time’ to 5 = ‘all of the time’). Items cover feelings of tiredness, nervousness, hopelessness, restlessness, depression, worthlessness, and difficulty coping. Higher summed scores indicate greater psychological distress. This scale was only used in the Site 1 program, as it was a required component of the Primary Health Network’s mental health programs. The K10 has excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90) and has been validated across diverse populations [30].

Healthcare utilisation

(1) “In the last 4 weeks, have you gone to hospital for treatment” with response options “yes” or “no” for both inpatient and outpatient treatment separately. If “yes”, they were asked to specify how many times (2). “In the last 4 weeks, how many times have you visited your GP?”, with numeric responses (3). “In the last 4 weeks, have you seen any of these health providers? (Apart from treatment in the hospital)” with responses “yes” or “no” to a list of 20 different healthcare providers (4). “Are you worried about going to hospital?” with responses “yes” or “no”.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarised by frequencies with percentages for categorical variables, means with standard deviations (SD), and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. To compare carers and non-carers, independent-samples t-tests were conducted assuming unequal variances due to the substantial difference in group sizes. Paired t-tests assessed changes pre- post- intervention within the carer sample. Data distributions were assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of histograms. For normally distributed continuous variables, means and standard deviations were reported with parametric tests used. For non-normally distributed variables, medians and interquartile ranges were reported alongside means to provide complete distributional information. Paired t-tests were used despite some non-normal distributions due to their robustness to moderate departures from normality in samples of this size. When categories contain fewer than 5 participants, counts were suppressed to ensure the protection of participant identification. Pre- and post-intervention outcome values were compared using paired t-tests using SPSS version 27. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated for all comparisons, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects respectively. No formal power analysis was conducted as this was a secondary analysis of existing program data rather than a prospectively designed study.

Results

Participant characteristics

On August 9, 2024, 992 participants had been enrolled in the social prescribing programs since commencement. Of these, 821 had completed the intervention and were eligible for analysis (Fig. 1), with 171 participants comprising: 77 still undertaking the intervention, 51 unable to be contacted, 25 declining further contact, and 18 who were unable to complete the program for other reasons. 155 were identified as potential carers, based on the social prescriptions they received. After examining the case notes, 62 were determined to not be carers, with the social prescriptions in caring domains being for their carers rather than themselves. The final sample of carers was 93 adults (16 men and 77 women), with a mean age of 53.9 years (SD: 15.57, range: 22 to 87) (Table 1). This equated to 11.3% of the eligible sample identified as carers.

Comparison of carers and non-carers

Using the broader dataset, the subset of carers was compared to non-carers. Carers were more likely to be female than non-carers and most carers were women (Table 1 and Supplementary Material). The small number of male carers (n = 16) limited gender-disaggregated analyses. Mental health diagnoses were present in 95.6% of the total sample (97.8% of carers, 95.3% of non-carers), reflecting the program’s focus on individuals with mental health needs. Among carers with mental health diagnoses, the most common categories were stress-related disorders (including adjustment disorders and stress reactions, n = 51, 54.8%), followed by mood disorders (including depression and bipolar disorder, n = 18, 19.4%), and anxiety disorders (n = 17, 18.3%). Comorbid mental health conditions were present in 53.8% of carers (n = 50). Due to small numbers within diagnostic subgroups, we did not conduct subgroup analyses by specific mental health conditions, focusing instead on the overall carer sample to ensure adequate statistical power for meaningful interpretation. Carers received an average of 2 more social prescriptions (M = 7.32, SD = 5.85) than non-carers (M = 5.28, SD = 4.50; t(106.394) = −3.252, p =.002). The magnitude of the differences in the means (mean difference = −2.045, 95% CI: −3.292 to −0.798) was medium (d = − 0.438). While the social prescribing intervention was 12-weeks in duration (i.e., 84 days), we noted that both carer and non-carer groups were engaged for a considerably longer duration (22 weeks, i.e. 152.24 days). The remainder of the analysis focused on the carer sample only, as our research question specifically concerned the effectiveness of social prescribing for carers. While we presented comparative data between carers and non-carers to characterise differences in intervention intensity and baseline characteristics, our primary interest was in understanding pre-post changes within the carer group to determine whether social prescribing could benefit this specific population.

Carer social prescription types

Carers received at least one social prescription in the caring category. While the average of caring social prescriptions was 2 per carer, it ranged from one to ten (Table 2). Notably, 12 carers only received this single social prescription for caring. Carers also received a range of other social prescriptions, with many receiving a Mental and Emotional Wellbeing (54%) and/or a Physical Wellbeing (45%) social prescriptions.

Main outcomes

Participating in social prescribing was beneficial across all measured domains, however the improvement in general well-being was not statistically significant (Table 3). There was a statistically significant reduction in GP visits from the four weeks prior to commencing the program (M = 1.45, SD = 1.33) to the last four weeks of the program (M = 0.97, SD = 1.13, t [37] = 3.272, p =.002, d = 0.531). While there were slight reductions in inpatient (Mpre = 0.03, SD = 0.16, Mpost = 0.08, SD = 0.36, t [37] = −0.813, p =.422, d = − 0.132) and outpatient (Mpre = 0.24, SD = 0.68, Mpost = 0.32, SD = 0.96, t [37] = −0.424, p =.674, d = − 0.069) hospital visits, these reductions were not statistically significant. Most participants engaged with the link worker through several modalities, including phone, internet and face-to-face (Table 4). Discussion

This study addressed the significant gap in evidence for social prescribing interventions specifically targeting carers. Following intervention, carers showed significant improvements across multiple wellbeing domains and reduced GP utilisation. These findings are important given that carers experience substantial burden affecting their wellbeing, with many struggling to access existing support services despite their AUD$77.9 billion annual contribution to healthcare [2, 3]. Following the intervention, carers showed improvements in all measured domains, however the improvement in general well-being did not reach statistical significance. There was a statistically significant reduction in visits to the GP and reductions in inpatient and outpatient hospital visits, but these did not reach statistical significance. A secondary explorative analysis was conducted to determine if carers required a greater intensity of social prescribing than non-carers. Carers required an average of two more social prescriptions than non-carers. Only 13% of carers received a single social prescription for caring. The most popular social prescriptions were in the Mental and Emotional Wellbeing (54%) and/or a Physical Wellbeing (45%) categories. In terms of modality of engagement with the link worker, the phone was used by nearly all carers and accounted for the highest average engagement frequency and duration of use. Compared to internet (e.g., email) and face-to-face and video.

These findings are important because it is universally known that carers experience numerous challenges that affect their well-being and ability to provide care. Despite growing recognition of their value through policy reforms, comprehensive support for carers remains insufficient [3, 31]. While social service schemes offer financial aid, respite, counselling, education, coaching, and peer support, many carers struggle to access these resources [2]. This gap negatively impacts both the well-being of carers and care recipients, as well as the overall efficiency of the healthcare system [2]. Carers also experience moderate to high psychological distress, which worsens with prolonged caregiving [5–7]. The emotional strain and loneliness associated with caregiving can negatively impact mental health [32]. However, social participation and community engagement have been shown to enhance carers’ well-being by increasing social resources, alleviating loneliness, and improving life satisfaction [6, 7, 33, 34]. Peer support groups also play a key role, offering reflection and coping strategies to help carers manage their responsibilities more effectively [6, 35].

This study provides some evidence that carers could benefit from targeted and tailored interventions designed to meet their unique needs [36, 37]. Notably, when offered social prescribing options, carers demonstrated high engagement, accepting an average of seven referrals across multiple domains of health and wellbeing - significantly more than non-carers. This substantial uptake suggests that carers are not only looking for support but are highly responsive to diverse intervention strategies when made available. Social prescribing for carers offers tailored support and fosters social connections and potentially help them navigate the emotional and practical challenges of caregiving. Link workers connect people to social participation and community resources, helping to reduce isolation and loneliness while promoting holistic, individualised support [38, 39]. Given carers’ demonstrated receptiveness and unique support needs, social prescribing link worker programs should consider carers as a primary target population rather than a subgroup within broader community interventions. By addressing individual preferences, these programs enhance carers’ well-being, engagement, and outcomes [40–42].

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. As a pre-post observational study without a control group, we cannot definitively attribute observed changes to the social prescribing intervention, as improvements may have resulted from natural recovery, concurrent treatments, or life circumstance changes. The sample consisted exclusively of people with mental health or chronic conditions, so findings may not represent carers without these health issues. Our carer identification method through social prescriptions and case note review may have missed carers who did not receive caring-specific prescriptions, potentially leading to misclassification. A more robust approach would involve systematic screening using validated assessment tools at program entry. Our data collection did not systematically capture specific carer-care recipient relationships (spouse, parent, adult child), which may have varying support needs and intervention requirements. Data were collected by link workers, rather than independent staff. Our link workers were qualified social workers, differing from some international models and potentially limiting generalisability. Additionally, our small sample size limited subgroup analyses across demographic characteristics, particularly for diverse cultural groups and different socioeconomic backgrounds.

While we observed positive changes in GP visits, the inclusion of comprehensive hospital data and prospective hospital utilisation could offer a more robust assessment of the intervention’s impact on healthcare service use. Further, this analysis represents an initial cut-point of an ongoing social prescribing intervention. Our sample of 93 carers (11.3% of the total sample) is consistent with population estimates, and the predominately female composition (82.8%) aligns with national carer demographics [2]. However, the small number of male carers (n = 16) prevented gender-disaggregated analyses. We observed that 62 participants received caring social prescriptions intended for their carers rather than for themselves. Technically, these carers could have been enrolled in the social prescribing intervention as well. However, this often proves challenging, as carers frequently struggle to allocate time for their own needs. Social prescribing interventions could leverage this knowledge by offering double enrolment and double appointments to participants and their carers. Future studies should include specific questions about caring responsibilities during the intake process to ensure accurate classification.

Conclusion

Designing and implementing flexible, person-centred approaches that address the diverse needs of carers is imperative. This study highlights the potential of social prescribing link worker programs to address the multifaceted needs of carers and improve their wellbeing. By connecting carers to community resources and fostering social participation, social prescribing can mitigate the emotional, mental, and practical challenges of caregiving, ultimately benefiting carers, care recipients, and the broader healthcare system. To ensure sustained participation, accessible and convenient modes of interaction need to be considered. Future research should focus on refining these programs to enhance their effectiveness and accessibility, ensuring carers receive the comprehensive support they deserve.
