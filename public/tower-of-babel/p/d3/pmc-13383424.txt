Porphyromonas gingivalis outer membrane vesicles drive neuroinflammation via TGM2-mediated mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes
Authors: Pengye Zhang, Qingyu Zhu, Kairun Zhang, Shengcai Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPorphyromonas gingivalis (P.g), a key periodontal pathogen, is implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is a common event happened in neurodegenerative diseases, mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membranes (MAMs) play a potential role in AD pathology. This study aimed to investigate the impact of P.g derived outer membrane vesicles (P.g-OMVs) on neuroinflammation in the onset and progression of AD, and to elucidate the underlying mechanism.MethodsEight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice received bilateral gingival injections of P.g-OMVs (4 × 10⁸ particles) 3 times weekly for 8 weeks to assess cognitive impairment and neuroinflammation. RNA-seq identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and enriched pathway in brain tissues. In vitro, HT22 cells were treated with P.g-OMVs (5 µg/mL), and the expressions of inflammatory cytokines, transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) and ferroptosis markers were quantified. MAM formation and mitochondrial function regulated by TGM2, including mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation and membrane potential, were assessed, which further elucidated after knocking down TGM2 by siRNA.ResultsP.g-OMVs induced significant cognitive impairment and​ neurodegeneration in mice, evidenced by escape latency in the MWM (p < 0.001) and accumulation of amyloid β plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau. RNA-seq highlighted DEGs enriched in mitochondrial pathways, notably targeting TGM2. In vitro, P.g-OMVs triggered inflammation evidenced by increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-17, etc.) and reduced levels of IL-10 (p < 0.05), and led to the upregulation of TGM2 (p < 0.05). Crucially, co-localization of mitochondria and ER suggested that P.g-OMVs promoted MAM formation in neurons, accompanied by ​aberrant mitochondrial Ca2+ levels​ and membrane potential. Additionally, altered expressions of ferroptotic markers were observed, including increased level of ACSL4 (p < 0.01) and decreased levels of GPX4 and xCT (p < 0.01). TGM2 knockdown reversed the inflammation, ferroptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction and MAM formation.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that P.g-OMVs drive​ neuroinflammation and neuronal ferroptosis through a MAMs-associated mechanism. TGM2 acts as a key mediator, promoting MAM formation and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings highlight TGM2 represents a potential therapeutic target for neuroinflammatory conditions linked to oral pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08458-5.Keywords: Porphyromonas gingivalis, Outer membrane vesicle, Neuroinflammation, Mitochondria, TGM2

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases, is characterized by progressive cognitive impairment and functional impact [1], bringing economic and social burden to millions of people around the world [2, 3]. The accumulation of amyloid β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau (pTau) are clear evidence for the diagnosis of AD [4]. While these are canonical hallmarks, a growing body of findings suggest that upstream events, including altered cellular energy homeostasis and inflammation [5], occur at the preclinical stage and may drive disease progression. Recent clinical and epidemiological studies have established a compelling link between periodontitis and an elevated risk of AD [6–8]. This association is hypothesized to be mediated by the invasion of pathogens and their toxic molecules and persistent systemic inflammation [6]. Importantly, the evidence of subgingival microbiome and gingival crevicular fluid metabolome [9] suggested that periodontal microbial imbalance may play a role in the onset and progression of AD.

Porphyromonas gingivalis (P.g), a major periodontal pathogen [10], is suspected to be associated with neuroinflammation and AD’s pathology [11], with its DNA and toxic proteins, including gingipains and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), detected in the brains of AD patients [12, 13], raising the possibility of its direct involvement in neurodegenerative processes. However, further studies are needed to identify the exact virulence factors mediating these processes. Recent studies have pointed out that P.g-derived outer membrane vesicles (P.g-OMVs) probably act as the carrier of toxic factors that transmit the focal infection to the central nervous system [14, 15]. Crucially, as nano-sized lipid bilayer, OMVs possess the unique capacity to bypass or weaken the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) through pathways such as the disruption of tight junction proteins or endocytosis [16–18]. This is supported by the system inflammatory response and cognitive impairment after injection of P.g-OMVs [19–21]. However, the mechanism by which P.g-OMVs induce neuroinflammation is unclear and necessitates further investigation.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a common event happened in neurodegenerative diseases [22]. Specifically, the dynamic contact sites between the mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), known as mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs), are the key point in several cellular process, including Ca2+ signaling, lipid rebuilding and cell death [23]. MAMs are structurally maintained by functional proteins, notably those forming the mitochondrial Ca2+ channeling (MCC) complex [24]. This complex, consisting of GRP75, VDAC1 and IP3R3, is essential for efficiently regulating Ca2+ transfer from the ER to the mitochondria, which in turn influences mitochondrial energy production and cell fate [25]. Strikingly, extensive research has demonstrated that MAMs are upregulated in AD brains [26], leading to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and metabolic failure which is increasingly recognized as a driver of neuroinflammation and neuronal death [27]. Among the various pathways of regulated cell death, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death characterized by the accumulation of iron and lipid peroxides, has emerged as a critical mediator of neuronal loss in AD pathology [28]. Hence, it is necessary to comprehensively grasp the process whether P.g-OMVs induce neuronal loss through the formation of MAMs.

As a multifunctional enzyme, transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) catalyzes the cross-linking of substrates in a calcium-dependent way, as well as exerting its GTPase activity and protein kinase activity [29]. As a widely-studied member of the transglutaminase family, TGM2 is the predominant enzyme contributing to transglutaminase activity in the brain, especially enriched in neurons [30] with significant upregulation in AD brains [31, 32]. While aberrant expression of TGM2 has been linked to aggregation of toxic protein, neuron death and neuroinflammation, its impact on mitochondrial dynamics is particularly consequential [33, 34]. Critically, TGM2 has been identified to directly interact with GRP75, a component of MCC complex, acting as a key stabilizer of the formation of MAMs [35]. However, whether TGM2 plays a role in P.g-OMVs-induced neurotoxic pathology needs further investigation.

In this study, a novel mechanism linking periodontal pathology to neurodegeneration is demonstrated. After stimulation with P.g-OMVs in mouse models and neurons, we show that P.g-OMVs induce cognitive deficits and neuroinflammation by disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis. Mechanistically, P.g-OMVs upregulate TGM2 which acts as the essential mediator that promotes the aberrant formation of MAMs, leading to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and dysfunction. These findings suggest that P.g-OMVs trigger neuroinflammation via ferroptosis depending on TGM2-mediated MAM formation in neurons.

Methods

Bacteria culture and extraction of P.g-OMVs

Pure P.g of ATCC 33,277 strain was cultivated in BHI (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) broth in an anaerobic chamber supplemented with vitamin K (0.5 µg/mL), protohemin (5 µg/mL), yeast powder (1 mg/mL) and cysteine (0.5 mg/mL). After incubation at 37℃, the bacterial suspension reached an OD600 value of 0.5, indicating a concentration of about 1 × 109 CFU/mL. Then, P.g-OMVs isolation protocol referred to a previous study [36]. Specifically, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove bacteria. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes. Ultracentrifugation was performed at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C. After that, the samples were resuspended in PBS. Characteristics of P.g-OMVs were verified by nanoparticle-tracking analysis (NTA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The protein concentration and particle count of the isolated Pg OMVs were evaluated by the BCA Protein Assay Kit and NTA, respectively.

Animals model

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice provided by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions. All experimental equipment and procedures were examined and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Tenth People’s Hospital of Tongji University (No: SHDSYY-2022-3063). To study the effect of P.g-OMVs on neurodegeneration, 14 male C57BL/6J mice were used. After 1 week of adaptive feeding, the mice were divided randomly into two groups: Control group (n = 7) and P.g-OMVs group (n = 7). The sample size was determined using the resource equation method. Mice in P.g-OMVs group received a 5 − 0 silk ligature tied around their bilateral maxillary second molars for the entire experiment [13]. For infection, local injection of P.g-OMVs (4 × 108 particle per mouse) to bilateral palatal gingiva of the maxillary second molar was applied on the next day, 3 times a week for 8 weeks, while controls were injected with saline solution. To explore the altered pathways in the brain, 12 male C57BL/6J mice were used for RNA sequencing, divided into two groups (n = 6) as before. Mice received tail vein injections of 1 × 109 particles P.g-OMVs [37, 38] daily for 7 weeks, while the controls were injected with an equal volume of saline solution.

Following the establishment of the periodontitis model, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold physiological saline to remove blood. Brain tissues were then rapidly harvested. For RNA sequencing, a separate group was euthanized via an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), and fresh brain tissues were collected. All samples were immediately stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Behavioral tests

The open field test (OFT) and Morris Water Maze test (MWM) were conducted to assess anxiety-like behavior and memory functions, respectively. Mice were habituated to a behavioral testing room for at least 1 h before experimental testing, and behavioral tests were performed with the lights on. The behavioral apparatus was cleaned and wiped with 70% ethanol to remove odor clues left by the previous subject between trials.

For OFT, mice were released in the open-field center and allowed to explore for 10 min while being recorded by an infrared tracking system on the testing day. Edge time, center time, and total travel distance (50% arena area reference) were quantified. For MWM, animals were trained to locate a hidden escape platform (10 cm diameter) submerged 1.0 cm below the water surface in a circular pool (120 cm diameter). Training consisted of 4 trials per day for 5 consecutive days. Mice that failed to locate the platform within the 60-second limit were manually placed on it for 15 s. The platform position remained constant, while the starting position was rotated among four fixed points at the pool periphery. Following hidden platform training, a 60-second probe test was conducted with the platform removed to assess spatial memory. Data from animal experiments were analyzed by experimenters who were blinded to the experimental conditions.

RNA sequencing

After brain tissue collection, samples preserved in RNA stabilization reagent (Beyotime, China) were sent to Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing. Following outlier exclusion based on Pearson correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA), differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of Fold Change > 0.58 and P < 0.1.

Immunohistochemistry

The brain tissue was embedded in paraffin, and then sliced into 5-µm-thick sections. The sections were baked for 30 min, deparaffinized in Hisclear for 30 min, and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Tissue sections were placed in a humidified chamber and incubated with 20 µL of 0.125% trypsin at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by three 5-min washes with PBS. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections in 3% H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature (RT). To prevent nonspecific binding, sections were blocked with 5% BSA at RT for 2 h. Then, the sections were incubated with diluted primary antibodies (anti-IL-1β (1:200, Cell Signal,12242 S, United States), anti-IL-6 (1:200, Abclonal, A2222, China), anti-TGM2 (1:200, 145561, Proteintech, China), anti-GPX4 (1:200, A13309, Abclonal, China), anti-ACSL4 (1:200, A20414, Abclonal, China), anti-amyloid (1:200, ab32136, Abcam, China), anti-p-Tau (1:200, 12885, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After three 5-min PBS washes, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:200, A0216 or A0208; Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) and washed again with PBS. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB working solution for 2 min. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. The sections were placed on mounting plates, covered with neutral resin, and air-dried at RT before imaging.

Cell treatment and siRNA transfection

HT22 cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, MA, USA). To assess the effect of P.g-OMVs, cells were divided into Control and P.g-OMVs groups. HT22 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/mL. After achieving approximately 70–80% confluence, the cells were treated with P.g-OMVs (5 µg/mL) for 24 h. After that, samples were collected for corresponding experimental analysis.

For mechanistic studies, HT22 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/mL and cultured overnight to achieve 60–70% confluence. HT22 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and siTGM2/siNC (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To investigate the role of TGM2, cells were divided into three groups. (1) Control Group: Transfected with siNC; (2) P.g-OMVs Group: Transfected with siNC and treated with P.g-OMVs (5 µg/mL) for 24 h; (3) siTGM2 + P.g-OMVs Group: Transfected with siTGM2 and treated with P.g-OMVs (5 µg/mL) for 24 h. Knockdown efficiency was verified via Western blotting and qPCR prior to data collection. Samples were collected for corresponding experimental analysis. Cell viability assay

HT22 cells were placed into 96-well plate at a density of 50,000 cells/mL. Following overnight culture, cells were treated with P.g-OMVs (0, 1, 2, 5, 10 µg/mL) for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing CCK-8 (100 µL medium + 10 µL CCK-8) (BioSharp, China), and cells were incubated for another 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the data were analyzed to determine the optimal concentration for further studies.

OMVs staining

The protein concentration of P.g-OMVs was determined using a BCA assay. The vesicles were mixed with the dye working solution, PKH26(NW3216, Nonin Ltd, China), at a ratio based on protein mass. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and incubated for 10 min in the dark, then adding 10 mL of PBS. The vesicles were then re-isolated (using the standard extraction protocol mentioned before) to remove excess dye. Finally, the labeled vesicle pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of PBS. Cells were co-cultured with the labeled vesicles for 24 h. After removing the medium and washing with PBS, the cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with phalloidin (P9200, Solarbio, China). Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Western blotting

Protein samples were extracted from brain tissue in RIPA lysis buffer (300 µL, BioSharp, China). Protein concentrations of the harvested lysates were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Ruisi Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with BSA as the standard. Equal amounts of protein from each group were mixed with 5× loading buffer (BioSharp, China), heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and then separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The separated proteins were subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (1:10,000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, United States), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, Cell Signal,12242 S, United States), anti-IL-6 (1:1000, Abclonal, A2222, China), anti-IL-10 (1:1000, Abclonal, A2171, China), anti-IL-17 (1:1000, Abclonal, A10587, China), anti-TNF-α (1:1000, Abclonal, A11534, China), anti-TGM2 (1:1000, 145561, Proteintech, China), anti-ACSL4 (1:2000, Abclonal, A20414, China), anti-xCT (1:1000, Boster, BM5318, China), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, Boster, BM5231, China). The membranes were then washed three times, each lasting for 10 min, with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Specific protein bands were visualized using the Tanon 5200 Multi Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon). Band intensities were quantified by measuring gray values using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from brain tissues using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China). The quality and concentration of RNA were verified using a NanoDrop 2000, ensuring an A260/A280 ratio>1.8. Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Cat# RR036A, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR) was performed with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expressions of mRNA were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method, normalized to GAPDH. The sequences of forward and reverse primers are listed in Table 2.

Immunofluorescence staining

In vivo study, the maxillae were decalcified and embedded in paraffin, and then sliced into 5-µm-thick sections. In vitro study, after cell modeling, the cells were seeded on coverslips evenly and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. For IF staining, after antigen retrieval, blocking (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States), and permeabilization, the slices were incubated with anti-TOM20 (5 µg/mL, sc-17764, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Germany), anti-TGM2 (1:200, 145561, Proteintech, China), anti-NeuN (1:200, 94403 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) at 4℃ overnight. Live cell staining

HT22 cells were placed into Confocal Dish at a density of 50,000 cells/well. After modeling, cells were stained with MitoTracker Red (C1035, Beyotime, China) in combination with ER-Tracker Green (C1042S, Beyotime, China) or Fluo-4 AM (S1060, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Nuclei were labeled with Hoechst (C1022, Beyotime, China). Samples were then visualized under a confocal microscope. A colocalization analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) to quantify the overlap between the mitochondrial and ER probes (PCC, Colocalization Finder plugin, ImageJ).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (733) software. Experimental data repeated at least three times are presented as the mean ± standard mean error (SEM). Statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired t-test between two groups and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparison test among more than two groups, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Peripheral exposure to P.g-OMVs induces cognitive impairment and AD-like pathology in mice

P.g-OMVs were separated from P.g supernatant, whose morphology, particle size and antigenicity were respectively characterized by scanning electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis and ELISA [17, 20]. To verify the pathogenic potential of P.g-OMVs, we established a mice ligature-induced periodontitis model with P.g-OMVs injection at the second molar for 8 weeks, then behavioral tests were conducted to evaluate the outcomes subsequently (Fig. 1A). The OFT was performed to assess anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity. It was found that exposure to P.g-OMVs did not alter general activity and anxiety levels (Fig. 1B), as the treated mice displayed no significant differences in edge time, center time, distance and velocity compared to those of the control group (Fig. 1D-G). In contrast, performance in the MWM test revealed deficits in spatial learning and memory in the P.g-OMVs treated mice (Fig. 1C). While the latency to the platform was comparable between groups during the initial training phase (days 1–3), the experimental group exhibited a significantly delayed escape by day 5 (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, in the probe trial, P.g-OMVs treated mice performed fewer platform crossings and spent less time in the target quadrant compared to the control (Fig. 1I, J), suggesting that exposure to P.g-OMVs impaired memory of mice. Consistent with the results of these behavioral tests, IHC analysis demonstrated elevated levels of Aβ and pTau, the pathological hallmarks of AD pathology, in the brains of the experimental mice (Fig. 1K). Collectively, these results indicated that P.g-OMVs caused cognitive impairment and AD pathology in mice.

P.g-OMVs induce neuroinflammation and upregulation of TGM2 in mice brain

Given that P.g is known to exacerbate systemic inflammation through the invasion of toxic factors and the release of inflammatory cytokine, we first assessed the inflammation in the hippocampus. Treatment with P.g-OMVs resulted in a significant upregulation of Interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 in brain (Fig. 2A). Next, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on brain tissues from mice subjected to tail vein injection of P.g-OMVs to explore the upstream molecular mechanism. Differential expression analysis identified a set of significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Crucially, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis highlighted that these DEGs were enriched in mitochondrial and ER-related pathways, such as regulation of mitochondrion and protein refolding (Fig. 2B). Among the DEGs enriched in the mitochondrial pathways, Tgm2 exhibited the most pronounced upregulation pattern (Fig. 2C, D), which was consistent with the results of IHC (Fig. 2F). The protein-protein interaction network of TGM2 was constructed relying on STRING database, indicating the potential interactions with Hspa9 (GRP75), Kdr, and others (Fig. 2E). Finally, the colocalization IF staining confirmed that TGM2 was specifically upregulated in neurons (Fig. 2G), indicating TGM2 as a potential molecule involved in P.g-OMVs induced pathology.

P.g-OMVs are internalized into neurons and trigger the inflammatory response in neurons

To investigate the molecular mechanisms involved in neuronal inflammation, we utilized HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells and stimulated them with P.g-OMVs. Firstly, the CCK-8 assays revealed that P.g-OMVs induced cytotoxicity in a concentration-dependent manner, with no significant difference between 5 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL concentrations for 24 h (Fig. 3A). Therefore, 5 µg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments in vitro. While P.g-OMVs were reported to cross the BBB, their direct cellular uptake by neurons requires verification. By incubating HT22 cells with PKH26-labeled P.g-OMVs, we observed distinct intracellular accumulation of the vesicles (Fig. 3B), confirming that P.g-OMVs successfully entered into the neurons. At the protein level, P.g-OMVs stimulation led to a significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-17, and TNF-α, accompanied by a marked downregulation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 compared to the control group (Fig. 3C, D). Similarly, the results of qPCR presented elevated mRNA expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines and suppressed IL-10 expression (Fig. 3E). These findings suggested that P.g-OMVs were taken up by neurons and triggered inflammation response.

P.g-OMVs elevate the expression of TGM2 and the formation of MAMs in neurons

Based on the previous transcriptomic findings of TGM2, we validated its regulation in HT22 cells. The results of WB and qPCR demonstrated that P.g-OMVs stimulation notably increased the expression of TGM2 (Fig. 4A-C). In order to explore the subcellular localization of TGM2, we performed co-staining with TOM20, a mitochondrial marker, which revealed that TGM2 was predominantly enriched in mitochondria (Fig. 4D). For evaluating the localization and function of TGM2, we visualized MAMs using Mito-tracker Green and ER-tracker Red by fluorescence confocal microscopy. The results showed that P.g-OMVs treatment led to extensive co-localization of ER and mitochondria compared to the control group (Fig. 4E-F), suggesting the promotion of aberrant MAM formation in neurons. Interestingly, compared to the control groups, a marked elevation in mitochondrial Ca2+ concentration in neurons treated with P.g-OMVs was observed, indicating that P.g-OMVs probably facilitate mitochondrial Ca2+ influx (Fig. 4G). Given that the MCC complex, an essential part of MAMs, is responsible for Ca2+ transportation, we examined the expression levels of MCC complex further. The mRNA expressions of Vdac1, Hspa9 (GRP75) and Itpr3 (IP3R3) were increased in the P.g-OMVs groups (Fig. 4H). Overall, these results provided evidence that P.g-OMVs promoted the expression of TGM2 and MAM formation, accompanied by the elevated mitochondria Ca2+ accumulation.

P.g-OMVs induce mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal ferroptosis

Following the observation of Ca2+ overload in mitochondria, we examined whether P.g-OMVs affected mitochondrial function and induced cell death in neurons. The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was quantified using the JC-1 assay, which revealed a significant decrease in ΔΨm in the P.g-OMVs group (Fig. 5A), confirming severe mitochondrial dysfunction. Given the close association between mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation, we investigated whether neuronal ferroptosis, a recently recognized form of cell death that plays a critical role in AD pathology, would occur upon exposure to P.g-OMVs. The results of WB demonstrated a significant reduction of GPX4 and xCT, while the protein expression of ACSL4 was increased in P.g-OMVs group (Fig. 5B, C). Consistent with those, P.g-OMVs upregulated the mRNA levels of ferroptosis-related genes, including Ptgs2 and Ncoa4 (Fig. 5D). A similar trend was observed in the expression of GPX4 and ACSL4 in the hippocampus by IHC in mice brain in vivo (Fig. 5E), indicating P.g-OMVs triggered ferroptosis in mice brains.

TGM2 is indispensable for P.g-OMVs-induced aberrant MAM formation and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload

To determine the role of TGM2 in mediating mitochondria–ER contacts in neurons exposed to P.g-OMVs, we used small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting TGM2 (siTGM2) in HT22 cells, followed by P.g-OMVs stimulation. The knockdown efficiency was validated by the significant reduction of TGM2 at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 6A-C). The mRNA levels of MCC complex, including Hspa9, Vdac1 and Itpr3, were significantly suppressed in the siTGM2 group (Fig. 6D). Additionally, inhibition of TGM2 effectively reversed the excessive formation of MAMs induced by P.g-OMVs, as evidenced by the notable reduction in colocalization of mitochondria and ER (Fig. 6E). Functionally, the mitochondrial Ca2+ overload was largely abrogated in siTGM2 neurons (Fig. 6F). These results confirmed that TGM2 played a crucial role in MAM formation and mitochondrial Ca2+ regulation in response to P.g-OMVs.

TGM2 knockdown alleviates mitochondrial damage, neuron ferroptosis and neuroinflammation

Experiments were designed to identify whether inhibiting TGM2 could mitigate the downstream pathological phenotypes triggered by P.g-OMVs. As expected, TGM2 knockdown rescued mitochondrial dysfunction, which was impaired by P.g-OMVs, as shown by increased ΔΨm in siTGM2-treated neurons (Fig. 7A). Importantly, WB demonstrated that siTGM2 treatment resulted in the restoration of GPX4 and xCT, alongside a suppression of ACSL4 in neurons (Fig. 7B, C). Consistently, the altered mRNA expressions of Gpx4, Ptgs2 and Ncoa4 induced by P.g-OMVs were reversed by siTGM2 in neurons (Fig. 7D), indicating that TGM2 deficiency mitigated the ferroptotic phenotype. Finally, TGM2 knockdown attenuated the neuroinflammatory response, characterized by the decreased protein and mRNA expressions of IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-17, as well as the increased expression of IL-10 (Fig. 7E-G). Overall, these findings indicated that TGM2 played a vital role in P.g-OMVs-induced neuroinflammation, neuronal ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that P.g-OMVs induce AD-like pathology through a TGM2-dependent mechanism. Mechanistically, we revealed that P.g-OMVs could promote the aberrant formation of MAMs depending on TGM2, thus leading to increased mitochondrial Ca2+ flux, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal ferroptosis (Fig. 7H). Hence, our findings provide a novel pathologic understanding of the role of TGM2 in P.g-OMVs-induced neuroinflammation.

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by bacterial infection, is associated with the higher risk of developing AD [39, 40]. Significantly elevated levels of both peripheral and system inflammatory mediators [41] such as IL-1β [42] attract attention to the role of neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of periodontitis and AD [43]. Supporting a pathogen-driven inflammatory response, previous studies have typically verified that oral administration of P.g led to neuroinflammation, and the formation of Aβ with translocation of P.g to the hippocampi of mice [44]. Meanwhile, new findings have shed light on the P.g-OMVs with enriched virulence, great stability [45] and high efficiency of invasion and diffusion [15]. To better simulate the invasion route of P.g-OMVs, we established a ligature-induced periodontitis model with the local gingival injection of P.g-OMVs. The AD-like phenotypes and nervous inflammatory response were observed in experimental group (Figs. 1 and 2), in concordance with the previous studies reporting that oral gavage of P.g-OMVs penetrated the BBB and induced neuroinflammation and AD-like phenotypes [17]. The comparable results were found in vitro indicating that P.g-OMVs was successfully internalized into neurons and subsequently promoted neuroinflammation. Beyond the inflammatory response itself, there is a complex mechanistic link between neuroinflammation and neuronal cell loss which is also considered as one of hallmarks of neurodegenerative disease [5]. Among the various forms of cell death, ferroptosis, a non-cell autonomous process characterized by ROS production, iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [28], has emerged as a key driver of neurodegeneration [46]. Mechanistically, loss of ferroportin results in the iron dyshomeostasis and ferroptosis featured by suppressed GPX4, culminating in Aβ deposition and cognitive impairment [47]. Indeed, Gpx4 deletion in neurons exhibited AD-like phenotypes, emphasizing the role of ferroptosis in the brain [48–50]. Building on our previous findings that P.g triggers ferroptosis in peripheral organs [51, 52], ferroptosis was also identified in the brain. In our study, IHC analysis revealed the upregulated levels of ACSL4 and downregulated GPX4 in the hippocampus after P.g-OMVs treatment (Fig. 5), paralleling the shared alteration of ferroptosis-related markers in vitro studies. Collectively, these data suggest that P.g-OMVs elicit neuronal ferroptosis, providing a plausible mechanism that underlie the observed inflammatory pathology and memory deficits.

TGM2 has emerged as a multifunctional regulator in the nervous system, and its abnormal upregulation is increasingly recognized as a general event in neurodegenerative diseases [53, 54], including AD and Huntington’s disease. Recent gene-based exome-wide association studies (ExWAS) have further identified Tgm2 as a significant risk gene for AD and a potential drug target [55]. In this study, TGM2 was selected as the core target based on our preliminary transcriptomic findings, which showed significant upregulation of Tgm2 in response to P.g-OMVs treatment. This was further supported by IHC, revealing increased TGM2 expression in the hippocampus of treated mice, consistent with the results in vitro studies by WB, qPCR and IF. However, the mechanism by which P.g-OMVs upregulate TGM2 remains to be fully elucidated, but several pathways may be involved. P.g-OMVs contain a variety of virulence factors that can activate toll-like receptor 2 and 4 (TLR2/4), subsequently activating the host cell and triggering downstream inflammatory cascades [56]. Given that the Tgm2 promoter contains transcriptional regulators related to inflammation [57], stress response and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by P.g-OMVs may promote TGM2 expression. By knocking down TGM2 in HT22 cells, the inflammatory response, neuronal ferroptosis and mitochondrial damage triggered by P.g-OMVs were rescued, suggesting that TGM2 is not merely a response factor of cellular stress, but a primary signaling hub through which bacterial invasion initiate a neurotoxic cascade. To elucidate the intracellular mechanism, STRING database analysis highlighted a TGM2-GRP75 interaction, implying a potential role for aberrant ER-mitochondria contacts. MAMs, the dynamic and multifunctional contact sites between mitochondria and ER, are responsible for lipid metabolism, Ca2+ signaling [58] and mitochondrial dynamics [59]. Aberrant formation of MAMs is reported as a feature of AD brains and Aβ-exposed neurons [60, 61]. Our findings demonstrated that P.g-OMVs induced excessive formation of MAMs which facilitated upregulated Ca2+ flux from the ER to mitochondria, thereby driving mitochondrial dysfunction. This pathway is supported by evidence that Urolithin A can prevent diabetes-associated AD pathogenesis by reducing TGM2-dependent MAM formation and maintaining mitochondrial calcium and ROS homeostasis [62]. Consistently, we confirmed that inhibiting TGM2 reversed the increased formation of MAMs induced by P.g-OMVs, together with the altered mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation and mitochondrial membrane potential. Given that MAMs have been identified as primary sites of phospholipids peroxidation transmission in cells undergoing ferroptosis [63], our results suggested that TGM2 probably drove neuronal ferroptosis by promoting the formation of MAMs and disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis. Overall, these findings support TGM2 as a valid target for the preventing excessive MAM formation and neuroinflammation in AD pathology.

Despite these insights, several limitations warrant further investigation. Firstly, MAM formation was confirmed via confocal colocalization, while techniques such as transmission electron microscopy and proximity ligation assays would provide more detailed evidence of the contact sites. Secondly, the transport mechanisms of P.g-OMVs from the periphery to the brain remain unclear. Identifying the specific surface receptors mediated cellular internalization and the upstream signaling pathways of the regulation of TGM2 will be critical for a comprehensive understanding of their neurotoxic potential. Finally, AD pathology involves a complex interplay of diverse cells. While this study mainly focused on neurons, the contribution of microglia and astrocytes to the TGM2-mediated inflammation need future exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, our study reveals a novel pathogenic pathway from the oral cavity to the neuron. We demonstrate that P.g-OMVs drive neurodegeneration by upregulating TGM2, which promotes the formation of MAMs and mitochondrial calcium overload, ultimately resulting in neuronal ferroptosis and neuroinflammation. These findings highlight TGM2 as a promising therapeutic target for periodontitis-associated AD.
