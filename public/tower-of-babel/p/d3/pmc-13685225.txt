Pediatric stroke-like episodes temporally associated with pollen exposure in GJB1-related CMTX1: an expanded family case report with serial MRI and DTI follow-up
Authors: Teng Yao, Yuanrui Li, Yajing Cheng, Xiangyu Chen, Ganqin Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundX-linked Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1 (CMTX1), caused by pathogenic variants in GJB1 encoding connexin 32, is primarily an inherited peripheral neuropathy. A minority of patients develop transient central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction with reversible white matter lesions, which may mimic acute paediatric stroke. Triggers such as fever, infection and exercise have been reported, but allergy-related exposure has rarely been discussed and causal mechanisms remain uncertain.Case presentationA 9-year-old boy developed acute left-sided weakness, dysarthria and central facial-tongue paresis shortly after pollen exposure. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed symmetrical diffusion-restricted lesions involving the corpus callosum and bilateral centrum semiovale, with high signal on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), corresponding low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values and hyperintensity on T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), magnetic resonance venography (MRV), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies, autoimmune encephalitis antibodies and oligoclonal bands were unremarkable. Neurological deficits resolved within 12 h. Nerve conduction studies showed mild mixed sensorimotor polyneuropathy with demyelinating features and partial axonal involvement. Serial MRI showed progressive resolution, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) demonstrated no obvious displacement, interruption or reduction of major white matter tracts. Whole-exome sequencing and Sanger validation identified a hemizygous GJB1 c.623 A > G (p.Glu208Gly, p.E208G) variant in the proband; his mother was heterozygous and his brother was hemizygous. No recurrent stroke-like episode occurred during 2 years of follow-up, although reduced tendon reflexes persisted.ConclusionsIn children with acute stroke-like episodes and reversible white matter lesions, the coexistence of pes cavus, reduced tendon reflexes or abnormal nerve conduction should prompt evaluation for GJB1-related CMTX1. Pollen exposure was temporally associated with the episode in this patient, but an allergic mechanism remains unproven because allergy-specific biomarkers and cytokines were not obtained during the acute phase.

Background

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT) is the most common inherited motor and sensory neuropathy. It may follow autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive or X-linked inheritance patterns [1, 2]. X-linked Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1 (CMTX1) is caused by pathogenic variants in GJB1, which encodes connexin 32 (Cx32), and typically manifests as distal limb weakness, muscle atrophy, sensory loss and reduced or absent tendon reflexes [3, 4].

CMTX1 is not exclusively a peripheral nerve disorder. Transient CNS manifestations, including dysarthria, hemiparesis, ataxia, altered consciousness and reversible white matter lesions, have been described in a subset of patients, particularly children and young adults [5–8]. These episodes may be misdiagnosed as acute ischaemic stroke, encephalitis, acute demyelinating disease [9] or mild encephalopathy with reversible splenial lesion (MERS). Although the GJB1 p.E208G variant has been reported previously [10], paediatric presentations in which acute CNS symptoms precede clear recognition of inherited neuropathy remain clinically important.

The novelty of the present report does not lie in identifying p.E208G as a novel variant. Instead, this expanded case report focuses on the paediatric stroke-mimic presentation, the temporal association with pollen exposure, serial MRI and DTI follow-up, electrophysiological diagnostic clues, and a cautious discussion of overlap with MERS and possible inflammatory or allergic precipitants.

Case presentation

Pre-event history and acute onset

The proband was a 9-year-old boy admitted in August 2023 because of sudden left-sided limb weakness and dysarthria for 3 h. He had a history of pollen-associated symptoms, including a previous episode of transient aphasia after pollen exposure that resolved spontaneously after approximately 20 min without motor impairment. Approximately 3 h before admission, he was exposed to pollen. Within about 5 min, he developed shortness of breath, sweating and fatigue. Approximately 10 min later, he developed left-sided limb weakness, slurred speech and rightward deviation of the mouth. There was no known history of cerebrovascular risk factors.

Emergency examination

On admission, the patient was alert and oriented. Higher cortical function was preserved. Bilateral forehead wrinkles were symmetrical. The left eyelid showed slight ptosis, the left nasolabial fold was flattened, the mouth angle deviated rightward and tongue protrusion deviated rightward. Muscle strength was grade IV on the left and grade V on the right. Tendon reflexes were reduced bilaterally. There was no obvious lower-limb muscle atrophy, but bilateral pes cavus was present. General examination showed pharyngeal congestion and grade I enlargement of both tonsils.

Initial laboratory and neuroimaging findings

Emergency biochemical tests, including liver function, renal function, cardiac enzymes, electrolytes and creatine kinase, were within normal limits. Cranial MRI showed symmetrical lesions involving the bilateral centrum semiovale and corpus callosum. The lesions were hyperintense on DWI, hypointense on ADC maps and hyperintense on T2 FLAIR, consistent with diffusion restriction. MRA and MRV showed no arterial or venous abnormality. The distribution did not conform to a typical arterial vascular territory, supporting reversible white matter involvement rather than territorial infarction.

The available acute-phase record did not include allergy-specific biomarkers, including serum immunoglobulin E (IgE), eosinophil count, histamine, interleukin-6 or other cytokines. Epinephrine administration was not documented, and the use of antihistamines was unclear. Therefore, pollen exposure is interpreted as a temporally associated event rather than a proven allergic trigger.

Treatment, CSF studies and early clinical course

Central demyelination involving the corpus callosum and upper respiratory tract infection were initially considered. The patient received mannitol, corticosteroids and symptomatic treatment. Five hours later, left limb strength had largely returned to normal. Twelve hours after onset, neurological symptoms had completely resolved, including recovery from central facial and tongue paresis, and pharyngeal congestion and tonsillar swelling had improved. Lumbar puncture showed an opening pressure of 110 mmH2O. Routine and biochemical CSF tests were normal; autoimmune encephalitis antibodies were negative and CSF oligoclonal bands were absent (Tables 1 and 2).

Key electrophysiological abnormalities in the proband

Electrophysiology

Nerve conduction studies showed reduced motor conduction velocities in the median, ulnar, common peroneal and tibial nerves. The left median motor response amplitude was reduced. Sensory conduction velocities were reduced in the median and ulnar nerves, with reduced sensory amplitudes in the right median and bilateral ulnar nerves and prolonged sensory latencies. Sensory conduction in the bilateral superficial peroneal and medial plantar nerves was within the available reference range. F-wave occurrence was preserved in the median, ulnar and tibial nerves, but F-wave latency was prolonged in the bilateral median nerves.

Overall, the electrophysiological pattern suggested a mild mixed sensorimotor polyneuropathy with demyelinating features and partial axonal involvement. In the clinical context of pes cavus and reduced tendon reflexes, these findings provided an important clue to an underlying inherited neuropathy despite the predominance of acute CNS symptoms.

Follow-up MRI and 2-year follow-up

Follow-up MRI 5 days after onset showed residual halo-like abnormal T1/T2 signal in the bilateral centrum semiovale, but the extent of DWI hyperintensity had decreased compared with the initial scan. Contrast-enhanced imaging showed no abnormal meningeal enhancement. DTI showed no obvious displacement, interruption or reduction of white matter tracts in the lesion areas.

At 3 weeks after onset, DWI showed no significant abnormality. The FLAIR lesion in the corpus callosum had resolved, and lesions in the bilateral centrum semiovale had decreased in size and signal intensity. No definite caudate involvement was confirmed on the available images; therefore, the radiological description focuses on the clearly demonstrated corpus callosum and bilateral centrum semiovale abnormalities.

At 2-year follow-up, the proband had experienced no further stroke-like episodes. Limb strength was normal bilaterally. Tendon reflexes remained reduced, but no obvious distal muscle atrophy, gait deterioration or new functional limitation was observed. This follow-up suggests that the acute CNS event was monophasic during the observed period, whereas subtle peripheral neuropathic signs persisted.

Family history and genetic analysis

The proband, his mother, maternal grandmother and elder brother had pes cavus according to clinical observation or family report. The proband, his maternal uncle and elder brother had foot inversion. The maternal uncle was reported to have poor physical endurance, unstable gait and a tendency to fall, but he did not seek medical evaluation and his children were reportedly unaffected. The maternal uncle and grandmother were not formally examined; their symptoms were reported by family members. The mother declined further electrophysiological testing because of health concerns. The elder brother had experienced acute numbness and weakness of both upper limbs at the age of 8 years after strenuous exercise and heavy sweating, with subsequent lower-limb involvement and transient slurred speech lasting approximately 2 h. His symptoms improved within 1 day, and follow-up MRI 1 month later reportedly showed resolution of the original lesion. However, only paper medical records were available, and detailed laboratory, electrophysiological and imaging data could not be retrieved.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral venous blood samples from the proband and first-degree relatives. Whole-exome sequencing was performed, and candidate variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Variants were interpreted according to the standards of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). GJB1 variant analysis was performed with reference to NM_000166.6. The proband carried a hemizygous GJB1 exon 2 variant, c.623 A > G (p.Glu208Gly, p.E208G). The father did not carry this variant, the mother was heterozygous, and the elder brother was hemizygous. This segregation pattern supported a diagnosis of GJB1-related CMTX1. Sanger sequencing validation is shown in Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Discussion and conclusions

Clinical significance and novelty

This case illustrates that GJB1-related CMTX1 may initially present as a paediatric stroke mimic before progressive peripheral neuropathy becomes clinically prominent. The p.E208G variant itself has been reported previously [10]. The contribution of the present report is the integration of a paediatric CNS-first presentation, temporal association with pollen exposure, serial MRI/DTI follow-up, electrophysiological evidence of inherited neuropathy and family segregation data.

CMTX1 as a paediatric stroke mimic

The acute onset of hemiparesis, dysarthria and central facial-tongue paresis raised concern for acute stroke. However, several features argued against typical arterial infarction: vascular imaging was negative, the lesions were bilateral and symmetrical, the distribution did not follow a vascular territory, clinical recovery occurred within 12 h and MRI abnormalities improved dynamically. CMTX1 should therefore be considered in children or adolescents with stroke-like episodes when subtle peripheral neuropathy signs, such as pes cavus or reduced tendon reflexes, are present.

Imaging interpretation and overlap with MERS

The MRI pattern overlapped partly with MERS or cytotoxic lesions of the corpus callosum, because the lesions involved the corpus callosum and showed diffusion restriction with reversibility [11, 12]. However, the additional involvement of bilateral centrum semiovale, the presence of pes cavus and reduced tendon reflexes, abnormal nerve conduction and GJB1 segregation supported CMTX1-related CNS involvement rather than isolated MERS. DTI showing preserved tract continuity further suggests transient functional or intramyelinic injury rather than permanent structural destruction [13, 14].

Possible pollen or inflammatory trigger

The temporal relationship between pollen exposure and symptom onset raises the possibility that allergic or inflammatory stress contributed to the CNS episode. Previous reports have described CMTX1 episodes after fever, infection, exercise, hyperventilation or high-altitude exposure [5–8]. These triggers may transiently disturb ion-water homeostasis or gap-junction-mediated metabolic coupling in CNS myelin and glial networks [15]. However, in this patient, the allergic association remains speculative because IgE, eosinophils, histamine, interleukin-6 and other cytokines were not tested during the acute phase, and antihistamine or epinephrine use was not clearly documented. The episode should therefore be described as temporally associated with pollen exposure, not as definitively allergy-triggered.

Electrophysiology and family assessment

Electrophysiological testing was diagnostically important because acute CNS symptoms dominated the presentation. The combination of slowed motor and sensory conduction, pes cavus and reduced tendon reflexes indicated an inherited sensorimotor neuropathy [16, 17]. Family history further supported X-linked inheritance, but assessment of relatives was incomplete. Some family members were described only by family report, the mother declined nerve conduction testing, and the brother’s earlier clinical records could not be fully retrieved. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the family history and intrafamilial phenotype.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, the relationship between pollen exposure and CNS dysfunction is temporal and unproven. Acute-phase allergy-related biomarkers and cytokines were not available. Second, the elder brother’s original MRI, EMG and laboratory data were not traceable, limiting objective comparison between affected siblings. Third, the maternal uncle and grandmother were not formally examined; their symptoms were based on family report. Fourth, the mother declined electrophysiological testing. Fifth, functional validation of the GJB1 p.E208G variant was not performed. Sixth, although 2-year follow-up showed no recurrence of stroke-like episodes, longer follow-up is needed to evaluate progression of peripheral neuropathy. Finally, this is a single-family report and cannot estimate the frequency of CNS episodes, the recurrence risk or the causal role of environmental triggers.

Conclusions

GJB1-related CMTX1 should be considered in paediatric patients with acute stroke-like episodes and reversible white matter lesions, particularly when pes cavus, reduced tendon reflexes or abnormal nerve conduction are present. Serial MRI/DTI and electrophysiology can help distinguish CMTX1-related CNS involvement from arterial stroke, encephalitis or isolated MERS. Pollen exposure was temporally associated with the episode in this patient, but the mechanism remains uncertain and should be interpreted cautiously.
