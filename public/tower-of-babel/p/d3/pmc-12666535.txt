Measuring commercial cannabis availability: findings from a multi-state surveillance study in the US
Authors: Nigar Nargis, Samuel Asare, J Lee Westmaas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCannabis legalization enables greater access to commercial cannabis among adults. Little is known about the extent to which demand for cannabis is met by licit or illicit markets among states with medical-only or recreational cannabis laws, or no legalization.MethodsAnnual sales values of medical and recreational tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), illicit THC, hemp-derived cannabidiol (CBD) and hemp-derived THC were estimated for 2024 in 12 US states by combining and triangulating data from national- and state-level public sources, Euromonitor's Passport database, in-store audits (n = 142), retailer interviews (n = 78), and expert interviews (n = 10) of cannabis industry stakeholders. Introduction

Cannabis use in the United States (US) has reached historically high levels, with 15.1% of individuals aged 12 years and older reporting use in 2022—more than double the prevalence (7.6%) in 2013.1 Despite its continued classification as illegal under federal law, many US states have decriminalized cannabis-related activities such as possession, sharing, cultivation, and use.

In 40 states, three territories and the District of Columbia, medical cannabis laws (MCLs) have allowed medical professionals to prescribe cannabis to patients with qualifying medical conditions.2 An MCL endows states with regulatory oversight of patient registration, qualifying diseases and symptoms, product safety, labeling and packaging, dispensary and other distribution sites, and home cultivation.3

Among the jurisdictions with an MCL, 24 states with the three territories and the District of Columbia have also passed recreational cannabis laws (RCLs) that allow adults 21 years and older to buy, grow, possess, share, and use cannabis for non-medical purposes.4 Overall, cannabis legalization permits the establishment of a formal cannabis industry involving growers, processors, suppliers and retail sellers enabling legal access to cannabis.5

Cannabis contains a class of chemical compounds known as cannabinoids. Its consumption is primarily driven by psychoactive effects and other physiological impacts across multiple bodily systems. Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) is the principal psychoactive cannabinoid, while cannabidiol (CBD), another widely used compound, is generally considered non-psychoactive. Both THC and CBD have therapeutic potential, including anxiolytic, anti-inflammatory, pain-relieving, and other health-related effects commonly linked to medicinal use.6

State-level cannabis legalizations have been associated with greater availability of cannabis to adults, lower cannabis price, higher potency, and increased frequency of cannabis use among adults but not among youth.7 However, emerging evidence points to a range of adverse health outcomes, such as, elevated rates of emergency hospital visits,8 hospitalizations during pregnancy,9 excess utilization of healthcare services, calls to poison control centers, cannabis use disorder,10 and negative mental health effects associated with high-potency Δ9-THC products.11 The rapid expansion in the availability and consumption of cannabis and its derivatives presents significant public health challenges. These developments highlight the critical need for ongoing surveillance of the evolving commercial cannabis landscape.

Historically, estimating cannabis consumption through sales data was not feasible due to its illegal status. The advent of legal cannabis markets has created new opportunities to approximate population-level consumption using commercial sales data. However, comprehensive and comparable data remain scarce—especially in states where cannabis sales are not legalized and where residents may be exposed to illicit markets and/or engage in cross-border purchasing.

Few data sources have so far played a key role in providing information on national trends in prevalence and patterns of cannabis use, characteristics of individuals who use cannabis, the perceived availability of cannabis, and methods of consumption (eg, smoking, vaping). These include, among others, the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), the International Cannabis Policy Study (ICPS), the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), and the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS). However, the limited or complete lack of state-level data presents a significant barrier to monitoring cannabis markets at the state level—an essential step for informing evidence-based public health policies and guiding the development of effective state cannabis regulations. In this multi-state surveillance study, we measured state-specific commercial cannabis availability across 12 US states in 2024 at varying stages of cannabis legalization.

Given the substantial state-level variation in the structure and implementation of MCLs and RCLs, as well as differences in socio-demographic characteristics and broader policy environments, we hypothesized that the commercial availability of cannabis products would differ significantly by market segment (THC vs CBD) and legal status (legal vs illicit), even among states with similar legal classifications. We further posited that the ongoing trend toward legalization reflects a broader national demand for recreational cannabis and a corresponding effort by states to reduce criminal and administrative burdens associated with possession or use. Consequently, we expected that in states with only MCLs or no cannabis laws, cannabis demand for non-medical purposes will be met primarily through illicit channels. These channels may also include purchases of THC products with ambiguous legal status (eg, hemp-derived THC) or diversion from medical cannabis sources.

Methods

We assessed the market sizes in 12 US states in 2024 for four broad categories of cannabis products with THC, such as, legal medical THC, legal recreational THC, illegal THC, and hemp-derived THC, and hemp-derived CBD. Data collection

The American Cancer Society commissioned Euromonitor International to collect and collate data on cannabis market segments from five different sources:

Publicly available sources: These included databases of the state cannabis regulatory agencies for legal cannabis sales, tax rates and revenue data; the state medical cannabis administration for patient registry data; e-commerce sites for cannabis price data; the US Census Bureau for state-level population estimates; and media reports on state cannabis policies and market trends. State-level prevalence estimates of cannabis use in the past month among individuals aged 12 years or older were obtained from the NSDUH.12 These estimates, released annually by NSDUH, are based on a small area estimation method that combines state-level NSDUH data for two successive years with county and sub-county level census data from the state.

Passport: Passport is Euromonitor International's proprietary global market research database covering an array of consumer goods markets in more than 200 countries and jurisdictions. We obtained their US national-level data on annual retail values of medical, recreational, and illicit THC and hemp-derived CBD and THC markets.13 This database uses a combination of top-down and bottom-up approaches to estimate and triangulate national level market size. The top-down approach involves secondary research of industry annual reports, market studies, consumer insights, company financial data, state-level retail sales tax data and interviews of industry stakeholders. The bottom-up approach entails modeling state-level market size data from key states with availability of data on retail sales tax, number of retailers, share of those selling products of interest, annual average revenues per retailers, and shares of total sales that specific retail channels capture.

In-store audits: In-store shelf audits of 142 specialty smoke stores in the 12 states were conducted during February-March 2025. The objective was to collect data on annual sales revenue per store from hemp-derived CBD and THC products and create a product log to document the commercial cannabis market landscape. This ensured that sales data included the wide range of cannabis products currently available for purchase and helped the identification of splits between product categories for sale in retail channel, average per unit pricing, and product potency. It should be noted that specialty smoke stores constitute the largest retail channel for cannabis products besides e-commerce, convenience stores, liquor stores, and similar other outlets. The data on sales revenue obtained from specialty smoke stores were extrapolated to estimate total market sales revenue for hemp-derived THC and CBD based on relevant share of this channel in total market sales.

Hemp-derived CBD: Hemp-derived CBD national market data were obtained from the Euromonitor Passport national database for the US13 and used as the baseline to build market sizes by state following a top-down approach. The national-level CBD market size was transformed into state-proportions based on the state population share of the total US population. The state proportions were modified with additional inputs from secondary data on state regulatory environment for hemp-derived CBD products and validated through expert insights from industry stakeholders and retailer interviews. These steps are similar to those described in the top-down approach in Table 2 for hemp-derived THC.

Per capita market size: The size of each market segment was measured in current US dollars in 2024. To obtain per capita market sizes, the state-level sizes of market segments were divided by corresponding state-level population sizes available from the US Census Bureau.14

Results

State-level THC market size was substantial regardless of cannabis legalization status; however, states with no-CL had lower per capita THC market values than all MCL + RCL and MCL-only states except Utah (Figure 1). Across all states, products containing THC accounted for most (≥90%) of the combined cannabis market value of THC and CBD. MCL + RCL states exhibited the highest population-weighted average per capita combined cannabis market value ($348.90), followed by MCL-only ($279.85) and no-CL ($239.71) states.

MCL + RCL states

Recreational THC constituted the largest share of the commercial THC market across all four MCL + RCL states, followed by illicit THC (Figure 2A). Medical THC represented the third largest category, with relatively stable market shares ranging from 7% to 9% in Colorado, Massachusetts, and Illinois. In contrast, New Mexico exhibited a notably higher medical THC market share (16%). The hemp-derived THC market remains nascent, as reflected in its consistently low market share (1%-5%) across all states.

MCL-only states

In the absence of an RCL, illicit THC sales dominated the THC market across all four MCL-only states with wide variation from 48% in Oklahoma to 91% in New Hampshire (Figure 2B). Oklahoma is unique in having equal market shares of illicit THC (49%) and medical THC (49%). Its per capita medical THC sales value ($176) was also the highest among all states studied, resulting in total per capita cannabis and THC market values in Oklahoma comparable to those in MCL + RCL states. In contrast, New Hampshire is the only state among both MCL-only and MCL + RCL states where over 90% of the THC market was illicit.

The hemp-derived THC market share in MCL-only states was generally comparable to that in MCL + RCL states—ranging from 2% to 6% in New Hampshire, Oklahoma, and Utah—but was notably higher in Mississippi (17%).

No-CL states

In Idaho, Indiana, and Nebraska, illicit THC dominated the market, accounting for over 90% of total THC market value (Figure 2C). Hemp-derived THC comprised the remaining share, ranging from 3% in Idaho to 7% in Nebraska. Texas presented a contrasting profile: hemp-derived THC represented a substantial 34% of the overall THC market value, while illicit THC accounted for 65%, with medical THC making up the remaining 1%.

Discussion

By employing mixed methods to assess commercial cannabis availability and its key components, this study establishes a comprehensive framework for cannabis market surveillance. It is the first to quantify the magnitude and market share of various commercial cannabis segments. The findings show that state-level THC market size was substantial, accounting for ≥90% of the combined cannabis market value of THC and CBD, regardless of legalization status. The composition of THC markets—across legal recreational, medical, illicit, and hemp-derived segments—varied considerably, even among states with the same legalization status. However, in states with only MCLs or no cannabis laws, consumer demand was met primarily through illicit channels, purchase of hemp-derived THC products or diversion from medical cannabis sources, demonstrating that legalization status plays a critical role in shaping market dynamics.

The expansion of states’ legalization of cannabis has been accompanied by an increase in the number of cannabis dispensaries; most Americans now reside in counties with access to medical or recreational cannabis retail outlets.15 Also, accompanying states’ shifts to legalization has been a proliferation of cannabis-derived products that include edibles, topicals, liquid concentrates, clothing, and health and beauty products. This was enabled by the 2018 federal Agriculture Improvement Act (commonly known as the 2018 Farm Bill), which redefined hemp as cannabis containing no more than 0.3% Δ9-THC by dry weight.16 Hemp-derived CBD market segment in this study refers to this class of products.

Once the hemp plant passes the 0.3% dry weight test, companies can extract and concentrate cannabinoids to create legal, potentially intoxicating products with more than 0.3% THC, categorized as hemp-derived THC in this study. These include psychoactive compounds such as delta-8-THC (Δ8-THC), often available in states that have not legalized cannabis. In addition, although hemp-derived CBD with < 0.3% Δ9-THC is legal federally, and in some states sales of CBD are legal with minimal restrictions, other states have banned it or have more restrictive requirements (eg, medical use only, legal age for purchasing, maximum level of THC, etc.).17

In this complex web of state and federal-level cannabis policies, the hemp-derived THC market is flourishing in all states irrespective of their cannabis legalization status, as this study reveals. The market share of hemp-derived THC is, however, unusually high in Texas (34%) and Mississippi (17%) compared with other states (≤7.0%) evaluated in this study. Texas had a Compassionate Use Program allowing low-THC cannabis to use for medical purposes since 2015. Texas appears to be the largest market for hemp-derived THC products in the US, a position likely associated with, among other factors, the allowance of Δ8-THC as legal since a county judge temporarily blocked the state from classifying it as a controlled substance in November 2021.18 In Mississippi, the Hemp Cultivation Act was signed into law in 2020 that made hemp-derived THC products with a maximum THC concentration of 0.3% legal in Mississippi. The law allowed purchase from local or online retailers or personal conveyance from out-of-state sources.19

Recently introduced legislation in both states will offer the opportunity to evaluate their effects on subsequent sales. Mississippi lawmakers recently introduced two bills (in March 2025) that would ban hemp products from having higher than 0.3% concentration of any type of THC, ban the sale of synthetic hemps such as Δ8-THC, and require all hemp products be sold to people who are at least 21 years old.20 The lawmakers in Texas have also ramped up law enforcement efforts with a new bill proposed in April 2025 that seeks to regulate hemp-derived THC products.21 Ongoing monitoring of the hemp-derived THC market, relative to other segments, is warranted given the current context.

Aside from the ambiguities of federal and state laws, state-specific legal and regulatory challenges, socio-demographic factors, overall policy environment, and lack of legal recreational cannabis sales may have contributed to greater demand for hemp-derived THC products in these states. Further investigation is needed to assess the relative impact of each factor and to explore how strengthening state regulations could effectively address them.

The unregulated hemp-derived THC market poses significant public health concerns due to the widespread availability of highly intoxicating products with elevated THC concentrations. Consumers are incentivized to switch to these products as they are not taxed and deemed cheaper relative to the products that are regulated and taxed under RCLs, as the retailer and expert interviews in this study indicate. It highlights the urgent need to close the federal regulatory loophole and inform policymakers through ongoing surveillance of the rapidly evolving commercial cannabis landscape.

The elevated total THC market value observed in states with RCLs likely reflects greater sales volume rather than higher unit prices. While legalization could theoretically raise prices through product diversification, it more commonly reduces unit costs due to lower legal risks, improved production efficiencies, economies of scale, and heightened market competition.5,22 Thus, the higher market value in MCL + RCL states likely indicates greater consumption, consistent with national trends in cannabis use and the expanded accessibility and affordability enabled by an RCL.1 The retailer interviews in this study further revealed that older people (50 years and above) tend to buy CBD products most likely for their therapeutic effects, highlighting increasing cannabis use among older adults.23 Younger and middle-aged adults, on the other hand, were more likely to obtain THC products. Overall, this characterizes an age-related shift in cannabis consumption consistent with changing routes of cannabis administration in the recent past.24,25

The dispersion in the levels and shares of different cannabis market segments within MCL + RCL states can be driven by state-level heterogeneity in RCL and MCL policy regimes, the recency of policy adoption, state capacity and resources needed for cannabis regulation, as well as the status of cannabis legalization in bordering states influencing out-of-state demand. The four MCL + RCL states, for example, vary widely with respect to the specifics of legal limits for possessing cannabis at any one time: up to 1 ounce (28 grams) in Colorado and Massachusetts; up to 30 grams of cannabis flower in Illinois; and up to 2 ounces of cannabis flower, 16 grams of concentrate, and 800 milligrams of marijuana edibles in New Mexico (Appendix Table S2). State-specific possession limits may also lead some consumers to seek alternatives in unregulated hemp-derived THC or illicit markets. However, the extent of this shift may vary by state, resulting in differing market segment compositions. The influence of varying cannabis policies on patterns of use and associated health outcomes remains insufficiently studied, highlighting the need for a more in-depth analysis of the regulatory frameworks guiding their implementation.

In no-CL states, substantial demand for THC was met primarily through illicit channels. In MCL-only states (excluding Oklahoma), illicit THC sales exceeded those from medical channels, indicating unmet demand for recreational use. This may reflect diversion or misuse of medical THC, as well as reliance on illicit sources to satisfy recreational consumption.

In MCL + RCL states, recreational THC dominated the market, yet illicit sales remained significant. Evidence of increased cannabis purchases from licensed stores and declines in sourcing from dealers, friends/family, and home cultivation,5 as well as reductions in illegal cannabis plant seizures26 and contraband at the US–Mexico border,27 suggest that legal markets may gradually displace illicit sales over time. However, legalization may not eliminate illicit markets, as demonstrated by Colorado, where illicit THC still accounts for 23% of the market a decade after the enactment of its RCL. Persistent illegal sales warrant further investigation. States considering legalization should not expect complete market transition. Ongoing enforcement, consumer education, and competitive pricing in legal markets will be critical to reducing illicit activity.28

Strengths and limitations

The current study seeks to address the data gap regarding commercial cannabis availability. A key strength of this study lies in its systematic methodology for estimating the size of five distinct THC/CBD market segments and employing a mixed-methods approach that integrates both primary and secondary data sources. Due to the absence of a single comprehensive and reliable data source on cannabis market segments, we employed multiple complementary sources to enhance precision in the measurement of cannabis market sizes. The measurement approach was tailored to the availability of data across individual states, resulting in a methodology that is not fully standardized for replication in other jurisdictions. To address this concern, we have included as much detail as possible within the manuscript, with additional information provided in the Appendix. As the cannabis market continues to formalize and data reporting practices improve, the need for customized measurement approaches is expected to diminish.

A notable limitation in the estimation of illicit market size based on past-month cannabis use prevalence rates arises from the inclusion of individuals who obtain cannabis through home cultivation, irrespective of its legal status within their state of residence. In jurisdictions where home cultivation is prohibited, such activity contributes to the illicit cannabis market. Conversely, in states that permit home cultivation, it may represent a legal source of cannabis. Consequently, aggregating all home cultivators under illicit market estimates may result in an overestimation of the illicit market size in states where home cultivation is legally sanctioned. However, when calculating national average THC spending per user and state-level cannabis user counts, this bias may be attenuated if the proportion of users relying on home cultivation is relatively uniform across states. An additional complexity arises when individuals who cultivate cannabis at home also engage in purchases from commercial sources, potentially confounding market segmentation. Future research should aim to more rigorously assess these sources of bias to improve the accuracy of illicit market size estimations.

Despite considerable heterogeneity in state-specific market outcomes, patterns of sales across legalization status yielded information that states pursuing legalization may wish to consider. More state-specific research is required to take into account nuances in the regulatory aspects of cannabis legalization within each state.

Conclusion

THC sales are considerable, not only in states with an RCL but also in MCL-only and no-CL states. Demand in states without an RCL is largely met by purchases of THC from illicit sources, through diversion of medical THC, and/or by purchases of unregulated hemp-derived THC. The persistence of illicit THC sales even in legal markets, and the emergence of hemp-derived THC in both legal and non-legal markets, highlights regulatory gaps and challenges in market oversight. These findings underscore the need for integrated policy approaches that align enforcement strategies with public health objectives and consumer education. This will ensure safe, equitable, and effective cannabis market regulation.
