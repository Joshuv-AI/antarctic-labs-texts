Rethinking Perioperative Corticosteroids in Esophageal Cancer Surgery: Evidence From an Integrative Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Tomohiko Yasuda, Akihisa Matsuda, Nobutoshi Hagiwara, Yoshiharu Nakamura, Hiroshi Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEsophagectomy remains a highly invasive procedure associated with substantial postoperative morbidity. Pulmonary complications, anastomotic leakage, and in‐hospital mortality are of particular concern. Perioperative corticosteroids are often administered to attenuate excessive inflammatory responses; however, the clinical impact in esophageal cancer surgery remains inconclusive, with studies yielding inconsistent findings and lacking integrated long‐term evaluation. This meta‐analysis aimed to comprehensively assess the effects of perioperative corticosteroid administration on both short‐term complications and long‐term survival in patients undergoing esophagectomy.MethodsA systematic literature search identified randomized controlled trials and observational studies published to October 2025 that examined corticosteroid use during esophagectomy. Primary outcomes comprised postoperative pneumonia, anastomotic leakage, and in‐hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes comprised respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, wound infection, hepatic and renal dysfunction, cardiac complications, and overall survival. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) and hazard ratios were calculated using random‐effects models.ResultsNineteen studies involving 38 316 patients were included. Perioperative corticosteroid use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of pneumonia (OR = 0.71), in‐hospital mortality (OR = 0.79), and respiratory failure (OR = 0.30) compared with no corticosteroids. No increase in anastomotic leakage or wound infection rates were observed with versus without perioperative corticosteroids; long‐term survival was unaffected (hazard ratio: 0.85), and no excess risk of other complications was detected.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first meta‐analysis integrating both short‐ and long‐term outcomes, suggesting that perioperative corticosteroids may reduce major postoperative complications without evidence of adverse long‐term oncologic outcomes; however, these findings were largely driven by observational studies.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is a major global health burden because of its high incidence and poor prognosis [1]. Although recent improvements in surgical techniques and perioperative management have contributed to better outcomes, esophagectomy continues to be one of the most physically demanding oncologic procedures, for patients. Population‐based studies have reported substantial rates of postoperative complications and mortality after esophagectomy, highlighting the considerable risks associated with this surgery [1].

One of the key factors contributing to adverse outcomes is the systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgical trauma. This exaggerated immune activation can cause widespread tissue damage, impair organ function, and predispose patients to infectious complications [2, 3]. Glucocorticoids attenuate the acute‐phase response and reduce inflammation through suppression of pro‐inflammatory cytokine gene expression [4]. Accordingly, perioperative corticosteroid administration has been explored as a strategy to dampen inflammatory cascades and potentially reduce postoperative morbidity [3, 5]. However, glucocorticoid therapy is not without risks. Inhibitory effects on cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration may impair surgical wound healing, potentially increasing the likelihood of anastomotic leakage [6]. Furthermore, immunosuppression resulting from corticosteroid use can compromise host defenses, increasing susceptibility to surgical site infections and pulmonary complications, such as pneumonia, and potentially affecting tumor surveillance and long‐term oncologic outcomes, including recurrence [7].

While numerous studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and large‐scale observational analyses, have investigated the clinical consequences of perioperative corticosteroid use in esophagectomy, the findings have been inconsistent and inconclusive [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Importantly, to our knowledge, no previous meta‐analysis has comprehensively assessed both short‐term surgical outcomes and long‐term prognosis in this context. Therefore, this systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of perioperative prophylactic corticosteroid administration on a range of clinical outcomes in patients undergoing esophagectomy for esophageal cancer, with a focus on postoperative organ dysfunction, overall morbidity and mortality, and long‐term survival.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review and meta‐analysis followed the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The institutional ethical review was waived because of the nature of the study design [14]. Two independent investigators (TY and AM) performed a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library for studies published to 30 October 2025. The search terms comprised the following: (“esophagectomy” OR “oesophagectomy”) AND (“esophageal cancer” OR “esophageal neoplasm”) AND (“steroid” OR “glucocorticoid” OR “corticosteroid” OR “dexamethasone” OR “methylprednisolone”) AND (“perioperative” OR “preoperative” OR “intraoperative”). The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also manually screened.

Eligibility

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined a priori. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients undergoing esophagectomy for esophageal cancer, comparative study evaluating the impact of perioperative corticosteroid administration (preoperative or intraoperative) on clinical outcomes, and (2) both RCTs and retrospective studies. The exclusion criteria were defined as (1) duplicate studies, (2) studies that focused solely on biochemical markers (e.g., inflammatory cytokines or stress hormones) and did not report prespecified clinical outcomes or provide sufficient data for outcome extraction, and (3) case reports, reviews, letters, or meeting abstracts. Articles written in all languages were considered eligible.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes comprised major short‐term complications; that is, postoperative pneumonia, anastomotic leakage, and in‐hospital mortality. The secondary outcomes were acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), respiratory failure, wound infection, hepatic dysfunction, renal dysfunction, cardiac complications, and overall survival (OS).

Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted study data using a standardized form. Extracted variables comprised first author, year, country, study design, number of institutions, sample size, and baseline characteristics (i.e., age, sex, cancer stage, surgical approach, reconstruction method, corticosteroid regimen). Postoperative outcomes were assessed at the latest postoperative point reported in each study. Survival outcomes were evaluated using reported hazard ratios (HRs) or estimated from Kaplan–Meier curves when not directly provided. These outcomes were classified as primary or secondary, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion. The unpublished data required for the current meta‐analysis were requested through the corresponding authors of the included studies. All data were cross‐checked to reach consensus and entered into a spreadsheet for analysis.

Quality Assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated on the basis of the study design. RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool [15], and observational studies were assessed using the Methodological Index for Non‐Randomized Studies criteria [16]. Evidence certainty for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. GRADE pro software (McMaster University and Evidence Prime Inc., Hamilton, ON, Canada) was used to generate evidence profiles and summary tables [17]. All assessments were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consensus.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for binary outcomes to evaluate the effect of corticosteroids on postoperative complications. ORs < 1 favored the corticosteroid group. Pooled ORs were calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method under a random‐effects model. HRs with 95% CIs were used for OS; when not reported, HRs were estimated from Kaplan–Meier curves using graphical reconstruction. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q and the I2 statistic, with p < 0.05 and I 2 ≥ 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity, respectively. Meta‐analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration 2020; revman.cochrane.org). Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry for outcomes that included ≥ 10 studies. For secondary outcomes with < 10 studies, publication bias was not formally assessed owing to insufficient statistical power.

Results

Studies and Patient Characteristics

The initial database search identified 2542 records, of which 21 duplicates were removed. The remaining 2521 articles were screened by title and abstract, resulting in the exclusion of 2478 records. Full‐text review was performed for 43 articles, of which 24 were excluded for the following reasons: review study type (n = 10), lack of comparative analysis (n = 5), lack of postoperative outcomes data (n = 3), irrelevant surgical procedures (n = 2), absence of perioperative steroid use (n = 2), and other criteria. Nineteen studies were ultimately included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. The study selection process is outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure S1). The included studies, published between 1994 and 2025, comprised 9 RCTs [9, 11, 12, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] and 10 retrospective studies [8, 10, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31] involving 38 316 patients who underwent esophagectomy. Among them, 23 730 received perioperative corticosteroids, while 14 586 who did not receive perioperative corticosteroids served as controls. Most studies originated from East Asia, particularly Japan, South Korea, and China. Corticosteroids were administered either preoperatively or intraoperatively and included methylprednisolone and dexamethasone. Baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, respiratory dysfunction), and cancer stage, were reported in most studies. Surgical factors, including operative time, blood loss, reconstruction route, and anastomosis location, varied but were generally comparable between the steroid and control groups.

Among the retrospective studies, the average Methodological Index for Non‐Randomized Studies score was 19.1 (range: 16–21), indicating moderate to good methodological quality (Table S1). Risk of bias among the included RCTs, assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, showed some methodological concerns in several domains but was generally judged as low to moderate overall (Figure S2). On the basis of the GRADE framework, the quality of the evidence was rated as moderate for the primary outcomes (postoperative pneumonia, anastomotic leakage, and in‐hospital mortality), and low to very low for the secondary outcomes (e.g., respiratory failure, ARDS, and cardiac complications), primarily because of imprecision and risk of bias (Table S2).

Primary Outcomes

Thirteen studies involving 712 patients in the steroid group and 765 in the control group reported postoperative pneumonia. The pooled analysis showed a significantly lower risk in the steroid group (OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.52–0.96; p = 0.03) (Figure 1a) compared with the control group. Heterogeneity was negligible (I 2 = 0%, χ2 = 6.71; p = 0.75), and visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed no asymmetry (Figure 1a).

Forest plots show significant differences in postoperative pneumonia and in‐hospital mortality (a, c), but not in anastomotic leakage (b). Funnel plots show no asymmetry, suggesting low publication bias (a–c).

Anastomotic leakage was assessed in 19 studies involving 23 730 patients in the steroid group and 14 586 in the control group, with no significant difference between the groups (OR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.62–1.04; p = 0.09) (Figure 1b). Heterogeneity was low (I2 = 17%, χ2 = 20.4; p = 0.25), and the funnel plot appeared symmetrical (Figure 1b).

In‐hospital mortality was reported in 12 studies involving 23 297 steroid‐treated patients and 14 152 controls. Perioperative corticosteroid use was associated with a significantly lower risk of in‐hospital mortality (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.69–0.90; p = 0.0005) (Figure 1c) compared with no corticosteroid use. Between‐study heterogeneity was low (I 2 = 0%, χ2 = 4.38; p = 0.63), and visual inspection of the funnel plot showed no apparent asymmetry (Figure 1c).

Secondary Outcomes

The secondary outcomes comprised various organ‐specific complications and OS.

The incidence of ARDS did not differ significantly between the steroid and control groups, with no heterogeneity (OR = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.43–1.97; I 2 = 0%, χ2 = 2.30; p = 0.51) (Figure 2a). In contrast, respiratory failure rates were significantly lower in the steroid versus control groups, with moderate heterogeneity (OR = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.12–0.75; I 2 = 64%, χ2 = 13.78; p = 0.02) (Figure 2b).

Forest plots show a significant difference in respiratory failure (b), but no significant differences in ARDS (a), wound infection (c), hepatic and renal dysfunction (d, e), cardiac complications (f), or overall survival (g).

Wound infection, reported in seven studies, did not differ significantly between the groups (OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.22–1.07; p = 0.07), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 53%, χ2 = 14.92; p = 0.04) (Figure 2c).

Hepatic dysfunction, renal dysfunction, and cardiac complications showed no significant differences between the groups (hepatic: OR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.25–1.31; p = 0.18; renal: OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.30–2.05; p = 0.62; cardiac: OR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.41–1.10; p = 0.11). Heterogeneity was low across all analyses (Figure 2d–f).

OS was analyzed in three studies using Kaplan–Meier estimates. While one study reported better 5‐year survival in the steroid group compared with the control group, the other two studies showed no significant difference. The pooled HR was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.58–1.25; p = 0.41), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 42%) (Figure 2g). Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed for the secondary outcomes, consistent with our predefined criteria.

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analysis

Because the study by Hirano et al. [29] strongly influenced the pooled estimates, a sensitivity analysis excluding this study was performed. Subgroup analyses by study design and surgical approach (minimally invasive esophagectomy versus thoracotomy) were also performed to address potential methodological and procedural differences. These analyses are summarized in Table S3. In the sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Hirano et al., statistically significant differences were observed for anastomotic leakage and in‐hospital mortality. In subgroup analyses stratified by study design, statistically significant differences in postoperative pneumonia and in‐hospital mortality were observed in retrospective studies, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in RCTs. In subgroup analyses stratified by surgical approach, perioperative corticosteroid administration was associated with a significantly lower incidence of postoperative pneumonia in minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) (OR = 0.64; 95% CI, 0.46–0.88), whereas no statistically significant differences were observed for other primary outcomes in either minimally invasive or open esophagectomy.

Discussion

This meta‐analysis, mainly derived from retrospective studies, found that perioperative corticosteroid administration in patients undergoing esophagectomy was associated with a lower risk of postoperative pneumonia and in‐hospital mortality compared with no corticosteroid use. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups for the secondary outcomes, such as surgical site infection, hepatic and renal dysfunction, cardiovascular complications, or OS.

Esophagectomy imposes substantial inflammatory and oxidative stress, not only because of direct surgical manipulation of the lung and associated factors, such as atelectasis, alveolar recruitment, and reperfusion injury, but also as a result of high tidal volumes and airway pressures, high inspired oxygen concentrations, and shear stress in the contralateral ventilated lung [32, 33]. These stressors trigger the release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators, leading to pulmonary infiltration of immune cells and contributing to postoperative pneumonia [34]. By inhibiting the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway, corticosteroids suppress the transcription of proinflammatory cytokines and reduce acute‐phase reactants, such as C‐reactive protein, interleukin (IL)‐6, and IL‐8; thereby, attenuating the systemic inflammatory response syndrome [2, 4, 35]. These biological effects of corticosteroids, including reduced neutrophil infiltration into the alveoli and improved capillary permeability [34, 35], may be consistent with the observed reductions in postoperative pneumonia and in‐hospital mortality in the present meta‐analysis. Notably, the pneumonia benefit was evident in the MIE subgroup, a recently adopted approach associated with less chest wall trauma, but not in thoracotomy. Because postoperative pneumonia has been reported as an independent prognostic factor associated with poorer long‐term survival after esophagectomy [36], this reduction may be clinically meaningful.

No significant association was found between steroid administration and the incidence of surgical site infection or anastomotic leakage. Although concerns regarding delayed wound healing and increased infection risk have historically tempered the use of perioperative steroids, such adverse effects are typically associated with prolonged or chronic use [6]. Mechanistically, glucocorticoids may impair fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and keratinocyte growth factor expression, while inducing hyperglycemia and neutrophil/macrophage dysfunction [6]. However, the transient and controlled use of perioperative corticosteroids may limit these effects. The anti‐inflammatory properties of corticosteroids may improve microcirculation, reduce local edema, and mitigate tissue injury, potentially offsetting any negative impact on wound healing. This interpretation aligns with findings from a large randomized trial by Corcoran et al., which reported no increase in surgical site infections with intraoperative dexamethasone, even among diabetic patients, supporting the perioperative safety of corticosteroids [37].

Perioperative corticosteroid administration during esophagectomy showed no significant protective effects against hepatic dysfunction, renal impairment, or cardiovascular complications in the present analysis. In patients with esophageal cancer, systemic physiological reserves are often compromised because of lifestyle‐related factors, such as excessive alcohol consumption, smoking, malnutrition, and vitamin deficiencies [38, 39]. Intraoperative oxidative stress may activate Kupffer cells in the liver, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor‐alpha, IL‐1, and IL‐6, which can exacerbate hepatic injury [40]. Furthermore, systemic inflammatory responses can induce vascular endothelial injury and increase the risk of thrombosis, contributing to cardiovascular and renal dysfunction [2]. However, these organ complications are often multifactorial and driven predominantly by noninflammatory mechanisms, such as bleeding, hypoperfusion, sympathetic overactivity, electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia, and drug‐induced toxicity [41]. These factors may explain why corticosteroids exerted only limited effects in the present study. Additionally, organ‐specific inflammatory responses to systemic inflammatory response syndrome vary across organs, partly because of differential activation of signaling pathways such as NF‐κB and JAK–STAT [40].

In the present meta‐analysis, perioperative corticosteroid administration was not associated with a significant difference in long‐term OS after esophagectomy. Corticosteroids suppress a series of immune responses mediated by tumor‐specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes, including tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, that recognize tumor antigens through adaptive immunity and potentially impair tumor immune surveillance and increase the risk of recurrence or adversely affect long‐term outcomes [42]. Conversely, corticosteroids exert direct antitumor effects, such as inhibiting colony formation, migration, and proliferation of cancer cells [43]. These effects may explain why perioperative corticosteroid use was associated with improved OS in a previous meta‐analysis of colorectal cancer studies [44]. Regarding esophageal cancer, Blank et al. reported that intraoperative dexamethasone did not reduce 1‐year mortality [45]. Our analysis further indicates that perioperative corticosteroids were not associated with a significant difference in long‐term survival, suggesting that the short‐term organ‐protective and anti‐inflammatory benefits may be counterbalanced by opposing immunologic effects, while the transient nature of perioperative corticosteroid administration may limit sustained immunosuppression and thereby mitigate adverse long‐term oncologic consequences.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in the present study. First, heterogeneity in corticosteroid regimens across studies, including differences in steroid type, dosage, and timing of administration, may have influenced treatment effects and limited the clinical interpretability of the pooled estimates. Notably, although most studies administered a single preoperative dose of methylprednisolone, dosing strategies varied substantially. Formal subgroup analyses according to steroid type, dose, or timing were not feasible because of the limited number of studies within each category and the frequent overlap of regimen variables within individual studies. This limitation precluded reliable stratified comparisons and definitive conclusions regarding optimal perioperative administration. In addition, evidence from other surgical fields suggests that the choice of corticosteroid agent may influence clinical outcomes, reflecting differences in glucocorticoid potency and duration of action [46]. These observations underscore the need for future esophageal cancer–specific studies designed to directly compare steroid type, dosage, and timing. Moreover, definitions of postoperative complications, including pneumonia, respiratory failure, and organ dysfunction, were not uniform across the included studies, which may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity. Second, most included studies were retrospective, raising the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding, which may have led to an overestimation of treatment effects. As a result, several statistically significant associations observed in this meta‐analysis were primarily driven by retrospective studies, and their interpretation should therefore be made with caution. In contrast, the randomized controlled trials included in this meta‐analysis were generally underpowered to detect differences in relatively infrequent outcomes, such as postoperative mortality. This imbalance in study design may partly explain the discrepancy observed between retrospective studies and randomized controlled trials. Additionally, subgroup analyses indicated that the observed benefits were mainly derived from the retrospective studies. Third, data for many secondary outcomes were derived from fewer than 10 studies, precluding formal assessment of publication bias. Finally, survival data in some studies were reconstructed from Kaplan–Meier curves, potentially introducing estimation errors. Accordingly, the results for several secondary outcomes should be interpreted with caution, given the low certainty of evidence.

Despite these limitations, this study represents one of the most comprehensive meta‐analyses evaluating both short‐ and long‐term outcomes of perioperative corticosteroid use in esophageal cancer surgery. While previous meta‐analyses in adjacent surgical fields, including gastrointestinal, hepatic, and cardiac surgery, have primarily focused on isolated endpoints or short‐term outcomes [44, 47, 48, 49, 50], the present analysis provides an integrated assessment of postoperative complications across multiple organ systems together with long‐term survival. The observed benefits of steroid use are consistent with findings in other surgical fields, including hepatectomy and cardiac surgery, where corticosteroids have been shown to reduce postoperative inflammation, complications, and length of stay [48, 50]. These data collectively support the rationale for using steroids as a supportive intervention, particularly in esophagectomy, which is a procedure associated with pronounced systemic inflammation.

In conclusion, perioperative corticosteroid administration may be associated with a reduced risk of postoperative pneumonia and in‐hospital mortality in patients undergoing esophagectomy, without clear evidence of an increased risk of anastomotic leakage, other complications, or adverse long‐term oncologic outcomes. These observed benefits were primarily supported by observational studies, and should be interpreted with caution. Notably, a recent large randomized trial in gastric cancer demonstrated that intraoperative corticosteroid administration attenuated postoperative inflammatory responses without improving long‐term survival [51], underscoring the need for adequately powered randomized controlled trials in esophageal cancer to clarify whether these short‐term benefits translate into meaningful long‐term oncologic outcomes and to define the optimal dosing regimen, timing, and choice of agent.
