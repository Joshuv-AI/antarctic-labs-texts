Design and adaptation of a practice guideline for nonpharmacological management of pain in injured children in emergency medical services
Authors: Hojjatallah Poormoradi, Hoda Ahmari Tehran, Sarallah Shojaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Pain is one of the most common complaints among children in emergency medical services (EMSs). Unfortunately, it is often inadequately and poorly managed, resulting in unnecessary pain and suffering in pediatric populations. Given the barriers to pharmacological pain management for children in prehospital emergency settings, emergency medical technicians can effectively utilize nonpharmacological approaches to pain management without fear or concern. Practice guidelines are one of the effective factors in optimizing pain management for children in the prehospital setting. This study aimed to design and localize a clinical guideline for nonpharmacological pain management in injured children in EMSs.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Through a multistage evolutionary study conducted from April 2020 to February 2022, practice guidelines relevant to the research topic were extracted from databases and evaluated and critiqued using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) tool for guideline quality. The two selected practice guidelines with the highest scores were used to extract clinical recommendations, which were then synthesized into a localized clinical guideline after consensus in two Delphi rounds and a panel of experts.RESULTS:The recommendations were categorized into three sections: general recommendations (9 recommendations), pain assessment and measurement (10 recommendations), and nonpharmacological management (15 recommendations). These recommendations were extracted from two selected clinical guidelines, which were filtered from six guidelines after AGREE evaluation. After consensus among experts in two Delphi rounds, the recommendations were compiled into a localized clinical guideline.CONCLUSION:The clinical guideline for prehospital pain management in injured children can improve the quality-of-care programs, address existing barriers to pain management in children, and increase patient satisfaction. The developed guidelines are also used in the evaluation of emergency services’ performance in pain management and the accreditation of prehospital care. Future research should investigate the prevalence and management of pediatric pain in prehospital settings, outcome measures, barriers, and facilitating factors in this area.

Introduction

Pain is a global burden and an increasing issue in the healthcare system,[1] and it is the most common symptom in emergency environments, representing one of the most challenging clinical problems for emergency care providers, especially in pediatric populations.[2] The prevalence of acute pain in prehospital emergency settings is reported to range from 42% to 53%.[234] Some studies report a prevalence of 52% to 91% of pain in injured individuals.[56] In Iran, there are no statistical data on the prevalence and management of prehospital pain[7]; however, given the high incidence of accidents and injuries in the country, a high prevalence of acute (very sudden onset or rapid change) pain in this context is evident.[89]

The World Health Organization states that all countries should provide pain management as a fundamental duty and a health right.[10] Among these, the pediatric population is of particular importance as childhood injury is recognized as a global epidemic.[11] Although pain is one of the most common complaints among children in prehospital emergency settings, inadequate and poor management leads to unnecessary pain and suffering.[12] Inadequate pain management in children can have psychological, social, and physical consequences, including respiratory problems, tachycardia, hypertension, myocardial ischemia, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).[1314] It can also lead to other negative effects, such as fear of medical events and avoidance of medical care, which can extend into adulthood and impact their quality of life.[15] To improve the quality of care and reduce the number of at-risk children, a higher understanding of prehospital pain management in children is necessary.[16] Effective pain management has recently been identified as a key quality indicator for emergency medical services (EMSs).[17] Pain is a prevalent issue in pediatric populations, particularly in emergency settings, where it is often inadequately managed, leading to unnecessary suffering. The development of clinical guidelines is essential for optimizing pain management [Figure 1].

Development of clinical guidelines in pain management

Although prehospital pain management is primarily based on pharmacological interventions, these are particularly less used in children.[1819] Reasons such as lack of accurate pain assessment, fear of side effects, extensive barriers to the use of pain relievers, and others have been reported for the underuse of analgesics in children.[202122] These barriers should not justify untreated acute pain in children.[23] Given these obstacles, the use of nonpharmacological interventions is of great importance. The wide availability of nonpharmacological interventions for alleviating pain in children, including physical interventions (such as immobilization, proper positioning, cold and hot compresses, etc.) and psychological interventions (such as attention control techniques, relaxation methods, etc.), makes effective prehospital pain management possible and makes pain more tolerable.[242526]

Pain management should be considered a critical element of prehospital care,[15] and nurses need to be trained in terms of pain management.[14] Considering the service delivery model in prehospital emergencies, which is conducted by technicians and physician consultations via phone or a set of predetermined clinical protocols and guidelines, this allows technicians to make decisions based on these solutions.[1527] However, prehospital pain management for children is currently in an unfavorable and unacceptable state, indicating a lack of a standardized clinical guideline in this area.[28] Various studies have reported the importance of clinical guidelines, citing that the implementation of guidelines reduces medical errors, costs, and variations in service delivery; prevents the provision of nonstandard actions; and improves care outcomes and safe service delivery to patients.[293031] In recent years, practice guidelines for the emergency management of pediatric pain have been developed and implemented worldwide.[32] Most guidelines emphasize the use of nonpharmacological interventions alone or in combination with analgesics for managing traumatic and nontraumatic pain.[33]

Thus, the adaptation of guidelines is considered in societies where rich sources of information and expertise are not available.[34] Adaptation of guidelines is a systematic approach. In this process, the existing guidelines are modified to be used in the intended cultural and organizational context.[29] In addition, the aim of the fifth development plan of the country, as well as the strategic goal No. 75 of the Ministry of Health, emphasizes the development and use of medical guidelines and developing healthcare service standards by supporting the adaptation of clinical guidelines.[35]

Given the absence of a comprehensive clinical guideline for pain management, the development and adaptation of a clinical guideline for nonpharmacological management of acute traumatic pain in children is crucial. This population constitutes a significant portion of patients in the prehospital emergency system of the country. Considering the importance of pain management and its impact on the quality of prehospital care as the first line of treatment, as well as the vulnerability of the pediatric population, the research team aimed to develop and present a localized clinical guideline using the most up-to-date Practice Guideline on prehospital pain management in children. This was done through an evolutionary and multistage study with the help of experts in the field.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This research employed a developmental study method over several stages from April 2020 to February 2022 at Qom University of Medical Sciences using review and qualitative methods to localize a clinical guideline for nonpharmacological management of acute traumatic pain in EMS.

Study participants and sampling

Searching and retrieving sources (4/2020 to 8/2020)

In this stage, articles and practice guidelines related to prehospital pain management for children were sought using keywords associated with acute traumatic pain management like (“non-pharmacological management” OR “pain management”) AND (“injured children” OR “pediatric pain”) AND (“emergency medical services” OR “EMS”). Clinical guidelines were searched using a library study (books and articles about acute pain management) and electronic databases during 20120–2020, mainly from Medline, Science Direct, PubMed, Elsevier, Springer, ProQuest, Cochran Data Base, EBM Review, OVID, SID and specialized international databases, for the development and publication of clinical guidelines [Table 1].

In reviewing and searching for sources, 5800 articles and 16 clinical guidelines related to the research topic were initially extracted. Articles and guidelines were evaluated based on screening criteria (complete guideline version, appropriate organization, and up-to-date content). After reviewing the summary and abstract of the articles by the research team and eliminating duplicate articles, articles for which the full text was not available, sources focusing on hospital emergency settings, nontraumatic pain management, chronic pain, and adult age groups, finally, 180 articles and 6 comprehensive and specialized practice guidelines were selected.

Data collection tool and technique

Evaluating practice guideline using the AGREE tool (9/2020 to 12/2020)

In this stage, the quality of the six selected practice guidelines was assessed using the AGREE tool by four evaluators, consisting of one emergency medicine specialist, one doctorate in medical education, one doctorate in nursing, and one master’s degree in emergency nursing, who were selected by the research team. The AGREE tool consists of 23 key criteria across six domains and an overall assessment section [Table 2]. It is an internationally recognized standard with high credibility.[3637] Finally, after evaluation, extraction, and review of the relevant forms, Acute pain management scientific Evidence Australian and New Zealand College of Anesthetists (ANZCA) with a score of 86, and the guidelines for the management of acute pain in emergencies European Society for Emergency Medicine (EUSEM) with a score of 85, according to the calculated scores, were selected for the development of domestic guidelines.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Qom University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUQ.REC.1398.117). After being informed about the purpose of the study, all participants voluntarily gave their informed consent to participate in the study. The procedures of this study were following the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki on research involving human participants.

Statistical analysis method

Analyzing and reviewing selected practice guidelines and initial recommendations (1/2021 to 3/2021)

In this stage, content analysis of the reference Practice Guideline was conducted, and recommendations were categorized into general recommendations, pain assessment and measurement, and nonpharmacological management of pain in injured children in EMSs. These were presented in a table format to facilitate data extraction from the selected practice guidelines with the help of advisory professors. The draft was then reviewed for scientific content, appropriateness of the guideline writing model, and compliance with the guideline writing framework by the research team and five faculty members from the Nursing and Midwifery Schools of Qom and Shiraz. The apparent and content validity of the guidelines were established. This stage aimed to ensure that the most accurate and reliable recommendations were used for adaptation.

External review by Delphi panel members and target users (4/2021 to 8/2021)

In this stage, the classic Delphi method and an in-person panel of experts were used to achieve consensus on the recommendations. Triangulation was used to collect data from more than one source, and the Delphi method was used to gather opinions from experts and reach a consensus on the variables identified in the triangulation process. Specialists in the medical sciences with sufficient experience and expertise in pain management were invited to complete the draft clinical guideline and provide their comments and suggestions for each recommendation. Experts were selected through purposive sampling from medical universities in Qom, Shiraz, and Jahrom, representing a wide demographic range and relevant to the research topic. These included pediatricians, emergency medicine specialists, anesthesiologists and anesthetic care specialists, clinical pharmacists, emergency medical staff (including paramedics, experts, and senior experts), emergency center physicians, and nursing professors. After selection and willingness to participate, the research type, general and specific objectives, and methodology were provided in writing along with consent forms, and informed consent was obtained from the participants. In the first Delphi round, the draft clinical guideline was presented in writing along with a completion guide to the participants. For initial scoring, participants were asked to evaluate the draft individually using a 5-point Likert scale in five domains (applicability, priority, usefulness, conceptual clarity, and target users authorized to perform the recommendations) for each recommendation. They were to provide their comments and suggestions next to each recommendation and return the scored draft for review within 1–2 weeks. Given that the goal of the Delphi method in this study was to achieve consensus among the group of specialists, it was crucial to have a high response rate to obtain necessary feedback and increase the study’s credibility. Efforts were made to achieve a response and participation rate of up to 95%. According to Feizi and Iran-Doust, a response rate of 70% is usually considered ideal.[38] Delphi evaluations were conducted following ethical research principles, without personal biases affecting the structure and content of the draft guideline. As subsequent Delphi rounds are based on the first round, all relevant responses were collected. Responses were organized, similar comments were combined, and repetitive and marginal topics were removed to make the responses as concise as possible. After calculating the percentage of agreement among participants, which was scored in four domains (applicability, relevance, usefulness, and conceptual clarity of each recommendation), recommendations with over 75% agreement and consensus were retained, while others were set aside for drafting the next clinical guideline. Some members had suggested changes, which were incorporated after reviewing and organizing the responses into new proposed recommendations for the next Delphi round. In subsequent Delphi rounds, participants were asked to review and revise the responses. This process continued to correct, interpret, delete, and explain the strengths and weaknesses of the evidence until the highest level of agreement was reached. Typically, three to four rounds of Delphi are sufficient to reach consensus and convergence, but the number of rounds may vary based on criteria such as the stability of responses over consecutive rounds and achieving consistency in opinions.[39] In this study, the research team, which considered 75% agreement for consensus, achieved consensus for over 90% of the recommendations after two Delphi rounds.

Final review of the clinical guideline draft by the in-person panel of experts (9/2021 to 12/2021)

After two rounds of Delphi, some recommendations that had not achieved the desired consensus, along with certain suggestions and comments from the experts, were reviewed, revised, or removed. This stage was the final opportunity for panel members to reassess and adjudicate the recommendations. In this phase, the final draft of the clinical guideline, derived from the two Delphi rounds, was reviewed again with the participation of six Delphi panel members who had previously been involved in the study, in consultation with the research team.

The current draft, which included a set of consensus recommendations and several that had not reached the necessary consensus, along with the suggestions from experts in earlier stages, was discussed and reviewed in the presence of the experts. The main focus of this in-person meeting was to clarify ambiguities, revise or remove recommendations with less than 75% consensus, and make editorial corrections to the recommendations. Based on the feedback from the panel members and the research team, additional points on prehospital pain management were included alongside the consensus recommendations and discussions on those that had not reached consensus.

Finalization of the customized clinical guideline (1/2022 to 2/2022)

After the in-person expert meeting, the final review and finalization of the clinical guidelines were carried out with guidance from supervising professors and consultants. Ultimately, the clinical guideline included 34 recommendations categorized into three areas: general recommendations, pain assessment and measurement, and nonpharmacological management of acute traumatic pain in children in EMSs. These recommendations were developed and customized accordingly. The validity of the guideline is supported by its development through a systematic and evidence-based process, rigorous evaluation using the AGREE tool, expert consensus through the Delphi method, and a focus on practical implementation in EMS settings. These elements collectively contribute to the guideline’s reliability and effectiveness in improving pain management for injured children.

Discussion

The process of adaptation is a systematic approach to developing localized clinical practice guidelines for use in different cultures and organizational settings. The aim of adapting and localizing practice guidelines is to create a clear, reproducible, and transparent standard based on evidence-based principles. In the process of adapting the present guideline, while considering the evidence and existing knowledge, the patient’s care needs and the opinions of the caregiver were also taken into account. This approach not only ensures cost-effectiveness and prevents redundancy but also enhances the quality of healthcare services and improves clinical outcomes based on the best and most current evidence available. In the context of Iran, the adaptation of these guidelines is supported by national health policy goals to improve access and quality of healthcare for vulnerable populations. This initiative is essential for addressing the high prevalence of acute pain among injured children and aligns with Iran’s strategic health goals to reduce disparities in healthcare delivery. The rigorous evaluation methods used in developing these guidelines reflect a commitment to evidence-based practice, ensuring that healthcare providers are equipped with effective strategies for managing pain. Alongside the development of high-quality clinical guidelines, tools for evaluating them have been provided. The purpose of these evaluation tools is to assist potential users in assessing the guidelines.[41] Turner et al.,[42] in their study on the development of evidence-based practice guidelines (a comparative perspective), state that the quality assessment tool for guidelines should accurately address key elements of the guidelines, including the multidisciplinary guideline development group, consumer involvement, identification of clinical questions or problems, conducting systematic searches and reviewing evidence, drafting recommendations, external consultation, and ongoing updates and reviews. Currently, there are many global tools available for evaluating and assessing the quality of clinical guidelines. Researchers have identified tools such as ADAPTE,[43] AGREE,[36] and DELBI[44] as highly credible in terms of applicability and validity.[4145] In this study, the AGREE tool was used. Siering et al.[41] highlight that evaluation tools containing unclear questions or lacking response criteria should not be applied, which makes the AGREE tool valuable as it comprehensively addresses various aspects of guideline quality, focusing on methodological issues surrounding guideline development and reporting, as well as detailing the search strategy within the guidelines.

The AGREE evaluation tool has also been used as a reliable instrument in many studies on the adaptation of practice guidelines within the country. Examples include Maleki et al. in their study on the adaptation of practice guidelines for cardiovascular disease prevention for use in preventive clinics,[46] Davoodi et al.[47] on the adaptation of practice guidelines for managing stable angina, and Sadeghniiat-Haghighi et al.[48] on the adaptation of practice guidelines for sleep ability testing. In the present study, the AGREE tool was used to assess the quality of the extracted guidelines.

The study highlights the urgent need for effective pain management strategies for injured children in EMS, where the prevalence of acute pain is reported to range from 42 to 91%. Given the barriers to pharmacological interventions, such as fear of side effects and inadequate access to pediatric-specific medications, the development of a localized clinical guideline for nonpharmacological pain management is crucial. The guideline, which includes 34 comprehensive recommendations categorized into general recommendations, pain assessment, and nonpharmacological management, aims to improve the quality of care and enhance patient satisfaction in prehospital settings. The adaptation of a clinical guideline for nonpharmacological pain management in EMS not only addresses an urgent need in pediatric care but also adheres to essential health policy benchmarks that promote quality, equity, evidence-based practice, patient-centered care, and continuous improvement.

The finalized localized clinical guideline includes 34 recommendations categorized into three areas: general recommendations, pain assessment and measurement, and nonpharmacological interventions. These recommendations were derived from two rounds of Delphi surveys and a panel of experts. The final list included recommendations that achieved a consensus of over 75% from the Delphi participants. Out of the 36 recommendations in the initial draft, 28 (less than 80%) reached the desired consensus in the first round of Delphi. Since the Delphi process aims to continue until a majority of recommendations achieve consensus,[38] the recommendations that reached consensus, along with modifications and feedback from Delphi participants on those that did not, were carried over to the second round of Delphi. In the second round, five more recommendations achieved a consensus of over 75%. The reasons for the lack of consensus on some recommendations included insufficient supporting evidence, the absence of relevant facilities and equipment in ambulances for implementing those clinical recommendations, and inadequate training for some clinical recommendations in pediatric pain management, which requires specialized knowledge and training. Thus, after two rounds of Delphi, over 90% of the recommendations (33 out of 34) reached the desired consensus. Since only three recommendations did not achieve consensus, the Delphi process was not repeated. Instead, a panel of experts was convened to address the nonconsensus recommendations and finalize the consensus recommendations. During the panel, discussions, and exchanges of views took place regarding the remaining three recommendations, and only one recommendation, along with some additional notes, was approved and reached a consensus among the members. Additionally, the panel members, considering the importance of the topic and the target population of the guideline, provided their opinions and suggestions in the form of key points (listed below) that should be considered in the development of the localized clinical guideline. 1) Based on the research group’s opinion and the panel members’ approval, it is recommended that a course on prehospital pain management be included in the curriculum for undergraduate emergency medical students, given the importance of pain management in the prehospital emergency system. 2) All operational emergency medical technicians should assess and record pain in the pediatric population. 3) Necessary training in nonpharmacological pain management techniques and treatments for children should be provided, considering the culture, ethnicity, race, gender, and age of the children in each system. 4) Given the importance of pain assessment and management, pain assessment should be considered alongside other vital signs in all injured patients. Emergency prehospital managers should allocate a section in the automation system for prehospital emergencies (Asa Yaar) for evaluating and recording pain quality. 5) When providing nonpharmacological interventions, the consent of the parents and legal guardians of injured children should be taken into account. 6) The current guideline should be reviewed and updated every 5 years based on national and domestic resources, considering the increased capabilities of the prehospital emergency system and the growth of new studies and scientific evidence in the field of pediatric prehospital pain management.

Limitations and recommendations

The most important limitation of this study was its slow and time-consuming process. Therefore, the research team tried to reduce the time limit as much as possible by continuous follow-up, and all participants in the study cooperated throughout the study. In the present study, most specialists believed that the applicability of certain nonpharmacological recommendations in the guidelines requires equipment and facilities in ambulances. They suggested that by addressing some organizational and managerial barriers, it would be possible to further improve the quality of pain management for children in EMS. Future research should examine and determine the prevalence and management of acute pain in children in the country’s prehospital emergency services, along with the effective criteria, barriers, and facilitators in this area. Future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of these guidelines in practice and exploring barriers to their implementation in various healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This clinical guideline has been localized according to the facilities of Iran for managing acute traumatic pain in children in EMS. A structured, evidence-based, localized pain management protocol can significantly improve outcomes in injured children treated by prehospital emergency departments, and it is hoped that proper use of this guideline can improve pain management in the prehospital setting. Additionally, the participatory approach in developing these guidelines emphasizes patient-centered care, which is a core principle in both global and Iranian health policies. By involving stakeholders such as healthcare professionals, patients, and families, the guidelines are made more relevant and sensitive to the needs of children and their caregivers. Implementing these guidelines, along with robust monitoring and evaluation mechanisms, will enhance the quality of emergency care for children in Iran, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes and greater patient satisfaction.

Implementing these guidelines will significantly enhance the quality of emergency care for children, ultimately leading to better health outcomes and increased patient satisfaction.
