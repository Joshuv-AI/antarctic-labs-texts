Stigma, isolation, and depression in Chinese and Korean American dementia caregivers
Authors: Xiang Qi, Eunjung Ko, Jia Yin Ruan, Jing Wang, Tek Leong, Ju Hye Jung, Jin Su, Yaolin Pei, Mary S. Mittelman, Bei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAffiliate stigma may cause depressive symptoms among Asian American dementia caregivers, yet few studies have examined the underlying mechanisms or cross‐ethnic differences.METHODSWe analyzed data from 338 older dementia caregivers (176 Chinese Americans, 162 Korean Americans; mean age 68.8 years) who completed measures of affiliate stigma, social isolation, loneliness, and depressive symptoms.RESULTSHigher stigma was associated with more severe depressive symptoms (p < 0.001). Mediation analyses suggest this effect is partly explained by increased social isolation (12.8% mediated) and, especially, greater loneliness (44.4% mediated) among highly stigmatized caregivers. The stigma–depression relationship was stronger for Korean than for Chinese American caregivers (p for interaction < 0.01).DISCUSSIONThese findings identify affiliate stigma as an important risk factor for dementia caregivers’ depressive symptoms, operating through both objective and emotional forms of social disconnection. Culturally sensitive efforts to reduce caregivers’ stigma and social disconnection may improve mental health outcomes in Asian American communities.

BACKGROUND

Dementia family caregivers commonly experience high levels of stress, 1 and roughly one‐third have been diagnosed with depression. 2 In Asian communities, caregiving occurs in the context of strong cultural norms of filial piety and family duty, which can amplify stress. 3 Among Asian American caregivers, dementia‐related stigma is pervasive: 91% report stigma in their caregiving experience. 4 Affiliate stigma refers to caregivers internalizing negative stereotypes and public stigma directed toward their family member with dementia, 4 leading them to feel ashamed or devalued. Prior research showed that affiliate stigma often leads to low quality of life and poorer mental health outcomes in caregivers. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, the pathways of how affiliate stigma leads to poor mental health are understudied.

Chinese and Korean Americans represent two large and fast‐growing Asian ethnic groups in the United States 9 and confront unique cultural and systemic challenges in dementia caregiving. They share strong filial norms that shape dementia caregiving yet differ in immigration histories, community resources, and cultural expressions of stigma. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Many first‐generation Chinese and Korean American caregivers have limited English proficiency and awareness of support services in the United States, contributing to isolation and underutilization of formal support resources. 10 , 11 Misconceptions and stigma surrounding dementia in these cultures (e.g., attributing symptoms to fate, wrongdoing, or “craziness”) further hinder early diagnosis and help seeking. 12 , 14 Studies of Korean American caregivers have documented high caregiver burden and psychological stress, along with significant barriers to accessing culturally relevant support services. 15 Similarly, Chinese American caregivers in qualitative research described their caregiving journey as “a lonely journey,” especially for those with language barriers. 16

Social isolation generally refers to having a small social network or infrequent social contact, whereas loneliness is the subjective feeling of being alone or socially disconnected despite one's actual network size. Social isolation and loneliness are well‐known risk factors for poor mental health among older Asian Americans. 17 , 18 Dementia caregiving is labor‐intensive and emotionally draining, which often leads caregivers to become isolated and hesitant to engage with friends or the community. 19 Internalized stigma may cause caregivers to withdraw from social interactions for fear of judgment, resulting in fewer social ties and a deepened sense of loneliness. The emotional toll of handling caregiving largely alone can manifest as profound loneliness. Culturally shaped stigma and fear of judgment can directly contribute to caregivers’ social disconnection, which might, in turn, lead to depression among caregivers. 20 Taken together, it is plausible that distress arising from affiliate stigma might cause increased social isolation and loneliness, ultimately leading to more depressive symptoms.

Cultural values and norms could influence how stigma affects mental health. Korean American caregivers have been noted to be especially reluctant to acknowledge caregiving challenges or use counseling services due to cultural stigma and fear of judgment. 3 , 21 Many downplay their difficulties and avoid discussing caregiving burdens, equating seeking help with personal failure. 12 , 21 , 22 By contrast, some Chinese American caregivers, particularly those more acculturated, appear more open to seeking help and discussing their situation. 11 These patterns suggest that stigma might exact a different social and emotional toll on Chinese versus Korean heritage caregivers. However, existing studies on such differences have been mostly qualitative and limited in scale (e.g., small interview studies), so quantitative evidence is sparse. It is possible that the same level of stigma could yield different outcomes among Chinese and Korean American caregivers, because of cultural attitudes and available support networks.

Existing literature indicates that affiliate stigma is prevalent among Asian American dementia caregivers and is associated with low quality of life and poorer mental health. However, little is known about the specific pathways through which stigma affects depressive symptoms in distinct ethnic groups or whether the association differs between Chinese and Korean Americans. In light of these gaps, this study examines the association between affiliate stigma and depressive symptoms among older Chinese and Korean American dementia caregivers and whether this association is mediated by social isolation and loneliness. We further test whether caregiver ethnicity moderates the relationship between affiliated stigma and depressive symptoms.

We hypothesized that higher affiliate stigma would be associated with more depressive symptoms (Hypothesis 1) and that this link would be mediated by increased social isolation and loneliness (Hypothesis 2). We also anticipated that ethnicity would moderate this relationship (Hypothesis 3), such that the strength of the association between stigma and depressive symptoms might differ for Chinese versus Korean American caregivers. METHODS

Participants

This study utilized baseline survey data from the (New York University Caregiver Intervention—Enhanced Support) (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05461495) project, an ongoing randomized controlled trial of a culturally tailored caregiver support intervention for Chinese and Korean American dementia caregivers. 13 The baseline sample included 338 older family caregivers recruited from the New York City (NYC) metropolitan area between May 2023 and September 2025. Participants were recruited by collaborating with community organizations serving Asian American adults and caregivers. At baseline, caregivers completed a comprehensive questionnaire interview (available in English, Chinese, or Korean) administered by trained bilingual clinical social workers. Eligible individuals were (1) primary family caregivers of a relative with memory problems; (2) self‐identified as Chinese or Korean; (3) age 50 or older; (4) at high risk of cardiometabolic symptoms, such as high cholesterol, diabetes, hypertension, obesity; and (5) living in the NYC metropolitan area and not planning to move in the next 12 months. Individuals with serious psychiatric conditions that would impede participation were excluded. All study procedures were approved by the university's Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. 23 The details of the parent trial are described elsewhere. 13

Measurements

Independent variable: affiliate stigma

Caregivers’ perceived stigma was measured using the modified 12‐item Affiliate Stigma Scale. 24 This scale assesses the degree to which caregivers feel shame, embarrassment, or concerns due to their affiliation with a family member who has dementia. Items cover domains of cognitive, affective, and behavioral stigma. Each item is typically rated on a seven‐point Likert scale, yielding a total score range of 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater stigma. This scale has been validated among diverse populations in the United States. 25 , 26 In this study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.75).

Dependent variable: depressive symptoms

Caregivers’ depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9), which assesses the frequency of depressive symptoms (e.g., depressed mood, sleep problems) over the past 2 weeks. 27 PHQ‐9 scores range from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting more severe depressive symptoms. PHQ‐9 is a well‐validated measure in diverse populations, including Chinese and Korean Americans. 28 , 29 In our sample, α = 0.87. We treated the PHQ‐9 score as a continuous outcome.

Mediators: social isolation and loneliness

Social isolation was assessed with the Lubben Social Network Scale‐6 (LSNS‐6), 30 which evaluates the frequency of the caregivers’ social contacts with friends and family. We reverse‐coded the LSNS‐6 so that higher scores indicate having a smaller social network (i.e., greater isolation), with scores ranging from 0 to 30. In our sample, α = 0.78. Note that in this study, “social isolation” refers to objective social network size/availability rather than subjective feelings. 31 Loneliness was measured using the five‐item UCLA Loneliness Scale. 32 Caregivers reported how often they felt a lack of companionship, left out, or isolated on a five‐point scale. Total scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness (α = 0.95).

Moderator: ethnicity

Caregiver self‐reported ethnicity was the dichotomous moderator, coded 1 = Chinese American and 0 = Korean American.

Covariates

Based on prior studies on stigma and mental health, 8 , 26 , 33 we included several covariates. Caregiver demographics included age (years), sex (female/male), education level (high school or less/more than high school), and annual household income. We also included English proficiency (self‐rated ability on a 1 to 4 scale), self‐rated health (1 = poor to 5 = excellent), and number of chronic medical conditions (e.g., heart disease, hypertension, lung disease, diabetes). Caregiving context variables included the care recipient's relationship to the caregiver (spouse/partner, parent, other), care recipient's dementia severity (Functional Assessment Staging Test stages grouped into early [stages 1 to 3], moderate [stage 4], and moderately severe‐severe [stages 5 to 7]), 34 and the care recipient's insurance status (Medicare only, Medicaid only, or both). Another one was the caregiver's level of upset over the care recipient's behavioral problems, measured by the Revised Memory and Behavior Problems Checklist (range 0 to 4, higher scores indicate greater caregiver upset by the patient's memory and behavioral symptoms). 35 Finally, we adjusted for years of caregiving and the caregiver's respite opportunities (number of days per week with 1+ hours off from caregiving).

Statistical analyses

We first computed descriptive statistics for all variables, overall and by ethnic group. Group differences were examined using t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables. We also assessed zero‐order Pearson correlations among the key variables (affiliate stigma, depressive symptoms, social isolation, and loneliness).

We used multiple‐variable linear regression models to examine the association between stigma and depressive symptoms (Hypothesis 1). To test Hypothesis 2 (mediation effects), we conducted separate mediation analyses (using the PROCESS macro) to examine each proposed mediator in the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms. In each model, affiliate stigma was the independent variable (X), and the variable of depressive symptoms served as the outcome (Y). Each mediation model estimated the total effect of X on Y, the direct effect of X on Y controlling for the mediator, and the indirect effect of X on Y through the mediator. We used bias‐corrected bootstrapping (5000 resamples) to compute 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects. All models were adjusted to all covariates described above, and regression coefficients in mediation models were standardized (β). 36

Furthermore, we tested whether caregiver ethnicity moderated the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms. Using PROCESS, we specified depressive symptoms as the outcome, stigma as the predictor, and ethnicity as the moderator (W). The key term was the interaction stigma × ethnicity. A significant interaction (p < 0.05) would suggest moderation. We probed a significant interaction by calculating simple regression slopes for each ethnic group. As with the mediation models, all continuous variables were standardized in the moderated regression. All analyses were performed using STATA MP 18.0 and SPSS 27 (with PROCESS version4.2), with a two‐tailed significance level of α = 0.05.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the sample characteristics by ethnicity. Caregivers had a mean age of 68.8 years (standard deviation [SD] = 9.4) and were predominantly female (82.2%). Chinese and Korean American caregivers were similar in age and gender distribution. However, Chinese caregivers had lower educational attainment, lower household income, and lower English proficiency than Korean American caregivers (all p < 0.001). Caregivers in both groups rated their own health on average as fair (2.8 on a five‐point scale), with no significant ethnic difference. Chinese caregivers reported more chronic health conditions than Korean caregivers (mean 3.6 vs 2.9, p < 0.001). Korean caregivers reported being more upset by the care recipient's memory and behavioral problems compared to Chinese caregivers (p < 0.001), but Korean caregivers also had more frequent respite opportunities (days off per week). On average, caregivers had been providing care for about 4 years; Chinese caregivers had a slightly longer caregiving duration than Koreans (mean 4.6 vs 3.2 years, p < 0.001). Table 2 displays the correlations among the four key variables. Affiliate stigma was positively and significantly correlated with depressive symptoms (r = 0.52, p < 0.001) and with loneliness (r = 0.54, p < 0.001). Loneliness was strongly positively correlated with depressive symptoms (r = 0.50, p < 0.001).

Mediation effects of social isolation and loneliness

Figure 2 summarizes the path coefficients for the mediation effects of social isolation and loneliness on the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms, controlling for all covariates. The details of all coefficients are provided in Table S1. In the model with social isolation as mediator (Figure 2A; Table S1 Model 2), stigma significantly predicted greater social isolation (β = 0.17; SE = 0.02; p < 0.001). In turn, higher social isolation was associated with higher depressive symptoms (path b: isolation → depressive symptoms; β = 1.30; SE = 0.42). Importantly, we found a significant indirect effect of affiliate stigma on depressive symptoms through social isolation. The direct effect of affiliate stigma on depressive symptoms remained significant even after accounting for isolation (β = 0.15; SE = 0.04), indicating partial mediation. The proportion of the total effect mediated by isolation was 12.8%.

Path diagrams of mediation effects of social isolation and loneliness.

In the model with loneliness as the mediator (Figure 2B; Table S1 Model 3), affiliate stigma had a strong positive effect on caregivers’ loneliness (β = 0.22; SE = 0.04; p < 0.001). Loneliness, in turn, was a robust predictor of depressive symptoms (β = 0.32; SE = 0.07; p < 0.001), such that lonelier caregivers exhibited higher depressive symptoms (p < 0.001). The indirect effect of stigma on depressive symptoms via loneliness was significant. In fact, the mediation through loneliness appeared to be a relatively strong pathway: the product of coefficients was 0.07, and 44.4% of the total effect of stigma on depressive symptoms was explained by loneliness.

Moderation effect of ethnicity

We next examined whether the association between stigma and depressive symptoms differed by ethnicity (Figure 3; Table S1 Model 4). In the moderated regression including the stigma × ethnicity interaction term and all covariates, we found a significant moderation effect by ethnicity on the stigma–depression relationship (β interaction = −0.22, SE = 0.07; p < 0.01), indicating that the slope of depressive symptoms on stigma was not the same for Chinese and Korean caregivers. We probed this interaction by calculating the simple regression slopes for each ethnic group. The results revealed that stigma had a stronger positive association with depressive symptoms among Korean caregivers compared to Chinese caregivers. Specifically, for Korean caregivers, higher stigma was strongly linked to higher depressive symptoms (simple slope β = +0.74, p < 0.001), whereas for Chinese caregivers, the relationship, though positive, was more modest in magnitude (simple slope β = +0.40, p < 0.01). DISCUSSION

In this study of older Chinese and Korean American dementia caregivers, we found that caregiving affiliate stigma was a significant risk factor for depressive symptoms, and much of its association was channeled through social isolation and loneliness. To our knowledge, this is among the first quantitative studies to delineate these mediation pathways in an Asian American dementia caregiving context and to compare two ethnic subgroups. The results highlight both commonalities and differences in how stigma operates for Chinese versus Korean American caregivers, yielding insights with important cultural and clinical implications.

Consistent with our hypotheses and prior research, higher stigma was associated with greater depressive symptoms in the overall sample. 5 , 6 , 8 , 33 This finding reinforces the notion that when caregivers internalize stigma – feeling ashamed, blaming themselves, or perceiving that others devalue them because of the care recipient's illness – their mental health suffers. 5 , 26 , 33 The significant association is consistent with caregiver studies in East Asia 6 , 8 , 33 and elsewhere. 5 , 26 Notably, even after accounting for a range of covariates (e.g., demographics, caregiving duration), stigma retained a direct association with depressive symptoms. This suggests that beyond the objective strains of caregiving (e.g., time demands, care recipients’ behavioral symptoms), the internal experience of stigma adds an independent burden on caregivers’ mental health. 37

Our mediation findings shed light on how social connections affect the pathways from affiliate stigma to depressive symptoms. We found that both objective and subjective social disconnection significantly mediated the link between stigma and depressive symptoms. In essence, caregivers with higher stigma were more likely to withdraw from their social networks and to feel profoundly lonely, 7 , 16 which in turn made them more vulnerable to depressive symptoms. This aligns with theoretical frameworks such as the Stress Process Model that stigma acts as a chronic psychosocial stressor that erodes social support and fosters alienation. 38 Our finding that loneliness carries a larger portion of the effect than social isolation highlights that subjective perceptions of relational loss may have greater psychological consequences than structural social networks alone. 39 Even if some caregivers maintain a nominal social network, stigma may prevent them from truly connecting or feeling understood, leading to loneliness. 7 , 26 Indeed, cultural stigma (like losing face) has been shown to directly heighten loneliness among Chinese American caregivers, 16 , 40 and a recent review identified social withdrawal as a key consequence of caregiver stigma leading to negative emotions and worse mental health outcomes. 5 Our quantitative results bolster these observations by demonstrating a measurable pathway from stigma to isolation/loneliness to depressive symptoms.

A notable finding of this study is that the relationship between stigma and depressive symptoms was more pronounced in the Korean American group compared with their Chinese American counterparts. Several cultural explanations may help interpret this difference. One possibility is that Korean culture, while sharing the value of filial piety with Chinese culture, imposes even stricter norms around not discussing family problems and maintaining a facade of strength. 13 , 15 Indeed, prior research noted that Korean Americans often experience a strong cultural stigma around caregiving burdens and mental health service use. 3 , 22 Korean caregivers frequently mentioned stigma and fear of judgment from their community, which made them reluctant to seek support. 12 Although Korean caregivers in our sample reported more frequent respite opportunities, this may reflect practical caregiving arrangements (e.g., assistance from other family members or community‐based services) rather than greater comfort in seeking psychological or social support. The underlying cultural stigma may still inhibit open sharing of distress, contributing to stronger emotional suppression and internal turmoil – and, in turn, a greater impact of stigma on depressive symptoms. 14 , 22 , 41 In contrast, Chinese American caregivers, while certainly not immune to stigma, might have had relatively more avenues for support or a slightly different attitude due to acculturation. 11 , 42 Our study's sample of Chinese Americans included some caregivers who had engaged with support organizations (e.g., CaringKind in NYC) and who expressed more willingness to “be thick‐skinned” and ask for help. Those caregivers had learned that involving family or friends and using available resources can benefit their relatives and themselves. 13 Such openness could buffer the negative effects of stigma.

Additionally, the larger Chinese American community and well‐established infrastructure in areas like NYC may facilitate more access to culturally sensitive services (e.g., Chinese‐language support groups, education workshops), potentially mitigating isolation for those who choose to utilize them. 42 In contrast, Korean American communities, while close‐knit, may have fewer dedicated services and might place a higher emphasis on enduring dementia caregiving burdens privately. 3 , 15

Our findings carry practical implications for healthcare providers, community organizations, and policymakers. First, screening for affiliate stigma in caregivers could be a valuable addition to routine assessments. Caregivers who endorse feelings of shame or concealment related to their role might be at higher risk for depressive symptoms and could be proactively offered tailored interventions. Second, interventions that address social isolation and loneliness should be integral to caregiver support programs. For example, establishing peer support groups where Chinese and Korean American caregivers can connect in their native languages and share experiences openly could directly target loneliness. 10 Our study underscores that culturally informed language and approach are not just matters of etiquette but can influence whether caregivers partake in interventions that reduce social disconnection. Third, addressing stigma directly via psychoeducation could be beneficial. Educating families and communities about dementia as a medical condition (not a moral failing) may reduce negative judgments and self‐blame. There is emerging evidence that brief anti‐stigma workshops for caregivers can improve knowledge and reduce feelings of shame. 43 Additionally, given the particularly strong effects observed in Korean caregivers, community leaders in Korean American settings (such as church pastors or Korean physicians) might be engaged to openly discuss dementia and caregiving to chip away at stigma. 22

Several limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional data limited our ability to draw causal conclusions among affiliate stigma, loneliness, and depressive symptoms, although our theoretical model and prior evidence lend plausibility to this pathway. Second, all measures were based on self‐report questionnaires. There may be response biases, especially given the sensitive nature of stigma and mental health in these cultures. Some caregivers might underreport stigma or depressive symptoms due to social desirability, which could attenuate observed relationships. Third, our sample, while sizeable and drawn from community settings, was regionally focused and may not generalize to all Chinese or Korean American caregivers elsewhere. NYC has unique immigrant community dynamics and resources; caregivers in more isolated areas might experience even greater isolation, or those in different generational cohorts might have different attitudes. Fourth, our Chinese American sample was culturally heterogeneous (e.g., including immigrants from mainland China vs Hong Kong vs Taiwan), which we did not differentiate. Cultural heterogeneity within “Chinese” groups could influence experiences of stigma and support.

CONCLUSION

Caregiving affiliate stigma has a detrimental impact on the mental health of Chinese and Korean American dementia caregivers, but its effects may not be immutable. Through culturally sensitive interventions that build supportive social networks and normalize seeking help, we can alleviate stigma's effects. Efforts to promote “connection over isolation” and to replace shame with solidarity are key to enhancing the mental health and resilience of these caregivers. The present findings underscore an urgent message: tackling stigma and loneliness among dementia caregivers is not only a matter of improving quality of life, but it is also a strategy to prevent depressive symptoms and support caregivers, particularly in culturally diverse aging societies.
