Predictors of depression among farmers in rural: A community-based study in South India
Authors: Reenaa Mohan, Candy Friola, DS Karthiga Devi, R Sindhuri, Archana Subbaraj, V Sravanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Farmers, particularly in rural areas, face a variety of unique stressors that place them at a heightened risk for depression.Methods:A community-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted for 6 months among farmers who are residing in the field practice area of Rural Health Training Centre. Consecutive sampling was applied. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in their house using a predesigned structured questionnaire after obtaining consent. Data were entered in Epi info software version 7, and analysis was done in SPSS version 21.Results:Among 212 farmers, it was found that the majority of participants (29.2%) were in the age group of 31–40 years. Females 71.2% were predominant, and 54.2% were illiterates. It was found 58% had minimal depression, and 36% had mild depression. The odds of occurrence of mild to moderate depression was found to be 5.62 (95% CI 1.08–6.37) times higher among farmers with comorbidities when compared to farmers without comorbidities, and there was 4.90 (95% CI 1.84–14.31) times higher odds of developing mild to moderate depression among farmers with addictions when compared to those without addictions. The Nagelkerke R2 value for the model is 24.3%.Conclusion:Addictions and comorbidities were found to be significant predictors of mild to moderate depression, highlighting the need for targeted interventions.

Background

Depression is a prevalent mental health condition that significantly affects emotional wellbeing, social interactions, and work performance. Among farmers, especially those in rural settings, the risk of depression is amplified due to a combination of unique stressors. Economic instability caused by fluctuating market prices, the unpredictability of climate and weather patterns, the physically taxing nature of agricultural work, and the isolation inherent to rural living all contribute to heightened vulnerability.[1] Studies indicate that farmers often report higher levels of depression compared to other occupational groups, underscoring the profound impact on their personal health and the productivity of their farms.[234]

In rural India, this issue is particularly pressing, given the limited access to mental health services and widespread lack of awareness about mental health conditions. Even though the Indian government has introduced a series of initiatives to address the growing concern of depression among farmers, recognizing the deep impact of economic hardship, climate-related stress, and rural isolation on their mental health schemes like Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-Kisan) scheme, farmers face many challenges.

Depression among farmers is a critical public health concern in these areas, where stigma around mental illness often prevents individuals from seeking help. The consequences extend beyond personal suffering, affecting families, communities, and the broader agricultural sector.[235] Addressing this issue requires targeted interventions, including increasing mental health resources, fostering community support networks, and implementing policies that reduce economic and environmental stressors. Promoting awareness and reducing stigma are equally essential to empower farmers to seek the help they need.[67] With this background, this study was conducted to find the prevalence of depression among farmers and to identify factors associated with depression.

Material and Methods

Study settings and design

A community-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted over 6 months (October 2022 to April 2023) by the Department of Community Medicine (DCM), Sri Manakula Vinayagar Medical College and Hospital (SMVMCH), in the rural field practices area covered by Rural Health Training Centre (RHTC). The primary occupation of the participants is agriculture.

Sample size and sampling

Based on the prevalence of depression among farmers in a study conducted by Patil Aet al.,[8] it was found to be 33.9%. With 6.5% absolute precision, at 95% confidence interval (CI), the sample size was calculated to be 204. With 10% non-response rate, the sample size was calculated to be 212.

Data collection procedure

After obtaining informed written consent, a predesigned structured questionnaire was used. Face-to-face interviews were conducted among farmers in field practice areas during their free time in their house and in the field area. Privacy and confidentiality were maintained. The principal investigator was trained and clarified the questions to the participant throughout the interview. Sociodemographic details, comorbidities, and addiction were also collected from all participants using pretested structured questionnaires. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scale was used to assess Depression. All ethical principles were followed throughout the study (Approval number: EC/15/2021).

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were entered in Epi info software version 7 developed by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in Atlanta, Georgia (US) and were analyzed in Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21 developed by IBM for data management. Discrete variables such as depression, sociodemographic variables, and reasons for depression were expressed as frequency and percentages. Logistic regression was done to find out the factors associated for depression among farmers.

Results

Out of 212, the majority of the participants, 62 (29.2%) and 56 (26.4%), were in the age group of 31–40 years and 41–50 years. Females (71.2%) were predominant among the participants. More than half of the participants (54.2%) were illiterates. Three fourth of the participants lived in the nuclear family. More than 90% of the participants belonged to Hindu religion and were married. Of 212 participants, 79.7% belonged to class V Socio Economic status [Table 1].

Binary logistic regression was performed to find out the predictors of mild to moderate depression among farmers. Out of nine variables (age, gender, education, type of family, religion, marital status, SES, comorbidities, and addictions) entered in the model, only two variables, presence of addictions and comorbidities, were found to be the significant predictors for mild to moderate depression after adjusting for other confounding factors.

The odds of occurrence of mild to moderate depression was found to be 5.62 (95% CI 1.08–6.37) times higher among farmers with comorbidities when compared to farmers without comorbidities, and there was 4.90 (95% CI 1.84–14.31) times higher odds of developing mild to moderate depression among farmers with addictions when compared to those without addictions. The Nagelkerke R2 value for the model is 24.3% [Table 3].

Discussion

In terms of sociodemographic characteristics, the age distribution of our participants aligns with studies from other rural areas, where farmers are commonly middle-aged, with a higher proportion aged between 31 and 50 years. The predominance of female participants (71.7%) in this study contrasts with many studies on farmers’ mental health, where male participants are typically the majority.[24] This could reflect regional gender dynamics in farming in Puducherry or sampling differences. Over half (54.2%) of participants were illiterate, and nearly 80% belonged to the lowest socioeconomic class, highlighting economic vulnerabilities that have been associated with higher risks of depression in agricultural communities.[56]

The prevalence of depression in this study indicates that a majority of farmers (58%) had minimal depression, while 36% had mild and 6% had moderate depression. These findings are consistent with previous studies from India, which report a high prevalence of mild to moderate depressive symptoms among farmers.[7] This trend may be due to chronic financial stress, inadequate social support, and limited access to mental health care, all factors known to influence mental health in rural farming communities. Comparatively, a study conducted in Maharashtra found similar rates, with 34% of farmers experiencing mild depression, highlighting a common pattern across rural India.[8]

The association of comorbidities and addictions with depression among farmers is also notable. Farmers with comorbid conditions, such as diabetes or hypertension, had a 5.62 times higher likelihood of experiencing mild to moderate depression than those without. This finding aligns with studies showing that chronic illness can amplify depressive symptoms due to the additional physical and financial burdens it imposes.[9] Additionally, addiction behaviors, particularly alcohol and betel nut chewing, were significant predictors of depression, with a 4.90 times higher likelihood of mild to moderate depression in those with addictions compared to those without. This association is well documented in the literature as substance use is often used as a coping mechanism but can exacerbate mental health issues.[10]

While our findings are largely consistent with existing research, they emphasize unique sociocultural factors in rural Puducherry, such as the high prevalence of nuclear families and the predominance of female farmers. These factors might influence social support networks and access to mental health resources when participants reported past addictions or medical history and the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias in the study. For instance, nuclear family structures in rural areas may limit social support, which has been shown to have a protective effect against depression.[11] Further, the lack of access to higher education and economic resources among participants indicates that mental health interventions in such areas need to be both accessible and tailored to low-income, low-literacy populations.[12]

Conclusion

This study highlights the urgent need for mental health resources and support services tailored to rural farming communities, especially in regions like Puducherry. Interventions should focus on managing health conditions and providing resources for addiction cessation as these factors significantly impact mental health outcomes. Addressing these areas could improve the quality of life for farmers and support a healthier rural economy.
