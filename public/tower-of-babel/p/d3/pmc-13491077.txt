Chinese Population-Wide Lipid Trajectories From Age 20 to 85 Years
Authors: Yanyan Zhang, Yijin Pei, Yanzhi Yan, Mengyao Liu, Fangchao Liu, Keyong Huang, Shufeng Chen, Jie Cao, Chong Shen, Xiaoqing Liu, Ling Yu, Zhengyuan Zhou, Liancheng Zhao, Dongsheng Hu, Jianfeng Huang, Dongfeng Gu, Tong Wang, Jianxin Li, Xiangfeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting studies focused on the association of lipid trajectory classifications with cardiovascular outcomes, whereas the natural life-course pattern and sex-specific modulation by obesity are poorly characterized.ObjectivesThis study sought to characterize the sex-specific lipid trajectories and examine the role of obesity indicators in shaping them.MethodsIn a longitudinal cohort of 83,487 Chinese adults (median follow-up: 11.00 years; Q1-Q3:6.80-11.72) with 2-4 repeated lipid measurements, we applied generalized additive mixed model to characterize age-related lipid trajectories and assess modifications by obesity indicators (body mass index and waist circumference).ResultsTotal cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) exhibited 3-phase trajectory (increase, plateau, slight decrease), whereas triglyceride and remnant cholesterol showed 2-phase (increase, decrease). Female subjects showed delayed trajectory transitions (13-17 years) and more adverse late-adulthood lipid profiles than male subjects did. Obesity indicators were positively associated with elevated lipid levels (all P < 0.001), with stronger association in male subjects than in female subjects (eg, per 1-SD body mass index increase, β-coefficient for TC: 3.43 [95% CI: 3.15-3.71] vs 2.71 [95% CI: 2.48-2.94] mg/dL, respectively; Pinteraction < 0.001). Notably, the lipid-lowering associations of adiposity reduction attenuated with age in male subjects, whereas in female subjects they remained stable for triglyceride and remnant cholesterol (eg, the mean differences between obese and non-obese groups defined by waist circumference 28.96 [95% CI: 23.13-32.14] and 3.19 [95% CI: 2.72-3.66] mg/dL) or showed “L-shape” pattern with inflection around age 40 for TC, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and non-HDL-C.ConclusionsOur findings highlight sex-specific impact of obesity on lifelong lipid trajectories, suggesting sex- and age-specific strategies for early lipid management and obesity intervention.Key Words: lipid, obesity, sex difference, trajectory

Blood lipids are the primary contributor of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases and global disease burden.1 High low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) accounted for 87.7 million disability-adjusted life-years worldwide in 2021, reflecting a 27% increase since 2000.2 Although the lipid levels have been reported to decline in Western countries,3 they continue to increase in China,4 amplifying the cardiovascular disease burden.

Blood lipid profiles varied by sex across the life course.5 Cross-sectional studies6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 have depicted age-specific lipid distributions in both sexes, with male subjects displaying distinct patterns from female subjects. However, findings regarding lipid level changes at different ages are inconsistent. This inconsistency may stem from the inherent limitations of cross-sectional designs, which cannot capture dynamic within-individual changes over time, underscoring the need for longitudinal analyses. Existing longitudinal research has predominantly focused on identifying trajectory subgroups (eg, stable-high, increasing, decreasing) and linking them with clinical outcomes.13, 14, 15 A study16 noted sex differences in the rate of lipid changes across the lifespan, yet comprehensive, population-based analyses describing continuous lipid trajectories over time in both sexes are scarce.17 This knowledge gap hampers refinement of guideline-based lipid thresholds, which currently lack age and sex specificity.18 The European Atherosclerosis Society recently has called for greater attention to sex-specific lipid patterns throughout the life course to refine preventive strategies.19 Therefore, characterizing the age-related lipid trajectories in both sexes is of utmost importance, which may help prompt early initiation of personalized interventions and treatments.

In parallel, obesity is a well-established risk factor of dyslipidemia, and effective weight management has been shown to optimize lipid levels.20 Previous meta-analysis of randomized trials21,22 suggested that every 10-kg weight loss may reduce cholesterol levels by 5%-10%. Associations between obesity and lipid profiles also show sex-specific differences. Whereas some cross-sectional studies suggested stronger associations of body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) with total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) in female subjects,23,24 cohort and Mendelian randomization studies25, 26, 27 reported stronger predictive value in male subjects. These inconsistencies likely reflect methodological limitations, such as reliance on single time-point assessments or limited obesity metrics. Most existing studies were limited by short follow-up periods, small sample sizes, isolated assessment of obesity indices (BMI or WC), and lack of integrative models to evaluate sex interactions.

To address these gaps, we leveraged data from a large-scale, long-term Chinese cohort to achieve 2 objectives: 1) to characterize the natural, continuous trajectories of 6 lipid markers (TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, non-HDL-C, remnant cholesterol [RC]) across adulthood in both sexes; and 2) to assess how these trajectories are shaped by obesity indicators (BMI, WC) in a sex- and age-specific manner.

Methods

Study population

Participants were derived from the China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) study, which includes the China MUCA (China Multi-Center Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Epidemiology), initiated in 1998, the InterASIA (International Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Disease in Asia), conducted from 2000 to 2001, and the CIMIC (Community Intervention of Metabolic Syndrome in China & Chinese Family Health) study, conducted from 2007 to 2008. The China MUCA and InterASIA studies were followed up once during 2007-2008. Subsequently, all 3 subcohorts were each followed up in 2012-2015 and 2018-2021. Detailed descriptions of the study population and methods have been published elsewhere.16,28 This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Fuwai Hospital in Beijing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Among 113,448 participants aged 18 years or older, 109,156 participants were followed up successfully. After excluding participants with major chronic disease (myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cancer) at baseline (n = 3,103), those with missing baseline lipid data (n = 5,177), and those without any available lipid measurements at all follow-up surveys (n = 17,389), a total of 83,487 participants with 217,239 measurements were included in the final analysis (Supplemental Figure 1).

Measurements

According to the standard protocols and questionnaires, all measurements were collected at each visit to capture dynamic changes.

Fasting blood samples (10 hours) were collected to assess glucose and lipid levels. The TC, TG, and HDL-C were measured in the laboratory that participated in the Lipid Standardization Program of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The LDL-C level was estimated using Martin/Hopkins method and its extended equation as previously described.29,30 Non-HDL-C was calculated as TC minus HDL-C. RC was calculated as TC minus HDL-C minus LDL-C, which has been used in many previously published studies.13,31 In individuals on lipid-lowering medication, TC values were divided by 0.8.32

Trained investigators conducted baseline and follow-up surveys to collect demographic information (age, sex, region, area, and education level), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity, and diet), and medications. Smoking was defined as having smoked ≥400 cigarettes, 500 g tobacco leaves, or 1 cigarette every day for 1 year or more. Alcohol drinking referred to consuming alcohol at least once per week during the past year. A modified diet score was defined according to previous article33 (≥500 g/d fruits and vegetables, ≥200 g/wk of fish, ≥125 g/d of soybean products, <75 g/d of red meat, and ≥50 g/mo of tea). Individuals who had ideal physical activity were those with ≥150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity, ≥75 min/wk of vigorous physical activity, or ≥150 min/wk of both. In addition, we collected female-specific data on the menopausal transition (ie, age at which periods had stopped).

Height and weight were measured in barefoot participants wearing light clothes. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. WC was measured at 1 cm above the navel. The elevated obesity indicators were defined as BMI ≥23 kg/m2,34 or male/female subjects with WC ≥90/80 cm,35 according to World Health Organization–recommended Asian-specific cutoffs. Blood pressure was collected 3 times after a 5-minute rest in seated position to obtain the average level.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study participants were presented as mean ± SD or median (Q1-Q3) for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables, respectively. Sex-specific characteristics were compared using Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon test, or chi-square test.

Considering the potential nonlinear relationship of lipid trajectories with age and the uneven intervals of repeated lipid measures, we applied the generalized additive mixed model to estimate the lipid trajectories over the lifespan stratified by sex. This stratified modeling strategy inherently assesses potential interactions. The model does not require equally spaced or complete observations for all subjects, allowing for different distributions of variables. We specify lipid levels as a smooth and nonlinear function of age. The parameters of the smoothing curve were estimated by using the restricted maximum likelihood and penalized regression spline. Repeated lipid measurements were used to fit the generalized additive mixed model models, with age as a fixed effect and participants’ identity number as random effects. The models were stratified by sex and adjusted for area (urban/rural), region (northern/southern China), subcohorts, and time-varying variables, including survey year, education level (less or more than high school), smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity (ideal or not), diet score (<2 or ≥2), BMI, glucose, and systolic blood pressure. We employed the random forest method to perform multivariate imputation by chained equations,36 which was used to impute missing data for covariates by sex. Detailed missing data handling and multiple imputation, covariates selecting, model fitting, diagnostics, and validation were provided in the Supplemental Appendix (Supplemental Methods, Supplemental Tables 1 to 5, Supplemental Figures 2 to 5). The inflection points in lipid trajectories were identified through derivative analysis, with the derivative of 0 indicating changes in the trend direction. We further estimated the sex differences and the 95% empirical Bayesian simultaneous CIs of the fitted lipid trajectories. The 95% CI without covering 0 indicated a significant sex difference.37 The crossing age was defined as the point at which the sex difference equaled 0, indicating that male and female subjects experienced the same lipid levels. A sensitivity analysis was conducted among participants without taking lipid-lowering medication. Additional sensitivity analyses were presented in the Supplemental Appendix. These included restricting the analysis to a subpopulation within a specific age range with adequate sample size; adjusting for dietary factors (diet score and its components); using a complete-case data set (excluding participants with any missing values); and adjusting for birth cohort.

Multivariable sex-specific linear mixed-effect models were used to evaluate the association of time-varying obesity indicators (BMI, WC) with repeated lipid measurements. Adjustment variables included age, area, region, subcohorts, survey year, education level, smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity, diet score, glucose, and systolic blood pressure. Obesity indicators were standardized using z-scores to facilitate the comparison of effect sizes across different measurement units, with effect size (β coefficients) representing the change in lipid levels for per-SD increase in obesity indicators. Pairwise comparisons were made between each combination of the 2 obesity indicators. A subgroup analysis was performed stratified by the crossing age in both sexes to investigate potential age modifications, which were tested by including an additional interaction term in the models. To further explore the impact of obesity indicators on lipid trajectories across the lifespan, we depicted the longitudinal lipid trajectories in different BMI and WC groups by sex and compared the mean differences between high and low obesity groups. Considering the potential skewness distribution of TG, we performed a sensitivity analysis by log-transforming TG.

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2, R Foundation) and SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Inc) software. A 2-side P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study population

During a median follow-up of 11.00 (Q1-Q3: 6.80-11.72) years, participants contributed a median of 3 (Q1-Q3: 2-3) lipid measurements. Sex-specific distributions are detailed in Supplemental Table 6. Among 83,487 individuals, the mean age was 50.99 (SD: 11.70) years, with 32,822 (39.31%) being male. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1 by sex. Male subjects, compared to female ones, were more likely to be well educated, smoke, drink alcohol, and have higher levels of RC, while exhibiting lower levels of TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and non-HDL-C (P < 0.05). Statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between included and excluded participants, as detailed in Supplemental Table 7. However, most differences were not clinically meaningful.

Sex-specific lipid trajectories with age

TC level in male subjects dramatically increased from 141.56 (95% CI: 140.27-145.24) mg/dL at age 18 years to 175.04 (95% CI: 174.39-175.70) mg/dL at age 50. Subsequently, a plateau existed until 71 years old, followed by a slight decline (Figure 1). In female subjects, TC levels also rose substantially from 149.27 (95% CI: 146.05-152.49) mg/dL at age 18 years to 187.91 (95% CI: 187.45-188.38) mg/dL at age 64, followed by a plateau until age 80 and a subsequent decline. However, male subjects reached their plateau 14 years earlier than female subjects did. Similar patterns were noticed for LDL-C and non-HDL-C, with the plateau presenting 13 years (age: 51 vs 64 years) and 16 years (age: 46 vs 62 years) earlier in male subjects than those in female subjects. Notably, an inverse U-shape trajectory was observed for both TG and RC, with peak levels at age 42 years (150.20 mg/dL; 95% CI: 148.24-152.16 mg/dL) and 44 years (25.99 mg/dL; 95% CI: 25.72-26.26 mg/dL) in male subjects, and at age 58 years (141.53 mg/dL; 95% CI: 138.57-144.49 mg/dL) and 61 years (26.88 mg/dL; 95% CI: 26.71-27.06 mg/dL) in female subjects, respectively. Conversely, HDL-C levels showed a gradually upward trajectory in both sexes (Figure 1).

We further performed pairwise comparisons to visualize the sex differences in lipid trajectories (Supplemental Figure 6). For TC, TG, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and RC, the differences between male and female subjects increased with age, peaking at approximate age 36 years, and then gradually decreased and reached 0 at ages 46, 48, 49, 52, and 54 for TC, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, RC, and TG, respectively. After the crossing age, lipid levels in female subjects exceeded those in male subjects, with the difference amplifying with age. Notably, this crossing age closely matched the average age of menopause in our cohort (50 ± 3 years), suggesting a potential link to the menopausal transition. Interestingly, the maximum fluctuation in sex discrepancy was shown in TG (ranging from −30.51 [95% CI: −33.16 to −27.85] mg/dL to 20.72 [95% CI: 14.72-26.71] mg/dL), followed by TC, non-HDL-C, LDL-C, and RC. In contrast, HDL-C levels (mean difference: 3.47 mg/dL) were consistently higher in female than male subjects across their lifespan. Results remained unchanged when restricted to participants not receiving lipid-lowering treatment, included population within a specific age range with adequate sample size, adjusting for dietary factors using both the diet score and its components, using a complete-case data set (excluding participants with any missing values), and adjusting for birth cohort (Supplemental Figure 7-11, Supplemental Table 8).

Sex-specific relationship between obesity indicators and lipid trajectories

Considering obesity as an important risk factor of lipid levels, we estimated sex-specific associations between obesity and lipid levels in Table 2. After multivariable adjustment, obesity indicators were significantly associated with lipid levels (all P < 0.001), with stronger associations observed in male than female subjects. For TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, non-HDL-C, and RC, each 1-SD increment in BMI corresponded to a 3.43 (95% CI: 3.15-3.71) mg/dL, 22.75 (95% CI: 21.97-23.54) mg/dL, 3.82 (95% CI: 3.59-4.05) mg/dL, −3.55 (95% CI: −3.66 to −3.43) mg/dL, 6.96 (95% CI: 6.69-7.23) mg/dL, and 2.72 (95% CI: 2.63-2.81) mg/dL change in male subjects, respectively, whereas the corresponding values were 2.71 (95% CI: 2.48-2.94) mg/dL, 16.20 (95% CI: 15.62-16.79) mg/dL, 3.03 (95% CI: 2.84-3.22) mg/dL, −2.70 (95% CI: −2.80 to −2.61) mg/dL, 5.41 (95% CI: 5.19-5.63) mg/dL, and 1.98 (95% CI: 1.91-2.35) mg/dL in female subjects, respectively (Pinteraction < 0.001). The β-coefficients for the association between a 1-SD increment in WC and TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, non-HDL-C, and RC were 3.05 (95% CI: 2.77-3.33) mg/dL, 25.70 (95% CI: 24.91-26.91) mg/dL, 3.24 (95% CI: 3.00-3.47) mg/dL, −3.69 (95% CI: −3.80 to −3.57) mg/dL, 6.73 (95% CI: 6.46-7.00) mg/dL, 3.02 (95% CI: 2.93-3.12) mg/dL in male subjects and 2.17 (95% CI: 1.94-2.40) mg/dL, 19.24 (95% CI: 18.65-19.83) mg/dL, 2.39 (95% CI: 2.20-2.59) mg/dL, −3.06 (95% CI: −3.15 to −2.96) mg/dL, 5.23 (95% CI: 5.00-5.45) mg/dL, 2.28 (95% CI: 2.21-2.35) mg/dL in female subjects, respectively (Pinteraction < 0.001). Stronger associations of obesity indicators with lipid levels were also observed in male than in female subjects, especially before the crossing age. Sex-specific lipid trajectories in different obesity indicator groups

The sex difference in the lipid trajectories across different obesity indicator groups are shown in Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 12. Compared to participants with lower WC (male subjects: <90 cm; female subjects: <80 cm), those with higher WC shared similar trajectories but higher lipid levels, regardless of sex. Of particular note, significant sex heterogeneity was observed in the lipid differences across WC groups. In male subjects, the lipid differences between WC groups from ages 18-85 years declined from 30.26 (95% CI: 21.94-38.57) mg/dL to −3.38 (95% CI: −7.06 to 0.30) mg/dL for TC, from 94.15 (95% CI: 72.18-112.52) mg/dL to 8.94 (95% CI: −0.91 to 18.79) mg/dL for TG, from 25.71 (95% CI: 20.11-31.30) mg/dL to −0.60 (95% CI: −2.38 to 1.17) mg/dL for LDL-C, from 37.24 (95% CI: 30.14-44.32) mg/dL to 0.91 (95% CI: −2.67 to 4.30) mg/dL for non-HDL-C, from 11.22 (95% CI: 8.67-13.77) mg/dL to 0.88 (95% CI: −0.27 to 2.04) mg/dL for RC, but increased from −6.20 (95% CI: −7.59 to −4.81) mg/dL to −3.93 (95% CI: −4.75 to −3.10) mg/dL for HDL-C. However, female subjects exhibited relatively stable trends for TG (mean difference: 28.96 [95% CI: 23.13-32.14] mg/dL) and RC (mean difference: 3.19 [95% CI: 2.72-3.66] mg/dL), or approximate L-shape trends for TC, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C, with the inflection points at around age 40 years. Specifically, the differences for TC declined from 23.76 (95% CI: 16.24-31.29) mg/dL at age 18 years to 2.76 (95% CI: 1.82-3.70) mg/dL at age 40, and then plateaued at approximately 2.36 (95% CI: 1.383.35) mg/dL afterward. LDL-C differences similarly decreased from 20.01 (95% CI: 13.99-26.02) mg/dL at age 18 years to 4.01 (95% CI: 3.23-4.78) mg/dL at age 40, and then stabilizing around 3.01 (95% CI: 2.23-3.85) mg/dL thereafter. For HDL-C, the differences fell from −0.91 (95% CI: −3.38 to 1.57) mg/dL at age 18 years to −4.61 (95% CI: −4.98 to −4.29) mg/dL at age 40, and then stabilized at about −4.19 (95% CI: −4.53 to −3.86) mg/dL in later adulthood. For non-HDL-C, the differences dropped from 23.81 (95% CI: 16.69-30.92) mg/dL at age 18 years to 4.20 (95% CI: 1.43-6.98) mg/dL at age 40, and then remained at roughly 6.64 (95% CI: 5.70-7.58) mg/dL in later adulthood (Figure 2). Similar trends were observed for BMI (Supplemental Figure 12).

Discussion

Based on this large-scale longitudinal cohort, we identified distinct, nonlinear lipid trajectories across the adult lifespan, with clear sex differences and age-specific transition points. We quantified the impact of obesity indicators on lipid trajectories and found that the benefits of reduced adiposity on lipid levels significantly decreased with age in male subjects, but female subjects exhibited a stable or “L-shape” trend. These findings hold important implications for individualized lipid management and obesity prevention strategies (Central Illustration).

Chinese Population-Wide Lipid Trajectories: Sex- and Adiposity-Specific Patterns

The China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) study recruited 83,487 participants (32,822 male subjects and 50,665 female subjects) with repeated measurements of lipid profiles between 1998 and 2021. The analysis tracked trajectories of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), non-HDL-C, and remnant cholesterol (RC), in relation to body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC). Several distinct phases of lipid trajectories were identified across lifespan, with less favorable lipid profiles in male subjects before middle-age and in female subjects thereafter. Sex-specific lipid trajectories diverged by adiposity status; and the reduction in lipids attributable to lower adiposity over the life course attenuated with age.

Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on classifying lipid trajectory subgroups to predict clinical outcomes, our study uniquely depicts the natural course of lipid levels in real-world populations, we revealed that lipid trajectories followed consistent age-related phases—3 stages for TC, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C (increase, plateau, and slight decline) and 2 for TG and RC (increase followed by decrease). This pattern of age-related phases was also observed in previous cross-sectional studies. These studies indicated that the pattern is not unique to Asian populations;38, 39, 40 European or Western-ancestry population have also reported consistent results.7,41 This suggests that the fundamental biological processes of lipid metabolism across the lifespan may be universal across human population. Although the shape of the trajectories is conserved, quantitative differences in lipid levels exist across ethnicities. These disparities may be driven by a complex interplay of genetic background, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and distinct anthropometric characteristics (such as the higher visceral adiposity at a lower BMI) in Asian populations.42 The initial increase phase may be attributed to enhanced lipid accumulation, reduced rate of lipid clearance, and decreased lipoprotein lipase activity with age.43 The subsequent plateau or decrease phase typically began around the age of 40 years, at which point the rate of lipid change slowed,16 potentially due to the emergence of dysregulated molecules related to lipid metabolism and microbiome imbalances.44 Whereas the precise mechanisms remain unclear, the alterations in metabolic homeostasis, such as basal metabolism, liver sinusoidal endothelium, insulin resistance, hormone levels, and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors are thought to play a role.45 Identifying this early “window period” before metabolic inflection may offer a critical opportunity for primordial prevention.

Our findings illustrate a significant delay in lipid trajectory transitions among female subjects, who maintained rising lipid levels up to 13-17 years longer than male subjects did. Previous cross-sectional studies have documented sex differences in lipid profiles across age groups.6, 7, 8, 9 However, evidence from a single measurement is limited in capturing longitudinal changes and fails to account for the influence of lifestyle and environment transitions.46,47 Our study confirmed and quantified these patterns in a longitudinal context. Male subjects exhibited higher lipid levels than female subjects at the younger age, which may be partly attributed to a higher prevalence of unhealthy lifestyle habits among male subjects.48 Conversely, female subjects experienced more pronounced adverse lipid profiles following menopause, likely driven by declines in estrogen and associated metabolic regulation.49, 50, 51 Our findings provide age-specific lipid reference levels for both sexes and underscore the need for early lipid monitoring and timely intervention in female subjects approaching midlife.

We quantified how adiposity indicators modified lipid trajectories across age and sex. Using Asian-specific cutoffs of BMI and WC, our study demonstrated that these associations occur even at lower adiposity thresholds than those typically reported in Western populations, indicating heightened susceptibility in Asians. Obesity had a more pronounced impact on lipid levels in male subjects, particularly before the sex-specific crossing age, after which the benefits of adiposity reduction attenuated significantly. In contrast, these benefits in female subjects sharply declined before age 40 years and remained consistently low thereafter. As individuals age, the shrinking of adipose tissue, fat redistribution, and tissue dysfunction may collectively reduce the benefits of weight loss.52 Moreover, the increased chronic inflammation, fluctuating hormone levels, and multiple risk factors in the elderly population may further contribute to this decline.53,54 The sharp reduction in benefits for female subjects during their reproductive years could be attributed to their unhealthy lifestyle patterns, including overnutrition and physical inactivity during and after pregnancy.55 Therefore, our study highlights that early and proactive management of even modest weight gain is imperative for Asian populations and the importance of early obesity management in male subjects and sustained management throughout the life course in female subjects.

This study has several strengths. First, it used data from the ChinaPAR project, a prospective cohort study based on a large Chinese population with a prolonged follow-up period and high-quality outcome assessments. Second, this study used a longitudinal cohort for the first time to depict the sex-specific trajectories of 6 blood lipid profiles in Chinese adults, identifying the critical window period for lipid control in male and female subjects. Third, available repeated measurements of multiple risk factors allowed for the dynamic adjustment over time, providing more robust estimates of their age-dependent associations.

Study limitations

First, although excluding individuals with major chronic diseases at baseline helped reduce confounding, it limits generalizability to populations under medical management. Second, the extended follow-up period may introduce secular time period effects associated with rapid societal and health care changes in China, and the nonuniform follow-up intervals across participants could increase uncertainty in trajectory estimation. However, sensitivity analyses adjusting for birth cohort yielded largely consistent results, and the generalized additive mixed model framework’s capacity to handle unbalanced data, together with clustered survey waves and the large sample size, mitigates these concerns. Third, because our study focused on adult populations, we were unable to capture lipid trajectory characteristics before adulthood. Fourth, although the sample size in the very young and old age groups was relatively small, resulting in wider 95% CIs for the lipid trajectories, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the age range with adequate sample size yielded consistent findings, confirming the robustness of our primary results. Fifth, although we comprehensively adjusted for multiple potential confounders in our analyses, residual confounding may persist due to uncollected variables such as contraceptive use, reproductive history, hormone replacement therapy, which could theoretically influence the outcomes. Furthermore, although we used BMI and WC as key exposure variables, we lacked direct imaging measures of body fat distribution (eg, visceral adipose tissue volume). These issues require future studies. Finally, the robust and diverse nature of our cohort enhances the generalizability of our findings, which we believe are broadly representative of the Han Chinese. However, the extrapolation of these results to other regions and ethnicities requires careful consideration.

Conclusions

This study delineates sex-specific lipid trajectory patterns, and identifies distinct phases for lipid changes across lifespan. We highlighted the menopausal period as a critical window during which lipid levels in female subjects exceeded those in male subjects. We also quantify the impact of obesity indicators on lipid trajectories, revealing that the lipid-lowering benefit of reduced adiposity attenuated with age in male subjects but followed a relatively stable or “L-shape” pattern in female subjects, highlighting the importance of early adiposity interventions. Our findings provide valuable insights for optimizing lipid management tailored to both sexes.
