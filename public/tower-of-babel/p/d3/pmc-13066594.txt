Anthraquinones derived from soil actinomycetes combat multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Chenchen Wang, Xiaodan Li, Ziyi Zhang, Di Liu, Zhaoran Zhang, Wei Fang, Shaoyong Ke, Chen Tan, Zhaoyuan Wu, Manli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global spread of multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) poses serious threats to public health and food safety. In this study, the antibacterial potential of the anthraquinone compound 13394-2, derived from soil actinomycetes, was systematically evaluated. In vitro, 13394-2 exhibited potent activity (MIC as low as 0.25 μg/mL), rapid bactericidal effects, biofilm clearance, and concentration-dependent killing. Mechanistic studies revealed that membrane disruption, ROS accumulation, and ATP depletion are key bactericidal pathways leading to metabolic collapse. Transcriptomic, computational analyses and biophysical experiments revealed significant interference with protein, energy, and fatty acid metabolism, with FabF identified as a potential target. Toxicity assessments confirmed low cytotoxicity and good in vivo safety. In animal infection models, 13394-2 improved survival, reduced bacterial burden and tissue damage, and promoted wound healing. It also effectively eliminated drug-resistant S. aureus in simulated food and surface contamination models. These findings support the potential of anthraquinones as potential antibiotics against drug-resistant infections and environmental contamination.

Introduction

The extensive use of antibiotics has led to a continuous increase in resistant bacterial strains, thereby weakening the efficacy of conventional antimicrobial therapies and posing serious threats to human health, animal welfare, and ecological safety1,2. In 2019, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was associated with an estimated 1.27 million deaths and could contribute to more than 2.1 million deaths in the future3. Among AMR pathogens, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is one of the fastest-growing threats. The number of deaths attributable to MRSA globally rose from approximately 57,200 in 1990 to over 130,000 in 2021, an increase of more than twofold4. The global prevalence of MRSA has made previously manageable infections considerably more difficult to treat. MRSA not only is resistant to β-lactam antibiotics but also often displays cross-resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, including aminoglycosides, macrolides, and fluoroquinolones, thereby severely limiting therapeutic options5. Moreover, resistance in S. aureus has progressively extended beyond hospital settings into communities and livestock farming environments. The isolation rate of MRSA from food-producing animals continues to rise, and its ability to transmit across species significantly increases the risk of zoonotic infections6–8. As a typical “farm–clinic–food” cross-contamination pathogen, S. aureus has been frequently detected in animal-derived food products in several countries and often harbors antimicrobial resistance and virulence factors, thereby posing a direct threat to food safety and consumer health9–12. Therefore, against the backdrop of a stagnating antibiotic development pipeline, novel antibiotics or alternative therapeutic strategies are urgently needed.

Natural products, particularly microbial-derived metabolites, have long served as vital sources for the discovery and development of antimicrobial agents13,14. Since the successful clinical introduction of penicillin in the 1940s, more than 70% of approved antibiotics have been either directly derived from natural products or developed through structural optimization and semisynthesis on the basis of natural scaffolds15–17. These compounds often exhibit unique chemical architectures, diverse mechanisms of action, high selectivity, and low toxicity, making them central candidates in modern antimicrobial drug screening. In recent years, the global dissemination of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, including MRSA strains, has focused on natural product libraries as sources for novel antimicrobial leads. Over 60% of antimicrobial candidates currently in preclinical development are natural products or their derivatives, highlighting their pivotal role in the drug development pipeline18,19. Among microbial sources, soil actinobacteria represent one of the most prolific reservoirs of clinically used antibiotics, contributing to more than 60% of approved antibiotics, including streptomycin, erythromycin, gentamicin, tetracycline, and clavulanic acid20–22. In particular, members of the genus Streptomyces are considered the richest producers of antimicrobial natural products and possess extensive secondary metabolite biosynthesis-related gene clusters (BGCs)23,24. Taken together, these findings reinforce the critical value of soil actinobacteria as indispensable biological resources for antimicrobial drug discovery. Their continued exploration holds immense potential for combating S. aureus infections, especially those caused by resistant strains.

In our previous work, we successfully isolated a series of structurally anthraquinone compounds from soil-derived actinomycetes25. Initial investigations revealed promising antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, with notable efficacy against S. aureus26,27. Owing to their unique aromatic structures and multitarget biological activities, anthraquinone natural products have long been recognized as important lead scaffolds in the search for novel antimicrobial agents and have attracted considerable attention in the fields of medicinal chemistry and natural product research28,29. However, despite isolated cases demonstrating antimicrobial potential, comprehensive and systematic studies on anthraquinones remain scarce, especially those focusing on newly isolated, structurally undercharacterized compounds. Most existing reports are limited to preliminary in vitro activity assessments and lack in-depth mechanistic investigations or validation in animal infection models30,31. These research gaps have hindered the further development of anthraquinones as viable candidates in the field of antimicrobial drug discovery. As a clinically significant and globally prevalent pathogen, S. aureus plays a major role in both human infections and animal diseases, making it a high-priority target in antimicrobial research and development32. Therefore, the present study employs S. aureus as a model organism to systematically evaluate the antibacterial potential of newly isolated anthraquinone compounds. We aimed to assess their efficacy, elucidate the underlying mechanisms of action, examine their safety and toxicity profiles, and determine their in vivo therapeutic potential. This work is expected to provide potential antibiotics against MDR S. aureus and enhance our understanding of the bioactivity of anthraquinones.

Results

Anthraquinone compounds exhibited potent antibacterial activity against MDR S. aureus

In our previous studies, we successfully isolated a group of structurally anthraquinone compounds from soil-derived actinomycetes27. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, a systematic workflow involving strain cultivation, flask fermentation, structure elucidation, biological evaluation, and candidate selection was employed to obtain these anthraquinone compounds. In this study, we selected S. aureus as a model pathogen to systematically evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the isolated anthraquinones. Four structurally distinct compounds, 13394-1, 13394-2, 13394-3, and 13394-4 (structures shown in Fig. 1B), were selected on the basis of their superior activity against susceptible S. aureus strains in previous studies, and their efficacy against resistant strains was further tested. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against the MRSA strain ATCC 43300 were determined to be 1 μg/mL (13394-1), 0.5 μg/mL (13394-2), 1 μg/mL (13394-3), and 1 μg/mL (13394-4) (Fig. 1B). Among them, compound 13394-2 exhibited the most potent activity, highlighting its potential for further development. On the basis of the MIC results, compound 13394-2 was selected for growth curve analysis to validate its inhibitory activity. As shown in Fig. 1C–E, treatment with 13394-2 at various concentrations (0.25–2 μg/mL) significantly suppressed the growth of the S. aureus ATCC 43300, USA 300, and ATCC 25923 strains in a dose-dependent manner. High concentrations (1–2 μg/mL) resulted in nearly complete inhibition of bacterial growth, whereas lower concentrations (0.25–0.5 μg/mL) markedly delayed the exponential growth phase, indicating strong antibacterial potential even at sub-MIC levels. Furthermore, we evaluated the antibacterial activity of 13394-2 against six clinically isolated MDR S. aureus strains from different geographic regions with diverse phenotypes (Table 1). The results demonstrated that 13394-2 exhibited strong antibacterial activity against all the tested clinical isolates, maintaining low MIC values (0.5–2 μg/mL) (Table 1). The corresponding minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values also ranged from 0.5 to 2 μg/mL. Notably, for isolates from Zhejiang and Hubei Provinces, 13394-2 consistently demonstrated good activity with no significant loss in efficacy across different resistance profiles, indicating stable sensitivity toward resistant strains (Table 1). Bactericidal and antibiofilm activities of 13394-2 against MRSA

To comprehensively assess the antibacterial potential of 13394-2 against MDR S. aureus, a series of in vitro experiments were systematically conducted. First, a spot-plating assay was used to preliminarily evaluate the bactericidal activity of 13394-2 at 2× MIC against both MRSA ATCC 43300 and MSSA ATCC 25923 (Fig. 2A). The results indicated complete bacterial eradication for both strains at 2× MIC. A clear dose-dependent bactericidal effect was observed, wherein 13394-2 progressively suppressed colony formation as the concentration increased (Fig. 2A). Time-kill curve analyses further verified the dynamic bactericidal ability of 13394-2. At lower concentrations (0.25–0.5 μg/mL), the compound delayed bacterial proliferation and significantly inhibited growth (Fig. 2B). At 1 μg/mL, MRSA ATCC 43300 was completely eradicated within 16 h, with no regrowth observed, whereas a higher concentration (2 μg/mL) achieved total bacterial killing within just 2 h (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that 13394-2 has potent dose-dependent bactericidal activity. Given the crucial role of biofilm formation in antibiotic resistance33, the antibiofilm properties of 13394-2 were further evaluated via crystal violet staining. The compound significantly inhibited biofilm formation of both the ATCC 43300 (Fig. 2C) and USA300 (Fig. 2D) strains at low concentrations (0.125–0.5 μg/mL), as evidenced by a marked decrease in the OD₆₀₀ values, indicating a clear dose–response relationship. More importantly, 13394-2 also demonstrated biofilm eradication effects against mature biofilms at the same concentrations (Fig. 2C, D), indicating its dual ability to inhibit early biofilm development and dismantle established biofilm structures—an important advantage for overcoming the antibiotic barrier conferred by biofilms. To directly visualize its biofilm-disrupting capacity, SYTO green fluorescence staining was employed to observe biofilms formed by ATCC 43300. In the untreated control group, bacteria formed dense and intact three-dimensional biofilm structures with strong green fluorescence signals. In contrast, treatment with 13394-2 resulted in significantly weakened fluorescence intensity and visibly disrupted biofilm architecture, with a lower signal density than that of the vancomycin-treated positive control group, suggesting superior biofilm clearance performance (Fig. 2E). The results of the quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2F) corroborated the visual observations. Compared with the untreated group, the vancomycin-treated group pre sented no statistically significant reduction in fluorescence, whereas the 13394-2 group presented a significant decrease in fluorescence, further confirming its effective activity against mature biofilms. 13394-2 exerts antibacterial activity by disrupting membrane integrity and inducing metabolic dysregulation

Given its concentration-dependent bactericidal effects and biofilm formation, 13394-2 may act beyond conventional enzyme inhibition or synthesis interference and instead exert multifaceted physiological disruptions, including membrane damage and metabolic perturbation. The bacterial cell membrane is essential for regulating molecular transport and maintaining cellular homeostasis and is a known target of various antimicrobial agents34. We first employed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to observe the morphological changes in S. aureus ATCC 25923 following treatment with 13394-2 (Fig. 3A). In the untreated group, the cells displayed a smooth surface, intact morphology, and compact arrangement. In contrast, treatment with 1 μg/mL 13394-2 caused severe cell deformation, surface collapse, and cytoplasmic leakage (Fig. 3A). The extent of damage exceeded that observed in the daptomycin-treated control (5 μg/mL), indicating that 13394-2 likely acts via direct membrane disruption. To confirm the degree of membrane integrity impairment, propidium iodide (PI) staining was performed to assess membrane permeability. The results revealed a significant, dose-dependent increase in PI fluorescence intensity following treatment with 0.125–1 μg/mL 13394-2, suggesting that membrane rupture allowed PI to bind to intracellular DNA (Fig. 3B). Notably, at 0.25 μg/mL, the permeability effect was comparable to that of the 5 μg/mL daptomycin group (Fig. 3B). Additionally, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging of S. aureus stained with SYTO 9/PI (Fig. 3C) revealed a predominance of green fluorescence (live cells) in the untreated group, whereas 13394-2 treatment increased red fluorescence (dead cells), supporting its potent bactericidal action via membrane disruption (Fig. 3C). Membrane damage is often accompanied by oxidative stress and metabolic collapse. We thus measured intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels following treatment. 13394-2 significantly elevated ROS production in a dose-dependent manner, with high-concentration effects comparable to those of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), suggesting that ROS accumulation may contribute to the bactericidal mechanism (Fig. 3D). To further evaluate metabolic impact, intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels were quantified. Treatment with 13394-2 resulted in a rapid and significant decrease in ATP content, with near-complete depletion at 1 μg/mL, similar to the effect of the protonophore carbonyl cyanide3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that disruption of membrane function severely impairs cellular energy metabolism. Given that the proton motive force (PMF) is essential for ATP synthesis35, we utilized the fluorescent probe BCECF-AM to detect PMF changes. 13394-2 treatment significantly reduced PMF levels in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that the collapse of the membrane potential and proton gradient was a key component of the antibacterial mechanism of 13394-2 (Fig. 3F). 13394-2 induced metabolic reprogramming in S. aureus

The anthraquinone compound 13394-2 exerted antibacterial activity by disrupting membrane integrity and inducing metabolic dysfunction. However, phenotypic analyses alone were insufficient to fully elucidate the compound’s molecular effects on bacterial metabolism, functional pathways, and resistance regulation. To gain deeper insight into the molecular basis of its antibacterial mechanism, we performed transcriptome sequencing to compare the global gene expression profiles of S. aureus before and after treatment with 13394-2. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between the control and 13394-2-treated groups along the PC1 axis, with tight clustering within each group, indicating substantial transcriptional differences between the two conditions (Fig. 4A). A Venn diagram further illustrates the distribution of shared and unique genes across groups. A total of 2042 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were shared between both conditions, with 36 genes uniquely expressed in the control group and 97 genes specifically expressed in the 13394-2-treated group, suggesting that the compound induces specific transcriptional regulatory responses (Fig. 4B). To evaluate the consistency of the biological replicates and the reliability of the data, we conducted correlation analysis of gene expression levels across samples and calculated Pearson correlation coefficients (R²), which were visualized as a clustered heatmap. The treated group showed high intragroup consistency, with R² values ranging from 0.987 to 0.993, indicating excellent reproducibility. The control group also exhibited a high internal correlation (R² = 0.856–0.863), validating the robustness of the experimental design (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, the intergroup correlations between the treated and control samples were significantly lower (R² = 0.510–0.726), indicating substantial transcriptional divergence induced by 13394-2, which is consistent with the expected biological variation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Visualization of the DEGs via a volcano plot revealed 287 significantly upregulated genes and 372 downregulated genes in response to 13394-2 treatment, many of which be closely associated with the antibacterial mechanism of the compound (Fig. 4C). Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was then performed to explore expression patterns across samples, and the results were displayed as a heatmap. The treated samples formed a distinct cluster separate from the control samples, reflecting high intragroup similarity and intergroup divergence in gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These results corroborated the findings from the correlation analysis and supported the reliability of the transcriptomic data. Clustering analysis further grouped the DEGs into several subclusters on the basis of expression trends. Notably, the expression of some genes were significantly upregulated in the treatment group and downregulated in the control group, suggesting a close association with 13394-2 exposure and potential functional relevance (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To investigate the functional classification of the DEGs, we performed COG (clusters of orthologous groups) annotation analysis. The DEGs were enriched mainly in categories, such as “translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis,” “cell cycle control and cell division,” and “cell membrane/wall biogenesis” (Supplementary Fig. 1C). To further explore the biological significance of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to identify major functional trends. GO enrichment revealed that DEGs were predominantly associated with translation- and ribosome-related processes, including protein synthesis, ribosomal structure, and rRNA binding, indicating that the bacterial translational machinery is a primary target of the cellular stress response to 13394-2. Concurrent enrichment of pathways related to protein folding and stress responses further suggests activation of protein homeostasis mechanisms under compound exposure (Fig. 4D). KEGG pathway analysis supported these observations, showing that upregulated genes were mainly enriched in ribosome-associated pathways, along with metabolic processes, such as fatty acid biosynthesis, riboflavin metabolism, and thiamine metabolism, implying adaptive responses involving membrane remodeling and cofactor biosynthesis (Fig. 4E). In contrast, downregulated genes were concentrated in central carbon and energy metabolism, including the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and nicotinate metabolism, reflecting a global suppression of energy production and growth-related metabolic activity. This transcriptional pattern is consistent with a metabolic inhibition phenotype (Fig. 4E). Notably, enrichment of the quorum-sensing pathway among upregulated genes suggests that S. aureus modulates cell–cell communication in response to 13394-2-induced stress. Given the established role of quorum sensing in biofilm formation and antibiotic tolerance, this finding aligns well with the observed antibiofilm activity of 13394-2 (Fig. 4E). Heatmap analyses of representative pathways further highlighted coordinated upregulation of ribosomal and lipid biosynthesis genes, alongside marked repression of genes involved in energy metabolism, corroborating the enrichment results (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these transcriptomic data indicate that 13394-2 elicits a multifaceted stress response in S. aureus, characterized by enhanced translational and adaptive pathways coupled with suppression of energy metabolism and transport functions. The consistency between these transcriptional changes and the observed phenotypic effects provides molecular-level support for the antibacterial activity of 13394-2.

In addition, we further resolved the modulation of drug resistance and virulence changes in S. aureus after 13394-2 treatment. Antibiotic resistance database (CARD) and the virulence factors of pathogenic bacteria database (VFDB). CARD-based analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly associated with mechanisms, such as antibiotic efflux, target modification, and ABC transporter systems (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Among these genes, those related to “antibiotic efflux” constituted the largest proportion, suggesting that S. aureus attempts to counteract drug-induced stress by activating efflux pump systems (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Furthermore, DEGs were enriched across multiple resistance gene families and various antibiotic resistance pathways, indicating that 13394-2 has broad regulatory effects on multidrug resistance-associated genes (Supplementary Fig. 2A). These changes suggest a complex bacterial response aimed at maintaining survival under antimicrobial pressure. VFDB annotation revealed that the DEGs were associated mainly with immune modulation, nutrient/metabolic factors, and adhesion-related virulence categories (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Additionally, expression changes were detected in genes involved in toxin secretion, biofilm formation, and stress adaptation, implying that 13394-2 may disrupt the virulence regulatory network and attenuate the pathogenic potential of S. aureus (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Together, these findings suggest that 13394-2 enhances its anti-infective efficacy and clinical potential not only by disrupting bacterial metabolism and survival but also by simultaneously interfering with core antibiotic resistance mechanisms and virulence factor expression.

FabF is a potential molecular target of the anthraquinone compound 13394-2

13394-2 induced significant membrane disruption and transcriptional dysregulation of membrane-associated metabolic pathways in S. aureus. Given that bacterial membrane lipid biosynthesis is dependent on the fatty acid synthesis (FASII) system36, we hypothesized that 13394-2 may target a key enzyme within this pathway, thereby impairing membrane homeostasis. Interestingly, transcriptomic analysis revealed that the fabF gene itself did not exhibit significant changes in expression (Fig. 5A). However, the expression of multiple upstream and downstream genes in the FASII pathway were markedly dysregulated. Notably, genes encoding FabD (β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase III) and FabG (3-ketoacyl-ACP reductase), which function cooperatively with FabF during fatty acid elongation, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5A). In contrast, genes (ackA, dnak, and arok) involved in the initiation of fatty acid biosynthesis precursors were strongly downregulated (Fig. 5A). Other key enzymes in the FASII pathway, including fabH, also presented differential expression. Although fabF itself was not transcriptionally altered, the observed pathway imbalance suggests possible direct functional interference with FabF. As a rate-limiting enzyme in the FASII system, FabF (β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase II) plays a critical role in fatty acid chain elongation and is essential for maintaining membrane lipid synthesis and fluidity37. Moreover, previous studies have shown that emodin, an anthraquinone compound, can inhibit bacterial fatty acid synthesis by directly binding to the active site of FabF, thereby impairing its catalytic activity and inducing membrane damage. Meanwhile, the results of quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) further validated the trend of the transcriptome results and further confirmed the reliability of the transcriptome analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 3). Given the structural similarity between 13394-2 and emodin (both sharing a core anthraquinone scaffold) and the pathway-level disruptions observed in the transcriptomic data, we hypothesized that 13394-2 may directly bind to FabF, leading to compensatory or feedback expression changes in adjacent enzymes. To test this hypothesis, molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to predict potential binding interactions. The docking results revealed that 13394-2 binds to FabF with a calculated binding energy of −6.2 kcal/mol, comparable to that of the positive control emodin (−6.6 kcal/mol), with both compounds stably occupying the active site of FabF (Fig. 5B). The binding of 13394-2 was stabilized by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, primarily involving the residues GLU85, TRP26, LYS23, and ASP89 (Fig. 5C), which is consistent with the interaction pattern observed for emodin. Further MD simulations over a 100-ns timescale revealed that the 13394-2-FabF complex maintained overall structural stability, with root mean square deviation (RMSD) values fluctuating below 2.8 Å after 60 ns, indicating a stable binding conformation (Fig. 5D). The number of hydrogen bonds ranged from 0 to 7, with an average of approximately 2 per frame, suggesting robust hydrogen bonding with the binding pocket (Fig. 5E). Residue-level RMSF analysis revealed reduced flexibility in the FabF binding pocket, particularly around residues 255–270, after ligand binding (Fig. 5F), suggesting increased local rigidity upon complex formation. Binding free energy calculations via the MM/PBSA method estimated a binding energy of −15.89 kcal/mol for the 13394-2-FabF complex, indicating strong binding affinity. The decomposition of binding energies at the amino acid level revealed that electrostatic interactions (ΔEPB) contributed most significantly to the total binding energy, with GLU85, TRP26, LYS23, and ASP89 identified as key residues (Fig. 5G), which is consistent with the docking predictions. Together, these results suggest that FabF is a potential molecular target of 13394-2. To further verify the above calculation results experimentally, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used to characterize the interaction between the two. ITC experiments showed that the binding of 13394-2 with FabF exhibited a significant exothermic characteristic (Fig. 5H). Nonlinear fitting yielded a dissociation constant (KD) of approximately 10.2 μM, and a binding stoichiometry close to 1 (n ≈ 0.81), indicating that the two bind specifically in a near 1:1 ratio (Fig. 5H). Thermodynamic parameter analysis showed that this binding process was mainly driven by enthalpy (ΔH ≈ −100 kJ/mol), accompanied by an unfavorable entropy change (ΔS ≈ −239.8 J/mol K), which is highly consistent with the binding mode predicted in molecular simulations, which is dominated by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions (Fig. 5H). Further analysis of protein thermal stability revealed that 13394-2 significantly increased the melting temperature (Tm) of FabF (Fig. 5I), suggesting that small molecule binding can effectively limit protein conformational flexibility and enhance overall stability, consistent with the conformational stability characteristics of the complex observed in molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 5I). Simultaneously, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments further confirmed the direct interaction between 13394-2 and FabF, with the binding response gradually increasing with increasing compound concentration (Fig. 5J). The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) obtained from the kinetic fitting based on the 1:1 binding model was in the low micromolar range (approximately 48.8 μM), and the steady-state affinity fitting also showed good saturated binding characteristics (KD value of 56.5 μM), consistent with the affinity level measured by ITC (Fig. 5K). 13394-2 exhibited favorable cellular safety and potential as an antibiotic candidate

To evaluate the safety profile of 13394 series compounds, the cytotoxicity of the 13394 series compounds (13394-1, 13394-2, 13394-3, and 13394-4) was systematically evaluated in Vero, HeLa, and RAW cells. All compounds maintained high cell viability at low to moderate concentrations, exhibiting a dose-dependent trend of decreasing viability with increasing concentration. CC50 analysis showed that the overall CC50 values of each compound in the three cell lines ranged from approximately 8 to 12 μg/mL, with minimal differences between different compounds and cell lines (Fig. 6A–C). Notably, 13394-2 showed a slightly higher CC50 value in all three cell lines than the other compounds. To further determine its antibacterial selectivity, we calculated the selectivity index (SI), which quantifies the therapeutic window between antibacterial efficacy and host cytotoxicity. Given the CC₅₀ value of 11.40 μg/mL (minimum) in raw cells and the MIC of 0.5 μg/mL against MDR S. aureus, the resulting SI was 23. This value substantially exceeds the commonly accepted threshold for good selectivity (SI > 10), suggesting that 13394-2 has potent antibacterial activity with low toxicity toward mammalian cells, thereby supporting its development as a promising antibacterial candidate. Further hemolysis experiments showed that, within the tested concentration range (up to 128 μg/mL), the hemolysis rate of the 13394 series compounds remained at a low level, significantly below the safety threshold of 10% (Fig. 6D). These results indicated that this series of compounds exhibits good cell compatibility and erythrocyte safety in vitro. For in vivo toxicity evaluation, BALB/c mice were orally administered 13394-2 at a dose of 10 mg/kg. The results revealed no significant differences in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (Fig. 6E) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST, Fig. 6F) levels between the treated and control groups, indicating that there was no apparent hepatic injury. Similarly, renal function indicators (creatinine (CREA, Fig. 6G) and blood urea nitrogen (UREA, Fig. 6H) remained unchanged, suggesting no detectable nephrotoxicity under the tested conditions. 13394-2 exhibited anti-S. aureus activity across multiple experimental models

To comprehensively evaluate the in vitro and in vivo antibacterial efficacy of compound 13394-2, a series of S. aureus infection models were established. First, the in vivo anti-infective efficacy of compound 13394-2 was evaluated in a Galleria mellonella infection model. Following infection, the survival rate of Galleria mellonella in the vehicle-treated group declined rapidly, while the survival rate of Galleria mellonella treated with 13394-2 significantly increased, maintaining at approximately 60–70% at the observation endpoint. In contrast, the vancomycin-treated group showed a survival rate of 40% (Fig. 7A). In the lethal peritonitis model, mice in the infected group exhibited rapid mortality within 48 h, with a survival rate of only 20% (Fig. 7B). In contrast, oral administration of 13394-2 (2.5 mg/kg) significantly increased survival to 80%, indicating a strong protective effect in vivo (Fig. 7B). In the nonlethal peritonitis model, the bacterial burden was quantitatively assessed in liver, lung, and spleen tissues (Fig. 7C–E). The results revealed a significant reduction in colony-forming units (CFUs) in the liver following 13394-2 treatment compared with those in the vehicle group, with bacterial loads in the lungs and spleen also trending lower, albeit without statistical significance, which suggested the suppression of bacterial dissemination and organ colonization. Histopathological examination further confirmed the protective effect of 13394-2 on tissue integrity (Fig. 7F). Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining revealed disorganized cellular architecture, hepatocyte necrosis, and extensive vacuolar degeneration in the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 7F). In contrast, liver tissues from the 13394-2-treated group maintained structural integrity and showed markedly reduced inflammatory infiltration, which was comparable to that of the uninfected control group (Fig. 7F). These results indicated that 13394-2 not only controls infection but also has tissue-protective effects. To simulate real-world scenarios involving environmental contamination and food spoilage, we employed a cookware surface contamination model and a meat preservation model using S. aureus ATCC 43300. In the cookware model (Fig. 7G), the CFU counts decreased with increasing concentrations, and near-complete bacterial eradication was achieved at 8 and 16 μg/mL after 30 min, demonstrating excellent potential for disinfection applications. In the meat model, the 8 μg/mL group presented significantly lower CFU counts than the control and low-dose groups did, along with visibly delayed spoilage (Fig. 7H), indicating dose-dependent antibacterial and preservative effects on food products. In the skin wound infection model, ulcerative lesions were induced via ATCC 43300, followed by the topical application of 13394-2. The 13394-2-treated group presented clear scab formation by day 7 and nearly complete healing by day 11, whereas the vehicle group still presented redness, exudation, and persistent inflammation (Fig. 7I). The uninfected control group showed normal healing progression (Fig. 7I). These results demonstrated that 13394-2 not only effectively eradicated local infection but also promoted wound healing, exhibiting dual antibacterial and tissue-repair properties. Discussion

With the increasing threat of antibiotic resistance, particularly the increasing prevalence of MDR S. aureus strains, such as MRSA, options for effective clinical antimicrobial therapies have become severely limited38. Natural products, especially microbial-derived metabolites, are widely regarded as vital sources of next-generation antibiotics because of their structural diversity and novel mechanisms of action14. Among these, anthraquinone compounds have attracted attention owing to their aromatic scaffold and potential for chemical derivatization29. However, systematic evaluation of anthraquinones in the context of drug-resistant bacterial infections remains scarce, and there is currently no consensus on their mechanisms of action or molecular targets. In our previous work, we preliminarily identified several anthraquinone compounds isolated from soil-derived actinomycetes that exhibited antibacterial activity against S. aureus26,27. However, those studies were limited to MIC determination and did not address bactericidal potential, antibiofilm effects, mechanisms of action, or in vivo efficacy, leaving a gap in these aspects. To address this gap, the present study focused on the most active compound, designated 13394-2, and conducted a comprehensive investigation. This full-spectrum approach was designed to bridge the gap between compound discovery and clinical application and to broaden the potential of anthraquinone natural products in the treatment of drug-resistant bacterial infections.

In terms of its bactericidal mechanism, 13394-2 has distinctive features that differ markedly from those of traditional antibiotics. It exhibited a rapid killing rate, strong concentration dependence, and retained efficacy against bacteria embedded within biofilms, suggesting that it may overcome the diffusion limitations typically imposed by the biofilm matrix. Through a series of multidimensional experiments, we demonstrated that 13394-2 exerts its bactericidal activity primarily through membrane disruption, which leads to increased membrane permeability, induction of oxidative stress, and disruption of bacterial energy metabolism. This mode of action is clearly distinct from that of conventional antibiotics, which primarily inhibit DNA replication, protein synthesis, or cell wall formation17,39, and instead resembles the membrane-targeting behavior of certain antimicrobial peptides or lipopeptide antibiotics, such as daptomycin40,41. However, unlike those agents, which are often characterized by a large molecular size, challenging synthesis, and significant side effects, 13394-2 is a small-molecule natural product with a simple structure, synthetic accessibility, and wide potential for structural modification, making it an attractive candidate for further optimization. To date, most studies on anthraquinone-type natural products in antibacterial applications have remained at the preliminary stage, with attention largely focused on canonical molecules, such as emodin42. Although aloe-emodin has been shown to induce oxidative stress in bacteria, its effects on membrane integrity and physiological metabolic networks have not been fully elucidated43. In contrast, our study demonstrated that 13394-2 exhibited potent bactericidal activity at substantially lower concentrations. We thus propose a multidimensional, synergy-driven bactericidal model in which 13394-2 simultaneously disrupts membrane function and induces metabolic imbalance, expanding the mechanistic landscape of anthraquinone-based antimicrobial agents. Notably, anthraquinones have been reported to act via oxidative stress induction, resulting in bactericidal effects42,43. Similarly, our observation that rapid, concentration-dependent killing kinetics and membrane rupture induce metabolic collapse suggested that 13394-2 may possess increased lipid membrane affinity. This “membrane disruption + metabolic imbalance” dual-action mechanism could represent a killing strategy that is less susceptible to single-point resistance mutations than traditional single-target antibiotics44. Especially important was the ability of the compound to eradicate mature biofilms effectively, which is an uncommon property among current antibiotics and offers a promising strategy for the treatment of chronic and drug-resistant infections.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that treatment with 13394-2 led to the upregulation of bacterial ribosomal protein genes and the activation of quorum-sensing-related genes while simultaneously inducing significant downregulation of key metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and nicotinate metabolism. This dual pattern involves structural recovery accompanied by energy supply collapse, which suggests that under membrane stress, S. aureus attempts to reestablish homeostasis but ultimately fails because of insufficient metabolic support, leading to systemic metabolic collapse and cell death. This observation aligns with the model proposed by Belenky et al., in which antibiotics induce a cascade of metabolic overload, redox imbalance, energy depletion, and programmed cell death in bacteria45,46. The transcriptional perturbations triggered by 13394-2 thus reflect a “dual metabolic hijacking” strategy: on the one hand, the compound stimulates biosynthetic processes to intensify the metabolic burden, while on the other hand, it simultaneously suppresses energy-generating pathways, driving the cell into a state of imbalance and death. To our knowledge, this study provides systematic validation of such a metabolic-collapse-based antibacterial model in the context of anthraquinone-type molecules, offering mechanistic support for the design of “nonenzyme-inhibiting” small-molecule antibiotics. Moreover, by integrating transcriptomic insights with molecular modeling data, we identified FabF as a potential direct target of 13394-2. The FASII pathway is essential for maintaining membrane fluidity and structural integrity in Gram-positive bacteria47,48. Previous studies have shown that emodin, a structurally related anthraquinone compound, can exert antibacterial effects by binding to FabF in Streptococcus suis42. However, few studies have reported the specific binding of anthraquinones to S. aureus FabF. In our study, although fabF gene expression itself was not significantly altered at the transcript level, the dysregulation of its upstream and downstream enzymes under 13394-2 treatment provides indirect evidence supporting target engagement. Furthermore, the molecular docking results demonstrated favorable binding affinity, with hydrogen bonding and structural complementarity confirmed through molecular dynamics simulations and biophysical experiments, including ITC, DSF and SPR assay. These findings suggest that the interaction between 13394-2 and FabF is mediated via the inhibition of enzymatic activity or interference with protein conformation rather than transcriptional regulation. Overall, this study reinforces the mechanistic rationale for the use of FabF as a valid molecular target of anthraquinone antibiotics and expands the theoretical framework for the target-based development of membrane-active antimicrobial agents.

In addition to providing mechanistic and target-based insights, this study systematically validated the anti-infective efficacy of 13394-2 in multiple animal infection models. To date, there have been no published reports demonstrating that anthraquinone-type natural products can exert therapeutic effects on MRSA in systemic infection or chronic wound models. Most existing studies are limited to in vitro assays or superficial skin infection models and lack robust in vivo evidence27,49,50. In contrast, our study revealed that 13394-2 significantly improved survival rates, reduced bacterial burdens, and mitigated tissue damage in the animal infection model. Although vancomycin is commonly used as a positive control in mouse infection models, a concurrent vancomycin-treated group was not included in this study due to animal welfare considerations and adherence to the 3Rs principles. Our previous studies reported that vancomycin administered at 10 mg/kg resulted in approximately 60% survival when given 1 h post-infection, but only ~40% survival when treatment was delayed to 6 h51. In the present study, compound 13394-2 administered at 2 h post-infection at 2.5 mg/kg achieved ~60% survival, which is comparable to the established vancomycin benchmark at earlier treatment time points. Moreover, in a wound infection model, 13394-2 not only effectively controlled local infection but also accelerated wound healing, thereby exerting dual therapeutic effects on both antimicrobial activity and tissue repair. Additionally, the potent bactericidal effects of 13394-2 in food contamination and useful surface models highlight its versatility beyond clinical applications. These findings support its potential utility in food safety and hygiene disinfection, providing a basis for broader translational scenarios. In terms of safety, 13394-2 demonstrated a favorable selectivity index (>20) in mammalian cells and showed no detectable hemolysis or hepatotoxicity/nephrotoxicity at effective antibacterial concentrations. This safety profile is similar to that of many known natural antimicrobial compounds, particularly those with membrane-targeting mechanisms52–55, as 13394-2 maintained selectivity toward bacteria over mammalian membranes, with an important advantage for clinical development. Taken together, the results of this study build upon our previous screening of soil-derived Streptomyces metabolites and identify 13394-2 as a structurally anthraquinone compound with a multifaceted antibacterial mechanism. These findings transcend the limitations of earlier anthraquinone research, which often remained confined to MIC measurements and in vitro screening without mechanistic clarity. Unlike previous studies on anthraquinone analogs, this work established an integrated evaluation framework spanning structure, mechanism, and function, confirming the therapeutic potential of 13394-2 against drug-resistant infections. Moreover, this study provides a methodological paradigm and mechanistic rationale for the investigation of natural product-based antimicrobial agents. Future research should focus on structure optimization, target validation, synergistic drug interactions, and pharmacokinetics in animal models to accelerate the clinical translation of 13394-2 as a potential antibiotic against multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Conclusion

This study was based on a previously isolated anthraquinone antimicrobial molecule, 13394-2, from soil actinomycetes, which was shown to have significant anti-MDR S. aureus activity. Mechanistically, 13394-2 disrupted bacterial membranes and induced oxidative stress and metabolic collapse. Transcriptomic, molecular modeling analyses and biophysical experiments revealed FabF as a potential target, supporting a dual mechanism of membrane disruption and enzymatic inhibition. In vivo, 13394-2 effectively improved survival, reduced the bacterial load, and promoted wound healing while maintaining low toxicity. It also showed strong bactericidal activity in food and surface contamination models, underscoring its translational potential across clinical and industrial applications. Overall, 13394-2 is a potential natural antibiotic for combating resistant bacterial infections.

Materials and methods

Compounds, bacterial strains, cell lines, and culture conditions

The anthraquinone compounds were previously isolated from a soil-derived Streptomyces fermentation broth in our laboratory. The compound was purified via silica gel chromatography followed by semipreparative HPLC to >98% purity. Structural identification was completed in earlier work. A 10 mg/mL stock solution was prepared in DMSO, sterilized via a 0.22 μm filter, aliquoted into sterile 2 mL microtubes, and stored at –20 °C in the dark. The bacterial strains used included S. aureus ATCC 25923, methicillin-resistant strains ATCC 43300 and USA300, and six clinically isolated multidrug-resistant S. aureus strains. Strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) or on tryptic soy agar (TSA) at 37 °C. For long-term storage, the strains were preserved in TSB containing 20% glycerol at –80 °C. Vero cells, Hela cells and RAW 264.7 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The cells were used within 20 passages. Defibrinated sheep red blood cells were purchased from YuanYe Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Determination of the MIC and MBC

The MIC of compound 13394-2 was determined via the standard broth microdilution method56. Briefly, exponentially growing bacterial cultures were diluted to 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL and incubated with serially diluted concentrations of the compound in 96-well microtiter plates, with a final volume of 200 μL per well. After 16–18 h of incubation at 37 °C, the lowest concentration with no visible bacterial growth was recorded as the MIC. For MBC determination, 10 μL aliquots from wells with no visible growth in the MIC assay were plated onto TSA and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The lowest concentration that yielded no colony growth was defined as the MBC57. DMSO was included as a negative control in all the assays.

Growth curves and time-kill assays

The experiments were performed according to previous methods with minor modifications58. The impact of 13394-2 on S. aureus growth was assessed via the ATCC 25923, ATCC 43300 (MRSA), and USA300 strains. Cultures were diluted to 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL and treated with 13394-2 (0.25–2.0 μg/mL) or DMSO (control). The OD₆₀₀ values were recorded hourly for 24 h in a microplate reader at 37 °C. Growth curves were plotted to evaluate the inhibitory effects. For time-kill kinetics, ATCC 43300 cultures were similarly prepared and exposed to 13394-2 (0.25–2.0 μg/mL). Samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 16 h, serially diluted, and plated on TSA. After incubation (37 °C, 18–24 h), CFUs were counted to construct kill curves. A ≥ 3 log₁₀ CFU reduction indicated bactericidal activity. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Biofilm inhibition and fermentation assays

Crystal violet staining was used to assess the inhibitory and eradication effects of 13394-2 on S. aureus biofilms53,59. ATCC 43300 and USA300 were inoculated into 96-well plates containing TSB with 1% glucose and treated with 13394-2 either during biofilm formation or after maturation. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the wells were washed, stained with crystal violet, and destained with ethanol. The biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the OD₆₀₀. DMSO was used as a control. Additionally, biofilms formed on sterile glass coverslips were treated with 13394-2 (2 μg/mL) or vancomycin (2 μg/mL) for 24 h, stained with SYTO 9/PI for 30 min, and imaged via a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Nikon, Japan). The fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

S. aureus ATCC 25923 at logarithmic phase was adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 0.5 and treated with 13394-2 (1 μg/mL) or daptomycin (5 μg/mL) for 2 h; untreated cells served as controls60,61. Bacterial cells were collected via centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min), fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer), and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30–100%, 15 min each). After tert-butanol substitution and drying, the samples were gold-sputtered and imaged via a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, Nikon, Japan) at 10 kV.

Membrane permeability and live/dead cell staining

S. aureus strains were treated with 13394-2 (0.125–1.0 μg/mL) for 2 h at 37 °C. DMSO and daptomycin (10 μg/mL) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively57. The bacterial suspensions were collected, washed, and incubated with propidium iodide (PI, 5 μg/mL, Beyotime) in the dark for 20 min. After the PBS washes, the fluorescence (ex 535 nm/em 617 nm) was measured via a microplate reader. The SYTO9 dye was subsequently added and incubated for 15 min, after which the samples were imaged via confocal laser scanning microscopy (N-STORM, Nikon). Green and red fluorescence indicate live and dead cells, respectively.

ROS measurement

Bacterial culture was performed as described above. After 2 h of treatment with 13394-2 (0.125–1.0 μg/mL), the ROS levels were assessed via a DCFH-DA probe (10 μM, Beyotime)62. The fluorescence intensity (Ex 488 nm/Em 525 nm) was measured. Daptomycin (5 μg/mL) and H₂O₂ (100 μM) were used as positive controls.

ATP quantification

Intracellular ATP levels were measured via an ATP assay kit (Beyotime)63. Bacterial cells (1 mL) were lysed and reacted with ATP substrate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescence was detected with a microplate reader. CCCP (50 μM) was included as a positive control for ATP depletion.

Proton motive force (PMF) analysis

PMF disruption was evaluated via the use of BCECF-AM (5 μM, Beyotime), a pH-sensitive fluorescent probe64. After 30 min of staining in the dark, fluorescence (Ex 488 nm/Em 535 nm) was measured. CCCP served as a positive control.

Transcriptomic analysis

S. aureus ATCC 25923 was treated with 1 μg/mL 13394-2 for 2 h. Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol and sequenced via the Illumina platform (paired-end RNA-Seq). The data were quality controlled by fastp, mapped to the reference genome (GCF_000756205.1) via HISAT2, and quantified with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes were defined as those whose |log₂FC| > 1 and p < 0.05. All analyses were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd.).The raw transcriptomic sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI sequence read archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1392083.

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

qRT-PCR was performed to determine the relative mRNA expression levels of selected genes in S. aureus ATCC 25923. Bacterial culture and sample processing were conducted under the same conditions as those used for the transcriptomic analysis. Cells were harvested accordingly, and total RNA was extracted and subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR reactions were prepared using ChamQ SYBR qPCR master mix (Vazyme), and primers were synthesized by Wuhan Hecegene Technology Co., Ltd (Supplementary Table 1). Amplification was carried out on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad) under the following cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The 16S rRNA gene was used as an internal standard. Bio-Rad CFX96 Manager version 2.1. was used to analyze experimental data. The 2−ΔΔCt method was applied to analyze the fold change in gene expression.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations

The 3D structure of 13394-2 was generated in ChemOffice and saved as a MOL2 file. The predicted FabF structure (AF-Q6GIA3, AlphaFold) was prepared in PyMOL by removing water and phosphate groups. Molecular docking was performed via AutoDock Vina 1.5.665, and ligand–residue interactions were visualized in 2D and 3D via Discovery Studio 2019. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted in GROMACS 2022. Protein and ligand parameters were derived from the CHARMM36 and CGenFF force fields, respectively66. The complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box (1 nm buffer) and neutralized via gmx generation67. The long-range electrostatics were treated with a PME (cutoff: 1 nm), and SHAKE constraints were applied. The integration step was 1 fs. Energy minimization included steepest descent (3000 steps) and a conjugate gradient (2000 steps), with sequential relaxation of water, ions, and the entire system. The system was equilibrated under NPT conditions at 310 K, and a 100 ns production run was performed.

Expression and purification of FabF protein

The FabF gene (1245 bp, BX571856.1) was synthesized by Wuhan Hecegene Technology Co., Ltd. The gene was cloned into the pET28a expression vector digested with NheI and XhoI, resulting in an N-terminal His₆-tagged construct. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Transformed cells were cultured in antibiotic-containing medium, and protein expression was induced with IPTG when the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.6–0.8. After induction, cultures were incubated at a reduced temperature to promote soluble protein expression. Cells were harvested and resuspended in lysis buffer, followed by disruption by ultrasonication. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was loaded onto a Ni-NTA affinity chromatography column. The target protein was eluted using an imidazole-containing buffer with a final imidazole concentration of 200 mM. The eluted protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography for additional purification and buffer exchange. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE analysis, and the purified FabF protein was used for subsequent biophysical assays.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis

ITC experiments were performed using a TA NANO instrument to investigate the direct interaction between compound 13394-2 and the FabF protein. FabF protein (2 μM) and compound 13394-2 (80 μM) were exchanged into the same buffer system and degassed prior to analysis. A total volume of 300 μL FabF protein solution was loaded into the sample cell, while 50 μL of the compound solution was loaded into the syringe. The compound was injected into the protein solution in 20 successive injections with an interval of 200 s between injections. All experiments were conducted at 25 °C. After excluding the heat of dilution from the first injection, the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd), stoichiometry (n), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS) were calculated using NanoAnalyzer software.

Protein thermal stability analysis (nanoDSF)

Nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF), based on intrinsic protein fluorescence, was used to evaluate the effect of compound 13394-2 on the thermal stability of FabF. Purified FabF protein (0.5 mg/mL) was mixed with compound 13394-2 (4 μg/mL), ensuring identical solvent conditions across all samples. After incubation at room temperature for 30 min, samples were loaded into specialized capillaries and analyzed using a nanoDSF protein stability analysis system (Prometheus NT.48, NanoTemper Technologies). Thermal unfolding was monitored by increasing the temperature from 25 to 95 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min, while recording changes in intrinsic fluorescence. The melting temperature (T_m) of the protein was calculated using the nanoDSF software and used to assess the impact of compound binding on FabF thermal stability.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

Surface plasmon resonance analysis was conducted to characterize the interaction between FabF protein and compound 13394-2 using a Biacore T1k system (Cytiva). FabF protein (0.5 mg/mL) was immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip via standard amine-coupling chemistry, and a reference flow cell without immobilized protein was used as a control. A running buffer containing a constant solvent composition was used as the mobile phase. Compound 13394-2 was injected over the sensor surface at a series of concentrations ranging from 1.56 to 50 μM, with an association phase of 90 s and a dissociation phase of 60 s. The flow rate for each cycle was set to 30 μL/min. Sensorgrams were processed by reference subtraction and solvent correction, and the data were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation software with both kinetic (1:1 binding) and steady-state binding models to evaluate the interaction characteristics between FabF and the compound.

Cytotoxicity and hemolysis assays

The African green monkey kidney epithelial cell line Vero (ATCC CCL-81, RRID:CVCL_0059), human cervical carcinoma cell line HeLa (ATCC CCL-2, RRID:CVCL_0030) and RAW 264.7 (ATCC TIB-71, RRID:CVCL_0493) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were used to assess the cytotoxicity of the compound due to their high sensitivity to toxic agents and common use in the safety evaluation of anti-infective drugs. As non-transformed mammalian epithelial cells, they provide a reliable indication of potential cytotoxicity and support preliminary safety assessment. The cells exhibited stable morphology and growth throughout the study, with no evidence of contamination. In brief, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well) and incubated overnight in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO₂51. The cells were then treated with 13394-2 (1–128 μg/mL) or DMSO (<0.1%), and the untreated groups were used as controls. After 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added, and the OD₄₅₀ was measured after 1.5 h to assess cell viability.

For hemolysis, 2% defibrinated sheep red blood cells were incubated with 13394-2 (1–128 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h. PBS and 1% Triton X-100 served as negative and positive controls, respectively. After centrifugation, the supernatant OD₅₄₀ was measured. Hemolysis (%) = [(OD_sample–OD_PBS)/(OD_Triton – OD_PBS)] × 100%. A hemolysis rate <5% was considered negligible, whereas >20% indicated potential toxicity68.

Systemic toxicity assessment

Female SPF BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old; purchased from China Three Gorges University) were orally administered 13394-2 (10 mg/kg) (n = 5/group). Blood samples were collected on days 3 and 10 posttreatment. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and urea (UREA) levels were measured via an automatic biochemical analyzer to evaluate hepatic and renal functions69. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (approval no. HZAUMO-2025-0311).

Galleria mellonella larval infection model

Healthy Galleria mellonella larvae with intact cuticles and similar size and weight were selected and maintained under dark conditions prior to experimentation. S. aureus ATCC 25923 and ATCC 43300 were used as infection strains, with each strain tested in an independent experiment group consisting of 10 larvae per group. Bacterial cultures were grown overnight in appropriate medium, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended in sterile PBS. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an inoculum of 2 × 10⁷ CFU per larva, and the actual infection dose was confirmed by plate counting. Larvae were infected by injecting 10 μL of the bacterial suspension into the hemocoel via the last left proleg using a sterile microsyringe, taking care to avoid puncturing the gut. One hour post-infection, larvae were treated with either compound 13394-2 or vancomycin, each administered at a dose of 2 mg/kg via the same injection route. A corresponding vehicle-treated group was included as a control. Following treatment, larvae were placed in Petri dishes and incubated under constant conditions, and survival was monitored daily for 5 days. At the experimental endpoint, dead larvae were photographed to document mortality outcomes.

Lethal peritonitis infection model

A mouse model of lethal peritonitis was established via the use of S. aureus ATCC 25923 (6 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, 200 μL/mouse, intraperitoneally)70. Two hours postinfection, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5/group): the treatment group (13394-2, 2.5 mg/kg, gavage in 90% corn oil), the infected control group (vehicle only), and the uninfected control group (PBS, no infection). The mice were housed individually and monitored for survival over 7 days. The sample size for the animal experiments was determined based on previous experience with the infection model and similar published studies. In addition, ethical considerations and the principles of the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement) were taken into account to minimize animal use while ensuring reliable and interpretable results.

Nalesthal peritonitis model

To assess bacterial colonization inhibition, the mice were infected as described above but with a reduced dose (4 × 10⁸ CFU/mL)71. The animals were assigned to treatment or control groups (n = 3/group) and treated with 13394-2 (2.5 mg/kg) or vehicle. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and the livers, lungs, and spleens were aseptically harvested, weighed, and homogenized in 1 mL of PBS. Serial tenfold dilutions were plated on TSA and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h, and colony-forming units (CFU/g tissue) were counted. Liver tissues were also fixed in 10% formalin, embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis.

Wound infection model

To evaluate the antimicrobial and wound healing potential of 13394-2, a cutaneous wound model was established72. After ether anesthesia and dorsal hair removal, 10 mm superficial wounds were created on the mice via a sterile scalpel. S. aureus ATCC 43300 (1 × 10⁷ CFU/100 μL) was applied and allowed to adhere for 30 min. Mice were divided into treatment groups (13394-2, 1 mg/kg, 50 μL topically), infected control groups (vehicle), and blank control groups (uninfected). Wound progression (redness, exudate, scab formation, and healing) was documented every 2 days for 11 days.

Disinfection model for food-contact surfaces

To simulate kitchen/industrial contamination, clean plastic lunch trays were surface inoculated with 200 μL of S. aureus ATCC 43300 (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) and dried for 30 min73. Trays were then treated with 13394-2 solutions (2–16 μg/mL) for 30 min. Bacteria were eluted with PBS and plated on TSA to determine the residual CFU/cm² after 18–24 h of incubation.

Antimicrobial preservation model on meat

Fresh pork slices (2 × 2 × 1 cm) were sterilized with 75% ethanol and air-dried73. Fresh pork samples were obtained from the longissimus dorsi (pork tenderloin) of clinically healthy pigs. S. aureus ATCC 43300 (1 × 10³ CFU/50 μL) was applied, followed by 30 min of adherence. Different concentrations of 13394-2 (2–8 μg/mL) were applied topically, and the meat was incubated at 25 °C for 24 h. After treatment, the samples were homogenized in PBS, plated on TSA, and incubated for CFU/g meat enumeration. Visual spoilage indicators (color, odor, and texture) were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed with ≥3 biological replicates. The data are presented as the means ± SD. Multiple groups differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, and comparisons between two groups were performed using the t-test (GraphPad Prism 9.0). P < 0.05 was considered significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, NS: not significant).
