Efficacy of a structured rehabilitation program for patients with terminal cancer: A multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nanako Nishiyama, Yoshinobu Matsuda, Noriko Fujiwara, Keiichi Narita, Shunsuke Oyamada, Keisuke Ariyoshi, Yukiko Hosono, Kazuaki Kushi, Katsuaki Ichimaru, Ritsuko Yabuki, Keisuke Kaneishi, Kiriko Abe, Takahiro Fujihara, Yoshihito Kono, Kozue Suzuki, Tae Kinoshita, Takeshi Sasara, Masato Nakajima, Hironori Mawatari, Hiroki Sugawara, Kentaro Nobutani, Yuriko Shindoh, Koichiro Kobayashi, Takashi Yamaguchi, Ryuji Iwaki, Yosuke Kitazoe, Yoshiaki Kanai, Junya Ueno, Tesshin Fukumi, Ryouhei Ishii, Nobuhisa Nakajima

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining activities of daily living is of great importance for patients who have cancer, and dependency is associated with psychological distress. However, evidence for rehabilitation remains scarce in this setting. The objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of a structured rehabilitation program for maintaining activities of daily living among patients with terminal cancer.MethodsThis multicenter randomized controlled trial across 19 Japanese inpatient hospices/palliative care units enrolled patients who had terminal cancer with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 2–3, a life expectancy ≥3 weeks, and no severe symptoms. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1, stratified by performance status and site) to either a 3‐week structured rehabilitation program that incorporated key elements of rehabilitation for patients with terminal cancer or usual unstructured rehabilitation. The primary outcome was a change in the total modified Barthel Index from baseline to day 22. Secondary outcomes included the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality‐of‐Life Questionnaire Core 15–Palliative Care score and safety.ResultsBetween July 8, 2019, and February 20, 2024, 130 patients were randomized (59 to the intervention group, 71 to the control group; 56 patients [43.0%] were women). The primary analysis included 77 participants who had complete data available. The mean change in total modified Barthel Index was −1.31 (95% confidence interval [CI], −10.89, 8.08) in the intervention group and −15.51 (95% CI, −24.02, −7.01) in the control group. The between‐group difference was 14.21 (95% CI, 1.77, 26.64; p = .026), exceeding the minimally clinically important difference (9.25). Patient‐reported physical functioning on the European for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality‐of‐Life Questionnaire Core 15–Palliative Care instrument was also significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group. No serious harms occurred.ConclusionsStructured rehabilitation maintained activities of daily living better than unstructured rehabilitation in patients with terminal cancer, supporting its integration into routine care even in the last stage of cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with terminal cancer typically experience progressive declines in physical function and activities of daily living (ADLs). Functional decline generally occurs from 1 to 3 months before death and is widely regarded as inevitable. 1 However, even in this phase, many patients wish to maintain independence as long as possible. 2 Loss of physical function reduces quality of life (QoL) for patients and can cause profound psychological distress. 3 , 4 , 5 Dependency and feeling like a burden to others near the end of life are recognized contributors to suicidal ideation and the desire for hastened death. 5 The objective of rehabilitation in oncology is to maximize functioning and improve QoL. 6 , 7 In recent years, its role in supporting patients' daily lives has received increasing attention in palliative care settings. 8 , 9

Previous studies of rehabilitation in the oncology field demonstrated that exercise improves physical function and reduces symptom burden in patients with cancer. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, most of those studies targeted populations with potential for physical recovery, such as perioperative patients, those receiving anticancer treatment, cancer survivors, or patients in palliative care settings with life expectancies of 3–12 months. In contrast, studies focusing specifically on patients in the last days or weeks of life, for whom functional decline is regarded as inevitable, remain scarce. The available evidence consists mainly of observational studies or case reports that cannot establish its efficacy because of the lack of a control group, heterogeneity of participants (e.g., including patients with longer prognoses), and reliance on unvalidated proxy‐reported outcomes. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These limitations leave rehabilitation practice insufficiently defined, and the optimal structure of rehabilitation programs for this population remains poorly understood. Moreover, the unstructured nature of these interventions results in difficulties in the standardizing and delivering rehabilitation programs worldwide. Therefore, we developed a structured rehabilitation program and evaluated its efficacy to help maintain ADLs in patients with terminal cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

We conducted a multicenter, parallel‐group, single‐blind randomized controlled trial comparing a structured rehabilitation program with unstructured (usual) rehabilitation in patients with terminal cancer across 19 inpatient hospices/palliative care units (PCUs) in Japan. Eligible sites routinely provided rehabilitation as part of usual care.

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the National Hospital Organization Osaka National Hospital (approval number 19031) and from all participating sites. The trial was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (identifier UMIN000037298; registered July 7, 2019), and recruitment is complete. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The trial protocol has been published previously, 17 and a protocol synopsis is provided in the Supporting Materials. The trial followed Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2010 guidelines for design and conduct and 2025 guidelines for reporting. 18 , 19

Participants

The target population was patients with terminal cancer who were admitted to PCUs, where patients are typically hospitalized for end‐of‐life care, with a mean length of stay of approximately 1 month in Japan. 20 Site investigators (palliative care physicians) recruited eligible participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) first admission to a PCU; (2) aged 20 years and older; (e) clinical diagnosis of cancer; (4) not receiving ongoing curative treatment (e.g., surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy); (5) a European Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 2–3; 21 (6) a life expectancy ≥3 weeks, estimated by clinician judgement with reference to a Palliative Prognostic Index score ≤6; 22 (7) the ability to initiate rehabilitation within 1 week of PCU admission; and (8) the ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe symptom burden (a score of 4 on any of the three Support Team Assessment Schedule [STAS] items: pain control, symptom control, or anxiety); 23 , 24 (2) a planned respite admission within 1–2 weeks; (3) severe weight‐bearing restriction caused by impending pathologic fracture, precluding out‐of‐bed mobilization; or (4) scheduled for neurosurgical analgesia or percutaneous vertebroplasty. STAS was selected because it is brief, minimizes patient burden, and is commonly used in Japanese palliative care practice.

Randomization and masking

After eligibility confirmation and consent, participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to the intervention or control group using a centralized, web‐based system with a computer‐generated, permuted block sequence (variable block sizes of two, four, or six) stratified by ECOG PS and site. Allocation was concealed using a secure platform accessible only to site investigators responsible for participant enrolment and randomization. The allocation sequence was managed independently by a statistician. Site investigators assessed eligibility and general condition but did not deliver interventions or evaluate outcomes.

Participants were masked to treatment allocation (single‐blind) and were not informed of their group allocation during the intervention period. The trial statistician remained blinded throughout the study. Blinding effectiveness was not formally assessed.

Procedures

Before randomization, a therapist was appointed to each participant. Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group, and the therapist was informed of the allocation. Rehabilitation began within 1 week of PCU admission and continued for 3 weeks or until withdrawal; both groups received usual care thereafter. Therapists and palliative care physicians judged whether rehabilitation could be safely provided on the basis of each patient's clinical condition; no protocol‐defined vital‐sign thresholds were specified. Concomitant care, such as pharmacologic treatment for symptom control, psychological care, and nursing care, was unrestricted.

Interventions

Occupational therapists or physiotherapists who were working in the PCUs before the trial and had provided rehabilitation served as treating therapists in this trial. Before the trial began, the principal investigator delivered a 2‐hour lecture to all participating therapists on the structured rehabilitation program and how to use the accompanying guide. Therapists could treat participants in either group during the trial because of a lack of resources, but they were not permitted to treat participants in both groups simultaneously.

Intervention group

Patients received a structured rehabilitation program designed to support the maintenance of ADLs in this population. Therapists delivered the program using the guide developed by our multidisciplinary research group based on a literature review and expert consensus (see Table S1). During the intervention period, therapists reviewed the guide before each session to identify applicable items, documented adherence after each session, and could consult the principal investigator if uncertainties arose.

The program aimed to standardize key elements of rehabilitation practice and included representative components, including: (1) collaborative goal setting with the patient, (2) promoting patient‐centered rehabilitation by facilitating patients' engagement and autonomy, (3) scheduling sessions when the patient felt physically and psychologically able to participate, (4) adjusting rehabilitation content and intensity at each session according to the patient's condition, and (5) encouraging out‐of‐bed activity through collaboration with patients and nurses. Sessions were scheduled for approximately 40 minutes per day, 5 days per week, to ensure sufficient time to implement these program components and to accommodate the slower pace of patients nearing the end of life.

Control group

As a control intervention, unstructured usual rehabilitation was provided according to routine practice at each site, approximately 20 minutes per day, 5 days per week, based on a previous survey in which this schedule was commonly reported. 25 The rationale for using usual rehabilitation as the control intervention, rather than a no‐rehabilitation group, was that rehabilitation was already routinely delivered at all participating sites; therefore, using a no‐rehabilitation group was considered ethically inappropriate. Therapists treating control group participants were not permitted access to the guide.

Measurements

Therapists assessed ADLs using the modified Barthel Index (mBI). 26 , 27 , 28 The patients themselves reported QoL using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality‐of‐Life Questionnaire Core 15–Palliative Care (QLQ‐C15‐PAL). 29 , 30 Site investigators evaluated PS and symptom burden using the ECOG PS, the Palliative Performance Scale, 31 and the STAS. The mBI is an observation‐based measure of independence across 10 daily tasks and was selected for its more detailed five‐point grading of ADL independence and its previous use in hospice patients with cancer (in which 0 indicates complete dependence and 100 indicates full independence). 32 mBI scoring followed predefined criteria; when scoring was difficult, support was provided by the principal investigator and palliative care physicians not involved in recruitment. The EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL is a validated short form of the EORTC Quality‐of Life Questionnaire Core 30 adapted for palliative care settings. The STAS is a clinician‐rated scale assessing symptom burden and palliative care needs. All measures were used in validated Japanese versions. Assessments were conducted at baseline (day 1 of rehabilitation) and on days 8, 15, and 22. As follow‐up, survival status and place of care or place of death, if applicable, were recorded 30 days after day 22 or 30 days after withdrawal if this occurred before day 22. All outcomes were assessed locally at each study site, and a centralized assessment was not performed. In addition, patient backgrounds (e.g., age, sex, primary cancer site, metastasis) were extracted from medical records by site investigators.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the change in ADL level, measured by the total mBI score from baseline to day 22. Although we acknowledged a risk of bias introduced by the therapists' measurements, we decided not to have blinded evaluators for the primary outcome because of insufficient resources. Instead, to mitigate potential bias, we included the patient‐reported physical functioning domain of the EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL as a secondary outcome.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included: (1) patient‐reported QoL, assessed by EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL domain scores from baseline to days 8, 15, and 22; (2) the proportion of participants discharged alive during the protocol treatment period; and (3) the incidence of rehabilitation‐related adverse events. The proportion discharged alive was assessed by site investigators. Potential adverse events were assessed at the time of occurrence by the rehabilitation therapist and site physician, including severity, duration, management, and relation to the intervention; only events judged related to rehabilitation were defined as rehabilitation‐related adverse events.

Furthermore, changes in the total mBI score over time (time‐by‐group interaction) and the changes from baseline to days 8 and 15 were compared as secondary outcomes. In addition, the proportion of participants with a decrease ≥10 points in the total mBI score, corresponding to the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 9.25 rounded to 10, from baseline to days 8, 15, and 22 was also compared as a secondary outcome.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis compared between‐group differences in the change in total mBI score from baseline to day 22. We hypothesized that patients receiving the structured rehabilitation would demonstrate better maintenance of ADLs than those receiving usual rehabilitation. The MCID for the total mBI score was set at 9.25 points, extrapolated from the MCID of the 20‐point Barthel Index scale. 33 Assuming a standard deviation of 15, 74 participants (37 per group) would provide 80% power to detect a 10‐point mean difference between groups at a two‐sided significance level of 5%. Accounting for 43% attrition (revised from the initially assumed 18.3% during the trial because of higher‐than‐expected attrition during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic), the required sample size was set at 130 participants (65 per group).

All analyses followed the intention‐to‐treat principle and included all randomized participants who received at least one session of the assigned intervention. Baseline characteristics were descriptively summarized. The prespecified primary outcome was assessed using a two‐sample t‐test at a 5% two‐sided significance level, with point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the between‐group mean difference. The primary analysis used observed case (OC) data. Sensitivity analyses were conducted only for the primary outcome. Missing outcome data among participants alive were imputed using multiple imputation (for participants discharged alive, assuming functional maintenance) and delta‐adjustment methods (for participants who withdrew alive, assuming functional decline and missing not at random). Death before scheduled assessment was not considered missing and was not imputed, in accordance with the estimand framework from Addendum E9(R1) in the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use. 34 Secondary outcomes were analyzed with OC data. Effect sizes were calculated using the Cohen d measure for continuous variables. For the harm analysis, the frequency and incidence rates of adverse events in each group were calculated.

All analyses were two‐sided, with p values ≤ .05 considered statistically significant. The principal investigator and the data center reviewed the data and confirmed the absence of any missing, inadmissible, or abnormal data. All statistical procedures were prespecified in the analysis plan, and analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Trial conduct was monitored by an independent trial steering committee and a separate data monitoring committee, which conducted ongoing monitoring. No interim analyses were planned. A predefined stopping rule was applied if no participants were enrolled for 1 year.

RESULTS

Between July 8, 2019, and 20 February 20, 2024, 130 patients were enrolled across 19 PCUs and randomly assigned: 59 to the intervention group and 71 to the control group. Harm and efficacy analyses included 57 of 59 patients (96.6%) in the intervention group and 69 of 71 (97.2%) in the control group who received at least one rehabilitation session. The primary analysis of the primary outcome included 36 of 59 patients (61.0%) in the intervention group and 41 of 71 (57.7%) in the control group who completed the day‐22 assessment. Withdrawal rates in the intervention and control groups were 39.0% and 42.3%, respectively, primarily because of death or clinical deterioration, consistent with the advanced disease status of this population (Figure 1).

Primary outcome: ADLs measured by mBI

Changes in total mBI score, including sensitivity analyses, are presented in Table 2. Secondary outcomes

Other analyses of ADLs measured by the mBI

Longitudinal changes during the intervention period demonstrated a significant time‐by‐group interaction effect (F = 3.31; p = .040; Figure 2). The mean change in total mBI score indicated a significantly smaller decline in the intervention group than in the control group at days 8 and 15 in both OC and sensitivity analyses (Table 2). The proportion of participants with a decrease 10 points in total the mBI score from baseline, representing clinically meaningful functional decline, tended to be lower in the intervention group than in the control group at all measurement points; a significant difference was observed in the OC analysis at day 15 only, whereas significant differences were observed at all measurement points in the sensitivity analysis (Table 3).

Patient‐reported QoL

As shown in Table 4, the mean change in patient‐reported physical functioning domain scores on the EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL was significantly more favorable in the intervention group than in the control group on day 8 (mean difference, 9.66 [95% CI, 1.23, 18.09]; effect size, 0.46; p = .025) and day 15 (mean difference, 13.39 [95% CI, 2.61, 24.18]; effect size, 0.55; p = .016), with marginal statistical significance on day 22 (effect size, 0.49; p = .054). No significant between‐group differences were observed for global QoL, emotional functioning, or symptom scales, including fatigue, pain, and dyspnea.

Discharged alive from PCU

During the 3‐week protocol treatment period, five of 57 participants (8.8%; 95% CI, 1.43%, 16.12%) in the intervention group and eight of 69 participants (11.6%; 95% CI, 4.04%, 19.15%) in the control group were discharged alive, with no significant between‐group difference (−2.82%; 95% CI, −13.36%, 7.71%; p = .60).

Adverse events

No rehabilitation‐related adverse events, including serious adverse events, were reported in either group.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial specifically designed to evaluate the efficacy of rehabilitation for patients with terminal cancer—a population largely excluded from previous studies. 8 , 9 This study compared a structured rehabilitation program with unstructured usual rehabilitation among patients admitted to PCUs. The structured program supported the maintenance of ADLs and the physical functioning domain of QoL better than the control group, with no serious adverse effects reported.

In the intervention group, despite targeting patients in whom functional decline is generally considered unavoidable, total mBI scores—a measure of ADLs—were largely maintained over 3 weeks, and the between‐group difference exceeded the MCID of 9.25 points, a threshold anchored to patients' perceptions of meaningful change in daily functioning. In contrast, the control group showed a decline of approximately 15 points over 3 weeks, corresponding to a shift from moderate to severe dependence and representing clinically meaningful deterioration. 26 These results were supported by consistent findings across secondary measures, including less deterioration in the patient‐reported physical functioning domain of the EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL (effect sizes, 0.46–0.55, indicating a moderate effect; between‐group differences, approximately 10 points, comparable to the minimally important difference [5–10 points]), 35 , 36 , 37 and a consistently lower proportion of patients experiencing a decline ≥10 points in the total mBI score, although this difference was statistically significant in the OC analysis only at day 15. These findings suggest that the program may have helped preserve functional independence near the end of life, with clinically meaningful effects. Because maintaining normal daily life is a key concern for patients at the end of life, and functional decline is closely associated with suffering among patients and caregivers, this program may have helped alleviate this burden. 3 , 4 , 5 , 38

Regarding QoL, the physical functioning domain was significantly better in the intervention group than in the control group, whereas differences in other domains were not statistically significant, although numerical trends generally favored the intervention group. Previous studies in populations that were not terminally have mainly reported benefits of rehabilitation on emotional functioning and fatigue, 8 , 9 , 39 whereas our findings suggest that the effect in patients with terminal cancer may be more focused on physical functioning, potentially reflecting the greater emphasis on ADLs in the rehabilitation program used in this study. The absence of significant differences in other domains suggests that broader psychosocial outcomes were targeted less directly by the intervention and that incorporating psychological components into the rehabilitation program may be needed to address these domains. The physical functioning domain of patient‐reported QoL represents patients' perceptions of their mobility and ability to perform daily activities. 40 Therefore, the intervention may have helped not only to preserve ADLs objectively but also to reduce the patient‐perceived difficulty or effort required to perform daily activities.

The observed benefit may be explained by the structured program, which enabled therapists to provide individually relevant rehabilitation, even as patients' conditions deteriorated, through collaborative goal‐setting, patient‐centered rehabilitation, and facilitation of patients' engagement and autonomy. In addition, systematic delivery of multiple program components as a comprehensive package may have contributed to the effect, even if individual components may not have been sufficient in isolation. Delivered session lengths below the planned targets may have reflected clinical tailoring to patients' changing conditions near the end of life, rather than simply indicating insufficient dose delivery. Overall, the effect should be interpreted as that of the structured program as a whole, including both its components and the rehabilitation time required to deliver them; because of the study design, their relative contributions cannot be determined and remain uncertain.

No serious adverse events related to the protocol treatment were observed in either group, confirming the safety of the intervention in this population and supporting its acceptability to patients, families, and health care professionals. 8 , 9 , 41

A key strength of this study is the use of a control group (randomized controlled design), a homogeneous target population (patients with terminal cancer), and validated measures, including patient‐reported outcomes. However, several limitations should be considered. First, therapists were not blinded in the outcome assessment; therefore, assessment bias cannot be fully excluded. To mitigate these risks, mBI scoring followed predefined criteria, and difficult cases were discussed and adjudicated the scoring process. Moreover, patient‐reported physical functioning on the EORTC QLQ‐C15‐PAL was included as a secondary outcome to provide an independent assessment of functional status. Because the efficacy was observed in patient‐reported QoL measures and observer‐rated mBI scores, we believe a potential bias from therapist ratings is unlikely to change the conclusion. Second, therapists treated participants in both groups during the trial for practical reasons (i.e., each participating site had a limited number of therapists; therefore, exclusive assignment of therapists to each group was impossible). Although simultaneous treatment and access to the program guide was prohibited in the control group, some degree of cross‐group contamination cannot be completely excluded. Third, formal testing of therapists' understanding of the training content and monitoring of program delivery fidelity were not undertaken; therefore, variability in intervention fidelity across therapists and sites cannot be excluded. Fourth, the observed effects reflect the combined effects of structured rehabilitation delivered according to the guide and usual rehabilitation, and we cannot determine the efficacy of rehabilitation compared with no rehabilitation. Fifth, the withdrawal rates approached 40% in both groups, largely reflecting prognostic uncertainty and variable clinical trajectories in terminal cancer. Such attrition rates are not uncommon and are consistent with those reported in existing clinical trials in similar settings of approximately 40%–44%. 42 , 43 , 44 The primary analysis was prespecified as an OC analysis that included completers; sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded similar results, although the findings may not generalize to patients who did not complete the protocol. Sixth, although the point estimate for the primary outcome exceeded the prespecified MCID, the wide 95% CIs suggest limited precision; a smaller, clinically unimportant effect cannot be excluded. Finally, because this study was conducted in Japanese PCUs, generalizability to other health care systems, settings, or noncancer populations may be limited.

In conclusion, a structured rehabilitation program demonstrated greater efficacy than unstructured usual rehabilitation in maintaining ADLs among patients who had terminal cancer with a life expectancy of at least 3 weeks. The structured program was implemented safely in this population. These findings support the integration of structured rehabilitation into the routine care of patients with cancer, even in their last weeks of life. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and address methodological challenges, such as potential therapist‐related contamination, assessment bias, and the absence of a no‐rehabilitation control. A stepped‐wedge cluster randomized trial with blinded outcome assessment conducted in facilities where rehabilitation is not currently provided as part of usual care represents a promising methodological option.
