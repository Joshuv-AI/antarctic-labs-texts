GWKBR: a novel method integrating machine learning and Bayesian inference framework to improve genomic prediction accuracy
Authors: Xue Wang, Jicai Jiang, Zhe Zhang, Yi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-additive genetic effects pose significant challenges to traditional genomic prediction methods. Inspired by the ability of kernel-based machine learning methods to capture non-additive effects and the accurate genomic prediction of Bayesian methods, we developed a novel genomic prediction method, Genome-Wide Association Studies-Weighted Gaussian Kernel Bayesian Regression (GWKBR), which introduces a new form of covariance structure prior distribution and integrates machine learning techniques (weighted Gaussian kernel regression and Bayesian optimization), Bayesian inference, Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML), genome-wide association studies (GWAS), and cross-validation process. By constructing a weighted Gaussian kernel, the method effectively captures non-additive effects and account for the relative importance of different single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We assessed its genomic prediction accuracy against six existing genomic prediction methods (genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP), BayesB, BayesR, Stochastic-Lanczos-Expedited Mixed Models (SLEMM), polynomial kernel ridge regression (KPRR), and deep neural network genomic prediction (DNNGP)) using simulated data, human data, and multiple plant and animal datasets (spruce, wheat, maize, cattle, and pigs). Our results indicated that GWKBR could flexibly adapt to diverse genetic architectures and deliver robust genomic predictions, with its advantage being particularly evident in plant datasets strongly influenced by non-additive effects. Among all 23 traits analyzed, GWKBR achieved the highest average accuracy for 13 traits and the second-highest for seven traits. Our findings demonstrated the reliability and robustness of GWKBR for genomic prediction in human and diverse plant and animal species. The GWKBR software is available at https://github.com/Wangxuer521/GWKBR/.Keywords: Bayesian inference, machine learning, genomic prediction, complex trait

Introduction

Genomic selection (GS) [1] has become an essential tool in animal and plant breeding programs [2–4], greatly accelerating genetic gain. For example, the genetic gain rate for yield traits in US Holstein has increased from ~50% per year to 100% [5]; in layer hen breeding, the application of GS is expected to double genetic progress [6]; the selection cycle for palm oil breeding has been reduced from 19 years to 6 years [7]; and in humans, genomic prediction of disease risk offers individualized prevention strategies and is expected to become a major tool in healthcare [8].

Statistical models are key factors influencing the accuracy of genomic prediction. The most commonly used models are the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) models incorporating genomic information, such as genomic BLUP (GBLUP) [9], SNP-BLUP [1], and single-step GBLUP (ssGBLUP) [10, 11]. However, these models assume that all single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) explain the same proportion of the total genetic variance. Biologically, it is expected that SNPs close to major causal variants will explain more variance. Besides, they assume that genetic variants affect the phenotype solely in an additive manner, failing to capture interactions between markers. Actually, nonlinear genetic effects (e.g. genetic interactions) are widespread in biology, and these effects are either difficult to detect in functional studies or challenging to model in genomic prediction [12, 13]. To identify SNPs with substantial effects and increase the variance they are expected to explain, Bayesian methods such as BayesA [1], BayesB [1], BayesC() [14], and BayesR [15] have been proposed. However, as linear models, they still struggle to capture interactions between markers.

To overcome the limitations of existing models in nonlinear fitting, non-parametric models with nonlinear kernels have gained increasing attention. Nonlinear kernels offer more flexibility in assuming the distribution of SNP effects, breaking the limitation of independent and identical distribution in linear models. Besides, nonlinear kernels map SNPs into a higher-dimensional feature space, thereby capturing nonlinear or non-additive effects [16]. Long et al. [17] showed that the nonparametric Radial Basis Function regression model outperformed the parametric linear model under strong non-additive effects, and remained competitive when additive effects account for the majority of total genetic variation. Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) regression [18] is a semiparametric method, and Jiang et al. [19] and Martini et al. [20] demonstrated that the Gaussian kernel within the RKHS framework can effectively capture higher-order epistatic effects. Li et al. [21] reported that polynomial kernel ridge regression (), which combined a polynomial kernel with kernel ridge regression, had a significant advantage over traditional methods in capturing non-additive effects. These findings highlight the potential of the nonparametric models with nonlinear kernels to improve genomic prediction accuracy in animals and plants.

Over the past decade, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have provided unprecedented insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying complex traits [22]. Incorporating significant quantitative trait loci (QTLs) identified via GWAS as separate random effects or covariates in GBLUP or Bayesian mixture models is an effective approach [23–25], but these SNPs or QTLs explain limited phenotypic variance and inevitably lead to false positive associations [26], while the remaining SNPs are still assigned equal weights. Consequently, some studies have constructed SNP-weighted genomic relationship matrices [27–29]. Since GWAS signals are known to be erratic and to mimic the modeling averaging occurring in Bayesian variable selection models, Meuwissen et al. [30] recently proposed GWABLUP, which weights the SNPs according to the moving average of posterior probabilities derived from GWAS, demonstrating superior genomic prediction reliability over GBLUP. In addition to GWABLUP, Stochastic-Lanczos-Expedited Mixed Models (SLEMM) [31] is another window-based SNP-weighting approach that efficiently performs Stochastic Lanczos Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) analysis on millions of genotyped individuals and weights SNPs based on proximity and minor allele frequency (MAF). However, these strategies are confined to linear model frameworks that ignore non-additive effects, while conventional nonlinear nonparametric models cannot effectively distinguish the relative importance of various SNPs [16]. These limitations underscore the necessity of developing novel nonlinear nonparametric models that can incorporate SNP weighting for genomic prediction.

This study proposed a nonparametric method, GWAS-Weighted Gaussian Kernel Bayesian Regression (GWKBR), which uses a trait-specific kernel weighted by SNP posterior probabilities derived from GWAS, introduces a new covariance structure prior distribution, and performs hyperparameter optimization and kernel selection through Bayesian optimization and cross-validation. We compared GWKBR with GBLUP, BayesB, BayesR, SLEMM, KPRR, and deep neural network genomic prediction (DNNGP) across 23 traits from human, simulated, and multiple animal and plant datasets with varying heritabilities and genetic architectures. Finally, we demonstrated the reliability and robustness of GWKBR in genomic predictions of multiple species.

Methods

GWKBR

To reflect SNP-specific contributions and capture non-additive genetic effects, we construct a weighted Gaussian kernel by using SNP posterior probabilities derived from GWAS results as weights, and perform posterior inference within a Bayesian kernel regression framework. For the prior specification of the regression coefficients, REML-estimated variance components are incorporated to reduce the computational burden of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, while the matrix and the regularization factor are incorporated in the prior covariance structure to induce structured shrinkage on the coefficients. Unknown hyperparameters in the model are determined through the Bayesian optimization algorithm and cross-validation process. To accommodate heterogeneous genetic architectures, we also construct an unweighted Gaussian kernel matrix, enabling GWKBR to automatically select between weighted and unweighted kernels based on the data learning and fit the corresponding model. Overall, the GWKBR model comprises three main components: GWAS and SNP weight calculation, model construction, and kernel selection and genomic prediction. Kernel selection and genomic prediction

For the constructed and models, GWKBR selects the more appropriate model through an additional cross-validation process based on the reference population, where represents the number of repetitions and refers to the number of cross-validation folds ( and are set to 2 and 5, respectively). First, the reference population individuals are evenly divided into groups, with groups randomly combined as the training set and the remaining group serving as the test set. Second, for each fold, GWAS is performed on the training set, and the ​ matrix is constructed based on the posterior probabilities of GWAS results to fit the model, while the matrix is also constructed to fit the model. The genomic prediction accuracy of both the ​ and models is then evaluated on the test set, repeating this for all folds. For genomic prediction accuracy, when the input phenotype is binary (i.e. encoded as 0 or 1), GWKBR automatically uses AUROC (area under the receiver operator characteristic curve) as the evaluation metric; for continuous phenotypes, it automatically employs the Pearson correlation coefficient for accuracy assessment. Third, the cross-validation process above is repeated times (i.e. GWAS analyses are performed), yielding genomic prediction accuracy results for both and models. Finally, the average genomic prediction accuracies of the and models are calculated, and the model with the higher average accuracy is selected as the final genomic prediction model. Then, GWKBR proceeds to the genomic prediction phase. Notably, to prevent information leakage from the validation population, all procedures described above were performed exclusively using the reference population, without introducing any information from the validation population.

As for the genomic prediction phase, if the final genomic prediction model is , a full GWAS is conducted using all individuals in the reference population. The weights for each SNP are calculated, and the matrix is constructed by combining these weights with the genotype information. Next, we fit the ​ model using the optimal hyperparameters determined during model construction and predict the genetic values (the total genetic value including additive and non-additive effects) of individuals in the validation population (split by cross-validation as described in the ‘Evaluating genomic prediction performance’ section below). On the other hand, if the final genomic prediction model is , we construct the matrix directly from genotype data, fit the model using the optimal hyperparameters determined during model construction, and predict the genetic values of individuals in the validation population.

Notably, the entire process above, including GWAS, SNP weight calculation, model construction, kernel selection, and genomic prediction process, is implemented automatically within the GWBKR program. The GWKBR software is available on GitHub at https://github.com/Wangxuer521/GWKBR/. Furthermore, GWKBR can directly perform model fitting and genomic prediction based on predefined models ( or ) and hyperparameters by specifying the corresponding command-line arguments. In practical application, when the reference population remains relatively stable, it is unnecessary to repeat kernel selection and hyperparameter optimization, as previously determined hyperparameters can be directly applied for model fitting and genomic prediction.

Existing methods

This study compared GWKBR with six existing genomic prediction methods: GBLUP, BayesB, BayesR, SLEMM, KPRR, and DNNGP. GBLUP [9] assumes that SNP effects are independent and identically distributed normal variables. BayesB [1] assumes that a proportion of markers () have no genetic effect, while the effects of the remaining markers follow a t-distribution. BayesR [15] assumes that marker effects follow a mixture of four zero-mean normal distributions with relative variance weights of 0, 0.01, 0.1, and 1. SLEMM [31] is a method that weights SNPs based on proximity and MAF and efficiently performs Stochastic Lanczos REML analysis. KPRR [21] is a machine learning method that combines a polynomial kernel with ridge regression and has shown certain improvements in capturing non-additive genetic effects. DNNGP [34] is a deep neural network-based method with a multilayer hierarchical structure, incorporating batch normalization, early stopping, and rectified linear activations to accelerate convergence. Details on these methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Simulation dataset

The QTL-MAS-2012 simulation dataset, provided by the 16th QTL-MAS conference (http://qtl-mas-2012.kassiopeagroup.com/en/dataset.php) [35], has been widely used for comparing the performance of alternative methods [36–38]. It consisted of 4000 individuals, including 3000 reference individuals from the first three generations and 1000 validation individuals from the fourth generation. The genotype data had five chromosomes, each simulated with 2000 SNPs. Three continuous traits (T1, T2, and T3) were simulated, with 50 randomly selected SNPs serving as QTLs for each trait. The effects of these QTLs followed a gamma distribution (shape = 0.42, scale = 5.4) [36].

Human dataset

We obtained data for three human diseases from the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA): coeliac disease (EGAS00000000057), hypertension (HT; EGAS00000000115), and type 2 diabetes (T2D; EGAS00000000115). The datasets included 5900 (2400 cases and 3500 controls), 6443 (2943 cases and 3500 controls), and 6475 (2975 cases and 3500 controls) samples, respectively. Coeliac samples were genotyped with the Illumina Hap300 array (295,453 SNPs) [39], while HT and T2D cases used the Metabochip custom array (196,725 SNPs) [40] and controls used the Illumina Hap300 array, yielding 19,395 common SNPs. SNPs with MAF < 0.01, genotype call rate (CR) <0.95, and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) test P-value<0.05 were excluded using PLINK [41]. After quality control, 258,053 SNPs (coeliac), 18,083 SNPs (HT), and 17,817 SNPs (T2D) were retained.

Chinese Holstein cattle

We collected data from 3180 Chinese Holstein cattle for two binary health traits: mastitis (MAST; 1648 controls and 1532 cases) and ketosis (KET; 2979 controls and 201 cases). All individuals were genotyped using the BovineSNP50 chip containing 54 609 SNPs from Illumina (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Genotypes were imputed using Beagle 5.4 [42]. SNPs with MAF < 0.01 and HWE test P-value<1.0E – 4 were removed using PLINK [41], retaining 44 994 SNPs.

Spruce dataset

The spruce datasets contained 1722 individuals with phenotypic data for three continuous traits: diameter at breast height (DBH), wood density (DE), and height (HT) [43]. The genotypic data was generated using a SNP-chip developed by Quebec Ministry of Forest Wildlife and Parks, which included 6932 SNPs. SNPs with genotype CR ˂0.95 and MAF ˂0.01 were removed, retaining 6785 SNPs.

Wheat dataset

The wheat dataset comprised 599 lines from a global wheat program of the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Centre, tested across diverse production environments and classified into four primary target environment groups [44]. Genotyping was performed using the 1447 Diversity Array Technology platform. Markers with MAF < 0.05 were removed, and 1279 markers were retained. This dataset is accessible through the R package ‘BGLR’ [45], with W1–W4 representing grain yield assessed in four environment groups.

Pig dataset

The pig datasets consisted of 3534 individuals from a single Pig Improvement Company (PIC) nucleus pig line, with phenotypic records for five traits [46]. Three representative continuous traits, T2, T3, and T4, were selected in this study. All animals were genotyped using the Illumina PorcineSNP60 chip, and SNPs with HWE P-value<1.0E – 4, CR < 0.95 and MAF < 0.01 were excluded. After quality control, 40 294 SNPs were retained.

German Holstein cattle

The German Holstein cattle dataset comprised 5024 bulls [36]. All individuals were genotyped using the Illumina Bovine SNP50 BeadChip [47]. After removing SNPs with HWE P-value<1.0E – 4, CR < 0.95 and MAF < 0.01, 42 551 SNPs were retained. Three continuous traits were available—milk fat percentage (MFP), milk yield (MY), and somatic cell score (SCS)—to represent different genetic architectures: (1) one or several genes with large effects (major genes) and a large number of genes with small effects (MFP); (2) a few genes with moderate effects and many genes with small effects (MY); and (3) many genes with small effects (SCS).

Maize dataset

The maize dataset included 2279 inbred accessions with two binary traits: yellow or white kernels (YWK; 14 controls and 1281 cases) and sweet or starchy kernels (SSK; 2940 controls and 141 cases) [48]. All lines were genotyped using genotyping by sequencing (GBS), resulting in 681 257 SNPs. After removing SNPs with MAF <0.01 and CR <0.95, 435 208 SNPs were retained.

Evaluating genomic prediction performance

To evaluate model performance, 5-fold cross-validation with ten replicates was employed for all methods. For each replicate, data were randomly divided into five folds, with four as the reference population and one as the validation population. Genomic prediction accuracy for each replicate was the average across five folds, and the overall accuracy was averaged over ten replicates. For continuous traits, Pearson correlation coefficient was used as the metric for accuracy. In simulated traits, the Pearson correlation was calculated between the additive genetic effect values and the predicted values, whereas in actual traits, it is calculated between the phenotype values and the predicted values. In addition, multiple t-tests were conducted based on the outcomes of ten replicates, with P-values adjusted using the Bonferroni method.

Computation time was used to evaluate the computing efficiency of each method. All computations were performed on an HPC cluster (CentOS Linux 7.8), using a single node per run. The node was equipped with 2× Intel Xeon Ice Lake 6348 CPUs (2.6 GHz) and 256G memory. However, DNNGP was conducted on a GPU node featuring NVIDIA HGX A100 GPUs (40 GB memory per GPU).

Furthermore, we performed post hoc interpretability analysis of GWKBR using KernelSHAP (SHAP KernelExplainer). For the background distribution, we randomly sampled 200 individuals from the training set and summarized them by k-means (k = 50). SHAP values were then computed for 200 randomly selected individuals from the test set, with nsamples = 1000 Monte Carlo samples. This analysis was based on a stable cross-validation fold, and global feature importance was reported as the mean absolute SHAP value across explained individuals, highlighting the top-ranked SNPs.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced GWKBR, a novel genomic prediction method that integrates Bayesian inference, weighted Gaussian kernel regression, REML, GWAS analysis, cross-validation, and Bayesian optimization algorithms. We evaluated its genomic prediction performance against six existing genomic prediction methods using simulated, human and multiple plant and animal datasets (spruce, wheat, maize, cattle, and pigs). We found that no single method consistently outperformed the others across all traits; however, GWKBR outperformed the other methods in the majority of traits. Among the 23 traits analyzed, GWKBR achieved the highest average accuracy in 13 traits and the second highest in seven traits (Table S1). These findings indicate that GWKBR can flexibly adapt to diverse genetic backgrounds and provide robust genomic predictions.

The robustness of GWKBR may derive from its flexible modeling framework, thereby flexibly adapting to traits with various types of genetic architectures. Firstly, GWKBR automatically selects the optimal kernel matrix through a cross-validation strategy. GWKBR employed the weighted kernel incorporating GWAS information for several datasets (simulation, German Holstein cattle, human, and maize datasets (Table 2)), indicating that this weighting strategy may be effective on these datasets. We observed that both SLEMM and GWKBR accounting for SNP weights achieved higher genomic prediction accuracy compared to GBLUP (Table S1). However, for the datasets where GWKBR employed the unweighted kernel (spruce, wheat, pig, and Chinese Holstein cattle datasets), SLEMM considering SNP weights did not demonstrate obvious advantages over GBLUP. This suggests that the two weighting strategies employed in this study may not provide effective information on these datasets, and GWKBR achieved higher accuracy by constructing unweighted kernels. Therefore, the integration of GWAS information does not invariably enhance predictive accuracy across all scenarios. This may be due to lower reliability of GWAS results in these datasets, potentially influenced by factors such as marker density, sample size or population structure. GWKBR evaluates the effectiveness of GWAS-derived weights through cross-validation [to reduce the effect of population-specific linkage disequilibrium (LD)] and flexibly selects between weighted and unweighted kernels. One limitation of this study is the lack of exploration of various weighting strategies. In addition to the weighting scheme used in this study, other weighting strategies exist, such as LDAK and LDSC models based on LD, as well as weighting methods based on functional annotation information [50]. Incorporating these strategies into the GWKBR framework deserves further investigation. Secondly, GWKBR utilizes Bayesian optimization to automatically determine the optimal hyperparameters λ and θ. This strategy combines Gaussian processes with acquisition functions to reduce the number of evaluations of the objective function, enabling efficient hyperparameter search. And this small number of hyperparameters will also substantially reduce the search space and computational complexity, conferring a computational advantage over machine learning algorithms that require tuning numerous hyperparameters (e.g. neural networks and ensemble methods). Compared to traditional grid search and manual tuning strategies, Bayesian optimization typically achieved superior genomic prediction performance and higher tuning efficiency, as it does not rely on manual expertise but flexibly adapts to different data input patterns and selects the optimal hyperparameter values for model fitting [51, 52].

Another key factor contributing to the robustness of GWKBR may be its consideration of non-additive genetic effects, as greater improvements in genomic prediction accuracy were observed for plant datasets with stronger non-additive effects (e.g. spruce and wheat), as discussed below. Studies have showed that incorporating non-additive effects into genetic evaluation models could yielded more accurate estimates of additive genetic variance and breeding values, thereby improving genomic prediction accuracy [53, 54]. However, conventional GBLUP and Bayesian methods consider only the additive inheritance, overlooking the potential for complex nonlinear relationships between markers and phenotypes. In contrast, GWKBR, as a nonlinear model, considers the non-additive effects through a (weighted) Gaussian kernel matrix. Studies have shown that the Gaussian kernel is able to capture non-additive genetic effects [19, 20, 55]. It maps the input data into a high-dimensional feature space, improving the ability of the model to capture complex nonlinear relationships. As shown in our results, GWKBR achieved greater improvements for the DBH and HT traits than for the DE trait in the spruce dataset. This may be because the dominance variance for growth traits such as HT and DBH was of the same magnitude or even larger than the additive variance, whereas wood traits (e.g. DE) were predominantly driven by additive effects with relatively small non-additive components [56]. In addition, GWKBR demonstrated substantial improvement in the wheat dataset, likely because the inheritance mode of grain yield in wheat is predominantly over dominance, with dominant gene action exerting a greater influence on the overall genetic variation of the trait than additive effects [57]. In contrast, the animal traits analyzed in this study were mainly governed by additive genetic effects. For example, in pigs, the dominance variances of the T2, T3, and T4 traits had been reported to be only 2%, 7%, and 1%, respectively [58]; for the German Holstein dataset, only additive effects were considered since EBVs were used as phenotypes [36]. This also explains why GWKBR showed a smaller advantage in the animal datasets of this study. Therefore, GWKBR may hold greater potential for traits that are strongly influenced by non-additive genetic effects.

BayesR, as an optimized Bayesian method, flexibly adapts to traits underlying diverse genetic architectures by modeling SNP effects as a mixture of four zero mean normal distributions with distinct variances [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that BayesR outperforms a variety of existing genomic prediction methods for both simulated and real traits with varied genetic architectures [26, 59, 60], which is further confirmed by our result that BayesR generally achieves higher genomic prediction accuracy than GBLUP and BayesB. Nonetheless, by leveraging its flexible modeling framework (as detailed above), GWKBR dynamically adapts to heterogeneous genetic backgrounds, achieving superior genomic prediction accuracy over BayesR in most scenarios. For GWKBR, the G matrix is incorporated into the prior covariance to preserve genetic similarity among individuals during the shrinkage of regression coefficients. Additionally, GWKBR integrates the additive genetic variance estimated by REML algorithm into the prior covariance and introduces a flexible regularization parameter λ to modulate the prior shrinkage intensity of regression coefficients, thereby balancing model fit and generalization ability. Accordingly, this prior leverages the population-structure information encoded in the G matrix and employs the regularization mechanism of λ to achieve targeted shrinkage of regression coefficients, thereby optimizing the bias-variance trade-off and improving genomic prediction accuracy.

In our study, GWKBR achieved higher computational efficiency than BayesB and BayesR (Table S4), primarily because variance components in the priors were pre-estimated using REML, thereby avoiding the substantial computational burden associated with MCMC sampling. Nevertheless, the complex computational procedures involved in GWKBR still rendered it less efficient than GBLUP and SLEMM, particularly SLEMM, which implements the stochastic Lanczos algorithm for REML within a mixed-model framework and enables rapid genomic prediction for large datasets [31]. Therefore, improving computational efficiency remains a key goal for future optimization of the GWKBR model. In addition, applying GWKBR to high-density markers may offer greater advantages. In our test scenarios, the accuracy of GWKBR generally increased with increasing marker density (Table S6). Markers with limited density often fail to ensure strong LD with all QTLs influencing traits. As marker density increases, the LD between markers and QTLs strengthens, allowing markers to explain a larger proportion of the genetic variance [61, 62]; meanwhile, the GWKBR model tends to construct a weighted kernel that more accurately reflects the relationships between individuals (Table S6), thereby achieving higher prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the increased QTLs in high-density markers may enhance the likelihood of identifying shared QTLs across populations [63], thereby improving the model’s transferability in cross-population genomic prediction. Moreover, the framework could be further extended in several directions. (i) Integrate biological prior knowledge. For example, SNPs near genes known to influence the target trait pathway can be assigned higher prior probabilities (π), embedding biological priors into the weighting process. (ii) Multi-kernel integration. With increasing availability of multi-omics data (e.g. transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic), GWKBR could be extended into a multi-kernel framework, constructing separate kernels for each omics layer and optimizing their weights jointly to improve genomic prediction. (iii) Incorporating large-effect SNPs. Combining transcriptome-wide association studies and eQTL mapping can identify significant SNPs regulating gene expression, which can then be included as covariates to further improve genomic prediction accuracy.

Conclusions

We proposed a method GWKBR, which combines Bayesian inference frameworks with machine learning strategies. GWKBR introduces a new prior distribution and integrates REML, GWAS analysis, weighted Gaussian kernel regression, Bayesian inference, cross-validation, and Bayesian optimization algorithm. Through simulations and real data analysis, we demonstrated the reliability and robustness of GWKBR in genomic predictions for human as well as various plants and livestock species, providing important insights for optimizing breeding strategies across species and traits.
