Evolving phase I oncology trials in the precision medicine era: design, outcomes, and geographic access
Authors: Y Katsuya, T Koyama, J Sato, M Okada, N Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver the past decade, early phase oncology trials have evolved from safety-focused designs to precision-oriented strategies. This study evaluated longitudinal trends, focusing on clinical outcomes, trial design evolution, and the impact of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) following its national insurance coverage in Japan in 2019.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included patients enrolled in early phase oncology trials at National Cancer Center Hospital (NCCH) between 2016 and 2023. Clinical, molecular, and geographic data were extracted from medical records. Categorical outcomes were compared using chi-square tests. Variables were compared between the early period (2016-2019) and the recent period (2020-2023).ResultsOver 8 years, 1243 patients were enrolled across 1642 early phase trials. The proportion of patients enrolled in genomically matched trials increased significantly from 10.5% in the early period to 28.4% in the recent period (P < 0.0001), in parallel with expanded CGP implementation, which rose from 44.4% to 82.8% (P < 0.0001). The overall objective response rate (ORR) was numerically higher in the recent period (12.6% to 15.9%), with an ORR of 29.1% observed in genomically matched trials. Small-molecule therapies demonstrated a numerical increase in ORR (9.8% to 18.2%), whereas antibody–drug conjugates maintained high ORRs (30.8% to 30.5%). The median travel distance to NCCH increased from 26.6 km to 32.7 km (P < 0.0001), indicating expanded nationwide referral.ConclusionsThe evolving early phase oncology trials, characterized by increased adoption of genomically matched trial designs, broader access to CGP under universal health coverage, and expanded nationwide referral networks, highlight a shift toward more precision in early drug development.Key words: early phase clinical trials, precision oncology, comprehensive genomic profiling, genomically matched therapy, cancer drug development

Introduction

Phase I clinical trials play a pivotal role in the development of oncology drugs by evaluating safety, pharmacokinetics, and early signs of efficacy.1 In the 2010s, early phase oncology trials in Japan were predominantly designed as bridging studies to replicate tolerability data in Japanese populations following studies conducted in Western populations.2, 3, 4, 5 However, over the past decade, Japan’s role in global oncology drug development has transformed significantly.6

National Cancer Center Hospital (NCCH) has emerged as a leading institution for early phase oncology trials in Asia, increasingly contributing to global first-in-human (FIH) studies and enrolling the initial patients worldwide.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 In 2023, 101 phase I trials were ongoing, including 39 FIH phase I trials. Notably, in 25% of these FIH trials, the global first patient was enrolled at our institution, and in 70% we participated in the initial dose-escalation cohort.14 This evolution reflects broader changes in trial design, such as the rise of biomarker-driven patient selection and incorporation of therapeutic efficacy endpoints in phase I settings—once limited to safety assessment.1,15, 16, 17

A defining factor in the evolution is the nationwide implementation of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) testing, which became eligible for insurance reimbursement in Japan in June 2019 across regions and cancer types. Nationwide implementation of insurance-covered CGP, with >100 000 centralized data integration through the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C-CAT),18 has significantly enhanced access to genomically matched clinical trials, promoting precision oncology. In contrast, access to CGP in other countries like the United States and in parts of Europe remains influenced by socioeconomic status, insurance type, or proximity to academic institutions.19, 20, 21, 22

Japan presents a unique health care context: universal health coverage,23 which ensures that nearly all approved drugs are covered by insurance reimbursement, providing significant patient-health benefits. Spanning >3000 km from north to south, Japan's geography is supported by an efficient public transport system that enables patients from remote regions to access early phase trial sites in urban regions like Tokyo. Consequently, NCCH has observed an expansion in the geographic catchment of clinical trial participants, supporting broader national engagement in early phase trials.24

Despite recent progress, no prior study has comprehensively examined the evolution of early phase oncology drug trials in Japan using institution-level clinical data to assess real-world patterns of trial participation, role of CGP testing, and treatment outcomes. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed early phase trials conducted at NCCH from 2016 to 2023 to evaluate longitudinal trends in clinical outcomes, trial design, and the impact of CGP implementation. We aimed to highlight the favorable environment Japan could offer: the established experience, well-developed clinical trial infrastructure, and consistent patient enrollment capabilities.

Methods

Patient selection and study design

This retrospective observational study included all patients who received at least one dose of investigational oncology drug as part of early phase clinical trials—including selected phase II studies—conducted at NCCH, from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2023. No specific exclusion criteria were applied. Patients could be included more than once if they participated in multiple trials.

The main objectives were temporal changes in genomically matched enrollment, objective response rate (ORR), and progression-free survival (PFS), alongside trial characteristic and geographic access metrics. The data cut-off for PFS was 1 July 2024.

The study period was divided into two prespecified eras: the early (2016-2019) and recent (2020-2023) periods. This division reflects the introduction of insurance coverage for CGP testing in Japan, which became available in June 2019 for advanced solid tumors.

Genomically matched trials were defined as clinical studies in which patients were enrolled based on the presence of predefined genomic alterations that aligned with the molecular target of the investigational drug. Studies based on other biomarkers such as immunohistochemistry were not defined as genomically matched trials.

Data collection and endpoints

Clinical data were abstracted from institutional electronic medical records and included patient demographics, trial enrollment characteristics, treatment exposure, efficacy and safety outcomes, and prognostic variables. Prespecified prognostic variables included age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), primary tumor type, and prior lines of systemic therapy. These variables were summarized descriptively.

Tumor response was evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.25 PFS was defined as the time from the date of enrollment in the study to radiologic or clinical disease progression, treatment discontinuation for any cause, or the date of last follow-up.

Travel time to NCCH was estimated using Google Maps, based on the public-transportation time from each patient’s residence to the hospital, assuming departure at 9:00 a.m. on a weekday. For patients residing in remote regions such as Okinawa, air travel, which is the only way to Tokyo, was included in the estimation.

This study was approved by the NCC Institutional Review Board (protocols UMIN000042487 and NCC 2014-148) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The individual consent for this analysis was waived.

The development status of all investigational drugs was assessed using publicly available databases, including GlobalData (GlobalData Plc, London, UK), ClinicalTrials.gov (United States National Library of Medicine, Bethesda, USA), and the jRCT platform (Japan Registry of Clinical Trials, Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and trial attributes. Group comparisons of categorical variables were carried out using the chi-square test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Time-to-event analyses for PFS were carried out using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. For patients participating in multiple trials, PFS were calculated separately per trial enrollment and included in time-to-event analyses as independent observations. Statistical analyses and graphical outputs were generated using GraphPad Prism, version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), and R, version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Geographic mapping of patient-residence data was conducted using QGIS, version 3.34.14 (QGIS Development Team, Beaverton, OR). This study adhered to the RECORD (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data) guidelines.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1642 trial participations were included in this analysis (Table 1). After excluding multiple enrollments, 1243 unique individuals were identified. Of these, 272 patients (22.2%) were enrolled in more than one clinical trial during the study period.

Among the participants, 51.3% were male. Most patients (69.4%) had an ECOG PS of 0; the remainder had a PS of 1. The median age was 60 years (range, 20-83 years).

The most common primary tumor sites were colorectal, non-small-cell lung, pancreas, ovarian, and bile duct cancers. Approximately one-third of cases involved cancer types classified as common by the World Health Organization.26 In addition, a substantial proportion of patients had malignancies more prevalent in Asian populations, including biliary tract and gastric cancers (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692).

Given that enrollment in phase I trials typically follows the completion of standard treatment regimens, the median number of prior lines of systemic therapy was three (range, 0-16). The number of patients enrolled in early phase clinical trials increased steadily over time, with annual enrollment exceeding 250 participants in recent years (Figure 1).

Tumor type: specific outcomes

Notably, the ORR more than doubled in certain tumor types, including non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), bile duct, kidney, and thymic cancers, malignant melanoma, sarcoma, and carcinoma of unknown primary (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692). The ORR for genomically matched trials increased from 22.9% to 29.1% (P = 0.55). In contrast, the ORR for non-genomically matched trials has not yet improved, decreasing slightly from 11.4% in the early period to 10.7% in the recent period.

Implementation of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) testing and efficacy comparing the two periods. The overall response rate more than doubled in certain tumor types, including non-small-cell lung cancer, bile duct, kidney, and thymic cancers, malignant melanoma, sarcoma, and carcinoma of unknown primary. early period, 2016-2019; recent period 2020-2023.

Treatment modality: specific outcomes

Among the various drug classes, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) demonstrated consistently high ORRs across both periods, maintaining a rate of ∼30%. Immune-checkpoint inhibitors and cytotoxic agents also showed relatively stable and favorable ORRs, ranging from 11.0% to 17.4%. Notably, the ORR for small-molecule therapies nearly doubled, increasing from 9.8% in the early period to 18.2% in the recent period. In contrast, other immune-oncology agents—including monoclonal and bispecific antibodies—exhibited lower response rates, although a modest improvement was observed (5.6% to 7.3%) (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692).

Median PFS did not show a statistically significant difference between the two periods [2.6 versus 2.8 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.93, P = 0.28]. In genomically matched trials, median PFS (mPFS) was numerically higher in the recent period (3.5 versus 4.2 months; HR 0.85, P = 0.57), whereas mPFS was shorter in non-matched trials (2.7 versus 2.1 months; HR 1.30, P = 0.031). In subgroup analyses by tumor type (both genomically matched and non-genomically matched trials), a significant improvement in PFS was observed in patients with sarcoma [HR 0.30, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.18-0.47, P = 0.017]. Although statistically nonsignificant, trends toward improved PFS were noted in patients with NSCLC, bile duct cancers, and gastric cancers (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692). PFS also varied across drug classes. ADCs consistently demonstrated the longest mPFS, increasing from 3.5 months in the early period to 4.2 months in the recent period (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67-0.94, P = 0.033). Small-molecule therapies also showed a notable improvement, with mPFS increasing from 2.8 to 3.5 months (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67-0.94, P = 0.033). In contrast, immune-checkpoint inhibitors and other immune-oncology agents exhibited relatively limited PFS improvement (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692).

Outcomes of genomically matched and non-genomically matched trials

The number of genomically matched trials significantly increased from 14.5% (14/96) (2016-2019) to 29.4% (25/85) (2020-2023). (risk ratio 2.03, P = 0.019) The proportion of patients enrolled in genomically matched trials also increased significantly from 10.5% during the early period (2016-2019) to 28.4% during the recent period (2020-2023) (risk ratio 2.70, P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692). The ORR across all trials showed a modest improvement, rising from 12.6% to 15.9% (risk ratio 1.26, P = 0.074).

Safety

Grade ≥3 adverse events (AEs), as defined by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, occurred in 20.8% of patients. The rate of treatment-related deaths over the entire observation period was 0.18%. Drug-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD) was reported in 1.6% of patients. Although most treatment discontinuations were attributed to disease progression, 5.8% of patients discontinued due to AEs, and 5.3% withdrew from treatment at their own request. (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692).

Among the 27 patients who developed ILD, 11 experienced grade 1 events, 10 had grade 2, 3 had grade 3, and 3 had grade 5 toxicity. Approximately one-half of these cases (13/27) occurred in patients receiving ADCs, whereas five cases were associated with immune-checkpoint inhibitors and five with small-molecule agents. Although ADCs were the most frequently implicated class, fatal (grade 5) ILD events occurred in two patients treated with small-molecule agents and one patient who received cell therapy.

Evolution of early phase trial design

The distribution of investigational drug classes shifted considerably over the study period. The proportion of trials involving cytotoxic agents declined, whereas small-molecule therapies consistently accounted for approximately one-half of all participants. Participation in trials evaluating ADCs and conventional immune-checkpoint inhibitors remained unchanged. Notably, a marked increase occurred in trials investigating other immune-oncology agents, such as monoclonal and bispecific antibodies. Enrollment in trials involving gene or cellular therapies remained relatively limited (Figure 3).

RR, response rate.

Although we primarily prioritized participation in dose-escalation cohorts, the number of patients enrolled in expansion cohorts—such as dose-optimization and backfill cohorts increased. In the recent period, 66.3% of the patients were enrolled in dose-escalation cohorts, whereas 31.1% were enrolled in expansion cohorts.

The proportion of patients with available genomic profiling results who were enrolled in genomically matched trials averaged 30.9% over the study period, with a consistent year-over-year increase. By 2023, this proportion had exceeded 40%.

Although large pharmaceutical companies sponsored most trials, ∼10% were phase I studies conducted by emerging biopharmaceutical companies, predominantly Japanese startups.

In recent years, expansion cohorts have increased in proportion, reflecting a growing emphasis on tumor- or biomarker-specific enrollment strategies. Although dose-escalation cohorts traditionally used an all-comers design, this approach has gradually decreased, with approximately one-half of recent dose-escalation trials adopting biomarker-driven or tumor-specific criteria (Supplementary Figure S4, and Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100692). The proportion of mandatory biopsies in dose-escalation cohorts remained stable at around 20% across both periods.

Development status of investigational drugs

The subsequent development status of all 181 investigational drugs evaluated at our institution was reviewed. Among these drugs, 46.4% were discontinued or had inactive development status, 21.5% remained in phase I trials, 11.0% advanced to phase II, 9.4% entered phase III, 3.3% were expected to receive regulatory approval, and 8.3% had already been approved for clinical use. Approximately one-half of the agents were either discontinued or designated as having inactive development status.

Implementation of genomic profiling testing

Following the introduction of national insurance reimbursement for CGP testing for patients with advanced solid tumors in 2019, 78.9% of patients enrolled in early phase clinical trials had undergone CGP testing before trial participation. During the study period, two tissue-based CGP panels (FoundationOne and OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel27) and one liquid-based panel (FoundationOne Liquid) were covered under the insurance system. Most CGP tests (83.0%) were tissue-based.

The overall rate of CGP testing increased significantly across all cancer types, rising from 44.4% in the early period (2016-2019) to 82.8% in the recent period (2020-2023) (risk ratio 1.86, P < 0.0001). In the early period, CGP testing was carried out primarily for research purposes. In contrast, during the recent period, a substantial number of cancer types reached a CGP testing rate of 100%.

Geographic expansion of patient referrals

When comparing the two periods, a significant shift in geographic distribution was observed. During the early period, patients were referred from 28 of Japan’s 47 prefectures, whereas in the recent period, this number increased to 43 prefectures.

The median distance from patients’ residences to NCCH increased significantly, from 26.6 km (range, 0.84-1175 km) in the early period to 32.7 km (range, 1.1-1927 km) in the recent period (P < 0.0001). A similar trend was observed for travel time, which also demonstrated a statistically significant increase (P < 0.0001), mirroring the pattern seen in travel distance (Figure 4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive, real-world analysis of early phase oncology drug trials conducted at a major national cancer center in Japan over 8 years.

Our findings highlight the increasing importance of early phase oncology trials in drug development, characterized by the emergence of novel therapies, and the growing number of trials focusing on specific populations. Importantly, our institution has demonstrated the capacity to support this transition by consistently enrolling patients in complex modern early phase trials. Early phase oncology trial design has increasingly shifted toward molecularly targeted strategies with improved clinical outcomes. In Japan, the timely introduction of insurance-covered genomic medicine has further increased patient engagement in trials, creating a synergistic environment that positions the country as a highly favorable setting for early drug development.

The enrolled patients’ characteristics reflect both clinical need and trial readiness. Most participants had good PS, and a substantial number had tumors prevalent in Asian populations, including gastric and biliary tract cancers. The increasing number of participants over time suggests enhanced institutional accessibility.

Treatment outcomes showed modest but meaningful improvements over time, particularly among patients enrolled in genomically matched trials. Key improvements were observed in genomic matching rates, trial enrollment volume, and response outcomes. Although the ORR of genomically matched trials reached 29.1% in the recent period, neither ORR nor mPFS demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two periods. These findings should therefore be interpreted as clinically meaningful numerical observations rather than statistical evidence of improvement. These results mirror global trends showing that biomarker-based early phase trials are increasingly associated with meaningful therapeutic efficacy, not only assessment of safety and tolerability.28,29 The high CGP implementation rate (82.8%) observed in our cohort underscores the effective integration of genome-driven medicine into routine oncology practice.

Among therapeutic modalities, small-molecule agents demonstrated the most notable improvement in clinical efficacy, with ORRs nearly doubling over the study period. In contrast, ADCs consistently showed the highest ORRs, but remained largely unimproved, suggesting a potential efficacy plateau. Immune-checkpoint inhibitors and cytotoxic agents maintained modest and stable responses, whereas other monoclonal and bispecific antibodies exhibited limited activity, with ORRs remaining below 10%. The modality-specific efficacy patterns likely reflect differences in target and patient selection. The improvement in small-molecule outcomes may be driven by more mature molecular targets and better-defined biomarker enrichment, whereas ADC efficacy appeared to plateau, suggesting a need for refined payload/target selection and resistance-informed combination therapies. Modest activity of immune-checkpoint inhibitors and limited responses with non-checkpoint antibodies underscore the importance of predictive biomarkers and data-rich designs that incorporate serial sampling to interrogate the tumor microenvironment dynamics and resistance mechanisms.30

Recent analyses have highlighted the potential risk of ILD among Japanese patients31; however, the overall incidence of ILD was low (1.6%) in our study, suggesting manageable safety profiles. The lower incidence of ILD observed in this study compared with reports from late-phase trials or real-world settings may be partly explained by differences in patient characteristics. Patients enrolled in early phase clinical trials generally have better PS, fewer comorbidities, and tend to be younger, traits that are known to be associated with a lower risk of drug-induced ILD.32

Although trial participation was once limited to patients residing near the capital city area (Kanto region), our data show a nationwide expansion in referrals, indicating increased awareness and accessibility of early phase trials. The next challenge is that despite broader national referrals, travel burden may still limit equitable participation. The decentralization of clinical trials may be useful for ensuring equitable access to novel therapeutics and enhancing trial diversity. Although there is an incentive for patients to come to our hospital from distant locations, issues such as transportation costs and distance were also revealed.33

Despite the encouraging trajectory, our findings also underscore ongoing challenges. Clinical outcomes in non-genomically matched trials have remained largely unchanged, emphasizing the need for more sophisticated biomarker discovery and validation strategies to optimize patient selection. Development programs relying on all-comers designs—once standard in early phase settings—are expected to further decline in favor of genomically stratified or biomarker-guided approaches. This trend reflects a broader shift toward precision drug development, where early phase trials increasingly function not only as safety assessments but also as efficacy-driven platforms for targeted therapeutics.

Most CGP tests used in patients of this study are tissue-based; however, this study does not compare tissue-based versus liquid-based CGP testing. As previously known,34 certain parameters like copy number variation show a lower detection rate compared with tissue-based testing, whereas liquid-based CGP testing has the advantage of shorter turnaround time compared with tissue-based testing. Nevertheless, as demonstrated in this study, genomic profiling is critically important for determining treatment strategies.

The study has several strengths, including a large sample size and comprehensive, high-quality clinical data with detailed annotation. However, limitations include retrospective design. Additionally, data on patients who were screened but not enrolled were not included in this analysis.

In conclusion, Japan’s infrastructure and policy environment have created favorable conditions for conducting early phase oncology trials. Continued investment in molecular profiling, trial network expansion, and industry-academic partnerships will be essential to maintain this momentum.
