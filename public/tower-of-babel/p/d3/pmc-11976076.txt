Malocclusion traits as risk indicators for depression in young adults: A population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmed A. Alsulaiman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough malocclusion has historically drawn attention for its effects on craniofacial development, oral function, and aesthetics, recent studies have started to investigate the possible association between malocclusion and other aspects of overall health, such as mental health.ObjectivesThis study aimed to analyze various dental malocclusion characteristics and their potential association with depressive symptoms among young adults. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey was used for data procurement.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted using a nationally representative population sample, including data from 3,806 participants surveyed between 1988 and 1994 under the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey III. The inclusion criteria were completion of the clinical assessment for malocclusion traits, being dentate, reporting no history of previous or current orthodontic treatment, reporting no dental pain, and responding to a questionnaire about psychiatric status. Statistical evaluations involved logistic regression modeling and chi-squared tests, considering the complex sampling design nature of the survey.ResultsCrude and adjusted logistic regression models indicated no significant association between malocclusion traits and major depressive episodes (MDEs). Conversely, the same models revealed a positive association between malocclusion traits (specifically, upper crowding and two or more cumulative malocclusion traits) and dysthymia in both crude and adjusted analyses.ConclusionSpecific malocclusion traits were associated with dysthymia, whereas no such associations existed with MDEs. These findings suggest that the association between malocclusion and depression is complex and multifaceted.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common mental health and psychological issues and has received substantial attention from healthcare providers (Brody et al., 2018). The American Psychiatric Association (APA) recognizes depression as a health condition that adversely influences emotions, thoughts, and behaviors (Gelenberg et al., 2010). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has estimated that roughly 8 % of American young adults aged >20 years experience depression between 2013 and 2016. The indicators and symptoms of depression vary in intensity, ranging from mild to severe, and can manifest as a loss of interest in friends and family (Brody et al., 2018). Depression affects mental and emotional well-being and physical health (Azevedo Da Silva et al., 2012).

Malocclusion, the third most prevalent oral health problem, is defined as an abnormality in dental alignment or improper alignment among the dental arches that exceeds the typical range (Gupta et al., 2016). It is a developmental anomaly of the skull and facial structures that affects the facial muscles, jaw, and tongue (Peres et al., 2007). The psychosocial consequences of malocclusion have been extensively studied, and research has consistently highlighted its undesirable influence on self-esteem, social performance, and overall quality of life, particularly in domains related to emotional well-being (Helm et al., 1985, Bellot-Arcís et al., 2013, Ben Gassem et al., 2023).

Emotional instability, introversion, anxiety, and unsociability are common in individuals with malocclusion (Kovalenko et al., 2012). They may experience heightened levels of psychological distress, encompassing symptoms that align with depressive states (Xiao et al., 2022). Despite accumulating evidence pointing toward the psychosocial and psychological effects of malocclusion, data on the association between malocclusion and depression are conflicting (Koskela et al., 2021).

Three small-scale studies (Zhang et al., 2012, Sebastiani et al., 2019, Rafiei et al., 2020) identified an association between malocclusion and depression, suggesting a potential link between malocclusion severity and mental health, but another large-scale study did not (Koskela et al. 2021). To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the association between malocclusion and depression in the form of major depressive episodes (MDEs) and dysthymia. This study aimed to investigate the association between dental malocclusion traits and depression (i.e., MDEs and dysthymia) in young US adults. Specifically, we hypothesized that specific dental malocclusion traits (particularly, upper crowding and multiple malocclusion traits) are associated with an increased risk of dysthymia in young adults.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was used to obtain data for this study. The NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the CDC, collects essential health data from 81 US counties across 50 states. Annually, it gathers nutritional, health, and societal data from a nationally representative sample using a multistage selection process. Initially, household interviews cover demographic, nutritional, and health-related questions. Participants are then instructed to visit mobile examination centers (MECs) to undergo thorough health evaluations, including laboratory, dental, physiological, and medical examinations Prior studies have described the survey methodology (Drury et al., 1996, National Center for Health Statistics, 1996). Participants willingly agreed to participate after being informed. The NHANES research procedures were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, adhering to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Drury et al., 1996).

Analytic sample

We used NHANES datasets containing data from the occlusal examination component and information on psychiatric interviews. Thus, the NHANES III (seventh edition of the NHANES survey) data gathered between 1988 and 1994 (i.e., 6-year data) were used in this study. The NHANES III involved a cohort of approximately 40,000 individuals, serving as a representative sample for an estimated 250 million noninstitutionalized civilians (Statistics, 1994).

Participants satisfying the following three conditions were enrolled in this study: first, completing the clinical assessment for malocclusion traits; second, reporting no dental pain, being dentate, and reporting the absence of any past or ongoing orthodontic treatment; and third, responding to a questionnaire regarding psychiatric status. The final analytical sample involved 3,806 participants, representing >49 million individuals in the US population.

Depression interview assessment

The Diagnostic Interview Schedule (DIS), as part of the NHANES III data collection, is an established and structured interview method aimed at diagnosing particular mental disorders in alignment with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) III criteria established by the APA. In NHANES III, individuals aged between 18 and 39 years were invited to participate in the DIS. It was used by trained interviewers within MECs and was confidential (Robins et al., 1981). Additional information on the administration of DIS and diagnosis of mood disorders has been reported elsewhere (Robins et al., 1981, Bland, 1992). The current study assessed two psychiatric disorders, MDEs and dysthymia, while adhering to the diagnostic criteria of the National Institute of Mental Health (Robins et al., 1981, Bland, 1992). Clinical assessment of malocclusion traits

Five licensed and calibrated dentists assessed malocclusion traits (incisor crowding, overjet, overbite, and posterior crossbite) in MECs for all individuals aged 8–50 years. The examiners’ intra- and inter-rater reliabilities ranged from 0.72 to 1.00 (Brunelle et al., 1996). Additional information regarding the reproducibility of oral examination components has been described earlier (Carlos and Brunelle, 1991, Brunelle et al., 1996, Drury et al., 1996).

Using a periodontal probe (PCP 2TM; Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) gauged in millimeters and placed parallel to the occlusal plane, maxillary and mandibular crowding were quantified by assessing the direct shift in position between the anatomical contact points of the front teeth in each arch. These five displacements indicated the level of anterior crowding in each jaw. The distance between the upper and lower incisors was measured in millimeters using a periodontal probe, starting from the center of the front surface of the most distal lower incisor and extending to the center of the front surface of the most distal upper incisor, aligned with the chewing surface. Overbite was evaluated by measuring the foremost upper right front tooth in millimeters using a periodontal probe. The left front tooth was used in participants in whom the upper or lower right front tooth was absent or damaged. Posterior crossbite was characterized by displacement, either buccally or lingually, beyond the cusps of at least two teeth positioned behind the canine, either unilaterally or bilaterally, while the patient was in occlusion (Brunelle et al., 1996).

The following criteria were used to define malocclusion: crowding (>2 mm), overjet (>3 mm), negative overjet (<0 mm), and overbite (>3 mm) (Little, 1975, Proffit et al., 2018). In addition, the number of malocclusion traits for each participant was summed and categorized as having at least two malocclusion traits, representing the cumulative malocclusion trait variable (Lambourne et al., 2007).

Statistical analyses

SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used to assess the collected data utilizing MEC examination weights and SURVEY methods. The associations among the primary outcomes, key predictors, and other covariates were analyzed using analytical chi-squared evaluations for categorical variables and independent weighted t-tests for continuous variables. The impact of malocclusion traits on depression was analyzed using logistic regression models, with the association presented as odds ratios with their corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Covariates significantly associated with the outcomes or main predictors or affecting the main predictor’s association by >10 % were deemed confounders. Potential interactions between the main predictors and the identified confounders were also explored. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Regarding sample variability, we accounted for the complex sampling design of the NHANES dataset by incorporating survey weights, stratification, and clustering provided by the NHANES. This approach helps manage variability and ensures that our results are representative of the US population. We also used appropriate variance estimation techniques to adjust for the sample design and ensure the robustness of our statistical inferences. Determination of the optimal sample size for the regression model followed a specific criterion: 50 events per variable plus an additional 100 participants. This formula, established through simulations by Bujang et al. (Bujang et al., 2018), is effective for calculating sample sizes in observational studies, irrespective of the observed effect size. Given that our study involved eight variables, the application of this formula yielded a minimum required sample size of 500 participants. This approach ensured the adequate statistical power and reliability of the regression analyses. The final analytical sample of 3,806 participants far exceeded this minimum requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Results

Descriptive result

The study participants (N = 3,806) had a mean age of 27.88 (range, 18–39) years. The sample was predominantly Caucasian (77.36 %), female (50.68 %), and educated beyond high school (84.38 %). Most participants were of middle-to-high SES (86.26 % combined), had normal weight (53.98 %), and were married (56.92 %). The racial composition included 10.24 % Blacks and 12.40 % Mexican Americans. Regarding BMI, 26.80 % of the participants were overweight and 15.46 % were obese. In terms of marital status, 36.50 % had never been married, whereas 6.58 % were widowed, divorced, or separated. All participants reported annual dental visits.

Overall, 10.27 % and 5.28 % of the participants reported experiencing MDE and dysthymia, respectively. Approximately 60 % of the participants exhibited upper crowding, whereas 68 % displayed lower crowding. Moreover, 25 % reported an increased overjet, with 1.21 % reporting negative overjet. One-third of the participants presented with a deep overbite, whereas 9.21 % had either a unilateral or bilateral posterior crossbite. Approximately 65 % of the participants had at least two cumulative malocclusion traits.

Discussion

This study identified that upper crowding and having multiple cumulative malocclusion traits significantly elevated the risk of dysthymia, but not MDE. The principal difference between MDE and dysthymia is symptom duration: MDE episodes last ≥2 weeks, whereas dysthymia involves milder, prolonged symptoms lasting ≥2 years (Skodol 1989). Recent studies have explored the complex association between malocclusion and depression. This study is the first to link specific malocclusion traits with MDE and dysthymia as defined by the DSM. We analyzed a subset of NHANES III participants, predominantly females, Caucasians, adults with at least a high school education, and individuals from middle- or high-SES categories. Most participants were of normal weight, were married, and reported annual dental visits.

Our results showed that the effects of certain malocclusion traits (particularly upper crowding and having two or more cumulative malocclusion traits) on depression are observed in the chronic milder form of depression (dysthymia). There are assertions regarding the potential long-term influence of facial features, particularly oral aesthetics, on psychological health (Borzabadi-Farahani, 2012). This influence seems to be particularly pronounced during life phases characterized by intense social and emotional interactions (Burden, 2007). A review of four high-quality studies established that individuals with malocclusion experienced adverse effects on their emotional and social well-being (Dimberg et al., 2015).

In addition, these individuals tend to exhibit a compromised body image, particularly in the anterior teeth, especially in those with crowding (Helm et al., 1985, Peres et al., 2007). Collectively, these findings suggest that malocclusion can increase vulnerability to psychological distress, depression, and negative psychological responses (Koskela et al., 2021). Psychosocial challenges are central to depression. Certain diagnostic criteria used to identify depression are, in essence, psychosocial difficulties themselves (Cabello et al., 2012).

The current evidence on the association between malocclusion and depression is conflicting. (Zhang et al., 2012) reported that depression levels in adults were significantly higher in class I, II, and III malocclusion groups than in the normal occlusion group. Two other studies yielded similar findings, establishing a significant association between anteroposterior discrepancies in malocclusion and depression severity (i.e., class I, II, and III malocclusion groups compared with the normal occlusion group) (Sebastiani et al., 2019, Rafiei et al., 2020). However, a high-quality study by Koskela et al. (Koskela et al. 2021) found no significant association between malocclusion and depression symptoms in children. These limited and contradictory findings highlight the need to further explore the complex nature of the association between malocclusion and depression.

Strength and limitations

The major strength of this study was its substantial sample size, making it the first extensive, nationally representative investigation of the association between dental malocclusion traits and depression. However, this study has some limitations. The data, collected between 1988 and 1994, may not reflect the current context of mental health and dental care, as societal and technological changes, such as the rise of smartphones and social media, influence these factors. The study’s reliance on the DSM-III criteria, now outdated by the DSM-5 and ICD-11, may limit its relevance to current diagnostic standards. Additionally, the lack of trauma history data, potential biases from unique NHANES III participants, and the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference, further constrain our findings. The racial and ethnic classifications used may also have affected data interpretation. These limitations underscore the need for more comprehensive studies.

Conclusion

Although our findings suggest that specific malocclusion traits are significantly associated with dysthymia, the lack of an association with MDEs underscores the complexity of the association between malocclusion and depression.
