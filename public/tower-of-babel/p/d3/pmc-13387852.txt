Developing Regionally Coordinated Infection Control Strategies for Carbapenem-Resistant and Carbapenemase-Producing Enterobacterales in Acute and Long-Term Care Hospitals: A Delphi Study
Authors: Naae Lee, Songhui Lee, Eunmi Kim, Seung Soon Lee, Won Sup Oh, Young Keun Kim, Eun Hee Song, Seungmin Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), particularly carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE), remain a major global public health concern. In Korea, reports from acute care hospitals (ACHs) have increased in absolute numbers, while those from long-term care hospitals (LTCHs) have risen both in numbers and in proportion. This Delphi study aimed to develop expert consensus to inform national CPE infection control policy by identifying key challenges, essential program components in ACHs and LTCHs, and strategies for regional coordination between healthcare facilities.MethodsA three-round Delphi study was conducted between October 2024 and February 2025 with 19 experts, including infectious disease physicians and infection control nurse leaders. The first round consisted of open-ended and multiple-choice questions to assess expert responses on the current practices and areas for improvement. Based on these responses, 85 items were rated on a 10-point Likert scale in the second and third rounds. Consensus was determined using content validity ratio, degree of convergence, and degree of consensus. Final strategies were then proposed by prioritizing items with the highest agreement and adapting them to the Korean healthcare context.ResultsOf 85 items evaluated, 56 achieved consensus. In ACHs, the highest priorities focused on strengthening surveillance activities for high-risk patients with CRE/CPE, antimicrobial stewardship, and healthcare-associated infection surveillance, supported by sufficient financial and workforce resources. In LTCHs, the top priorities included admission screening and testing for CRE/CPE supported by national reimbursement and effective communication within and between healthcare facilities to ensure timely information transfer. Experts prioritized implementing standardized transfer records to indicate whether patients are CRE/CPE carriers or belong to high-risk groups, and expanding national reimbursement for screening, testing, and related infection control activities as key measures to enhance information sharing and regional collaboration.ConclusionThe Delphi consensus identified key priorities to strengthen CRE/CPE surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship in ACHs, expand reimbursed screening testing in LTCHs, and establish standardized interfacility transfer communication. These findings support regionally coordinated policies to enhance CRE/CPE infection control in Korea provide evidence for national policy development.Keywords: Carbapenem-Producing Enterobacterales, Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales, Infection Control, Delphi Technique, Regional Coordination, Interfacility Communication

INTRODUCTION

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) are high-risk human pathogens that exhibit resistance to at least one carbapenem-class antibiotic.1 With high transmissibility and mortality, CRE pose a significant global public health threat due to high transmissibility and mortality.2 Particularly, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE), a major subset of CRE, are critical targets for infection surveillance and early containment efforts.3

In the Republic of Korea, the number of reported CRE cases has increased by 20–30% annually since it was designated a nationally notifiable infectious disease subject to complete enumeration in 2017.4 In 2018, 11,954 cases were reported (23.08 per 100,000 population), increasing approximately 3.5-fold to 42,683 cases in 2024 (83.25 per 100,000 population).5 The overall surge in CRE notifications has been largely driven by acute care hospitals (ACHs), which consistently accounted for the largest share. However, the proportion of cases originating from long-term care hospitals (LTCHs) also rose substantially, from 1,077 cases (7.0%) in 2019 to 5,815 (15.1%) in 2023, indicating their growing contribution to the national CRE burden.4

The World Health Organization (WHO), the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency have developed guidelines to prevent CRE/CPE transmission within healthcare institutions.1,5,6 However, ascertaining the extent of adherence in practice remains difficult. Also, the effectiveness of CRE infection control measures at the healthcare facility level remains limited. Numerous studies have highlighted the need for regionally coordinated response strategies.7,8 When developing CRE/CPE management measures, feasibility and effectiveness are essential. As policy recommendations cannot be fully substantiated by field data alone, expert consensus is needed to evaluate the feasibility, importance, and prioritization of such measures. Therefore, this Delphi study aimed to develop expert consensus to inform national CRE/CPE infection control policy by identifying key challenges, essential program components in ACHs and LTCHs, and strategies for regional coordination between healthcare facilities.

METHODS

Study design and procedure

This Delphi study aimed to develop policy directions and practical strategies for enhancing CRE/CPE infection control. Consensus was reached on key issues through three iterative rounds of expert panel surveys.

The study proceeded as follows (Fig. 1): First, 19 experts with extensive experience in infection control policy development and field practice were selected as panel members; each had more than a decade of professional experience in infection control and involvement in CRE/CPE-related activities. These experts, affiliated with the Korean Society for Healthcare-associated Infection Control and Prevention and the Korean Association of Infection Control Nurses, they had extensive knowledge of infection control in diverse healthcare settings, including LTCHs, and prior experience with on-site evaluations under the national infection control certification program.9 Second, in the first-round survey, we collected broad, open-ended expert responses on the current status and improvement needs for infection control in ACHs and LTCHs. Third, key main themes identified from the first round were used to construct second- and third-round questionnaires. Item clarity and content validity were reviewed and refined at each round. Finally, the responses from all three rounds were analyzed to derive consensus-based recommendations on essential program components for CRE/CPE infection control and feasible strategies for regional coordination within the Korean healthcare system.

To maintain panel anonymity, all Delphi surveys were conducted online. Demographic data were collected for descriptive purposes only, and no directly identifiable information was obtained. Individual survey links were distributed to each panelist, who were not informed of the identities or institutional affiliations of other participants. In the second and third rounds, feedback was provided only in aggregated form to preserve anonymity throughout the process.

Instruments

The Delphi questionnaires were developed based on a comprehensive review of national and international guidelines, policy documents, and prior studies on CRE/CPE and multidrug-resistant organism infection control. An initial pool of items was generated to capture a wide range of infection control practices, policy measures, and coordination strategies relevant to both ACHs and LTCHs.10 The first-round questionnaire comprised open-ended items to elicit expert opinions on current CRE/CPE infection control practices and improvement needs in ACHs and LTCHs. It also included structured Likert-scale items with space for additional open-ended responses. These items were informed by the 2024 national guidelines for healthcare-associated infection (HAI) management and core recommendations from major international organizations, including WHO, the U.S. CDC, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control. These structured items specifically assessed expert perceptions of guideline adherence, identified priority areas for improvement, and explored domains important to regional coordination and effective CRE/CPE control.

Drawing on the literature review—including the international standards noted above—and synthesis of first-round qualitative responses, an initial set of 85 candidate items was generated. The overall process of item generation, consolidation, and refinement across Delphi rounds is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. These items were systematically reviewed to remove redundancy, merge overlapping concepts, and exclude those considered insufficiently relevant or feasible within the Korean healthcare context. Then, the refined item set was organized into predefined domains by hospital type and thematic focus for use in the second and third Delphi rounds.

Based on the first-round responses, the second- and third-round questionnaires were refined to include items requiring quantitative consensus. In these rounds, panelists rated each item’s perceived importance for effective CRE/CPE control on a 10-point Likert scale (1 = least important, 10 = most important). Feedback summaries and mean scores from previous rounds were provided to facilitate reassessment and consensus convergence.

Through this three-round Delphi process, the questionnaire covered four domains by hospital type: 1) Guideline adherence and proposed improvements; 2) Recommended CRE/CPE content for existing guidelines; 3) Strategies to enhance information sharing for transferred patients; 4) Strategies for regional collaboration. Three independent infection control experts pre-reviewed the questionnaires for content validity, clarity, and comprehensiveness.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected via an online survey, distributed and returned by email, between October 2024 and February 2025.

In the first round, open-ended responses were categorized and coded for core themes, and structured item responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage). In the second and third rounds, 10-point Likert-scale items were used, and the interquartile range was calculated to assess consensus. Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hallym University Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2024-05-005). Before participation, panelists received an information sheet and a consent form. The information sheet detailed the study’s purpose, procedures, number of participants, estimated duration, potential risks and benefits, record confidentiality, data management/retention policies, and the voluntary nature of participation, including the right to withdraw at any time.

Consensus on adherence to infection control guidelines and priority areas for improvement

Expert panel evaluations of adherence to existing infection control guidelines for CRE/CPE in ACHs and LTCHs are presented in Table 2. In ACHs, adherence to “reprocessing of medical equipment” received the highest mean score (9.00), followed by “single-patient room or cohort isolation” (8.21) and “hand hygiene” (7.89). In contrast, “screening tests and surveillance cultures” (7.05) and “management of contact precautions for CRE/CPE patients” (6.89) showed relatively lower adherence, reflecting persistent challenges in implementing active surveillance and maintaining consistent contact precautions for high-risk patients. In LTCHs, adherence levels were markedly lower than those observed in ACHs, reflecting resource and infrastructure constraints. The highest-rated practices were “hand hygiene” (6.44), “single-patient room or cohort isolation” (6.39), and “reprocessing of medical equipment” (6.06), whereas “screening tests and surveillance cultures” (4.39) and “management of contact precautions” (4.11) again ranked lowest. These findings indicate that long-term care settings face substantial barriers to implementing core CRE/CPE control measures, particularly those requiring laboratory capacity or sustained contact precautions. Although modest differences in mean scores were observed for selected items, physicians and nurse leaders demonstrated broadly similar patterns, consistent with the aggregated consensus results shown in Table 2.

Consensus on emphasizing CRE/CPE-specific priorities within existing infection control guidelines

As presented in Table 4, eight of the 14 recommended items for ACHs and nine of the 13 items for LTCHs met all consensus criteria and were accepted with strong expert consensus. In ACHs, the highest-rated items were “implementation of an antimicrobial stewardship program” and “surveillance cultures for high-risk patients upon ICU admission” (both with a mean = 9.21), followed by “surveillance of HAIs” (mean = 9.00). These results indicate that experts recognize antimicrobial stewardship and early active surveillance as fundamental components of CRE/CPE prevention in acute care settings. For LTCHs, the top-rated items were “surveillance cultures at admission” and “communication within and between healthcare facilities” (both with a mean = 9.06), highlighting the need to prioritize standardized admission screening and communication and information exchange in long-term care. Conversely, items such as “chlorhexidine bathing,” “proton pump inhibitor (PPI) stewardship,” and “environmental surveillance cultures” received lower ratings and did not achieve consensus in either setting, reflecting limited perceived feasibility or lower priority for immediate implementation. Supplementary Table 3 compares mean ratings between physicians and nurse leaders. Overall, both groups demonstrated consistent prioritization patterns, with nurse leaders generally assigning higher scores, particularly in LTCHs. Although both groups prioritized antimicrobial stewardship, surveillance, and interfacility communication, a notable role-specific difference emerged: physicians rated “CRE/CPE surveillance for close contacts” significantly higher than nurse leaders in both hospital types. Aside from this specific item, professional perspectives on priority areas remained broadly aligned.

Although most items related to information sharing were rated highly, only seven were ultimately accepted. The top-rated item was “expanding national health insurance coverage for screening and management of multidrug-resistant organisms” (mean = 9.00, CVR = 1.00, CSS = 0.94), indicating strong perceived feasibility and urgency among experts. Other accepted items included “mandating inclusion of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs)-related information in transfer/discharge summaries” and “national reimbursement for surveillance cultures in LTCH patients, both of which were considered essential for improving continuity of infection control between healthcare facilities.”

For regional collaboration strategies, all items achieved a CSS value of ≥ 0.75; however, only the following two items were ultimately accepted: “standardized transfer form indicating CRE/CPE carrier or high-risk status” (mean = 9.00, CSS = 1.00) and “mandatory regional implementation of unit-based CRE infection control guidelines.” These results reflect the experts’ consensus that standardized communication tools and coordinated regional implementation frameworks are pivotal to establishing an integrated national response to CRE/CPE transmission. Both physicians and nurse leaders assigned high ratings to items addressing standardized transfer documentation, reimbursement for screening and surveillance, and information sharing during patient transfers, consistent with the consensus results shown in Table 5.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to establish consensus-based policy directions for effective CRE/CPE infection control by synthesizing expert opinions on guideline adherence, improvement areas, recommended infection control components, information sharing, and regional collaboration strategies. The Delphi survey revealed that adherence to infection control guidelines was generally higher in ACHs than in LTCHs, with both showing the lowest compliance in screening/surveillance cultures and contact precautions. Experts emphasized that institutional- and national-level support, particularly adequate national reimbursement for CRE/CPE screening in LTCHs, is a key determinant of successful implementation and long-term sustainability. Key priorities across both hospital types included antimicrobial stewardship, CRE/CPE screening for high-risk patients, HAI surveillance, and interfacility communication (e.g., mandating the inclusion of MDRO-related information in standardized transfer forms) as essential practices to strengthen infection prevention and control. These consensus priorities underscore the need for regionally coordinated infection control policies supported by continuous collaboration between institutions and policy frameworks.

Previous studies have consistently highlighted barriers, including insufficient infection control personnel, limited training resources, inadequate availability and management of isolation rooms, and lack of institutional support or financial incentives.15,16,17 Our findings are consistent with these barriers but extend existing knowledge by providing a prioritized set of regionally relevant strategies derived from expert consensus. To our knowledge, this is the first Delphi-based study in Korea to address CRE/CPE infection control, offering policy-relevant recommendations that move beyond descriptive assessments. Importantly, this study does not merely reiterate established infection control principles but systematically prioritizes them based on expert consensus, feasibility, and policy relevance within the Korean healthcare system. By applying multiple consensus metrics (CVR, CVG, and CSS), we identified priorities reflecting perceived importance, and the strength, convergence, and stability of expert agreement, providing a more robust and transparent basis for priority setting.

International case studies support the role of financial and policy-level interventions in improving infection control. In the U.S., the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services introduced a non-payment policy in 2008 for preventable HAIs, incentivizing hospitals to strengthen infection prevention efforts.18,19 In Europe, national surveillance systems, including the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network and the HAI- Network, are publicly funded and cover screening costs for designated patient groups.20 Similarly, Australia operates a federally coordinated system known as the Antimicrobial Use and Resistance in Australia, which integrates national data with financial support for MDRO surveillance.21

In South Korea, financial and structural barriers to infection control differ across facility types. In LTCHs and smaller ACHs, reimbursement is available for certain surveillance cultures, providing partial institutional support but not fully addressing financial concerns for patients or facilities. Concerns also remain regarding the costs of expanding isolation capacity and the potential increase in long-stay patients.22,23 While LTCHs receive reimbursement for operating isolation rooms, the absence of comprehensive coverage for admission or contact surveillance cultures continues to pose challenges.24 These imbalances highlight the need for infection control policies that integrate institutional-level financial and workforce support, national-level standardized reimbursement, and enhanced interfacility collaboration tailored to the Korean healthcare context.

Not all proposed measures achieved consensus, reflecting divergent expert views on feasibility and contextual fit. Adjunctive interventions such as feces and medical waste management, chlorhexidine bathing, and PPI stewardship were supported in some international guidelines.25 Yet, experts questioned their clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness in Korea, where evidence remains inconsistent.26,27 Infrastructure- and collaboration-heavy proposals—including expanding isolation capacity, automated laboratory reporting, referral networks, and regional infection control meetings—were judged administratively burdensome and lacking sustainable financial or policy support.28,29,30 Some structure- and collaboration-focused items in ACHs—such as expanding isolation capacity, automated laboratory reporting, referral networks, and regional infection control thresholds—did not meet CVR criteria despite high CVG/CSS, indicating heterogeneity in perceived essentiality despite generally favorable ratings. Rather than reflecting low importance, rejection of these items may indicate conditional or longer-term priorities requiring system-level investment and policy support, warranting further discussion with hospital administrators and policymakers and reconsideration in future consensus processes. These patterns suggest that expert disagreement was driven primarily by feasibility and scalability concerns rather than differing perceptions of importance.

Two items received particularly strong support: “requiring documentation of MDRO-related information in transfer and discharge summaries” and “standardizing infection-related transfer forms.” Inadequate interfacility communication regarding CRE/CPE carrier status or high-risk classification can increase the risk of transmission within both healthcare facilities and the community.31,32 Although university-affiliated ACHs generally document CRE status at transfer, surveillance cultures are not consistently performed across all facilities, which may result in unrecognized carriers and subsequent transmission, particularly in LTCHs where admission screening is not reimbursed.24 In both the U.S. and Europe, failures to provide standardized transfer documentation have led to inappropriate isolation and surveillance in receiving facilities, prompting regulatory agencies to recommend mandatory inclusion of infection-related information during patient transfers.1,33 Previous studies in Korea have also reported that failure to share MDRO-related information during transfers between LTCHs and ACHs contributed to CRE outbreaks.17 These observations support the policy recommendation to make CRE/CPE status and high-risk classification mandatory components of transfer summaries and discharge documentation, a critical strategy for regional containment.

In contrast, items such as “regular point prevalence surveys to assess regional CPE burden” and “mandatory regional compliance audits through site visits” received the lowest ratings among proposed strategies for information sharing and collaboration, suggesting expert disagreement regarding the feasibility or policy alignment of these specific measures. Nevertheless, these measures may warrant consideration in situations where CRE transmission remains uncontrolled, as they could provide an additional layer of oversight and accountability.

This study has several limitations. First, the Delphi panel comprised 19 experts, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Although participants represented diverse clinical and academic backgrounds, infection control personnel currently working in LTCHs were not included as panel members, which may have limited the representativeness of perspectives on day-to-day operational and implementation challenges specific to these settings. Second, the lack of consensus on several potentially important items (e.g., supplementary interventions like chlorhexidine bathing and PPI stewardship) highlights that expert opinions remain divided regarding the feasibility, effectiveness, and prioritization of these strategies in the Korean context. Since Delphi studies reflect expert perceptions at a specific point in time, this divergence in opinions highlights the need for further empirical evaluation before incorporation into practice guidelines, and applicability may vary by institutional resources. Third, consensus was not achieved on complex regional collaboration strategies—such as establishing regional referral networks or developing real-time communication channels—likely due to concerns about their feasibility under the current system, where financial support for surveillance and information sharing is insufficient.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first Delphi-based investigation in Korea to systematically gather expert consensus on CRE/CPE infection control. Through a rigorous three-round process involving experienced infection control specialists and nurse leaders, it defined a structured consensus framework that prioritizes feasible, context-appropriate strategies across both acute and long-term care settings. Importantly, this consensus highlights the need for regionally coordinated infection control systems that promote standardized interfacility communication and bridge gaps between acute and long-term care sectors. Beyond highlighting the importance of these strategies, the Delphi approach quantitatively operationalized expert agreement, providing a transparent rationale to support priority setting in future policy development. These findings lay the groundwork for practical regional networks and implementation models to contain CRE/CPE transmission within and across healthcare facilities.

In summary, this Delphi study identified expert-prioritized strategies to enhance CRE/CPE infection control across acute and LTCHs, highlighting the importance of realistic reimbursement mechanisms and standardized communication during patient transfers. Sustained implementation requires administrative and financial support through national and regional policy. Collectively, these findings provide consensus-based directions for future policy development and practical improvements in infection control systems.
