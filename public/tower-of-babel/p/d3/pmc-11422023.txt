Development of cost-effective inorganic arsenic extraction method for resource-limited regions: Analysis of efficiency and regulatory implications
Authors: Angstone Thembachako Mlangeni, Elizabeth Mhone, Alexander Ndhlovu Chibwana, Collings Chapachapa, Fatema Jagot, Lot Botha, Sydney Namaumbo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effectiveness of coke and lemonade extraction methods compared to the standard 1 % v/v HNO3 method for determining inorganic arsenic (iAs) concentrations in rice bran samples using a field-deployable method (Arsenator field kit). The limit of detection (LOD) for the methods was 45 μg kg−1, comparable to existing literature. The extraction efficiencies were assessed by comparing iAs recovery rates, with coke extraction yielding the highest recovery of 127.4 %, followed by lemonade at 116.2 %, and HNO3 at 100 %. Statistical analysis indicated strong correlations between the extraction methods, particularly between HNO3 and coke (Pearson correlation coefficient, 0.990), suggesting that coke extraction is a reliable alternative to the traditional HNO3 method. However, lemonade extraction showed a lower correlation (0.940) and higher false negative rates, indicating potential limitations for regulatory compliance. Notably, at the maximum contaminant limit (MCL) of 0.100 mg kg−1, coke extraction produced an 8 % false positive rate with no false negatives, while lemonade extraction had an 8 % false positive rate and a 17 % false negative rate. This study underscores the potential of coke extraction as a cost-effective and efficient alternative for assessing iAs levels in rice products, especially in resource-limited settings. Recommendations include the standardization of coke extraction protocols and the development of robust monitoring programs to ensure food safety and public health protection against arsenic contamination. Overall, the findings contribute valuable insights for improving arsenic detection methods and regulatory compliance in food safety practices.

Introduction

Rice, a dietary staple for nearly half of the world's population, plays a crucial role in the nutritional intake of numerous regions, especially in Asia and Africa [1] However, the increasing presence of inorganic arsenic (iAs) in rice has emerged as a significant and pressing health concern, raising serious health risks [2,3]. Inorganic As is known to be more toxic than its organic counterparts, posing considerable health risks when consumed in elevated amounts [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Prolonged exposure to iAs through rice consumption has been linked to a range of health issues, including cancers, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cognitive development problems [9,10].

The growing concern about arsenic in rice has prompted regulatory measures aimed at establishing maximum contaminants limits (MCL) for inorganic arsenic in rice products [11,12]. Arsenic, a naturally occurring metalloid element, exists in various forms, with inorganic arsenic being the most toxic. Common organic arsenic compounds include methylarsonic acid, dimethylarsinic acid, and arsenobetaine[7,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Inorganic arsenic can exist in various valency states, primarily as arsenite (trivalent +3) and arsenate (pentavalent +5) [19,20]. Given that rice serves as a dietary staple for over three billion people globally, the presence of iAs in rice poses a substantial risk to consumers.

To protect consumer health, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Commission have established MCL for iAs in rice and rice products [10,21]. This issue is particularly pertinent in Malawi, where the population, like many others, heavily relies on rice as a dietary staple [21].

The most toxic forms of arsenic in food are inorganic arsenic species (As (III) and As (V)), followed by organic arsenic compounds such as MMAV, DMAV, and TMA+ [10,22]. Although arsenic testing has a historical dating back to ancient methods like observing changes in the color of silver, the intentional use of inorganic arsenic as a wood preservative is now obsolete [3,23] Nevertheless, inorganic arsenic continues to enter our diets through contaminated drinking water and rice-based products raising concerns and highlighting the need for more comprehensive arsenic speciation data in food products [6,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

The traditional method of using 1–5% v/v for iAs extraction poses challenges in regions like Malawi, primarily due to its cost and limited availability. This situation necessitates the exploration of alternative extraction methods. Some alternatives, such as Coke and lemonade, are readily accessible and are being suggested as potential substitutes for HNO3 in extracting iAs from rice and rice bran. This need is heightened by the growing demand for rice bran for animal feed and nutritious baby food [6,15]. Given that inorganic arsenic is classified as a Class 1 carcinogen, having accessible testing methods is crucial for public safety [9,20].

However, food toxicity tests, including those for iAs, are still in their early stages in Africa, raising concerns about food safety. Additionally, many consumers in Malawi are unaware of the safety of various rice varieties, underscoring the need for an easy-to-use analytical method(s) for determination iAs levels in rice and rice bran. This need is heightened by the growing demand for rice bran for animal feed and nutritious baby food [7,16]. Given that iAs is classified as a Class 1 carcinogen, having accessible testing methods is crucial for public safety [10,21].

The justification for this study is rooted in the pressing global issue of arsenic contamination in rice, particularly in regions where rice is a staple food [3,15,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. With iAs classified as a potent carcinogen, addressing this contamination is crucial for protecting public health and mitigating potential health risks associated with rice consumption. The study is motivated by the urgent need to develop a reliable and easily accessible methods method(s) for extracting iAs in low-income countries, where advanced laboratory facilities are often unavailable. The high cost of laboratory equipment, such as HLPC and ICP-MS, coupled with the reliance on stable electrical energy -which is frequently inaccessible—highlights the necessity for cost-effective alternatives. In response, this study aims to identify suitable iAs extraction techniques that can be implemented in resource-limited settings. With iAs being classified as a potent carcinogen, the study aligns with the goal of protecting public health and addressing potential health risks associated with iAs in rice consumption. Moreover, the demand for rice bran is on the rise, necessitating iAs analyses for various applications, including animal feed and infant nutrition. Considering the limited research on food safety in the African context as well as the challenges related to traditional extraction methods for determining iAs in food staff products, this study fills a critical gap in the existing literature. By exploring the use of Coke, and lemonade as potential substitutes for HNO3, it opens the door to more practical and cost-effective arsenic testing methods. The hypothesis posits that coke and lemonade will serve as effective and affordable alternatives to HNO3 for extracting iAs from rice bran, primarily because they are readily accessible and less expensive. It is anticipated that coke extraction will yield results comparable to or better than those obtained using 1 % v/v HNO3 in terms of accuracy and reliability due to its acidic properties.

Additionally, the study expects these alternative methods to be suitable for field deployment, making them ideal for practical, field-based applications. If proven correct, the hypothesis would support the development of accessible and affordable arsenic testing methods, aligning with the needs of resource-constrained settings and contributing to improved food safety. The specific objectives of the study are to 1) evaluate the effectiveness of lemonade and coke as iAs extractants; 2) compare the arsenic extraction capacity of lemonade and coke with that of the conventional extractant, HNO3; 3) assess how coke's extraction capacity compares to that of lemonade; and 4) examine the arsenic content in rice bran from various Malawian rice varieties available on the market. Ultimately, this study seeks to promote safer dietary practices and ensure the well-being of populations relying on rice as a dietary staple.

Methods and experiments

Sample collection and preparation

Rice bran samples from three different cultivars were processed in rice mills in Lilongwe, with the samples categorized by variety and by district of origin. A total of five 5 distinct rice bran samples were collected: Kilombero rice from Karonga (labelled KA-Kilombero), Kilombero rice from Nkhotakota (labelled KK-KILO), Kayanjamalo rice from Mchinji (labelled MC-KYJ), Faya rice from Salima labelled (SA-FY), and Faya rice from Chikwawa (labelled CK-FY). For each type, approximately 50–150 g of rice bran sample were randomly collected and packed in zip-lock plastic bags. Each bag was meticulously labelled by cultivar type, the site of collection, (district of origin), and date of collection for reference in accordance with standard protocols [1,36]. The samples were then oven dried to a constant weight at 80 °C for 24 h. After drying the samples were thoroughly ground using a mortar and pestle in the laboratory and subsequently sieved through a mesh sieve of 45 [37,38] at the Natural Resources College (NRC) of the LUANAR.

Extraction methods and determination of iAs concentration in samples

Three different extraction methods were employed to assess efficiency of extracting iAs from the rice bran samples. These methods included 1 % v/v HNO3 extraction [1], Coke extraction, and Lemonade extraction. The following Paragraphs describe each of the methods in detail.

HNO3extraction. For this approach, a 1 % v/v nitric acid (HNO3) solution was prepared the concentrated nitric acid (70 % HNO3) using distilled water. Then, 50 mL of the prepared HNO3 solution was placed in a 250 mL beaker, and accurately weighed 5 g rice bran samples were added into the solution in the beaker. The contents were stirred thoroughly and the mixture was then boiled on an electric stove, maintaining a temperature within the range of 90–100 °C, for 20 min [1,39,40].

Coke Extraction. In the Coke Extraction method, 50 mL of Coke, purchased from a superstore, was placed in a 250 mL beaker, followed by the addition of accurately weighed 5 g rice bran samples. The contents were stirred thoroughly, and the mixture was boiled on an electric stove, maintaining a temperature within the range of 90–100 °C, for 20 min which imitated the HNO3 extraction.

Lemonade Extraction. For this method, lemonade purchased from a superstore, was used as the extractant. Fifty (50) milliliters (mL) of Lemonade were placed in a 250 mL beaker, and accurately weighed 5 g rice bran samples were added to create a mixture. The mixtures were heated on an electric stove, maintaining a temperature within the range of 90–100 °C, for 20–25 min which also imitated the HNO3 extraction.

For blanks, 1 % v/v HNO3, Coke and Lemonade extractants with zero addition of rice bran samples were prepared following the same procedure described above. After heating samples for 20 min, the extracts were allowed to cool at room temperature for 3–5 min and were then further cooled in a water bath for an additional 5 min [1,39] The cooled mixture were carefully transferred into an Erlenmeyer volumetric flask, and a tri-filter bung device equipped with detector slips (Red and Black filters) was used.

Subsequently, 2–3 drops of antifoam, 0.150 g (equivalent to one sachet) of sulfamic acid (Palintest, UK), and 0.500 g of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) (Palintest, UK) were added swiftly to the Erlenmeyer flask, one after the other [1,39]. Upon the addition of sodium borohydride, the flask was immediately sealed with the tri-filter bung device and left undisturbed for 20 min to allow reaction come to completion. Following this, a coloured spot on the filter paper was compared to the calibrated arsenic color chart to determine the concentration of iAs in μg. Alternatively, the filter paper was inserted into the Wagtech Arsenator (Palintest) to obtain the concentration of iAs in μg [1,39,40].

Data analyses

The gathered data underwent thorough scrutiny through rigorous analysis using the Minitab Statistical software version 19. Additionally, Microsoft Excel 2019 was employed to effectively visualize and present the data. This combined use of analytical tools ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the results obtained from the various extraction methods.

While lemonade produced slightly different values compared to those produced using 15 v/v HNO3 extraction indicated that Lemonade is a poor extractant compared to Coke. Thus, comparison of coke extraction with lemonade extraction produces a lower Pearson correlation coefficient (rho = 0.942), indicating a slightly weaker linear relationship between values produced in 1 % v/v HNO3 vs lemonade extraction methods compared to the 1 % v/v HNO3 vs coke extraction methods. The correlation between 1 % v/v HNO3 and coke extraction methods shows a stronger linear relationship compared to 1 % v/v HNO3 and lemonade extraction methods.

Compared to the reference extraction method values (here forth true values), at legislated MCL of 0.100 mg kg−1, 8 % of coke extraction method iAs values were false positive and none were false negative values; whereas 8 % Lemonade extraction iAs values were false positive and 17 % were false negative (Table 4). Similarly, at legislated 0.200 mg kg−1, both coke and Lemonade extraction indicated low false positive and false negative value. The study showed that coke extraction erroneously identified only 8 % of samples as possessing iAs above MCL of 0.100 (false positive) despite possessing safe levels of iAs; whereas Lemonade extraction erroneously identified only 17 % as below 0.100 mg kg−1 (false negative) despite possessing safe levels of iAs; and 8 % above MCL of 0.100 mg kg−1 (false positive) despite being higher. The findings suggests that coke extraction method is more conservative in its assessments, with a lower false negative rate (none in this case) but still has a notable false positive rate (8 %). The lemonade extraction method, on the other hand, has a higher false negative rate (17 %) at the MCL of 0.100 mg kg-1, indicating it is more likely to incorrectly classify samples with unsafe iAs levels as safe. Both methods perform better (lower false positive and false negative rates) at the higher MCL of 0.200 mg kg−1, suggesting they are more accurate when the regulatory threshold is higher. Thus, the evaluation shows that while both extraction methods have strengths and weaknesses in identifying iAs levels compared to the reference exaction method of 1 % v/v HNO3 extraction and maximum contaminants limits (MCL) thresholds, the coke extraction method generally performs better with a lower false negative rate at the MCL of 0.100 mg kg−1, whereas the lemonade extraction method has a higher false negative rate and similar false positive rate at this threshold.

The recovery efficiency tests revealed that 8 out of 12 iAs values extracted using the Coke method (67 %) were within ±22 % of the results obtained with 1 % v/v HNO3, which is considered acceptable. Additionally, 7 out of 12 (58 %) iAs values from Coke extraction were within ±17 % of 1 % v/v HNO3 values, while 9 out of 12 (75 %) values from Lemonade extraction fell within ±22 % of 1 % v/v HNO3 values (Table S1).

The overall relative standard deviations (RSD) between extraction methods were determined: Coke vs 1 % v/v HNO3 (±15 %), Coke vs Lemonade (±25 %), and Lemonade vs 1 % v/v HNO3 (±13 %). These RSD values were slightly higher than those reported by Mlangeni et al. [26] for iAs values determined on-site using FDM compared to those determined using HPLC-ICP-MS (14 %) and Bralatei et al. [26] for iAs values determined in a LAB using FDM compared to those determined using HPLC-ICP-MS (12 %) in which both were extracted using 1 % v/v HNO3. Nevertheless, variations between iAs values extracted using Coke compared to 1 % v/v HNO3 extraction (18 %) or Lemonade extraction (33 %) were insignificant, indicating good precision. However, the Coke extraction values within an acceptable range of 1 % v/v HNO3 were lower in this study than those reported by Bralatei et al. [41].

Pearson correlation coefficients between iAs values determined using 1% v/v HNO3 extraction and Coke extraction (0.990), and 1 % v/v HNO3 extraction and Lemonade extraction (0.940), indicated strong correlations (Table 1), with insignificant differences suggesting reproducibility of Coke-extracted iAs values when Lemonade extraction is employed. These experimental results demonstrated that 1 % v/v HNO3, a previously validated extractant, exhibited the highest extraction capacity for iAs, closely followed by Coke, which showed relatively weaker extraction capabilities. Statistical analysis confirmed significant differences in extraction capacities between 1/%v/v HNO3 and Coke, as well as between 1 % v/v HNO3 and Lemonade. However, no significant difference was observed in extraction capacities between Coke and Lemonade (Fig. 1, Table SM1).

Notably, Chikwawa-Faya consistently exhibited the highest iAs concentrations across both 1 % v/v HNO3 and Lemonade extraction methods, with concentrations of 87 μg kg−1 and 163 μg kg−1, respectively. Salima-Faya ranked second, displaying concentrations of 67 μg kg−1 with 1 % v/v HNO3 and 76 μg kg−1 with Lemonade extraction.

These results align with previous research by Gersztyn et al. [48], which noted that the solubility of iAs increases as the pH decreases. This observation explains the higher iAs concentrations obtained from Lemonade extraction, attributed to its lower pH due to its acidic nature. In contrast, the extraction efficiency of distilled water was lower, indicating that iAs is soluble in water but less effective compared to acidic extractants.

Importantly, all samples complied with regulatory limits for iAs in food. Both the World Health Organization's Codex Alimentarius and the European Commission have set a maximum limit of 0.10 mg kg−1 for iAs in rice products intended for children. Data clearly indicates that samples extracted with Lemonade exceeded this limit, specifically Chikwawa-Faya (0.163 mg kg−1) and Nkhotakota-kilombero (0.110 mg kg−1), making them unsuitable for children's products. However, the regulatory limit for certain puffed rice products is slightly higher at 0.30 mg kg−1, and all samples, regardless of the extraction method, remained below this limit, suggesting their suitability for adult consumption and puffed rice product production.

These findings are supported by extensive research efforts aimed at mitigating iAs contamination in rice and rice products. Studies by Refs. [3,16,41,49,50] have contributed to various methods for detecting and quantifying iAs. These methods, including HPLC coupled to Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS), are considered reliable but come with high costs and limited availability in routine analytical laboratories. More cost-effective methods for iAs detection, such as the Hydride Generation (HG) technique coupled with Atomic Fluorescence Spectrometry (AFS), have been explored [51]. Additionally, Bralatei et al. (2017, 2015) adapted a commercial arsenic field kit into a field-deployable method for iAs determination, highlighting the importance of developing portable and affordable techniques for iAs assessment, particularly in low-income countries with limited access to advanced laboratory equipment.

This study underscores the efficacy of different extractants for iAs extraction from rice bran and highlights variations in iAs concentrations across districts. It emphasizes the need for field-deployable methods to facilitate iAs assessment in regions lacking advanced laboratory facilities. Furthermore, this research contributes to efforts addressing iAs contamination in rice products and offers insights into meeting regulatory standards for safer consumption, contributing to broader discussions on arsenic in rice and the development of practical solutions for enhanced food safety.

Conclusions

The research introduces and evaluates coke and lemonade as cost-effective alternatives to 1 % v/v HNO3 for extracting inorganic arsenic (iAs) from rice bran. This study provides a comparative analysis of extraction efficiency among these methods. The results show that coke extraction, with a mean iAs recovery of 127.4 % compared to 116.2 % for lemonade and 100 % for 1% v/v HNO3, is not only comparable but in some cases more effective than the traditional 1 % v/v HNO3 method. This makes coke a viable option for field deployments where resources are limited. A detailed comparison highlights coke's superior performance in extracting iAs with an average Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.990 when compared to 1 % v/v HNO3. This indicates a strong correlation and high reproducibility. In contrast, lemonade extraction, although effective, showed a weaker correlation with a Pearson coefficient of 0.940 compared to 1 % v/v HNO3. The study also identifies the practical and regulatory implications of these methods. Coke and lemonade extractions are found to be effective in meeting regulatory standards, particularly for areas with high arsenic contamination. For instance, samples extracted using coke had an 8 % false positive rate and no false negatives at the 0.100 mg kg−1 maximum contaminable limit (MCL), while lemonade extraction had an 8 % false positive rate and a 17 % false negative rate. These findings contribute to improved food safety practices by providing reliable and cost-effective options for arsenic detection in regions with limited resources.

Based on the study's findings and innovations outlined in this study, several recommendations are proposed to enhance arsenic detection and food safety. Firstly, coke extraction should be adopted for field deployments. The study demonstrates that coke extraction not only performs comparably to the traditional 1 % v/v HNO3 method but also, in some cases, offers superior accuracy and reliability. This makes it a practical and effective option for on-site arsenic detection, especially in resource-limited settings. Secondly, standardizing coke extraction as an alternative to 1 % v/v HNO3 is recommended. By establishing standardized protocols for coke extraction, consistency and reliability in arsenic measurements can be achieved across different testing environments. This would facilitate easier comparison of results and improve overall testing accuracy. While lemonade extraction has potential, its slightly lower accuracy and higher false negative rate compared to coke suggest that further refinement is needed. Therefore, improving lemonade extraction techniques should be a priority. Research should focus on optimizing this method to enhance its reliability, particularly in areas with high arsenic contamination.

Additionally, the establishment of robust monitoring programs is crucial. Regular assessments of iAs levels in rice products should be implemented, with particular attention to regions known for high contamination, such as Chikwawa and Nkhotakota. Such programs will ensure compliance with regulatory limits and help protect public health. It is also important to educate stakeholders about iAs contamination. Increasing awareness among consumers, farmers, and other stakeholders about the risks associated with arsenic and the importance of using validated testing methods can mitigate health risks and promote safer consumption practices. Investing in cost-effective testing methods is another key recommendation. Further research should focus on developing affordable and accurate arsenic detection methods suitable for low-income countries. Innovations that reduce testing costs while maintaining accuracy will be crucial for widespread implementation and impact. Moreover, regular review and updating of regulatory limits is essential. Ensuring that safety standards reflect the latest scientific research and testing methods will provide adequate protection for consumers and adapt to new findings. Finally, supporting research on alternative extractants could expand the options available for arsenic detection. Investing in the exploration and validation of other potential extractants may lead to even greater efficiency or cost-effectiveness in testing methods. By following these recommendations, stakeholders can enhance arsenic detection practices, ensure regulatory compliance, and contribute to improved food safety and public health.
