Mechanisms of multi-layered Rayleigh noise in Brillouin optical correlation-domain reflectometry
Authors: Keita Kikuchi, Heeyoung Lee, Ryo Inoue, Kohei Noda, Yosuke Mizuno
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In Brillouin optical correlation-domain reflectometry (BOCDR), sinusoidal modulation is applied to the output frequency of a light source, with spatial resolution inversely related to the modulation amplitude. We have developed an effective method to estimate the modulation amplitude using the width of the noise spectrum caused by Rayleigh scattering, eliminating the need for an optical spectrum analyzer or modifications to existing equipment. However, the Rayleigh noise spectrum often displays a three-layered structure, complicating the identification of the appropriate spectral components for estimating the modulation amplitude. In this work, we investigate the origins of this three-layered Rayleigh noise spectrum and identify the directivity of an optical circulator as the source of the third noise component. As replacing the circulator with alternative optical components is not easy, it remains an essential part of the system. Our analysis shows that the third noise component exhibits significantly small variation in spectral width with changes in modulation frequency compared to the first and second components. This characteristic allows for the effective separation and identification of the third noise component, thereby enhancing the accuracy and convenience of modulation amplitude estimation in BOCDR.

Introduction

Optical fiber sensors utilizing Brillouin scattering for distributed strain and temperature measurements can achieve relatively high stability based on the frequency shift of the scattered spectrum1–5. This technology is expected to be applied to the structural health monitoring of social infrastructures. Among various measurement techniques, those using correlation-domain methods to achieve position resolution have attracted considerable attention for their ability to combine high spatial resolution and random accessibility, compared to time-domain6–9 or frequency-domain methods10,11. The correlation-domain method has two main configurations: Brillouin optical correlation-domain analysis (BOCDA)12–17, which uses stimulated Brillouin scattering via counter-propagating pump and probe lights, and Brillouin optical correlation-domain reflectometry (BOCDR)18–29, which relies on spontaneous Brillouin scattering from one end of the fiber under test (FUT). This study focuses on BOCDR.

In BOCDR, the light is frequency-modulated and split into two parts: one is injected to the FUT via an optical circulator, and the other interferes with the backscattered light from the FUT. The position where the phase difference in the frequency modulation between the two light paths is an integer multiple of 2π, creating a correlation peak, allows for the selective detection of scattered light at that position. By controlling the modulation frequency, the correlation peak can be swept along the FUT, enabling distributed measurements along its entire length. The spatial resolution of BOCDR is reported to depend on both the modulation amplitude and frequency18,19. Modulation amplitude has traditionally been measured by observing the modulated light spectrum using an optical spectrum analyzer (OSA)18 or a heterodyne detection system with an electrical spectrum analyzer (ESA)30. However, an OSA has limitations such as low frequency resolution, high cost, and bulkiness. While heterodyne detection systems using an ESA offer high-frequency resolution, they require separate measurement systems, reducing convenience27.

Therefore, we have developed a new modulation amplitude estimation method using Rayleigh noise spectrum observed in BOCDR (note that Rayleigh scattering occurs throughout the FUT, similar to Brillouin scattering), which enhances measurement accuracy and convenience without requiring changes to the experimental setup31,32. This method has been demonstrated to be applicable to any length of FUT. This method estimates the modulation amplitude by using the high-frequency component of two Rayleigh noise spectrum components that vary depending on the modulation frequency31–34. However, a third Rayleigh noise component was sometimes observed, making it unclear which component to use for estimating the modulation amplitude.

In this work, we investigate the origin of the third noise component and identify the directivity (DIR) of an optical circulator as its source. As substituting the circulator with other optical components is impractical, it remains a crucial part of the system. Nevertheless, our examination of the modulation frequency dependencies of the spectral widths of the three noise components reveals that the third component undergoes notably less variation than those of the first and second components. This distinctive feature allows for the effective separation of the third noise component from the others.

The beat frequency components from this interference are observed with an ESA, and the Brillouin gain spectrum (BGS) is derived. The peak frequency of the BGS, known as the Brillouin frequency shift (BFS), linearly depends on the strain or temperature applied to the FUT. Apart from this, an additional third Rayleigh noise component can arise if there are reflection points within the FUT, as illustrated in Fig. 427–29,35–37. These reflections can occur due to (i) the connection point between the FUT and the second port of the optical circulator29,36, (ii) Fresnel reflection at the FUT end28,29,35, or (iii) the DIR of the circulator, i.e., the component of light traveling from the first port to the third port37. In this study, we experimentally investigate the primary cause of this noise component in BOCDR. If the primary cause is (iii) the DIR of the circulator, the reflected light component at the circulator acts as the reference light, and if the reference light necessary for the original distributed measurement is absent, only the third noise component is expected to be observed. In addition, when the modulation frequency controlling the correlation peak interval is fixed, as the length of the FUT becomes shorter, the spectrum width is expected to narrow (refer to Fig. 5).Fig. 4Schematic illustration for the third-tier generation in Rayleigh noise spectrum.Fig. 5Schematic Rayleigh noise spectra observed when the reference path is interrupted.

Experiments

First, we investigated the potential causes of the third noise component, specifically (i) reflections at connector points and (ii) Fresnel reflection at the FUT end. The BOCDR setup used in the experiment is shown in Fig. 6. A 1550 nm distributed feedback laser was used as the light source. The pump light, Stokes light, and reference light were amplified to 24.0, 3.8 and 5.1 dBm, respectively, using erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs). A delay line of approximately 120 m was inserted into the reference arm to control the optical path length difference between the Stokes and reference arms and to ensure that the 1st-order correlation peak was located within the FUT. As shown in Fig. 7, two types of FUTs were prepared: (a) angled-physical-contact (APC)-connected and (b) fusion-spliced to the second port of the circulator. To suppress the influence of Fresnel reflections at the end of the FUT, bending losses were applied near the end. The total length of both FUTs, including the second port of the circulator, was approximately 21 m. The resolution bandwidth (RBW), video bandwidth (VBW), sweep time, and averaging count of the ESA were 3 MHz, 1 kHz, 150 ms, and 40 times, respectively. The peak-to-peak voltage amplitude of the function generator (FG) was 1.0 Vpp, and the modulation frequency was fixed at 2.0 MHz, where the multi-tiered structure of the Rayleigh noise spectrum was prominent. The Rayleigh noise spectra were observed accordingly.Fig. 6Experimental setup. EDFA: erbium-doped fiber amplifier.Fig. 7Structures of the FUTs including (a) a connector and (b) a fusion-spliced part. APC: angled physical contact.

Experimental setup. EDFA: erbium-doped fiber amplifier.

The observed Rayleigh noise spectra are shown in Fig. 8. Comparison of the two spectra confirms a three-tiered structure in both cases. Although there is a slight change in the spectrum due to the shorter fiber length in (b) compared to (a) because the original connector was cut and fusion-spliced, there is no significant change in the spectrum, including the third component. This result indicates that the primary cause of the third noise component is neither (i) the connection point between the FUT and the second port of the circulator or (ii) the Fresnel reflection at the end of the FUT.Fig. 8Rayleigh noise spectra measured using Fig. 7(a) (red) and 7(b) (blue). Subsequently, to investigate the influence of the circulator’s DIR, the reference light in the experimental setup shown in Fig. 6 was interrupted (i.e., the connector between the delay line and the EDFA was disconnected), allowing only the third noise component to be observed, as shown in Fig. 5. The FUT used was the one in Fig. 7a and bending loss positions were varied in (α) 1–21 m (every 2 m) and (β) 0–1 m (every 0.2 m) from the circulator. The spectra were observed for each case. The voltage amplitude of the FG was 1.0 Vpp, and the modulation frequency was 2.0 MHz. The VBW, sweep time, and averaging count of the ESA were 1 kHz, 30 ms, and 60 times, respectively, with the RBW set to 3 kHz for (α) and 1 kHz for (β).

The observed spectra are shown in Fig. 9. The spectrum width narrows as the bending loss position changes, i.e., as the FUT length shortens, which is consistent with the principle described earlier. In (β), the spectrum width at 0 m is slightly visible due to Rayleigh scattering light generated within the waveguide in the circulator. From these results, we can conclude that the primary cause of the third Rayleigh noise component is the DIR of the circulator.Fig. 9Rayleigh noise spectra measured using different FUT lengths of (α) 1.0–21 m and (β) 0–1.0 m.

Rayleigh noise spectra measured using different FUT lengths of (α) 1.0–21 m and (β) 0–1.0 m.

Removing the circulator from the BOCDR experimental setup to suppress this third noise component is difficult, as replacing it with other optical components is not efficient. Therefore, we experimentally verified that the modulation frequency dependence of the Rayleigh noise spectrum can be used to separate each component. We reused the experiment setup shown in Fig. 6 and the FUT shown in Fig. 7a. The voltage amplitude of the FG was 1.0 Vpp, and the modulation frequency was varied from 1.6 to 2.7 MHz (in 0.1 MHz steps). The Rayleigh noise spectrum was observed at each modulation frequency, and the frequency dependence of the spectrum width for each component was investigated. In addition, the modulation amplitude was estimated using the obtained Rayleigh noise spectrum width and compared with the true modulation amplitude measured using an optical heterodyne detection system shown in Fig. 10. In Fig. 10, Laser 1 was set with the same conditions as in the Rayleigh noise spectrum observation experiment, and Laser 2 was adjusted to have a center frequency difference of approximately 7 GHz with Laser 1 by controlling the driving voltage.Fig. 10Experimental setup for measuring the true modulation amplitude based on optical heterodyne detection.

Conclusions

We have clarified that the third component of the three-layered Rayleigh noise spectrum observed in BOCDR is caused by the DIR of the optical circulator. Given that replacing the circulator with alternative optical components is not practical, the circulator remains an indispensable part of the system. However, our analysis of the modulation frequency dependences of the spectral widths of the first, second, and third components revealed that the third component exhibits significantly less variation than those of the other two. This characteristic can be utilized to distinguish the third component from the others. Consequently, the second component can be effectively exploited to accurately estimate the modulation amplitude, leading to precise determination of the spatial resolution. Our findings thus hold considerable significance for improving both the convenience and accuracy of spatial resolution estimation in BOCDR.
