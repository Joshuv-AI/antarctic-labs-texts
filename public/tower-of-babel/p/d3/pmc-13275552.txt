C1orf226 Promotes Glioma Stemness and Oncogenesis in Glioma via PLK1‐Mediated Wnt/β‐Catenin Signaling
Authors: Yanbing Lu, Chao Jiang, Hongfei Wu, Junkui Shang, Bin Wang, Shundong Cang, Jiewen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionGlioma stem cells (GSCs) are pivotal drivers of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance; however, the underlying regulatory mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to identify novel oncogenic drivers in glioma via a comprehensive multiomics analysis.MethodsWe integrated three datasets to screen potential oncogenic drivers. Functional experiments were performed to examine the effects of C1orf226 on glioma cell proliferation, cell cycle, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and apoptosis. Additionally, tumor sphere formation assays, ALDH+/CD133+ cell detection, and stemness marker analysis were used to evaluate the role of C1orf226 in GSC stemness. Mechanistic studies involving immunoprecipitation (IP) and ubiquitination assays were applied to characterize the interaction between C1orf226 and PLK1, as well as their regulation on β‐catenin stability and Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. Rescue assays further verified the functional crosstalk between C1orf226 and PLK1. Xenograft mouse models were used in vivo to assess the impacts of C1orf226 knockdown on tumor growth and stemness markers.ResultsC1orf226 was significantly upregulated in glioma tissues compared with normal brain tissues. Its high expression correlated with shorter patient survival and served as an independent prognostic factor in the CGGA cohort. In vitro, C1orf226 exerted oncogenic function by facilitating cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, EMT‐like processes, and stemness, and suppressing apoptosis. Mechanistically, C1orf226 interacts with PLK1 to block its degradation, thereby activating PLK1 and downstream Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. Rescue experiments verified that PLK1 overexpression restored β‐catenin stability, Wnt/β‐catenin target gene expression, and GSC properties impaired by C1orf226 knockdown. In vivo, C1orf226 knockdown restrained xenograft growth and reduced stemness marker levels, and PLK1 co‐expression abrogated these effects.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that C1orf226 is a PLK1‐dependent regulator of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling and GSC plasticity, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target in glioma.

Introduction

Glioma represents the most prevalent and lethal form of malignant brain tumors and constitutes approximately 80% of all malignant tumors occurring within the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. The high percentage of gliomas among malignant CNS tumors emphasizes the urgent need for continued research and advancements in treatment options. The current standard treatment involves surgical resection, combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Despite advancements in the development and application of various treatment options, the 5‐year survival rate for patients is low (5%–7%). This low survival rate can be largely attributed to two significant factors: the frequent recurrence of tumors and the emergence of drug resistance [2]. These challenges hinder treatment efficacy, making it imperative to develop novel therapeutic strategies and improve patient survival. Thus, identifying key molecular drivers and mechanisms of glioma progression and recurrence is critical.

The therapeutic resistance of glioma is predominantly driven by GSCs, a rare subpopulation with self‐renewal and differentiation capacities [3]. GSCs are being increasingly recognized as key drivers of tumor initiation, progression, recurrence, and treatment resistance in gliomas. Clinical studies have demonstrated that GSC enrichment correlates with higher WHO tumor grades and poorer patient prognosis [4]. Notably, GSCs actively interact with the tumor microenvironment, and targeting these cells can improve the outcomes of immunotherapy [5]. scRNA‐seq of public data showed that high GSC risk scores correlated with chemotherapy resistance [6]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GSC‐targeted therapies are promising strategies for glioma treatment.

The Wnt/β‐catenin pathway represents a system that has been conserved throughout evolution. It governs cell proliferation, differentiation, and stem cell maintenance [7]. Under basal conditions, the destruction complex (APC/axin/GSK‐3β) targets β‐catenin for proteasomal degradation. When the Wnt ligand binds, β‐catenin translocates to the nucleus, where it triggers the activation of downstream target genes. In GSCs, the hyperactivation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway maintains stem cell properties, inhibits differentiation, and thus directly promotes tumorigenesis [8]. Mechanistically, this pathway drives the upregulation of neuroligin 3 expression to promote GSC proliferation and tumor microenvironment remodeling [9]. These results indicate that Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, a pivotal regulator of GSC biology, could be a potential therapeutic target in gliomas.

Currently, approximately 90% of human proteins have been identified and annotated through proteomics analyses; however, 10% remain poorly characterized, including chromosome‐specific open reading frame (CxORFx) proteins [10]. Recent studies have focused on clarifying the possible functions of uncharacterized ORF genes in different types of cancer [11]. Certain ORF genes are significantly linked to various oncogenic mechanisms in gliomas. Notably, compared with that in primary glioma, C10orf10 is expressed at higher levels in recurrent gliomas and facilitates glioma cell growth [12]. C15orf39 is a novel MAPK substrate that is frequently dysregulated in cancers [13]. C1orf226 (chromosome 1 open reading frame 226) is an uncharacterized protein linked to tumor immunity in breast cancer [14]. Previous analysis of the protein–protein interactions (PPIs) in glioma revealed that the protein partners of C1orf226 are involved mainly in protein ubiquitination and degradation, as well as dephosphorylation processes [15]. C1orf226 has been identified as a novel predictor of the response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [16]. C1orf226 is a gene located on human chromosome 1, and the specific function of its encoded protein remains poorly understood. Chromosome 1 is the largest human chromosome, and abnormalities in certain regions (such as amplifications or deletions) are associated with various tumors. Consequently, C1orf226 may be closely related to multiple types of cancer. However, the mechanism underlying the function of C1orf226 in glioma malignancy, particularly with respect to cancer stemness, remains unclear.

In this study, we revealed that C1orf226 expression was significantly high in both glioma cells and tissues and that increased C1orf226 expression was associated with a poor prognosis for patients. C1orf226 promoted the malignant phenotypes and GSC‐like properties of glioma cells. Mechanistically, we identified a novel function for C1orf226 in the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. Our data showed that C1orf226 directly binds to PLK1, regulating PLK1 stability and thereby activating Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. Therefore, this study revealed that β‐catenin expression is regulated by the C1orf226‐PLK1 signaling axis, which consequently mediates the modulation of GSC stemness maintenance and tumorigenic potential driven by the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection

Transcriptomics data from glioma and associated clinical profiles were systematically collected from several sources, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [17], the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) [17], the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), REMBRANDT, and the Gravendeel datasets [18]. RNA‐seq data of normal brain tissues from the Genotype‐Tissue Expression Project (GTEx) [19] were used as normal control to distinguish aberrant expression patterns in glioma. Proteomic data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) dataset [20] further characterized protein expression in glioma and facilitated mechanistic investigation. Together, these diverse datasets form a robust foundation for the analysis and interpretation of glioma biology. Images of immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to validate C1orf226 protein expression were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) [21]. Single‐cell sequencing data on the association between C1orf226 and tumor functional states were retrieved from CancerSEA [22]. The detailed clinicopathological features of patients in TCGA‐Glioma and CGGA cohorts are summarized in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. The public datasets analyzed in this study are summarized in Table S3.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was employed to determine the differentially enriched biological pathways in gliomas with high and low levels of C1orf226 expression [23]. For each comparison, 1000 permutation tests were performed to assess the statistical significance of the results. Gene sets that met the criteria of an adjusted p value < 0.05 (using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction) and an absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|) of at least 1 were considered statistically significant. This analytical framework enabled systematic identification of pathway‐level alterations associated with changes in C1orf226 expression dynamics.

Differential Expression Analysis

In this study, three prominent bioinformatics databases (TCGA, CGGA, and CPTAC) were utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that distinguish glioma tissue from normal brain tissue. For transcriptomic analysis of DEGs (TCGA and CGGA), raw RNA‐seq read counts were processed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0). A negative binomial generalized linear model was applied to estimate dispersion and determine statistical significance. RPKM normalization was performed on raw read counts to visualize the expression levels and compare the results across samples, while statistical testing was conducted on raw counts as recommended by the DESeq2 workflow. Genes exhibiting a |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p < 0.05 were defined as significant DEGs. For the CPTAC proteomic dataset, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between glioma and normal brain tissues were identified using the provided log2‐transformed, median‐centered protein intensities. Proteins with |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plots were created using the ggplot2 package to facilitate the visualization of the distribution of these significant DEGs and DEPs.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Six distinct human glioma cell lines, namely, U118, LN18, U87MG, LN229, U251MG, and LN215, along with a normal human astrocyte cell line known as NHA, were purchased from either the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) or the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures located in Shanghai, China. HEK‐293T cells were also obtained from the ATCC to complement these glioma cell lines and were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cells were subjected to a short tandem repeat (STR) analysis to ensure the authenticity and integrity of the cell lines used in the experiments. In addition, routine mycoplasma detection was conducted periodically, and all cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination during the entire experimental period. These glioma cells were maintained in DMEM or Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, both of which were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cultures were incubated in a controlled environment with a humid atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Cell Transfection and Lentivirus Infection

Lentiviral vectors containing shRNA sequences specifically targeting the human gene C1orf226, as well as a negative control shRNA, were obtained from Genema (Shanghai, China). For lentiviral infection, glioma cells (2 × 105) were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured to 60%–70% confluence. The cells were then infected with either the negative control (NC) lentiviral shRNA, the C1orf226‐targeting lentiviral shRNA, the C1orf226‐overexpressing lentivirus, or the empty vector control. Polybrene (8 μg/mL; Sigma–Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Germany) was added to the medium to enhance infection efficiency. Stable cell lines with integrated shRNA or overexpression constructs were subsequently selected using puromycin (8 μg/mL; Sigma–Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Germany). All shRNA target sequences are listed in Table S4.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR and Western Blotting

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA with a Takara reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR amplification was carried out on an ABI 7500 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. All primer sequences are listed in Table S5. For the western blot analysis, protein samples were separated via SDS‐PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked to prevent nonspecific binding, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Detailed information on primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Table S6. The membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were visualized using ECL reagent, with β‐actin as the loading control.

CCK‐8 Assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated utilizing a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8; Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). Briefly, cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Then, the cells were treated with a CCK‐8 solution (10 μL CCK‐8 in 100 μL DMEM) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a Biotek Synergy H1 microplate reader, which facilitated the quantification of cell proliferation based on the measured absorbance. Each experimental condition included triplicate wells, with three biologically independent replicates. Viability data are reported as percentages normalized to those of the untreated control wells.

Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Assays

The cells were first fixed in ice‐cold 70% ethanol at 4°C for 24 h prior to cell cycle analysis. After fixation, cells were treated with RNase A (100 μg/mL) at 37°C for 30 min to remove RNA interference. Cells were then stained with propidium iodide (PI, 50 μg/mL) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Cell cycle distribution was detected using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed with ModFit LT software. For the apoptosis assay, the cells were double‐stained with Annexin V‐FITC (5 μL) and PI (5 μg/mL). The cells were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. The proportion of apoptotic cells was quantified using flow cytometry. Data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.8; BD Biosciences). All results were presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) of triplicate samples.

Flow Cytometry Assay

The cells were harvested and washed with a permeabilization wash buffer, then resuspended in flow cytometry staining solution (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL via hemocytometer counting. For surface markers staining, cells were incubated with a FITC‐conjugated anti‐CD133 antibody (1:50, Cat# 11‐1331‐80; BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Unbound antibodies were removed by washing the cells twice with ice‐cold flow cytometry buffer. Samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and fluorescence compensation was set using single‐stained controls. Data analysis and gating were performed with FlowJo software. The ALDEFLUOR assay was conducted using an ALDEFLUOR kit (#01700; StemCell Technologies, Canada). Viable cells (1 × 106 cells/mL in assay buffer) were divided into two groups: the experimental group was incubated with BAAA substrate (1:12 dilution), and the control group was treated with the DEAB inhibitor. All samples were incubated at 37°C for 45–60 min in the dark. After rinsing with ice‐cold PBS, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 530 nm). ALDH+ cells, defined by fluorescence intensity above the DEAB control, were quantified using FlowJo software.

Ubiquitination and Cycloheximide Chase Assays

The cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes to 80%–90% confluence and co‐transfected with the indicated plasmids. After 48 h, the cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG‐132 (50 μM, HY‐13259; MCE, USA) for 6 h. Total protein lysates were prepared for immunoprecipitation, and ubiquitinated proteins were examined via immunoblotting with anti‐ubiquitin antibodies according to standard co‐IP procedures. In the cycloheximide chase degradation assay, transfected cells at 80% confluence were incubated with cycloheximide (CHX, 10 μg/mL, HY‐12320, MCE, USA). The cells were harvested at various time points after CHX treatment (0, 6, 12, and 18 h). Protein was extracted using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentration was then quantified using an enhanced BCA protein assay kit (P0009; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were loaded onto a gel and separated for western blotting. β‐actin was used as a loading control.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining and Immunoprecipitation (IP) Assay

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for fixation, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Primary antibodies targeting the designated proteins were incubated with the cells overnight at 4°C, after which Cy3‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:800; Beyotime) were added. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, Cat# C1005; Beyotime) and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. For IP assays, cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation using normal IgG (sc‐2025; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti‐PLK1 (1:200, Cat #ab278053; Abcam, USA), and anti‐C1orf226 (1:200, Cat# HPA052251, China) antibodies. Protein A/G agarose beads (sc‐2003; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA) were then added, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at 4°C with constant agitation. Antibody‐bead complexes were collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 5 min at 4°C and washed four times with ice‐cold lysis buffer. After the final wash, immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted in 2 × Laemmli buffer and separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE. Protein interactions were detected via immunoblotting with corresponding antibodies.

Tumor Sphere Formation Assay

Tumor sphere formation was assessed as previously described [8]. Briefly, single‐cell suspension (40,000 cells per well) was seeded into Corning ultralow‐attachment 6‐well plates. These cells were cultured in serum‐free DMEM/F‐12 (Invitrogen) supplemented with β‐FGF (10 ng/mL), B27 (20 ng/mL), and human EGF (20 ng/mL). After 7 days of incubation, primary spheres were harvested, quantified using a hemocytometer, resuspended, and passaged in fresh medium. After 14 days, the secondary spheres (> 50 μm diameter) were photographed under an inverted microscope at 100× or 200× magnification, and their number was quantified using ImageJ software.

GST Pull‐Down Assay

The full‐length human PLK1 cDNA was amplified by PCR with EcoRI/XhoI‐flanking primers, then cloned into the pGEX‐4T‐1 vector to generate a GST fusion plasmid. This plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, and bacteria were incubated overnight at 18°C. Bacterial lysates were affinity‐purified using glutathione‐Sepharose 4B beads. For the pull‐down assays, beads coupled with GST‐PLK1 or GST were incubated with the cell lysates in binding buffer at 4°C for 2 h. After extensive washing, bound proteins were eluted and detected by western blotting with anti‐C1orf226 antibody. Empty GST was used as a negative control.

TOP/FOP Luciferase Reporter Assay

shRNA‐expressing cells were co‐transfected with TOPflash or mutant FOP‐flash plasmid, together with Renilla luciferase plasmid serving as an internal control, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). At 24–48 h post‐transfection, dual‐luciferase reporter assays were performed with the Promega detection system following the manufacturer's instructions. Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to the Renilla luciferase signals. Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activity was determined by calculating the TOP/FOP ratio, and all results were presented as relative ratios.

In Vivo Tumorigenesis Assay

Female BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group) for in vivo tumorigenicity. After different treatments, the cells were diluted with PBS to 5 × 106 cells/mL and were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor length and width were measured weekly to monitor tumor growth. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: (length) × (width)2/2. After 6 weeks of observation period, the mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation. Excised tumors were weighed, cut into two halves, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunohistochemical staining. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhengzhou University.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3 per group). Data are presented as the means ± SDs for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were performed using two‐tailed t‐tests for two‐group comparisons and one‐way ANOVA for multigroup comparisons, with a significance threshold p < 0.05. Analyses were carried out using SPSS26.0 and GraphPad Prism10. GSEA (v4.3.2; Broad Institute) was applied to identify enriched biological pathways.

Results

C1orf226 Expression Is Upregulated in Glioma

We performed differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues from multiple databases to identify genes with aberrant expression in glioma. Using the TCGA databases, we identified a total of 6568 DEGs, including 3355 upregulated genes and 3213 downregulated genes (Figure 1A). A total of 2002 DEGs were identified in samples from the CGGA database, with 906 upregulated genes and 1096 downregulated genes (Figure 1B). Meanwhile, 2780 DEPs (830 upregulated proteins and 1950 downregulated proteins) were identified using the CPTAC database (Figure 1C). Venn analysis showed 178 overlapping upregulated DEGs across the three databases (Figure 1D). By further intersecting these candidates with prognostic genes from TCGA and CGGA, we finally obtained five key genes: MMP2, C1orf226, C1R, CHI3L2, and TGFBI (Figure 1E). Among them, C1orf226 has been poorly studied in glioma. Transcriptomic data from TCGA (Figure 1F) and GEPIA (Figure 1G) both revealed markedly higher C1orf226 expression in glioma tissues than in normal tissues. CPTAC (Figure 1H) and HPA (Figure 1I) proteomic data further verified elevated C1orf226 protein levels in tumor samples.

High C1orf226 Expression Correlates With Malignant Clinicopathological Features and a Poor Prognosis for Glioma Patients

We applied a Cox regression model across multiple cohorts to assess whether C1orf226 is a potential independent prognostic factor. Univariate analysis revealed a significant correlation between C1orf226 expression and patient prognosis in the TCGA dataset. The hazard ratio (HR) was 1.254, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.072–1.468, and p = 0.005 (Figure 2A). Similar results were validated in the CGGA dataset (HR = 1.369, 95% CI = 1.251–1.498, p < 0.0001; Figure 2C). Multivariate analysis further confirmed that C1orf226 served as an independent prognostic predictor in the CGGA cohort (HR = 1.156, 95% CI = 1.061–1.260, p < 0.001; Figure 2D). However, this independent prognostic effect was not observed in the TCGA cohort (p = 0.707; Figure 2B). Moreover, patients with high C1orf226 expression had consistently worse clinical outcomes than those with low expression. This trend was observed across TCGA, CGGA, Rembrandt, and Gravendeel datasets (Figure 2E). We then explored the correlation between C1orf226 expression and clinicopathological features using TCGA and CGGA datasets. The heatmap based on the CGGA dataset showed distinct differences in tumor grade, chemotherapy status, IDH mutation, and 19q co‐deletion (Figure 2F, upper panel). Analysis of the TGGA dataset indicated significant differences in grade, IDH mutation status, 19q co‐deletion status, and ATRX expression between the high‐ and low‐C1orf226 subgroups (Figure 2F, lower panel). Similarly, C1orf226 expression was noticeably increased in high‐grade gliomas compared with low‐grade gliomas, as observed in two GEO datasets (Figure 2G). Collectively, these results highlight the value of C1orf226 as a dual diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in glioma.

C1orf226 Silencing Suppresses the Malignant Phenotypes of Glioma

We first compared C1orf226 expression in glioma cells with those in normal brain cells to explore its biological function in glioma. The results showed that endogenous C1orf226 mRNA (Figure 3A, upper panel) and protein (Figure 3A, lower panel) were upregulated in six glioma cell lines. Next, we constructed stable C1orf226 knockdown and overexpression cell lines via lentiviral infection (Figure 3B). The CCK‐8 assay demonstrated that C1orf226 knockdown markedly inhibited cell proliferation in the two glioma cell lines (Figure 3C). The flow cytometry analysis provided compelling evidence for the role of C1orf226 in regulating both the cell cycle and apoptosis. Specifically, C1orf226 knockdown‐induced cell cycle arrest (Figure 3D) and increased cell apoptosis (Figure 3E). Conversely, C1orf226 overexpression exerted opposite effects (Figure 3D,E). Western blot analysis showed that C1orf226 knockdown increased the levels of proapoptotic proteins (Bax, cleaved caspase‐3, and cleaved caspase‐9) and decreased antiapoptotic proteins (Bcl‐xl and Bcl‐2) (Figure 3F). As expected, C1orf226 overexpression caused the reverse changes in these apoptosis‐related proteins (Figure 3F). In addition, C1orf226 promoted the EMT‐like process in glioma cells (Figure 3G). Taken together, these findings indicate that C1orf226 acts as a tumor promoter in glioma cells.

C1orf226 Expression Correlates With Stem‐Like Properties in Glioma Cells

Cancer stemness is a key mechanism driving glioma tumorigenesis. We used the single‐cell database CancerSEA to explore the biological phenotypes regulated by C1orf226 and analyzed its correlation with stemness in glioma. Single‐cell database [24] (EXP0057) from CancerSEA showed that high C1orf226 expression was significantly correlated with stemness (Figure 4A). Likewise, GSEA results confirmed that high C1orf226 expression was positively associated with stemness‐related gene sets in the TCGA database (Figure 4B). Correlation analysis also identified strong and significant correlations between C1orf226 and stemness‐related genes (Figure 4C). Next, we performed sphere formation assays to evaluate the self‐renewal capacity of GSCs. C1orf226 knockdown led to a decreasing trend in the number and size of tumor spheres in LN229 and U251MG cells. In contrast, C1orf226 overexpression markedly increased sphere number and size in U118 cells (Figure 4D). CD133 is a classic surface marker of GCSs. Flow cytometry showed that the proportion of CD133‐positive cells decreased after C1orf226 knockdown and increased after C1orf226 overexpression (Figure 4E). Western blot results showed that C1orf226 silencing downregulated stemness‐related genes in glioma cell lines, whereas the overexpression of C1orf226 increased their expression (Figure 4F). Overall, our results demonstrate that C1orf226 exerts a strong tumorigenic role in glioma by maintaining stem cell properties.

C1orf226 Mediates the Activation of the Wnt/β‐Catenin Pathway

We investigated the downstream signaling mechanisms of C1orf226 to explore how it drives glioma malignancy. GSEA showed that high C1orf226 expression was correlated with the enrichment of multiple Wnt pathways (Figure 5A). We further confirmed the connection between C1orf226 and the Wnt pathway by retrieving the top 100 genes most strongly correlated with C1orf226 from the GEPIA database and subjecting them to pathway analysis in the REACTOME_PATHWAYS database. The Reactome pathway analysis revealed that Wnt/β‐catenin signaling was among the top 10 enriched pathways (Figure 5B). GEPIA analysis also revealed a strong positive correlation between C1orf226 and β‐catenin (Figure 5C). A TOP/FOP‐flash luciferase reporter assay was performed to evaluate Wnt/β‐catenin activity. C1orf226 knockdown significantly reduced TOP/FOP luciferase activity, whereas its overexpression increased this activity, indicating altered Wnt/β‐catenin transcriptional activity (Figure 5D). IF assays confirmed that C1orf226 knockdown notably reduced β‐catenin expression in the nucleus, whereas C1orf226 overexpression facilitated β‐catenin nuclear translocation (Figure 5E). Moreover, C1orf226 silencing downregulated β‐catenin, phospho‐β‐catenin, and their downstream targets (Figure 5F,G and Figure S1). Taken together, these findings confirmed that C1orf226 activates the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway in glioma cells.

C1orf226 Interacts With PLK1 and Maintains Its Protein Stability

We aimed to clarify how C1orf226 regulates glioma progression and Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation. We also systematically screened potential C1orf226‐interacting partners via integrated analysis of three PPI databases. By integrating data from the BioGRID, HitPredict, and GeneMANIA databases, six candidate interactors were identified: PLK1, AP2M1, NKAP, MYH13, NAF1, and PLK2 (Figure 6A). Notably, PLK1, one of the identified interactors, is a well‐known regulator of GSCs and the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. We subsequently investigated the physical interactions between C1orf226 and PLK1 in the two glioma cell lines by performing IF staining. The results showed the significant colocalization of C1orf226 and PLK1 in the cytoplasm (Figure 6B). Subsequent IP assays further confirmed the direct interaction between C1orf226 and PLK1 in two glioma cell lines (Figure 6C). In a GST pull‐down assay, the fusion protein GST‐PLK1 pulled down C1orf226 in HEK‐293 T cells (Figure 6D). Next, we explored the regulatory relationship between C1orf226 and PLK1. First, we detected PLK1 expression after altering C1orf226 levels. LN229 cells transfected with HA‐C1orf226 exhibited a dose‐dependent increase in PLK1 protein (Figure 6E). In U251MG cells, C1orf226 knockdown with two independent shRNAs downregulated PLK1 protein, while PLK1 mRNA expression remained unaltered (Figure 6F). As anticipated, C1orf226 overexpression prolonged the half‐life of PLK1 in LN229 cells (Figure 6G), and C1orf226 silencing shortened PLK1 half‐life in U251MG cells (Figure 6H). Moreover, the reduction of PLK1 caused by C1orf226 knockdown was reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG‐132 in U251MG cells (Figure 6I). Similarly, C1orf226 silencing enhanced PLK1 polyubiquitination in U251MG cells, whereas C1orf226 overexpression reduced PLK1 polyubiquitination in U118 cells (Figure 6J). Collectively, these findings suggest that C1orf226 binds to PLK1 and maintains its stability in glioma cells.

The C1orf226‐PLK1 Axis Increases β‐Catenin Protein Stability, Thereby Activating the Wnt/β‐Catenin Pathway and GSCs

Earlier research has indicated that PLK1‐mediated β‐catenin phosphorylation is a recognized mechanism for increasing β‐catenin stability and activating Wnt/β‐catenin signaling. Based on our findings described above, we hypothesized that the regulation of the Wnt pathway by C1orf226 may depend on PLK1. We performed CHX chase assays to assess β‐catenin turnover. As expected, C1orf226 knockdown shortened the half‐life of endogenous β‐catenin protein, while ectopic PLK1 expression partially reversed this effect (Figure 7A). Ubiquitination assays revealed that C1orf226 knockdown promoted β‐catenin polyubiquitination, which was attenuated by PLK1 overexpression (Figure 7B). Consistently, PLK1 overexpression reversed the C1orf226 knockdown‐induced suppression of the expression of stemness markers and nuclear levels of target genes of the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin pathway (Figure 7C). We subsequently assessed the effect of the C1orf226–PLK1 axis on the ratio of GSCs in gliomas mediated by the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. Flow cytometry demonstrated that the decreased proportion of ALDH+/CD133+ glioma stem cells upon C1orf226 knockdown was restored by LiCl treatment or PLK1 overexpression (Figure 7D,E). Collectively, these data indicate that C1orf226 interacts with and stabilizes PLK1, which may contribute to the regulation of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling and GSCs in glioma.

C1orf226 Knockdown Inhibits Tumorigenesis In Vivo

Based on the in vitro results, we established a nude mouse model to assess the effect of C1orf226 on glioma tumorigenicity in vivo. Animal experiments showed that C1orf226 knockdown suppressed tumor growth (Figure 8A) and reduced tumor weight (Figure 8B) and tumor volume (Figure 8C). Notably, the inhibition of tumor growth was partially reversed by PLK1 overexpression. IHC staining showed that C1orf226 upregulated stemness markers in a PLK1‐dependent manner (Figure 8D). These results indicate that C1orf226 knockdown restrains glioma tumorigenesis in vivo by downregulating cancer stem cell markers via PLK1.

Discussion

Despite advances in multimodal therapy including maximal safe resection and adjuvant radio‐chemotherapy, glioma remains the most prevalent and lethal primary CNS malignancy. Emerging evidence has highlighted that GSCs drive gliomagenesis, therapeutic resistance, and recurrence through self‐renewal and differentiation [24]. Nevertheless, effective targeted therapy against GSCs remains a major challenge.

Although some studies have investigated the relationships between CxORFx proteins and tumors, the functional role of C1orf226 in glioma remains unclear. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to comprehensively investigate the role of C1orf226 in malignant phenotypes and stem‐like properties of gliomas. Analysis of public genomic datasets showed that elevated C1orf226 expression was closely correlated with aggressive glioma subtypes and poor patient survival. In this study, functional analysis revealed that C1orf226 prominently promoted the malignant progression of glioma cells. GSCs hinder tumor eradication. Here, we report for the first time that C1orf226 may affect GSCs' characteristics. Multiple bioinformatics approaches consistently confirmed a strong positive correlation between C1orf226 expression and GSCs signatures. C1orf226 promoted glioma cell self‐renewal and stemness marker expression. These findings provide a basis for further exploring the molecular mechanisms by which C1orf226 modulates GSCs.

We screened potential interacting partners of C1orf226 using three PPI databases and identified six candidate proteins, among which PLK1 was prioritized. IP and IF staining confirmed the direct interaction between C1orf226 and PLK1. We further found that C1orf226 stabilized PLK1 by inhibiting its ubiquitination‐mediated degradation. Emerging evidence reveals a reciprocal regulatory axis between β‐catenin and PLK1 during tumorigenesis. PLK1 phosphorylates GSK‐3β or β‐catenin at Ser60 to stabilize β‐catenin, promote its nuclear translocation, and activate Wnt‐dependent transcription, thus driving oncogenic activities [25]. Our results indicated that C1orf226 was highly enriched in Wnt‐related pathways. Mechanistically, C1orf226 silencing accelerated ubiquitination‐dependent degradation of β‐catenin and reduced its protein level, and this effect was partially rescued by PLK1 overexpression. These data suggest that C1orf226 modulates Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activity via regulating PLK1. Rescue experiments further confirmed that the effect of C1orf226 on GSCs is tightly linked to PLK1‐mediated β‐catenin stabilization. This relationship highlights a mechanistic pathway in which C1orf226 facilitates the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway activation through its effects on PLK1, ultimately contributing to the maintenance of GSCs. As a serine/threonine kinase, PLK1 phosphorylates a wide range of substrates. It can directly target antiapoptotic proteins such as Mcl‐1 and Bcl‐xL to regulate their stability and binding capacity toward proapoptotic proteins [26]. In our study, we also investigated the effect of C1orf226 on the expression of Bcl‐2 family apoptosis‐related proteins in p53‐mutant cells. Given the relationship between C1orf226 and PLK1, the speculation that the effect of C1orf226 on apoptosis in p53‐mutant cells may also depend on PLK1 is reasonable.

Although this study yields novel findings, several limitations need to be addressed. First, the expression pattern and clinical relevance of C1orf226 were analyzed solely based on public databases, without validation using clinical tissue samples. Accumulating preclinical data have shown that PLK1 inhibitors improve temozolomide sensitivity by impairing DNA repair, suppressing ATM/ATR signaling, and inducing prolonged G2/M cell cycle arrest [27]. Future work will explore whether the C1orf226–PLK1–Wnt/β‐catenin axis mediates chemotherapy resistance in glioma. While we confirmed that C1orf226 stabilizes PLK1, whether PLK1 phosphorylation is required for this regulatory axis remains to be clarified. In addition, our verification of C1orf226 function in glioma stemness was limited to in vitro experiments and subcutaneous xenograft models; further validation in primary GSCs and orthotopic models is still needed. Moreover, since C1orf226 has been rarely studied to date, exploring its structural domains, protein interaction networks, and epigenetic regulation may help identify novel therapeutic targets. C1orf226 could serve as both a prognostic biomarker and a combinatorial therapeutic candidate for glioma.

In conclusion, we identified C1orf226 as a promising gene targeting GSCs. C1orf226 is upregulated in glioma tissues, and its high expression predicts poor clinical outcomes. In vitro experiments showed that C1orf226 knockdown suppressed the malignant phenotypes and stemness of glioma cells. Mechanistically, C1orf226 directly interacts with PLK1 to stabilize β‐catenin, activate Wnt signaling pathway, and maintain malignant phenotypes and stemness in glioma cells. These findings suggest that C1orf226 is a novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, as well as a potential therapeutic target for glioma patients.
