Evaluation of recombinant Taenia multiceps HSP70 antigen in a dipstick-based rapid diagnostic test for ovine coenurosis
Authors: Seyma Gunyakti-Kilinc, Figen Celik, Harun Kaya Kesik, Muhammet Uslug, Sami Simsek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoenurus cerebralis, the larval stage of Taenia multiceps, causes coenurosis, a severe neurological disease in sheep associated with significant economic losses. Ante-mortem diagnosis remains difficult due to non-specific clinical signs and the limited applicability of laboratory-based methods under field conditions. Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) has previously shown promising immunoreactivity in laboratory assays, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic antigen. These findings demonstrate that rTmHSP70, although suitable for laboratory-based assays, is not compatible with dipstick-format rapid diagnostics.ConclusionThe study underscores the importance of assay format compatibility, antigen purity, and epitope accessibility in the development of reliable field-applicable diagnostic tests for coenurosis. Introduction

Coenurosis is a severe parasitic disease caused by Coenurus cerebralis, the larval stage of Taenia multiceps, and primarily affects sheep and goats by invading the central nervous system (CNS). The disease is characterized by progressive neurological symptoms, including ataxia, circling, blindness, and eventually death, leading to substantial economic losses in small ruminant production, particularly in endemic regions. In addition to its veterinary importance, T. multiceps has zoonotic potential, with sporadic human cases reported worldwide [1, 2]. Diagnosis of coenurosis in live animals remains challenging. Clinical signs are often non-specific and may overlap with those of other neurological or metabolic disorders such as listeriosis, pregnancy toxemia, oestriasis, brain abscesses, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, sinusitis, cerebral edema, and neoplastic conditions [3, 4]. Definitive diagnosis is generally based on post-mortem examination or imaging techniques, which are impractical or inaccessible under routine field conditions. Consequently, the lack of reliable, rapid, and field-applicable diagnostic tools represents a major obstacle to effective disease control.

Serological approaches have been proposed as an alternative for the ante-mortem diagnosis of coenurosis; however, published studies in this area remain limited and have produced variable results. Guo et al. [5] evaluated recombinant lactate dehydrogenase B (rTm-LDHB), derived from the T. multiceps transcriptome, in an ELISA format and reported a sensitivity of only 71.4% in experimentally infected goats, concluding that this antigen was not sufficiently specific for immunodiagnostic purposes. In contrast, recombinant glutathione S-transferase (rTm-GST) obtained from C. cerebralis showed higher sensitivities (up to 92.8%) and was suggested as a potential diagnostic antigen [6]. Other recombinant antigens, including the oncosphere proteins Tm16 and Tm18, as well as fatty acid-binding protein (TmFABP), have mainly been investigated in vaccination or immunogenicity studies rather than for diagnostic applications [7, 8].

Heat shock proteins (HSPs), particularly HSP70, have attracted increasing attention as potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets in parasitic infections. HSPs play a critical role in protein folding, stress tolerance, and parasite survival within the host environment [9, 10]. HSP70 is a highly conserved molecular chaperone that has been shown to be immunologically relevant in several parasitic diseases, including leishmaniasis and echinococcosis, where it has been proposed as a serodiagnostic marker [11, 12]. Nevertheless, HSP-based diagnostic approaches have yielded inconsistent results. For example, Wang et al. [13] evaluated HSP70 derived from C. cerebralis cysts in an ELISA format and reported moderate specificity, although sensitivity was assessed using a very limited number of positive sera. Similarly, an immunochromatographic study using recombinant HSP from Echinococcus granulosus demonstrated very low positivity rates, highlighting potential limitations of HSP-based rapid diagnostic formats [14]. From a diagnostic perspective, HSP70 has previously demonstrated promising performance in laboratory-based immunoassays. Previous studies have demonstrated that recombinant TmHSP70 is immunoreactive in laboratory-based assays, supporting its potential relevance as a diagnostic antigen [15]. Similar observations have been reported for HSP70 homologues in other cestode and protozoan parasites, reinforcing the rationale for investigating this protein as a diagnostic candidate [11, 12]. These findings provided a strong scientific basis for evaluating whether the demonstrated immunoreactivity of rTmHSP70 could be translated into a rapid, field-applicable diagnostic format. Furthermore, the use of HSP70 was intentionally pursued to test the broader hypothesis that antigens performing well in high-specificity laboratory assays may not necessarily retain their diagnostic performance in rapid test formats. Thus, beyond antigen evaluation, the present study aimed to explore the format-dependent limitations of HSP70-based diagnostics, an aspect that remains insufficiently addressed in the current literature. In this context, the observed lack of signal in the dipstick assay does not undermine the biological relevance of HSP70 but rather highlights critical constraints related to antigen concentration, epitope accessibility, and signal generation in lateral flow–type systems. These insights are valuable for guiding future antigen selection and assay design strategies for the rapid diagnosis of coenurosis.

In Türkiye, recombinant T. multiceps HSP70 (rTmHSP70; 23 kDa) was previously produced and evaluated for diagnostic use. While this antigen exhibited 100% sensitivity and specificity in Western blot, whereas ELISA sensitivity and specificity were 66.6% and 52.6%, respectively [15]. These findings suggest that, although rTmHSP70 is immunoreactive, its diagnostic performance may be highly dependent on the assay format and antigen presentation. Although ELISA-based assays allow high-throughput screening, their diagnostic accuracy is strongly influenced by antigen purity, epitope accessibility, and cross-reactivity with other parasitic infections [16]. More sensitive methods such as SDS-PAGE-based antigen fractionation and Western blot offer improved specificity but are not suitable for routine field use [17]. Rapid diagnostic formats, including dot-ELISA and dipstick assays, represent a potential compromise by combining visual readout with minimal reagent requirements, making them attractive for low-resource and field settings [18].

Given these considerations, the present study was designed to evaluate the format dependency of diagnostic antigen performance by assessing whether a recombinant antigen with proven laboratory immunoreactivity could be successfully translated into a dipstick-based rapid diagnostic assay for ovine coenurosis. In this context, rTmHSP70 was used as a model antigen to explore the limitations and constraints imposed by rapid test formats. By systematically optimizing assay conditions and comparing rTmHSP70 with a partially purified hydatid cyst antigen, the study aimed to determine whether the immunoreactivity observed in laboratory-based assays could be translated into a reliable, field-oriented diagnostic format.

Materials and methods

Source of serum samples

Serum samples from sheep with coenurosis were obtained from animals showing clinical neurological signs and slaughtered for routine meat production. Following slaughter, the heads were opened, and coenurosis was confirmed by post-mortem examination of the brain. Blood samples were collected after slaughter, therefore, no live animal handling or experimental intervention was performed for the purposes of this study.

Serum samples from hydatid cyst-infected sheep were obtained from the existing serum collection of our laboratory. These samples had been collected previously during another independent study, based on post-mortem organ inspection at slaughterhouses confirming hydatid cyst infection. No additional animal sampling was conducted for the present study [19].

A total of 18 sera from sheep with confirmed coenurosis and 11 sera from non-infected control sheep were included in the dipstick assay experiments. In addition, 20 sera from hydatid cyst-positive sheep were used for comparative antigen evaluation.

Recombinant rTmHSP70 expression and purification

The rTmHSP70 antigen used in this study was derived from a previously characterized recombinant construct containing the TmHSP70 gene. Glycerol stocks of confirmed recombinant plasmids were revived, propagated in E. coli, and used to transform the expression host E. coli BL21 (DE3), followed by IPTG-induced protein expression. Following induction, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed. The His-tagged rTmHSP70 protein was purified under denaturing conditions using a nickel-affinity purification system, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Eluted protein fractions were collected, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C until further use. Purified rTmHSP70 protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE to confirm molecular weight and purity. Western blot analysis was performed to assess immunoreactivity using sera from sheep naturally infected with C. cerebralis as well as sera from non-infected control animals. Bound antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-sheep IgG and visualized with an appropriate chromogenic substrate [15].

Dipstick assay design

Nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad) were used as the solid phase for dipstick assay development and cut into strips measuring approximately 0.5 cm × 5 cm. The test line was prepared by manually immobilizing purified rTmHSP70 antigen onto the membrane using predefined antigen volumes and concentrations determined during preliminary optimization experiments. The antigen was applied as a narrow line perpendicular to the direction of sample migration. The control line was prepared by immobilizing anti-sheep IgG antibodies (H + L or Fc-specific, depending on the experimental setup) at predefined dilutions to ensure assay validity and consistent control signal development. After application of test and control reagents, the membranes were air-dried at room temperature to allow stable protein adsorption and subsequently assembled into dipstick formats suitable for manual handling. The assay was designed to permit capillary flow of serum samples across the membrane without the use of additional buffers or instrumentation, mimicking field-applicable testing conditions. No enzymatic or nanoparticle-based detection system was employed in the dipstick assay, unlike Western blot analysis, which used horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and chromogenic substrates, signal development relied solely on the direct visual detection of antigen-antibody complexes formed under capillary flow conditions.

Optimization of dipstick assay conditions

Optimization of the dipstick assay was performed through a series of systematic experiments aimed at identifying conditions that would maximize signal intensity while minimizing background noise and non-specific reactions. Key parameters evaluated during optimization included serum dilution, antibody concentration on the control line, antigen loading concentration on the test line, and the type of secondary antibody used. Sera from sheep with confirmed C. cerebralis infection, as well as sera from non-infected control animals, were used throughout the optimization process. Serum samples were tested at multiple dilution ratios (ranging from 1:25 to 1:500) to assess the impact of antibody concentration on signal development. To optimize assay performance, anti-sheep IgG antibodies (H + L and Fc-specific) were tested at serial dilutions ranging from 1:100 to 1:1000, starting from a stock concentration of 1 mg/mL. Different antibody dilutions were evaluated to determine their effect on control line clarity and overall assay performance. Antigen loading on the test line was also varied to assess whether increasing the amount of immobilized rTmHSP70 could enhance signal generation. Each experimental condition was assessed visually after sample application, focusing on the presence or absence of a visible test line, the intensity of the control line, background staining, and overall assay clarity. Conditions yielding consistent control line development but no test line signal were recorded and compared across experiments. Dipstick assay results were evaluated visually and were not interpreted in a blinded manner.

Comparative antigen evaluation

To determine whether the lack of detectable signal in the dipstick assay was attributable to the rTmHSP70 antigen itself or to limitations of the dipstick format, a comparative antigen evaluation was performed. For this purpose, a partially purified hydatid cyst antigen with a higher total protein content was included as an alternative test antigen. Dipstick assays were prepared using the partially purified hydatid cyst antigen immobilized on the test line under the same optimized conditions established for the rTmHSP70-based assay. Positive and negative serum samples were applied to these dipsticks using identical serum dilutions and incubation conditions. The resulting test and control line signals were visually evaluated and compared with those obtained using rTmHSP70. This comparative approach allowed assessment of the influence of antigen composition and protein concentration on signal generation. While the partially purified hydatid cyst antigen produced visible test line signals with positive sera, non-specific reactions and false-positive signals were also observed with negative sera, highlighting the importance of antigen purity and specificity in dipstick-based diagnostic assays.

Results

Expression and Immunoreactivity of rTmHSP70

The recombinant rTmHSP70 protein was successfully expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) following IPTG induction under optimized conditions. SDS-PAGE analysis of purified protein fractions revealed a distinct and reproducible band at approximately 23 kDa, consistent with the expected molecular weight of the recombinant antigen (Supplementary Fig. 1). The presence of this band confirmed successful expression and purification of rTmHSP70 under denaturing conditions. Western blot analysis demonstrated strong and specific immunoreactivity of rTmHSP70, with all coenurosis-positive sera reacting at the expected molecular weight, while no reactivity was observed with negative control sera. When probed with sera obtained from sheep naturally infected with C. cerebralis, a clear and specific immunoreactive band corresponding to the 23 kDa rTmHSP70 protein was consistently observed (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, no immunoreactive bands were detected when sera from non-infected control sheep were used. These findings confirmed that rTmHSP70 retains antigenic epitopes capable of being specifically recognized by antibodies generated during natural C. cerebralis infection. Collectively, the SDS-PAGE and Western blot results verified that rTmHSP70 was successfully produced, structurally intact, and immunologically reactive, thereby justifying its further evaluation in a rapid diagnostic assay format.

Dipstick assay optimization using rTmHSP70

Following confirmation of rTmHSP70 expression and immunoreactivity, the antigen was evaluated in a dipstick assay format. The dipstick prototypes consistently exhibited proper capillary flow characteristics, and fluid migration across the nitrocellulose membrane was observed under all tested conditions. Control lines prepared with anti-sheep IgG antibodies developed clearly and reproducibly, indicating correct assay assembly and functionality. Systematic optimization experiments were conducted to assess the impact of serum dilution, antibody concentration, and antigen loading on test line signal development. Positive and negative sheep sera were tested at multiple dilutions ranging from 1:25 to 1:500. In parallel, different concentrations of anti-sheep IgG (H + L and Fc-specific) antibodies were evaluated on the control line, and varying amounts of rTmHSP70 were immobilized on the test line. Despite these extensive optimization efforts, no visible signal was observed on the test line when rTmHSP70 was used as the immobilized antigen. This lack of signal was consistent across all tested conditions, including higher antigen loading amounts and lower serum dilutions, where antibody concentration would be expected to be maximal. Importantly, the absence of a test line signal occurred despite consistent and robust control line development, indicating that assay failure was not due to impaired fluid migration or technical malfunction (Fig. 1). These results demonstrate that, although rTmHSP70 is immunoreactive in Western blot analysis, it does not generate a detectable signal in the dipstick assay format under the tested conditions.

Comparative evaluation with partially purified hydatid cyst antigen

To determine whether the absence of signal in the rTmHSP70-based dipstick assay was attributable to intrinsic properties of the antigen or to limitations of the assay format, a comparative evaluation was performed using a partially purified hydatid cyst antigen characterized by a higher total protein content. Dipstick assays were prepared using this antigen immobilized on the test line, while all other assay parameters including serum dilutions, antibody concentrations, and assay conditions were kept identical to those used in the rTmHSP70 experiments. When sera from C. cerebralis-infected sheep were applied, visible test line signals were observed, indicating successful antibody-antigen interaction and signal generation. However, non-specific reactions were also detected when negative control sera were tested, resulting in false-positive signals. These non-specific reactions were reproducible and occurred despite the use of identical control line conditions, suggesting that insufficient antigen purity and the presence of cross-reactive epitopes contributed to reduced assay specificity. The comparative results indicate that increasing total protein content on the test line can facilitate visible signal generation in dipstick assays but may simultaneously compromise diagnostic specificity when antigen purity is inadequate. In contrast, the failure of rTmHSP70 to produce a detectable signal, despite confirmed immunoreactivity, suggests that factors such as antigen concentration, epitope accessibility, or molecular orientation on the nitrocellulose membrane may critically limit its performance in rapid test formats (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The development of rapid diagnostic tests for parasitic diseases requires not only the identification of immunoreactive antigens but also their compatibility with the selected assay format. In the present study, recombinant rTmHSP70 exhibited strong and specific immunoreactivity in Western blot analysis, in agreement with previous findings demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity of this antigen under laboratory conditions [15]. These results support the biological relevance of HSP70 as an antigen expressed during T. multiceps infection. However, this immunoreactivity could not be translated into a dipstick-based rapid diagnostic format, demonstrating that antigen performance is highly dependent on the diagnostic platform. Western blot analysis relies on denatured proteins immobilized under conditions that expose linear epitopes and allow prolonged antibody-antigen interaction, supported by enzymatic signal amplification [17]. In contrast, dipstick assays depend on passive capillary flow, limited interaction time, and visual signal detection without amplification. Consequently, antigens that perform well in blot-based assays may fail in rapid formats, particularly when antigen concentration or epitope accessibility is suboptimal, as previously noted for several parasitic infections [16]. One plausible explanation for the absence of signal in the rTmHSP70-based dipstick assay is the limited amount of antigen that can be immobilized onto the nitrocellulose membrane without adversely affecting flow dynamics. Despite multiple optimization attempts including increased antigen loading, adjustments in serum dilution, and modifications of antibody concentrations no visible test line signal was obtained. This suggests that the number of antigen-antibody complexes formed under dipstick conditions was insufficient to produce a visually detectable response. Additionally, the molecular orientation of rTmHSP70 on the nitrocellulose membrane may have masked critical epitopes required for effective antibody binding, further limiting signal generation. The partially purified hydatid cyst antigen was included as a heterologous positive control to verify that the dipstick format itself was capable of generating a visible signal under identical experimental conditions. It should be emphasized that this antigen is not homologous to rTmHSP70 and was not intended to serve as an alternative T. multiceps antigen. Therefore, this comparison does not directly distinguish whether the observed limitation is antigen-specific or exclusively format-specific. Instead, it demonstrates that signal generation in the dipstick assay is technically feasible, while also highlighting the trade-off between signal intensity and diagnostic specificity when crude or partially purified heterologous antigens are used.

The influence of antigen purity and composition was further clarified through comparative experiments using a partially purified hydatid cyst antigen. In this case, visible test line signals were obtained with sera from infected animals, indicating that higher total protein content can enhance signal generation in dipstick assays. However, this apparent increase in sensitivity was accompanied by non-specific reactions and false-positive results with negative sera. Such findings clearly demonstrate the inherent trade-off between sensitivity and specificity in rapid diagnostic test development and confirm that increased protein content alone cannot compensate for insufficient antigen specificity.

The false-positive reactions observed with the partially purified hydatid cyst antigen are most likely attributable to the presence of cross-reactive epitopes shared with other parasitic or host-derived proteins. Non-specific binding associated with crude or partially purified antigens is a well-recognized limitation in serological diagnostics, particularly in endemic regions where polyparasitism is common [2, 16]. These results reinforce the necessity of using well-defined and highly specific antigens in the development of reliable rapid diagnostic tests.

From a broader perspective, the present findings are consistent with previous reports on heat shock protein-based diagnostics in parasitic infections. Although HSP70 has been shown to elicit humoral immune responses and to perform well in laboratory-based assays, its diagnostic performance in rapid or immunochromatographic formats has been inconsistent. Similar limitations have been reported for HSP-based assays in echinococcosis, where promising laboratory results could not be reliably translated into field-applicable rapid tests [12, 14]. These observations suggest that the highly conserved nature of HSP70, while biologically advantageous, may restrict its utility as a standalone antigen in rapid serological formats due to epitope conservation and limited signal amplification. Importantly, the inability of rTmHSP70 to generate a detectable signal in the dipstick assay should not be interpreted as a lack of immunological relevance. Rather, it reflects format-dependent constraints that underscore the need to align antigen selection with the intended diagnostic platform. Although HSP70 is generally considered a highly conserved protein, the absence of cross-reactivity in Western blot analysis can be explained by the exposure of species-specific linear epitopes under denaturing conditions. In contrast to native or membrane-bound formats, SDS-PAGE and Western blotting disrupt protein conformation, allowing antibodies to recognize linear epitopes that may be unique to T. multiceps. These species-specific epitopes may be masked, improperly oriented, or sterically hindered in dipstick-based formats, thereby reconciling the high specificity observed in Western blot analysis with the lack of detectable signal in the rapid test format. As demonstrated in this study, antigen immunoreactivity alone is insufficient for successful rapid test development, additional factors such as antigen concentration, molecular size, epitope exposure, and membrane-binding behavior must be carefully considered during assay design.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that rTmHSP70, despite exhibiting strong and specific immunoreactivity in Western blot analysis, is not compatible with a dipstick-based rapid diagnostic format for ovine coenurosis. The inability to generate a detectable test line signal under optimized conditions highlights the critical importance of assay format compatibility in diagnostic antigen selection. Comparative evaluation using a partially purified hydatid cyst antigen showed that signal generation in dipstick assays can be achieved through increased protein content, but at the cost of reduced specificity and false-positive reactions. These findings emphasize that antigen immunoreactivity alone is insufficient for the successful development of field-applicable rapid diagnostic tests and that factors such as antigen purity, epitope accessibility, and membrane-binding behavior are decisive determinants of assay performance. Importantly, the observed negative outcome should be interpreted as a format-dependent diagnostic limitation rather than as a lack of immunological relevance of rTmHSP70. Overall, this study provides a platform-oriented framework for antigen evaluation and offers a cautionary perspective to guide future efforts in the development of reliable rapid diagnostic assays for C. cerebralis infection in sheep. Future research should therefore focus on the evaluation of alternative T. multiceps antigens, the design of epitope-focused or chimeric constructs, the incorporation of signal amplification strategies, and the assessment of alternative rapid diagnostic platforms to overcome the limitations identified in this study.
