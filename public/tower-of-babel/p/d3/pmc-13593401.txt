A Multicenter, Mixed Methods Study of United States Heart Teams and Health Policy: The EVOLVE Study Design and Protocol
Authors: Megan Coylewright, Dhaval Kolte, Gina Kruse, Rakesh M Suri, Sammy Elmariah, Susan R Strong, Elizabeth M Perpetua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of patients with aortic stenosis is rapidly evolving, along with the “heart team,” a multidisciplinary group collaborating in program management. There is scant research providing rigorous data on the variability in heart teams’ approach to optimal patient care delivery. This investigation aimed to describe the current health care delivery strategies and attitudes of contemporary heart teams at diverse hospitals across the United States and provide key insight into future health policy and guideline directions.MethodsWe will conduct a convergent parallel design mixed methods study integrating quantitative (survey) and qualitative (semistructured interviews, coded using qualitative research software) data to develop a deeper understanding of heart valve team roles, functions, health care delivery strategies, and clinician perceptions of the impacts of health policy mandates on team and patient experience. Guided by a grounded theory approach, we will generate themes inductively from perspectives expressed by the participants. Heart valve teams will be recruited until saturation of themes and to maximize variation in practice settings (academic status, geographical location, transcatheter aortic valve replacement program volume, program tenure). We anticipate 8 to 20 programs and 40 to 100 participants. This study is approved by the Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board and adheres to Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study guidelines for mixed methods research.ConclusionsThis study is designed to be the largest investigation to date of United States heart teams focused on aortic stenosis, aimed to inform health policy and guideline efforts to streamline care, address continued inequities and undertreatment, and optimize patient outcomes.Keywords: aortic stenosis, health policy, heart team, heart valve team, multidisciplinary valvular heart disease, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Introduction

The management of patients with aortic stenosis (AS) rapidly evolved following the first-in-human transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in 2002.1 Today, TAVR is the first-line treatment option for many patients with severe AS in the United States (US), with an American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association class I guideline recommendation for symptomatic patients over 65 years of age, although formal research on TAVR heart teams’ composition, activities, and beliefs is uncommon.2,3

Heart teams were first formalized in protocols for large, randomized trials comparing interventional and surgical treatments for coronary artery disease.4,5 The earliest mandate for patients considering TAVR began in the randomized trials of TAVR vs medical therapy, with members of both subspecialties agreeing upon patient suitability for randomization.6,7 This heart team approach was further formalized in the first Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services approval for TAVR in 2012, with a national coverage determination (NCD): in order to be reimbursed for the procedure, patients had to be under the care of a heart team, with cardiac surgeons and interventional cardiologists performing TAVR together. However, although there was a requirement for patients to undergo in-person visits with 2 cardiac surgeons, there was no requirement for a visit with an interventional cardiologist.8

As TAVR became more predictable and low risk, and the global burden of AS became more evident, calls for more efficient, equitable, and effective care delivery systems increased. A step toward this included the revised NCD in 2019, reducing the involvement of 2 cardiac surgeons to 1 while adding a requirement for a visit with an interventionalist; removing specific risk categories (ie, high vs intermediate risk of surgery); and reducing volume requirements for hospital participation, expanding the number of hospitals able to offer TAVR as an option.9 This study begins at a time before the reopening of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services NCD delineating the payer mandate for a heart team approach in the care of patients with AS; it is anticipated this may change, given growing evidence on the role of TAVR for patients with asymptomatic AS.10

Today’s heart teams face distinct clinical scenarios as the field has evolved. For many patients, treatment selection and procedural planning have been simplified as devices and planning software have iterated, and teams have gained extensive experience in the 15 years since the Food and Drug Administration approval.11 Heart teams also encounter increasing complexity for other patients, such as when lifetime management of valve disease is introduced into decision-making for both the initial valve selection as well as valve-in-valve interventions. In addition, there is greater recognition of the increasing overall burden of AS, with nearly half of patients with severe AS being untreated.12 Although automated clinician notifications based on imaging results may address some of this gap, there remains a need to drive improvements in efficiency to meet increasing demand.13

Research is available on the basic heart team composition and activities such as heart team meetings14 and spans coronary artery disease,15, 16, 17 heart failure,18,19 pregnancy/congenital heart disease20 and valvular heart disease.21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Most of this research describes international experience in single centers; there are no studies to date on heart valve team care delivery in the United States.3 To study the associations of heart valve team care delivery and outcomes, there is a primary need to first rigorously describe heart valve team concepts and phenomena. At the core of heart teams’ successes are the values and beliefs of heart team members, which shape how they approach clinical problems, individually, collectively, and from a systems perspective in which policy shapes operational reality. High-quality research in this area is best served by a mixed methods approach, which combines both qualitative and quantitative data to provide both objective measures and data capturing the nuance of real-world experiences of heart teams and how they have evolved. With the integration of both types of data, mixed methods research is able to provide the methodology needed to study complex human relationships, interactions, and structures of care that define heart teams and affect their patient outcomes.26

The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend a heart team assessment of all patients with severe AS when intervention is considered yet required, for reimbursement, only when TAVR is utilized.2,8,9 It is unknown if heart teams agree that in today’s environment, all patients require a multidisciplinary team for assessment, procedural planning, and procedural performance; heart teams have already begun designating how and which patients are discussed at weekly multidisciplinary meetings, ad hoc meetings, or through other methods to use time and resources more efficiently.27,28 Notably, without an analogous payer requirement, patients referred directly to cardiac surgeons may not be offered an assessment from a multidisciplinary heart team.29 It is also not well understood how local heart teams would interpret and implement a renewed ability to select which patients would be jointly seen in clinic or require a dual-operator procedural approach. Finally, a key question is how heart teams would plan to use additional time freed from these tasks, for activities such as high-quality and value-based efforts, including outreach to address underdiagnosed and undertreated patients in the community.

Purpose

This investigation aims to describe the current composition, activities, and health care delivery strategies of contemporary heart teams at diverse hospitals across the United States, and through qualitative analysis, further understand the attitudes and beliefs of heart team members that shape care delivery patterns. As such, this is the first, large, multicenter, mixed methods study of the heart valve team with input from multidisciplinary team members on the front lines of caring for patients with AS.

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

We will conduct a convergent parallel design mixed methods study that will integrate quantitative (survey) and qualitative (semistructured interviews) data from heart valve teams at US hospitals providing care to patients with AS and offering aortic valve replacement (semistructured interview guides included in Appendix) (Central Illustration). Convergent parallel design in mixed methods research involves the simultaneous collection and analysis of both quantitative and qualitative data, with convergence following data collection to provide a greater understanding of heart teams’ attitudes, beliefs, and composition. We will compare and synthesize results from both methods to develop a deeper understanding of heart valve team roles, functions, health care delivery strategies, and clinician perceptions of the impacts of health policy mandates on team and patient experience. This study is approved by the Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board. This research adheres to the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study guidelines for mixed methods research.30 Robert Wood Johnson Foundation audit trail procedures are followed.31

The semistructured interview guides were prepared by the multidisciplinary research team, comprised of advanced practice clinicians, interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and a patient representative with extensive experience in heart team clinical practice and research, and with substantial input from a qualitative research expert. The quantitative surveys include a multiple-choice questionnaire on hospital program and participant characteristics, and the Maslach Burnout Inventory , whose emotional exhaustion subscale correlated with a single-item burnout measure in a validation study among physicians (r = 0.64; p < 0.0001).32 Content validity and face validity of the program/participant survey were determined with a group of experts comprised of 2 interventional cardiologists, 2 advanced practice clinicians who also serve as senior administrative leaders, and a patient representative. The Maslach Burnout Inventory is optional.

Site selection

Purposive sampling for saturation will be employed in 2 phases to recruit heart teams practicing in diverse settings (academic status, geographical region, TAVR program volume, program tenure) (Table 1). First, hospitals involved in the Heart Valve Collaboratory CORE Network,33 those at larger systems traditionally engaged in research and academic efforts, and diverse institutions in which the research team has clinician or leadership contacts will be invited in the first cohort. If saturation of themes is not obtained, a second cohort will be pursued to maximize variation of heart teams included, with a focus on ensuring inclusion of centers in rural areas, nonacademic or nonresearch centers, centers that treat underrepresented patient populations, and lower TAVR volume centers. Sites and participants will be recruited via email and recruitment webinars.

Quantitative methodology

One quantitative survey will be administered at each site, focused on information about heart team composition; hospital volume; the processes related to heart team meetings, clinic appointments, and procedures; and the perceived impacts of health policy on patient wait times to procedure, staff burden of work, and burnout. The survey may be completed by any member of the heart team; it will first be presented to the person in the role of valve coordinator. The survey and optional Maslach Burnout Inventory will be administered and scored using REDCap Cloud (nPhase Inc).

Qualitative methodology and sample size

We aim to conduct in-depth, semistructured interviews with key hospital staff, including individuals who are directly involved in the clinical care of patients with AS (eg, physicians, nurse practitioners or physician associates, structural heart/valve coordinators), as well as those responsible for the overall oversight of the aortic valve heart team program (eg, directors of structural heart disease and interventional cardiology, chief/chairs of cardiology, service line administrators). The final sample size will be based on theoretical saturation (the point at which no new codes or concepts emerge from successive interviews). Saturation is a concept from grounded theory and is used to ensure significant rigor of the investigation. Gathering additional data until saturation is identified is essential to determine whether the sample is sufficient to describe and define the phenomenon being studied and to contribute to content validity.34 Transcripts from the first 10 interviews will be reviewed for theoretical saturation, and semistructured interview guides will be revised accordingly.30,35 Based on prior literature, we anticipate sampling 8 to 10 hospitals before reaching theoretical saturation; if saturation is not reached, a second cohort of 8 to 10 hospitals will be recruited.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 We anticipate interviewing up to 5 heart team members per site, aiming for inclusion of primary heart team roles as described. This translates to an a priori sampling threshold of at least 5 of each heart team member discipline or role, or a minimum of 40 participants and a total of up to 100 participants.41,42 Data collection and analysis will proceed simultaneously, with the final sample size based on theoretical saturation.

Interviews will be held remotely or in-person, recorded using the Zoom (Zoom Video Communications) meeting platform. All interviews will be recorded and transcribed using artificial intelligence software within Zoom. All interviews will be conducted by 1 researcher (E.M.P.), selected by the study team for balance of neutrality and extensive clinical, research, and administrative experience with heart teams across the US. Semistructured interview guides will undergo revision by the study team (M.C., E.M.P.) following initial interviews to ensure that framing of questions and related probes remain neutral, inviting positive, negative, and neutral perspectives on all issues that arise in the interviews. Emerging needs for follow-up questions and probes will be included to ensure that the participant responses are of sufficient depth to meet our objectives. Each interviewee will be informed that the length of the interview time will be kept to under 45 minutes, and $100 remuneration will be provided to participants upon completion of the interview and/or survey.

Each interview transcription will undergo a systematic deidentification process by research team members and research assistants, removing all identifying features prior to analysis. All names of people, locations, hospital names, and other identifiers will be removed and replaced with general terms (ie, “hospital” or “cardiac surgeon”). Transcripts will also be reviewed for accuracy with input from the lead interviewer, as well as feedback from 2 coders. Any edits to clarify words misidentified in transcription, as well as replacement of identifiers, are tracked.

Data analysis—Qualitative

Guided by a grounded theory approach, we will generate themes inductively from perspectives expressed by the interviewees, rather than using a priori themes or testing predetermined hypotheses.43 The multidisciplinary investigator team will work collaboratively to identify themes. First, 2 investigators (D.K., M.C.) will independently review transcripts, field notes, and hospital documents from the initial 2 sites, applying codes to text segments as concepts become apparent, and then convene with the larger team for initial confirmation. Then, 10 additional transcripts will be reviewed, along with assigned parent codes, to reach consensus over differences in code structure.

Codes will be defined within categories, organized initially around the key concepts of the heart team’s interaction (clinic encounters; heart team meetings; TAVR procedures), and reviewed again after additional interviews are performed. We will use the constant comparative method, in which essential concepts from interview data over successive interviews are coded and compared to previously coded segments to ensure consistent application of codes and to identify recurrent themes across the data. We will continue this iterative process of refining codes and describing themes until no new concepts emerge in the remaining transcripts, reflecting theoretical saturation in the dataset.

To enhance rigor and ensure validity, we will use triangulation. We will triangulate by: (1) having multiple investigators code data and participate in analysis and interpretation, (2) performing interviews with multiple key informants, and (3) collecting hospital documents and protocols about the care of patients with aortic valve disease. To enhance reliability, we will ensure there is no drift in the definition of codes by constantly comparing data with codes and documenting code definitions and examples in the codebook, and by calculating intercoder agreement with a target kappa of >0.8 for selected, high-volume parent codes.44

Objectivity and reliability will be ensured by a review of all identified themes by a broader, multidisciplinary team composed of cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, advanced practice clinicians, patients, and qualitative researchers. Multiple video-based meetings will be held to review identified themes and a large volume of representative quotes by the larger team, with modification of themes and clarification of quotes within the broader interview context when needed. Validity will be ensured by the concept of construct validity, in which the findings are compared and verified with results from prior studies. The qualitative data analysis will be performed using NVivo (Lumivero).

Data analysis—Quantitative and transformative

Data from the quantitative survey will be collected and analyzed separately (Power BI, Microsoft) using descriptive statistics. Once qualitative analysis has moved into primary themes, the team will begin mixed methods integration. Quantitative survey data will inform qualitative themes, providing context and serving to further inform differences between participants and programs. For limited themes, further analysis will include the transformation of some qualitative content into quantifiable categories using clear, transparent coding rules vetted with the broader research team. Quotes will be collected to provide an audit trail and back up to prespecified criteria for transformation.45 This process will be used to generate further inferences possible from combining multiple types of data. The integration strategy is contingent upon themes as the data are understood and, thus, is not specified a priori. If possible, qualitative and quantitative data will be combined in a joint display for further illustration of themes.26,46

Ethics and privacy

All participants will be informed that participation is completely voluntary and that their personal data will be protected. Quotes used to support identified themes will be referenced only to the role in the heart team (ie, valve coordinator). Participants have the right to refuse any question posed by the interviewer, including participant demographic characteristic information. Edited interview transcripts will be stored on a secure server and uploaded into NVivo (Lumivero) qualitative data analysis software. All identifying Zoom recordings will be destroyed at the conclusion of the study. Transcripts will be retained according to institutional review board policies.

Participants will not be identifiable in any publication or dissemination activity, and confidentiality is ensured by using study numbers and heart team role only in analysis. Ethics approval was obtained from Western Copernicus Group (study #139118, IRB #20251294).

Possible outcomes and benefits of the study

From our collective experience working as members of heart teams at institutions across the US, as well as outside the US, we anticipate marked variation in the composition, activities, attitudes, and beliefs of heart teams based on features, including demographic characteristic diversity; team experience; geography; and relationship of the hospital to other institutions (ie, hub and spoke model), among others. Our study is designed to provide a breadth of viewpoints that encompass the experience of most heart teams across the country for use in the development of guidelines and health policies that seek to provide guidance on best practices in the care of patients with AS. We anticipate heart teams will communicate how local heart team composition and activities have changed over time, and provide input on how heart teams ought to evolve to address today's challenges, and the anticipated future needs for teams caring for patients with AS.

Conclusion

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement is now the first-line treatment option for many patients with severe, symptomatic AS in the US, and the “heart team” has been lauded as a necessary component of any program offering transcatheter aortic valve therapies.47 It is widely accepted that the increased collaboration between cardiac surgeons and interventional cardiologists has contributed to the safe and effective dissemination of TAVR over the past 15 years, with this partnership enabled through a payer mandate. This research aims to describe not only current practice patterns of contemporary heart teams but also provide critical on-the-ground input from a diverse group of clinicians and team members about future directions. This research does not have a precedent in US heart valve teams; prior research efforts focused on heart teams have not utilized qualitative, interview-based methods to understand true practice patterns and insights from front-line clinicians and essential team members who are well-informed to describe current health care needs and gaps. Findings from the EVOLVE study will be an important contribution to any health policy or guidelines on the use of a heart team approach to improve outcomes for cardiovascular patients.
