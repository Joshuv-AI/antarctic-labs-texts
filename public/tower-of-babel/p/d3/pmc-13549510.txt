Durability of Drug-Eluting Balloon-Mounted Stents in Symptomatic ICAD: A Propensity-Matched Landmark Analysis of RO-ZES Versus Wingspan or Medical Management Beyond 24 Months
Authors: Omid Shoraka, Oz Haim, Samantha Miller, Joanna M. Roy, Vinay Jaikumar, Basel Musmar, Omar Tanweer, Jan-Karl Burkhardt, Pascal M. Jabbour, Adnan H. Siddiqui, Farhan Siddiq, Ameer E. Hassan, Ramesh Grandhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic disease can be managed with medical therapy, risk factor control, angioplasty, and stenting. The Wingspan stent demonstrated limited efficacy and higher adverse outcomes than medical management in the SAMMPRIS trial (Stenting Versus Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis). Drug-eluting stents, including the Resolute Onyx Zotarolimus-eluting stent, have demonstrated superior outcomes beyond 1 year. This study assessed outcomes at ≥24 months after stenting.METHODS:This retrospective multicenter study compared patients diagnosed with severe symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic disease who underwent Resolute Onyx Zotarolimus-eluting stent stenting between March 2018 and May 2021 with patients from the percutaneous angioplasty and stenting and the aggressive medical management arms of the SAMMPRIS trial. Individuals who remained alive, stroke-free, and under follow-up at 24 months were included. Propensity score matching was used to align the 3 arms, and landmark analysis using inverse probability weighting, accounting for differential survival to 24 months, was used as complementary sensitivity analysis. The primary outcomes measured were postlandmark (ie, 24 months) stroke recurrence, intracranial hemorrhage, and all-cause mortality.RESULTS:After applying the 24-month landmark and propensity score matching, 204 patients (68 per arm) were eligible (median follow-up, 36 months). Unweighted rates of stroke (P=0.147), intracranial hemorrhage (P=0.362), and mortality (P=0.168) did not differ significantly among groups. Weighted multivariable logistic and Cox regression analyses, adjusting for residual postmatch differences, similarly showed no significant differences in stroke recurrence. Kaplan-Meier analysis did not demonstrate a significant difference in overall survival across the 3 arms (log-rank P=0.139).CONCLUSIONS:Although previous studies have demonstrated the superiority of the Resolute Onyx Zotarolimus-eluting stent compared with the Wingspan system and aggressive medical management for the treatment of severe symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic disease, the present landmark analysis suggests that these benefits may not consistently extend beyond 24 months. One speculative explanation is that the drug elution–related benefit of the Resolute Onyx Zotarolimus-eluting stent may attenuate over time, but this was not directly assessed in the present study. Prospective, controlled trials are necessary to confirm the implications of our long-term findings.

Intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) is a major contributor to the development of ischemic stroke, accounting for ≈20% of cases worldwide.1 Patients with symptomatic ICAD have a high risk of stroke recurrence.2 Current management strategies for stroke prevention in patients with ICAD include antithrombotic and statin therapy, risk factor modification, angioplasty, and stenting.1 To date, only 2 stent systems have received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration under a Humanitarian Device Exemption for ICAD treatment: the NEUROLINK (Guidant Corporation, Boston Scientific, Indianapolis, IN) and Wingspan (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) systems.3,4 The NEUROLINK stent, a balloon-mounted stent (BMS), initially demonstrated acceptable outcomes5; however, further investigations were discontinued as stent technology rapidly advanced.3 The Wingspan system is a self-expanding stent (SES) that was approved in 2005, showed early promise,6 gained widespread interest, and was evaluated for use in the treatment of severe symptomatic ICAD in several prospective clinical trials.7–10 Despite initial optimism, however, these studies revealed that stenting did not significantly reduce the risk of recurrent stroke. Moreover, stenting was associated with more adverse outcomes, including higher rates of stroke and mortality during the initial procedure, which were not noted with medical management (MM) alone, even during delayed follow-up.

Technological advancements in stent design and deployment methods have led to the off-label use of various stents for treating severe ICAD. These stents use different deployment techniques, including self-expanding and balloon-mounted systems.3 Among the newer stent designs, the Resolute Onyx Zotarolimus-eluting stent (RO-ZES; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) is a BMS with drug-eluting properties.11 Recently published studies have shown that RO-ZES offers promising clinical outcomes for patients with symptomatic ICAD, demonstrating superiority over the Wingspan system and MM alone over a period of >1 year.12–14

In this study, we examined clinical outcomes ≥24 months after patients underwent treatment for severe symptomatic ICAD with RO-ZES stents to determine whether the drug-eluting properties of RO-ZES provide a sustained benefit compared with the Wingspan bare-metal SES or aggressive MM. To assess this, the rates of stroke recurrence, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and all-cause mortality ≥24 months after stenting were compared in patients with ICAD treated with RO-ZES and those who underwent percutaneous angioplasty and stenting (PTAS) or were managed with aggressive medical therapy within the SAMMPRIS trial (Stenting Versus Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis).7

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective, multicenter study was conducted across 6 centers, involving multiple interventionalists. Patients treated for severe symptomatic ICAD with RO-ZES between March 2018 and May 2021, with follow-up extending through October 2024, were included. Institutional review board approval was obtained with a waiver of informed consent at each participating institution. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines were used in preparing this report. Patients enrolled in the SAMMPRIS trial who underwent PTAS or MM served as the historical comparison cohorts for propensity-matched analysis. Data are available on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Patient Populations

RO-ZES Cohort

We retrospectively collected data from patients who underwent endovascular stenting with RO-ZES for treatment of symptomatic ICAD. Patients who received RO-ZES stenting were adults (≥18 years) who experienced recurrent nondisabling strokes or transient ischemic attacks with 70% to 99% stenosis of an intracranial artery. All patients had experienced at least 1 ischemic event despite receiving the best medical therapy, reflecting current clinical use in treatment-refractory cases. Best medical therapy consisted of guideline-based secondary stroke prevention for symptomatic ICAD, including antiplatelet therapy (commonly dual-antiplatelet therapy in the early treatment period), together with active management of vascular risk factors, such as elevated systolic blood pressure and low-density lipoprotein levels, diabetes, smoking, and obesity.7 Because this was a 24-month landmark analysis, patients were eligible for inclusion in the postlandmark cohort if they were alive, stroke-free, and under active follow-up at 24 months after the index procedure or enrollment. The 24-month time point was selected to reduce bias from early deaths or events and to evaluate long-term outcomes among patients who remained event-free at that point. For the postlandmark cohort, follow-up was measured from the 24-month landmark to death or last documented clinical contact, with censoring at the time of last follow-up. Patients who were lost to follow-up before 24 months were not eligible for inclusion in the postlandmark analysis.

SAMMPRIS Cohorts

Data from the SAMMPRIS trial were reviewed to identify patients in the PTAS group who underwent stenting using the Wingspan system and those randomized to the aggressive MM arm. Similar to our RO-ZES cohort, these patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) who experienced transient ischemic attack or nondisabling strokes with 70% to 99% stenosis of an intracranial artery. All patients in the RO-ZES cohort had experienced recurrent ischemic events despite best medical therapy, whereas, in the SAMMPRIS trial, failure of medical therapy before enrollment was not required. As a result, identical inclusion criteria could not be applied retrospectively, and this inherent clinical difference was retained and acknowledged in subsequent analysis and interpretation. All SAMMPRIS cases meeting stenosis severity and qualifying ischemic event were included for comparative analysis.

Propensity Matching and Landmark Analysis

To minimize the baseline differences and reduce confounding, propensity score matching was performed among patients who were eligible for the landmark analysis. Propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model incorporating baseline covariates, including age, sex, race, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, affected artery, stenosis ratio, and dual-antiplatelet use at the qualifying event. The resulting predicted probabilities were then used to perform a 1:1:1 nearest neighbor matching without replacement, matching individuals across the 3 treatment arms based on proximity in their estimated probability profiles. During this process, 68 patients treated with the Wingspan system, 68 patients who underwent maximal MM, and 68 patients treated with the RO-ZES system were matched for subsequent analysis of long-term follow-up outcomes.

To address potential selection bias arising from differential survival and follow-up before the 24-month landmark, we estimated each patient’s probability of being alive, stroke-free, and under follow-up at 24 months using the full baseline cohort. These probabilities were derived from a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating age, sex, race, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, affected artery, qualifying cerebrovascular event, time from qualifying event to enrollment, treatment type, and arterial stenosis ratio. The inverse of each estimated probability was applied as an analytical weight (inverse probability weighting [IPW]) in all postlandmark regression models within the matched sample. This weighting assigned greater influence to individuals who were less likely, based on their baseline characteristics, to have reached the landmark, thereby rebalancing the analysis to approximate the composition of the original baseline population. In parallel, unweighted descriptive outcome rates were also calculated from the landmark time point forward but were interpreted conditionally on survival and event-free status at 24 months. Integrating IPW into the postlandmark models functioned as a sensitivity adjustment, improving representativeness of the postlandmark estimates and mitigating bias from analyzing only the healthiest survivors.

Data Collection

The collected data included demographics (age, sex, and race), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia), smoking status, qualifying cerebrovascular event (recurrent transient ischemic attack or stroke), history of stroke and death within 24 months after stenting, and lesion characteristics (affected artery, stenosis length, and stenosis ratio). The exposure variable was stent type (RO-ZES or Wingspan system).

The primary outcomes were recurrent ischemic stroke in the same vascular territory, ICH, and all-cause mortality occurring beyond the 24-month time point. Symptomatic events were clinically adjudicated and confirmed by neuroimaging, and asymptomatic strokes were identified based on scheduled follow-up imaging through comparison with prior studies. Time-to-event analyses were performed from the 24-month landmark to the occurrence of the first postlandmark event or censoring. Outcome counts represent the number of individuals who experienced recurrent events, rather than the total number of events in the population, because some individuals had multiple recurrences ≥24 months after stenting.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) when appropriate, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess normality and the Levene test to check for homogeneity of variance. For parametric data with equal variance, comparisons were made using a 2-tailed Student t test, whereas the Welch t test was applied when variances were unequal. Categorical variables were analyzed using either the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test, depending on expected cell counts in the contingency table.

Binary logistic regression, both unadjusted and adjusted, was used to assess associations between stent type and outcomes. The weighted unadjusted model was designated as the primary model, while the weighted adjusted model was considered a secondary model. Adjusted analyses were controlled for baseline variables that remained imbalanced between the 2 groups despite propensity score matching, defined as having a standardized mean difference (SMD) ≥0.15. These variables included race, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, stenosis location, preprocedure arterial stenosis ratio, type of qualifying cerebrovascular event, and time from qualifying event to enrollment. Time-to-event outcomes were evaluated using Cox regression analysis. Postlandmark logistic and Cox regression analyses incorporated IPW to account for the probability of surviving and remaining stroke-free to the 24-month landmark. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1) and IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0.0.

Results

Prelandmark Eligibility

A total of 547 patients were included at baseline before propensity matching, comprising 116 in the RO-ZES arm, 207 in the Wingspan arm, and 224 in the MM arm (Table 1). At 24 months, 389 patients (71.1%) remained alive, stroke-free, and under follow-up, thereby meeting eligibility for the postlandmark analysis. This included 68 patients (58.6%) from the RO-ZES cohort, 155 (74.9%) from the Wingspan cohort, and 166 (71.1%) from the MM group. During the prelandmark period (0–24 months), stroke occurred in 84 patients (15.4%) overall, with the lowest rate in the RO-ZES group (4.3%, n=5), the highest rate in the Wingspan group (18.8%, n=39), and 17.9% in the MM group (n=40). A total of 54 patients (9.9%) were lost to follow-up before 24 months, including 34 (29.3%) in the RO-ZES cohort, 8 (3.9%) in the Wingspan cohort, and 12 (5.4%) in the MM cohort (P<0.001).

In the postlandmark matched cohort, the mean age was 62.9 ± 11.3 years (SMD=0.104), and 60 patients (29.4%; SMD=0.107) were female (Table 2). Hypertension was present in 166 patients (81.4%; SMD=0.151) and diabetes in 97 (47.5%; SMD=0.158). The qualifying cerebrovascular event was stroke in 128 patients (62.7%) and transient ischemic attack in 76 (37.3%; SMD=0.162). These variables were generally well balanced across treatment arms. Despite propensity matching, some residual differences in the distribution of postlandmark variables persisted. Notably, the RO-ZES group included a higher proportion of Hispanic cases (41.2%), whereas the Wingspan and MM groups had predominantly White patients (73.5% and 75.0%, respectively; SMD=0.418). Current and prior smoking were reported in 73 (35.8%) and 28 (13.7%) patients, respectively (SMD=0.304), with the highest prevalence in the Wingspan group (42.6% and 14.7%). Hyperlipidemia was present in 160 patients (78.4%; SMD=0.240), again with the highest proportion in the Wingspan group (85.3%). The median time from qualifying event to the index procedure or enrollment was shorter in the RO-ZES cohort (6.5 [IQR, 2–30] months) compared with the Wingspan (7 [IQR, 4–17]) and the MM (8 [IQR, 3–20.5]) groups (SMD=0.327).

Postlandmark Matched Lesion Characteristics

Stenosis location varied across treatment arms between the anterior and posterior circulations (SMD=0.301), with a greater proportion of anterior circulation lesions in the MM group (66.2%) and a higher proportion of posterior circulation lesions in the RO-ZES group (54.4%). The mean preprocedural stenosis ratio also differed across the 3 treatment arms (RO-ZES, 79.0 ± 11.4%; Wingspan, 75.4 ± 9.2%; and MM, 75.9 ± 7.8%; SMD=0.242; Table 3). Lesion distribution by involved artery differed considerably among groups (SMD=0.622): RO-ZES was more frequently used for lesions involving the petrous internal carotid artery (13.2%) and vertebral artery (47.1%), whereas the Wingspan system was more commonly applied in the supraclinoid internal carotid artery (10.3%), M1 (25.0%), M2 (10.3%), and basilar artery (13.2%) segments and MM for patients with cavernous internal carotid artery (10.3%) and M1 segment (30.9%) lesions. Finally, the Wingspan group had a significantly greater length of stenosis compared with the RO-ZES group (SMD=0.655).

Postlandmark Long-Term Outcomes

Unweighted postlandmark recurrences of stroke in the same arterial region and ICH were evaluated in all treatment groups (Table 4). No significant differences were observed in the rates of stroke (RO-ZES, 11.8%; Wingspan, 4.4%; and MM, 4.4%; P=0.147) or ICH (RO-ZES, 2.9%; Wingspan, 1.5%; and MM, 0.0%; P=0.362). Although the mortality rate was higher in the MM group (4.4%), followed by the Wingspan (1.5%) and RO-ZES (0%), these differences were not statistically significant (P=0.168).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate no significant differences in postlandmark (ie, 24 months) rates of stroke, ICH, or mortality among patients treated with the RO-ZES stent in real-world clinical practice compared with those treated with the Wingspan system or aggressive MM in the SAMMPRIS trial. These results were consistent across multiple complementary statistical approaches designed to minimize bias and enhance comparability among cohorts. To account for differences in follow-up completeness and survival to the 24-month landmark, particularly the higher prelandmark loss to follow-up observed in the retrospective RO-ZES group compared with the prospectively followed SAMMPRIS arms, the IPW modeling was used as an additional means of sensitivity analysis. Each patient was assigned a weight corresponding to their estimated probability of being alive, stroke-free, and under follow-up at 24 months, thereby reweighting postlandmark analyses to better reflect the baseline cohort composition and reduce survivorship bias. Baseline heterogeneity was further harmonized using propensity score matching, which balanced demographic and vascular risk factors across the 3 treatment arms. Given that patients in the RO-ZES group had experienced at least 1 ischemic event despite receiving the best medical therapy, they likely represented a more advanced stage of disease compared with SAMMPRIS participants, who were enrolled after their first qualifying cerebrovascular event. Additional adjusted logistic and Cox regression models were applied to adjust for any residual imbalances. Across all analyses, no statistically significant differences were observed between the treatment arms in long-term stroke recurrence or mortality beyond the 24-month landmark.

Previous evidence has consistently demonstrated that the Wingspan system offers no advantage and, in some trials, performed worse than aggressive MM in preventing recurrent stroke among patients with severe symptomatic ICAD. After its US Food and Drug Administration approval under the Humanitarian Device Exemption in 2005 for the treatment of patients with ≥50% intracranial stenosis with recurrent ischemic events,4 the Wingspan system, a bare-metal SES, gained attention through clinical studies aiming to evaluate its potential to improve outcomes for patients with severe symptomatic ICAD.6 However, the SAMMPRIS7 and Vitesse Intracranial Stent Study for Ischemic Stroke Therapy trials8 demonstrated that the Wingspan system did not offer significant advantages in improving clinical outcomes over MM alone. As a result, current guidelines continue to recommend MM as the standard of care for patients with severe symptomatic ICAD.15,16 Both trials, however, have been subject to considerable methodological criticism. Key concerns include the inclusion of patients irrespective of stroke mechanism, the relatively early timing of stenting after the qualifying event, and potential variability in operator experience with the Wingspan system.17,18 The current study attempted to address these prior criticisms within its RO-ZES arm design. However, it is unlikely that factors such as timing or operator experience would have a significant impact on long-term stent performance beyond the 2-year mark. One notable methodological difference that may still influence comparative outcomes is patient selection: ≈35.3% of participants in the Wingspan arm of SAMMPRIS were not demonstrably refractory to medical therapy at the time of enrollment,17 whereas all patients in the RO-ZES cohort were enrolled for stenting after having failed optimized MM. This suggests that the RO-ZES population may have represented a higher-risk group.

Evidence suggests that a BMS offers several technical advantages over an SES, including higher radial force, greater precision in placement, and improved control over expansion.19 Nevertheless, an SES may be preferable in cases where there is a substantial diameter mismatch between the proximal and distal segments of the lesion.20 Early iterations of BMS, however, were limited by low flexibility,19 which influenced their applicability. These technical distinctions suggest that there is a potential for differing clinical outcomes between BMS and SES. In a meta-analysis involving 3049 patients, Abdollahifard et al21 reported that BMS use was associated with a 3% lower combined incidence of major and minor strokes and mortality at 1-year follow-up compared with SES. Similarly, Qureshi et al22 conducted a meta-analysis that identified ≈3% lower rate of stroke and mortality with BMS over a median follow-up period of 1 year. In addition, SES has been associated with higher rates of in-stent restenosis within a year of follow-up compared with BMS.21–23 This observation highlights the potential short-term advantages of BMS, which may be particularly beneficial because of their mechanical properties, during the early postprocedural phase.24 However, most studies included in these meta-analyses focused on follow-up periods of <1 year. Although prior evidence has left uncertainties regarding the long-term performance of BMS relative to SES, the present findings support that long-term SES outcomes might eventually approximate those of BMS beyond 2 years of implantation.

The off-label use of RO-ZES as a balloon-mounted drug-eluting stent for treating patients with severe symptomatic ICAD has shown promising clinical outcomes at 72-hour,25 30-day,12 1-year,13 and long-term follow-ups compared with both the Wingspan system and MM.14 Technical evaluations reveal that RO-ZES and its latest iteration, the Onyx Frontier, have overcome the lower flexibility associated with earlier generations of bare-metal stents.26–28 Despite the promising outcomes in studies published to date, findings from the current study indicate that although the device offers early benefits, these advantages may not consistently translate into better clinical outcomes beyond 24 months compared with the Wingspan system and aggressive MM. This decline in efficacy could also be attributable to the limited long-term bioactivity of the drug-eluting coating on the stent.29 These findings align with those from a meta-analysis by Piccolo et al30 involving 26 616 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention that demonstrated a nondivergent pattern of clinical outcomes beyond 1 year of follow-up between drug-eluting stents and BMS. This finding reinforces the notion that the therapeutic advantage conferred by drug-eluting coatings diminishes over time, ultimately converging with outcomes observed in bare-metal stents within a 1- to 2-year period.

Limitations

This preliminary study includes the longest available comparison of the performance of the RO-ZES system with the older Wingspan system or aggressive MM using a matched landmark framework extending beyond 24 months; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of the RO-ZES arm is inherently subject to selection bias and confounding factors. In addition, as mentioned previously, patients in the RO-ZES cohort experienced symptomatic neurological events despite best medical therapy, whereas the SAMMPRIS study did not require failure of medical therapy. Although our matching strategy addressed baseline variables available in all cohorts, this intrinsic difference may contribute to outcome differences and should be considered when interpreting results. To further mitigate residual imbalance, multivariable modeling was utilized; nevertheless, these approaches cannot eliminate unmeasured confounding.

Second, the relatively small sample size (N=204), along with the low incidence of outcome events, may have resulted in limited statistical power and an increased risk of type II error, potentially obscuring true differences between groups. Third, as a landmark analysis, unweighted outcome estimates are conditional on survival and event-free follow-up at 24 months. Although IPW was used as a sensitivity analysis to minimize differential survival and follow-up bias, residual selection effects cannot be entirely excluded.

Fourth, interpretation of the prelandmark outcomes in Table 1 should be made with caution, as these data were included to illustrate differences in follow-up completeness and landmark eligibility across cohorts. They are not intended to imply comparative treatment effects during the initial 2-year period. The cohorts differ fundamentally in design and clinical context, with RO-ZES representing a real-world, retrospective population and the Wingspan and MM groups derived from the prospective SAMMPRIS trial. Shoraka et al14 have previously investigated the long-term outcomes of RO-ZES in a propensity-matched analysis, demonstrating significantly lower rates of recurrent stroke and ICH compared with PTAS and MM. However, those findings also suggested that the relative benefit of drug-eluting stents may attenuate beyond 24 months. This observation motivated the present study’s landmark-based approach, designed to separate the early phase effects from long-term postlandmark outcomes while minimizing survival and follow-up bias through IPW modeling.

Fifth, we acknowledge that this study is subject to the inherent limitations of the SAMMPRIS trial. A key limitation is the lack of a standardized angiographic follow-up protocol in the PTAS arm ≥24 months, which prevents us from comparing the in-stent restenosis rates between the RO-ZES and Wingspan systems. Finally, both SAMMPRIS arms serve as historical controls, and standards of care have evolved because the original SAMMPRIS trial was conducted. Nevertheless, because SAMMPRIS is still considered a significant trial and influences current clinical guidelines regarding stenting versus medical therapy in ICAD, we believe that it is essential to evaluate the performance of RO-ZES against this widely cited and guideline-defining study.

Despite these limitations, this study addresses an important gap in the literature because no prior investigations have evaluated the performance of balloon-mounted drug-eluting stents compared with self-expandable bare-metal stents or aggressive MM beyond 2 years. These findings may stimulate further research and encourage the design of prospective trials with larger sample sizes to enhance statistical power, improve the precision of estimates, and validate the robustness of the observed results.

Conclusions

Although previous studies have demonstrated the superiority of RO-ZES compared with the Wingspan system or aggressive MM in the treatment of patients with severe symptomatic ICAD, the present landmark analysis suggests that these benefits may not consistently extend beyond 24 months. Importantly, these findings reflect a negative study, in which no definitive comparative advantage was observed for any cohort beyond the landmark period. Potential explanations for these observations include the inherent early clinical advantages that a BMS offers compared with an SES system. In addition, a speculative explanation is that any drug elution–related benefit of RO-ZES may attenuate after 24 months although this mechanism was not directly assessed in the present study. Given the exploratory nature of this analysis, prospective controlled trials are essential to further evaluate the duration of RO-ZES’s clinical advantages compared with the Wingspan stents.
