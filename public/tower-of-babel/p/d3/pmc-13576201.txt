Towards sustainable production of biofuels via milking the cells of Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 engineered for free fatty acid excretion
Authors: Akihiro Kato, Nobuyuki Takatani, Shin-ichi Maeda, Makiko Aichi, Tatsuo Omata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFree fatty acids are considered a potential source of biodiesel because they are excreted by engineered cyanobacterial cells, eliminating the processing costs associated with cell recovery and extraction. Although the cyanobacterial mutants engineered for the production of free fatty acids do excrete significant amounts of the product, the yield per dry cell weight is low due to their rapid proliferation and the consequent substantial cell biomass, which makes it impossible to support sustainable biofuel production. Development of strategies to increase the production of free fatty acids per cell is urgently needed.ResultsTo increase the per-cell production of free fatty acids, we attempted to increase the allocation of photosynthetically fixed carbon into fatty acid production by restricting cell proliferation. Growth limitation of free fatty acid-producing cells was achieved by cultivating dAS2T, a free fatty acid-producing mutant of Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 defective in active nitrate uptake, in media containing nitrate as the sole source of nitrogen. Although the nitrogen-limited cultivation increased the per-cell yield of free fatty acids, it significantly decreased the extracellular free fatty acid level, reducing the volumetric productivity. Overexpressing an endogenous free fatty acid-efflux pump in the dAS2T strain elevated extracellular free fatty acid level to those seen under nitrate-sufficient conditions, while maintaining slow cell growth. By decoupling the production of fatty acids from cell growth in this manner, excretion of 0.90 g of free fatty acids per g of dry cell weight was achieved in 240 h, with a free fatty acid productivity of 1.8 mg L−1 h−1. This represents the highest yield of free fatty acids obtained to date without significantly decreasing the volumetric productivity.ConclusionGrowth limitation and enhancement of excretion of the product are essential to increase the per-cell yield of the free fatty acid production system using cyanobacteria. The high yield and productivity of extracellular free fatty acids achieved in this study suggest that the cyanobacteria-based production of free fatty acids provides a reliable approach for sustainable biofuel production through the “milking” strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02782-2.Keywords: Cyanobacteria, Biofuel production, Free fatty acids, Growth limitation, Efflux pump, Per-cell yield, Productivity, Milking

Background

In response to the increasing demand for sustainable and renewable energy sources, biofuel production using photosynthetic microorganisms has been the subject of intensive research in recent years. Eukaryotic microalgae are considered to be suited for biodiesel production because they accumulate large amounts of triacylglycerol (TAG) in the cells under various stress conditions [1, 2]. Although the chemical energy contained in the intracellularly accumulated TAG is larger than the energy used to cultivate the cells, the energy return on investment (EROI) of the TAG-based biodiesel is smaller than 1 [3, 4]. The low EROI is primarily attributed to the energy-intensive nature of the processes involved in TAG extraction, such as cell drying, cell disruption, and solvent recovery [3, 5, 6]. Although a large amount of energy can be saved by omitting the cell drying step, the efficiency of TAG extraction is low when wet cells are extracted with an organic solvent, leaving most of the TAG remaining in the oil cake [3]. Therefore, energy recovery from the oil cake is essential to increase the energy return. This could be done by conversion of biomass in the cell residue into methane, ethanol, etc., but requires additional energy, making it difficult to achieve a positive energy balance [6]. Another problem is that the per-cell production of TAG is insufficient to allow for large-scale production in a sustainable manner; TAG accumulates to 50% or higher of total cell biomass in various species of microalgae when subjected to nutrient starvation or other stress conditions [1], but even when all the TAG is successfully extracted from the cells, the amount of the resultant cell residue would be equivalent to that of extracted TAG. Thus, replacement of 10% of fossil fuel with TAG-based oil would produce 1.4 × 109 tonnes of cell residue per year, which corresponds to ~ 50% of annual cereal production [7, 8]. It is difficult to consume or to recycle this amount of biomass in our economy.

To overcome the current challenges in biofuel production using microalgae, the "milking" of algal cells [9, 10] could be an alternative strategy. Milking of algal cells involves continuous removal of photosynthetic products out of the living cells. This can be mediated by passive efflux and active extrusion of the products from the cells. It can also involve solvent extraction and may be assisted by application of pulsed electric fields or mechanical pressure, etc., but the key point is that the cells must be kept alive so that they can be used for further production. Milking saves the energy required for collecting and drying the cells. Furthermore, the per-cell yield of oil can increase beyond the limitations of the intracellular space available for product storage. Therefore, milking is a more feasible approach for producing algae-based biofuels than the conventional method, which relies on intracellular lipid accumulation. However, research aimed at milking of microalgal cells has not progressed significantly, and one of the main reasons for this situation is the lack of knowledge about membrane transporters that can transport fuel-related compounds out of the cells.

Cells of the cyanobacterial mutants that are engineered to produce free fatty acids (FFAs) excrete FFAs into the growth medium [11–14]. Cyanobacteria have also been shown to possess an RND-type exporter that can export FFAs [15]. This suggests that cyanobacteria could be promising hosts for biofuel production via milking. However, the yield of extracellular FFA is generally low, about 0.1 g per gram of dry cell weight (DCW) [11, 12, 16]. By overlaying the culture with isopropyl myristate (IM), which effectively extracted FFA from the culture medium and thereby helped its excretion out of the cells, a volumetric FFA productivity of 1.5 mg L−1 h−1 and an FFA yield of 0.36 g g−1 DCW were attained in the dAS1T mutant of Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 (hereafter referred to as S.7942) in 432 h of cultivation [17]. Although the productivity was similar to those of intracellular lipids observed in cultures of various eukaryotic microalgae (Table 5 in Ref. [18]), the proportion of FFA to the total of the FFA and cell biomass, which is calculated to be 26%, is still small compared with the proportion of the TAG in the biomass of TAG-producing algal cells. To establish cyanobacterial FFA production as a practical alternative to the algal TAG system, it is necessary to increase the yield of FFA without reducing the volumetric productivity of FFA. In this study, we attempted to achieve this by decreasing the partitioning of carbon into cell biomass by limiting cell growth. The FFA-producing strain dAS2T [15], which is deficient in the high-affinity ABC-type nitrate transporter but is otherwise genetically identical with the basic FFA-producing strain dAS1T [19], was used to produce FFA during nitrate-limited growth. It is shown that enhancement of active FFA export and removal of FFA from the culture medium are essential to achieve robust FFA production under the conditions of growth limitation. Under the optimized conditions, the yield of extracellular FFA increased to 0.9 g g−1 DCW, which means that the proportion of FFA to the sum of FFA and the cell biomass is 47%, a situation similar to the nitrogen-starved microalgal cells where TAG intracellularly accumulates to ~ 50% of the cell biomass. The issues to be addressed to further increase the yield and productivity of FFAs to achieve milking production of FFA using cyanobacteria are discussed.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

All the mutant strains used in this study were constructed previously in this laboratory, using the “Small-plasmid-cured” (SPc) derivative of S.7942 [20] as the wild-type (WT) strain (Table 1). Since the SPc strain is cured of the resident small plasmid pUH24 [20], it can harbor the pUH24-based expression vector pSE1 and its derivatives, which were also constructed previously in this laboratory (Table 1). The pRND1 plasmid is a derivative of pSE1 used for overexpression of the FFA exporter genes rndA1B1 under the control of the Ptrc promoter [15]. The FFA-producing strains dAS1T and dAS2T were constructed from the wild-type SPc strain and an ∆nrtABCD mutant NA3, respectively [15, 19]. The construction of the mutant involved inactivation of the endogenous acyl-acyl carrier protein (acyl-ACP) synthetase gene (aas) and introduction of a foreign thioesterase gene (‘tesA) as in the preceding studies [11, 12], but a nitrogen-responsive promoter PnirA was used to achieve maximal expression of the ‘tesA gene under nitrate-limited growth conditions [21]. A graphic abstract illustrating the effects of the gene modifications on the FFA biosynthesis pathway is shown in Figure S1. The cyanobacterial cells were grown photoautotrophically at 30˚C, using nitrate or ammonium as the sole source of nitrogen. The media were prepared by supplementing the nitrogen-free derivative of the BG11 medium [22] with KNO3 (7.5, 15, 30 or 60 mM) or (NH4)2SO4 (3.75 mM). All the media were buffered with 20 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 8.2). The cells were precultured in the ammonium-containing medium with aeration under low-light conditions (50 µmol photons m−2 s−1) to the linear phase of growth (OD730 = 0.5–1.5) and then inoculated into 50 mL of fresh medium placed in a test tube with a diameter of 30 mm to give an OD730 value of 0.01. The cell suspensions were overlaid with 20 mL of IM. IM was used as the organic solvent to create an aqueous-organic two-phase culture system because it has no positive or negative effect on the growth of WT S.7942 [17]. The cultures were incubated under high-light conditions (180 µmol photons m−2 s−1) with aeration with air supplemented with 2% (v/v) of CO2 as described previously [17].

Analytical methods

Optical density of the cyanobacterial cultures was measured at 730 nm using a Shimadzu UV1700 spectrophotometer. Dry weight of the dAS1T cells in liquid cultures was estimated from the values of OD730 of the cultures, using the linear relationship between the OD730 values and the measured DCW values, as was previously determined for the OD730 range of 2.0 to 13.8 (y = 0.255x − 0.044, r2 = 0.942) [17]. Because the OD730 values of the dAS2T and dAS2T/pRND1 cultures were lower than 2.0 in the early stage of cultivation, DCW in a unit volume of culture was measured as described in Kato et al. [17] for the OD730 range of 0.41 to 5.8 and the data obtained from 17 biological replicates of dAS2T and dAS2T/pRND1 cultures were fitted to a linear relationship (y = 0.218x + 0.014, r2 = 0.979, Figure S2), which was used for estimation of DCW in the dAS2T and dAS2T/pRND1 cultures. Total protein was determined by a colorimetric assay (Bio-Rad Protein Assay, Bio-Rad), using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a standard.

FFA determination

The concentration of FFA in the aqueous layer in the two-phase cultivation system was enzymatically determined using the Free Fatty Acid Quantification Kit (BioVision) as described previously [19]. FFA in the organic (IM) layer was determined also by the enzymatic method, but the samples were diluted 50-time with the Assay Buffer in the kit to avoid artificial coloration of the reaction mixtures by IM as described previously [17]. FFA titer was the total amount of the extracellular FFA, which was normalized to the culture volume and expressed in g L−1. FFA yield was obtained by dividing this value by the amount of DCW per L and expressed in g g−1DCW. The term FFA productivity was defined as the average FFA release rate during the experiment and expressed in mg L−1 h−1.

Immunoblotting analysis of the ‘TesA protein

A rabbit polyclonal antiserum against a synthetic peptide corresponding to the C-terminal portion of the E. coli ‘TesA protein (PFIADWMAKQLQPLVNHDS) was produced by Sigma-Aldrich. For extraction of the soluble proteins from the FFA-producing cyanobacterial mutants, cells were collected from 30 mL of the cultures by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 500 µL of the extraction buffer (10 mM HEPES–KOH;pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2). The cell suspension was mixed with 100 mg of glass beads in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and the cells were disrupted by bead beating for an hour at 4 °C using a Bug Crasher (TAITEC). The resultant homogenate was centrifuged at 17900g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove the cell debris. Proteins in the extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE on a 15% gel in a buffer system of Laemmli [23] and then electro-transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Immobilon-P, Merck Millipore). After blocking with 1% BSA in TBS (50 mM Tris–HCl; pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl), the membrane was incubated overnight with a 1:1000 dilution of the antiserum against ‘TesA in TBS at room temperature. Goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (Bio-Rad) was used as the second antibody to detect the anti- ‘TesA IgG. Specific protein bands were visualized using the ECL Western blotting analysis system (GE Healthcare), using a luminescent image analyzer (LAS-3000mini, Fujifilm).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with KaleidaGraph (Synergy software) for Tukey’s HSD test.

Results

Nitrogen-limited growth of an FFA-producing strain can be sustained by removal of the product from the medium

The FFA-producing strain dAS2T is deficient in the ABC-type nitrate transporter (Fig. S1). Cyanobacterial mutants defective in active uptake of nitrate grow slowly in the presence of high concentrations of nitrate, relying on passive entrance of nitrate into the cells [24]. This makes it possible to achieve nitrate-limited growth in batch cultures (i.e., without using chemostat cultures) [25, 26]. The dAS2T cells grew well with ammonium, which is readily assimilated to help growth of the mutant and represses ‘tesA expression (see below), thereby minimizing FFA production in the cell (Fig. 1, −IM). However, the cells grew only slowly with 60 mM nitrate (Fig. 1, −IM). The growth of dAS2T in nitrate-containing media was improved by overlaying the cultures with IM (Fig. 1, + IM), which effectively extracts FFA from the growth medium and promotes FFA excretion out of the cell by maintaining a concentration gradient [17]. Without IM overlay, dAS2T cells are unable to grow with 15 mM of nitrate due to toxicity of intracellularly accumulated FFAs [15], but with IM overlay, nitrate-limited growth of dAS2T was achieved in a range of extracellular nitrate concentrations from 60 mM down to 7.5 mM (Fig. 1). At lower nitrate concentrations, the mutant cells exhibited reduced growth rates and a more yellowish pigmentation (Fig. 1).

Nitrate-limited growth enhances expression of thioesterase from the nirA promoter

The PnirA promoter, which was used to express ‘TesA thioesterase to generate FFAs from acyl-ACP (Fig. S1), is repressed in the presence of ammonium in the medium [27]. Ammonium-responsive repression of the PnirA- ‘tesA fusion was previously confirmed in dAS1T by semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis [19]. To analyze the expression level of the thioesterase protein encoded by ‘tesA, an antiserum against the protein was raised in a rabbit and used for immunoblotting analysis of the soluble protein fraction obtained from WT and dAS1T (Fig. 2A). When the extracts from ammonium- and nitrate-grown cells were compared, a protein band of an apparent molecular mass of 20.6 kDa was stained specifically in the sample from nitrate-grown dAS1T cells (Fig. 2A, the rightmost lane). The 20.6-kDa protein was therefore identified as ‘TesA.

When nitrate is used as the nitrogen source, the PnirA promoter is more active in nitrate-limited cells than in nitrate-sufficient cells [21, 26, 28]. In agreement with the property of the promoter, the content of ‘TesA was higher in dAS2T cells than in dAS1T cells (Fig. 2B). Also, the ‘TesA content was increased in the dAS2T cells as the nitrate concentration in the growth medium was decreased (Fig. 2B). The use of dAS2T thus enabled limitation of cell growth and enhancement of ‘TesA expression at the same time.

FFA production as well as cell growth is reduced by nitrate limitation in dAS2T

Growth and FFA production profiles of the nitrate-limited dAS2T cells were determined (Fig. 3Ab) and compared with those of the nitrate-sufficient dAS1T cells (Fig. 3Aa). As reported previously [17], dAS1T cells grew well in a medium containing 15 mM of nitrate in the presence of IM overlay (Fig. 3Aa). The FFA titer at t = 240 h was 0.38 g L−1 of culture (Fig. 3C), giving an FFA productivity of 1.6 mg L−1 h−1 and an FFA yield of 0.18 ± 0.01 g g−1 DCW (Fig. 3C). These figures were similar to those obtained previously (i.e., 1.8 mg L−1 h−1 and 0.22 ± 0.01 g g−1 DCW, respectively) in 240 h of cultivation [17]. As expected, growth of dAS2T, which is defective in the active nitrate uptake mechanism, was slower than that of dAS1T, despite the high nitrate concentration (30 mM) in the medium (Fig. 3Ab). The DCW obtained during the 240 h of cultivation was 0.65 g L−1, which corresponded to only 29% of that obtained in the dAS1T culture, 2.2 g L−1 (Fig. 3C). The FFA titer was 0.18 g L−1 at t = 240 (Fig. 3Ab), giving an FFA productivity of 0.75 mg L−1 h−1 and an FFA yield of 0.28 ± 0.08 g g−1 DCW (Fig. 3C). Thus, nitrate-limited growth of the FFA-producing strain decreased the volumetric productivity of FFA, although the FFA yield was higher in dAS2T than in dAS1T (Fig. 3Ba and 3Bb, triangles).

Expression of an FFA efflux pump enhances FFA production under the conditions of growth limitation

To enhance FFA excretion during growth under the nitrogen-limited conditions, the pRND1 plasmid, which encodes the periplasmic and cytoplasmic membrane components of an RND-type FFA efflux pump of S.7942 [15], was introduced into dAS2T to construct the dAS2T/pRND1 strain (Table 1). The dAS2T/pRND1 cells excreted more than twice as much FFA as dAS2T (Fig. 3Ab and 3Ac, red circles). The amount of excreted FFA was equal to or even slightly higher than that excreted by the dAS1T cells (Fig. 3Aa), giving an FFA productivity of 1.8 ± 0.03 mg L−1 h−1 during the 240 h of cultivation (Fig. 3C). Although dAS2T/pRND1 grew faster than dAS2T during the initial stage of cultivation (t < 100 h), the final cell density was essentially the same as that of dAS2T (Figs.3 Ab and 3Ac, white circles). The FFA yield of dAS2T/pRND1 cells was 0.61 ± 0.03 g g−1 DCW after 240 h of cultivation (Fig. 3Bc and 3C), being three times and twice higher than those obtained with dAS1T and dAS2T, respectively (Fig. 3C).

Partitioning of biomass between FFA and cell can be modulated by altering the degree of growth limitation

Figure 4 shows the effects of nitrate concentration on growth and FFA production of the dAS2T/pRND1 cells. The results obtained with 30 mM of nitrate (Fig. 4Aa and 4Ba) were essentially the same as those obtained under the same conditions in a separate experiment (Fig. 3Ac and 3Bc). With 15 mM of nitrate in the medium, the dAS2T/pRND1 cells grew slower than with 30 mM of nitrate and ceased growing at around t = 144 h (Fig. 4Ab). Excretion of FFA, however, proceeded as in the cultures grown with 30 mM of nitrate, attaining an FFA productivity of 1.8 ± 0.12 mg L−1 h−1 during the 240 h of cultivation (Fig. 4C). The FFA yield was as high as 0.90 ± 0.07 g g−1 DCW at t = 240 h (Fig. 4Bb and C).

When the cells were grown with 7.5 mM of nitrate, both the rates of cell growth and FFA excretion were greatly reduced compared to those in the other conditions (Fig. 4Ac). Up to 192 h post-inoculation, the FFA yield was equivalent to that obtained at a nitrate concentration of 15 mM in the medium, but it subsequently declined sharply due to a rapid increase in DCW (Fig. 4Bc), which presumably indicated proliferation of FFA non-producing mutants that had arisen from natural mutation. Thus, the dAS2T/pRND1 cells grown in the medium containing 15 mM of nitrate achieved the highest FFA yield reported to date for the FFA-producing cyanobacterial mutants (Table 2), with the FFA productivity being the second highest level, following that achieved in the fast-growing cyanobacterium Synechococcus sp. PCC 11901 (S.11901, Table 2). The productivity achieved in S.11901 is by far the highest among the reported results (7.3 mg L−1 h−1), but the FFA yield is low (0.11 g g−1 DCW). This suggested that the robust growth of the cells, which results in the accumulation of large amounts of cell biomass, does not contribute to increasing the per-cell production of FFA.

Discussion

In the production of useful substances using photosynthetic microorganisms, productivity and titer are often used as the criteria for evaluation. These are effective for producing small quantities of fine chemicals, but for the mass production of commodity chemicals such as biofuels, the yield per cell is of critical importance. In this study, we aimed at increasing the per-cell yield of the cyanobacteria-based FFA production system by suppressing cell proliferation and thereby increasing the allocation of photosynthetically fixed carbon to the products. To this end, cells were grown under nitrogen limitation, which refers to a state where trace amounts of combined nitrogen is continuously supplied. It is important to note that nitrogen limitation differs from nitrogen deprivation. Under the conditions of nitrogen deprivation, cyanobacteria degrade photosynthetic pigments, cease photosynthesis and growth, and enter a dormant state [29]. However, under nitrogen limitation, although photosynthetic pigment levels decrease, cells continue to proliferate slowly ([24, 26]. Fig. 1). Cyanobacteria possess a high-affinity nitrate transporter, enabling them to efficiently take up nitrate even from media containing low concentrations of nitrate (< 100 µM) [21, 24, 25], thereby maintaining nitrogen sufficiency. Consequently, they immediately enter a nitrogen-deprived state once nitrate in the medium is depleted, making it difficult to achieve nitrogen-limited conditions. We created the nitrogen-limited conditions by culturing the ∆nrtABCD strains deficient in the nitrate transporter in media containing high concentrations of nitrate, i.e., forcing them to grow on slow, passive diffusion of nitrate into the cells (Fig. S1). Although physiological information on such cells is limited, metabolic flux analysis of the ∆nrtABCD strain of Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 (S.6803) indicates that carbon flux across all metabolic pathways, including the Calvin cycle, is reduced to one third of that under nitrogen-sufficient conditions [30]. The substantial reduction in FFA as well as in DCW in nitrate-limited dAS2T cells compared to nitrogen-sufficient dAS1T cells (Figs. 3, 5A) is thought to result from this metabolic suppression. It is noteworthy that under the same culture conditions, dAS2T/pRND1 maintained its DCW while releasing more FFAs (Figs. 3, 5A). Although further investigation is needed, it is conceivable that the feedback control imposed on the carbon assimilation pathway due to growth limitation was relaxed by the increased release of FFAs. Even when the external nitrate concentration was reduced from 30 to 15 mM to further decrease the growth of dAS2T/pRND1, the amount of FFA released showed little decrease (Figs. 4, 5B). These results represent the first successful decoupling of cell proliferation and material production by enhancing FFA excretion. A yield of 0.90 g g−1 DCW and a productivity of 1.8 mg L−1 h−1 thus attained for the extracellular FFA (Figs. 4Bb and 4C, Table 2) are comparable to those of intracellular lipids in various microalgae (Table 5 in Ref. [18]). The results indicate the importance of uncoupling of FFA production from cell proliferation for improvement of the FFA yield (Fig. 5), providing a technical basis for further increasing the FFA yield of genetically engineered cyanobacterial cells.

It is important to estimate the EROI of biofuels produced using the FFAs excreted by the dAS2T/pRND1 cells in open ponds under outdoor conditions. Cultivation examples of unicellular cyanobacteria in open ponds are scarce, and no directly usable data exists. However, most cyanobacteria and microalgae share the common characteristics of being mostly unicellular, preferring relatively low light conditions (< 100 µmol photons m−2 s−1), and growing under mild conditions at around 25°C. Furthermore, both have a cellular C/N ratio of around 5–7 [31, 32], meaning they have a much higher N content than plants, and both share the quantitative and economic importance of nitrogen in the culture medium, so the costs of the culture equipment and cultivation are thought to be similar. Therefore, we attempt to estimate the EROI value here, based on the results regarding biodiesel production using nitrogen-starved Chlorella cells [3]. According to the estimation by Lardon et al. [3], production of 1 MJ of biodiesel from the Chlorella cells requires 2.32 MJ of energy input. Since a large portion of the energy input is used to dry the cells prior to TAG extraction, omitting this step reduces the energy input to 1.66 MJ per 1 MJ of biodiesel. However, due to inefficient extraction of TAG from wet cells, 1.23 MJ of TAG remains in the oil cake [3]. It is important to note, however, that the total amount of energy in extracted and unextracted TAG is 1.3 times that of the energy input (2.23 MJ vs. 1.66 MJ). Thus, if a low-cost solvent capable of completely extracting TAG from wet cells were available, the EROI of the TAG could increase to ~ 1.3. Alternatively, if the algal cells excrete all their TAG into the external medium, and an efficient method is developed to collect the TAG, the EROI will be approximately 1.3. Although the excretion of TAG by algal cells is unlikely, the present results show that FFAs are efficiently excreted by cyanobacterial cells. The high levels of FFA productivity and yield achieved in dAS2T/pRND1 (Table 2) are similar to those attained in TAG production by eukaryotic algae [18]. This allows rough estimation of EROI of the FFA-based biodiesel to be 1, or even higher, based on the estimated energy balance of TAG-based biodiesel production [3]. It should be noted, however, that extraction of FFAs from liquid cultures using IM overlay is costly. Development of other efficient methods to harvest the FFAs is essential to achieve the EROI value of 1 or higher, by fully exploiting the potential of the dAS2T/pRND1 mutant.

An EROI value of 1 is too low for FFA-based biodiesel to be a useful biofuel source. Additionally, an FFA yield of 0.9 g g−1 DCW is insufficient for the sustainable, large-scale biofuel production. However, it is important to note that the EROI of FFA production can be increased by raising the FFA yield and productivity. For example, increasing the productivity of N-limited cells from 1.8 mg L⁻1 h⁻1 (as achieved in this study) to 7.3 mg L⁻1 h⁻1 (as achieved by the ∆fadD::tesA mutant of S.11901, as shown in Table 2), all while maintaining slow growth, would result in an FFA yield of 3.7 g g⁻1 DCW. An EROI value of 4 or higher would then be expected. To achieve this, biomass production by the cells would need to increase by about 2.5 times, i.e. (3.7 + 1) divided by (0.9 + 1). It has been demonstrated that Synechococcus elongatus UTEX 2973 (S.2973), a fast-growing cyanobacterium closely related to S.7942, exhibits photosynthetic activity twice that of the S.7942 when illuminated with high-intensity light (> 800 µmol photons m−2 s−1) [33]. Therefore, it is possible to increase the photosynthetic activity of the FFA-producing S.7942 mutants through gene engineering to meet the demand for increased CO2 fixation.

Although it is theoretically possible to develop strains with high FFA productivity and yield, practical approaches are needed to achieve this. A major problem with FFAs is that their intracellular accumulation is toxic [12, 15, 17, 19]. Therefore, it is necessary to further increase the cellular FFA excretion ability so that FFAs do not accumulate inside cells even at high production levels. Second, a more general issue to consider is that regardless of whether a substance is cytotoxic or not, its production and excretion outside the cell inevitably reduce the cell proliferation rate. Consequently, when strains with reduced productivity due to mutations appear, these strains proliferate preferentially, severely impairing productivity ([34, 35], see also Fig. 4Bc). Since mutations are unavoidable, conditions must be established that promote the survival of high-productivity strains over low-productivity strains. For cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae, strong light conditions of normally non-permissive intensity may provide such conditions. Photosynthetic organisms are subject to photoinhibition when exposed to strong light. However, the sensitivity to this varies greatly depending on the species. In the case of the closely related cyanobacterial strains S.7942 and S.2973, the former exhibits growth inhibition and is unable to grow under 800 µmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1 of light irradiation, while the latter demonstrates high photosynthetic activity and proliferates rapidly even under 1,500 µmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1 of light irradiation [36]. S.2973 has been shown to have a high capacity for the conversion of light energy to chemical energy, which is consumed in rapid proliferation, preventing excess energy to accumulate in the cell to cause photoinhibition [33, 36]. Although slower growth is inevitable for cells that produce useful substances, it may be possible to make cells more tolerant to high-intensity light by increasing their energy consumption in the production and excretion of the substances [37]. Achieving this will enable high productivity, tolerance to sunlight, and stable cultivation simultaneously. The technical challenges that need to be addressed are as follows: (1) increasing the photosynthetic capacity of the cells to the level of the rapid-growing cyanobacterial strains, (2) further increasing the flux of carbon to FFA synthesis rather than cell proliferation through metabolic engineering, (3) further promoting the excretion of FFAs outside the cell, and (4) coordinating these three approaches to enhance tolerance to high-intensity light outdoors (Fig. 5B). These challenges are not easy to achieve, but they can be addressed experimentally. Overall, excretion of the product into the extracellular space represents a clear advantage of the cyanobacterial FFA production system over the TAG production system using microalgae. The present results establish a reliable experimental foundation for biofuel production using a milking strategy with photosynthetic microorganisms.

Conclusion

Growth limitation is necessary to increase extracellular FFA yield per cell in engineered cyanobacterial mutants. However, this leads to reduced cell growth and FFA titer, resulting in decreased volumetric FFA productivity. Enhancing FFA export enables high per-cell yields without reducing volumetric productivity, even under growth-limited conditions. Further strengthening FFA export and production capacity would allow cells to convert more solar energy into the chemical energy of FFAs.
