Tubeless Insulin Pump Combined with a Cloud-Based Data Management Platform: Real-World Evidence from 14,765 French People with Diabetes
Authors: Nathalie Jeandidier, Arthur Bagel, Alice Bonin, Gabriel Guigand, Bruno Guerci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aimed to examine glucose metrics and insulin delivery patterns in children, adolescents, and adults with type 1 (T1D) or 2 (T2D) diabetes in France using the tubeless Omnipod DASH® pump with and a continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) sensor connected to myDiabby Healthcare® Data Management Platform (DMP).MethodsTime-stamped CGM and insulin data were extracted from the DMP on December 6, 2023 for 17,344 users whose first data point from the tubeless pump occurred after January 1, 2020. The study population included users with sufficient pump and CGM data (≥ 90 days of use) and ≥ 15.5% of CGM use days reaching > 70% coverage. Analyses were performed by type of diabetes and age group.ResultsAmong 14,757 users included in this analysis, most reported having T1D (93.7%), the median age was 33 years (Q1–Q3, 16–51), and the median duration of pump use was 545 days for people with T1D and 505 days for people with T2D (1.49 and 1.38 years, respectively). People with T1D spent a median of 52.5% (Q1–Q3, 43.4–62.5) of time in range (70–180 mg/dL, TIR) and a TIR ≥ 70% was attained by 12.6% of users. The median time below range (TBR, < 70 mg/dL) was 3.7% (Q1–Q3, 2.1–6.1). For users with T2D, median TIR was 66.9% (Q1–Q3, 54.0–77.8), with 42.8% of users achieving a TIR ≥ 70%. Over 90% of all users consumed less than 60 UI/day.ConclusionThis robust and scalable analysis of a database of substantial quantity, density, and quality found that tubeless pump users achieved moderate glycemic outcomes overall with favorablesafety outcomes in particular, and used the pump consistently. Such databases could be useful for research and patient care, and further work will show how best to use them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01814-8.

Introduction

Over the past decade, diabetes management has advanced considerably, tending to move away from multiple daily injections (MDI), with the widespread adoption of continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) and, more recently, automated insulin delivery (AID) systems [1–5]. These integrated technologies—which combine insulin pumps with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and algorithm-based insulin adjustments—have been shown to improve glycemic control, reduce time spent in hypo- and hyperglycemia, and enhance quality of life for people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and insulin-requiring type 2 diabetes (T2D) [5–8]. As a result, AID systems are increasingly recommended as the most effective form of insulin delivery for people requiring intensive insulin therapy [9]. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of individuals still rely on MDI or standard (non-automated) insulin pumps. In many countries, the combination of external insulin pumps with CGM-based self-monitoring of interstitial glucose has become the standard of care [1–3, 9, 10].

Between 2018 and 2023, the number of individuals using insulin pumps in France rose from approximately 76,000 to 90,000 [11–13]. Among them, tubeless pumps represent a growing segment of interest. In a recent French survey, users of tubeless pumps and hybrid closed-loop systems reported the highest levels of treatment satisfaction when compared to traditional pumps or MDI [14]. However, real-world data describing the use of tubeless pumps—particularly in France—remain otherwise limited.

The discrepancy between performance and adoption tendencies reflects the existence of both system-level and individual-level barriers, including limited eligibility for reimbursement, as well as patient concerns about loss of control, increased oversight of daily behaviors, and discomfort with automation [15, 16]. For new technologies to have the greatest impact, people with diabetes must not only have access to them but also actively engage with their use. Sustained benefit depends on effective integration into daily routines and ongoing collaboration with healthcare professionals (HCPs). One key enabler of this process is the use of cloud-based data management platforms [17, 18]. These platforms play a complementary role in modern diabetes care by compiling time-stamped insulin delivery and glucose data from connected devices and generating standardized summaries. They support remote interactions between patients and HCPs, enabling early therapeutic adjustments and shared decision-making [17, 19–22]. In France, remote monitoring for people with insulin-treated diabetes was recognized in 2023 as a reimbursable medical service for people with T1D and T2D whose glycemic management is insufficient despite a well-managed intensified insulin therapy, and subsequently expanded to include insulin-treated gestational diabetes [23, 24]. According to 2023 estimates, 1,196,500 people with diabetes—including 71% with insulin-dependent T2D, 27% with T1D, and 2% with insulin-treated gestational diabetes—could benefit from a reimbursed remote monitoring service in France [23]. For researchers, these platforms also offer high-quality, device-generated data that reflect real-world use patterns and treatment behaviors—data that are often difficult to obtain in conventional clinical trials [25]. Such data could facilitate a deeper understanding of the use of insulin pumps and/or CGM devices under real-world conditions [26–30]. In France, however, such platforms remain underused for research purposes.

This study was designed to address the need for additional real-world data on tubeless insulin pump use in France using the currently underused data management platforms. To this end, we developed a methodology for the preparation and analysis of data from a data management platform and applied it to describe the use of a tubeless pump. Specifically, we aimed to investigate routinely assessed glucose metrics and insulin delivery patterns in a large database of children, teenagers, and adults living in France with T1D or T2D using the most common combination of tubeless insulin pump and data management platform (Omnipod DASH® and myDiabby Healthcare®) with a CGM sensor.

Methods

Study Design

This study was a national, retrospective, cross-sectional analysis of children, adolescents, and adults living with T1D and T2D in France who used a CGM sensor (all CGM sensors were included) in conjunction with a tubeless insulin pump (the Omnipod DASH, which was the most frequently used such pump at the time of extraction) and who uploaded their devices’ data to myDiabby Healthcare, an extensive database management platform for connected medical devices related to diabetes [11, 27].

Collected Data

The data were extracted from the database management platform on December 6, 2023. Users were included in the extraction if they had at least one data point uploaded from the studied IDS to the platform and have consented to the reuse of their data (non-opposition statement, as required by French regulations; see the Ethics section). The extraction encompassed all data uploaded by eligible users to the database management platform from the initial to the final day of platform use or until the extraction date.

Extracted data included age at the extraction date, gender, and type of diabetes, as well as time-stamped interstitial glucose levels from users’ CGM systems and time-stamped insulin infusion data from their IDS (quantity of insulin administered and the type of administration [basal perfusion or bolus]). Age, gender, and type of diabetes were self-reported by users (or by their parents, for minors) when they first started using the platform. CGM and IDS data were imported by users from the connected medical devices, either manually or automatically, depending on the device.

Study Population

Eligibility criteria were designed to ensure the availability of sufficient data to meet the study objectives, while maintaining the representativeness of real-world conditions and including the widest possible population.

People meeting the following criteria were included in the “included population”: self-reported T1D or T2D, age of 2 to 100 years, initial IDS data point registered after January 1, 2020 (i.e., the year the Omnipod DASH first became available in France), and no obvious outliers for insulin or CGM data. Furthermore, users were required to have a minimum of 90 days of IDS use documented in the myDiabby Healthcare database. The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Study flowchart. Outcomes relating to pump data were calculated over the period between the initial and the final insulin data points from the studied pump. A day of IDS use was defined as a day with at least one data point from the pump uploaded to myDiabby Healthcare.

Outcomes relating to CGM data were determined over the period between the first and the last days of CGM use within the IDS analysis period. A day of CGM use was defined as a day with at least 70% coverage of CGM data (i.e., 68 15-min readings per day out of a possible 96). This cutoff was chosen on the basis of available recommendations for the required density of CGM data for the assessment of glycemic outcomes [31]. Additionally, to enable a reliable analysis of the AGP profile over periods longer than 3 months, we defined a minimum data quality threshold for each observed period: at least 15.5% of days with CGM use with a minimum of 70% of data captured per day. This threshold aims to maximize data collection while providing a comprehensive and robust view of long-term glycemic profiles. The 15.5% ratio is supported by the literature, which demonstrated that glycemic parameters calculated from 14 days of CGM use accurately reflect the preceding 90-day period [32]. This data density cutoff has also been previously used (Personal communication; Keuthage W, 2021 EASD Virtual Meeting). Days of use did not need to be consecutive, allowing for flexibility in real-world CGM usage.

Outcome Assessment

Users’ glycemic profiles were calculated from all CGM data captured during the CGM analysis period (Fig. 2). The glycemic profile was determined using the Iglu R package [33, 34] and included the following metrics: time in range (TIR) (70–180 mg/dL), time below range (TBR) (< 70 mg/dL), time above range (TAR) (> 180 mg/dL), and glucose management indicator (GMI) [34]. Tubeless pump use was calculated for each day over the entire IDS analysis period (Fig. 2). Outcomes included daily basal insulin, daily bolus insulin, total daily insulin, as well as the number and frequency of boluses and the quantity of insulin administered per bolus.

Statistical Methods

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3) and R studio (Version 2023.09.1.494). All parameters were calculated for each person and summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables or proportions for categorical variables. Results are reported by type of diabetes and by age group. P values with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 were considered significant.

Ethics

This study was not subject to approval from a regulatory ethics committee, in accordance with article R1121-1 of the French Public Health Code. All procedures in this study complied with the laws and regulations regarding the management of personal information applicable in France, as well as the European General Data Protection Regulation. This study was conducted in accordance with the French reference methodology MR-004 (Deliberation No. 2018-155 of May 3, 2018). Each participant consented to the reuse of their data uploaded to myDiabby Healthcare for research purposes and was provided with an information note and non-opposition form before the extraction. Participants had 14 days to oppose the processing of their data, once they received the information note. The study was registered under number F20231031093949 in the Health Data Hub French project directory.

Results

Study Population

The extracted population comprised 17,344 people, of whom 15,643 (90.2%) were included in the study (Fig. 1). A total of 14,757 met the criteria for analysis (analyzed population), representing 85.1% of the extracted population. Among them, the majority (n = 13,827, 93.7%) reported having T1D, while 930 (6.3%) reported having T2D.

The demographic characteristics of the analyzed population are presented in Table 1. Women accounted for 54.5% of users (n = 6993). The median age was 33 years (Q1–Q3, 16–51) and most users (n = 8885, 60.2%) were aged at least 26 years. Young adults (18–25 years) represented 11.5% of users (n = 1700), while children and adolescents (≤ 17 years) comprised 28.3% (n = 4172) of the analyzed population. As people with T1D accounted for almost all analyzed users, their demographics were comparable to the overall study population (Table 1). Women accounted for 43.3% of users with T2D (n = 355), which was lower than for people with T1D (n = 6640, 55.2%). People with T2D were older than those with T1D, with a median age of 62 years (Q1–Q3, 53–69) versus 31 years (Q1–Q3, 16–48) for people with T1D.

The IDS was used by people with T1D for a median duration of 534 days (Q1–Q3, 332–740), accounting for 93.3% (Q1–Q3, 78.3–99.7) of the entire analysis period of the pump (Table 2). Similarly, CGM sensors were worn for a median duration of 498 days (Q1–Q3, 301–708), accounting for 83.4% (Q1–Q3, 63.1–95.8) of the analysis period of the CGM system. The durations of use of both the pump and CGM sensors were quite similar across the three age groups, although a slight difference was observed in CGM use during the analysis period between users aged 18–25 years (74.7%) and those ≥ 26 years (86.7%) (Table 2). For people with T2D, the IDS was used for a median duration of 485 days (Q1–Q3, 300–686) and the CGM sensors were worn for a median duration of 440 days (Q1–Q3, 270–651), with usage rates of 92.9% (Q1–Q3, 77.6–99.6) and 80.1% (Q1–Q3, 62.1–93.0) within their respective analysis periods.

CGM analysis periods achieve a high coverage rate (percentage of days with ≥ 70% of data captured). More importantly, this coverage remains stable, regardless of the total duration of the analysis period: 84–91% of users have more than 50% coverage, while 58–74% exceed 75% (Fig. S1). A similar pattern is observed even more strongly for the use of the Omnipod DASH system. Coverage remains extremely stable and high, regardless of the length of the analysis period: 95–99% of users have more than 50% coverage, and 74–92% exceed 75% (Fig. S2). Overall, data coverage and quality are sufficient to provide an accurate and representative picture of systems use.

Overall, people with T1D spent 52.5% (Q1–Q3, 43.4–62.5) of their time within the target range. The median TIR for pediatric users and young adults was 48.3% (Q1–Q3, 40.9–56.9) and 47.5% (Q1–Q3, 38.4–58.0), respectively, while it reached 55.9% (Q1–Q3, 47.0–65.3) for adults aged ≥ 26 years. A TIR ≥ 70% was achieved by 12.6% of users with T1D (n = 1739), with the highest proportion observed for the subgroup of adults aged ≥ 26 years (n = 1283, 16.1%). People with T1D spent 42.7% (Q1–Q3, 32.4–52.2) in hyperglycemia (> 180 mg/dL, TAR). The median TAR was lower in adults aged ≥ 26 years (39.3%; Q1–Q3, 29.4–49.2) than in young adults and children (47.8%; Q1–Q3, 36.8–57.5 and 46.6%; Q1–Q3, 37.7–54.4, respectively). The median time spent in hypoglycemia (< 70 mg/dL, TBR) was 3.7% (Q1–Q3, 2.1–6.1), with users aged ≤ 17 years showing the highest value (4.0%; Q1–Q3, 2.4–6.3) (Supplemental data, Table S1).

For people with T2D, the median TIR reached 66.9% (Q1–Q3, 54.0–77.8), with 42.8% of users (n = 398) achieving a TIR ≥ 70%. The median TAR was 30.9% (Q1–Q3, 20.4–43.3) and TBR reached 1.5% (Q1–Q3, 0.6–3.2) (Supplemental data, Table S1).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the largest real-world analysis to date of tubeless pump users in France (14,757 people): by using the MyDiabby Healthcare platform as data source, approximately half of the total Omnipod DASH users over the period 2020–2023 in France were analyzed. Data successfully captured use patterns under real-world conditions, showing devices were used appropriately within the scope of clinical recommendations. Users achieved levels of TBR and TIR expected of standard pump therapy, revealing persistent use of both the pump and CGM sensors, and confirming the diversity of insulin use patterns and glycemic profiles according to available, self-reported descriptors (patient age and type of diabetes). Overall, the quality of the results and their comparability with available literature confirm the soundness of the proposed methodology and support it as a potential complement to the methods used in clinical trials.

The present study revealed persistent use of both the tubeless pump and CGM sensor over a median duration of use of 470 to 550 days, i.e., 15–16 months, for both devices. Users were highly adherent over this extended period of use, using the devices for 80–90% of the analysis period, regardless of age group and diabetes type, supporting the benefits of tubeless pump therapy for people with T1D or T2D observed in other studies, including improved treatment satisfaction compared to MDI [36], behavioral and psychosocial benefits [37, 38], and reduced interference with daily life compared to tubed pumps and MDI [14]. The persistent use of these devices further suggests that therapeutic education provided by HCP is well understood and integrated by people with diabetes [39, 40].

The device appears well prescribed to the appropriate population and adequately used by this population who achieve generally expected glycemic outcomes, but remain below the recognized standards of glycemic control in terms of TIR, TBR, and TAR. Indeed, users achieved moderate glycemic stability overall, and favorable in terms of time spent in hypoglycemia. Over 50% of users, irrespective of age group or type of diabetes, spent less than 4% of their time below range, as recommended by International Consensus targets [31]. One in eight users with T1D achieved the target of a TIR ≥ 70% with a median TIR among people with T1D of 52.5%, reflecting expected outcomes with standard pump therapy. Results were better among users with T2D, with approximately 43% reaching the recommended target and a median TIR of 66.9%. Results also depended on user age. Among users with T1D, adults aged 26 years and over hit their glycemic most often. This aligns with the longitudinal evolution of glycemic management with age. Adolescents achieve these targets least often and adults aged at least 25 years achieve them the most [41].

The analyzed population predominantly had T1D, consistent with two recently published French surveys [13, 14], and reflecting the greater use of insulin pumps among people with T1D compared with T2D. Indeed, according to 2023 data from the French THIN database, tubeless insulin pumps being used by only 1% of people with insulin-treated T2D [12]. Many reasons may explain this discrepancy, though further research is required to fully understand the barriers to technology use in people with T2D.

As a result of high availability of both pump therapy and data management platforms in France (reimbursed by the National Health Insurance for all eligible patients), selection bias was limited. Additionally, exclusion criteria were designed exclusively to provide the cleanest data possible, with no prior selection, in an attempt to retain as broad a sample as possible. Indeed, after removal of all the patients who had erroneous data according to initial checks, only 5.6% of the population did not meet our minimum use criteria. As such, the demographic characteristics of our T1D population (55% of women and nearly 30% of children) were consistent with those of another large French cohort of 16,505 people equipped with insulin pumps and/or CGM systems [42]. The greater proportion of women in our study and other French cohorts of people with T1D treated with insulin pumps [13, 14, 43, 44] contrasts with the majority of men (57%) among the overall T1D population observed in the ENTRED study based on health insurance data [45]. This discrepancy could be explained by the previously reported greater use of insulin pumps by girls and women than by boys and men [46, 47]. Finally, although data on insulin pump use in the T2D population are scarce, the demographic characteristics of our T2D cohort appear similar to those of the French PRODIGE cohort in terms of age [32], as well as to those of patients from the OpT2mise randomized trial [48].

The glycemic parameters of the people included in this analysis also align with other real-world data. A cross-sectional study of 3184 people performed at the Steno Diabetes Center in Copenhagen, Denmark found similar results: people with T1D using insulin pumps with unintegrated CGM achieved a median TIR of 54.9%, with a median TBR of 3.2% [49]. Regarding the Omnipod DASH, specifically, favorable management of the risk of hypoglycemia was described in two retrospective North American observational studies involving adults and children with T1D and adults with T2D after 3 months of use of the tubeless insulin pump [50, 51]. In a British retrospective observational study, 276 people with T1D who used the system over a median period of 3 years achieved a mean TIR of 59.6 ± 18.7%, a value consistent with our findings [52]. TIR observed in pediatric users with T1D was also in line with that reported in a Finnish registry study of children treated with conventional insulin pump therapy and wearing CGM [53], both falling short of the 70% target recommended by the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes [54]. Nevertheless, glycemic stability remains a clinical challenge as, despite reported improvements in maintained glycemic stability throughout the USA and Europe, people with T1D or T2D still struggle to achieve their glycemic goals, regardless of the therapy used [41, 47, 55, 56].

The analysis of CGM data was conducted over an extended period (approximately 15 months), naturally including phases of intermittent device use (vacations, travel, summer periods). Unlike the standard approach, which typically relies on analyzing 14 days as representative of a 90-day period, we adapted the methodology to include the entire monitoring period, provided that the user had sufficient high-quality data, in order to obtain a comprehensive and representative view of long-term glycemic profiles. Our approach maximizes the amount of available data while ensuring that the analyzed profiles accurately reflect patients’ real-life glucose patterns. Accordingly, the selected data quality criteria strike an optimal balance between representativeness and feasibility. This methodological approach strengthens the validity and reliability of the study’s conclusions.

Regarding insulin use, over 90% of the people in the analyzed population used the IDS according to prescription requirements, consuming < 60 UI/day, indicating most patients had relatively low needs [57]. Indeed, the studied T2D population appears to be a specific subgroup with low insulin needs (≤ 60 UI/day) and good glycemic control, indicating the system suits their profile. Though not representative of all T2D users, the results support its effectiveness for this group. The differences in insulin use patterns otherwise aligned with available literature: age is a well-known factor influencing insulin requirements in people with T1D, with needs increasing from infancy to adolescence (due to a transient period of insulin resistance) and decreasing throughout adulthood [58–62]. Interestingly, boluses were more frequent for children, probably linked to regular snacking as well as more frequent supervision and adjustment of glucose levels.

This study presents some notable strengths, including the size of the population (approximately half of all Omnipod DASH users in France between 2020 and 2023), its density (over 4 out of 5 of days contained sufficient CGM coverage [25, 31, 47]), and the reliability of the collected data (all data was uploaded directly from the device to the platform without any intermediary).

Despite these strengths, the study had some inevitable limitations. The biggest was the absence of some important contextual data such as HbA1c levels, confirmation of the IDS and CGM initiation and discontinuation dates, frequency of exercise, detailed demographics, and other lifestyle descriptors which are simply not present in the data management platform which are known to influence glycemic stability [20, 63, 64]. This led to some necessary methodological compromises. As we were unable to determine with certainty the initiation dates of either the IDS or the CGM, it was not possible to establish a reliable, clinically relevant baseline from the data alone. Indeed, on the basis of data alone, it was extremely likely that most users initiated the IDS and/or CGM sensor before using the data management platform. Therefore, as neither a common clinical index date nor the device used prior to the studied IDS could be determined with certainty, the data could not be temporally aligned. This means that longitudinal analysis at the population level could not identify clinically interpretable evolutions of device use or glycemic outcomes. Additionally, some time-varying factors could not be accounted for. For instance, enrollment in structured telemonitoring programs using data management platforms (i.e., involving standard follow-up practices beyond usual follow-up without such a platform) has expanded significantly between 2020 and 2023. Indeed, use of data management platforms really only became standardized from 2023 onwards [23]. Therefore, while the results of this study do not reflect modern telemonitoring practices, they highlight potential avenues for improving diabetes management in France, particularly among pediatric users with T1D. Finally, the study was conducted during the period of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have had an impact on the management of diabetes among patients [65–67].

On the basis of these considerations, it appears necessary, for a more complete and interpretable analysis, to expand this data and combine them with external sources and additional descriptive parameters to allow for reliable temporal alignment, better characterize observed particularities in the data (e.g., gaps in the data or particularly high or low insulin use), confront the trends with clinical decisions (e.g., physician or user-initiated changes to the insulin infusion profile or change in follow-up parameters), and propose more detailed user profiles which may prove useful for decision-making. Data on device use, daily variations in insulin requirements, and glycemic outcomes in the general French population will become essential for future patient selection, as well as for the identification of abnormal behaviors requiring support and education of the patient/caregiver [63, 68, 69]. There is also a growing need for reports on adverse events related to pump use, which could be easily integrated into the items collected by these databases [70].

Conclusions

This study described the real-world use of a tubeless pump (Omnipod DASH) with a CGM sensor, as well as the glycemic outcomes of 14,757 children, adolescents, and adults with T1D or T2D in France, using data from the myDiabby Healthcare platform. Users showed high persistence and adherence, and glycemic parameters were consistent with previous studies, indicating levels of TBR and TIR expected with standard pump therapy. Although the study lacked some contextual data and longitudinal alignment, it provides a robust foundation for future analyses incorporating additional sources for more insightful patient follow-up. These findings highlight the potential of cloud-based data platforms for structured remote monitoring and real-world evidence generation. Such data management platforms offer significant opportunities to improve patient care, support research, and inform post-market surveillance.
