Single-cell and spatial transcriptomes reveal oligodendrocyte remodeling fatty acid metabolism microenvironment of lung cancer brain metastases
Authors: Yuechao Yang, Yang Gao, Zhe Qi, Sen Li, Zhisu Wang, Deheng Li, Lei Chen, Changshuai Zhou, Mingtao Feng, Xin Chen, Bin Hao, Xiaojun Wu, Liangdong Li, Yiqun Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain metastases represent a prevalent and lethal complication in advanced lung cancer, where the brain microenvironment is pivotal in facilitating metastatic tumor progression. Recent findings indicate the presence of oligodendrocyte infiltration within lung cancer brain metastases (LCBM). Lipid metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a critical feature of the brain metastatic niche, influencing both tumor cell survival and immune cell function. This study aimed to determine whether oligodendrocytes undergo lipid metabolic reprogramming in LCBM and whether their derived fatty acids functionally promote tumor proliferation and immune modulation.MethodsSingle cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq), spatial transcriptome sequencing, lipidomics, and lipid tracing techniques to comprehensively analyze metabolic alterations in oligodendrocytes and their dynamic interactions with tumor and immune cells. Western blotting, qPCR and multiple immunofluorescence staining were used to detect the expression of fatty acid synthesis enzymes in oligodendrocytes and immunosuppressive markers in macrophages.ResultsAnalysis of independent scRNA-seq datasets revealed markedly increased lipid synthesis activity in oligodendrocytes within LCBM. Hypoxic conditions and co-culture with metastatic lung cancer cells further enhanced the expression of fatty acid synthases in oligodendrocytes. Spatial transcriptomics and scRNA-seq indicated that tumor cells and macrophages were major recipients of oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids, exhibiting enhanced uptake and degradation. Treatment with oligodendrocyte-conditioned medium significantly increased the expression of fatty acid uptake proteins in tumor cells and macrophages. Furthermore, oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids supported tumor cell proliferation and polarized macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype. Notably, scRNA-seq identified a macrophage subpopulation (Mac_FABP4) as a candidate mediator of lipid-driven immune suppression.ConclusionThese findings delineate the pivotal role of oligodendrocytes in LCBM, revealing their contribution of lipids that enhance tumor proliferation and induce immunosuppression, thereby suggesting that glial lipid metabolism may represent a potential therapeutic target warranting further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08447-w.Keywords: Oligodendrocytes, Tumor microenvironment, Fatty acid metabolism, Macrophage

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with brain metastases representing one of the most devastating complications [1]. Up to 40% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients develop brain metastases, associated with extremely poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options [2]. The unique biological characteristics of brain metastases, the complex interactions between metastatic tumor cells and the brain microenvironment, have become a growing area of interest [3–6]. Comparative next-generation sequencing (NGS) profiling suggests that brain dissemination is driven by mechanisms beyond single-gene alterations [7–9], and the unique microenvironment of the brain poses additional challenges to metastatic tumor cells, making brain metastases even more crucial.

Unlike extracranial sites, the brain presents a highly specialized microenvironment composed of resident glial cells, a restrictive blood-brain barrier (BBB), and distinct metabolic constraints. Emerging evidence suggests that successful colonization and outgrowth of metastatic cancer cells depend not only on their intrinsic properties but also on their ability to adapt to and co-opt the local microenvironment [10, 11]. Recent studies analyzing multi-omics data from paired primary lung cancer and brain metastases have revealed metabolic susceptibility as a novel therapeutic target [12]. Lipid metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a critical feature of brain metastasis. Several landmark studies have established this concept: Ferraro et al. showed that HER2⁺ breast cancer brain metastases overexpress fatty acid synthase (FASN), and its genetic or pharmacological inhibition reduces intracranial tumor growth [13]. Sala et al. demonstrated that p53 inactivation drives breast cancer brain metastasis by upregulating SCD1 and CD36, and that astrocytes supply fatty acids to enhance cancer cell proliferation and brain colonization [14]. Zou et al. revealed that astrocytes are enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids, which activate PPAR signaling in cancer cells to promote brain metastasis [15]. Importantly, these studies focused primarily on breast cancer and melanoma; lung cancer—the most common source of brain metastases—remains underexplored in this context.

Despite these advances in understanding lipid metabolism dynamics, the cellular orchestrators within the brain niche remain poorly defined. While previous research has highlighted the roles of astrocytes [16] and microglia [17] in supporting metastatic tumor growth and immune evasion, the contribution of oligodendrocytes, the myelin-producing glial cells of the central nervous system, remains poorly understood. Recent studies in brain metastases have shown that oligodendrocytes have significant infiltration around the tumor microenvironment and may affect neighboring cells through metabolic remodeling and paracrine signaling [3–5]. However, whether and how oligodendrocytes contribute to the metabolic remodeling of the brain metastatic niche in lung cancer has not been elucidated.

In this study, we focus on the role of oligodendrocytes in orchestrating lipid metabolic reprogramming during lung cancer brain metastases (LCBM). Using scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, in vitro and in vivo models, and clinical sample validation, our findings suggest that oligodendrocytes may serve as metabolic supporters within the brain metastatic microenvironment. Specifically, oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids are taken up by tumor-associated macrophages, promoting their immunosuppressive polarization, and by tumor cells, enhancing their proliferative capacity. Together, these findings identify oligodendrocytes as central regulators of metabolic crosstalk in LCBM and underscore the importance of glial-lipid interactions as a potential therapeutic target for brain metastasis.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

This study employed a LCBM tissue microarray constructed from the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) cohort. Samples were systematically collected from 36 patients treated at FUSCC between June 2017 and October 2023, regardless of age, gender, or disease stage. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection, and the study protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center.

Single-cell dissociation

The single-cell RNA-seq experiment was performed by experimental personnel in the laboratory of NovelBio Co., Ltd. Three cases of lung cancer brain metastasis tissues were removed and kept in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec) until processing. The tissue samples were processed as described below. Briefly, samples were first washed with PBS, minced into small pieces (approximately 1mm3) on ice and enzymatically digested with Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and DNase I 30U/mL for 35 min at 37 °C, samples were sieved through a cell strainer and centrifuged at 100 r/min. After the supernatant was removed, the pelleted cells were suspended in 5 ml red blood cell lysis buffer (Miltenyi Biotec) to lyse red blood cells. PBS containing 0.5% BSA was added to the samples, mix well. After washing, the cell pellets were re-suspended in PBS containing 0.5% BSA. Single cells were then stained for viability assessment.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The scRNA-Seq libraries were generated using the 10X Genomics Chromium Controller Instrument and Chromium Single Cell 3’ V3 Reagent Kits (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). Briefly, cells were concentrated to approximately 1000 cells/µL and loaded into each channel to generate single-cell Gel Bead-In-Emulsions (GEMs). After the RT step, GEMs were broken and barcoded-cDNA was purified and amplified. The amplified barcoded cDNA was fragmented, A-tailed, ligated with adaptors and index PCR amplified. The final libraries were quantified using the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the size distribution of the libraries were determined using a High Sensitivity DNA chip on a Bioanalyzer 2200 (Agilent). All libraries were sequenced by illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) on a 150 bp paired-end run.

Data processing

scRNA-seq data analysis was performed by NovelBio Co.,Ltd. with NovelBrain Cloud Analysis Platform. We applied fastp (v0.19.7) with default parameter filtering the adaptor sequence and removed the low-quality reads to achieve the clean data [18]. Then the feature-barcode matrices were obtained by aligning reads to the human genome (GRCh38) using CellRanger v3.1.0. We applied the down sample analysis among samples sequenced according to the mapped barcoded reads per cell of each sample and finally achieved the aggregated matrix. Cells contained over 200 expressed genes and mitochondria UMI rate below 20% passed the cell quality filtering and mitochondria genes were removed in the expression table.

Seurat package (v3, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was used for cell normalization and regression based on the expression table according to the UMI counts of each sample and percent of mitochondria rate to obtain the scaled data. The fastMNN function from R package scran (v1.6.2) was used to apply mutual nearest neighbor method to correct for batch effect among samples [19]. PCA was constructed based on the scaled data with top 2000 high variable genes and top 10 principals were used for tSNE construction and UMAP construction. Utilizing graph-based cluster method, we acquired the unsupervised cell cluster result based the PCA top 10 principal and we calculated the marker genes by FindAllMarkers function with wilcox rank sum test algorithm under following criteria:1. Log2FC > 0.25; 2. p value < 0.05; 3. min.pct > 0.1. In order to identify the cell type detailed, the clusters of same cell type were selected for re-tSNE analysis, graph-based clustering and marker analysis.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis

To elucidate the biological implications of the DEGs and marker genes, GO analysis was performed [20]. GO annotations were downloaded from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), the Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/) and UniProt (http://www.UniProt.org/). Fisher’s exact test was applied to identify the significant GO categories, and an FDR was used to correct the p values.

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was used to explore the significant pathways of the DEGs and marker genes based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [21]. Fisher’s exact test was used to identify significant pathways, and the threshold of significance was defined by the p value and FDR.

Gene enrichment analysis

For gene enrichment analysis, Fisher’s exact test was applied to calculate the p value for each gene set. Raw p values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis tests using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Such enrichment analysis was applied to annotations including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. To characterize the relative activation of a given gene set, we performed QuSAGE (2.42.0) analysis [22].

SCENIC analysis

To assess the regulatory strength of TFs, we applied the SCENIC (v0.11.2) workflow [23], which is a new computational method for the construction of regulatory networks and identification of different cell states from scRNA-seq data, using the 20-thousand motif database for RcisTarget and GRNboost.

Pseudotime analysis

Single-cell trajectory analysis was performed with Monocle2 (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release) utilizing DDR-Tree and default parameters to determine the dramatic translational relationships among cell types and clusters [24]. Before Monocle analysis, marker genes of the Seurat clustering result and raw expression counts of the cell that passed filtering were selected. Branch expression analysis modeling (BEAM analysis) was utilized for branch fate-determined gene analysis based on pseudotime analysis.

Monocle3

To construct single-cell trajectories based on gene expression changes, Trajectories function of Monocle3 (v1.3.1) was used with the default parameters [25]. The find_gene_modules function was used to group gene populations into modules.

CytoTrace

Cellular Trajectory Reconstruction Analysis using gene Counts and Expression (CytoTRACE, v0.3.3), a computational method, was applied to predict the differentiation state of cells from single-cell RNA-sequencing data through counting the number of expressed genes per cell.

Cell–cell communication analysis

To enable a systematic analysis of cell–cell communication molecules, we applied cell communication analysis based on the CellPhoneDB(v1.1.0) [26], a public repository of ligands, receptors and their interactions. Membrane secreted and peripheral proteins of the cluster was annotated. Significant mean and Cell Communication significance (p value < 0.05) was calculated based on the interaction and the normalized cell matrix achieved by Seurat Normalization.

Differential gene expression analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes among samples, the function FindMarkers with wilcox rank sum test algorithm was used under following criteria:1. Log2FC > 0.25; 2. p value < 0.05; 3. min.pct > 0.1 which was more conducive to obtain different functional genes among samples.

Spatial transcriptomics statistical analysis

We applied fastp with default parameter filtering the adaptor sequence and removed the low-quality reads to achieve the clean data [18]. Then the feature-barcode matrices were obtained by aligning reads to the human genome (GRCh38, ensembl191) using SpaceRanger v1.1.0. In order to minimize the sample batch, we applied the down sample analysis among samples sequenced according to the mapped barcoded reads per spot of each sample and finally achieved the aggregated matrix.

Seurat package (v3, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was used for spot normalization and regression. PCA was constructed based on the scaled data with all high variable genes and top 10 principals were used for UMAP construction. Utilizing graph-based cluster method, we acquired the unsupervised the cell cluster result based the PCA top 10 principal and we calculated the marker genes by FindAllMarkers function with wilcox rank sum test algorithm under following criteria:1. log2FC > 0.25; 2. p value < 0.05; 3. min.pct > 0.1. Spatial feature expression plots were generated with the SpatialFeaturePlot function in Seurat (version 3.1.3) and the STUtility R package (version 1.0.0).

Integration of scRNA-seq and spatial data

Single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics datasets were integrated using the Seurat transfer learning framework. The scRNA-seq dataset was used as the reference and the spatial transcriptomics dataset as the query. After quality control and normalization, highly variable genes shared between the two datasets were identified and used for integration. The reference scRNA-seq data were normalized, scaled, and subjected to principal component analysis, followed by cell type annotation based on established marker genes or clustering results. Transfer anchors between the reference and query datasets were then identified using Seurat’s FindTransferAnchors function, typically with canonical correlation analysis or reciprocal PCA reduction. These anchors represent matched transcriptional states across modalities and were used to transfer cell type labels or cell type probability scores from the scRNA-seq reference to each spatial spot using TransferData. For each spatial location, prediction scores were calculated across reference cell types, and the cell type with the highest prediction score was assigned as the dominant transferred identity.

Animal studies

All animal experiments adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of FUSCC. Male BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Institute of Experimental Animal Science and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. To establish brain metastases, 5 × 10⁵ luciferase-labeled H2030 or PC9 cells in 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were injected into the left ventricle using a 26G needle. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) prior to the procedure. Metastasis formation was confirmed by in vivo bioluminescence imaging using the Xenogen IVIS system (Spectral Instrument Imaging, Lagoon X), following intraperitoneal injection of 150 µL of 1.5% D-luciferin and 1.5% pentobarbital sodium. Mice were monitored and euthanized four weeks post-injection.

For stable knockdown of FASN in oligodendrocytes, MO3.13 cells were transduced with lentiviral particles encoding shRNA targeting FASN (shFASN) or a non-targeting control (shNC) as described (Cell Transfection section). Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blotting and qPCR. Luciferase-labeled H2030-BM cells (5 × 10⁵) were mixed with either control (shNC) or FASN-knockdown (shFASN) MO3.13 oligodendrocytes at a 1:1 ratio (5 × 10⁵ each) in 100 µL of PBS. The cell mixture was then injected into the left ventricle and subjected to bioluminescence imaging following the same procedures as described for the brain metastasis model establishment above. Mice were euthanized when they developed signs of neurological deficit or weight loss > 20%, and survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test.

Isolation and purification of brain-metastatic cells

Brain metastases were identified via bioluminescence imaging. After euthanasia, metastatic brain lesions were aseptically dissected. Tissues were minced and digested in RPMI-1640 or DMEM with 0.2% collagenase I at 37 °C for 1 h, with gentle agitation every 20 min. The digest was centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in 0.25% trypsin, and incubated for 10 min. Cells were then centrifuged, resuspended in complete medium (RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin), and cultured in 25-cm² flasks. Brain-metastatic cells were selected with puromycin and expanded to establish stable lines (H2030-BM1, PC9-BM1), which were re-inoculated into mice to derive H2030-BM and PC9-BM cell lines.

Targeted lipidomics sequencing

After slow thawing of the samples at 4 °C, an appropriate amount of the sample was added to 200 µL methanol and vortexed. Then, 10 µL of the internal standard mixture was added, followed by 800 µL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE). The mixture was vortexed again and sonicated in a low-temperature water bath for 20 min, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. Next, 200 µL of mass spectrometry-grade water was added, and the mixture was vortexed. Samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, and the upper organic phase was collected and dried under nitrogen. For mass spectrometry analysis, the samples were re-dissolved in 200 µL of 90% isopropanol/acetonitrile solution, vortexed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was then collected for analysis.

The samples were separated using a LC-30AD Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system, employing C18 and Amino columns. For the C18 column, the conditions were as follows: column temperature 45 °C, flow rate 0.35 mL/min, mobile phase composition A: 70% acetonitrile + 30% water + 5 mM ammonium acetate, and B: isopropanol solution. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–5.0 min, B from 20% to 60% linearly; 5.0–13.0 min, B from 60% to 100% linearly; 13.1–17.0 min, B maintained at 20%. For the Amino column, the conditions were: column temperature 40 °C, flow rate 0.4 mL/min, mobile phase composition A: 2 mM ammonium acetate + 50% methanol + 50% acetonitrile, and B: 2 mM ammonium acetate + 50% acetonitrile + 50% water. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–3.0 min, B maintained at 3%; 3.0–13.0 min, B from 3% to 100% linearly; 13.0–17.0 min, B maintained at 100%; 17.1–22.0 min, B maintained at 3%. Throughout the analysis, the samples were placed in a 10 °C automatic injector. To minimize the impact of instrument signal fluctuations, the samples were analyzed in a random sequence.

Available RNA-sequencing

ScRNA-seq data were obtained from the NCBI GEO database (GSE131907), comprising 33,280 cells from 10 samples (six primary lung cancers and four brain metastases). Additional validation datasets included GSE148071 (n = 22; 22,247 cells) and OMIX007088 (n = 24; 190,289 cells) [27, 28]. Data were processed using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0).

Cell culture and reagents

Human NSCLC cell lines (H2030, PC9) and MO3.13 oligodendrocyte cells were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal protein amounts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes.The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris buffered saline Tween (TBST), followed by incubation with primary antibodies: FASN (Cat. #66591-1-Ig; Proteintech; 1:5,000), ACLY (Cat. #67166-1-Ig; Proteintech; 1:1,000), SREBP1 (Cat. #66875-1-Ig, Proteintech; 1:2,000), SREBP2 (Cat. #28212-1-AP; Proteintech; 1:1,000), FABP5 (Cat. #66299-1-Ig; Proteintech; 1:2,000), CD36 (Cat. #66395-1-Ig; Proteintech; 1:1,000) and GAPDH (Cat. #81115–1-RR; Proteintech; 1:5,000) overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat. #SA00001-1/SA00001-2; Proteintech; 1:2,000). The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Share Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and the intensity was analyzed with image processing software.

Detection of fatty acids and triglycerides and staining of lipid droplets

The amount of fatty acids in the cell lines was determined using the Amplex Red free fatty Acid Assay Kit (S0215S)(Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The triglyceride content in the cell lines was determined using the Amplex Red Triglyceride Assay kit (S0219S) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The levels of fatty acids and triglycerides in MO3.13, PC9-bm and H2030-bm cells were detected by kits. Lipid droplets in the cell lines were stained using the lipid droplet Green fluorescence detection kit (BODIPY 493/503) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tissue immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed following standard protocols. Briefly, paraffin-embedded brain metastasis tissue Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) with 0.05% Tween-20 for 15 min. After blocking with 5% normal donkey serum, 1% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: MBP (Cat. #10458-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:50), OLIG2 (Cat. #13999-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500), Pan-CK (Cat. #26411-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), KI-67 (Cat. #66555-6-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000), CD36 (Cat. #66395-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:200), and CD206 (Cat. #60143-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:200). After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI counterstaining (1 µg/mL, 5 min). Stained slides were scanned by BNK Technology Co., Ltd. using a high-resolution whole-slide scanner. All images were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji) software. For tissue microarray (TMA) analysis, H-score was calculated as [1×(% cells 1+) + 2 × (% cells 2+) + 3 × (% cells 3+)]. Staining intensity (0–3) and the percentage of positive cells were scored independently by two blinded pathologists.

Cell transfection

To establish cell lines with stable knockdown of FASN and CD36, cells were transduced with lentiviral particles encoding pLKO.1-GFP-puro-FASN shRNA and pLKO.1-GFP-puro-CD36 shRNA (QEgene, Shanghai, China). Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the pLKO.1 vector and the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Seventy-two hours post-transfection, viral supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45-µm membrane. Target cells were then infected with the viral particles in the presence of polybrene. Following puromycin selection for 3–4 weeks, stable clones were verified for FASN and CD36 knockdown via RT-qPCR and western blotting and subsequently used for downstream experiments.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA), and reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Osaka, Japan). Quantitative PCR was conducted with SYBR Premix. All procedures, including RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR, were carried out following established protocols [29]. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Cell viability assay (CCK8)

Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay. NSCLC-BM cells, 24 h post-transfection, were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well in 100 µl of complete medium and incubated at 37 °C. At the end of each time point, 10 µl of CCK8 reagent (Yeason, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in triplicate, and experiments were repeated at least three times independently.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The data were recorded as mean ± standard error of the mean. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc tests for multiple groups.

Results

Single cell transcriptome atlas of LCBM

To comprehensively characterize the tumor microenvironment of LCBM, we collected LCBM samples from three patients for scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics. Among these, two patients had EGFR mutations, while one had EGFR wild-type. We also performed a joint analysis with data from GSE131907, which includes scRNA-seq data from six primary lung cancer samples and four brain metastasis samples (Fig. 1A).

Single cell transcriptome atlas of LCBM (A) Schematic representation of the study design. (B) UMAP plot demonstrating the cell distribution from primary lung cancer and LCBM (C) Feature plots presenting classical marker genes for the annotated cell types. (D) Heatmap identifying the expression of selected marker genes in the annotated cell types. (E) Bar graphs show the proportion of cells in different groups. All experiments were repeated three times (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, ns > 0.05)

We constructed a combined gene expression matrix containing 52,226 cells. Of these, 24,724 cells were from the primary lung cancer lesions, and 27,502 cells were from the brain metastases. Using a resolution of 2.0, we identified 24 distinct cell clusters, which were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots (Fig. 1B). To determine the major cell types, we annotated each cluster based on cell-type-specific marker genes. We confirmed that each cell type expressed its known marker genes with high specificity (Fig. 1C-D).

Almost all patients showed the presence of these 10 cell types; however, their proportions varied significantly (Fig. 1E). T cells, epithelial cells, and macrophages were the most abundant cell types. Oligodendrocytes were only found in the brain metastases. Additionally, we confirmed that the proportions of various cell types in the brain metastasis microenvironment differed from those in primary lung cancer lesions. For instance, T cells, stromal cells, mast cells, and dendritic cells were more abundant in primary lung cancer lesions than in brain metastases. When grouped according to EGFR mutation status, we observed differences in cell distributions. For example, the number of T cells in the EGFR wild-type brain metastasis samples was lower than in EGFR mutant brain metastasis samples, while the number of T cells in the primary lung lesions was slightly higher in EGFR wild-type than in EGFR mutant samples. Additionally, the number of dendritic cells, mast cells, and plasma cells in both primary lesions and brain metastases was associated with EGFR mutation status.

Oligodendrocytes are involved in fatty acid metabolism in the microenvironment of brain metastases from lung cancer

To investigate microenvironmental differences between primary lung tumors and brain metastases, we performed QuSAGE enrichment analysis across five functional categories: immune response, metabolism, cell proliferation, signaling pathways, and cell–cell interactions (Fig. 2A).

Oligodendrocytes are involved in fatty acid metabolism in the microenvironment of brain metastases from lung cancer (A) QuSAGE enrichment analysis comparing pathway activation across five categories—immune response, metabolism, cell proliferation, signaling pathways, and cell interactions—in primary lung cancer and brain metastasis lesions. (B-C) Sc RNA seq analysis of each cell tumor fatty acid metabolism-related pathway enrichment analysis. (D) Gene expression analysis of fatty acid synthesis, utilization, and transport genes in primary lung tumors and brain metastases

In the ALL CELL analysis, macrophages and T/NK cells were identified as key players in immune-related pathways. Specifically, macrophages were notably enriched in the “B cell receptor signaling pathway” and “Neutrophil extracellular trap formation”, while T_NK cells showed significant activation in the “T cell receptor signaling pathway” and the “PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer” (Fig. 2A).

In the metabolic category, oligodendrocytes within brain metastases were significantly enriched for “Fatty acid metabolism” and “Linoleic acid metabolism” pathways. In contrast, epithelial cells and macrophages in primary lung tumors were more involved in alternative metabolic processes (Fig. 2A). With regard to cell–cell interaction pathways, stromal cells played major roles in “ECM–receptor interaction” and “Focal adhesion” processes. Interestingly, oligodendrocytes in brain metastases were specifically enriched in the “Gap junction” pathway, suggesting a unique mode of intercellular communication in this context.

Previous studies have indicated that in the brain tumor microenvironment, tumor cells undergo a metabolic shift from glucose metabolism to fatty acid metabolism [13, 30]. Therefore, we focused our analysis on gene set activity related to fatty acid metabolism pathways. The results revealed that oligodendrocytes in brain metastases are prominently involved in the “Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids”, “Fatty acid elongation”, and “Fatty acid metabolism” pathways (Fig. 2B). In contrast, macrophages and epithelial cells in brain metastases showed greater enrichment in the “Fatty acid degradation” pathway (Fig. 2B). Next, we combined the primary lesion scRNA-seq data from GSE148071 and the scRNA-seq of LCBM from OMIX007088 for verification (Figure S1A). The results indicated that oligodendrocytes played a dominant role in the fatty acid synthesis process (Fig. 2C).

Next, we explored the expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis, utilization, and transport in brain metastases. In brain metastases, key genes involved in fatty acid synthesis, such as SCD, ACLY, and SREBF1, were expressed at higher levels in oligodendrocytes. However, fatty acid utilization molecules, including CPT1, ACOX1, and MCAD, were expressed at lower levels in oligodendrocytes (Fig. 2D). The results from the validation set also revealed that the key genes for fatty acid synthesis were mainly expressed in oligodendrocytes in the brain metastasis microenvironment of lung cancer (Figure S1B).

These findings suggest that, compared to primary tumors, oligodendrocytes in the brain metastasis microenvironment play a crucial role in fatty acid synthesis, but their capacity to utilize fatty acids is weaker than that of macrophages and epithelial cells.

Oligodendrocytes supply fatty acids to tumor cells

Owing to the pronounced heterogeneity between brain metastases and primary lung tumors, we developed lung cancer cell lines with enhanced tropism for brain colonization to facilitate subsequent investigations. To this end, cells were injected intracardially into BALB/c nude mice, after which brain-metastatic cells were harvested and subjected to three successive rounds of in vivo selection (Fig. 3A). Through this iterative enrichment process, we established brain-seeking sublines (H2030-BM and PC9-BM), which exhibited brain metastasis rates of 55% and 60%, respectively (Fig. 3B). Targeted lipidomic sequencing was performed on oligodendrocytes, with those co-cultured in conditioned medium derived from LCBM cells serving as the treatment group (Fig. 3C). Volcano and bubble plots revealed differentially expressed lipid species, highlighting a significant increase in the lipid MGMD(16:0/20:3) following co-culture with brain metastasis-derived lung cancer cells (Fig. 3D-E). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these differential lipids were primarily involved in “Fatty acid biosynthesis”, “Pathways in cancer”, and other metabolic pathways (Fig. 3F).

To investigate the potential transfer of fatty acids between oligodendrocytes and tumor cells, we conducted a series of co-culture experiments to analyze intracellular fatty acid changes. The results showed a significant increase in both fatty acid and triglyceride levels in oligodendrocytes and tumor cells following co-culture (Fig. 3G). Lipid droplet staining further revealed that lipid droplet content significantly increased in oligodendrocytes after co-culture, while tumor cells not only exhibited an increase in lipid droplet accumulation but also displayed alterations in lipid droplet size and morphology (Fig. 3H).

We next examined changes in fatty acid synthesis enzymes in oligodendrocytes. Co-culture of MO3.13 oligodendrocytes with brain metastasis-derived lung cancer cells under normoxic conditions significantly upregulated the expression of fatty acid synthesis enzymes, including FASN and ACLY (Fig. 3I). Similarly, expression of SREBP1, a key transcription factor regulating lipid biosynthesis, was markedly increased, while SREBP2 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 3K).

Under hypoxic conditions, fatty acid synthase expression in oligodendrocytes was further elevated, and the effect of co-culture with conditioned medium (CM) from metastatic lung cancer cells on fatty acid synthesis was amplified (Fig. 3J). A similar trend was observed for lipid biosynthesis transcription factors, where the influence of tumor cells on SREBP1 expression in oligodendrocytes was further enhanced in hypoxia (Fig. 3L).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that paracrine interactions between lung cancer cells and oligodendrocytes—especially under hypoxic conditions—drive lipid metabolic reprogramming in oligodendrocytes. This reprogramming is characterized by increased fatty acid synthesis and activation of lipid biosynthetic pathways, potentially contributing to the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

ScRNA-seq analysis revealed that fatty acid uptake receptors (CD36, MFSD2A, and FATP) were predominantly expressed in tumor cells and macrophages within the tumor microenvironment of LCBM (Fig. 4A). Functional enrichment analysis further indicated that pathways related to fatty acid uptake and degradation were significantly upregulated in macrophages and tumor cells compared to other cell types (Fig. 4B).

When oligodendrocytes were co-cultured with tumor cells or macrophages, expression of the fatty acid-binding protein FABP5 was significantly increased (p < 0.05), implicating oligodendrocytes in the regulation of lipid transport (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this, co-culture of tumor cells or macrophages with oligodendrocyte-conditioned medium led to a significant upregulation of CD36 expression in both cell types (p < 0.05), suggesting enhanced fatty acid uptake capacity (Fig. 4D).

At the single cell level, using the scRNA-seq validation set (GSE131907, OMIX007088), we found that CD36 in macrophages was positively correlated with macrophage CD163 (r = 0.9, p < 0.05), LTA (r = 0.9, p < 0.05), IL10 (r = 0.87, p < 0.05) and MRC1 (r = 0.89, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E), while FABP5, SREBF1, FASN, ACLY and SCD in oligodendrocytes were positively correlated with macrophage IL10 expression (Figure S2A). In TCGA database, the expression of macrophage immunosuppressive markers was positively correlated with the expression of CD36 (Fig. 4F).

To directly assess the functional importance of fatty acid synthesis in oligodendrocytes, FASN expression was silenced using shRNA, followed by co-culture with macrophages. western boltting and qpcr verified the efficiency of FASN knockdown (Fig. 4G), and the intracellular fat of oligodendrocyte was significantly reduced after FASN knockdown (Fig. 4H). Quantitative PCR analysis showed a significant reduction in key immunosuppressive markers in macrophages, including IL-10, ARG1, CD206, CD163, and TGF-β (all p < 0.05; Fig. 4I). To evaluate the functional role of oligodendrocyte fatty acid synthesis in vivo, we co-injected luciferase-labeled H2030-BM cells with shNC or shFASN MO3.13 oligodendrocytes into the left ventricle of nude mice. Mice receiving shFASN oligodendrocytes showed significantly reduced intracranial tumor burden compared to the control group (Fig. 4J) and exhibited prolonged survival (Fig. 4K). Next, we investigated the role of the fatty acid receptor CD36 in recipient cells. CD36 was knocked down in HMC3 cells (Fig. 4L). Treatment with oligodendrocyte-conditioned medium (MO3.13-CM) increased the expression of immunosuppressive polarization markers in HMC3 cells at the mRNA level. Importantly, CD36 knockdown in HMC3 cells reversed this MO3.13-CM-induced upregulation of these markers (Fig. 4M). In tumor cells, CD36 knockdown was confirmed (Fig. 4N). Under MO3.13-CM stimulation, CD36-knockdown H2030-BM cells exhibited significantly reduced proliferative capacity compared to control cells (Fig. 4O).

Spatial transcriptomics reveal the metabolic and immune characteristics of brain metastases

Spatial transcriptomic sequencing was conducted on brain metastases from three lung cancer patients to investigate the spatial distribution of key cellular markers. These included oligodendrocyte markers OLIG2 and MBP, immunosuppressive macrophage marker CD206, inflammatory macrophage marker CD80, and the fatty acid uptake protein CD36 (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that oligodendrocytes were predominantly localized at the tumor margins. Within the tumor microenvironment of LCBM, macrophages exhibited an immunosuppressive phenotype and were largely infiltrated within the tumor mass. Expression of CD80 was minimal, whereas CD36 was primarily expressed within tumor regions (Fig. 5A).

To validate the capacity of tumor cells and macrophages to uptake fatty acids and to further assess the infiltration pattern of oligodendrocytes, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence analysis. Cells co-expressing MBP and OLIG2 were identified as mature oligodendrocytes, and the immunofluorescence findings were consistent with the spatial transcriptomics data. Oligodendrocytes were mainly detected at the periphery of tumor lesions and occasionally infiltrated into tumor parenchyma (Fig. 5B).

Furthermore, a brain metastasis tissue microarray and multiplex immunofluorescence were used to explore the relationship between CD36 expression, macrophage polarization, and tumor cell proliferation. Co-expression of CD36 and CD206 suggested an association between fatty acid uptake and the immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype. Furthermore, co-localization of CD36 with pan-CK indicated that tumor cells also utilize fatty acids, potentially supporting tumor growth (Fig. 5C). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity demonstrated that CD36 expression positively correlated with KI-67 levels (r = 0.331, p = 0.002), and CD206 expression showed a stronger positive correlation (r = 0.522, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5D). Next, a CCK8 assay demonstrated that oligodendrocytes (MO3.13) promote the proliferation of lung cancer brain metastasis cell lines (H2030-BM and PC9-BM). Knockdown of FASN in oligodendrocytes significantly attenuated this proliferative effect (Fig. 5E).

Finally, pathway enrichment analysis of spatially resolved transcriptomic data confirmed that oligodendrocytes in the metastatic brain microenvironment are involved in fatty acid metabolism, in agreement with findings from scRNA-seq (Fig. 5F-H).

A total of 14,161 macrophage cells were analyzed and classified into six subclusters, including Mac_CCL18, Mac_CX3CR1, Mac_FABP4, Mac_LYVE1, Mac_MMP12, and Microglia. Notably, FABP4 + macrophages represent a key subset linking lipid metabolism with immune responses [31] (Fig. 6A). In terms of cell count, Mac_LYVE1 was more abundant and Mac_CCL18 was less abundant in brain metastases compared to primary lung tumors (Fig. 6B). Using SCENIC analysis, distinct transcriptional activities were identified in these subclusters (Fig. 6D). In Mac_FABP4, high transcriptional activity was observed for KLF4, MYB, TCF7L2, TBX18, and NPAS2, which are known to play significant roles in tumor metabolism and immunity. KLF4 enhances lipid utilization by regulating fatty acid oxidation (FAO) genes such as CPT1A, and inhibits anti-tumor immunity through the suppression of the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing proinflammatory cytokines and activating STAT6, which promotes IL-10 and ARG1 expression [32]. Next, we explored the differences in each type of macrophage genes between primary lung cancer with brain metastases from lung cancer, we found that MAC_FABP4 subgroup, in the brain metastases, RACK1, ELOB, IGLC2, AREG, SMIM25 significantly higher expression (Fig. 6E).

Pseudo-temporal analysis revealed differences in the differentiation trajectories of Mac_FABP4 between primary lung lesions and brain metastases (Fig. 6F). In primary lung lesions, the differentiation trace began with Mac_CCL18, proceeding through other subsets and ultimately ending with Mac_FABP4, while in brain metastases, it started with Mac_FABP4 and ended with other subsets. Gene Set Activity Analysis using QuSAGE revealed that metabolism-related pathways, including “Metabolic pathways”, “Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis”, “Fatty acid metabolism”, and “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)”, were significantly activated in the Mac_FABP4 subgroup in brain metastases (Fig. 6G). Key tumor-related pathways such as mTOR signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, and EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance were also activated in brain metastases compared to primary lung tumors. Immune-related enrichment analysis indicated that Mac_LYVE1 may play a more significant role in tumor immune regulation (Fig. 6G).

Single cell cell-to-cell interaction analysis suggested potential interactions between macrophages and oligodendrocytes, where tumor cells may act as receptors and oligodendrocytes as ligands (Fig. 6H). Notably, THBS2 in oligodendrocytes and CD36 in macrophages were identified as potential interacting molecules, underscoring the important role of macrophages in absorbing fatty acids from oligodendrocytes (Fig. 6I). Further analysis of the fatty acid degradation pathway in the Mac_FABP4 subgroup showed that genes involved in fatty acid utilization were highly expressed in this subset (Fig. 6J).

We also utilized TCGA data to analyze the correlation between FABP4 expression and markers such as CD36 and CD163. FABP4 was positively correlated with both CD36 and CD163 (Fig. 6K). Additionally, we separated macrophages into FABP4-high and FABP4-low groups in the scRNA-seq database. Our results showed that CD163 expression was significantly higher in FABP4-high macrophages than in FABP4-low macrophages, while CD36 expression was also significantly correlated with FABP4. Notably, CD36 was highly expressed in FABP4-high macrophages, but almost absent in FABP4-low macrophages (Fig. 6L). Finally, we assessed the prognostic significance of FASN, CD36, and FABP4 in lung cancer patients. Analysis of TCGA data revealed that high FASN expression was significantly associated with worse prognosis, while neither FABP4 nor CD36 exhibited prognostic value (Figure S3A).

Discussion

Brain metastases from lung cancer are clinically devastating, and the unique brain microenvironment profoundly influences tumor progression [33]. Unlike extracranial sites, the brain harbors specialized resident cell types—neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes [28]. Previous studies have established that glial-tumor interactions play crucial roles in brain metastasis biology. For instance, astrocytes secrete exosomes that promote chemoresistance, while microglia can adopt pro-tumorigenic phenotypes in response to tumor-derived signals [11]. However, the role of oligodendrocytes—the myelin-forming cells of the central nervous system—remains largely unexplored. Emerging single-cell transcriptomic surveys have noted oligodendrocyte presence in brain metastases [3, 4, 34]. Our previous work showed that oligodendrocytes exhibit distinct “endoplasmic reticulum stress” and “protein secretion” signatures in LCBM microenvironment [35]. Mechanistically, oligodendrocytes secrete ERBB3, which promotes copper uptake by tumor cells and facilitates brain metastasis [36]. Nevertheless, the metabolic function of oligodendrocytes in LCBM has remained unclear. Here, we systematically characterized oligodendrocyte-tumor-immune crosstalk using multi-omics and functional assays.

Several studies have established the importance of fatty acid metabolism in brain metastases. Ferraro et al. showed that HER2⁺ breast cancer brain metastases overexpress FASN, and genetic or pharmacological inhibition of FASN reduces brain tumor growth [13]. Sala et al. demonstrated that p53 inactivation drives breast cancer brain metastasis by upregulating SCD1 and CD36, and that astrocytes supply fatty acids to enhance cancer cell proliferation and brain colonization [14]. Zou et al. revealed that astrocytes are enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids, which activate PPARγ signaling in cancer cells to promote brain metastasis [15]. However, these studies focused exclusively on breast cancer and melanoma. Lung cancer is the leading cause of brain metastases, yet its fatty acid metabolism in the brain microenvironment remains underexplored. Moreover, oligodendrocytes have not been systematically examined in this context. At the single-cell level, no study has identified which cell type exhibits the highest fatty acid synthetic activity in LCBM, nor how oligodendrocytes contribute to lipid metabolism. We do not rule out the possibility that astrocytes or other cells could also provide fatty acids, but our results indicate that in LCBM, oligodendrocytes are likely to be the main source of lipids.

The brain metastatic microenvironment is characterized by hypoxia, restricted glucose availability, and a unique lipid-rich milieu [30, 37]. These conditions force metabolic adaptation and intercellular metabolic coupling [13, 38]. While lipid metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of brain metastases [12, 39], the cellular origin of lipids remained unclear. To resolve this, we performed pathway analysis of single-cell transcriptomes from LCBM and paired primary lung tumors. We found that oligodendrocytes in brain metastases are strongly enriched in fatty acid synthesis pathways, including “biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids,” “fatty acid elongation,” and “fatty acid metabolism” (Fig. 2B–C). This enrichment was specific to oligodendrocytes; macrophages and tumor cells instead showed high expression of fatty acid degradation genes (Fig. 2D). Our targeted lipidomics further confirmed that oligodendrocytes co-cultured with brain-metastatic lung cancer cells accumulate lipid species and activate fatty acid biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 3). Collectively, these results suggest that oligodendrocytes represent a major, previously unrecognized source of fatty acids within the LCBM microenvironment, shifting the focus from tumor-cell-autonomous lipid synthesis to potential glial-supplied lipids.

Having identified oligodendrocytes as a major fatty acid source, we next investigated which cells receive these lipids. Spatial transcriptomics and scRNA-seq showed that the fatty acid uptake receptors CD36, MFSD2A, and FATP are highly expressed on tumor cells and macrophages, but not on oligodendrocytes themselves (Fig. 4A). Functional enrichment confirmed that uptake and degradation pathways are active in these recipient cells (Fig. 4B). Co-culture with oligodendrocyte-conditioned medium significantly upregulated CD36 in both tumor cells and macrophages (Fig. 4C–D). Consistent with previous reports, CD36 has been recognized as a key lipid transporter mediating fatty acid uptake in both cancer cells and macrophages, and its expression correlates with M2 polarization and immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment [40–42]. MFSD2A and FATP family members are also involved in fatty acid transport across cell membranes [43].Thus, oligodendrocytes function as lipid donors and tumor cells/macrophages as lipid recipients, identifying a novel metabolic reprogramming axis within the lung cancer brain metastasis microenvironment.

We then examined the functional consequences of this lipid transfer. For macrophages, oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids promoted an immunosuppressive phenotype. In co-culture experiments, oligodendrocyte-CM increased the expression of CD163, CD206, IL10, ARG1, and TGFβ in macrophages (Fig. 4I). Knockdown of FASN in oligodendrocytes or CD36 in macrophages abolished these effects (Fig. 4M). It is well established that the availability and metabolic state of fatty acids in the tumor microenvironment can influence macrophage polarization [44–46]. Tumor cells, through cytokine secretion or direct metabolic exchange, can alter macrophage metabolism and promote their polarization towards the M2 phenotype [47]. This M2 polarization is supported by fatty acid metabolism, enabling macrophages to further facilitate tumor immune escape and progression. Our study suggests that, in addition to the intrinsic metabolic reprogramming of macrophages, there is a unique supply of fatty acids in the brain metastasis microenvironment-specifically provided by oligodendrocytes. These fatty acids contribute to M2 macrophage polarization, creating a metabolic niche that favors tumor growth and metastasis. For tumor cells, oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids enhanced proliferation. CCK8 assays showed that oligodendrocyte-CM significantly increased the growth of H2030-BM and PC9-BM cells, an effect blunted by FASN knockdown in oligodendrocytes or CD36 knockdown in tumor cells (Figs. 4O and 5E). In the LCBM tissue microarray, CD36 expression positively correlated with Ki-67, and CD206 showed an even stronger correlation (Fig. 5D). Together, these results demonstrate that oligodendrocyte-supplied fatty acids simultaneously drive two pro-metastatic processes: tumor cell proliferation and macrophage-mediated immune evasion.

To further dissect the macrophage compartment, we analyzed 14,161 macrophages from LCBM and primary tumors. We identified a distinct FABP4⁺ macrophage subpopulation enriched in brain metastases. FABP4⁺ is a key lipid chaperone that regulates fatty acid transport and metabolism in macrophages. Previous studies have shown that FABP4⁺ macrophages accumulate lipids, promote M2 polarization, and contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [48, 49]. SCENIC analysis revealed that FABP4⁺ macrophages are driven by transcription factors KLF4 and TCF7L2, known regulators of lipid-associated immune tolerance (Fig. 6D). Pseudotime analysis further indicated that in brain metastases, FABP4⁺ macrophages represent an early differentiation state that gives rise to other subsets, suggesting they are a hub for metabolic-immune crosstalk (Fig. 6F). Spatial transcriptomics and CellPhoneDB predicted that oligodendrocytes interact with FABP4⁺ macrophages via the THBS2–CD36 ligand–receptor pair (Fig. 6I). Moreover, it has been reported that FABP4⁺ macrophages secrete IL‑6 to suppress T cell activity and that FABP4 links dysregulated lipid metabolism with immune modulation in cancer [50]. These results suggest that FABP4⁺ macrophages may serve as candidate downstream mediators of oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids in immune suppression.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our in vivo model used intracardiac injection, which bypasses early steps of the metastatic cascade; future studies using orthotopic or spontaneous metastasis models will be needed to validate our findings in a more physiological context. Second, while our data identify oligodendrocytes as a major source of fatty acids in the LCBM microenvironment, we cannot exclude the possibility that other cell types (astrocytes, endothelial cells) also contribute to lipid supply. Third, we acknowledge that direct in vivo evidence of lipid transfer from oligodendrocytes to tumor cells or macrophages is currently lacking. Although our spatial transcriptomics and in vitro co-culture data support this possibility, definitive proof would require isotope tracing or lineage-specific lipid labeling. Fourth, the integration of multiple public scRNA-seq datasets inevitably carries batch effects, inter-cohort variability, and tumor-type heterogeneity. While we corrected for batch effects, residual confounding may still influence some transcriptional associations. Fifth, advanced computational frameworks and machine learning could further strengthen our conclusions by enabling deeper integration of multi-omic datasets and predicting metabolic vulnerabilities [51, 52], we plan to apply such approaches in future work.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a critical role for oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acid metabolism in orchestrating the immune and proliferative landscape of LCBM. Targeting the oligodendrocyte–macrophage–tumor cell axis, particularly pathways involved in lipid synthesis and transport, may offer new therapeutic strategies for brain metastases.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies oligodendrocytes as a previously unrecognized cellular hub that remodels the fatty acid metabolic microenvironment of lung cancer brain metastases. Using integrated scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, lipidomics, and functional assays, we demonstrate that oligodendrocyte-derived fatty acids are taken up by tumor cells (enhancing proliferation) and tumor-associated macrophages (promoting immunosuppression). In vivo knockdown of FASN reduces brain metastatic growth and prolongs survival, providing proof-of-concept that this glial-metabolic axis is therapeutically actionable. While further validation in immunocompetent models and larger patient cohorts is needed, our findings provide clues for targeting metabolic crosstalk in the brain metastatic niche.
