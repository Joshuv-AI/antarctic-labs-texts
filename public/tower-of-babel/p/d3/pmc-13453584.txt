Risk stacking in peripheral artery disease (PAD): Integrating genetics, cardiometabolic disease and social determinants of health to explain worsening PAD outcomes
Authors: Matthew Nudy, Lawrence Sinoway, Faisal Aziz, Gerd Brunner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral artery disease (PAD) contributes disproportionately to disability, limb loss, and premature mortality, and U.S. major amputation rates have risen in the past decade among increasingly younger adults. Traditional risk tools incompletely explain these trends. We propose to examine a hypothesis generating risk-stacked model in which three interacting layers: 1) genetic susceptibility via lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]; 2) cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKM; chronic kidney disease/diabetes mellitus/insulin resistance); and 3) social determinants of health (SDOH) jointly influence PAD incidence, phenotype at presentation (critical limb-threatening ischemia), and outcomes (major adverse limb events [MALE], including amputation). We summarize the independent and joint associations of elevated Lp(a), CKM/diabetes mellitus/insulin resistance, and adverse SDOH with PAD onset and MALE and practice-level implementation strategies (one-time Lp(a) testing, SDOH screening/routing, intensified preventive therapies). We hypothesize that high Lp(a) and diabetes/insulin resistance synergize to impair collateralization and promote thrombosis-prone plaque biology, while adverse SDOH delay diagnosis and limit access to guideline-directed care, together driving worse PAD outcomes. This conceptual framework supports universal one-time Lp(a) measurement, aggressive prevention, and embedded SDOH workflows while informing trials of equity-focused delivery models for patients with PAD. These factors may refine PAD risk stratification and align prevention resources to those at highest risk of limb loss.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Peripheral artery disease, Critical limb-threatening ischemia, Major adverse limb events, Lipoprotein(a), Social determinants of health

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD), the cause of peripheral artery disease (PAD) is the major cause of death in the world today [1]. In the U.S. alone, over 250 billion dollars is spent annually on the management of patients with ASCVD [2,3]. Since the ground breaking work of Kannel et al., in 1961 [4], continued research in this area has identified several factors which contribute to the risk of atherosclerotic disease leading to the development of progressively more sophisticated risk assessment tools that can be used to direct individual patient therapy as well as in the planning for community level interventions to reduce the risk of incident atherosclerotic disease. These include 10-year risk assessment tools for primary ASCVD prevention (patients without known ASCVD) populations such as the pooled cohort equations [5] and more recently the PREVENT equation [6] which incorporates kidney disease and an estimate of the social vulnerability index into ASCVD risk. In contrast, Life's Essential 8 from the American Heart Association (AHA) offer a broader framework for ASCVD prevention at both the individual and community level [7]. In addition, the American Heart Association's cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) construct provides a staged framework that integrates adiposity, metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and clinical cardiovascular disease into a unified continuum of risk. CKM stages range from stage 0 (no CKM risk factors) to stage 4 (clinical cardiovascular disease in the setting of CKM), with stage 3 representing subclinical cardiovascular disease or high predicted risk [8]. While these models do not specifically estimate the risk of PAD, there is a need to develop multifaceted novel risk assessment tools for PAD.

Despite significant therapeutic advances in PAD over the course of past six decades, the burden of PAD is increasing at an alarming rate, with a rapid rate affecting progressively younger population. This can be seen with the rising rates of acute critical limb threatening ischemia (CLTI) among patients with PAD. Recent national data show that major lower-extremity amputations (Below Knee Amputation or Above Knee Amputation) have risen since ∼2012 as the mean age falling by ∼6-10 years through 2021 [3]. Unfortunately, majority of the patients undergoing major limb amputations do not undergo any revascularization attempt before limb loss [9]. Parallel analyses indicate an increase in the incidence of stroke and myocardial infarction burden in younger and middle age adults [10]. To highlight the importance of this issue, Mehta et al. [11] have recently argued that the national stall in life expectancy gains is due to plateauing of improvement in cardiovascular mortality.

Another important observation is that the phenotype of atherosclerotic disease, including PAD is changing with an increasing number of patients now presenting with diffuse small vessel disease [12]. We hypothesize, that with the modern tools of endovascular therapy and vascular surgery, revascularization involving named blood vessels has become relatively straight forward, but the revascularization options for small vessel disease is more challenging, the amount of end organ damage is greater leading to more major amputations, larger myocardial infarctions, increase in heart failure and an increase in the disability of strokes [13]. The next logical question is: What are the major factors responsible for this shift in phenotype, the increase in disease at younger ages and the worse outcomes? At this juncture we believe it is critical to not only emphasize the factors that enhance the risk of having PAD but also these factors that predispose patients to worse outcomes. We propose that a “severity and risk stacked model” be considered when approaching ASCVD, specifically PAD (Fig. 1). In this conceptual schema, genetic, metabolic and environmental factors are given major consideration. The genetic risk layer is determined by lipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)] plasma concentrations, the CKM level is determined by the presence or absence of diabetes/insulin resistance and the environmental level factors are determined by social determinants of health (Fig. 1). In this paper, we focus our assessment on PAD.

Lp(a): Genetic risk layer

There is a large body of literature showing that high levels Lp(a) is an important mediator of ASCVD risk [14,15]. Two mechanisms have been proposed to explain how Lp(a) increases ASCVD risk: enhanced intimal deposition of the Lp(a) thereby intensifying the atherosclerotic process or by prothrombotic effects [16]. Twenty-four percent of the U.S. population have elevated levels of Lp(a) [17]. Lp(a) is ∼80–90% genetically determined and blood levels remain relatively constant over a lifetime. It should be noted that Lp(a) levels are higher in African Americans and in those of Southeast Asian descent than in white people [18]. Genetic differences contribute to much of the observed variations in Lp(a) levels across racial and ethnic groups as well as gender. Multiple studies showed that women tend to have a 5-10% higher Lp(a) levels than men [19]. The reason for these findings are unclear but may be due to isomer size of the LPA gene [20].

There are a number of reasons why high levels of Lp (a) can worsen the phenotypic presentation of ASCVD. First, elevated Lp(a) is associated with increased plaque volatility with rapid plaque progression and with a higher necrotic core content [21,22]. It is interesting to note that there is a particularly strong relationship between Lp(a) concentrations and peripheral arterial disease (as opposed to cerebral and coronary disease). This association may be curvilinear (EPIC and Copenhagen) suggesting worse disease at the highest concentrations. In general, associations between Lp(a) and coronary disease are consistent but more modest than those noted for Lp(a) and PAD [23]. Beyond its association with ASCVD broadly, growing data support a causal role for Lp(a) in PAD and adverse limb outcomes. In the Copenhagen General Population Study, individuals with Lp(a) at or above the 99th percentile had a nearly 3-fold higher risk of PAD than those below the 50th percentile (HR 2.99, 95% CI 2.09–4.30), and among those with PAD, the rate of MALE events was also approximately 3-fold higher (incidence rate ratio 3.04, 95% CI 1.55–5.98) [24]. The concordant association between circulating Lp(a) concentrations and LPA genotypes strengthens the argument for causality.

A second important issue regarding Lp(a) is that it can reduce small vessel collateral blood vessel formation [25] and in clinical studies in those with severe occlusions and in those with chronic total occlusions there is an inverse relationship between Lp(a) levels and the degree of coronary collateralization [26]. Collateral formation and recruitment are important mechanisms to limit tissue loss and preserve organ function in the presence of large vessel obstruction. Taken together, these findings suggest that Lp(a) may be more than a marker of incident PAD and may instead contribute to a more severe PAD phenotype through proatherogenic, prothrombotic, and collateral impairing effects. It has also been shown that a high Lp(a) is associated with the presence of complex CAD [27]. It should also be noted that high Lp(a) is associated with worse outcomes and may be detrimental in diabetic patients with hyperlipidemia [26]. The National Lipid Association recommends every adult have an Lp(a) concentration measured at least once during their adult life [28]. At present there are no approved therapies to reduce elevated Lp(a), although there are several agents that are presently being evaluated in clinical trials including small interfering mRNAs and antisense oligonucleotide agents.

CKM Syndrome: Metabolic and Renal Risk Layer We intentionally anchor the metabolic layer of the risk-stacked model to the AHA CKM construct rather than to diabetes alone. CKM staging captures progression from excess or dysfunctional adiposity and early metabolic dysfunction to established CKD, subclinical cardiovascular disease, and ultimately overt clinical cardiovascular disease. In this framework, advancing CKM stage may identify patients more likely to develop diffuse distal PAD, impaired collateralization, reduced tissue perfusion, and limb-threatening presentations. Because this article is hypothesis-generating, we do not quantitatively test interactions between CKM stage and Lp(a). We propose this as an important priority for future cohort-based analyses.

Diabetes and insulin resistance are major determinants of atherosclerotic risk and are considered in traditional risk assessment and treatment approaches. Indeed, clinical guidelines recommend more aggressive lipid lowering in diabetic patients [29]. From a mechanistic standpoint, diabetes and insulin resistance impair lipolysis, elevate free fatty acids and VLDL levels, are associated with the formation of small dense LDL particles, endothelial dysfunction, and increase oxidative stress, and inflammation within blood vessels walls. These factors accelerate plaque development and worsen the plaque phenotype and lead to vascular remodeling. Imaging and pathology work demonstrate that diabetic plaque is rich in macrophages [30] and often have thin fibrous caps which are associated with a high risk of future events [31]. Diabetes also leads to diffuse, distal and microvascular atherosclerosis. These changes lead to worse outcomes [32] for both vascular and coronary disease. In addition to these effects, diabetes is also associated with poor collateral development [33,34] leading to larger myocardial infarcts and to a greater risk of developing congestive heart failure [35]. It is also reported that the risk-reducing effect of revascularization on amputation/mortality appears attenuated in the presence of CKD, helping explain why PAD outcomes are worse when kidney disease coexists. In a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 observational studies including 554,270 PAD patients, CKD was associated with nearly double the risk of amputation (pooled effect 1.94, 95% CI 1.90–1.97) compared with no CKD. The same meta-analysis found ∼2× higher mortality in PAD patients with CKD (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.99–2.08), with a graded increase in both amputation and mortality as CKD severity worsened [36]. The CKM construct also has direct therapeutic relevance in PAD. The 2024 ACC/AHA PAD guideline recommends GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with PAD and type 2 diabetes to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events. In the LEADER trial, a double-blind randomized controlled trial testing liraglutide versus placebo in 9340 diabetic patients, liraglutide reduced a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction and nonfatal stroke [liraglutide group (13.0%) than in the placebo group (14.9%), HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.78 to 0.97]. In addition, liraglutide was also associated with fewer diabetic foot ulcer-related amputations (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.95) [37]. For SGLT2 inhibitors, randomized-trial meta-analytic data do not show a clear overall PAD or increased amputation signal [38], although newer observational data suggest lower risks of incident PAD and PAD-related outcomes with SGLT-2 inhibitors versus DPP-4 inhibitors [39].

In diabetics/insulin-resistant patients, CAD is more diffuse and microvascular, less responsive to PCI (with higher restenosis/need for repeat procedures), more likely to include unrecognized (“silent”) infarction, and long-term medication adherence is suboptimal. Diabetic patients are also more likely to develop silent myocardial infarctions. These findings need to be considered in light of the dramatic increases in the incidence of obesity, diabetes and insulin resistance being seen in the US. This epidemic is likely the main contributor to the deceleration of cardiovascular and overall life expectancy being seen in America today.

The social determinants of health

According to the World Health Organization, “the social determinants of health (SDOH) are the non-medical factors that influence health outcomes. They are the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, and the wider set of forces and systems shaping the conditions of daily life” [40,41].

In addition to the effects of Lp(a) and metabolic issues, we believe SDOH challenges are a major reason why ASCVD, specifically PAD remains such a critical issue. Not only do these challenges increase disease incidence, but they also alter phenotype presentation, the severity of disease on presentation and the outcomes of interventions. Specific issues include lifestyle decisions, suboptimal adherence to medical treatment, and sociodemographic issues. The presence of structural bias, poverty, low health literacy, food and housing insecurity, and lack of access to healthcare and social services can all lead to atherosclerosis. Individuals with social risk factors and harmful behaviors often develop chronic health conditions which cause 7 of 10 deaths among Americans and consume 70% of the cost of care [42].

Powell-Wiley et al. have proposed a conceptual framework in which important SDOH challenges lead to psychosocial and environmental stress which in turn leads to chronic stress and the development of atherosclerotic risk factors. This leads to the development of atherosclerotic vascular diseases characterized by poor outcomes [43]. For example, patients with PAD and SDOH have disproportionately higher rates of major limb amputations [44]. Similarly, patients living far from hospitals are more likely to present with the worst form of PAD [45]. Studies show patients from lower socioeconomic strata are more likely to present later after symptom onset, have less access to primary PCI, and receive lower use of guideline-based therapies. These issues contribute to larger myocardial infarcts, lower post event ejections fractions and higher in-hospital mortality even after adjusting for comorbidities. Social deprivation is also strongly associated with higher amputation rates and lower likelihood of limb-saving revascularization. Black patients in the U.S. have ∼2× higher PAD-related amputation risk than White patients, largely mediated by SDOH and access to care, not biology/genetics. Black patients remain at a higher risk of limb loss, even after undergoing peripheral arterial bypass operations, suggesting that they present with more aggressive form of disease [46]. Electronic medical record (EMR) systems already collect most of the SDOH factors and the proposed SDOH risk score could be incorporated fairly expeditiously into clinical practice to improve screening for high-risk individuals.

Integrating these factors and future directions

Existing risk models such as the pooled cohort equations or PREVENT estimate the absolute risk of cardiovascular events. In contrast, the proposed risk-stacked framework seeks to explain heterogeneity in disease phenotype and severity, particularly the emergence of diffuse small vessel disease and limb-threatening ischemia. By integrating genetic susceptibility (Lp[a]), cardiometabolic disease (CKM), and SDOH, the framework emphasizes biologic interaction and disease presentation, not simply risk prediction. The key new concept presented is that the convergence of genetic (Lp[a]), CKM (chronic kidney disease/diabetes/insulin resistance), and social (SDOH) risk layers, effect disease presentation, accelerates atherosclerosis onset and worsens event severity. We propose that these risk domains interact biologically and socially rather than operating independently to alter ASCVD risk, presentation and disease severity. It is important to note that the SDOH modifies both biological and behavioral pathways amplifying inflammation, metabolic stress, and delaying care access.

This 'risk stack' framework highlights important points for intervention. First Lp(a) screening should be performed in all adults at least once in their lifetime. Even before novel RNA-based therapies are clinically available efforts to aggressively address other risk factors should be entertained. With regards to diabetes and IR, aggressive management of chronic blood sugar markers and lipid indices and efforts to lower environmental stress including dietary interventions and structured regular exercise regiments to slow the atherosclerotic process and enhance muscle flow capacity. With regards to SDOH, systematic efforts to address and attenuate these issues are critical to managing diabetes and ASCVD outcomes and patient quality of life. Digital health technologies may provide scalable tools to mitigate some of the SDOH barriers faced by patients with stacked risk factors. Telemedicine and mobile health platforms can facilitate remote monitoring of metabolic risk factors, improve medication adherence, and enhance physical activity engagement among high-risk patients. Several recent studies using mobile applications and wearable technologies have demonstrated improvements in cardiovascular risk metrics, physical activity, and physiologic parameters such as heart rate variability in high-risk patients. [47,48] Such approaches may be particularly valuable for patients with PAD who face geographic barriers to specialty care. These efforts will require coordination of efforts across payers, health systems and providers.

Conceptually, the proposed “Risk Stack Score” would integrate variables across the three domains and not replace the CKM or PREVENT equation. The genetic layer would include Lp(a) concentrations and potentially polygenic risk scores. The metabolic layer would include CKM staging variables such as diabetes status, insulin resistance, and chronic kidney disease. The environmental layer would include validated SDOH metrics such as social vulnerability index or neighborhood deprivation indices as well as responses to SDOH self-assessment surveys [49]. Initial modeling could use Cox proportional hazards models with interaction terms to test additive and multiplicative effects on PAD incidence, MALE, and mortality, followed by external validation in large cohort datasets. A future ‘Risk Stack Score’ should be developed as a PAD-specific prognostic model rather than as a general ASCVD risk tool. Candidate predictors would ideally span three domains central to our proposed framework [1]: genetic risk, including measured Lp(a) concentration and, where available, LPA genotype or polygenic risk [2]; CKM burden, including diabetes status, hemoglobin A1c, chronic kidney disease, obesity, hypertension, smoking, and established CKM stage; and [3] social/environmental risk, including insurance instability, area deprivation, transportation barriers, food insecurity, health literacy, and delayed presentation to specialty care. Because PAD outcomes such as MALE, amputation, and death are competing and clinically distinct events, initial model development could use time-to-event methods such as Cox proportional hazards models for all-cause outcomes and competing-risk models for limb-specific outcomes, with prespecified interaction terms to test whether Lp(a), CKM burden, and SDOH act additively or multiplicatively. More flexible machine-learning approaches may be explored in secondary analyses, but any such approach should be benchmarked against transparent regression-based models.” Any future Risk Stack Score would require rigorous assessment of both discrimination and calibration before clinical use.

Importantly, these three risk layers may interact through shared inflammatory pathways. Adverse SDOH drive chronic HPA axis activation and glucocorticoid resistance, generating sustained elevation of interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 in turn activates the LPA gene promoter, potentially amplifying Lp(a) concentrations above genetically determined baselines, while simultaneously potentiating Lp(a)- and OxPL-associated cardiovascular risk — an interaction recently demonstrated in secondary prevention patients where Lp(a)-associated MACE risk was only significant in those with elevated IL-6 (HR 1.18; p-interaction = 0.01) [50]. CKM-related endothelial dysfunction and impaired collateral formation compound this environment. The result is a biologically integrated syndrome in which social adversity amplifies genetic susceptibility (Fig. 1) [43].

In closing, we believe an important research effort would be to understand and to quantify the potential additive or synergistic effects of the 3 levels of risk described. This would require using established data sets including ARIC, MESA, WHS, REGARDS, and UK Biobank; integrate SDOH data to model the impact of these factors on phenotypic presentation and disease severity. In the end, we need to develop a validated, data-driven 'Risk Stack Score' to inform targeted prevention, treatment as well as community-level action.
