Distribution of serogroup and pathogenicity Island markers of uropathogenic Escherichia coli from inpatients in a tertiary care hospital
Authors: Yalda Malekzadegan, Ali Abdi, Reza Khashei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) are associated with considerable prolonged hospital stay and morbidity worldwide. This work was proposed to study Pathogenicity Island (PAI) markers (as an element containing some important virulence genes) and O-antigen serogroups related to UPECs obtained from inpatients with urinary disorders. This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on a total of 126 no repetitive UPEC isolates from three Shiraz teaching hospitals. Isolates were identified using standard microbiological procedures. The PAIs and serogroups genes were screened using PCR.ResultsO-serogroups were identified in 67 (53.1%) of the UPEC isolates. Overall, serotype O25 had the highest incidence (n = 26; 20.8%). Serogroups of O4, O6, O7, O16 and O21 were not detected in our isolates. All of isolates carried at least one of the examined PAI markers. PAI IV536 was the most prevalent PAI (97; 77.6%), followed by PAI II536 (32; 25.6%), PAI III536 (13; 10.4%) and PAI I536 (12; 9.6%). Among serogroups, O25 showed the highest frequency among PAIs.ConclusionThe results of current study enhance the understanding of the importance of serogroups and PAI that could be a beneficial tool for detecting pathogenic UPEC which can be used to develop treatments and prevention strategies for UTIs.Keywords: Uropathogenic Escherichia coli, O-serogroups, Pathogenicity Islands, Urinary tract infection

Introduction

Extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC), are one of the most predominant Gram-negative infectious agents causing extraintestinal infections [1, 2]. As a common pathotype of ExPEC, uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) strains have been associated with more than 80% of community-acquired cystitis, more than 70% of uncomplicated acute pyelonephritis, and 3.6–12.6% of complicated UTIs (cUTIs) with urosepsis [3, 4]. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are associated with prolonged hospital stay and estimated annual incidence of at least 150 million morbidities across the world [4, 5]. The use of medical devices, such as urinary catheters, has been associated with incidence of UTIs in hospital environments [4].

Pathogenic and commensal strains of E. coli contain surface antigens, including O (lipopolysaccharide), H (flagellum), and in some cases K (capsule) that are involved in serological typing [5]. In the 1940s, Fritz Kaufmann and colleagues identified the E. coli serotyping scheme and so far, more than 187 different serogroups have been described [6]. O-antigen as a main component has been used for serotyping of clinical strains of UPEC which are mostly related to serogroups O1, O2, O4, O6, O7, O8, O15, O16, O18, O21, O22, O25, O75, and O83 [4, 6].

UPEC strains have virulence genes encoding adhesins, toxins, iron acquisition mechanisms, serum resistance, and capsule on pathogenicity islands (PAIs) that allow them to cause infection [7]. These virulence determinants facilitate bacteria adherence to and invasion of the uroepithelial cells and overcome the host immunity, leading to the development of UTIs [7, 8]. PAIs and their virulence genes disseminate through horizontal gene transfer by transduction, conjugation, and transformation mechanisms [7, 9]. Different PAIs among UPEC strains include PAI I536, PAI II536, PAI III536, PAI IV536, PAI ICFT073, PAI IICFT073, PAI IJ96 and PAI IIJ96 [8], in which for example, PAIs [I to IV] from strain 536 encode fimbriae (P-fimbriae, P-related and S fimbriae, α-hemolysin, iron siderophore system, yersiniabactin siderophore system, CS12 fimbriae, and F17-like fimbrial adhesion) [9].

As important virulence genes of the UPEC strains are located on PAIs and also some serogroups are related to more pathogenic strains; therefore, this work was proposed to study pathogenicity island markers and O-antigen serogroups related to UPEC isolates obtained from inpatients with different urinary disorders in three teaching hospitals.

Methods

Study design and bacterial isolates

This retrospective study was conducted on a total of 126 nonrepetitive UPEC isolates from a previous study [10]. Inclusion criteria consist of hospitalized patients with symptomatic UTI, including cystitis, pyelonephritis and urosepsis and exclusion criteria were patients with asymptomatic UTI. This study was in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and an ethical permission was sought from the institutional by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1398.129). As in the current work only leftovers from clinical specimens were used (from previous study with Approval No. IR.SUMS.REC.1395.S77), the institutional ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences waived the need for informed consent.

Preparation of template DNA

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted by thermal lysis method and used for all PCR reactions [5]. The DNA quality was confirmed based on the OD 260/280 ratio using a NanoDrop. Prepared template DNAs were stored at − 20 ◦C.

Characterization of the lipopolysaccharide O antigens by polymerase chain reaction

All of the UPEC isolates were subjected to monoplex PCR assays for detection of O1, O2, O4, O6, O7, O8, O15, O16, O18, O21, O22, O25, O75, and O83 serogroups with the specific primers as described by Oskouie et al. [1]. PCR was performed with a Bio-Rad thermal cycler T100™ (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following conditions: Initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min was followed by 30 cycles of primer annealing at 55 °C for O1, 58 °C for O2 and O25, and 56 °C for O4 and O16 for 30 s, as well as an extension at 72 °C for 30 s and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplification products were analyzed using 1.5% agarose gel with KBC power load dye (CinnaGen Co. Iran).

Detection of pathogenicity Islands markers by polymerase chain reaction

Using the mentioned primers [11], four PAIs (PAI I536, PAI II536, PAI III536, and PAI IV536) were investigated. The conditions of PCR amplification were applied according to Sabaté et al. [12]. Briefly, the program for PCR was consisted of 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 1 min and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR reactions were tested using UPEC strains 536 as PAI marker controls.

Statistical analyses

Chi-square test using SPSS™ software, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA) was performed to determine the statistical significance of the data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Among our archive (126 non-duplicate UPEC isolates), the frequencies of male and female was 50 and 76, respectively. Among them, 42 (33.3%) patients suffered from cystitis, 72 (57.1%) patients with pyelonephritis, and 12 (8.7%) afflicted by urosepsis. Mean age of patients were 48.9 ± 28.8 years.

According to our findings, distribution of O-serogroups in the UPEC isolates were 67 (53.1%). Therefore, 59 (46.8%) isolates that could not be assigned to different serogroups were categorized as non-detected serogroups (NDS). Overall, serotype O25 had the highest incidence (n = 26; 20.8%), followed by O8 and O15 (n = 8; 6.4%), O83 (n = 7; 5.6%), and O2 and O18 (n = 5; 4%). Frequencies of O1, O22 and O75 were less than 5%. Serogroups of O4, O6, O7, O16 and O21 were not detected in our isolates and thereby belong to NDS. There was no significant difference between the presence of O-serogroups and clinical diseases (cystitis, pyelonephritis and urosepsis).

The four pathogenicity islands were identified in UPEC isolates using PCR. All of isolates carried at least one of the examined PAI markers. Of the four PAIs identified among isolates, PAI IV536 was the most prevalent PAI (97; 77.6%), followed by PAI II536 (32; 25.6%), PAI III536 (13; 10.4%) and PAI I536 (12; 9.6%). There was a significant difference between the presence of PAI III536 and three types of UTI (p < 0.05). Among serogroups, O25 showed the highest frequency among PAIs (Table 1). So that PAI II536 and PAI IV536 had the most common pattern (17.5%). Simultaneously presence of two and three PAIs was found among 46 and 2 UPEC isolates, respectively. Discussion

UTI is a common infectious disease both in the hospital and the community that UPEC is the cause of 80% of them [13]. In the current study, 126 UPEC isolates which were previously recovered from inpatients with pyelonephritis, cystitis and urosepsis, evaluated to presence of O-serogroup and PAI. UPEC possess a wide range of virulence factors, including PAIs that move with horizontal gene transfer mechanisms [14]. In line with this study, several investigations indicated the role of UPEC as the principal pathogen responsible for UTIs [1, 5, 6, 15–17]. UTI in women is more frequent than men with an estimated rate of 50–60% of women suffering from at least one episode during their lifetime [18]. We found 60.3% of patients were female that is accordance with some previous studies [1, 3, 18, 19]. The UTI higher incidence in women might be influenced by intercourse related issues, such as shortness urethra, poor hygiene, sexual activity and the usage of spermicides [3, 18].

O-antigen as somatic antigen is made up of repeated oligosaccharide units which is used to classify and serogrouping UPEC strains [17]. Among fourteen serogroups assessed in the present survey, nine of them, including O25, O8, O15, O83, O2, O18, O1, O22 and O75 detected that O25 was the most common serogroup (20.8%), while O4, O6, O7, O16 and O21 were not determined. In agreement with our results, O25, O15, O8, and O75 were the most commonly detected O-serogroups among the UPECs isolated from northwest of Iran [1]. According to several investigations in Iran, O25 was the most common serogroup in UPEC isolates which is line with our results [5, 20–22]. Therefore, it seems that O25 as a common serotype plays an important role in causing UTI which should be considered. Several research also in Iran [5], Iraq [6, 17], Lithuania [3], and Germany [23] have reported O1, O8, O15 and O6 as the most frequently isolated serogroups.

In the present survey there was no significant difference between the presence of O-serogroup and three clinical diseases observed, while in the study of Mostafavi et al.., O25 and O1 were significantly higher than other serogroups in pyelonephritis [5]. Likewise, in a study conducted in Lithuania O15 and O22 were prevalent serogroups among pyelonephritis cases [3]. Variability in O serogroups distribution could be due to differences in type of infection, region, or even type of settings (hospital or community) [5].

UPEC isolates display a wide genomic variety, due to their multiple virulence factors transmitted with horizontal gene transfer, including PAIs [24]. In our research, all of the isolates were carried at least two of the examined PAI markers. PAI IV536 is known to be the most common PAI in UPEC strains [7–9, 12, 13, 21, 25–27]. Similarly, we found that PAI IV536 was the most prevalent PAI (77.6%). A notable point in our study was the low prevalence of PAI I536, which is common in all the aforementioned studies. However, the rates of PAIs in some investigations were different from our data. For example, in study of Navidinia et al., and Sabaté and co-workers, the rate of PAI III536 was lower than our study [12, 28]. Conversely, high frequency of PAI I536 (55%) was reported in Brazil [11]. In our study, PAI II536 was the second most prevalent PAI that was dissimilar to a study from Turkey [25]. This variation could be caused by difference in size, gene arrangement, and sequence of PAIs among the different isolates in the various regions [7].

Moreover, we found some isolates carry simultaneously different PAIs. Similarly, this finding has been reported by Niakan and colleagues in Tehran, Iran [13]. PAI II536 and PAI IV536 were the most frequent pattern (17.5%) in the current study, but in Niakan et al., study [13], PAI III536 and PAI IV536 were higher among other PAI combination patterns (3%). The high rate of PAI IV536 which is termed to high-pathogenicity island (HPI), is due to its stability and fixed on the chromosome unlike the other PAIs that are unstable and easily lose [9]. Various virulence genes are located on PAI, including iron uptake system, toxins and fimberial adhesins that seems to be important for UPEC fitness, efficacious colonization and successful survival in the urinary tract. Therefore, strains that express several PAIs at the same time can be more pathogenic in comparison to strains that express one of them [26].

To our knowledge, the current study is the first work in southwest of Iran that characterizes the pathogenicity island markers and O-antigen serogroups related to UPEC isolates in different disease groups and warrants further intensive researches. The main limitation of the present study is that other O-serogroups and PAIs due to financial limitations are not investigated that should be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

Strain O-serogrouping can be used as an epidemiological tool. This study showed that O25, O15 and O8 were the most prevalent serogroups amongst UPEC isolates. Indeed, PAI IV536 was the most common PAI determinant in our UPEC isolates. The results of current study enhance the understanding and importance of different serogroups and PAIs involved in pathogenesis of UPEC isolates.
