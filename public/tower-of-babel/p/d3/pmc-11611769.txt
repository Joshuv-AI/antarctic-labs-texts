Prevalence and outcomes of germline pathogenic variants of homologous recombination repair genes in ovarian cancer
Authors: Maiko Miwa, Masakazu Kitagawa, Yuka Asami, Mayumi Kobayashi‐Kato, Takafumi Watanabe, Aiko Ogasawara, Kengo Hiranuma, Hisamori Kato, Motonobu Saito, Yohei Miyagi, Tomoyasu Kato, Hiroshi Yoshida, Yukihide Momozawa, Takashi Kohno, Kouya Shiraishi, Kosei Hasegawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGermline pathogenic variants (PVs) are pivotal in gynecological oncology. We focused on the prevalence, clinicopathological features, and survival impact of homologous recombination repair (HRR) PVs in patients with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC). This was a multicenter retrospective cohort study, and 1248 patients with EOC were registered. Eligible patients (n = 1112) underwent germline DNA analysis for 26 cancer predisposition genes, including nine HRR‐related genes, such as BRCA1/2, BRIP1, PALB2, RAD51C/D, and ATM. The associations between clinicopathological factors and HRR‐related PVs were examined. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were conducted. Among 1091 analyzed patients, 153 (14.0%) carried PVs and 140 (12.8%) were HRR‐related. HRR‐PV‐positive status significantly correlated with serous carcinoma (22.9% vs. 4.8%, P < 0.0001) and advanced disease (18.5% vs. 5.9%, P < 0.0001). The HRR‐PV‐positive group exhibited higher prevalence of personal breast (12.9%) and familial breast/ovarian (29.2%) cancer history. HRR status independently improved overall survival in stage III/IV disease (P = 0.04) but not progression‐free survival. HRR‐related germline PVs exhibit distinct clinicopathological features with survival implications. Variants were significantly associated with serous carcinoma and advanced disease, underscoring the importance of genetic testing to develop individualized EOC treatment strategies. Considering the study period (2000–2019), the limited use of bevacizumab and poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitors as maintenance therapy should be recognized.Keywords: epithelial ovarian cancer, germline pathogenic variant, homologous recombination repair, progression‐free survival, regression analyses

INTRODUCTION

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) accounted for approximately 324,398 new cases in 2022, with 206,839 annual deaths worldwide. EOC is the eighth most common cause of cancer‐related mortality among women globally. 1 Reportedly, approximately 10%–15% of all EOCs originate from germline BRCA pathogenic variants (PVs), 2 , 3 and in the high‐grade serous subtype, germline BRCA PVs are found in approximately 20%–25% of cases. 4 , 5

Advances in next‐generation sequencing have identified 11 genes associated with hereditary ovarian cancer. 6 The American Society of Clinical Oncology Guidelines propose the evaluation of at least six homologous recombination repair (HRR)‐related genes, including BRCA1, BRCA2, RAD51C, RAD51D, BRIP1, and PALB2, and four mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency‐related genes, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. 7 Of these genes, BRCA1, BRCA2, RAD51C, RAD51D, and BRIP1 are related to DNA HRR pathways. HRR pathway deficiency (homologous recombination deficiency) is caused by their loss of function. Patients with germline BRCA1/BRCA2 PVs have better prognoses because they respond better to chemotherapy. Bolton et al. reported that patients with germline PVs in BRCA1 or BRCA2 showed improved 5‐year overall survival (OS; 36% in non‐carriers, 44% in BRCA1 carriers, and 52% in BRCA2 carriers). 8 The SOLO‐2 study, a double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled, phase 3 trial, confirmed that olaparib provided a survival benefit compared with placebo of approximately 12.9 months in patients with platinum‐sensitive, relapsed ovarian cancer and a BRCA1/2 PV. 9 Other clinical trials have shown that treatment with poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) results in significant survival benefits, particularly in patients with BRCA1/2 PVs and/or homologous recombination deficiency. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 However, there is limited information on the relationship between these non‐BRCA HRR‐related PVs and clinical features. Additionally, there are limited data on non‐HRR‐related gene variants.

Next‐generation DNA sequencing technology has enabled simultaneous analysis of multiple genes in a short time. Studies have identified RAD51D, PTEN, TP53, BRIP1, RAD51C, MSH6, and MSH2 PVs as high risk for ovarian and breast cancers. 16 Additionally, germline PVs in 12 breast and/or ovarian cancer susceptibility genes have been studied; for genes other than BRCA1/2, the mutation frequencies were ≤1.0%, with only PALB2 showing significant association with risk (OR 5.79, 95% CI 1.09–30.83). The corresponding HRs for ovarian cancer–specific death were 0.61 (95% CI 0.34–1.11) for BRCA1 and 0.82 (95% CI 0.51–1.29) for BRCA2. 17

An analysis of germline variants among patients with ovarian cancer revealed that those with germline mutations, particularly BRCA 1/2 PVs, had significantly longer OS. Moreover, PVs in genes other than BRCA1/2, including non‐HRR related genes such as MSH6, BRIP1, PMS2, TP53, and MLH1, were studied. 18

The clinical features of hereditary PVs in ovarian cancer predisposition genes other than BRCA1/2 are not well known.

We aimed to determine the frequency of genes associated with cancer susceptibility among patients with EOC, identify the clinical characteristics linked to these genetic PVs, and evaluate their impact on prognosis by HRR status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients, samples, and clinical data

This was a multicenter retrospective cohort study. Patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer, fallopian tube cancer, or primary peritoneal cancer between January 2000 and December 2019 at four medical centers in Japan were included. A total of 1248 patients from whom >500 ng of germline DNA was extracted were eligible.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center Research (approval number: 2015‐278) and by each participating institution (Saitama Medical University International Medical Center: 18‐046; Kanagawa Cancer Center: 2017‐74; Fukushima Medical University: 29275).

Multigene panel testing

Germline DNA was extracted from the patient's blood or tissue using the Qiagen FlexiGene DNA Kit, the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit, or the Qiagen Allprep DNA/RNA mini kit. We analyzed 26 known cancer predisposition genes included in the Myriad myRisk Hereditary Cancer Test (ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, CHEK2, NBN, NF1, PALB2, PTEN, STK11, and TP53 and APC, BARD1, BMPR1A, BRIP1, CDK4, CDKN2A, EPCAM, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, PMS2, RAD51C, RAD51D, and SMAD4). We examined all exonic regions and five adjacent base pairs of intronic sequences from the 26 genes. Detailed experiments were performed according to previous papers. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Genome mapping was performed using Parabricks v3.1.3 (Nvidia). 23 GRCh38 was used as the reference genome. The pipeline used in this study implements algorithms equivalent to those of BWA (v0.7.15) 24 and GATK (v4.1.0). BAM files were exported from the read mapping. 25 Germline variants were called using the HaplotypeCaller in GATK.

Variant classification of germline variants

We annotated the clinical significance of each variant using the known clinical significance in ClinVar, population data, computational data, and functional data following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines. Germline variants were considered pathogenic if they fulfilled the following criteria: (1) global minor allele frequency <0.005 in ExAC4 and/or Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (ToMMo)5; (2) null variants (nonsense, frameshift indel, and splice‐site variants) and missense variants classified as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” in the ClinVar 6 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/); and (3) high‐impact variants, such as stop‐gain, stop‐loss, and start‐loss defined by SnpEff v4.37. Finally, an expert panel identified the pathogenic variants. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were further validated using Sanger sequencing.

Statistical analysis

Each clinical factor and HRR PV status (HRR‐positive or HRR‐negative) was analyzed using a t‐test or chi‐squared test. OS was defined as the number of days from the date of treatment initiation to death from any cause. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was calculated as the time between treatment initiation and tumor progression or death from any cause. They were plotted as Kaplan–Meier survival curves. The log‐rank test was used to examine the significance of differences in survival between groups. The effects of various factors on OS and PFS were calculated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models using JMP17.1 (SAS Institute. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. We performed univariate and multivariate survival analyses using a Cox proportional hazards regression model.

A univariate and multivariable Cox regression model was fitted with PFS as the outcome and the following predictors: age at diagnosis, HR status (positive or negative), International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, primary treatment (primary debulking surgery [PDS] or interval debulking surgery [IDS]), and residual tumor on PDS or IDS. Analogous analyses of OS were also conducted.

RESULTS

Frequencies of germline pathogenic variants

Among the 1091 patients with EOC tested, PVs in any of the 26 analyzed genes were found in 153 patients. Five patients had two PVs and 158 PVs were identified in this cohort. PVs in the 26 genes in the panel were categorized into HRR PV (BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, PALB2, RAD51D, RAD51C, BARD1, ATM, CHEK2) and non‐HRR PV (TP53, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM, MUTYH, NF1, APC, PTEN, BMPR1A, CDH1, CDH4, CDKN2A, NBN, SMAD4, STK11) groups. No PVs were detected in CHEK2, BMPR1A, CDH1, CDH4, CDKN2A, NBN, SMAD4, and STK11 in this cohort (Table S1). The details of the five patients with two PVs are as follows: BRCA1 and BRCA2, BRCA1 and RAD51, BRCA1 and MUTYH, RAD51C and NF1, BARD1 and TP53.

As shown in Table 1, Figure 2A, and Table S1, the HRR PVs were observed in 140 (12.8%) patients. BRCA1 (n = 74, 6.8%) was the most frequently observed PV, followed by BRCA2 (n = 38, 3.5%). Non‐BRCA HRR PVs were observed in 28 patients (2.6%). The most frequently observed gene in the non‐BRCA HRR group was RAD51D (10 patients), followed by ATM (seven patients). As for non‐HRR PVs, MMR genes, including EPCAM PVs, were detected in six patients (0.5%).

Association between HRR‐related PVs and clinicopathological factors

The frequency of pathogenic variants in each gene based on histology and FIGO stage are shown in Table S2. Tables 1 and 2 summarize the frequencies of PVs based on the clinical and pathological factors. HRR‐PV status was investigated for each factor. No associations were found between HRR‐PV positivity and age or body mass index. Higher serum CA125 levels were also observed in the HRR‐positive group than in the HRR‐negative group (883 U/mL vs. 311 U/mL, P = 0.0076). HRR PVs were mostly observed in serous carcinoma compared to other histological types (22.9% vs. 4.8%, P < 0.0001). Among 484 serous carcinoma patients, 65 (13.4%) and 31 (6.4%) had BRCA1 and BRCA2 PVs, respectively. Fifteen (3.0%) non‐BRCA HRR PVs were also observed in serous carcinoma patients. A higher frequency of HRR PVs was also observed in patients with stage III/IV disease (18.5% vs. 5.9%, P < 0.0001).

Survival analysis

HRR PVs may positively affect the survival of patients with EOC. We compared the survival between the two groups using the log‐rank test with Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

We compared patients with HRR‐related PVs (HRR‐positive group) with those without PVs. Because the HRR PVs were primarily observed in patients with stage III/IV disease, we performed survival analyses in stage III/IV ovarian cancer. Patients with Lynch syndrome and PVs in non‐HRR‐related genes were excluded from the analysis, since the biology of their cancer is different from that of patients without PVs. A total of 534 patients with stage III/IV disease were analyzed. The median follow‐up duration was 49 months. We identified 101 patients (18.7%) with HRR PVs. As shown in Figure 2B, no PFS difference was observed between the HRR‐positive and no PVs groups (P = 0.56). The median PFS in the HRR‐positive and no PVs groups was 22 and 18 months, respectively. By contrast, the median OS of the HRR‐positive group was significantly longer than that of the no PVs group (78 months vs. 65 months, 95% CI 1.034 to 1.913, P = 0.04) (Figure 2C).

We subsequently divided the population into three groups, (A) patients with PVs in BRCA1/2, (B) patients with PVs in HRR‐related genes other than BRCA1/2, and (C) patients without PVs, and performed survival analysis. As shown in Figure 3A,B, no PFS and OS difference was observed between the BRCA1/2 PVs group and the no PVs group, but the BRCA1/2 PVs group may have longer OS (78 months vs. 65 months, P = 0.05).

Additionally, we investigated the potential survival impact of BRCA1, BRCA2, non‐BRCA HRR, and non‐HRR PVs by comparing each group with patients without PVs. As shown in Figure S1, none of the PV groups showed statistically significant effects on PFS and OS compared to the group without PVs. However, BRCA1 and BRCA2 PVs may have longer OS (BRCA1 vs. no PVs 89 months vs. 65 months, P = 0.077; BRCA2 vs. no PVs 78 months vs. 65 months, P = 0.36). The number of patients with non‐BRCA HRR or non‐HRR in this analysis was limited for comparison.

DISCUSSION

Of the 1091 patients with EOC analyzed in this study, PVs in HRR‐related genes were observed in 140 (12.8%) patients and BRCA1/2 PVs were found in 112 (10.3%) patients. In stage III/IV disease, patients who had HRR‐related and BRCA1/2 PVs accounted for 18.4% and 15.3%, respectively. Serous carcinoma was identified in 74.5% (114/153) of the patients with PVs. In serous carcinoma, the prevalence of HRR‐related PVs and BRCA1/2 PVs was 22.9% (111/484) and 19.8% (96/484), respectively.

Patients with HRR‐related PVs exhibited improved OS compared to those without PVs, and multivariate analysis demonstrated that HRR‐related PVs were independent prognostic factors for OS in patients with stage III/IV disease. Regarding the prevalence of germline BRCA1/2 and/or HRR‐related PVs, Norquist et al. reported the prevalence of PVs of germline BRCA and other predisposing genes in ovarian cancer, using data from GOG218 and 262 trials. In these trials, 95.5% were stage III/IV patients and 14.6% of BRCA1/2 PVs were found in 1915 patients. They also reported that the frequencies of germline non‐BRCA HRR related genes and DNA mismatch repair genes were 3.3% and 0.4%, respectively. 26 The prevalence of BRCA1/2 and non‐BRCA HRR PVs in our study was similar to that reported previously. The prevalence of germline PVs may differ among ethnicities and/or regions.

No reports have addressed germline HRR‐related PV status in Japan. The CHARLOTTE study that investigated the prevalence of germline BRCA1/2 PVs in EOC in a Japanese multicenter study reported a relatively higher prevalence of BRCA1/2 PVs compared to that of the global population. That study found 14.7% (93/634) of germline BRCA1/2 PVs in EOC and 24.1% (78/324) of germline BRCA1/2 PVs in stage III/IV. 27 Our study demonstrated a relatively low frequency of BRCA1/2 PVs compared to that study. This may be due to the different backgrounds of the patients included in each study. In the CHARLOTTE study, the proportions of patients with a family history (first or second) of breast cancer and ovarian cancer were 18.6% and 5.7%, respectively, whereas in our study, they were 10.2% and 5.4%, respectively. It is possible that the backgrounds of the patients differed between the CHARLOTTE study and our study. CHARLOTTE may have included more patients with a family history of breast/ovarian cancer because this study was the first to evaluate germline BRCA PVs in patients with EOC in Japan.

Several studies have reported the prevalence of BRCA1 and BRCA2 germline PVs in patients with ovarian cancer and in their family histories. Alsop et al. reported that 75 of 141 (53.2%) patients with germline BRCA1/2 PVs had a family history of breast/ovarian cancer. 3 As mentioned previously, in the CHARLOTTE study, the proportion of patients with a familial history (first or second) of breast cancer and ovarian cancer was 18.6% and 5.7%, respectively. In BRCA1 or BRCA2 PVs patients, 64.5% had a familial history of breast/ovarian cancer. In our study, 32.1% of the patients with BRCA1/2 PVs had first or second familial breast/ovarian cancer history (P < 0.0001). In the HRR‐PV‐positive group, the prevalence of family cancer history was 29.3%. Although our study was retrospective, a high frequency of family cancer history was observed in BRCA1/2 PV‐positive patients. Careful examination of the family history of breast/ovarian cancer may lead to increased germline testing and identify more HRR‐PV‐positive patients.

HRR‐related PVs are more frequently observed in patients with serous carcinoma or stage III/IV disease, therefore we performed the survival analysis only in patients with stage III/IV disease. As shown in Figure 2B,C, patients who had HRR‐related gene PVs exhibited longer OS than those who did not (P = 0.04). Pennington et al. reported that germline BRCA1/2 and HRR PVs exhibited better OS compared with no HRR PVs in 367 patients (median survival 70 months vs. 41 months, P = 0.001, HR 0.5, 95% CI 0.4–0.8 and 59 months vs. 41 months, P = 0.05, HR 0.7, 95% CI 0.5–1.0). 28 Our data are consistent with those of the aforementioned study. The PFS of patients who had HRR‐related PVs and those who did not were similar in each study. This may indicate that patients with HRR‐related PVs have a better prognosis after first disease progression. These patients likely responded well to multiple chemotherapy regimens. In the present study, we compared the OS and PFS of BRCA1, BRCA2, non‐BRCA HRR‐related, and non‐HRR‐related PVs with those of patients without PVs. All groups showed better OS trends but were not statistically significant. Because of the small number of patients in each group, these findings are inconclusive, therefore it is important to analyze data from larger cohorts to establish treatment strategies for these groups.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, we were unable to distinguish the grade of serous carcinoma (high or low) in our cohort. Because low‐grade serous carcinoma was diagnosed as part of the serous borderline tumor, we believe that most serous carcinomas in our cohort were high grade. However, it is possible that the serous carcinomas in our study included some low‐grade serous carcinomas. Allison et al. reported outcomes of advanced stage serous carcinoma and the prevalence of low‐grade serous carcinoma was 4.3%. 29 Therefore, the influence of prognosis on low‐grade serous carcinoma was potentially minimal. Second, bevacizumab and olaparib were approved in the first‐line setting in Japan in 2013 and 2019, respectively. Almost none of the patients in our study received either PARPi or bevacizumab as first‐line treatment, therefore our PFS data may not reflect current practice. Last, due to the scope of our study, we did not exclude patients with somatic PVs in HRR‐related genes, as their inclusion could potentially confound the analysis.

HRR‐related PVs were identified in 12.8% of patients with EOC and 18.5% of those with stage III/IV disease. Germline HRR‐PV status impacts the prognosis of EOC and is considered an important indicator for treatment selection.
