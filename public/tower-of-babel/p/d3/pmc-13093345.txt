Recurrent Hypoglycemia in Two Late‐Preterm Infants With Transitional Disorder of Glucose Mobilization—Case Series
Authors: Suresh Chandran, Yu Shan Ting, Skanthakumar Abhirami, Fabian Yap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGreat emphasis is placed upon addressing hyperinsulinism in at‐risk infants with recurrent hypoglycemia. We report two preterm infants with recurrent nonhyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia due to transient impairment of glucose mobilization, warranting diazoxide use. Inadequate response to glucagon stimulation test indicates hepatic enzyme immaturity especially with negative genetic testing for glycogen storage disorders.Keywords: diazoxide, glucagon, hyperinsulinism, hypoglycemia, preterm, small‐for‐gestational‐age

Introduction

Hypoglycemia in the neonatal period is of immediate clinical concern, as glucose is the primary neuronal energy source. Severe or recurrent hypoglycemia is associated with seizures and neurological impairment [1]. However, there is a lack of consensus about the glucose level at which morbidities occur. The American Academy of Pediatrics and the Pediatric Endocrine Society recommend different thresholds for managing an infant at risk of hypoglycemia, and we opted for the latter guideline [2, 3]. Although neonatal hypoglycemia may be the first sign of severe underlying disease, it is mostly a transient benign phenomenon. As the etiology of hypoglycemia is not apparent at presentation, clinicians often depend on clinical circumstances to determine its origin. In addition to pathological causes of neonatal hypoglycemia, including hyperinsulinism, counter‐regulatory hormone deficiency, and glycogen storage disease (GSD), it is essential to consider the maturity of the infant as a contributor to low blood glucose. Although very low birth weight babies and those born before 27 weeks are at high risk of hypoglycemia, less preterm, and larger‐sized infants ought to do so.

Glucose is the principal energy substrate of a developing fetus, while glycogen stored in the liver is the primary source of rapidly available glucose in newborns [4]. Fetal growth and metabolism depend on the continuous transplacental glucose transfer while maintaining equilibrium with maternal glucose, a process coordinated by an interplay of hormones and enzymes [5]. Insulin, a dominant anabolic hormone in fetal life, activates glycogen synthase to promote glycogen synthesis and storage and inhibits hormone‐sensitive lipase to promote lipogenesis. By suppressing glucagon, a known activator of gluconeogenesis, insulin indirectly inhibits gluconeogenic enzyme activity in the liver [6]. Insulin action is modulated by corticosteroids, which regulate enzyme expressions required for glycogen synthesis. Glycogen deposition mainly accumulates in the third trimester when corticosteroid action increases progressively until term [7, 8]. Having adequate energy stores and an intact gluconeogenesis system, the healthy term infant can adapt to the interruption of placental glucose supply at birth to undergo a smooth metabolic transition. These mechanisms are compromised even in late preterm and small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) infants.

Newborns following cord ligation navigate through the glucose nadir in the first 2 h of life by activating counterregulatory hormones that oppose insulin action and promote glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and lipolysis, providing primary (glucose) and secondary (ketones) energy fuels to vital organs. Glycogenolysis produces glucose at 4–6 mg/kg/min in term infants by activating glycogen phosphorylase, depleting the stores from 50 to 5 mg/g of the liver within 12 h. In contrast, preterm and SGA infants have lower glycogen stores and depend on gluconeogenesis as the main pathway for glucose production [9].

Gluconeogenesis is particularly limited in preterm infants with low rate‐limiting enzyme cytosolic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase levels. Furthermore, hepatic glucose‐6‐phosphatase (G‐6‐Pase), a rate‐limiting enzyme for gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, is low in both the fetus and preterm infants, making them vulnerable to hypoglycemia [9, 10]. These delayed postnatal increments of enzyme activity need to be differentiated from GSD. Ketogenesis is also limited due to deficient fat stores (< 2% of body weight) [9, 11]. This increased susceptibility to hypoglycemia in preterm/SGA infants needs to be identified and corrected with close glucose monitoring to prevent the risk of brain injury [12].

The increasing use of diazoxide to treat hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia (HH) and a rise in severe drug‐induced adverse events have been increasingly reported [13, 14]. Biochemical confirmation of hyperinsulinism after 48 h of life in at‐risk infants requiring a high glucose infusion rate (GIR) to maintain normoglycemia is critical, and allowing time for spontaneous resolution is optimal. If non‐HH persists, evaluation for adequacy of feeds, weight gain, and intactness of hepatic enzyme systems to provide glucose is paramount. We illustrate two cases to depict a streamlined approach to prolonged non‐HH in preterm infants.

Case Report

Case 1

Case History/Investigation and Treatment

A 35 + 5‐week‐old male infant was born to a nonconsanguineous couple by cesarean section for fetal distress. The pregnancy was complicated with pre‐eclampsia at 30 weeks. A fetal scan at 35 weeks gestation showed growth restriction and oligohydramnios. Maternal serologies for intrauterine infections were unremarkable. Autoimmune workups were negative.

At 35 + 3 weeks, she was admitted in early labor and was hypertensive, requiring treatment. Two days later, fetal cardiotocography showed poor variability and unprovoked decelerations, which necessitated emergent delivery. The infant was asymmetrically SGA with a birth weight of 1600 g (< 1 percentile), an occipitofrontal circumference of 30 cm (10th percentile), and a length of 43 cm (11th percentile). Apgar scores were 9 at 1 and 5 min of life, respectively. Cord gases showed an arterial pH of 7.129 and a base excess of −4.2.

The baby had respiratory distress, requiring continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) support for the first 4 h of life. The postnatal course was complicated by recurrent asymptomatic hypoglycemia (Figure 1). The first feed was pasteurized donor human milk through an orogastric tube at 1 h of life. Capillary blood glucose (CBG) done at 2 h of life was unrecordable, requiring a 10% dextrose (2 mL/kg) intravenous mini bolus. An umbilical venous catheter was inserted, and dextrose infusion was continued with a maximum GIR of 10.3 mg/kg/min to maintain normoglycemia. Subsequently, feeds were gradually increased, and GIR was weaned. From days 7 to 23 of life, multiple attempts were made to increase enteral feeds and reduce GIR, but hypoglycemic episodes recurred each time the GIR was < 4 mg/kg/min. Infective markers, liver function tests, and inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) screen returned normal.

Three critical blood samples (Table 1) showed undetectable insulin levels while hypoglycemic, ruling out hyperinsulinism. As cortisol levels were inappropriately low, an ACTH stimulation test (synacthen) obtained an appropriate cortisol response of 684 nmol/L at 60 min, ruling out adrenal insufficiency. Even though the weight gain was 168 and 290 g from birth on days 16 and 23, respectively, the free fatty acid levels were low in the critical blood samples, rising to normal values on day 31. This temporal change could be explained by the baby's increasing body weight and rise in lipid stores. Despite normalizing free fatty acid levels on day 31, ketones remained low. Typically, ketone body catabolism generates acetyl‐CoA that is terminally oxidized within the tricarboxylic acid cycle; the inability to do so in this baby may signal the immaturity of the enzyme systems involved.

Milk feeds of the infant were fortified on Day 7 of life to increase the caloric content. The child was successfully weaned off intravenous glucose on Day 25 of life. However, the clinical course was complicated by blood‐streaked stools and was managed as Bell‐stage 1B necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). The eventual cause of the blood‐streaked stools was deemed to be secondary to cow's milk protein allergy with spontaneous resolution of hematochezia following gut rest for 3 days. His serial abdominal X‐rays and infective markers were unremarkable, and the stool and blood cultures were sterile. He was re‐established on feeds by Day 37 of life using breast milk following the elimination of cow's milk in the maternal diet.

On day 41, the infant weighed 2455 g and was on 180 mL/kg/day of milk feeds. A 6‐h safety fasting study (SFS) was conducted before discharge. At the third hour of fasting, he developed hypoglycemia with a plasma glucose level of 2.1 mmol/L and undetectable insulin, failing the fasting study. A glucagon stimulation challenge was then done during the episode of hypoglycemia. A measure of 0.5 mg of intramuscular glucagon was administered but showed an inadequate glycemic response, as shown in Table 2. A diagnosis of GSD was considered. However, further blood tests did not reveal hyperuricemia or hypercholesterolemia, and genetic testing (Invitae [USA] hypoglycemia panel) yielded no disease‐causing variants. Hence, the cause for the failed glucagon stimulation test was attributed to hepatic enzyme immaturity.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The infant was converted to 2‐hourly fortified breastmilk feeds. On day 47 of life, the child had gained 946 g and was discharged home with stable CBG, with emergency action plans with parents for hypoglycemia. At home, parents monitored CBG while on 2‐hourly feeds. He was reviewed on day 78 of life and had gained 2300 g since birth. He was successfully converted to 3‐hourly feeds, and after 3 weeks, his glucose profile remained stable during a 6‐h resolution fasting study (RFS).

At 15 months of age, his weight was 9.2 kg (15th percentile), head circumference was 47 cm (56th percentile), and height was 76 cm (11th percentile), with neurodevelopmental assessment appropriate for age.

Case 2

Case History/Investigation and Treatment

A female infant was born at 36 + 5 weeks to a nonconsanguineous couple. Family history, antenatal events, maternal serology, and fetal scans were unremarkable. Paramedics conducted delivery at home with Apgar scores of 9 at 1 and 5 min of life, respectively. Her growth parameters were a birth weight of 2385 g (19th percentile), occipitofrontal circumference of 30 cm (third percentile), and length of 46 cm (31st percentile). The baby was latched on to the breast soon after birth.

On admission to the special care nursery at 60 min of age, 25 mL of formula milk was given. A CBG check at 2 h was unrecordable. An intravenous mini bolus of 10% dextrose and continuous infusion were provided. Recurrent asymptomatic hypoglycemic episodes complicated her immediate neonatal period (Figure 2), requiring glucose boluses and escalation of GIR to 10 mg/kg/min. Gradual attempts to reduce GIR with increments of feeds failed on multiple occasions. On Day 7, critical blood samples were collected during an episode of hypoglycemia (Table 1). Biochemical results showed inappropriate insulin levels with normal cortisol and growth hormone response. Screening for infective markers was negative. Her newborn screening for IEM was normal, and liver function tests were unremarkable. She was commenced on diazoxide (3 mg/kg/day) and hydrochlorothiazide (1 mg/kg/day) and subsequently increased the diazoxide dose stepwise to 9 mg/kg/day.

With stable glucose levels and a weight gain of 154 g while on 168 mL/kg/day of milk feeds, a 6‐h SFS was done on Day 17 of life, which failed at the fourth hour of the fast. Critical blood samples on termination of SFS showed appropriate insulin response. She was restarted on enteral feeds and remained stable on milk feeds. On Day 21 of life, she developed feed intolerance with abdominal distention, suggestive of NEC Bell stage 1A. Vital signs were stable. Diazoxide was discontinued, feeds were switched to parenteral nutrition, and antibiotics were initiated. Hemograms, infective markers, and abdominal X‐rays were unremarkable. Symptoms subsided within 48 h, and feeds restarted after 5 days of gut rest. She could tolerate full feeds over 5 days with no episodes of hypoglycemia. She gained 450 g by day 32 of life. She underwent a 6‐h SFS, which she failed again at the fourth hour, but blood samples showed appropriate insulin response, ruling out HH (Table 1).

On day 33 of life, a glucagon stimulation test failed to show an appropriate response by raising glucose levels > 1 mmol/L, suggesting impaired gluconeogenesis/glycogenolysis (Table 2). Ultrasound scans of the hepatobiliary system, liver function tests, and infective markers were unremarkable. Genetic testing for GSD (Invitae panel) was negative. Her glucose profile remained stable while on 2‐hourly feeds, and she was discharged with home glucose monitoring on day 37 of life.

Outcome and Follow Up

On follow‐up, she remained well with normal CBG and adequate weight gain. When reviewed on day 64 of life, her weight was 4500 g (25–50th centile), head circumference was 37 cm (10–25th centile), and length was 56.9 cm (50–75th centile). She was moved to 3‐hourly feeds, and in 2 weeks, with stable glucose levels, she passed a 6‐h RFS.

She was seen at 8 months of age with a weight of 7.4 kg (25–50th percentile), head circumference of 43 cm (25–50th percentile), and length of 69 cm (50–75th percentile). Her neurodevelopmental assessment was within normal limits.

Discussion

These two cases serve as a reminder that hypoglycemia in preterm infants is not necessarily always due to hyperinsulinism. The glucagon stimulation tests established the inability of these late preterm infants with adequate weight gain to raise glucose levels. Since testing using an extended genetic hypoglycemia panel for GSD was negative, we postulate that the transient inability to release stored glucose may be the mechanism behind this phenotype in these late preterm infants.

We described two preterm infants with recurrent hypoglycemia, requiring high GIR to maintain normoglycemia. Although hyperinsulinism among preterm infants may be severe and persistent [15], HH in this high‐risk group is often transient and resolves spontaneously in most of them with early and adequate feeding and monitoring. Even though case 2 met the HH criteria initially, biochemical resolution of hyperinsulinism was demonstrated on Day 17. In both infants, appropriate insulin suppression while hypoglycemic was established. The glucagon stimulation test failed to achieve a rise in glucose levels by ≥ 1 mmol/L, consistent with impairment of glucose mobilization [16]. Genetic testing (Invitae hypoglycemia panel; 120 genes) did not reveal any mutations causing hypoglycemia, especially congenital hepatic enzyme deficiency, that is, GSD. The spontaneous resolution of hypoglycemic episodes over 2–3 months following frequent feeding and adequate weight gain showed the restitution of glycogen stores and recovery of physiologically delayed maturation of hepatic enzymes over time in these late preterm infants.

Although SGA and prematurity are major risk factors for transient and prolonged forms of HH, hypoglycemia is not necessarily pathological but rather due to physiologic hepatic immaturity and deficient energy stores. Hume et al. showed that preterm infants with recurrent hypoglycemia had abnormally low activity of G‐6‐Pase [17]. In most of the 76 preterm infants, liver tissues obtained during necropsy, G‐6‐Pase activity was on or below the lower limit of the normal range [18]. In our reported cases, in the background of negative genetic screening for GSD, glucagon unresponsiveness is not due to a genetic disease but rather a disorder of glucose mobilization, which we speculate was due to delayed maturity of enzyme activity, supported by spontaneous recovery over time.

The transient form of HH is known in preterm and SGA infants due to a lower glucose threshold for insulin suppression, reflecting a delay in fetal islet cell adaptation, which often resolves a few days after birth [19]. Current practice is to delay the medical treatment for HH with diazoxide for 7–10 days, as a spontaneous resolution may occur. Prolonged or persistent forms of HH requiring high GIR can cause brain injury, warranting early diazoxide treatment [20]. Case 2 cautions against the liberal use of diazoxide therapy even though the initial work‐up suggested HH, which could be transitional. The premature and SGA nature of these infants imposes a cumulative risk of developing severe gastrointestinal adverse effects to diazoxide [21].

Adequate glycogen stores, intact and functional glycogenolytic, gluconeogenic, and lipogenic enzymes, and an appropriate counterregulatory hormonal response are critical factors in extrauterine adaptation [7]. The SGA and preterm infants contend with many challenges in glucose homeostasis in the immediate neonatal period. Glycogen content in the fetal liver slowly accumulates from 3.4 mg/g in the first trimester to 50 mg/g at term. This enables an infant with normal glycogen stores to maintain glucose levels for 8–12 h, even without feeds [22]. The constant fetal supply of blood glucose via the uteroplacental unit is abruptly terminated at birth, and the infant adapts to independent hepatic glucose production. Soon after birth, preterm and SGA infants experience a “physiological fasting” period. While breast feeding is established, hepatic glycogen content decreases to reach low concentrations by 12 h of age [10]. SGA infants, in addition, have deficient glycogen stores, which may explain the inadequate response to parenteral glucagon [23].

In SGA and preterm infants, utilizing these already limited stores is made further challenging by hepatic enzyme immaturity. Following birth, skin‐to‐skin care and breastfeeding are initiated, facilitating glucose homeostasis while stabilizing the vital signs [24, 25]. Regular feeding of preterm infants ensures a constant energy supply for vital organs. The infant must depend on hepatic glucose release to maintain normoglycemia with increasing feeding intervals. This can be achieved if adequate glucose is released by glycogenolysis or gluconeogenesis between feeds [7]. Hepatic G‐6‐Pase is the common terminal enzymatic step for glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. Type 1 GSD, a genetic deficiency of hepatic G‐6‐Pase, is characterized by fasting hypoglycemia [26]. In the fetus, hepatic G‐6‐Pase is expressed only at low levels and reaches 10% of adult activity at term. In full‐term appropriate‐for‐gestational‐age infants, this enzyme activity increases to adult levels only in the first week of life [27]. However, in the SGA infant, there is delayed postnatal development of hepatic G‐6‐Pase, which renders further susceptibility to hypoglycemia. Chronic fetal hypoxia in growth retarded infants stimulates adrenaline secretion, which leads to the activation of glycogen phosphorylase and accelerated glycogenolysis, depleting glycogen stores. Placental insufficiency leads to diminished fetal glucose transfer, causing hypoinsulinemia and thereby hindering protein and triglyceride storage [7]. This results in a clinical phenotype akin to a “transitional glycogen storage disorder.” The low hepatic G‐6‐Pase activity in preterm infants may also account for diminished parenteral glucagon responses [7, 10]. This was evident in both infants, who failed a glucagon stimulation test that was carried out despite having adequate postnatal weight gain. This points toward an inability to utilize existing glycogen stores rather than having an inadequate substrate for metabolism alone. This has been previously described by Jackson et al., who studied 78 preterm infants and found 22 with inadequate glycemic response to glucagon. In their cohort, this was transitional, as all eventually developed an adequate response to glucagon [16].

Deficiencies of counter‐regulatory hormone response contribute to the risk of hypoglycemia in preterm and SGA infants [28, 29]. Hawdon et al. demonstrated a lower plasma cortisol level in 33 SGA infants compared to non‐SGA infants during hypoglycemia [29]. This blunted cortisol response to stress in SGA infants has been postulated to be due to a transient down‐regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary axis in response to chronic intrauterine stress [7]. Case 1, SGA infant had low random cortisol values during hypoglycemia but could mount an appropriate response to the ACTH stimulation test, indicating mild or relative adrenal insufficiency. Other authors have alluded to the mechanism being due to impaired activity of the placental enzyme 11 β‐hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase‐2 in pregnancies affected by growth restriction and pre‐eclampsia, which could result in early postnatal adrenal insufficiency [30, 31].

Conclusion

Hypoglycemia is a common metabolic disorder in preterm and SGA infants. Screening and intervening during the navigation through the glucose nadir in the early hours of life in infants at risk of hypoglycemia is paramount. In addition to HH, several mechanisms can be responsible for recurrent hypoglycemia in late preterm and SGA infants, including hepatic enzyme immaturity, which spontaneously improves over time. This complex clinical scenario can potentially result in missed diagnoses and unwarranted use of diazoxide if a systematic approach is not employed.
