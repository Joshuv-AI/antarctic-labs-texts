Circadian rest/activity rhythms and change in MRI‐derived brain volumes: Differences by age in a cognitively healthy sample
Authors: Marc A. Kaizi‐Lutu, Daniel D. Callow, Jill A. Rabinowitz, Brendan P. Lucey, Yang An, Mark N. Wu, Vadim Zipunnikov, Arnold Bakker, Christos Davatzikos, Eleanor M. Simonsick, Keenan A. Walker, Luigi Ferrucci, Susan M. Resnick, Adam P. Spira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCircadian rest/activity rhythms (RARs) change across adulthood, and alterations in RARs have been associated with cognitive decline, Alzheimer's disease (AD), and dementia. Little is known, however, about associations of RARs with subsequent changes in brain structure.METHODSWe investigated cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between RARs from wrist actigraphy and AD‐relevant brain volumes from magnetic resonance imaging in cognitively unimpaired adults aged ≥ 50 and whether age moderated these associations.RESULTSIn cross‐sectional analyses, weaker and more fragmented RARs were associated with smaller medial temporal region volumes. Longitudinally, more regular and less fragmented RARs were associated with better maintenance of amygdala volume over time. Further, associations between RAR fragmentation and global atrophy were strongest among the oldest participants.DISCUSSIONResults link RARs with medial temporal lobe and global brain volumes and indicate that these associations may differ by age. Research is needed on the effects of RAR‐focused interventions on brain health.

BACKGROUND

The circadian system regulates the timing of molecular and physiological processes, as well as behaviors including food intake, sleep, and physical activity. 1 Aging is associated with changes in these rhythms, 2 , 3 with older adults commonly exhibiting phase shifts toward earlier peak activity and bedtimes compared to younger adults. 4 , 5 Circadian rest/activity rhythms (RARs) derived from data collected continuously from accelerometers worn in the natural environment over several days can be used as ecologically valid estimates of circadian rhythmicity. 6 , 7 In addition to a phase advance, studies of RARs find older adults exhibit lower rhythm strength, greater fragmentation throughout the day, and lower day‐to‐day stability. 2 , 3 Mounting evidence links alterations in RARs to cognitive impairment, as well as subsequent cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and dementia in older adults. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Despite evidence of links between RARs and cognitive outcomes, little is known regarding associations of RARs with measures of brain structure. In a cross‐sectional study of older adults from the Netherlands, Van Someren et al. (2019) found that rhythm fragmentation accounted for 19% of the variance in medial temporal lobe volume, surpassing the variance explained by age (15%). 13 In addition, Liu et al. (2024) conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of RARs and brain volumes using data from the UK Biobank and found that greater rhythm strength was associated with larger cerebral white matter volume. 14 Somewhat surprisingly, they found that day‐to‐day stability was linked to greater ventricular volumes; however, analyses did not adjust for intracranial volume. These studies provide evidence of important associations between RARs and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐derived measures of brain health, but the cross‐sectional design precludes analysis of the association of RARs with change in brain structure over time. To advance knowledge in this area, we examined cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations of RARs with brain region volumes relevant to Alzheimer's disease (AD) in a US community‐based sample of middle‐aged and older adults. Considering the aforementioned age‐related changes in RARs, we also explored the moderating effect of age.

METHODS

Participants

We studied participants from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA), a longitudinal, observational study that aims to characterize cognitive and physical health changes that occur with aging. Participants were eligible for inclusion in analyses if they had data from both actigraphy and MRI. The study baseline constituted the first available visit for adults ≥50 years of age with these assessments. Of the N = 353 participants with valid actigraphy and MRI data, we excluded nine participants who had cognitive impairment not meeting MCI criteria, MCI, or dementia, leaving a total analytic sample of 344 participants. We analyzed follow‐up data only from visits at which participants remained cognitively normal (i.e., excluded visits [N = 45] at and after a participant met criteria for cognitive impairment, MCI, or dementia). Adjudication of cognitive status is described in detail elsewhere. 15

The frequency of BLSA follow‐up visits depends on age, with adults aged <60 years scheduled every 4 years, those aged 60 to 79 years scheduled every 2 years, and those ≥80 years scheduled annually. At enrollment and subsequent visits, participants gave written informed consent. All study procedures were approved by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Intramural Research Program Institutional Review Board.

Wrist actigraphy and RAR metrics

Participants were outfitted with an actigraph (Actiwatch‐2, Philips–Respironics, Bend, OR, USA) just prior to departure from their BLSA clinic visit and were instructed to wear the actigraph continuously for 7 days on their non‐dominant wrists. The actigraph records movement in activity counts (ACs) and also tracks ambient light levels using a built‐in accelerometer and photometer, respectively. Participants were asked to fill out sleep diaries each morning and evening and recorded when they got up to start their day, the times they got into bed at night with the intention of sleeping (i.e., “lights out”), the timing of any naps, and removal of the actigraph.

We exported activity‐count data in an epoch‐by‐epoch format using Actiware version 6.0.9 software (Philips Respironics) and preprocessed the data using R software version 4.0.4 (R Core Team, 2020). First, we excluded 24‐h periods with >5% (>72 min) of data missing. Next, to create a more symmetric distribution, we log‐transformed the ACs and averaged the log‐transformed AC within 144 ten‐minute bins. Finally, we estimated the mean participant‐specific RARs by averaging the daily curves over valid actigraphy data collection periods. Across analyses, the mean participant‐specific RAR was reflected in 144 ten‐minute log‐transformed ACs.

Three non‐parametric RAR metrics, which do not assume that RARs always manifest a cosinor (parametric) shape, were computed using the ActCR package version 0.3.0 (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/ActCR/index.html) in R. We computed relative amplitude (RA; rhythm strength), interdaily stability (IS; consistency of rhythm across days), and intradaily variability (IV; within‐day rhythm fragmentation). 16

Systematic review: The authors reviewed the literature using PubMed and Web of Science. We cited prior studies investigating cross‐sectional associations of RARs and regional brain volumes. Interpretation: Our findings establish a link between RARs and change in medial temporal brain volumes over time. Further, our findings suggest that more fragmented RARs in the oldest adults may indicate faster trajectories of deteriorating brain health. Future directions: This study provides evidence suggesting that RARs may be early biomarkers of or contributors to brain atrophy, especially in the medial temporal lobe. Nonetheless, these findings warrant replication in larger, more diverse cohorts, and the outcome should be extended to include AD‐specific biomarkers. Future studies should also assess how changes in RARs predict trajectories of brain atrophy. Intervention trials testing whether improving circadian rhythms can preserve brain health are also needed.

MRI

A priori, brain regions of interest were selected. The global brain volumes of interest were white matter, gray matter, and ventricular volume. In addition, we selected medial temporal lobe brain regions affected in dementia, and particularly AD, specifically the parahippocampal gyrus, hippocampus, and amygdala. These regions are among the earliest affected by AD and predict future cognitive decline. 17 , 18 , 19 Brain region volumes were quantified from T1‐weighted scans acquired on a 3T Philips Achieva MRI scanner using a magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence (repetition time [TR] = 6.8 ms, echo time [TE] = 3.2 ms, flip angle = 8°, image matrix = 256 × 256, 170 slices, pixel size = 1 × 1 mm, slice thickness = 1.2 mm). Anatomical labels and quantitative measures of global and regional brain volumes (in cubic centimeters) were derived using the Multi‐atlas region Segmentation using Ensembles of registration algorithms and parameters (MUSE) approach. 20 Briefly, MUSE employs multiple atlas registration algorithms to generate an ensemble of labeled maps and label estimates for each voxel. During label fusion, a subset of atlases most similar to the target scan is selected, and final voxel labels are assigned via weighted voting. This process yields a detailed parcellation of the target brain scan into anatomical regions, allowing for robust regional volumetric analyses.

Other participant information

Participants 1reported their sex, race/ethnicity, age, and educational attainment. At each visit, body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) was computed based on height and weight measurements. In addition, at each visit, data on participants’ medical comorbidities were collected during a nurse‐administered medical interview including a history of heart attack, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, cancer, and Parkinson's disease.

Statistical analyses

We calculated descriptive statistics for baseline participant characteristics and ran separate linear mixed‐effects models to examine the cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between baseline RAR metrics and changes in brain volume outcomes. The baseline RAR parameter was included in the model to test the cross‐sectional association of baseline RAR with baseline brain volume outcomes, and an interaction of the baseline RAR with time was included in the model to test the association of the baseline RAR with longitudinal change over time in brain volumes. Participant sex, race, baseline age, years of education, BMI, and intracranial volume at age 70 were included as covariates in all models. Time was the follow‐up interval in years from baseline. Interactions of sex × time, race × time, baseline age × time, years of education × time, and baseline BMI × time were also included as covariates in the models to account for the potential longitudinal effect of the covariates on the outcomes. Participant sex was coded as −0.5 for female and 0.5 for male. Race was coded as a dummy variable, with White adults and individuals who identified as Other for race coded as 0 and Black or African American adults coded as 1. In all linear mixed‐effects models, intercept and time were included as random effects with an unstructured covariance.

Interaction analyses of age and baseline RARs with brain volume outcomes were conducted to determine whether age moderated the cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations of baseline RAR with brain volume. We added a baseline age × baseline RAR interaction term to test whether age moderated the cross‐sectional association of baseline RAR and baseline brain volume outcomes. Interaction terms of baseline age × baseline RAR × time were also added to the models to assess whether age moderated the association of baseline RAR with change over time in brain volume. If the age moderation effect was significant to the p < 0.01 level, we further derived associations between baseline RAR and brain volumes at three predetermined age points (65, 75, and 85) within the model.

Model assumptions were evaluated using scatterplots and Q–Q plots. Cook's distance was examined for each model to identify influential observations. The vast majority of observations did not exceed a Cook's distance of one, suggesting that no individual data point exerted undue influence on model estimates. Only one participant had observations with a Cook's distance greater than one. After removing this participant, our estimates did not meaningfully change, so we elected to include their data. To control for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure to cross‐sectional and longitudinal main‐effect tests separately. FDR was applied to ventricular, white matter volume, and gray matter volumes jointly and to a priori gray matter subregions (parahippocampal gyrus, hippocampus, amygdala) separately.

Sensitivity analyses that adjusted for comorbidities were also run to determine whether associations of baseline RARs with brain volume outcomes were affected by comorbidities. A count variable reflecting the number of comorbidities participants endorsed at baseline was created, which included a history of heart attack, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, cancer, Parkinson's disease, and depression (possible range = 0 to 9); this variable was included as a covariate across analyses. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1. Linear mixed‐effects models were fit using the “lmer” function in the lme4 package.

RESULTS

The 344 participants had a mean baseline age of 72.9 ± 10.4 years; 197 (57.3%) were women, and 77 (22.4%) identified as Black or African American (Table 1). Participants completed an average of 5.56 ± 0.88 (range 3 to 7) nights of actigraphy data. There were 120 participants (34.9%) with only baseline RAR and MRI assessments. For participants with more than one visit, mean follow‐up was 1.18 ± 1.10 years and mean number of visits was 2.27 ± 1.29. There were 46 participants (13.4%) under age 60 years, 191 participants (55.5%) between 60 and 79 years, and 107 participants (31.1%) aged 80 years and older.

Ventricular volume

There were no significant cross‐sectional main effects of RARs with ventricular volume (Table 2). Greater IV was associated with faster longitudinal increases in ventricular volume (B = 1.47, p = 0.022). However, age moderated the longitudinal association of IV with ventricular volume, and the association of IV with change in ventricular volume became stronger with advancing age (p for interaction = 0.010). Greater IV was associated with significantly faster subsequent increases in ventricular volume at ages 75 and 85 but not 65 (Table 3).

White matter volume

There were no cross‐sectional or longitudinal associations of RARs with white matter volume (Table 2); nor were there significant age moderating effects on associations between RARs and white matter volume.

Gray matter volume

There were no significant cross‐sectional or longitudinal main effects of RARs with gray matter volume (Table 2); however, there was a significant age moderating effect on the association between IV and change in gray matter volume (p for interaction = 0.008), such that at age 65, greater IV was marginally associated with slower gray matter volume atrophy (p < 0.05), and at age 85 greater IV was associated with significantly faster atrophy (p < 0.01) (Table 3).

Parahippocampal volume

Higher RA (B = 0.88, p = 0.024) and lower IV (B = −0.73, p = 0.027) were associated with greater parahippocampal volume (Table 2). There were no longitudinal main effect associations of RARs with parahippocampal volume and no significant age moderating effects.

Hippocampal volume

In cross‐sectional analyses, higher RA (B = 0.98, p = 0.013) and lower IV (B = −0.84, p = 0.012) were associated with greater hippocampal volume (Table 2). There were no other significant cross‐sectional or longitudinal main effects, and no significant age moderating effects.

Amygdala volume

In main effects, greater IS (B = 0.03, p = 0.017) and lower IV (B = −0.04, p = 0.002) were associated with slower longitudinal decreases in amygdala volume (Table 2). Notably, the longitudinal association between greater RA and slower decreases in amygdala volume approached significance (B = 0.04, p = 0.065). There were no significant age moderating effects.

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for comorbidities

After further adjustment for a history of heart attack, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, cancer, Parkinson's disease, and depression, the direction and significance of associations remained relatively consistent, with one exception. While the interaction of age and IV with gray matter volume over time remained significant (p = 0.002), the point estimate for age 65 was no longer significant (B = 5.52, p = 0.058).

Adjustment for multiple comparisons

After adjusting p values using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, only the cross‐sectional associations of RA and IV with parahippocampal volume and the longitudinal association between IV and ventricular volume were no longer significant. All other main effects remained significant after adjustment.

DISCUSSION

We investigated associations between RARs and regional brain volumes in cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged and older adults. Cross‐sectional analyses revealed significant associations of greater RA and lower IV with greater hippocampal and parahippocampal volumes. Longitudinally, greater IV was a significant predictor of decreasing amygdala volume and increasing ventricular volume over time. Additionally, lower IS was a significant predictor of decreasing amygdala volume. We found age‐moderated longitudinal associations of IV with changes in ventricular and gray matter volume. These associations were the strongest among the older participants compared to the younger ones. In addition, the directions of associations between IV and gray matter volume reversed in the youngest compared to oldest participants. Our findings suggest RARs are markers of or potential contributors to brain atrophy, including in the medial temporal lobe, a region central to AD.

Our findings of an association between lower IV and greater hippocampal volume are consistent with those of Van Someren et al., who found that higher IV was linked to smaller medial temporal lobe volume. 13 Unlike Van Someren et al., however, we also observed associations of RA and IS with medial temporal lobe brain volumes. These discrepancies may be due to the use of a visual rating score in Van Someren et al. versus automated segmentation and quantification of actual volume in the present study to quantify medial temporal lobe volume. 13 Although our longitudinal analyses revealed significant associations of greater IS and lower IV with better maintenance of amygdala volume over time, these are the first longitudinal findings of this type that have been reported, so replication in future longitudinal studies is needed. Collectively, these findings link weaker, more fragmented, and less stable RARs to atrophy in medial temporal lobe regions that are vulnerable to age‐related atrophy and neurodegenerative processes. 21 , 22 , 23

Three pathways could describe the mechanisms underlying the associations between RARs and medial temporal lobe volume: (1) circadian rhythm alterations could promote hippocampal and amygdala atrophy, (2) hippocampal and amygdala atrophy could alter circadian rhythms, or (3) hippocampal and amygdala atrophy and circadian rhythm disruption could result from a common cause, such as widespread neurodegenerative processes or age‐related changes in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the brain's circadian pacemaker located in the anterior hypothalamus. The SCN is entrained by the light–dark cycle and regulates oscillators in other brain areas, including the hippocampus and amygdala. 24 , 25 , 26 The SCN undergoes age‐related atrophy that is associated with changes in circadian rhythms. 27 , 28 , 29 Furthermore, disruptions in SCN function are observed in neurodegenerative diseases. 30 , 31 Deletion of clock genes in the SCN of rodents resulted in neurodegeneration and atrophy in the hippocampus, 32 , 33 and in humans, chronic circadian misalignment, caused by desynchrony between the timing of the circadian clock and the time of day due to constant transmeridian travel, has been linked to lower medial temporal lobe volume. 34 Additionally, aging‐related changes in the SCN and circadian rhythms may negatively affect sleep, 35 and insufficient and fragmented sleep have been associated with brain volume loss and AD pathology. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Thus, circadian rhythm dysregulation may increase sleep disruption that, in turn, contributes to medial temporal lobe atrophy. Notably, the amygdala is sensitive to sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality, 41 , 42 , 43 and chronic insomnia is associated with amygdala atrophy. 44

Our study identified age as a significant modifier of the association between RARs and brain structure. In longitudinal analyses, age moderated the associations of IV with ventricular and gray matter volume. At age 65, greater IV was associated with slower subsequent ventricular enlargement and slower rates of atrophy in gray matter. This suggests that greater IV at age 65 is protective against brain atrophy, an unexpected finding given that lower rhythm fragmentation is generally considered to be a marker or predictor of good or sustained health. 45 A possible explanation is that individuals with higher rhythm fragmentation at age 65 may have higher levels of physical activity compared to those at 75 and 85. Conversely, greater IV at ages 75 and 85 was associated with a twofold increase in the rate of ventricular volume enlargement over time compared to that at age 65, and greater IV at age 85 was associated with faster subsequent gray matter atrophy. The change in direction of the association at older ages could be due to the potential bidirectional relationship between brain atrophy and circadian rhythm disruption where circadian rhythm disruption both contributes to and is a result of brain atrophy. 35 As the deleterious cycle continues, daily rhythm fragmentation and the accompanying neurodegeneration and atrophy may accelerate as damage accumulates. Unfortunately, there are no other longitudinal studies with which to compare our findings, and replication in future studies is needed.

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to examine both cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between RARs and regional brain volumes in middle‐aged and older adults and the first to investigate effect modification by age. It also has some limitations. Study participants were generally highly educated and cognitively unimpaired at baseline, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to the broader population. We did not adjust for new‐onset comorbidities and only conducted sensitivity analyses with variables collected at the baseline visit. Several associations observed in uncorrected analyses did not survive multiple‐comparison adjustment, underscoring the importance of replication in independent samples. Our study did not include assessments of AD‐specific pathology (i.e., amyloid, tau), and the number of MRI assessments differed by age. The fewer MRI assessments among younger versus older participants limited our ability to confidently make comparable inferences between these age groups. Additionally, the study had limited follow‐up for wrist‐actigraphy data collections. Studies that follow participants for years with several repeated waves of actigraphy data are necessary to understand the extent to which RARs change over time and the associations of these changes with regional brain volumes. Furthermore, RARs are not measures of the endogenous circadian rhythm, for which an intensive in‐lab protocol is necessary. They are instead ecological estimates of diurnal rhythmicity that may be influenced by an individual's social and work schedules and, of course, light exposure. Studies with intensive in‐lab protocols that have repeated collections of circadian oscillators in the absence of zeitgebers are necessary to assess an individual's circadian rhythmicity.

In conclusion, we identified cross‐sectional and longitudinal links between RARs and global and medial temporal brain atrophy in middle‐aged and older adults and found that these associations varied by age. Greater rhythm fragmentation, IV, emerged as the most consistent predictor of lower regional brain volume across all analyses; however, this association differed as a function of age, with greater IV at late middle age predicting less atrophy and greater IV later in older adulthood predicting greater atrophy. Further research is needed to assess whether changes in RARs over time predict subsequent brain volume trajectories and whether the RAR can therefore be leveraged as an early biomarker of neurodegenerative disease risk and as a target for interventions aimed at preserving brain health.
