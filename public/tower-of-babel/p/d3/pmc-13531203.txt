Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture During Valve-in-Valve TAVR in a Degenerated Surgical Aortic Valve
Authors: Iulian Calin, Lucian Dorobantu, Angela Georgescu, Silviu Dumitrascu, Irina Macovei-Dorobantu, Silvia Deaconu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundValve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (ViV-TAVR) in degenerated surgical bioprostheses, including less characterized valves such as those manufactured by Braile Biomédica, may be limited by constrained expansion and high residual gradients, particularly in small surgical valves.Case SummaryAn 89-year-old man presented with acute heart failure due to degeneration of a 23-mm Braile surgical aortic bioprosthesis with inherently small true internal diameter. ViV-TAVR with a 23-mm self-expanding valve resulted in a high residual gradient despite appropriate positioning. Given the absence of alternative therapeutic options, intentional high-pressure postdilatation using a noncompliant balloon was performed. This achieved bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF), confirmed by fluoroscopy and computed tomography, leading to optimal transcatheter valve expansion, marked gradient reduction, and clinical improvement.DiscussionThis case provides imaging-confirmed evidence of BVF in a degenerated Braile valve performed in the clinical setting, establishing the structural behavior of this specific prosthesis in human anatomy.Take-Home MessagesSmall surgical bioprostheses with limited or poorly characterized true internal diameter are at high risk for severe residual gradients after ViV-TAVR. BVF, guided by multimodality imaging, can be a decisive strategy to optimize transcatheter valve expansion and hemodynamics.Key words: aortic stenosis, bioprosthetic fracture, degenerated biological valve, heart failure, residual gradients, valve-in-valve

History of Presentation

An 89-year-old man was admitted for acute decompensated heart failure. He presented with progressive dyspnea at rest and orthopnea over the preceding 2 days, associated with palpitations and rapid ventricular response. On admission, he was in NYHA functional class IV.

Past Medical History

The patient had a 23-mm Braile bioprosthesis (manufactured by Braile Biomédica, São José do Rio Preto, Brazil) implanted in 2006. Key comorbidities included prior coronary artery bypass grafting (left internal mammary artery–left anterior descending artery patent), previous percutaneous coronary intervention to the circumflex, chronic atrial fibrillation, moderate mitral regurgitation, hypertension, and anemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Acute heart failure decompensation was considered multifactorial, with potential contributors including degeneration of the surgical aortic bioprosthesis, myocardial ischemia, tachyarrhythmia-mediated ventricular dysfunction, and progression of degenerative mitral valve disease.

Investigations

The electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation with flat T-waves. Laboratory tests revealed moderate anemia (hemoglobin: 10.2 g/dL), elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (4,900 pg/mL), and preserved renal function. Transthoracic echocardiography/transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a severely degenerated aortic bioprosthesis with severe regurgitation and moderate stenosis (Video 1). The left ventricle showed mild concentric hypertrophy and moderately reduced function (left ventricular ejection fraction: 40%-45%). Coronary angiography confirmed a patent left anterior descending artery bypass graft and circumflex stent. Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) and dedicated valve-in-valve (ViV) planning demonstrated a small true internal diameter (ID) of approximately 18 mm, despite the labeled size of 23 mm (Figure 1), as specified in the ViV Aortic app (KRUTSCH, MHI, Bapat). This marked discrepancy between labeled size and true ID was consistent with the original Braile valve design, and high risk for patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and incomplete transcatheter heart valve expansion were considered. Coronary heights were optimal for the right coronary artery, but low height of the left coronary was noted (7 mm). A suspected small pseudoaneurysm at the noncoronary sinus was also noted.

According to manufacturer documentation, the Braile valve is constructed with bovine pericardial leaflets mounted on a polyoxymethylene (polyacetal) stent, incorporating an external 316L stainless-steel reinforcing wire for structural support and radiographic identification.

Preliminary ex vivo testing has showed that Brazilian aortic bioprostheses, including Braile models, can be fractured using high-pressure balloons.1 This report provides imaging confirmation of successful bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) in a clinical setting, involving a Braile surgical bioprosthesis during a ViV-transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) procedure, demonstrating the clinical translation of prior ex vivo mechanical findings.

Management

Given the patient’s advanced age, high surgical risk, and severe degeneration of the aortic bioprosthesis, the heart team recommended ViV-TAVR.

Given the small true ID of the surgical valve, the absence of alternative transcatheter options, and the lack of available data regarding valve fracture feasibility, the procedural strategy from the outset included the intention to achieve surgical ring modification, either deformation or fracture, if necessary, to optimize transcatheter valve expansion and reduce the risk for severe PPM.

The procedure was performed under conscious sedation using a transfemoral approach. Temporary right ventricular pacing was established via femoral venous access. Valve crossing and guidewire exchange were performed using a straight-tip 0.035-inch nitinol wire (Terumo Corporation) on a 5-F AL 1.0 catheter (Terumo Corporation). On a Confida wire (Medtronic), the degenerated surgical bioprosthesis was predilated with a 20-mm Atlas balloon (Bard Peripheral Vascular) under rapid pacing, using manual inflation with a 50-mL Luer lock syringe connected to the balloon by a stopcock valve.

A 23-mm self-expanding transcatheter heart valve Medtronic Evolut FX was then implanted in the aortic position (Video 2). After implantation of the 23-mm self-expanding transcatheter valve, echocardiographic assessment demonstrated a markedly elevated residual transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, consistent with constrained valve expansion within the small surgical frame (Figure 2).

In the absence of alternative corrective options, intentional high-pressure postdilatation was performed using a 20-mm noncompliant Atlas balloon. The 20-mm noncompliant balloon was selected because, although the valve was labeled as 23 mm, preprocedural CT confirmed a constrained external diameter of approximately 20 mm. Using a 20-mm balloon provided a 1:1 ratio with the external frame and a 2-mm oversize relative to the 18-mm true ID, which was considered as the mechanical requirement to achieve fracture of the ring while protecting the aortic root.

The balloon was positioned within the surgical valve frame and initially inflated rapidly using a manual syringe injection to fully expand the balloon, followed by further pressurization with an indeflator to achieve high inflation pressures, according to previously described BVF techniques.2 During inflation, at approximately 16 atm, a sudden release of the balloon waist accompanied by an abrupt pressure drop was observed, consistent with BVF. Fluoroscopy confirmed valve fracture (Figure 3, Video 3). Transthoracic echocardiogram assessment demonstrated a marked reduction in the transvalvular gradient with a mean gradient of 12 mm Hg, with no significant regurgitation (Figure 2).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postprocedurally, the patient had transient acute kidney injury and anemia, both resolved with conservative management and intravenous iron. He was discharged at 1 week in NYHA functional class II. Nine-month follow-up confirmed a well-functioning transcatheter valve with no significant gradient or regurgitation.

Discussion

ViV-TAVR has become an established alternative to redo surgery in patients with failed surgical bioprostheses at high or prohibitive surgical risk.3 However, ViV procedures remain limited by the constrained expansion imposed by the surgical valve frame, particularly in small bioprostheses, leading to residual gradients and PPM, both of which are associated with increased morbidity and mortality.

A fundamental principle in ViV-TAVR planning is the preprocedural assessment of bioprosthetic fracturability. Attempting in vivo BVF without prior knowledge of the valve's mechanical response poses significant risks. If the surgical frame had not been fracturable, aggressive postdilatation would not have improved the gradient and could have led to catastrophic aortic root injury. In such scenarios, selecting a smaller transcatheter heart valve or accepting a higher baseline gradient might be a more appropriate strategy. Our decision to proceed was supported by the ViV Aortic app, which explicitly identifies the Braile valve as fracturable, allowing for a planned procedural strategy.

A critical determinant of ViV outcomes is the true ID of the surgical valve, which often differs substantially from the labeled size. Prior studies and registry data have consistently shown that valves with a true ID ≤18 to 19 mm are at the highest risk for severe residual gradients after ViV-TAVR.4,5 In the present case, despite a labeled valve size of 23 mm, the true ID was 18 mm, fully explaining the markedly elevated postimplantation gradient after transcatheter valve deployment.

BVF has emerged as an effective strategy to overcome this limitation.2 Bench testing and multicenter clinical experience have demonstrated that high-pressure inflation with noncompliant balloons can fracture or remodel the frames of many commonly used surgical valves, leading to improved transcatheter valve expansion, increased effective orifice area, and significantly reduced gradients.2,6 Importantly, performing BVF after TAVR has been shown to provide superior hemodynamic results compared with fracture before valve deployment.7

Nevertheless, not all surgical valves are considered fracturable. Valves with metallic frames have demonstrated resistance to fracture, prompting alternative concepts such as bioprosthetic valve remodeling, in which controlled frame deformation rather than true fracture improves valve expansion.6 These observations underscore the importance of understanding the material composition and mechanical behavior of each surgical valve type when planning ViV procedures.

The surgical valve in this case, manufactured by Braile Biomédica, is constructed on a polyacetal (polyoxymethylene) stent with an externally mounted stainless-steel reinforcing wire. The procedural strategy incorporated an upfront intention to achieve surgical ring modification, either deformation or fracture, according to the persistence of residual gradients after TAVR. The documented abrupt pressure drop, loss of balloon waist, fluoroscopic changes, and postprocedural CT demonstrate true BVF, rather than bioprosthetic valve remodeling.

The intraprocedural data confirm that complete fracture can be achieved safely despite the combined polymeric and metallic reinforcement, successfully resolving the residual hemodynamic gradients under clinical conditions. Beyond its mechanistic implications, the case reinforces several practical principles emphasized in contemporary practice: meticulous preprocedural imaging, recognition of true ID over labeled size, anticipation of PPM, and readiness to escalate to BVF when hemodynamics remain suboptimal.

In summary, this report provides imaging-confirmed evidence of successful BVF in a Braile surgical aortic bioprosthesis, supporting the broader applicability of high-pressure valve fracture strategies in carefully selected patients with small or poorly characterized surgical valves.

Conclusions

This case illustrates successful ViV-TAVR with documented complete fracture of a degenerated surgical aortic bioprosthesis, enabling optimal transcatheter valve expansion in a small surgical valve. Comprehensive multimodality imaging was essential for procedural planning and confirmation of BVF.
