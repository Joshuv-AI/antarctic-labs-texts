Impact of incretin therapies on biochemical and imaging outcomes in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Nicole Ann Tesoro, Frederick Berro Rivera, Nathan Ross B. Bantayan, John Vincent Magalong, Romaine Tomada, Maria Antonia Wong, Gabriel Tangco, Polyn Luz Pine, Jasvin Kurundrayil Manha, Elora Shyama Tanni, Christine Lin, Sung-Ki Lee, Kyla Lara-Breitinger, Martha Gulati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver condition. Incretin therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and emerging dual agonists, have shown promise in improving steatohepatitis.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of incretin therapies compared with standard therapies in patients with MASLD.MethodsSystematic search of Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) from database inception to August 2025 was done. Eligible RCTs enrolled adults with MASLD or biopsy-confirmed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), comparing incretin therapies with placebo, oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs), or insulin. Outcomes were changes in liver enzymes, liver fat, and fibrosis resolution.ResultsTwenty-four RCTs (n = 2158) were included. Compared with placebo, incretin therapies reduced ALT (SMD -0.46; 95 % CI -0.67 to -0.25), AST (SMD -0.41; 95 % CI -0.66 to -0.17), and liver fat (SMD -0.55; 95 % CI -1.38 to 0.27). When compared to insulin, ALT (SMD -1.02; 95 % CI -1.98 to -0.05), AST (SMD -0.62; 95 % CI -1.21 to -0.04), and liver fat (SMD -0.62; 95 % CI -0.92 to -0.32) were significantly reduced. Incretin therapies improved non-invasive fibrosis markers. Steatohepatitis resolution was greater vs placebo but not insulin. Hepatic and steatohepatitis outcomes appeared greater among patients with diabetes.ConclusionIncretin therapies improve liver enzymes, reduce liver fat, and promote steatohepatitis resolution in MASLD with an acceptable safety profile. Benefits may be greater in patients with coexisting diabetes. Effects on histologic fibrosis remain inconclusive, underscoring the need for larger and longer trials.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is the most common chronic liver condition globally, affecting one in four adults worldwide [[1], [2], [3]]. More than half of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) and up to 80 % of those with obesity are estimated to have MASLD [4,5]. Among children, global prevalence is 7–10 %, increasing to over 30 % in those with obesity [6].

Rates vary globally, ranging from 13 to 18 % in sub-Saharan Africa to ≥35 % in parts of the Middle East and South America, and is rising worldwide with increasing obesity and urbanization [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Studies highlight the concerning progressive trajectory of MASLD, including metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [7,11]. By 2030, MASLD is expected to be the leading indication for liver transplantation, with a substantial economic burden [11,12]. Also, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in MASLD patients, underscoring its systemic cardiometabolic burden [13,14]. MASLD is therefore frequently encountered in preventive cardiology and cardiometabolic clinics.

Resmetirom is the only FDA-approved therapy for adults with MASH with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis; however, no medications are approved specifically for MASLD without steatohepatitis/fibrosis [15]. Primary management for MASLD continues to rely largely on lifestyle modification [16]. However, incretin-based therapies have attracted interest as potential cardio-hepatic disease-modifying agents. Incretin therapies include glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and dual GLP-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonists. These are used for treatment of type 2 DM and obesity, and have been demonstrated to promote weight loss, reduce visceral fat, and improve insulin sensitivity. Current liver and diabetes society guidance supports GLP-1 RAs when clinically indicated for obesity and/or type 2 diabetes, though they are not yet recommended as MASLD-specific therapy [16]. Beyond the systemic effects, clinical trials of incretin therapies have demonstrated reductions in aminotransferases, hepatic steatosis, and non-invasive markers of inflammation and fibrosis [17,18]. Newer dual agonists like tirzepatide show even greater metabolic benefits and may further reduce hepatic fat and slow disease progression [19].

Despite encouraging results, the overall efficacy of incretin therapies in MASLD remains uncertain due to the heterogenous trial designs and endpoints. Because cardiologists increasingly initiate and co-manage incretin therapies for cardiometabolic indications, clarifying hepatic benefits and limitations is directly relevant to preventive cardiology. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the impact of incretin therapies on hepatic and metabolic outcomes in MASLD patients and guide clinical practice and future research.

Methodology

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry from their initiation until August 22, 2025, to identify all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating incretin therapies in MASLD (including MASH, NAFLD, and alcohol-related liver disease). The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) with a comprehensive set of keywords for the population (e.g., 'nonalcoholic fatty liver disease', 'MAFLD', 'alcoholic liver disease'), the intervention (e.g., 'semaglutide', 'tirzepatide', 'dual agonist'), and study design ('randomized controlled trial'). Studies focused on other chronic liver diseases or primary alcohol-related liver disease were excluded; trials that used alcohol-consumption thresholds to define NAFLD/MASLD populations were eligible.

After removing 351 duplicates from an initial 1641 records, the remaining 1290 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. This initial screening was assisted by the OpenAI API, utilizing the gpt-5-mini large language model (LLM) and a custom Python script, which categorized studies based on predefined PICO criteria. This automated process resulted in 68 studies being selected for full-text retrieval. Full-text articles were retrieved for 50 of these studies, which then underwent a final eligibility review, again assisted by the LLM. This final check resulted in the exclusion of a further 26 studies, leaving a final 24 studies for inclusion in the systematic review. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO with ID CRD420251208026.

Data abstraction

For the 24 included studies, data were abstracted from the full-text articles using the same custom Python script and LLM. Data extracted included study characteristics (e.g., study duration), participant demographics (e.g., sample size, age, sex), baseline clinical data (e.g., BMI, DM, liver enzymes), and key efficacy and safety outcomes. The complete automated extraction for each study was subsequently verified for accuracy and completeness by five independent human reviewers (R.T., M.W., G.T., P.P., and E.T.), with a senior reviewer (N.T.) resolving any discrepancies. Definitions: BMI was recorded as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Imaging outcomes included magnetic resonance imaging-proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF, a quantitative measure of liver fat) and liver stiffness measured by transient or MR elastography. Fibrosis was assessed using the Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) score and the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index. Key efficacy and safety outcomes were also abstracted, including liver injury biomarkers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), weight changes, and categorical endpoints such as steatohepatitis resolution and fibrosis regression.

Data analysis

Trial- and arm-level characteristics were summarized descriptively (counts and percentages for categorical variables; means ± SD or medians [IQR] for continuous variables). Efficacy and safety outcomes were synthesized using comparator-specific random-effects meta-analyses (placebo, oral hypoglycemic agents [OHAs], insulin). For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMD; Hedges’ g) were pooled using inverse-variance DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models to accommodate variability in outcome reporting (e.g., absolute values vs change-from-baseline). For outcomes reported consistently in the same units across studies (BMI [kg/m²] and liver stiffness [kPa]), mean differences (MD) were pooled. For categorical outcomes, risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the log scale using random-effects inverse-variance methods; zero-cell trials were continuity-corrected (0.5). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I². Forest plots were produced for all outcomes to aid visualization. Statistical significance was set at two-sided α = 0.05. All analyses were conducted in Stata/MP 14 (StataCorp).

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB2) tool. Certainty of evidence for primary outcomes was rated using the GRADE approach, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis was done for DM status. To minimize exposure misclassification and improve interpretability, we restricted inclusion to trials that enrolled either exclusively patients with DM (100 %) or exclusively no DM (0 %) populations. This binary stratification approach was adopted to ensure that within-group estimates accurately reflect the metabolic profile of the population analyzed.

Results

Twenty-four RCTs (n = 2158) met inclusion criteria, wherein 23 contributed data to at least one pooled analysis (Supplemental Figure 1). Among the 2158 adults with MASLD, 1175 were assigned to incretin therapies and 983 to control. MASH was confirmed by liver biopsy using MASH—CRN criteria in histology-based studies, and by validated non-invasive approaches in others, including imaging modalities (e.g., MRI-PDFF for steatosis; transient or MR elastography for liver stiffness) and fibrosis measures (e.g., ELF, FIB-4 index). Participants were randomized to receive an incretin medication versus placebo, OHAs, or basal insulin and were followed for a median of 24 weeks.

Liver outcomes

Liver fat percentage change

Three studies (n = 181) comparing incretin therapies with insulin showed a significant reduction in liver fat percentage favoring incretin therapies (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.62; 95 % CI, −0.92 to −0.32; p < 0.001), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I² = 0 %) (Fig. 1A). Only one small trial (n = 51) evaluated incretin therapies against OHAs and reported no significant difference (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.04; 95 % CI, −0.59 to 0.51; p = 0.881) (Fig. 1B). Six trials (n = 209) comparing incretin therapies with placebo demonstrated a non-significant reduction in liver fat percentage (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.55; 95 % CI, −1.38 to 0.27; p = 0.189), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 86.7 %) (Fig. 1C).Fig. 1Forest plots on the effect of incretin therapies on liver fat percentage change. Liver enzymes

When compared with insulin (three studies, n = 193), incretin therapies were associated with a significant reduction in percent ALT change (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.02; 95 % CI, −1.98 to −0.05; p = 0.040), although heterogeneity was high (I² = 90 %) (Fig. 2A). Comparisons with OHAs (five studies, n = 477) did not show a significant effect (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.26; 95 % CI, −1.27 to 0.75; p = 0.612), with high inconsistency (I² = 96 %) (Fig. 2B). Four studies (n = 241) comparing incretin therapies with insulin showed a significant reduction in AST (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.62; 95 % CI, −1.21 to −0.04; p = 0.037), although heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 80 %) (Fig. 2D). Six studies (n = 568) comparing incretin therapies with OHAs found no significant difference (SMD [Hedges’ g] = 0.26; 95 % CI, −0.67 to 1.20; p = 0.580), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 96 %) (Fig. 2E). In contrast, twelve studies (n = 1466) comparing incretin therapies with placebo demonstrated a significant reduction in AST (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.41; 95 % CI, −0.66 to −0.17; p = 0.001), with moderate to high heterogeneity (I² = 68 %) (Fig. 2F).

Across the available evidence, incretin therapies demonstrated consistent improvements in ALT and AST compared with placebo and were superior to insulin in reducing liver fat, ALT, and AST. In contrast, comparisons with OHAs yielded highly heterogeneous and inconclusive results. These comparisons were based on a small number of trials and involved heterogeneous drug classes, making OHAs-specific analyses unfeasible and resulting in highly inconsistent pooled estimates.

Steatohepatitis resolution and fibrosis regression

Five trials evaluating steatohepatitis resolution (n = 1762) showed higher resolution rates with incretin therapies compared to controls (overall random-effects RR 1.25; 95 % CI, 1.16 to 1.34; p < 0.001), with low overall heterogeneity (I² = 12.8 %). This effect was primarily driven by placebo-controlled trials (RR 1.25; 95 % CI, 1.15 to 1.37; I² = 32 %), whereas the single insulin-controlled trial did not show a significant effect (RR 1.16; 95 % CI, 0.83 to 1.62; p = 0.387). For fibrosis regression, among four studies (n = 1479) identified, incretin therapies did not significantly increase the likelihood of fibrosis regression compared to controls (RR 1.08; 95 % CI, 0.97 to 1.20; p = 0.187), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 52.5 %) (Fig. 3B).

Non-invasive fibrosis measures

Two trials (n = 119) demonstrated a modest but non-significant reduction in FIB-4 index for incretin therapies compared with insulin (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.56; 95 % CI, −1.16 to 0.03; p = 0.064; I² = 59.8 %) (Supplemental Figure S2A). Across five trials (n = 244), incretin therapies were associated with a significant reduction in liver stiffness measured by FibroScan compared with placebo (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −3.17; 95 % CI, −5.47 to −0.86; p = 0.007), with high heterogeneity (I² = 96.8 %) (Supplemental Figure S2B). Only one small study (n = 51) evaluated incretin therapies versus OHAs and showed no significant difference in FIB-4 (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.16; 95 % CI, −0.71 to 0.39; p = 0.578) (Supplemental Figure S2C). For the ELF score, five studies (n = 1042) demonstrated a significant reduction favoring incretin therapies compared with placebo (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −3.47; 95 % CI, −5.48 to −1.47; p = 0.001), with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 98.0 %) (Supplemental Figure S2D). FIB-4 analyses of incretin therapies versus placebo (three studies; n = 190) did not reach statistical significance (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −2.17; 95 % CI, −5.62 to 1.28; p = 0.217) (Supplemental Figure S2E).

Weight loss

Five trials (n = 228) comparing incretin therapies with placebo showed a non-significant reduction in percent weight change (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.49; 95 % CI, −1.09 to 0.10; p = 0.103), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 78.4 %) (Supplemental Figure S3A). Comparisons with OHAs (five studies, n = 448) likewise yielded a non-significant pooled effect (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.26; 95 % CI, −0.94 to 0.42; p = 0.448), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 91.5 %) (Supplemental Figure S3B). In contrast, four studies (n = 241) comparing incretin therapies with insulin demonstrated a significant reduction in percent weight change favoring incretin therapies (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.48; 95 % CI, −2.49 to −0.46; p = 0.004), although heterogeneity remained high (I² = 91.7 %) (Supplemental Figure S3C).

Additionally, six trials (n = 299) comparing incretin therapies with placebo showed a statistically significant reduction in BMI (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.41; 95 % CI, −0.81 to −0.00; p = 0.048), with moderate to substantial heterogeneity (I² = 64.2 %) (Supplemental Figure S3D). Comparisons with OHAs (five studies, n = 448) showed no statistically significant effect (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.50; 95 % CI, −1.16 to 0.16; p = 0.140), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 90.9 %) (Supplemental Figure S3E). Four studies (n = 241) comparing incretin therapies with insulin demonstrated a significant reduction in BMI favoring incretin therapies (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.41; 95 % CI, −2.45 to −0.37; p = 0.008), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 92.1 %) (Supplemental Figure S3F).

Safety profile

Gastrointestinal and serious adverse events

Across 17 trials (n = 3166) that reported GI adverse events, incretin therapies were associated with a higher risk of GI events compared to all other comparators (overall RR 1.13; 95 % CI, 1.08 to 1.19; p < 0.001), with high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 68.9 %). The effect was consistent when compared to placebo (RR 1.11; 95 % CI, 1.04 to 1.18), OHAs (RR 1.13; 95 % CI, 1.04 to 1.23), and insulin (RR 1.25; 95 % CI, 1.10 to 1.42) (Supplemental Figure S4A).

In 20 trials (n = 2951) that reported serious and/or life-threatening adverse events, incretin therapies did not increase the risk of serious adverse events compared to comparators (overall RR 1.01; 95 % CI, 0.99 to 1.02; p = 0.55), with no heterogeneity detected (I² = 0 %) (Supplemental Figure S4B).

Hypoglycemia and pancreatitis

Fifteen studies (n = 2550) showed no difference in hypoglycemia risk (overall RR 1.00; 95 % CI, 0.99 to 1.02) for incretin therapies when compared with placebo, insulin or OHA. Heterogeneity was negligible overall (I² = 0 %) (Supplemental Figure S4C).

Finally, across 15 trials (n = 2065), there was no evidence of an increased risk of drug-related pancreatitis with incretin therapies versus any comparators (overall RR 1.00; 95 % CI, 0.99 to 1.01), with I² = 0 % (Supplemental Figure S4C).

Sensitivity analyses by diabetes status

Sensitivity analyses stratified by diabetes status were limited by the small number of eligible studies and should be interpreted as exploratory. ALT levels significantly decreased among participants with diabetes receiving incretin therapies compared with placebo (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.01; 95 % CI, −2.04 to −0.03; p = 0.03), but not among those without diabetes (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.40; 95 % CI, −1.06 to 0.26) (Supplemental Figure S5). Incretin therapies also significantly reduced AST compared with insulin (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.52; 95 % CI, −1.02 to −0.02), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 81 %) (Supplemental Figure S6). Weight loss was significantly greater with incretin therapies compared with insulin among participants with diabetes (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.47; 95 % CI, −2.71 to −0.23; p = 0.02). No significant differences in percent weight change were observed between incretin therapies and placebo or OHA comparators (Supplemental Figure S7). Compared with insulin (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.61; 95 % CI, −0.91 to −0.32) and placebo (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −1.76; 95 % CI, −2.64 to −0.89), incretin therapies significantly reduced percent liver fat (Supplemental Figure S8). Resolution of steatohepatitis (RR = 1.40; 95 % CI, 1.07 to 1.83; p = 0.01) and fibrosis regression (RR = 2.29; 95 % CI, 1.66 to 3.16; p < 0.001) were observed among diabetic patients receiving incretin therapies; however, each outcome was supported by only a single study (Supplemental Figure S9). Gastrointestinal adverse events were consistently more frequent with incretin therapies compared with comparators (RR = 3.40; 95 % CI, 2.46 to 4.68), regardless of diabetes status. In contrast, rates of hypoglycemia (RR = 0.36; 95 % CI, 0.14 to 0.93), pancreatitis (RR = 1.40; 95 % CI, 0.39 to 5.05), and serious adverse events (RR = 0.75; 95 % CI, 0.27 to 2.13) were similar between diabetic patients receiving incretin therapies and control groups (Supplemental Figure S10). The FIB-4 index did not significantly change when comparing incretin therapies with insulin (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.55; 95 % CI, −1.14 to 0.03; p = 0.06) or OHAs (SMD [Hedges’ g] = −0.15; 95 % CI, −0.70 to 0.39; p = 0.58) (Supplemental Figure S11). No eligible studies were available for incretin therapies versus placebo comparisons. Data on ELF and FibroScan scores were excluded due to the absence of extractable diabetes-stratified data.

Liver outcomes

Our analysis showed that incretin therapies significantly reduced ALT and AST versus placebo and insulin, whereas comparisons with OHAs were heterogeneous and inconclusive. Mechanistically, incretin therapies reduce hepatic fat accumulation through weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced de novo lipogenesis [13]. This is supported by trials on liraglutide and exenatide versus insulin which showed improvements on intrahepatic fat, hepatic enzymes, and fibrosis after over 24 weeks of follow-up duration [[32], [33], [35]]. Short trials (<12 weeks) showed no significant liver fat reduction, whereas benefits became evident with therapy lasting ≥ 24 weeks [[41], [42]]. This suggests sustained incretin therapy treatment is required to achieve meaningful reductions in hepatic steatosis. Meanwhile, despite our heterogenous results against OHAs, several studies have shown that incretin therapies produce greater and more consistent reductions in aminotransferases and liver fat than DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors [[43], [44], [45]]. The highly heterogeneous findings versus OHAs likely reflect substantial pharmacologic diversity among comparators (e.g., metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and pioglitazone), small trial numbers within each subclass, and frequent open-label designs, limiting the interpretability of pooled OHA estimates.

The most clinically meaningful finding was the significantly increased likelihood of steatohepatitis resolution by about 25 % versus placebo, consistent with histology-based trials such as LEAN and semaglutide phase studies [17,[22], [46]]. Steatohepatitis resolution is strongly associated with reduced liver-related morbidity and mortality [[20], [21], [47], [48]], making this endpoint more prognostically relevant than isolated enzyme reduction.

While fibrosis regression did not reach statistical significance, non-invasive markers, such as liver stiffness and ELF score, consistently favored incretin therapy. The absence of histologic fibrosis benefit likely reflects the relatively short median follow-up (24 weeks), as fibrosis remodeling typically requires longer durations of therapy than studied [[17], [18], [19]].

Our review included GLP-1RAs and emerging dual agonists. Although early dual-agonist trials (e.g., tirzepatide and other investigational agents) suggest larger metabolic effects, the number of eligible RCTs and the heterogeneity of dual-agonist mechanisms (GLP-1/GIP vs GLP-1/glucagon) limited formal class-level subgroup meta-analysis. Head-to-head trials and adequately powered subgroup analyses will be needed to determine whether dual agonists offer incremental hepatic benefits beyond GLP-1RA monotherapy.

Weight loss

Incretin therapies produced greater weight and BMI reductions compared with insulin, reinforcing their metabolic advantage in cardiometabolic disease states [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. These effects are thought to occur through central appetite suppression and by delaying gastric emptying, thereby enhancing satiety and reducing caloric intake [54]. In contrast, insulin has anabolic and lipogenic effects leading to weight gain [55]. Findings are found to be stronger at higher doses of incretin therapies or combination therapies [53,[56], [57]]. Weight loss outcomes versus OHAs were highly variable due to the pharmacologic heterogeneity of OHA classes [[58], [59]]. Given that ≥5–10 % weight reduction is associated with histologic improvement in MASLD [60], weight loss likely contributes substantially to the observed hepatic benefits and subsequent reduced cardiovascular risk factors and events [[61], [62]].

Adverse effects

Gastrointestinal adverse events were more frequent with incretin therapy, but serious adverse events, hypoglycemia, and pancreatitis were not increased. An increase in gastrointestinal events with incretin therapies is expected due to the established effect on gastric emptying, which often improve with continued therapy or slower titration [[63], [64], [65], [66], [67]]. The overall safety profile supports the suitability of incretin therapies in patients with MASLD.

Sensitivity analyses by diabetes status

In exploratory analyses, hepatic and steatohepatitis outcomes appeared more pronounced in patients with diabetes. These stronger effects likely reflect a greater reversal of the pathogenic factors in MASLD, such as obesity-driven insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia [68,69]. Meanwhile, in the absence of diabetes such benefits are blunted, and placebo lifestyle interventions achieved similar weight loss. Regardless of diabetes status, incretin therapies still had significantly higher risk of GI adverse events and without increases in hypoglycemia, pancreatitis, or serious adverse events. However, these findings were limited by small study numbers and should be interpreted cautiously. Fibrosis regression and FIB-4 changes remained modest [70].

Notably, we were unable to perform sex-specific or ethnicity-specific analyses due to insufficient reporting. Given known sex differences in cardiometabolic disease progression, this represents an important gap for future research.

From a cardio-hepatology perspective, these findings support incretin therapies as agents that may improve hepatic steatosis and steatohepatitis while also addressing shared cardiometabolic drivers (weight, glycemia, and insulin resistance). However, improvements in surrogate hepatic markers should not be overinterpreted as proven reductions in long-term liver outcomes, particularly given the short median follow-up and limited histology data. Future trials should incorporate longer follow-up, relevant liver outcomes, and standardized non-invasive imaging endpoints (including multiparametric MRI measures such as cT1 where feasible), and should evaluate pragmatic treatment strategies relevant to cardiometabolic clinics (e.g., combination therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors or emerging MASH therapies such as resmetirom) [71,72].

Limitations

This analysis is limited by significant heterogeneity across agents, dosing regimens, and trial duration of follow-up. Subgroup analyses, including those by diabetes status, were exploratory. Analyses by sex or ethnicity were not possible due to insufficient data. Finally, background therapies were not standardized, which may have influenced pooled estimates.

Conclusion

Incretin therapies improve aminotransferases, reduce liver fat, and promote steatohepatitis resolution in MASLD with an acceptable safety profile. The strongest evidence of benefits observed was with incretin therapies compared to placebo and insulin, and in patients with coexisting diabetes. While histologic fibrosis regression remains inconclusive, consistent improvements in non-invasive markers and metabolic parameters support their role as disease-modifying cardiometabolic agents. Longer, adequately powered trials are required to determine their impact on fibrosis progression and long-term liver outcomes. Comparative studies among incretin therapies and evaluation of treatment response by sex, obesity severity, and diabetes status, will help support a more targeted therapeutic approach.

Clinical perspectives

None.

Competency in medical knowledge

Incretin therapies (GLP-1 RAs and dual agonists) improve liver enzymes, hepatic steatosis, weight, and BMI in patients with MASLD. These agents offer a promising adjunct to lifestyle changes, especially to patients with type 2 diabetes or obesity who are at increased risk of disease progression.

Translational outlook

Incretin therapies provide both hepatic and cardiometabolic benefits, but their effect on liver fibrosis remains uncertain. Larger, histology-based trials are needed to determine long-term outcomes, identify optimal candidates, and support approval for treatment of steatotic liver disease or steatohepatitis.
