Relationship between subendocardial viability ratio and cardiovascular disease risk in hypertensive patients
Authors: Lingxiao Wang, Jianxiong Chen, Xinqi Wang, Yunyun Mou, Xinyi Li, Lin Jin, Zhaojun Li, Ling Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR), which correlates with coronary flow reserve, has been proposed as an indirect prognostic marker for cardiovascular disease (CVD). This study aimed to investigate the relationship between SEVR and cardiovascular risk scores, and to evaluate its value in identifying increased cardiovascular risk in hypertensive population.MethodsA total of 1,380 participants (782 hypertensive patients and 598 normotensive controls) were enrolled. Demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters, arterial stiffness, left ventricular structural and functional indices, and 10-year atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) risk calculated via the China Atherosclerotic CVD Risk Prediction model (China-PAR) were collected and analyzed. Multivariable linear regression and logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify independent determinants of SEVR and factors linked to high ASCVD risk (>10%).ResultsMean SEVR was significantly lower in the hypertensive group than in the control group (130.75% vs. 136.38%, P<0.001). SEVR was negatively correlated with age, heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), E/e' ratio, arterial velocity index, and arterial pressure-volume index (all P<0.001). Multivariable linear regression identified age (β=−0.106, P=0.001), HR (β=−0.618, P<0.001), SBP (β=0.024, P=0.023), arterial pressure volume index (API) (β=−0.338, P<0.001), and hemoglobin (β=0.219, P=0.001) as independent determinants of SEVR. Logistic regression (Model 2, adjusted for sex, smoking, SBP, and HR) showed that SEVR was independently associated with a 10-year ASCVD (10Y-ASCVD) risk >10% [odds ratio (OR) =0.953, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.940–0.965, P<0.001]. Male sex (OR =1.973, P=0.001), current smoking (OR =2.665, P=0.001), and SBP (OR =1.015, P=0.003) were significant risk factors, while HR was protective (OR =0.949, P<0.001). In risk stratification, the high-risk group (10Y-ASCVD risk >10%) had significantly lower SEVR than the low-risk group in both hypertensive (122.72% vs. 131.92%, P<0.05) and control subjects (123.91% vs. 137.67%, P<0.05). Receiver operating characteristic analysis revealed that SEVR identified high ASCVD risk with an area under the curve of 0.706 in controls and 0.630 in hypertensives.ConclusionsSEVR is inversely associated with 10Y-ASCVD risk in individuals with hypertension and remains an independent indicator after adjustment for conventional risk factors. As a noninvasive and cost-effective marker reflecting myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance, SEVR has the potential to add complementary value to current cardiovascular risk assessment models, thereby refining risk stratification in patients with hypertension.Keywords: Subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR), China Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction model (China-PAR), cardiovascular risk factors, hypertension

Introduction

Hypertension, as a major global public health challenge, is a primary modifiable risk factor for malignant cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (1). One of the core pathophysiological mechanisms involves increasing arterial stiffness, leading to elevated central arterial systolic blood pressure (SBP), reduced diastolic blood pressure (DBP), increased left ventricular afterload, and impaired diastolic function (2,3). These hemodynamic changes, especially reduced diastolic perfusion pressure, directly damage the myocardium and impair oxygen delivery to the subendocardium. This leads to subendocardial ischemia even without significant coronary stenosis, thereby increasing cardiovascular event risk (4-6).

To quantify the balance between oxygen supply and consumption in the subendocardial myocardium, Buckberg et al. proposed the concept of subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR) in 1972 (5). SEVR non-invasively reflects the perfusion reserve of the subendocardial myocardium by calculating the ratio of diastolic to systolic pressure-time integral in the central arterial pressure waveform (7). SEVR is correlated with coronary flow reserve and is an effective indicator for evaluating coronary microvascular function in patients with hypertension (6). Clinical observations, through comparisons between invasive and non-invasive methods, have validated that SEVR reliably reflects myocardial oxygen supply-demand imbalance (8). SEVR reduction independently predicts future cardiovascular events in hypertensive patients, providing a new perspective for early identification of high-risk individuals (4). Furthermore, as a key determinant of SEVR, the assessment of arterial stiffness itself is closely related to cardiovascular risk, further supporting the clinical value of SEVR (9,10).

In the practice of cardiovascular risk stratification, scoring systems derived from cohort studies serve as indispensable tools. The China Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction model (China-PAR) score, developed by the China Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) Risk Project, integrates traditional risk factors including age, blood pressure, lipid levels, smoking status, and diabetes to evaluate the 10-year ASCVD (10Y-ASCVD) risk among the Chinese population (11). A systematic review and meta-analysis have confirmed that this score demonstrates excellent predictive efficacy (12). The China-PAR score has found extensive application in clinical practice. Nevertheless, conventional scoring systems predominantly rely on static risk factors and fail to integrate physiological markers that reflect the dynamic equilibrium of arterial function and myocardial perfusion, such as SEVR.

Therefore, the relationship between SEVR and the China-PAR risk score, along with their combined value, remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the correlation between SEVR and the China-PAR score in hypertensive patients, and to assess the incremental value of SEVR in identifying high-risk individuals beyond conventional risk stratification, thereby offering new evidence for more precise cardiovascular risk assessment. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0372/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study is a cross-sectional observational study. Subjects aged ≥18 years were recruited from Health Examination Center at Shanghai General Hospital between January 2023 and November 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: complete echocardiographic and SEVR measurement data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) a history of peripheral vascular disease; (II) acute cardiovascular events (e.g., acute myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome) within the past three months; (III) arrhythmias (including those requiring cardiac pacing); (IV) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50%; and (V) severe comorbidities (e.g., liver or kidney failure or malignant tumors) that could significantly affect cardiovascular health. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of participants were enrolled in the final analysis. The detailed process of cohort selection is presented in Figure 1.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital (No. 2021KY057). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Demographic and clinical data

Self-reported data on chronological age, gender, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption were collected. Height and weight were measured using calibrated instruments with participants wearing light clothing without shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula: BMI = body weight (kg)/[height (m)]2.

General information

SBP, DBP, and heart rate (HR) were measured using the PASESA AVE-2000Pro device (DAIWA Healthcare, Shenzhen, China) after ≥5 min of seated rest, with three consecutive measurements taken and the average value was recorded. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥140 mmHg, DBP ≥90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication within 2 weeks, according to the “2010 Chinese Hypertension Guidelines” and the “2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension” (1,13,14). The hypertension group was outpatients diagnosed as hypertension according to the above standards. The control group was age-matched outpatients without hypertension during the same period.

Laboratory examination

Venous blood samples were obtained after overnight fasting (≥8 h). Biochemical parameters including hemoglobin, creatinine, albumin, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). All biochemical analyses were performed using standardized laboratory methodologies.

Cardiovascular risk scores

The 10Y-ASCVD risk was calculated using the China-PAR risk prediction model, a tool specifically developed for the Chinese population (12). Risk factors included age, sex, SBP, smoking status, history of diabetes, region, physical examination parameters (blood pressure, waist circumference), and fasting blood tests (TC, HDL-C).

According to the risk stratification standard of the China-PAR model, participants were divided into a high-risk group and a moderate-low-risk group. High risk was defined as an absolute risk >10% derived from the China-PAR score, while moderate-low-risk was defined as an absolute risk ≤10%, which encompasses the two original categories: low risk (<5%) and moderate risk (5–10%).

The rationale for combining moderate-risk and low-risk into a single group was as follows: (I) the primary objective of this study was to identify independent factors associated with high ASCVD risk; (II) moderate-risk and low-risk patients follow highly similar clinical management pathways: both prioritize lifestyle interventions, have comparable LDL-C control targets (<2.6 vs. <3.4 mmol/L), are eligible for moderate-intensity statin therapy, and share consistent follow-up monitoring content and intervals. Therefore, combining the two groups is both clinically reasonable and analytically efficient (11,15).

Measurement of arterial stiffness

The arterial velocity index (AVI) and arterial pressure volume index (API) were noninvasively assessed via brachial oscillometry (PASESA AVE-2000Pro, DAIWA Healthcare, Shenzhen, China) according to manufacturer specifications.

Measurement of SEVR

SEVR values were obtained using the CONNEQT pulse vascular biomonitoring device (CARDIEX, Sydney, Australia), based on SphygmoCor® technology. SEVR is calculated as the ratio of the diastolic pressure-time index (DPTI) to the systolic pressure-time index (SPTI): SEVR = DPTI/SPTI ×100%. DPTI represents the area under the diastolic portion of the central aortic pressure waveform, while SPTI represents the area under the systolic portion. SEVR measurements were obtained under standardized conditions: the room was maintained at 22–25 °C, and participants were instructed to rest seated for 5 minutes while wearing light clothing.

Echocardiographic examination

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Philips EPIQ7 color Doppler ultrasound system with an S5-1 transducer (frequency range: 1–5 MHz). All measurements were conducted by a single sonographer with over 5 years of experience in cardiac ultrasound diagnostics. In the parasternal long-axis view, the following parameters were measured using M-mode: left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), end-diastolic left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPWD), diastolic interventricular septal thickness (IVSD), and LVEF, which was calculated using the biplane Simpson’s method. In the apical four-chamber view, the following parameters were measured using pulsed-wave Doppler and tissue Doppler imaging: peak early diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E), peak early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e’), and peak late diastolic mitral inflow velocity (A). Derived indices, including E/e’ and E/A ratios, were calculated based on these measurements.

Statistical analysis

Data were categorized and presented as frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables. For quantitative variables, normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range). To compare the mean values between two groups, the independent t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for skewed distributions. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with SEVR. All participants were analyzed together, and group assignment (hypertensive vs. control) was entered as a covariate in the multivariate models to account for potential differences between the two groups. Pearson correlation coefficients (for continuous variables) and Spearman correlation coefficients (for categorical variables) were used to assess correlations between the studied variables.

To evaluate the independent factors associated with high 10Y-ASCVDR, the dependent variable was defined as a binary outcome: high-risk group (>10%) vs. moderate-low-risk group (≤10%). Univariate logistic regression was first performed, and variables with a P<0.05 were then entered into a multivariate logistic regression model using the stepwise regression. Independent variables included age, gender, smoking status, and echocardiographic parameters. The diagnostic performance of SEVR in identifying individuals with 10Y-ASCVDR >10% was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The optimal cutoff value was determined by selecting the point closest to the upper left corner of the ROC curve, reflecting the balance between sensitivity and specificity. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for each parameter to assess its predictive accuracy. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General characteristics of the study population

A total of 1,380 patients were enrolled, including 445 males (32.25%) and 935 females (67.75%). Of these, 782 patients were in the hypertensive group and 598 in the control group. The mean ages of the hypertensive and control groups were 52 years and 51 years, respectively. Significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, DBP, and HR (P<0.05). The proportion of participants with a 10Y-ASCVDR >10% was significantly higher in the hypertensive group (12.8%, n=100) compared to the control group (9.4%, n=56) (P=0.047) (Table 1).

Logistic analyses of relative factors for high 10Y-ASCVDR

Table 4 presents the logistic regression analysis of SEVR association with 10-year high ASCVDR. In Model 1 (univariate analysis), SEVR showed a significant inverse association with high 10Y-ASCVDR [odds ratio (OR) =0.973, P<0.001]. Model 2 (basic risk factor-adjusted) additionally adjusted for male sex, current smoking, SBP, and HR. SEVR maintained its significant inverse association with enhanced effect size (OR =0.953, P<0.001). Male sex (OR =1.973), current smoking (OR =2.665), and SBP (OR =1.015) were significant risk factors (all P<0.01), while HR showed a protective effect (OR =0.949, P<0.001). Model 3 (fully adjusted) incorporated age, HDL-C, TC, and hypertension. SEVR lost statistical significance (OR =0.996, P=0.805). Significant independent predictors included: increasing age (OR =1.725), male sex (OR =23.273), HDL-C (OR =0.002), TC (OR =2.807), and hypertension (OR =8.087) (all P<0.05). Current smoking showed borderline significance (OR =4.304, P=0.092).

Application of SEVR in cardiovascular risk stratification

In Figure 3, both the hypertensive and control groups were further divided into two subgroups based on 10Y-ASCVDR: high-risk group, defined as 10Y-ASCVDR >10%, and the low-risk group, defined as 10Y-ASCVDR ≤10%. The results showed that in the hypertensive group, the SEVR values were (122.72±15.09)% in the high-risk subgroup and (131.92±21.93)% in the low-risk subgroup. In the control group, the SEVR values were (123.91±13.47)% in the high-risk subgroup and (137.67±21.18)% in the low-risk subgroup. The SEVR values in the high-risk subgroups were significantly lower than those in the low-risk subgroup (P<0.05).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the clinical significance of SEVR in patients with hypertension. Key findings include: (I) reduced SEVR is independently associated with higher 10Y-ASCVD risk, an association robust to age adjustment; (II) SEVR provides incremental predictive value beyond conventional indicators (e.g., blood pressure, HR) when added to the China-PAR model; (III) this predictive utility is more pronounced in individuals with lower baseline traditional risk, suggesting potential for early identification of occult high-risk individuals; (IV) SEVR is significantly correlated with SBP and HR, reinforcing myocardial oxygen supply-demand imbalance as a core mechanistic pathway; (V) these findings are primarily applicable to specific hypertensive subgroups and require prospective validation.

The observed female predominance in this cohort likely reflects selection bias: exclusion criteria for severe cardiovascular complications disproportionately excluded males, and females demonstrated better study adherence. The inclusion of perimenopausal/postmenopausal women—a group at elevated cardiovascular risk—may have further contributed. Importantly, the association between SEVR and ASCVD risk remained robust after rigorous adjustment for confounders, indicating minimal influence on the study conclusions.

The China-PAR model incorporates a series of established cardiovascular risk factors, including age, gender, SBP, smoking status, history of diabetes, region, waist circumference, TC, and HDL-C. Among these, hypertension is one of the major contributors to an elevated 10Y-ASCVD risk. In the present study, although the hypertensive group exhibited a higher proportion of high-risk individuals, it is noteworthy that 56 participants in the control group also had a 10Y-ASCVD risk >10%. This observation reflects the multifactorial nature of ASCVD risk: even in the absence of a diagnosis of hypertension, the accumulation of other risk factors (e.g., older age, dyslipidemia, smoking) can still result in a high‑risk score. Consequently, the binary distinction between “hypertensive” and “control” groups does not perfectly separate high-risk from low-risk individuals, and some control participants may carry a substantial risk burden. This overlap underscores the importance of using continuous risk assessment tools rather than relying solely on diagnostic labels, and it supports the rationale for incorporating SEVR as an additional physiological metric to refine risk stratification.

More importantly, SEVR captures pathophysiological information beyond traditional risk indicators. Lower SEVR remained independently associated with higher China-PAR scores even after adjusting for blood pressure and HR, suggesting it reflects early subclinical microvascular damage and impaired myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance (16,17). In our cohort, SBP, HR, and hemoglobin were independent predictors of SEVR, consistent with prior reports (7,8). Elevated SBP increases afterload, while tachycardia shortens diastolic perfusion time—both exacerbating oxygen demand-supply mismatch (8). The higher resting HR in hypertensive patients aligns with this mechanism, and recent evidence links arterial stiffness to diastolic dysfunction (18,19), reinforcing impaired diastolic perfusion as a common pathway. Integrating SEVR with static risk tools thus enhances risk stratification accuracy and provides mechanistic insights (11,20), aligning with the shift toward personalized, lifelong risk assessment (21).

Our findings corroborate existing evidence. A 12-month prospective study demonstrated that SEVR predicts cardiovascular events in hypertension (4), and reduced SEVR is associated with microvascular dysfunction and arterial stiffness (22). As an indicator of myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance, low SEVR signifies subendocardial ischemia risk, complementing studies focused on coronary stenosis (8,23). SEVR is particularly sensitive in conditions affecting myocardial perfusion, such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD), where SEVR ≤130% predicts higher mortality (24,25). These findings underscore SEVR’s dual clinical utility in assessing functional ischemia and predicting long-term cardiovascular risk.

Interestingly, the predictive performance of SEVR varied across populations. Although the inverse SEVR-ASCVD risk association was consistent in both hypertensive and control groups, discriminative ability (e.g., AUC, specificity) was superior in controls. This may reflect the complex risk profile in hypertension, which attenuates the discriminative power of individual physiological parameters. In lower-risk individuals, SEVR-derived signals of vascular dysfunction and reduced myocardial perfusion reserve may serve as more sensitive markers for early, occult risk. Thus, SEVR may be particularly valuable for refining risk stratification in traditionally low-to-moderate-risk populations, facilitating earlier prevention. This observation aligns with SEVR’s integrative nature, capturing coronary microcirculation, aortic buffering capacity, and oxygen-carrying efficiency (24,25).

In this study, we collapsed the original three-tier risk stratification (low, moderate, high) into a binary outcome variable (high risk >10% vs. moderate-low risk ≤10%) for logistic regression analysis. This decision was based on two main considerations. From a clinical perspective, moderate-risk and low-risk patients follow similar routine prevention pathways, whereas high-risk patients require more intensive interventions. According to the “2024 Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management (Primary Care Edition)”, moderate-risk and low-risk individuals share the same management framework: both take lifestyle modifications (diet, exercise, weight control, smoking cessation, and alcohol restriction) as the cornerstone. In terms of lipid-lowering pharmacotherapy, both recommend moderate-intensity statins as first-line treatment with the same initiation threshold. Both require regular risk reassessment and adjustment of the regimen based on LDL-C levels during follow-up monitoring. In contrast, high-risk patients require lower LDL-C targets and earlier initiation of combination therapy. Therefore, merging moderate-risk and low-risk into a single “moderate-low-risk” category aligns with real-world clinical decision-making. This dichotomization is also consistent with current primary prevention guidelines (26).

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional, single-center design precludes causal inference. Second, sex imbalance may limit generalizability. Third, heterogeneity in antihypertensive medication use may have introduced residual confounding, and no subgroup analysis by treatment status was performed. Fourth, these findings are from a hypertensive cohort; applicability to normotensive individuals or other cardiovascular diseases requires validation. Fifth, the control group included 56 individuals with a 10Y-ASCVD risk >10%, which may have raised its baseline risk and biased group comparisons. Hypertension is only one risk factor; age, lipid profile, and smoking are equally important. This distribution, though real-world, calls for stratified analyses. Future studies will adopt stricter criteria or exclude such high-risk controls, and stratify by age, lipids, or metabolic abnormalities to validate SEVR’s independent contribution across different risk spectra. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to assess the generalizability and clinical utility of SEVR.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SEVR is inversely associated with 10Y-ASCVD risk in individuals with hypertension and remains an independent indicator after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors. These findings suggest that SEVR may capture additional features of the cardiovascular interplay that are not fully reflected by traditional risk markers. As a noninvasive and cost-effective biomarker, SEVR may provide complementary value to existing cardiovascular risk assessment models, thereby improving risk stratification and supporting more individualized preventive strategies in hypertensive populations.
