The association between medicinal herbs consumption and body weight and composition: a hospital based cross-sectional study
Authors: Vahid Arabi, Bahareh Sasanfar, Mina Darand, Mohammadtaghi Sarebanhassanabadi, Seyed Mostafa Seyedhosseini, Sara Jambarsang, Maryam Motallaei, Marzieh Taftian, Fatemeh Sadat Mirjalili, Amin Salehi-Abargouei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvidence on the effect of medicinal herbs on body weight and composition are conflicting. This study aimed to investigate the association between the frequency of medicinal herbs consumption and anthropometric indices in patients undergoing coronary angiography. This cross-sectional study was conducted among 662 subjects aged 35–75 years who were referred for coronary angiography to Afshar Hospital, Yazd, Iran. Anthropometric indices were evaluated using standard tools, and the frequency of medicinal herbs consumption was assessed by a predefined questionnaire. Weekly or daily consumption of saffron was positively associated with abdominal obesity based on waist circumference and also body mass index (BMI, P < 0.05). In addition, A higher intake of foeniculum vulgare and sumac was associated with increased waist circumference and percentage of visceral fat, respectively (P < 0.05). However, weekly or daily consumption of cinnamon was associated with a lower visceral fat percentage (P = 0.01). Body fat percentage was significantly lower in subjects who did not consume Carum carvi L and Matricaria chamomilla (P < 0.05). Furthermore, a weekly/daily intake of Mentha pulegium L and Matricaria chamomilla, and Monthly intake of Lavandula angustifolia were associated with a higher percentage of skeletal muscle (P < 0.05). Medicinal herbs consumption might be associated with anthropometric indices. Future prospective studies are recommended to confirm these findings.Keywords: Herbal medicine, Anthropometric indices, Obesity, Coronary artery diseaseSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Obesity and overweight, defined by abnormal and excessive accumulation of fat in adipose tissue, are important factors in the incidence of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), diabetes, cancer, and depression1–4. Lifestyle changes, the use of anti-obesity drugs, nutritional interventions, and increased physical activity, are suggested for weight loss5,6. As the anti-obesity products might have adverse effects on human health, the use of natural products for weight loss has considerably increased7,8.

Recent studies have shown that the presence of effective compounds in medicinal herbs, such as cinnamon, ginger and cumin, can significantly improve anthropometric indices9–11. In addition, some animal studies have shown that by inhibiting appetite, Nigella sativa might have anti-obesity properties, thereby improving anthropometric indices12. Cinnamon, a plant belonging to the Lauraceae family, is regarded as an anti-bloating, blood purifier, antifungal, antiviral, and aids in digestion13,14. Cinnamon consumption has been associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity, possibly linked to its active compounds such as cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and cinnamic acid15,16, and some studies have shown that cinnamon may activate transient receptor potential-ankyrin receptor 1 (TRPA1) and genes related to lipid metabolism, thereby increasing the oxidation of fatty acids and by reducing the rate of gastric emptying and decreasing serum ghrelin levels, it has anti-obesity properties15,17. However, some studies did not find any link between cinnamon consumption and anthropometric indices such as body mass index, waist circumference, and body weight18, which further studies need to be done. Matricaria chamomilla which is one of the most popular and well-known medicinal herbs in the world19,20, due to having bioactive compounds such as essential oils (e.g. α-bisabolol and chamazulene) coumarin, phenolic and flavonoid compounds can play an important role in preventing obesity and its metabolic disorders21–27. Recent studies have shown that phenolic and active compounds in Matricaria chamomilla have a protective role against oxidative stress by modulating signaling pathways related to energy metabolism, adipogenesis and inflammatory processes. Also, in vitro experiments show that compounds such as apigenin and hydroxy-4-methoxy cinnamic acid in Matricaria chamomilla flower can inhibit the activity of alpha-amylase and maltase enzymes, modulate digestion and absorption of carbohydrates and thereby play an effective role in reducing obesity and improving body metabolism25,28–31. Some studies on saffron have shown that consumption of this medicinal herb can have a significant effect on improving anthropometric indices such as waist circumference32,33. Also, the results of a randomized clinical trial study show that sumac consumption can significantly reduce body weight, body mass index and waist circumference34. However, the results of other studies were inconsistent and did not have a significant effect on weight loss, body mass index and waist circumference in people with consumption of saffron and sumac35,36.

Medicinal herbs have long been favored and consumed by communities, especially the Iranian community, due to their affordability and accessibility. The dearth of extensive research, the presence of beneficial compounds for cardiovascular health, and the potential for improved anthropometric indices prompted us to initiate a cross-sectional analytical study. We aimed to investigate the correlation between the frequency of herbal medicine consumption and anthropometric indices in patients undergoing coronary angiography.

Materials and methods

Participants

From June 2020 to November 2021, this observational and cross-sectional study called Iranian-CARDIO was conducted on adult outpatients who were referred to Afshar Hospital, which is a Referral Hospital, affiliated with the University of Medical Sciences in Yazd, Iran, for coronary angiography37. The detailed protocol of the Iranian-CARDIO is reported elsewhere37. We informed the participants about the purpose of this study and obtained their written consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran (IR.SSU.SPH.REC.1400.224), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Adults aged 35–75 years old who underwent coronary angiography were eligible for inclusion in this study, while individuals with infection, malignancy, inflammatory disease, any other organ failure, and those taking supplements were not. Those affected by serious diseases such as chronic heart failure, individuals with a history of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), myocardial infarction (MI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), chronic kidney disease stage 3 or above, specific liver disease or receiving medication for liver disease, specific perception or psychological disorder, immunodeficiency, grade 3 obesity (BMI ≥ 40.0 kg/m2), pregnancy and lactation, and restriction of food intake through the mouth were not allowed to participate in the study.

Medicinal herbs intake assessment

The frequency of medicinal herbs consumption such as Cinnamon, Cuminum cyminum, Carum carvi L, Zataria multiflora, Mentha, Mentha pulegium L, Ginger, Foeniculum vulgare, Saffron, Echium amoenum, Matricaria chamomilla, Lavandula angustifolia, Alcea rosea, Sumac, Nigella sativa, Cardamom, Garlic, and Turmeric was assessed using a researcher-developed questionnaire. Intake frequency was categorized as ‘never’, ‘monthly’, or ‘weekly/daily’. The questionnaire has been described in detail elsewhere38.

Anthropometric and body composition assessment

While the participants were wearing light clothes, their body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg on a scale (Omron BF51, Korea), and the height was measured in the standing position by a wall-mounted tape to the nearest 0.1 cm. Waist circumference (WC) was measured using a non-stretchable measuring tape to the nearest 0.1 cm in a standing position using a standard protocol37. Using an Omron BF51 scale, the percentages of body fat, skeletal muscle, and visceral fat were measured. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

Assessment of other variables

We collected information on each participant’s sociodemographic characteristics including marital status, menstrual status, economic status, job, educational levels, addiction to cigarettes, drugs and alcohol, and diabetes. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to evaluate the participants’ daily physical activity39, and the results were expressed as metabolic equivalent (MET) hours per week40.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using Stata software version 14 (STATA Corp., College Station, TX, USA). The analysis of covariant (ANCOVA) with Bonferroni correction was used to compare anthropometric indices across tertiles of medicinal herbs intake in the crude and adjusted models. The analyses were first adjusted for age, energy (continuous) and sex (categorical) in the first model and additionally for married (categorical), menopausal status (yes vs. no), physical activity (continuous), economic status (low, medium, high), job (categorical), educational level (categorical), smoking (non, former, current), drug addiction, Diabetes (yes, no) and BMI (continuous) in model 2. Multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni test. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.

In this study, the mean physical activity in those with overweight and obesity was significantly lower compared to normal-weight participants. Anthropometric indices of participants across category of herbs intake

The mean anthropometric indices in the categories of medicinal herbs consumption in the crude and adjusted models are shown in Table 3. The mean waist circumference of those who consumed Foeniculum vulgare daily/weekly was significantly higher than those who did not consume it in the fully adjusted model (P = 0.001). Visceral fat was significantly lower in subjects who consumed cinnamon on a daily/weekly basis compared to those who did not, in the adjusted model (P = 0.01). Conversely, those who consumed Mentha, Saffron, and Nigella Sativa on a daily/weekly basis had higher mean visceral fat than those in the non-consuming group, in the crude model (P < 0.05). This higher mean visceral fat was found in higher sumac consumers in both crude and modified models, too (P < 0.05). The mean body fat percentage in the subjects taking cinnamon, Cuminum Cyminum, Carum Carvi L, Mentha, ginger, Lavandula angustifolia, and Turmeric on a daily/weekly basis was significantly higher in the crude model than those in non-consumers (P < 0.05). After adjusting for confounding variables, a higher body fat percentage was associated with a higher consumption of Carum Carvi L (P ≤ 0.05). Subjects in the second tertile of Matricaria chamomilla had a higher body fat percentage (P ≤ 0.05). However, people who were in the second tertile of sumac consume had a lower mean body fat percentage in the crude model (P = 0.01). In contrast, the monthly users of Sumac had a higher mean percentage of skeletal muscle mass than the non-consuming group in the crude model (P = 0.003). This higher percentage of skeletal muscle was found in the adjusted model in daily/weekly users of Mentha pulegium L, too. Also, individuals in the second tertile of Matricaria chamomilla and Lavandula angustifolia consumption had higher percentage of skeletal muscle in the adjusted model (P < 0.05). In contrast, higher consumption of cinnamon and Cuminum Cyminum was associated with a lower mean percentage of skeletal muscle in the crude model (P < 0.05). Higher consumption of Mentha was also significantly associated with a higher body mass index in the crude model (P < 0.05). This increase in BMI was observed in daily/weekly saffron consumers in the adjusted model (P = 0.02).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this cross-sectional study is among the first to examine associations between consumption of a wide range of medicinal herbs and anthropometric indices. In this sample, saffron consumption was associated with higher abdominal obesity and BMI; higher intake of Foeniculum vulgare was associated with greater WC; greater cinnamon consumption was associated with lower visceral fat percentage; Carum carvi consumption was associated with higher body fat percentage; sumac consumption was positively associated with visceral fat percentage; and higher consumption of Mentha pulegium was associated with greater skeletal muscle percentage.

Several studies found no effectiveness of saffron on anthropometric indices including, weight, BMI, and WC41,42. In addition, a meta-analysis by Tahmasbi et al. did not show any significant effect of saffron supplementation on body weight, BMI, WC, and also hip circumference43. Several mechanisms have been proposed for saffron intake as a potential way for obesity management44. Some studies suggest that saffron may reduce food intake via anorectic effects. In addition, due to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant constituents, saffron has been proposed to inhibit adipocyte differentiation and influence glucose and lipid metabolism44; however, evidence remains inconclusive. Nevertheless, in the present study, saffron consumption was directly associated with abdominal obesity and BMI, whereas no significant associations were observed between saffron intake and weight, waist circumference, or other body composition parameters. This positive association may be attributable to the fact that saffron is often consumed in calorie-dense foods and desserts, which could confound the observed relationship.

Saghafi et al. found that fennel had no significant effect on body weight, BMI, WC, hip circumferences, or fat distribution in postmenopausal women with excess body weight, compared to the control group45. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis revealed no association between foeniculum vulgare consumption and body composition such as body weight, BMI, and WC46. However, we found that a higher consumption of foeniculum vulgare was related to the higher WC. Further investigations are needed to evaluate the effect of fennel on both appetite and body composition. In addition, phytoestrogens, active biological compounds in fennel, might lead to a slight raise in body weight and fat45.

In our study, cinnamon was inversely associated with visceral fat percentage. In line with our investigation, a study by Ziegenfuss et al. revealed that cinnamon extract supplementation for 12 weeks led to a statistically significant reduction in fat mass, in prediabetic overweight subjects47. Also, in a randomized controlled clinical trial (RCT) by Vafa et al. taking three grams of cinnamon per day for eight weeks significantly decreased body weight, BMI, and body fat mass48. A systematic review of 21 RCTs with 1480 participants showed that cinnamon supplementation significantly reduced BMI, body weight, and WHR49. The possible beneficial associations between cinnamon consumption and body composition have been attributed to improvements in insulin sensitivity and antioxidant effects50. Previous evidence has revealed that cinnamon extracts might activate glycogen synthase, stimulate glucose uptake, and inhibit glycogen synthase kinase 3β51–53. In addition, cinnamon with its antioxidant effects can beneficially affect hormone signaling through adipokine cross-talk54,55. For instance, Chrysohoou et al. showed an inverse link between antioxidant capacity and body fat54. Moreover, evidence that catechin-rich teas can enhance fat oxidation suggests that some compounds in cinnamon might exert similar effects, although direct evidence is limited56.

There were limited studies assessing the link between carum carvi and body composition. A RCT by Kazemipoor et al. showed that the supplementation of caraway extract significantly reduced body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage in overweight and obese women57. These effects have been attributed to bioactive compounds that may modulate gut microbiota balance and stimulate apoptosis in preadipocytes58,59. However, in our cross-sectional data we observed a positive association between carum carvi consumption and body fat percentage This apparent contradiction may reflect the difference between standardized extract supplementation used in RCTs and the varied, self-reported dietary consumption captured in our study (differences in preparation, dose and frequency), as well as residual confounding inherent to observational designs.

We found that the higher sumac consumption was associated with the higher visceral fat percentage. Previous studies have reported inconsistent results on the relationship between sumac intake and anthropometric indices. A study among thirty adults with dyslipidemia showed no significant changes in waist-to-hip ratio, hip circumference, and waist circumference during use of sumac capsules (500 mg/twice daily)60. Whereas, Consuming 3 g of sumac per day for 12 weeks significantly reduced body weight, BMI, waist and hip circumference, body fat, and visceral fat level in overweight or obese women34.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating the relationship between the consumption of a wide range of traditional herbs and anthropometric indices. However, there are several limitations to consider. The cross-sectional design prevents any inference of causality. While the homogeneity of our study population may increase internal validity, it limits the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations. Another limitation is the reliance on self-reported frequency of herbal intake, without detailed information on portion size or dose, which may affect effect estimates and interpretation of the observed associations. Although we attempted to control for several confounding variables, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out. Additionally, given the large number of herbs and outcomes assessed, some analyses are exploratory in nature, and residual multiple-testing bias cannot be excluded. Moreover, differences in study design, exposure assessment, dosage, and intervention conditions between our study and previous randomized trials may partly explain the observed inconsistencies. Future studies should aim to collect quantitative intake data to more accurately assess the potential impact of medicinal herbs on health outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this cross-sectional sample, consumption of Foeniculum vulgare was associated with higher waist circumference, while higher cinnamon intake was associated with lower visceral fat percentage. Non-consumers of Carum carvi and Matricaria chamomilla had lower body fat percentages. Higher tertiles of Mentha pulegium, Matricaria chamomilla, and Lavandula angustifolia intake were associated with greater skeletal muscle percentage. Participants who did not consume saffron had lower BMI. Prospective studies with a better design and a higher number of participants should be conducted to investigate the causalities between herbs consumption and anthropometric indices.
