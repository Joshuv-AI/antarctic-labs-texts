Hematological, inflammatory and serological responses among COVID-19 patients admitted to intensive care unit
Authors: Mirette A Morgan, Sarra E Saleh, Azza H Salamoni, Mohammad Y Alshahrani, Khaled M Aboshanab
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: To correlate hematological, inflammatory indicators and serological responses among COVID-19 patients to point out the significant biomarkers for disease management and prognosis.Materials & methods: Standard analytical and molecular methods were used to assess various inflammatory and serological Responses among COVID-19 patients (ICU- (n = 99) and non-ICU patients (n = 64) as compared with health control (n = 40).Results: Significant differences in the Hb, WBC, Lymphocyte count, CRP and serum ferritin (p < 0.05) were observed. Patients' IgM/IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 were associated with increased CRP, LDH and serum ferritin levels.Conclusion: A significant association between serum IgG/IgM and ICU admission was observed. Although serum ferritin and LDH can offer information about the extent of inflammation, they are exclusive factors for ICU admission.Keywords: : COVID-19, diagnosis, IgG, IgM, RT-PCR, SARS-CoV2

Introduction

The global response to this severe pandemic disease relies heavily on clinical laboratory testing, which plays a vital role in early diagnosis, treatment and epidemiological surveillance. Molecular, serological and biochemical diagnostic methods have been applied in the detection of COVID-19 cases [1,2]. The RT-PCR-based molecular methods have become the gold standard technique used across the world for confirming COVID-19 infection based on the qualitative detection of the nucleic acids of SARS-CoV-2 [3]. However, it might show a false negative result, it can only detect 71% of COVID-19 infections depending on the time of symptoms appeared [4]. To prevent possible cross-reaction with other prevalent coronaviruses, the assay should include at least two molecular targets. The optimum design typically involves at least one structural region and one nonstructural region to reduce the impact of genetic drift [5]. A comprehensive retrospective study examining the epidemiological characteristics of COVID-19 patients in the northern region of Iran showed that the positive RT-PCR results did not show any significant variations between males and females [2]. However, there was a substantial increase in positive cases as age advanced [2]. In another study in 2023 in Pakistan, the findings indicated that the E and N genes, which serve as additional and secondary targets, showed simultaneous positive results alongside the ORF1b (encoded nonstructural proteins 12–16) and Rdrp gene (encoded RNA-dependent RNA polymerase [6]. Moreover, it was confirmed that both the E and N genes play a critical role in the early detection of COVID-19 [7]. Serological tests are performed to detect IgG, IgM, IgA, or total antibodies against SARS-CoV-2. They are economical and simpler to perform than RT-PCR and aid in SARS-CoV-2 antibody seroprevalence [8,9]. Interestingly, there are currently several commercially available serological assays [10]. A cross-sectional study showed that among all non-hospitalized patients examined, 82% tested positive for IgG antibodies and 38% tested positive for IgM antibodies [11]. Interestingly, 31% of the patients had both positive IgM and IgG antibody values [11]. However, no gender-based disparity was observed in the IgM-positive group [12,13].

Elderdery et al. found that hemoglobin (Hb), red blood corpuscles (RBC) count, hematocrit (HCT) and some other hematological parameters were all significantly decreased in COVID-19 subjects compared with controls [14]. Another study in Southern Thailand revealed that both white blood cell (WBC) and platelet counts have been identified as independent predictors of mortality [15]. Platelets to white blood cells ratio (PWR) was a better predictor of mortality and facilitated the early recognition of patients requiring intensive care upon admission [15]. Elderdery et al. also found that there is no significant difference in total WBC, lymphocyte and platelet count median values between patients of the SARS-CoV2 group and the control group [14]. As for the Lymphocytes, another study in Greece showed that lymphopenia is not an effective indicator for distinguishing between COVID-19 patients and negative cases and it may be associated with other causes [16].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, or NLR, is a well-established prognostic factor that has been shown to independently correlate with mortality in the general population of COVID-19 subsets. Furthermore, NLR has been demonstrated to predict the prognosis of pneumonia and is considered when making decisions about the hospitalization and recuperation of COVID-19 patients who have pneumonia as previously reported [17–19]. Moreover, Regolo et al. (2023) revealed that both the NLR and CRP as continuous variables with PaO2/FiO2 ratio (P/F) and the findings revealed that CRP and lymphocytes are significantly and directly linked to P/F, while the effect of inflammation on P/F, was also mediated by neutrophils in addition to CRP [20].

Numerous inflammatory biomarkers have been reported with severe COVID-19, revealing an immunochemical profile due to a “cytokine storm” such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and ferritin that can be useful in assessing disease severity [21,22]. A retrospective analysis was done showing the CRP levels in severe cases of COVID-19 are higher compared with non-severe cases, and this difference is statistically significant with a p-value less than 0.001 [23]. Monitoring CRP levels continuously can give important information about the development of the disease and how well it responds to treatment [24]. The higher initial CRP levels in COVID-19 patients can be associated with development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and overall lung deterioration [25]. In the context of COVID-19, Shakaroun et al. revealed that elevated ferritin levels have been associated with severe disease, showing a strong ability to predict ICU admission, the requirement for mechanical ventilation and mortality while in the hospital [26]. Nonetheless, tracking ferritin levels throughout the hospital stay did not offer extra predictive significance [26]. Alroomi et al. research illustrated that elevated ferritin levels independently forecast mortality among patients with SARS-CoV-2 while they are in the hospital [27]. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an inflammatory marker that converts lactate to pyruvate to produce energy and serves as a broad indicator of acute or chronic tissue injury of the lung as well as impaired blood flow and oxygen delivery [12,28]. Masumoto et al. found that COVID-19 patients and pre-existing cardiovascular conditions who had a significantly elevated LDH level (LDH >355 U/l) upon hospital admission were at a higher risk of mortality during their hospital stay [29]. While Mir et al. supposed that an LDH >240 U/l in patients >60 years old, could imply the serious phase of the disease [30]. Although several studies have been done in this era, it was obvious the presence of discrepancies in the laboratory data and their correlation to the health status of COVID-19 patients and disease prognosis [24–27].The huge variability and conflicting information in COVID-19 patient clinical and laboratory findings have made it difficult for the medical community to gain a comprehensive understanding and develop clear, reliable guidelines for managing COVID-19 cases. Moreover, most of these differences in such results were highly attributed to the geographical areas, patients' clinical conditions, immunological status, age, and type of tested laboratory findings which still necessitate further studies and analysis. The objective of our study was to assess some clinically relevant serum proteins, providing analysis of the obtained findings to use for disease monitoring, prognosis and control. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate and statistically correlate the inflammatory and serological responses among COVID-19 patients in Egypt when compared with healthy individuals. In addition, we evaluated the respective data for the COVID-19 ICU patients compared with non-ICU patients for the rationale of optimizing COVID-19 diagnosis, disease prognosis, management and proper time intervention.

Materials & methods

Collection & processing of blood samples

Blood samples were gathered from 163 patients who had been diagnosed with COVID-19 and were admitted to Om El-Masreen-El Giza Hospital, El Giza and Abbasia Chest Diseases Hospital, Cairo, Egypt during the period from January 2022 to June 2022. Among these patients, 99 (60.7%) were admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) due to the severity of their condition and 64 (39.3%) were non-ICU. Additionally, we included a control group of 40 healthy individuals for comparison. The inclusion criteria were: positive COVID-19 patients, positive RT-PCR according to https://diagnostics.roche.com/global/en/products/instruments/cobas-6800-ins-2693.html (accessed on 29 August 2023), both sexes were presented, age from 27–81 years old, unvaccinated against COVID-19 plus suffering from signs and symptoms of SARS-CoV2 infections according to Center Disease and Control (CDC) guidelines [31]. The exclusion criteria were negative COVID-19 patients, negative RT-PCR, vaccinated patients against COVID-19 and absence of signs and symptoms of SARS-CoV2 infections.

The criteria that have been established for the healthy control group were negative RT-PCR, age from 27 to 78 years old, both sexes are presented, no history of previous COVID-19 vaccination, absence of signs and symptoms signs and symptoms of SARS-CoV2 infections according to CDC guidelines [31], and negative SARS-CoV2 sera IgM/IgG.

Blood samples were obtained through venipuncture and then centrifuged to obtain serum which is used in hematological and serological tests. The serum samples were stored at 2–8°C until being processed. The whole study was approved by the Ethics Committee Faculty of Pharmacy at Ain Shams University, Cairo, Egypt (ACUC-FP-ASU RHDIRB2020110301 REC # 203). The demographic information including median age and sex of the tested patient group and control group were determined.

Real-time RT-PCR

A nasopharyngeal swab was collected from 163 patients to detect SARS-CoV-2 using the RT-PCR method. The collected swabs were inserted into sterile tubes containing 2–3 ml of Copan Universal Transport Medium System (UTM-RT) (https://www.copanusa.com/sample-collection-transport-processing/utm-viral-transport/) (accessed on 29 August 2023) (Murrieta, CA 92562), BD™ Universal Viral Transport System (UVT) (https://www.bd.com/en-us/about-bd/distributors-landing-page) (accessed on 29 August 2023) (Brockton, MA 02301, USA), or 0.9% physiological saline which protects the virus viability for 48 hours at room temperature or refrigerated. Real-time-polymerase Chain reaction (RT-PCR) using Cobas® 6800 System (Roche Diagnostics International AG, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) (https://diagnostics.roche.com/global/en/products/instruments/cobas-6800-ins-2693.html) (accessed on 29 August 2023) was used for the qualitative detection of nucleic acids from SARS-CoV-2. Gene targets are Rdrp gene for detecting the ORF1a/b sequence, a nonstructural region that is distinctive to SARS-CoV-2 (target 1), and the E gene (target 2). The result can be summarized as follows: (a) Positive result: if both ORf1a/b gene (target 1) and E gene (target 2) are detected in the sample or if only ORf1a/b is detected; (b) Presumptive positive result: if only E gene (target 2) is detected, this suggests a potential presence of the virus, but further confirmation may be required according to the Cobas® SARS-CoV-2 guidelines. (https://diagnostics.roche.com/global/en/products/params/cobas-sars-cov-2-qualitative-test.html) (accessed on 29 August 2023) The Ct (Cycle threshold) value in COVID-19 using the Cobas 6800 system refers to the cycle number at which the fluorescence signal generated by the amplification of the target viral RNA reaches a detectable threshold which is a semi-quantitative measure used to determine the amount of viral RNA present in a sample. Ct value according to The CDC guidelines is ≤35 [31]. The Ct value is inversely related to the initial concentration of the target genetic material in the sample. A lower Ct value generally suggests a higher concentration of the target, while a higher Ct value indicates a lower concentration.

Laboratory tests

COVID-19 patients versus healthy control

Hematological analysis

In the study, various parameters were performed on 163 patient group and 40 control group (asymptomatic and gave negative results of SARS-CoV-2 using the RT-PCR method) including complete blood count, CRP, LDH and serum ferritin. Blood count analysis was performed using a Celltac Alpha+ MEK-1303K hematology analyzer (https://eu.nihonkohden.com/en/products/invitrodiagnostics/3-part-diff/celltac-mek-1303.html) (accessed on 28 August 2023). This analyzer provides a comprehensive assessment of RBCs, WBCs, platelets, Hb concentration, hematocrit and other related parameters.

Analysis inflammatory markers

For the quantification of CRP and ferritin, a turbidimetric reaction method was employed using Automation Mindray CL 1000i” analyzer (https://www.mindray.com/en/products/laboratory-diagnostics/chemiluminescence-immunoassay/medium-test-volume/cl-1000i) (accessed on 28 August 2023), this method utilizes the formation of antigen-antibody complexes, causing turbidity that can be measured spectrophotmetric method. The increase in turbidity is proportional to the concentration of CRP and ferritin in the serum sample. The LDH levels were measured using a Diatron P500 as previously described in the guidelines. (https://diatron.com/p500--intelligent-walk-away-chemistry) (accessed on 28 August 2023).

Serological analysis

Serological tests are performed using Alinity i (Abbott, Abbott Park, Illinois, USA to analyze anti SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, IgG and IgM. The cutoff of IgG determination is 1.4 Index (S/C) while the Cutoff of IgM is 1.00 Index (S/C). (https://www.corelaboratory.abbott/int/en/offerings/immunoassay.html) (accessed on 28 August 2023).

ICU versus non-ICU patients

The hematological, inflammatory (CRP, LDH and serum ferritin) and serological data (IgM/IgG) of the COVID-9 patients (including those admitted to ICU (ICU patients, n = 99) and those not admitted to ICU (non-ICU, n = 64) have been retrospectively evaluated and analyzed using SPSS 20 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20) and tested for correlation.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using Excel 365, Minitab 20 and SPSS 20 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 20) accepting as a statistically significant result a p-value less than 0.05. Count and Percentage were calculated for qualitative variables while descriptive statistics were calculated for quantitative variables. Missing data and mistyping errors have been checked.

Among these patients, 99 were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and 64 were not admitted to the ICU (hospitalized, non-ICU). Additionally, a control group consisting of 40 healthy individuals (not infected with SARS-CoV-2) was used as a control group. All patients were clinically diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 using RT-PCR analysis. The age range of the patients was between 27 and 81 years, with a mean age of 57.8 ± 1. The control group of healthy individuals had an age range of 19–78 years, with a mean age of 46.07 ± 2.6. Out of the total 163 patients infected with SARS-CoV-2, there are 93 (57.06%) patients were males and 70 (42.94%) were females. While in the Control group, there are 24 (60%) individuals were males and 16 (40%) were females (Table 1).

Discussion

Targeting the Rdrp and E genes of Sars-CoV-2 using RT-PCR allows the reliable detection of SARS-CoV-2 in patient samples [31]. However, to our knowledge there are no reports of whether gender-related differences could influence the accuracy and sensitivity of the assay. Variations in gene expression patterns, immune responses, and viral load dynamics between males and females might impact the detection and quantification of these genes. Among the 78.5% COVID-19 patients who tested positive for the presence of Rdrp gene and E gene. However, there were no notable differences observed in positive RT-PCR outcome between males and females.

Waryah et al. 2023, found that only the E gene was positive in 8.2% of patients, while ORF1b targets were negative in patients [6]. In this study, the results indicate that some individuals initially tested positive for the E gene only (6.7% of the total number of patients), which might suggest a weaker or early-stage infection [6]. The RdRp gene is detected later and is sustained through the course of infection, whereas the E gene is generally detected earlier. The higher Ct values for the RdRp gene suggest a lower viral load in the later stages of infection. Complementary testing for both the Rdrp gene and the E gene improves sensitivity and accuracy, leading to a more comprehensive assessment of COVID-19 cases [7]. These results may be due to variations in assay design, target genes, laboratory protocols and the onset of symptoms. In our analysis, and to ensure accurate results and to minimize the possibility of false negatives using Cobas 6800, we took another sample, especially when there is clinical suspicion of infection or if the initial test results are inconclusive.

Moreover, our findings revealed noteworthy variations in several hematological markers among SARS-CoV-2 infected patients in comparison to the control group. Elderdery et al. 2022, found that Hb, RBC count, HCT, MCH and MCHC values were all significantly decreased in COVID-19 subjects compared with the control group [14]. While in our study it was found that, Hb, RBC, HCT and MCV are significantly decreased in COVID-19 patients compared with the control group. Elderdery et al. 2022 also reported that there were no statistically significant differences in the values of total WBC count and lymphocyte count between patients in the SARS-CoV-2 group and the control group [14]. Myari et al. 2022 observed that lymphopenia does not provide utility in the screening of COVID-19 patients [16]. Our study showed that there was a significant difference in WBC and lymphocytes between the two groups. The changes in Hb and HCT levels may suggest the presence of anemia, which is frequently noticed in a variety of infections. Furthermore, the shifts in RBC count and MCV may cause possible disturbances in erythropoiesis and the morphology of red blood cells as a reaction to the viral infection as previously reported [6]. A reduction in lymphocyte count and high WBC level may be linked to a compromised immune response and the virus's impact on immune cell populations and may link to worse clinical outcomes, including an increased likelihood of requiring intensive care, mechanical ventilation and an elevated risk of mortality [7].

By comparing COVID-19 ICU patients and non-ICU patients' certain patterns and differences were observed. ICU patients have severe COVID-19 symptoms. They frequently encounter breathing difficulties and necessitate mechanical ventilation or other advanced respiratory assistance. Conversely, non-ICU patients have milder symptoms and do not require intensive care. Studies have shown that among ICU patients, males have a higher likelihood of severe illness (66.67%) than females (33.33%). While among non-ICU patients, studies show that there are a greater number of female COVID-19 patients (57.81%) compared with males (42.19%) [1,2]. The COVID-19 ICU patients encompass a broad age spectrum, spanning from younger adults to the elderly. Nevertheless, older adults, especially those aged 60 and above (age mean ± SE = 60.2 ± 1.4) or with pre-existing health conditions, exhibit a higher likelihood of necessitating intensive care than those with mild symptoms (age mean ± SE = 54 ± 1.5) which is a significant difference with p-value = 0.003).

Notably, in this study, the mean values of Hb, HCT, RBC, MCV, MCH and WBC were higher in ICU patients compared with the non-ICU patients. Conversely, the mean value of lymphocytes is notably lower in ICU patients when compared with those not requiring intensive care. Lymphocytes have a crucial function in mounting the immune response against viral infections and the reduced number of lymphocytes in ICU patients might highlight immune irregularities or suppression and could potentially increase patients' susceptibility to complications and subsequent infections. Moreover, our study demonstrated that patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 exhibited elevated levels of inflammatory parameters, specifically CRP, LDH and ferritin, in their serum samples compared with the control group of healthy individuals. The correlation between elevated CRP and disease severity emphasizes its potential role as an early indicator of the inflammatory state associated with COVID-19 progression. Elevated LDH levels might reflect the impairment of lung function, given that the enzyme is released when cells are damaged, and the serum ferritin elevation can contribute to tissue damage and organ dysfunction.

It has been observed in this study that CRP levels are higher in ICU patients compared with non-ICU patients. This suggests that ICU patients exhibit a more pronounced inflammatory response. This observation raises the possibility that elevated CRP levels may serve as an indicator of the severity of the disease as previously demonstrated by Luo et al. [24]. On the other hand, serum ferritin levels have been found to be higher in non-ICU patients compared with ICU patients in contrast to a previous study [26] which found that ferritin Levels in ICU Patients was higher compared with non-ICU patients.

The finding that ferritin levels are higher in non-ICU patients compared with ICU patients suggests that ferritin may not be a reliable indicator of disease severity in COVID-19. While ferritin levels can provide insights into the inflammatory response, they may not be the sole determining factor for ICU admission. In addition, our results showed that there was no statistically significant difference in LDH levels between COVID-19 patients in the ICU and those who were not in the ICU. The lack of a significant difference in LDH levels may suggest that LDH alone might not be a strong predictor of ICU admission in COVID-19 cases in this study. However, other factors, such as clinical symptoms, oxygen levels, radiological findings and other biomarkers, might still contribute to the decision-making process for ICU care. Our findings are in a accordance with a recent study that has confirmed that procalcitonin, CRP, D-dimer and serum ferritin are useful indicators of acute renal impairment and end-organ damage in COVID-19. Procalcitonin is a potent predictor of COVID-19 hospitalized patients' severity and mortality [32]

Monitoring these hematological parameters alongside serological tests for antibodies can provide valuable prognostic information. Higher levels of CRP, LDH and ferritin in conjunction with the presence of IgM or IgG antibodies may indicate a heightened immune response, inflammation and tissue damage in individuals with a robust antibody-mediated defense against the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Such a combined assessment offers valuable insights into the clinical trajectory of COVID-19 patients, aiding in prognostic evaluation and treatment decision-making. Moreover, the connection between elevated ferritin levels and the presence of IgM or IgG antibodies highlights the potential role of iron dysregulation in the disease process. Managing iron levels could be another avenue for treatment, as abnormal iron metabolism has been associated with heightened inflammation and immune response. The relationship between detectable IgM or IgG antibodies and lymphopenia can contribute to preventing lymphocyte depletion which may be crucial for improving clinical outcomes in COVID-19 patients.

The absence of detectable IgM or IgG antibodies in some COVID-19 patients which is associated with abnormal hemoglobin levels, red blood cell indices and platelet count may suggest a delayed or weaker immune response to the virus. This could potentially lead to a prolonged infection and a more severe disease course, during which the inflammatory response can negatively impact erythropoiesis. The delayed or impaired immune response may contribute to the development of anemia [33]. The IgG antibodies are known for their long-lasting presence in the body compared with IgM antibodies [33]. The higher prevalence of IgG antibodies in both ICU and non-ICU patients suggests that IgG responses persist for a longer duration, potentially contributing to sustained protection against the virus [11]. Vaccination that can induce a robust and sustained IgG response may be more effective in conferring immunity and reducing the severity of COVID-19 cases [11]. Combining serological tests with hematological tests can serve as an alternative approach for diagnosing COVID-19 in situations where RT-PCR testing might not be readily available or when test results are inconclusive. Healthcare professionals might use a combination of available tests, clinical evaluation and patient history to make informed decisions regarding diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusion

Finally, from this study we can conclude that each diagnostic method offers unique insights into different aspects of the infection, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of COVID-19 in patients. A well-coordinated diagnostic strategy is essential to optimize COVID-19 diagnosis and patient management. A significant association between the appearance of serum IgG or IgM and the possibility of not being admitted to the ICU was also observed. Although serum ferritin and LDH levels can offer information about the extent of inflammation, they might not be the exclusive factor influencing the decision for ICU admission. Using serological and hematological tests together can provide a broader perspective on a person's health status.
