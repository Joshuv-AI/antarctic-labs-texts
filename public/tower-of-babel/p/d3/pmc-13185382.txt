Burnout among emergency department healthcare workers in Palestine and Türkiye: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Motasem Hamdan, Ramazan Erdem, Aynur Toraman, Havva Ceylan, Anan Rashed, Bilal Jawabreh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout is a common concern among healthcare workers in hospital emergency departments (EDs). This study examines the level of burnout and associated factors among healthcare workers in EDs of Palestinian and Turkish hospitals. It also assessed the relationship between prevalence of burnout and workplace violence as well as with job turnover in EDs.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 14 Palestinian hospital EDs in the West Bank and 3 hospitals in Isparta Province in Türkiye. All ED physicians, nurses, and other staff across the 17 hospitals (≈ 660 workers) were invited to participate in the study. A total of 377 completed the survey (Palestine 227; Türkiye 150). The Maslach Burnout Inventory- Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel was used to assess occupational burnout. Data collection was done between June and November 2024.FindingsA majority (70.0%) of the Palestinian participants experienced burnout associated with emotional exhaustion (score ≥ 27) compared to 48.7% in Türkiye (p < 0.001), 51.4% reported burnout related to depersonalization (score ≥ 10) compared to 43.3% in Türkiye (p = 0.282), and 53.3% reported burnout related to personal accomplishment (score ≤ 33) compared to 62.6% in Türkiye (p = 0.207). Unadjusted odds ratios indicate a significant prevalence of burnout, characterized by having high scores on the emotional exhaustion (≥ 27) and/ or depersonalization (≥ 10), especially among Palestinian compared to Turkish participants (OR 2.581; 95% CI 1.640–4.063; p < 0.001), among physicians (OR 3.718; 95% CI 1.946–7.103; p < 0.001) and nurses (OR 3.248; 95% CI 1.882–5.605; p < 0.001) compared to other ED workers, as well as among those exposed to any type of workplace violence (physical/non-physical) during the past year (OR 4.287; 95% CI 2.666–6.895; p < 0.001). The adjusted odds ratios showed that burnout is significantly associated with the job category, where physicians (OR 2.499; 95% CI 1.244–5.018; p = 0.01) and nurses (OR 2.147; 95% CI 1.135–4.059; p = 0.019) are more likely to experience burnout than other ED workers, as well as with exposure to workplace violence (OR 3.304; 95% CI 1.948–5.606; p < 0.001). The findings showed that burnout syndrome was associated with a higher intention to leave the EDs, with 57.8% of individuals experiencing burnout expressing a desire to quit EDs (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe study indicates a high prevalence of burnout among ED workers in both countries, particularly among physicians and nurses. Workplace violence is a significant factor associated with burnout and, subsequently, turnover from EDs. To reduce the risk of burnout on health workers and services, it is essential to adopt strategies such as providing counseling, skills development, maintaining adequate staffing, and mitigating violence against ED workers.Keywords: Burnout, Emergency departments, Healthcare workers, Prevalence, Associated factors

Introduction

Burnout is a common concern in healthcare systems. It is an occupational health phenomenon resulting from job-related chronic workplace stress that needs appropriate management [1]. Burnout is a psychological syndrome that develops as a protracted reaction to ongoing interpersonal pressures at work [2].

Burnout is highly prevalent among workers in hospital emergency departments (EDs) [3]. Working in EDs is often considered a highly stressful occupation due to the inherent challenges of trauma and injury care, the significant patient volume, insufficient resources, and elevated instances of violence against healthcare professionals [4, 5]. Workplace violence is defined as “any activity associated with the job or any event that occurs in the work environment that involves the intentional use of physical force or emotional abuse against an employee and results in physical or emotional injury and consequences” [6]. Studies show that workplace violence significantly contributes to burnout among emergency healthcare workers [7, 8]. In addition to that, turnover intention was positively associated with exposure to different types of violence [9]. Evidence also indicates that employees suffering from substantial burnout are more inclined to exhibit turnover intentions [10]. Research evidence also demonstrates that burnout adversely affects the physical and mental health of healthcare workers, as well as the quality of patient care [11, 12]. Additionally, it has implications for systems and institutions, including increased absenteeism, turnover, job dissatisfaction, and escalating costs [13–15].

Maslach and colleagues [16] introduced a conceptualization of burnout to the literature, characterizing it as a progressive phenomenon marked by fatigue, cynicism, and diminishing commitment among social care providers. As a psychological syndrome with three dimensions—emotional exhaustion, depersonalization or cynicism and detachment from work, and a reduced sense of accomplishment—the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) [17] has been regarded as a crucial framework for evaluating burnout. The model [18] defines weariness, energy depletion, and fatigue as facets of emotional exhaustion. Depersonalization, viewed as a cynical dimension, is marked by withdrawal, loss of idealism, irritability, negative or inappropriate attitudes, and detached concern [18]. The inefficacy component, reflecting reduced personal accomplishment, is characterized by low morale, an inability to cope, and diminished productivity or capability [18].

Healthcare systems and institutions must develop policies and solutions to prevent burnout and protect the well-being of health workers in EDs [19]. Appropriate management of the issue necessitates empirical evidence regarding the nature of burnout and related factors among health workers in EDs. There is a deficiency of studies that systematically examine burnout among various categories of health workers in emergency departments in both countries.

Employing a comparative cross-national design is methodologically important for understanding how structural, organizational, and contextual factors shape burnout among ED healthcare workers. This approach provides a unique opportunity for inquiry and comparison with other countries to gain valuable insights. The aim of this study was to examine the level of burnout and associated factors among healthcare workers in EDs at Palestinian and Turkish hospitals. It also assessed the relationship between prevalence of burnout and workplace violence as well as with job turnover in EDs. This study overlaps with a research conducted in Türkiye and Palestine on workplace violence in EDs [20]. The study settings exhibit common social and cultural traits, yet they vary in terms of health system resources and capacities. Türkiye is a growing economy with a universal health care system. Conversely, Palestine is profoundly impacted by a prolonged political conflict and difficult economic circumstances, rendering it a unique subject for analysis and comparison with other countries enjoying more stable conditions and health care systems to extract insights, particularly regarding the welfare of health workers in EDs. The study takes place in both countries in public hospitals that similar emergency care systems and service characteristics, which include rapid assessment, acute treatment, monitoring for moderate injuries or illnesses, and critical care for serious case such as cardiac arrests, strokes, and traumas. Additionally, the EDs in both settings demonstrate similar staffing arrangements, including medical personnel, nursing, and support staff. The collaboration among researchers from the respective countries has substantially enhanced the study’s design and facilitated access to the hospitals involved in the research. This study reinforces the knowledge about burnout and its determinants among healthcare workers in EDs operating within a political-conflict context. Specifically, the study was conducted during the turmoil that followed the post-October 2023 war on Gaza and the subsequent aggression against Palestinians in the West Bank.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study adopted a cross-sectional comparative design. Fourteen hospital EDs run by the Palestinian Ministry of Health in the West Bank were included in the study. In Türkiye, the study was conducted in the three EDs of the hospitals in Isparta Province; two of them are run by the Turkish Ministry of Health and one is a public university teaching hospital. Isparta Province EDs were chosen for comparison based on their similarities to the Palestinian departments. All EDs in both countries are public hospitals. Moreover, all the hospitals in both countries serve as the primary providers of emergency medical services within the regions in which they operate. The sizes of the studied hospital ranged from 30 to 850 beds.

Population and sample

All the full-time workers in the studied EDs, which include physicians, nurses, clerks, receptionists, and support health personnel, totaled 660 individuals (424 in Palestine and 236 in Türkiye) who were invited to participate in the study using a census approach. Trainees in the EDs were excluded from the study. The study sample consisted of 377 participants (227 in Palestine and 150 in Türkiye) who completed the survey: 97 physicians, 198 nurses, and 82 other workers. The overall response rate was estimated at 53.5% in Palestine and 63.6% in Türkiye; the survey was completed by 52.3% and 70.2% of physicians, 58.6% and 68.2% of nurses, and 30.9% and 48.5% of other workers, respectively. Several attempts were made to follow up and reach the target group to encourage their participation in the study. The none-responses can be attributed to the unavailability during the period of data collection or unwillingness to participate.

Study tool

The Maslach Burnout Inventory - Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel- MBI-HSS (MP) was used to assess burnout in health workers who care for patients with life threatening illness and injuries in EDs [21]. The MBI-HSS (MP) is a copyright protected published by Mind Garden [21]. Permission was obtained to use the instrument. The language of the original tool is in English, but translations into many languages including Arabic and Turkish are provided with the license. To ensure validity, the translations were reviewed, and back translations were also conducted to verify accuracy and content validity through a review by bilingual experts and the research teams. It worth mentioning that the Arabic version of the tool was already validated in our previous study in Palestine [20].

The MBI measures burnout as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1] is a highly reliable and validated tool used in burnout research [22]. It is a measure of job burnout grouped in three subscales: emotional exhaustion (EE) measures feelings of being emotionally overextended and exhausted by one’s work (9 items), depersonalization (DP) measures unfeeling and impersonal responses toward recipients of one’s service/ care (5 items), and personal accomplishment (PA) measures feelings of competence and successful achievement in one’s work (8 items). The survey items are rated using a 7-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = a few times a year or less, 2 = once a month or less, 3 = a few times a month, 4 = once a week, 5 = a few times a week, and 6 = every day). Based on Maslach and colleagues [23], an emotional exhaustion score (range 0–54 points) ≤ 16 indicates low burnout, 17–26 moderate burnout, and ≥ 27 reflects high burnout. A depersonalization score (range 0–30 points) ≤ 5 indicates low burnout, 6–9 reflects moderate burnout, and ≥ 10 reflects high burnout. The personal accomplishment score (range 0–48 points) ≥ 40 indicates low burnout, 34–39 reflects moderate burnout, and ≤ 33 reflects high burnout. Empirical studies indicate that exhaustion and depersonalization are the primary dimensions of high workplace burnout, while lack of professional accomplishment is viewed as both a cause and a consequence of this syndrome [24–26]. High levels of burnout on the emotional exhaustion and depersonalization subscales result in job inefficacy [27, 28]. Extreme burnout manifests itself when an individual is emotionally drained and shows a negative attitude towards their work or patients. Elevated degrees of emotional exhaustion or depersonalization are seen as the main causes of burnout among healthcare workers [29]. This study, aligns with earlier research [30, 31], considered burnout present when health workers have high levels of emotional exhaustion (≥ 27) and/ or depersonalization (≥ 10).

The internal consistency of the MBI subscales was tested using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The results of the samples of Palestine and Türkiye were as follow: α = 0.926 and 0.905 for the EE, α = 0.857 and 0.904 for the DP, and α = 0.884 and 0.908 for the PA. In addition to that, data on workplace violence were concurrently gathered from the same participants [20], assessing their exposure to any form of workplace violence, whether physical like hitting, kicking, slapping, choking, biting, pulling, or pushing health workers, or nonphysical like verbal abuse, which is any discourse that makes someone feel less important than they are [32]. Data on the frequencies of exposure to different types of workplace violence during the past 12 months was collected. Moreover, the intention to quit work using at EDs was measured using 5-point Likert scale (very likely, likely, not decided, maybe, never). The survey was administered in local languages.

Data collection

Data was collected in Palestine using Google Forms due to the difficulties reaching participants imposed by the wave of violent conflict during the research. The survey link was shared with the EDs workers through direct contacts as well as emails and social media platforms. Printed surveys were administered to the EDs workers by the research team in Türkiye. We recognize that the disparity in data collection methods brought about by the conditions in Palestine may lead to mode effects, which may result in different social desirability and response patterns. The participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous. The survey was provided with a cover letter that included the aim of the study, survey completion guidelines, and informed consent to participate in the study. Data collection was conducted between June and November 2024 in both countries. Ethical approvals were obtained from the ethics committees of Al-Quds and Suleyman Demirel Universities.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 for Windows (Armonk, NY, IBM Corp.) [33]. Descriptive statistics were performed for the participant characteristics and the study variables. Chi-square (χ2) and Fisher’s exact (F) tests were used to assess the differences in the MBI subscale scores and job categories and countries. Binary logistic regression was used to the assess the factors associated with the prevalence of burnout syndrome among study participants, odds ratios and 95% CI and adjusted odds ratios and 95% CI were calculated. The regression analysis also included the exposure to any type of workplace violence during the past year. The frequencies of exposure to any type of workplace violence were classified as (Yes/No) based on whether the participant had experienced any form of workplace violence (physical or non-physical) at least once during the past 12 months. Moreover, Chi-square tests (χ2) were used to assess the associations between prevalence of burnout and intention to quit work in EDs recorded as likely and unlikely. A P-value < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance in all analyses.

Findings

Participant characteristics

A total of 377 ED health workers participated in the study, 227 (60.2%) in Palestine and 150 (39.8%) in Türkiye (Table 1). The majority (63.4%) were males, higher in Palestine (75.8%) than in Türkiye (44.7%) (p < 0.001). The age of 52.5% of the participants was more than 30 years, 59.9% in Palestine and 41.3% in Türkiye (p < 0.001). Nurses were the largest group (52.5%), predominantly in Palestine (67.4%), followed by physicians (25.7%) and then other health workers (21.8%) (p < 0.001). Most of the participants (79.6%) had an education level of a bachelor’s degree or above, higher in Palestine (88.1%) compared to Türkiye (66.7%) (p = 0.026). Moreover, 63.1% of the participants had less than five years of experience in emergency, greater in Türkiye (74.0%) than in Palestine (55.9%) (p < 0.001). In addition, 18.6% of the participants are employed in EDs that receive fewer than 4000 patients, 40.3% receive between 4000 and 8000 patients, and 41.1% receive more than 8000 patients on a monthly basis (p = 0.264).

Burnout prevalence

The findings on the MBI subscales (Table 2) show that in Palestine 70.0% of the participants reported high level of burnout related to emotional exhaustion (score ≥ 27) compared to 48.7% in Türkiye (p = 0.001), 51.4% reported a high level of burnout related to depersonalization (score ≥ 10) in Palestine compared to 43.3% in Türkiye (p = 0.282), and 53.3% reported a high level of burnout (score ≤ 33) related to personal accomplishment in Palestine compared to 62.6% in Türkiye (p = 0.207). Only the differences in relation to emotional exhaustion levels are statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Factors associated with prevalence of burnout

Binary logistic regression was conducted to examine the factors associated with prevalence of burnout syndrome (Yes/No), among EDs workers, defined as having high scores on the emotional exhaustion (score ≥ 27) and/ or depersonalization (score ≥ 10). The results (Table 4) show that there were no significant associations between the prevalence of burnout and the gender, age, education level, experience of the study participants, or the average number of visits received in the EDs (p > 0.05). The unadjusted odds ratios (Table 4) show that burnout was more prevalent among Palestinian participants than Turkish ones (OR 2.581; 95% CI 1.64–4.063; p < 0.001), among physicians (OR 3.718; 95% CI 1.946–7.103; p < 0.001) and nurses (OR 3.248; 95% CI 1.882–5.605; p < 0.001) compared to other workers in EDs, and also among those who experienced workplace violence in the past year (OR 4.287; 95% CI 2.666–6.895; p < 0.001).

After adjustment, job category and exposure to workplace violence remained associated with burnout. Physicians were 2.5 times more likely to experience burnout than other ED workers (adjusted OR 2.499; 95% CI 1.244–5.018; p = 0.012), and nurses had more than twice the odds of burnout compared with other staff (adjusted OR 2.147; 95% CI 1.135–4.059; p = 0.019). Exposure to workplace violence was strongly associated with burnout, with exposed workers having over three times higher odds of burnout compared with those not exposed (adjusted OR 3.304; 95% CI 1.948–5.606; p < 0.001). Country was not retained in the final multivariable model after adjustment, indicating that the observed difference in burnout prevalence between Palestine and Türkiye was explained by other factors included in the model (adjusted OR 1.229; 95% CI 0.694–2.176; p = 0.479). The final model is a good fit, whereas the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients shows that the predictors significantly explain the outcome (χ²=46.505; p < 0.001).

Experience of burnout and the intention to quit EDs

The relationship between experiencing burnout syndrome and the intention to quit EDs was examined (Table 5). The results show a significant relationship between experiencing burnout and intention to leave work in EDs. In the overall research group, 57.8% of the individuals experiencing burnout are likely to leave work in EDs, compared to 22.1% of those without burnout are likely to leave work in EDs and the differences are statistically significant (p < 0.001). Moreover, 64.1% of those who have burnout in Palestine compared to 32.7% without (p < 0.001). Similarly, 45.3% of those with burnout in Türkiye compared to 14.1% without are likely to leave EDs (p < 0.001).

Discussion

What distinguishes this study is conducting a comparative analysis involving different worker categories across 17 EDs in Turkish and Palestinian hospitals. The findings show a high level of burnout among workers in the EDs in both countries (Table 2). Compared to previous findings, this study reveals elevated burnout levels among ED workers in Palestine [13]. Specifically, the emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment surpassed the previous rates [13]. Additionally, Palestinian ED workers demonstrate significantly higher burnout levels in emotional exhaustion and depersonalization compared to ED workers in Türkiye and other regional and international studies [14, 34–38]. Meanwhile, our findings indicate a higher level of burnout among ED workers in the Isparta Region compared to studies recently conducted in other Turkish hospitals [7, 39, 40]. Fatigue primarily linked to high workloads due to overcrowding of patients, understaffing, and severity of cases treated in EDs is associated with burnout [41, 42]. Previous evidence shows that excessive work hours and high demands contribute to emotionally exhausted employees who lack a sense of personal accomplishment and are feeling depersonalized toward their jobs [26]. The situation in Palestine is intensified by persistent conflict [43], severe movement restrictions, harsh economic conditions [44] curtailing the ability of the government to pay salaries, and insufficient resources in public hospitals [45]. Healthcare providers exposed to violence and conflict in the Middle East countries have increased risk of burnout [37]. In our findings, the fact that Palestinian ED workers reported a comparably lower burnout related to personal accomplishment than their Turkish counterparts, despite higher burnout levels on the other MBI dimensions, can be attributed to the national commitment and resilience of Palestinian health professionals, enabling them to deliver care despite challenging conditions [46]. This is linked with Sumud, an Arabic term denoting perseverance or steadfastness, that is recognized as a Palestinian cultural construct and an essential element of resistance for Palestinians enduring prolonged military occupation [47].

Physicians and nurses in both countries exhibit significantly higher levels of burnout compared to other EDs workers (Table 3), aligning with findings from national and international studies [13, 14, 38, 40]. Physicians and nurses in EDs are at the forefront of care access and are believed to be at the greatest risk of burnout [48]. In Palestine, long working hours, heavy workloads, a lack of medication and equipment, constant exposure to traumatic events, and violence, as well as the stress of working in a conflict-affected area, appear to be the primary factors contributing to elevated burnout rates among Palestinian professionals compared to their Turkish counterparts [13, 49–51]. Research indicates that organizational factors such as work pressure, exposure to workplace violence, interdepartmental conflict, and feeling of underappreciated by the management [52], along with individual factors like a lack of coping mechanisms and resilience among health professionals in EDs, are the main causes of burnout [53].

The study found that there is a strong link between burnout and experiencing workplace violence, either physical or non-physical, in the past year among participants from both countries (Table 4) (p < 0.001). This data is in line with other studies [9, 13, 34, 37, 39]. Evidence show a high prevalence of violence against ED health workers in Palestine and Türkiye [20, 42]. High burnout has significant administrative and organizational implications, including lower productivity and reduced quality of work, increased absenteeism and turnover, and a decline in job satisfaction, and even increased costs from errors or litigation [48, 49]. Moreover, workplace violence exposure is likely to significantly affect the well-being and burnout levels of emergency health workers [54, 55]. However, the possibility of reverse causality must be acknowledged. While workplace violence might have contributed to ED workers burnout, it is also probable that individuals already experiencing burnout are at a higher risk of experiencing or reporting such violence.

Burnout is associated with job dissatisfaction and negative work experiences, and factors like emotional exhaustion and a lack of personal accomplishment are particularly key predictors of turnover intention [10, 56–58]. Job turnover affects the quality of healthcare, customer satisfaction, and costs within healthcare institutions. Time and resources are required to recruit and train qualified emergency professionals besides the expenses associated with delivering substandard care due to staff burnout [59]. Research indicates that turnover intention is more common among employees experiencing high levels of burnout in healthcare settings [5, 14]. Our results (Table 5) indicate a significant association between high burnout levels and the intention to leave EDs. The results align with existing evidence from the two settings [10, 13], especially the turnover of physicians and nurses in emergency care, highlights a considerable challenge for healthcare systems in providing essential emergency services.

Limitations

The study utilized a cross-sectional approach, which may present certain limitations. Recall biases may affect responses related to violent events that occurred one year prior, as well as lead to socially desirable responses to sensitive questions. Only Isparta provided the Türkiye sample, which may limit methodological generalization to the entire country. In addition to that, there was a discrepancy in the acceptability of participating in the survey; 53.5% in Palestine and 63.6% in Türkiye. The use of face-to-face data collection in Türkiye and online surveys in Palestine might have involved some mode effect biases. Using different survey methods can yield different responses to the same questions. Additionally, the use of an online survey in Palestine may introduce selection bias, as it restricts participation to individuals who possess familiarity with the platform and access to the internet. We cannot also ignore the chance of non-response bias, since people who are feeling burnt out might have chosen not to take part in the study because they didn’t want to share their burnout status, which could have influenced the estimates of burnout prevalence. The differences in the staffing patterns of EDs in the two countries may have impacted on cross-country comparisons. Finally, the categorization of clerks, receptionists, and auxiliary staff into a single group may have concealed discrepancies.

Conclusions

The study findings reveal high prevalence of burnout among EDs workers in both countries, while also highlighting variations in burnout subscales associated with the health system structures and contextual differences across the examined countries. Burnout related to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization is particularly prevalent among Palestinian participants, likely influenced by the ongoing conflict, psychological distress and demanding conditions in EDs. Care providers in EDs, particularly physicians and nurses, are the most affected groups, which subsequently influences the quality and cost of care delivered to patients. The findings indicate that workplace violence is a significant factor associated with burnout and, subsequently, turnover from EDs. To reduce the impact on workers’ well-being and the quality of emergency services, it is essential to implement both organizational and individual strategies to address burnout and simultaneously reduce turnover among EDs workers. Priority interventions should include providing counselling and well-being support, offering skills development training in coping and resilience, maintaining adequate staffing, and implementing strategies to mitigate violence against ED workers.
