Silicon-based agent alleviating intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice by inhibiting ferroptosis via activating AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway
Authors: Xiao Liu, Binglong Wang, Xujiao Wang, Qin Ye, Jianfei Wu, Guo Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroptosis is a death mode centered on iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation pathways. Recent studies have shown that inhibiting ferroptosis can alleviate intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (II/R) injury. Hydrogen (H2) can mitigate II/R injury by anti-oxidative stress, while Silicon (Si)-based agent can generate a large amount of H2 in the alkaline environment of the intestine during II/R. However, it is unclear whether Si-based agent can alleviate II/R injury. By establishing a mouse model of II/R injury, we examined the histopathological changes, apoptosis, inflammatory factors, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis levels in the small intestine. We found that the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway is activated in the small intestine of mice after II/R, and Si-based agent can further activate this pathway, alleviating intestinal injury, inhibiting apoptosis, reversing inflammation and oxidative stress, and suppressing ferroptosis in mice after II/R. Furthermore, by administering AMPK agonists and inhibitors, as well as ferroptosis inhibitors for further exploration, we discovered that the hydrogen generated by Si-based agent may inhibit ferroptosis and alleviate II/R injury through the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway. This study provides theoretical and experimental evidence for elucidating the pathogenesis of intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury and for the clinical application of Si-based agent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52405-x.Keywords: Si-based agent, AMPK, Sirt1, Ferroptosis, Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injurySubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Drug discovery, Gastroenterology

Introduction

Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (II/R) injury arises from the blockage and subsequent recanalization of intestinal blood flow, commonly occurring in conditions like acute intestinal obstruction, mesenteric artery embolism, and shock, with high morbidity and mortality1. II/R-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction facilitates the translocation of intestinal endotoxins, triggering the body to produce and release numerous inflammatory mediators and cytokines that disseminate through the circulatory system. Once intestinal barrier damage surpasses its regenerative capacity, life-threatening toxemia and shock may ensue2. Currently, its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, involving multiple processes such as oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and cell apoptosis. In recent years, ferroptosis, a novel iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has been identified as a key contributor to II/R injury3,4. The core mechanism of ferroptosis involves the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products due to reduced glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity. During intestinal ischemia/reperfusion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts from hypoxia-reoxygenation and iron ion overload serve as crucial inducers of ferroptosis, directly exacerbating intestinal epithelial cell death and mucosal damage3,5. In fact, ferroptosis has been found in intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury, and ferroptosis inhibitors can alleviate intestinal ischemia/reperfusion-related injuries6,7. Studies indicate that after II/R, reduced glutathione (GSH) levels decrease, ROS accumulates, followed by increased lipid peroxidation and intestinal damage, which collectively trigger ferroptosis8,9.Thus, identifying an antioxidant drug capable of inhibiting ferroptosis holds promise for alleviating II/R injury.

Hydrogen (H2) is a reducing small molecule gas with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-allergic, and anti-apoptotic effects10,11. At present, oral hydrogen-rich water is a convenient and safe way to deliver molecular hydrogen, but H2 in water evaporates over time and may disappear before reaching the stomach or intestine12,13. A recently reported Silicon (Si)-based agent exhibits minimal reaction in the strongly acidic gastric environment (pH 1.0) but generates substantial amounts of H2 in the weakly alkaline intestinal environment (pH 8.0)14. Therefore, compared with hydrogen-saturated water, the intake of Si-based agent may produce high concentrations of hydrogen in the intestine for a long time, and the reaction products silicon and silicon dioxide are non-toxic, so they are suitable for medical applications. Si-based agent can be administered in vivo, and the produced H2 is absorbed by the digestive organs and circulates in the systemic vascular system15. In relevant reports, the serum hydrogen level was increased in normal rats that had consumed Si-based agents for 1 week; additionally, oral administration of Si-based agents enabled the stable and continuous generation of large amounts of hydrogen in the gastrointestinal tract of mice with ulcerative colitis and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), effectively alleviating gastrointestinal symptoms, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress responses16–18. Additionally, the effectiveness of this Si-based agent has been demonstrated in mouse Parkinson’s disease models and rat renal ischemia/reperfusion injury models, both showing reduced oxidative stress16,19. However, its role in II/R injury and whether it involves the regulation of ferroptosis remain unclear.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a serine/threonine protein kinase activated in response to insufficient energy supply, and its activation regulates cellular energy metabolism20. Silent information regulator 1 (Sirt1) can regulate biological processes such as cellular metabolism, cell survival, and stress response, and is related to various diseases such as tumors, ischemia/reperfusion injury, and sepsis21,22. Studies have shown that AMPK can activate nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase at the transcriptional level, increasing the NAD⁺/NADH ratio and thereby activating Sirt123. Our previous research has confirmed that activating the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway exerts anti-inflammatory, antioxidative stress, and anti-apoptotic effects, alleviating II/R injury2. In addition, studies have reported that hydrogen-rich water can activate the AMPK-Sirt1-FOXO3a signaling pathway to protect nerve cells from amyloid β-induced cytotoxicity24. However, it is unclear whether the activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway can inhibit ferroptosis and whether it mediates the process of Si-based agent alleviating II/R injury.

This study aims to investigate whether oral Si-based agent can inhibit ferroptosis by activating the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway, thereby alleviating II/R injury. Previous studies have confirmed that II/R can activate the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway and trigger ferroptosis, and inhibiting ferroptosis can mitigate the damage. Hydrogen has antioxidant effects and can activate the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway, but the relationship between this pathway and ferroptosis remains unclear. Oral Si-based agent can generate a large amount of hydrogen gas in the alkaline environment of the intestine, and it is unknown whether they alleviate II/R injury through this mechanism. Therefore, we established a mouse II/R model combined with drug agonist and inhibitor interventions to elucidate the mechanism from the perspectives of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway and ferroptosis, providing a basis for clinical translation.

Results

The mouse II/R model was successfully established, and Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor alleviated intestinal mucosal injury after II/R

Visual observation of small intestinal tissue (Fig. 1A): The small intestinal tissue in the Sham group was bright red, with normal peristalsis and strong arterial pulsation. the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) occlusion turned the intestine pale, weakened peristalsis, and halted pulsation, indicating the completion of the small intestinal ischemia model. SMA reopening led to dark purple-red coloration, enhanced peristalsis, resumed pulsation, with edema and partial necrosis, indicating the completion of the reperfusion model. Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) (Fig. 1B) showed that: Sham group showed abundant microcirculatory flow. SMA clamping blocked flow (ischemia confirmed); reopening restored flow, though lower than Sham levels, confirming successful II/R modeling.

The mouse II/R model was successfully established, and Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor alleviated intestinal mucosal injury after II/R. (A) Visual observation of small intestinal tissue; (B) Intestinal laser speckle blood flow imaging; (C) HE staining of mouse small intestinal mucosa (×100), scale bar = 100 μm; (D) Chiu’s score of small intestine; (E) Serum DAO level. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. Sham group; ##P < 0.01 vs. II/R group (n = 8).

As shown in Fig. 1C: the small intestinal mucosal structure in the Sham group was complete, with normal villi and gland morphology; after II/R, the villi in the lamina propria of the small intestinal mucosa fell off significantly, the glands were severely damaged, and capillaries were dilated, with significantly increased Chiu’s score and serum Diamine oxidase (DAO) level. However, after oral administration of Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor, the degree of villous edema and congestion was reduced, small intestinal mucosal injury was alleviated, Chiu’s score was decreased, and the serum DAO level after II/R was reduced (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D,E).

Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor alleviated the inflammatory response in the small intestinal tissue of mice after II/R

Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) are important pro-inflammatory factors involved in II/R injury25,26. The results showed (Fig. 2A–D): the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and MPO in small intestinal tissue significantly increased after II/R (P < 0.01), while Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor significantly reduced the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and MPO in small intestinal tissue after II/R (P < 0.01), indicating that Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor alleviate small intestinal tissue inflammatory response after mouse II/R.

Oral si-based agent reduced apoptosis and inhibited ferroptosis in the small intestinal tissue of mice after II/R

No obvious apoptotic cells were found in the Sham group; apoptotic cells significantly increased after II/R (P < 0.01), and small intestinal tissue cell apoptosis decreased after administration of Si-based agent and ferroptosis inhibitor (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A,B).

Fe2+ and malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations are positively correlated with ferroptosis, while GSH concentration and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity are negatively correlated with ferroptosis. The results showed (Fig. 3C–F): MDA and Fe2+ levels increased after II/R, while oral Si-based agent reduced the levels of the above two indicators (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). After II/R, GSH and SOD activities decreased, while oral Si-based agent increased GSH level and SOD activity (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01), indicating that ferroptosis increased after II/R, while oral Si-based agent could inhibit ferroptosis after mouse II/R.

Si-based agent further activated the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway after II/R, and upregulated the expression of the key ferroptosis inhibitor protein GPX4

The expression levels of p-AMPK/AMPK and Sirt1 proteins in mice in the Sham group were low, while the expression level of GPX4 protein was high. However, after II/R, the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway was activated, and the expression level of GPX4 protein decreased, indicating increased ferroptosis. As shown in Fig. 4A–D: after oral administration of Si-based agent, the expressions of p-AMPK/AMPK and Sirt1 were upregulated, and the expression of GPX4 protein was also upregulated (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01), indicating that Si-based agent further activated the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway after II/R and inhibited ferroptosis. However, the relationship between AMPK-Sirt1 and ferroptosis is still unclear, so we used AMPK agonist and AMPK inhibitor in subsequent experiments to explore the relationship between the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway and ferroptosis.

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that Si-based agent can alleviate intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury by activating the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway to inhibit ferroptosis. We found that Si-based agent can significantly improve intestinal mucosal pathological injury, reduce inflammatory response and cell apoptosis, and inhibit lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, such as reducing Fe2+ accumulation, decreasing MDA level, increasing GSH level and SOD activity, and upregulating GPX4 protein, and these effects are related to the activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway. The possible mechanism is shown in Fig. 9. While the physicochemical properties of the Si-based agent and a substantial body of prior literature strongly support that its intestinal protective effects are mediated by sustained H2 generation in the alkaline intestinal environment, it is important to acknowledge that the present study did not directly measure local H2 concentrations in vivo15–18. Therefore, the conclusion that H2 is the definitive causal agent, and that it acts specifically via the AMPK-Sirt1-ferroptosis axis, is inferential and based on integrating our pharmacological data with established evidence. Further studies employing direct H2 detection are warranted to confirm this mechanistic cascade.

Possible pathway mechanism diagram of this study.

In this study, a mouse model of II/R was established by 60 min of intestinal ischemia followed by 2 h of reperfusion. HE staining showed that after reperfusion, edema, detachment, congestion, hemorrhage, and vasodilation appeared in the lamina propria of the small intestinal mucosa, with increased Chiu’s score. Intestinal laser speckle blood flow imaging showed that intestinal blood flow was blocked after clamping the SMA, indicating the completion of the intestinal ischemia model; intestinal blood flow recovered after recanalizing the SMA but was lower than that in the Sham group, indicating the successful establishment of the mouse II/R model. Early tissue ischemia can induce intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction. Subsequent reperfusion can trigger oxidative stress, apoptosis, massive release of inflammatory factors, and immune activation26. DAO is a marker enzyme in the intestinal villus epithelial cells of mammals, and the level of DAO in the blood increases after intestinal injury26. TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β are important pro-inflammatory factors involved in II/R injury. MPO is a specific enzyme of neutrophils, and its activity can reflect the aggregation and invasion degree of neutrophils in intestinal tissue25,26. In this experiment, the inflammatory factors, lipid peroxidation indicators, and cell apoptosis in the small intestinal tissue of mice in the II/R group were significantly higher than those in the Sham group, indicating that the damaged small intestine released a large number of inflammatory mediators and related oxidative stress factors. We also found that the expression of the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway was significantly upregulated after II/R, which may be due to insufficient energy and increased AMP/ATP ratio during ischemia/reperfusion, leading to phosphorylation of AMPK and subsequent activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway.

This study reveals that the protective effect of Si-based agent on II/R injury is closely related to the inhibition of ferroptosis. II/R injury is related to various types of regulated cell death (RCD), including apoptosis, necrotic death, and autophagy. However, with the discovery of ferroptosis, new potential mechanisms of RCD have attracted attention6. Ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic iron-dependent cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products and the depletion of membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids, was proposed in 201227. Iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation pathways are central mediators of the ferroptosis process28. Excessive iron regulates ferroptosis by generating lethal reactive oxygen species through the Fenton reaction, while GSH depletion and/or GPX4 inhibition trigger ferroptosis through the accumulation of intracellular lipid ROS and overwhelming lipid peroxidation27,28. GPX4 is important for maintaining lipid redox homeostasis and is a negative regulator of ferroptosis2,29. Moreover, ROS attack membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids, causing extensive lipid peroxidation that damages membranes and leads to cell death6. Accumulating evidence implicates ferroptosis in various diseases, including neurological disorders, ischemia/reperfusion injury, renal failure, heart disease, and cancer6,27,30–32. Recent studies have further revealed that ferroptosis is associated with intestinal diseases, including II/R injury, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal cancer6. It has been reported that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in II/R injury, potentially serving as a lethal process triggered by reperfusion33. In assessment, MDA reflects oxidative stress levels, while SOD indicates the body’s capacity to scavenge oxygen free radicals34. Thus, GSH, MDA, and SOD can reflect the overall oxidative stress level and lipid peroxidation level of intestinal tissue. In our study, we observed that intestinal ischemia/reperfusion resulted in Fe2+ accumulation, increased MDA levels, decreased GSH levels and SOD activity, as well as downregulated expression of GPX4, a key ferroptosis inhibitor. These findings indicate that the redox homeostasis was disrupted after intestinal ischemia/reperfusion, thereby triggering ferroptosis. It is possible that the oxygen free radical burst during the reperfusion phase can directly trigger lipid peroxidation, while intracellular iron overload caused by ischemia exacerbates ROS generation through the Fenton reaction, jointly inducing ferroptosis.

The increase in MPO activity in II/R not only reflects neutrophil infiltration, but MPO itself can directly induce lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. But the inflammatory response caused by II/R injury is a dynamic process involving multiple cell types: ischemia and hypoxia directly induce ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells, and ruptured cells release large amounts of reactive oxygen species, Fe2+, and lipid peroxidation products (such as MDA); Macrophages, as the core immune cells in the tissue microenvironment, are rapidly recruited and activated under these damage-associated molecular patterns and oxidative lipid signaling. Macrophages participate in injury repair by engulfing ferroptosis cells and debris, clearing some pro-inflammatory mediators and peroxidation products. However, they can also be activated by ferroptosis-induced signals, releasing pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6, amplifying the inflammatory cascade reaction and exacerbating intestinal mucosal damage35,36. Therefore, ferroptosis is the core hub of inflammation amplification and immune recruitment. Recent studies have further revealed the dual role of myeloid cells in regulating ferroptosis37,38. Davidson et al.37 confirmed that myeloid cells have a strong inherent protective effect against ferroptosis, and can resist lipid peroxidation through their own antioxidant system to maintain immune function; Kim et al.38 found that under sustained iron induced cell death conditions, myeloid cells can undergo functional changes and release immunosuppressive molecules such as oxidized lipids (such as PGE2) to regulate immune responses. This study observed a significant increase in MPO activity in intestinal tissue after II/R, indicating a large infiltration of neutrophils; at the same time, the ferroptosis markers (increased Fe2+ and MDA levels, and decreased GSH and SOD levels) showed significant changes. It is speculated that in II/R injury, myeloid cells (such as neutrophils) may resist ferroptosis through inherent protective mechanisms, but sustained pro-ferroptosis signals can still induce functional changes (such as MPO release exacerbating lipid peroxidation). This suggests that the interaction between ferroptosis and immune cells is an important pathological basis for II/R injury.

In recent years, research on the role of H2 in biology and medicine has rapidly expanded. Several hydrogen delivery systems have been explored, including inhalation, oral hydrogen-rich water, injection of hydrogen-rich saline, and direct incorporation39. To our knowledge, these methods have not been widely used clinically due to lack of efficacy and operational difficulties. Hydrogen administration through injectable hydrogen-rich saline may provide more accurate hydrogen concentration and has been shown to alleviate ischemia/reperfusion injury in organs such as the heart, lung, liver, intestine, and kidney in animal models16. However, the amount of hydrogen dissolved in solution is limited. At room temperature and pressure, hydrogen can dissolve in water up to a maximum of 0.8 mM (1.6 mg/L). Under the alkaline conditions of the small intestine (pH 7.5–8.8), silicon reacts spontaneously with water to generate H2 (Si + 2 H2O → SiO2 + 2 H2↑), a well-established reaction. Kobayashi et al. demonstrated that the Si-based agent reacts with water even within the neutral-to-mildly alkaline pH range of 7.0–8.6, continuously generating hydrogen15. Notably, the hydrogen production capacity of Si-based agent reported in the literature varies considerably: at pH 8.0 (1 h), the hydrogen yield is approximately 55 mL/g; at pH 8.5 (24 h), it reaches 554 mL/g; and at pH 11.0 (24 h), it can be as high as 1400 mL/g 15,16,40. This marked variation is determined by both pH and reaction time: a one-unit increase in pH leads to a higher OH⁻ concentration, which accelerates the silicon oxidation reaction and hydrogen generation, and importantly, the OH⁻ concentration does not decrease significantly during the reaction, ensuring sustained hydrogen production. Moreover, longer reaction times result in greater hydrogen production from the Si-based agent, peaking at around 24 h15,40.

This study found that oral Si-based agent improved small intestinal mucosal injury after II/R, reduced the release of inflammatory factors, decreased small intestinal tissue cell apoptosis, and reduced Fe2+ concentration, MDA level, increased GSH level and SOD activity, and upregulated the key inhibitory factor of ferroptosis GPX4 in small intestinal tissue, suggesting that Si-based agent may inhibit ferroptosis by regulating iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation. Based on the previous discussion, Si-based agent reduced the release of pro-inflammatory signals (such as TNF-α, IL-6, MDA, etc.) from the source by inhibiting ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells, thereby inhibiting excessive recruitment and activation of immune cells and breaking the vicious cycle of inflammation ferroptosis.

Activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway after II/R is an adaptive survival mechanism, but it is insufficient to exert a protective effect. Oral administration of Si-based agent can significantly enhance the protein expression of p-AMPK/AMPK, Sirt1, and the key inhibitor of ferroptosis GPX4 in intestinal tissue after II/R, suggesting that the protective effect of Si-based agent in inhibiting ferroptosis may be related to the activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway. To further verify the relationship between the ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of Si-based agent and the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway, this study injected AICAR and compound C through the tail vein at the beginning of reperfusion to activate and inhibit AMPK, respectively, to verify our conjecture. AICAR has been confirmed as a specific activator of AMPK, which can activate AMPK without affecting the levels of ATP, ADP, and AMP by mimicking the stimulating effect of AMP on AMPK41,42. Compound C is used as an AMPK inhibitor and is commonly used in many AMPK signal studies43. The results showed that the AMPK agonist exerted protective effects on II/R injury similar to Si-based agent, with consistent trends in changes. Both treatments upregulated the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway and GPX4 protein expression, effectively alleviated the pathological injury of the small intestine induced by mice II/R, reduced small intestinal mucosal cell apoptosis, and inhibited inflammatory response, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis; after administration of the AMPK inhibitor compound C, the above protective effects were counteracted with opposite trends. This study indicates that AMPK is an important regulator of inflammatory response, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and ferroptosis, suggesting that the protective effect of Si-based agent in inhibiting ferroptosis on intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury may be mediated by activating the AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway. After being activated, AMPK can regulate downstream molecules through phosphorylation, promote the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2(Nrf2), and enhance its transcriptional activation of antioxidant genes such as GPX4. As a downstream target of AMPK, Sirt1 can stabilize Nrf2 through deacetylation and directly upregulate GPX4 expression44,45. In this study, Si-based agent significantly increased the expressions of p-AMPK, Sirt1, and GPX4 proteins, while inhibiting AMPK reversed the anti-ferroptosis and intestinal protective effects of Si-based agent, confirming that the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway is the pathway through which Si-based agent exerts intestinal protective effects. This finding reveals the molecular target of Si-based agent in regulating ferroptosis and provides a new perspective for understanding the cellular protective mechanism of Si-based agent.

Compared with previous studies, the innovations of this study are: (1) It is the first to associate Si-based agent with the ferroptosis mechanism of II/R injury, expanding the biological functions of Si-based agent; (2) It clarifies the mediating role of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway in the anti-ferroptosis effect of Si-based agent, providing an experimental basis for the treatment targeting this pathway. However, this study still has limitations: (1) There are differences between the pathophysiological states of animal models and clinical patients, necessitating further verification of the role of Si-based agent in different models; (2) The optimal dosage, timing, and long-term safety of Si-based agent still need to be optimized; (3) The specific hydrogen concentration released by oral Si-based agent in mice has not been measured.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the Si-based agent alleviates intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury in mice by activating the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway and inhibiting ferroptosis. These findings provide robust preclinical experimental evidence for the protective effect of Si-based agents against intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury, suggesting their promising potential for clinical application.

Materials and methods

Animals and model establishment

SPF-grade adult healthy male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. They were provided with sufficient food and water, maintained in a 12-h light/dark cycle, and housed at a room temperature of 21–25 °C. All animal procedures involved in this experiment were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zigong Fourth People’s Hospital (approval number: EC-2023-076) and comply with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH publication, 8th edition, 2011). The study was conducted following the ARRIVE guidelines (PLoS Bio 8(6), e1000412,2010).

The mice were fasted for 12 h before modeling, with free access to water. Anesthetize mice with 1–2% isoflurane, make an incision along the midline of the abdomen. In the II/R group, isolate the SMA and clamp it with a sterile non-invasive artery clamp. After 60 min, release the artery clamp to restore blood flow for 2 h. In the Sham group, only perform laparotomy to isolate the SMA without clamping3. A laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) instrument was used to monitor intestinal blood flow changes during modeling to confirm successful II/R model establishment. All operations were performed under sterile conditions. During modeling, 10 ml/kg of 37 °C normal saline was injected intraperitoneally for fluid resuscitation. Accidentally deceased mice during modeling were replaced to maintain group sizes.

Experimental design

To explore the effect of hydrogen released by Si-based agent on II/R injury in mice and the role of ferroptosis in II/R injury, 32 C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 8): Sham group, II/R group, II/R + Si-based agent group (II/R + Si group), and II/R + ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 group (II/R + Fer group). Detection indicators included: HE staining to observe the pathological changes of small intestinal tissue; DAO kit to detect the expression level of DAO in serum; terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining to detect the apoptosis of small intestine; enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect the level changes of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in small intestine; Biochemical methods were used to measure the changes in levels of MPO, GSH, Fe2+, SOD, and MDA in small intestinal tissue; Western blot to detect the protein expression of p-AMPK/AMPK, Sirt1, and GPX4.

To further explore the mechanism of Si-based agent in alleviating II/R injury via the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway, 40 C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 8): Sham group, II/R group, II/R + Si group, II/R + AMPK agonist AICAR group (II/R + AI group), and II/R + Si-based agent + AMPK inhibitor compound C group (II/R + Si + CC group). Detection indicators included: histopathological changes and apoptosis of small intestine tissue; DAO level in serum; inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis levels in the small intestine; AMPK-Sirt1 signaling pathway and GPX4 protein expression levels.

Drug treatment

II/R + Si group: Mice were fed with 1.0% Si-based agent 1 week before modeling; II/R + Fer group: Ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 10 mg/kg was intraperitoneally injected 1 h before II/R2. II/R + AI group: AMPK was activated by injecting AICAR (100 mg/kg) through the tail vein immediately at the onset of reperfusion2; II/R + Si + CC group: Mice were fed with 1.0% Si-based agent 1 week before ischemia/reperfusion, and compound C (0.25 mg/kg) was administered via the tail vein immediately at the onset of reperfusion to inhibit AMPK46. Silicon was purchased from Adamas (Adamas, China), Ferrostatin-1 and AICAR were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA), and AMPK inhibitor was purchased from CSNpharm (CSNpharm, USA).

Sample collection

After the experiment, mice (weighing 19–23 g) were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg of 2% pentobarbital sodium. Blood samples were collected from the mouse cardiac apex, allowed to stand for 2 h, and centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 15 min. The supernatant was stored at – 80 °C for serum DAO level determination. After blood collection, 3 cm of small intestinal tissue was taken 5 cm from the end of the ileum for HE staining, TUNEL staining, Western blot, and determination of the expression levels of intestinal inflammatory factors and oxidative stress markers.

Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)

LSCI was used to monitor microcirculatory blood flow changes in living mice, featuring non-invasiveness, non-contact, and rapid imaging47. The laser speckle flow imager (Rivard, China) was activated, and anesthetized mice were placed under the laser at three time points: before SMA clamping, 10 min after clamping, and 10 min after clamp release. The small intestine was gently exteriorized and positioned vertically 12 cm below the non-contact laser probe, with microcirculation monitored for 1 min. Raw speckle images (640 × 640 pixels) were acquired at 50 fps (exposure time T = 5 ms) under 780 nm laser irradiation2,48.

HE staining

Small intestinal tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices for HE staining. After sealing, observe under an electron optical microscope (× 100 times). One slice per mouse was randomly selected, and three fields of view per slice were photographed. Scoring was performed by two professional technicians in a double-blind manner according to the intestinal Chiu’s scoring criteria.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL staining is used to detect the degree of cell apoptosis. The small intestine tissue dehydrated with gradient sucrose is detected using the in situ cell death detection kit (Roche, Switzerland) instructions, sliced, fixed, permeabilized, blocked, reacted, stained with DAPI, and sealed. Observe the fluorescence microscope (× 100 times) in the dark field, count the number of apoptotic cells and total cells per unit area in each group of slides, and calculate the apoptosis ratio (number of apoptotic cells/total cell number × 100%).

Determination of DAO level in serum

The change of serum DAO activity can reflect the damage of small intestinal mucosa26. In this study, serum DAO activity was determined with a specific detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Determination of inflammatory factor levels

The small intestinal tissue was prepared into 10% homogenate, and the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the small intestinal tissue were determined according to the instructions of the ELISA kit (Lianke Bio, China); the level of MPO in the small intestinal tissue was determined by biochemical method according to the instructions of the MPO detection kit (Elabscience, China).

Determination of ferrous ion (Fe2+), glutathione (GSH), and lipid peroxidation indicators

Fe2+, GSH, MDA, and SOD detection kits (Elabscience, China) were used to evaluate the concentrations of Fe2+, GSH, MDA, and SOD in small intestinal tissue, respectively. Take 10% small intestinal tissue homogenate, and all steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from small intestinal tissues and quantified by BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). For target proteins and β-actin run on separate gels, a parallel loading and processing strategy was used: equal volumes of protein samples were loaded onto two SDS-PAGE gels of the same concentration (one for target proteins, the other for β-actin), and both gels were processed in parallel under identical conditions. After transfer onto PVDF or NC membranes and blocking with 5% skimmed milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-p-AMPK (1:500), anti-AMPK (1:1000), anti-Sirt1 (1:500), anti-GPX4 (1:1000), and anti-β-actin (1:5000) (all from Affinity, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, Solarbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using an ECL kit, and band gray values were quantified with ImageJ software. Relative protein expression was calculated as the ratio of the target protein gray value (Gel 1) to the β-actin gray value (Gel 2) in matching lanes; the mean relative expression level of the Sham group was normalized to 1, and the relative expression of all other groups was expressed as fold changes relative to the Sham group.

Statistical methods

The measurement data in this experiment were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 and SPSS 21.0 statistical software were used for data analysis. One-way analysis of variance was used for comparison between multiple sample means, and P < 0. 05 was considered statistically significant.
