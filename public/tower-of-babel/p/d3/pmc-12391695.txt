Injectable sustained-release danshensu sodium-loaded nanoparticle hydrogel targets macrophages to improve myocardial microenvironment for myocardial infarction treatment
Authors: Jianqiu Yang, Jing Wang, Zhihua Zeng, Zhenyu Chen, Dingguo Wang, Yongbing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial infarction (MI) induces cardiomyocyte necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and ventricular remodeling, leading to heart failure. To address this, we developed an intelligent cardiac patch, SMM@Gel, composed of a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive PVA-TSPBA hydrogel matrix reinforced via solvent exchange and salting-out technology, loaded with mannose-functionalized, danshensu sodium-loaded hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (Sa@mPDA-Man). This design makes sustained drug release and ROS scavenging come true. In vitro, studies demonstrated SMM@Gel promoted endothelial tube formation (24 ± 3 nodes vs. 6 ± 2 in controls) and M2 macrophage polarization (CD206+ cells) while reducing inflammation (iNOS downregulation). In vivo, experiments in MI rats revealed SMM@Gel preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF: 52.3 % ± 4.1 % vs. 38.5 % ± 3.2 % in the saline group), normalized ventricular dimensions (EDV/ESV) and enhanced wall thickness. Histological analysis showed reduced infarct size (18.7 % ± 2.5 % vs. 32.1 % ± 3.8 %), decreased inflammation, and improved neovascularization. RNA-seq identified pathways linked to angiogenesis, inflammation resolution, and extracellular matrix remodeling. These findings highlight SMM@Gel's potential as a dual-action therapy for MI, combining ROS scavenging, anti-inflammatory effects, and angiogenic promotion to mitigate post-MI remodeling and preserve cardiac function.Keywords: Cardiac patch, Myocardial infarction, Hydrogel, Intelligent biomaterials, Macrophage targeting

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, and ischemic/hemorrhagic disorders such as myocardial infarction (MI), represent the leading global health threat [1]. MI induces irreversible myocardial necrosis via coronary artery occlusion, resulting in up to 25 % loss of left ventricular myocytes within the first hours of infarction [2], followed by an inflammatory cascade that drives cardiomyocyte death, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, pathological remodeling, and fibrotic scar formation [3]. These fibrotic lesions compromise electrical conductivity and contractile synchrony, escalating risks of left ventricular dysfunction, heart failure (HF), and sudden cardiac arrest through infarct size-dependent collagen accumulation and structural alterations [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Despite advancements, post-MI clinical management remains inadequate: acute interventions focusing on reperfusion therapy (percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI] or thrombolysis) and pharmacological regimens (antithrombotic agents, β-blockers, statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEIs]) aim to salvage viable myocardium but face critical limitations [7]. PCI availability is constrained by catheterization laboratory resources (feasible in only 60 % of hospitals), thrombolysis is restricted to <12-h symptom onset without contraindications, and dual antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding risks (e.g., gastrointestinal/intracranial hemorrhage). Additionally, β-blockers may exacerbate acute heart failure, while ACEIs pose hypotension risks, with contraindications in bradycardia, asthma, or renal artery stenosis. Long-term management is further challenged by a 50 % 5-year survival rate across HF phenotypes [8,9], and heart transplantation—through curative—is limited by donor shortages, graft rejection, and immunosuppression-related complications [3,8]. Collectively, these therapeutic gaps underscore the urgent need for innovative strategies to mitigate adverse post-MI remodeling and improve clinical outcomes.

Over the past two decades, biomaterials like cardiac patches have emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue [10]. Cardiac patches, often composed of natural or synthetic scaffolds—with or without loaded therapeutic agents—provide mechanical reinforcement to the weakened ventricular wall. These prefabricated constructs, designed to meet individual patient needs, serve as platforms for delivering "cargo" such as cells, growth factors, exosomes, drugs, genes, and gas signaling molecules to ischemic regions, minimizing systemic side effects [11]. Biomimetic hydrogel patches have garnered significant attention in myocardial tissue engineering due to their ability to replicate the physical and biological properties of natural myocardium, supporting cell regeneration and repair of infarcted tissue [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Key attributes of these patches include elasticity, contractility, adhesive capacity to adapt to cardiac shape changes during systole and diastole, and essential biological properties like biocompatibility, biodegradability, and wet adhesion [[17], [18], [19]].

Danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza), a traditional Chinese herbal medicine, and its bioactive component danshensu [3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)lactic acid] have a long history of use in treating cardiovascular diseases, including coronary heart disease, MI, angina pectoris, and atherosclerosis [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Structurally featuring a catechol group, danshensu exerts antioxidant effects that reduce ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury in the heart [24,25]. In our prior research, we designed mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (mPDA) with hollow interiors and external mesopores, offering high specific surface area and robust drug-loading capacity. By encapsulating danshensu sodium and modifying the nanoparticles with mannose (Man)—a macrophage-targeting moiety—we created Sa@mPDA-Man nanoparticles [26]. Concurrently, we utilized the biocompatible PVA-TSPBA hydrogel as a matrix for a bio-smart patch, leveraging its sensitivity to reactive oxygen species (ROS)—a hallmark of infarction—to enhance hydrogel degradation. The low viscosity of the PVA-TSPBA liquid also aligns with the requirements for cardiac patch application [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. However, preliminary studies revealed that the loose network of PVA-TSPBA hydrogel lacked sufficient mechanical strength to support the infarcted myocardial wall, which is prone to rupture. To address this, we developed a salt-extracellular gel system by homogenizing the polymer network via solvent exchange and inducing uniform crystallization through salting, using sodium citrate (SC) as a salting-out solvent. This approach yielded the PT-SCSal-Exogels matrix, which, when loaded with Sa@mPDA-Man, formed the bright cardiac patch named SMM@Gel [31].

This study comprehensively characterizes the adhesive, biocompatible, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidative properties of SMM@Gel in vitro. In vivo, evaluations include its myocardial protective effects, angiogenic promotion, and cardiac repair capabilities in an SD rat MI model. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis via RNA-sequencing of infarcted heart tissues aims to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying SMM@Gel's therapeutic effects, with a focus on pathways involved in angiogenesis, inflammation resolution, and myocardial remodeling. By integrating mechanical support with targeted drug delivery and biomimetic design, this work positions SMM@Gel as a multifunctional biomaterial with translational potential for addressing the unmet clinical needs in ischemic heart disease treatment.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All reagents and materials used in this study were sourced from commercial suppliers and validated for experimental use. Cell culture media and supplements included Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, catalog no: 11965118), penicillin-streptomycin (PS, catalog no: 15140122), and fetal bovine serum (FBS, catalog no: 10099-141C), all purchased from Invitrogen (CA). Cytotoxicity and apoptosis assays utilized the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime Biotech, C0040) and Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide Staining Kit (Meridian Technology, M247364). Chemical reagents included glutaraldehyde (Aladdin, G105905), ethanol (McLean, E809063), poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (Aladdin, P477925), dopamine hydrochloride (Aladdin, D408026), Danshensu sodium (Aladdin, D408026), ammonium hydroxide (Aladdin, A112085), 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (Aladdin, T140693), 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid (Aladdin, B181368), N, N′, N′-tetramethyl-1-mercapto-3-propanediamine (Aladdin, T106825), and tetrahydrofuran (Aladdin, T120775). Antibodies targeting CD31 (ab182981), troponin T (ab209813), CD206 (ab64693), iNOS (ab178945), Collagen III (ab184993), and α-actinin (ab108198) were obtained from Abcam, with fluorescent secondary antibodies including goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 594, ab150080) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, ab150113). Antioxidant capacity was evaluated using 1,1-diphenyl-2-trinitrophenylhydrazine (DPPH, Sigma-Aldrich, 1898-66-4).

Preparation of hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles loaded with sodium salvinorin (Sa@mPDA)

The specific synthesis methodology entailed the sequential execution of the following steps: Initially, 1 g of F127 and 0.5 g of DA were dissolved in a homogeneous mixture comprising 50 ml of water and 50 ml of ethanol. This solution was then stirred at a constant speed of 500 revolutions per minute for 30 min. Subsequently, 2 ml of TMB was injected slowly into the solution, with continuous stirring for another 30 min. Following this, 5 ml of NH4OH was injected into the mixture to catalyze the self-polymerization of DA. After allowing the reaction to proceed for 30 min, Sa, at a concentration of 0.05 mg/ml, was directly added and dispersed uniformly within the aforementioned solution under sonication for 1 h. It was observed that the variation in the amount of the added MnCO significantly impacted the loading capacity. The resultant products were subsequently centrifuged and rigorously washed at least five times with water and ethanol to guarantee the complete removal of the block copolymer template. Ultimately, the Sa@mPDA powders were successfully obtained after being dried at a temperature of 60 °C for 12 h.

Preparation of hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (Sa@mPDA-Man) loaded with sodium danshensu for targeting action

Sa@mPDA-Man was synthesized by binding ring-opened mannose to the polymer Sa@mPDA: 100 mg of Sa@mPDA and 42.5 mg of mannose were dissolved in 2 ml of DMSO. The solution was stirred at a temperature of 40 °C for 5 h. Subsequently, the solution underwent purification through dialysis, utilizing distilled water with a molecular weight cutoff of 3500 Da for 48 h. The solution was dried in a vacuum oven using the freeze-drying method, and Sa@mPDA-Man powder was obtained after 12 h.

Characterization of hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (Sa@mPDA-Man) loaded with sodium danshensu for targeting action

Sample characterization utilized Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 (FTIR), K-Alpha (XPS/XRD), TESCAN MIRA LMS (SEM), FEI Talos F200S (TEM), and Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (DLS). FTIR characterized chemical functional groups, XPS/XRD analyzed surface composition/crystallinity, SEM/TEM visualized microstructures, and DLS measured particle size distributions.

Biocompatibility of hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (Sa@mPDA-Man) loaded with sodium tension by targeted action

The cytotoxicity of Sa@mPDA was evaluated using both leaching and direct contact methodologies. The sterilized complexes were incubated in a culture medium for 24 h. Subsequently, H9C2 cardiomyoblasts, at a density of 4 × 103 cells per well, were inoculated into 96-well plates and maintained in the conventional medium for 24 h. After this initial incubation, the medium was replaced with 100 μL of leachate at varying concentrations, namely 0, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 mg/ml. On days 1, 3, and 5 of the experiment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, dissolved in 90 μL of the medium, were added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm and converted into a metric of cell activity.

Preparation of ROS-responsive composite hydrogels containing functionalized nanoparticles

TSPBA linker was synthesized by reacting 4-(bromoethyl)phenylboronic acid (1 g, 4.6 mmol) with N, N, N′, N′-tetramethyl-1,3-propanediamine (0.2 g, 1.5 mmol) in DMF (40 ml) at 60 °C for 24 h. The reaction mixture was washed three times with THF, followed by precipitation with 100 ml THF to collect the white solid. After three additional THF washes, the residue was suspended in 20 ml THF, centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and lyophilized to yield 0.6 g (70 %) of white solid TSPBA.

PVA DMSO solution preparation: Generally, a 10 % w/v PVA DMSO solution is prepared by dissolving 1 g of PVA powder in 10 ml of DMSO and stirring vigorously at 120 °C for 5 h.

Preparation of solution A: Solution A was prepared by adding 2500 μg of Sa@mPDA-Man nanoparticles (NPs) to 10 ml of a solution containing 10 % wt/wt PVA DMSO and ultrasonically mixing for 5 min.

Preparation of solution B: 5 ml of 6 wt% TSPBA solution was prepared and mixed with 5 ml of 10 % sodium citrate solution, and solution B was prepared by stirring and mixing for 5 min in a magnetic mixer.

Preparation of PVA salt-exo-gel: The PVA salt-exo-gel was prepared using a solvent exchange-assisted salting out method. Solution A and solution B were loaded into a double-barrel syringe and injected simultaneously. They were then heated to 37 °C to form a gel within 10 s. The degree of salting out increased with time.

Composite hydrogel characterization involved structural analysis via TESCAN MIRA LMS scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscopy (AFM). Chemical compositions of SC, PVA, PVA-SCSal-Exogels, TSPBA, PT, and PT-SCSal-Exogels were determined using Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Drug release profiles were measured under varying hydrogen peroxide concentrations (125 μM, 250 μM, 500 μM) and pH conditions (pH 7.4/5.0 PBS) via UV–visible spectrophotometry (Shanghai Mabel Instrument Co., Ltd.). Self-healing ability of composite hydrogels

Composite hydrogel in vitro cell migration assay

H9C2 migration was evaluated using scratch and transwell assays. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates with high-glucose DMEM +10 % FBS until 90 % confluence. A linear scratch was created using a sterile 1 ml pipette tip, followed by PBS washing to remove detached cells. Serum-free DMEM (control) or serum-free DMEM containing sample extracts was added for 24 h. Phase contrast images were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h post-scratch, with gap closure quantified using NIH ImageJ software.

For transwell migration, H9C2 (1 × 104 cells/well) suspended in 1 % FBS DMEM was added to 24-well transwell inserts (Corning). Lower chambers contained 500 μL sample extracts (PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels, MM@Gel, SMM@Gel) or control medium. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C/5 % CO2, non-migrated cells were removed from upper insert surfaces with cotton swabs. Migrated cells on lower surfaces were stained with crystal violet (Beyotime) and visualized microscopically.

Composite hydrogel in vitro angiogenesis test

In vitro, angiogenesis assays were conducted to investigate the angiogenic potential of various sample extracts. Specifically, 24-well plates were coated with 300 μL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel (sourced from BD Biosciences) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to achieve solidification. Following this, HUVEC cells (at a concentration of 2 × 104 cells per well) were inoculated onto the Matrigel substrates. Evaluation of composite hydrogels for SD rat infarction modeling

SD Rat Myocardial Infarction Model Establishment: Male Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks old, 180–200 g) were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation, tracheal intubated with a 16G trocar needle and ventilated. A left thoracotomy at the third/fourth intercostal space exposed the heart, followed by permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery using a 6-0 nylon suture. Animals were randomized into five groups (n = 18/group): sham, saline, PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels and SMM@Gel. At 30 min post-ischemia, 100 μL saline or respective hydrogel was injected into infarct border zones. Sham-operated controls underwent identical surgery without artery ligation or injections. Chest cavities were closed, and penicillin was administered to prevent infection. Postoperative care included intraperitoneal carprofen (5 mg/kg, 100 μL) for analgesia and 5 wt% glucose (100 μL) for hydration.

Echocardiographic Evaluation of Cardiac Function in Rats: On days 7 and 28 of post-myocardial infarction, the rats were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. Subsequently, by using ultrasound equipment, transthoracic echocardiography was performed on all groups (Esaote, MyLab™). M-mode tracking was recorded along the long axis of the left ventricle. The LVEF, LVDd, and LVDS percentage values were measured, and at least three cardiac cycles were analyzed per image.

RNA-seq evaluation

RNA Isolation and Quality Assessment: Total RNA was isolated from samples using TRIzol (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The quantity and purity of the total RNA were determined using a NanoDrop ND - 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA). RNA integrity was examined by a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA) and confirmed via agarose electrophoresis. Only RNA samples with a concentration >50 ng/μL, RIN value > 7.0, OD260/280 ratio >1.8, and total amount >1 μg were used for downstream experiments.

mRNA Capture and Fragmentation: PolyA-containing mRNA was purified twice using oligo(dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads Oligo (dT), item no. 25–61005, Thermo Fisher, USA). The captured mRNAs were fragmented at 94 °C for 5–7 min using the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module, Item No. E6150S, USA).

cDNA Synthesis: The fragmented RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase, No. 1896649, CA, USA). E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB, No. m0209, USA) and RNase H (NEB, no. m0209; NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module, No. E6150S, USA; and NEB, item no. m0297, USA) was used for second - strand synthesis, converting DNA - RNA hybrids into DNA duplexes. dUTP Solution (Thermo Fisher, item no. R0133, CA, USA) was added to create blunt-ended double-stranded DNA. An A base was added to each strand end for adapter ligation. The fragments were then size-screened and purified using magnetic beads.

Library Preparation and PCR Amplification: The second strand was digested with UDG enzyme (NEB, item no. m0280, MA, US) and PCR-amplified. The PCR conditions were: pre - denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 8 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. This generated a library with a fragment size of 300bp ± 50bp.

Sequencing: Bipartite sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform (LC Bio-Technology CO. Ltd., Hangzhou, China) using the PE150 sequencing mode according to standard procedures.

Data Analysis: Genes or transcripts were assembled and quantified using FPKM with StringTie software. Differential gene expression between samples was analyzed with the R package edgeR. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the genes were carried out using DAVID software.

Research on cellular pathway mechanisms

Raw264.7 was inoculated in cell flasks and cultured in high glucose DMEM containing 10 % FBS. When the cells were adhered to the wall and multiplied to 90 %, the medium was replaced with 2 ml of leaching solution of PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels and SMM@Gel. Negative controls were left untreated, and positive controls were incubated with DMEM containing IL-4 (40 ng/ml) for 1 day. After collecting the supernatants, STAT6/VEGF staining was performed, observed, and photographed under a fluorescence-inverted microscope (Leica DMi8). Subsequently, different groups of supernatants were incubated with H9C2 cells and Bend.3 cells for 48 h. Bend.3 cells were stained with MEK1/2 and ERK1/2, and H9C2 cells were stained with α-Actinin and cTnT and observed and photographed under a fluorescence inverted microscope. The fluorescence intensity was measured and quantified utilizing the ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distributions was first verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test to determine the appropriateness of parametric statistical methods. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess differences across multiple groups, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test to identify specific pairwise comparisons.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were generated with Origin 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of functionalized dopamine nanoparticles

In the current research, hydrogels have been widely used in cardiac repair, especially smart stimuli-responsive hydrogels. They can precisely regulate drug release according to the changes in the disease condition, enhance the therapeutic effect, and thus provide a new approach to the treatment of myocardial infarction [32]. Smart reactive hydrogels can improve compatibility with biological tissues, reduce the inflammatory response, and promote tissue repair by introducing bioactive groups or natural components so as to match the characteristics and repair requirements of cardiac tissues [33]. However, there are still several technical challenges in translating these materials into clinical applications. The key issues among them are how to design the mechanical properties of such hydrogels to support the damaged heart and how to precisely regulate the responsiveness of hydrogels to physiological signals and release drugs to ensure that they can adapt to the dynamic changes in the microenvironment during the process of cardiac tissue injury and repair. First, hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles targeting macrophages containing sodium Danshensu were prepared, as shown in the preparation schematic of Fig. 1A observation under transmission electron microscopy. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that all nanoparticles were spherical and that mPDA had a hollow porous structure with C, O, and N contents of 79.39 %, 12.62 %, and 7.99 %, respectively. Preparation and characterization of salt-exogel drug release system

As shown schematically in Fig. 2A, the fabrication of PVA + TSPBA hydrogels followed our previously reported approach: we first synthesized the ROS/pH dual-responsive crosslinker TSPBA via borate ester bonds, confirmed by NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S1A and B). The loosely crosslinked PVA + TSPBA network underwent salt-extrusion gelation, a process homogenizing the polymer matrix through solvent exchange—replacing dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with hydrophilic sodium citrate (SC)—to induce salt-mediated crystallization. This transformed the low-crosslinking-density hydrogel into a PVA salt-exogel with a dense, homogeneous network of embedded crystalline domains (Fig. 2A), enhancing mechanical properties by enabling energy dissipation during deformation through the gradual unraveling of these domains while resisting initial deformation.

Scanning electron microscopy revealed that PT-SCSal-Exogels and SMM@Gel exhibited a less porous, more tightly crosslinked microstructure compared to the loosely crosslinked, highly porous architecture of PVA + TSPBA (Fig. 2B). Liquid displacement measurements quantified porosity as 82.99 ± 2.17 % for PVA, 76.19 ± 3.02 % for PVA + TSPBA, and 60.72 ± 4.31 % for PT-SCSal-Exogels (Fig. 2J), consistent with SEM observations. Rheological analyses (dynamic time and frequency scans) showed PVA remained in a liquid state (G″ > G′), while PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels, and SMM@Gel exhibited gel-state characteristics (G′ > G″), confirming successful crosslinking (Fig. 2C–F).

PT-SCSal-Exogels demonstrated rapid self-healing at 37 °C, fully recovering mechanical integrity within 1 min (Fig. 2G), a property corroborated by dynamic shear rheology showing the hydrogel transitioned to a liquid under high shear (1000 % strain) and reverted to a gel state at low shear (1 % strain, Fig. 2H). Swelling tests indicated an inverse relationship between crosslinking density and water uptake: PVA showed a 10.35 ± 0.91 % swelling rate, PVA + TSPBA 8.56 ± 1.02 %, and PT-SCSal-Exogels 5.96 ± 0.95 % (Fig. 2I), reflecting the denser network structure of salt-extruded gels that restricts solvent penetration.

Functionalized characterization of nanoparticles in SMM@Gel

As shown in Fig. 6A, we verified the different functionalized characterizations of nanoparticles in composite hydrogels: a) macrophage targeting function, b) vasculogenic function, and c) pro-macrophage polarization function. Mannose modulates macrophage phagocytosis by acting on mannose receptors on macrophages. Macrophages that promote wound healing tend to express more mannose receptors, and thus, mannose promotes phagocytosis of inflammatory cells by macrophages and exerts an anti-inflammatory effect. We used scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy to observe the macrophage phagocytic performance of NPs (Sa@mPDA) and Man-NPs (Sa@mPDA-Man) co-cultured with macrophages at different time points (1 h, 2 h) (Fig. 6B and C); at the first-hour NPs were phagocytosed 1.33 ± 0.56 %, Man-NPs were phagocytosed 16.33 ± 2.76 %; at the second-hour NPs were phagocytosed by 9.63 ± 1.26 %, and Man-NPs were phagocytosed by 39.67 ± 2.33 % (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, In vitro tube formation assays revealed that SMM@Gel extracts significantly enhanced endothelial network formation compared to control groups. The SMM@Gel group displayed a mean node count of 24 ± 3, which was statistically higher than MM@Gel (13 ± 1), PT-SCSal-Exogels (12 ± 2), PVA + TSPBA (9 ± 2), and blank (6 ± 2) groups (Fig. 6E and F). Macrophage polarization studies showed that LPS-induced inflammation skewed Raw264.7 cells toward an M1 phenotype, characterized by elevated iNOS expression in blank, PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels and MM@Gel groups. In contrast, SMM@Gel treatment shifted macrophages to an M2 phenotype, as indicated by increased CD206 expression (Fig. 6G, Fig. S7A and B). Furthermore, flow cytometry was used to confirm further and quantify the expression of iNOS and CD206 in macrophages co-cultured with the Blank, PVA + TSPBA, PT-SCSal-Exogels and SMM@Gel groups after LPS stimulation (Fig. 6H–J, Fig. S7C). This dual functionality arises from SMM@Gel's composite design: the PVA-TSPBA hydrogel scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) via chemical group interactions, while Sa@mPDA-Man contributes anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties through dopamine, danshensu sodium, and mannose. Together, these components synergistically promote M2 polarization and reduce oxidative stress in the microenvironment.

The administration of SMM@Gel hydrogel resulted in a markedly enhanced LV ejection fraction, reduced LV dimensions, and increased wall thickness. These changes were accompanied by a notable reduction in wall stress and the facilitation of LV remodeling, which collectively suggest that SMM@Gel hydrogel effectively prevented the exacerbation of heart failure in the early phase following myocardial infarction.

The results showed that many macrophages were mobilized and recruited in the infarcted area after myocardial infarction in all groups. iNOS expression of macrophages in the SMM@Gel group (0.71 ± 0.02) was much lower than that in the Saline group (3.01 ± 0.23), the PVA + TSPBA group (1.75 ± 0.22) and the PT-SCSal-Exogels group (1.28 ± 0.11), indicating that the SMM@Gel microenvironment inhibited more macrophages from polarizing toward M1 (Fig. 9C). In addition, the SMM@Gel group had more macrophage CD206 expression (5.18 ± 0.23) than the Saline group (2.10 ± 0.12), the PVA + TSPBA group (2.85 ± 0.32), and the PT-SCSal-Exogels group (3.99 ± 0.23), suggesting that the SMM@Gel microenvironment induced more M2-type macrophages (Fig. 9D). Meanwhile, The M1/M2 ratio of 0.15 ± 0.12 in the SMM@Gel group was much smaller than that in the Saline group(1.41 ± 0.42), PVA + TSPBA group (0.69 ± 0.13) and PT-SCSal-Exogels group (0.32 ± 0.13) (Fig. 9E). Moreover, As shown in Fig. S10, the semiquantitative results of immunofluorescence staining were also consistent with that. The results showed that monocytes infiltrated tissues and locally differentiated into macrophages, which were then polarized into the M2 phenotype in the immunomodulatory microenvironment by SMM@Gel. As shown in Fig. 9F, CD31 immunofluorescence staining was applied to evaluate the regeneration of microvessels at the infarct site; neoangiogenesis facilitated the repair of infarcted myocardium. We have demonstrated that SMM@Gel promotes endothelial cell migration, lumen formation, and proliferation, which are key angiogenesis elements. The semiquantitative results of fluorescence intensity indicated that microvessel regeneration was more favorable in the SMM@Gel group (2.23 ± 0.04) than in the Saline group (1.00 ± 0.02), the PVA + TSPBA group (1.07 ± 0.01), and the PT-SCSal-Exogels group (1.07 ± 0.02) (Fig. 9G). Finally, we used cTnT/CollagenIII immunofluorescence co-staining to evaluate the myocardial condition (Fig. 9H). In pathological conditions, such as myocardial hypertrophy, a large number of collagen fibers proliferate, which disorganizes the myocardial structure and forms scar or organ sclerosis, etc., and these alterations can lead to a decrease in myocardial contraction, a blockage of myocardial blood supply, and an increase in oxygen consumption, which can, in turn, induce cardiac insufficiency, CollagenIII Semiquantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity showed that collagen appeared in the infarct region in the SMM@Gel group, but much less than in the Saline group (Fig. 9I); and cTnT showed that more normal cardiomyocytes appeared in the SMM@Gel group (Fig. 9J).

Exploration of RNA-seq and intercellular mechanisms in SMM@Gel for myocardial infarction treatment

RNA-seq analysis was performed on myocardial infarction tissues (day 28) to elucidate the potential pro-myocardial repair mechanism of SMM@Gel. Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the selected myocardial infarction tissues showed good agreement and could be further analyzed (Fig. 10A). The volcano map identified 189 down-regulated genes and 716 upregulated genes in the SMM@Gel group compared to the blank group (P < 0.05, fold change >2.0) (Fig. 10B). The heatmap additionally demonstrated the ten most significantly upregulated genes (e.g., Mapk8, Vegfa, Strp4) and down-regulated genes (e.g., Big2, Abra, Fam111a) in the SMM@Gel group relative to the Blank group(Fig. 10C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes positively regulated immune responses, anti-inflammatory processes, and myocardial recovery activities (Fig. 10D). In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed positive regulation of cardiac development and Mapk pathway by the SMM@Gel group, which is consistent with the results of the GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 10E and F). Regarding inflammatory immune regulation, GO enrichment analysis also expressed concordance with GSEA, and the SMM@Gel group played a positive role in inflammatory immune regulation (Fig. 10G–I). Regarding promoting angiogenesis, GO enrichment analysis also expressed a high degree of consistency with GSEA results, with the SMM@Gel group expressing a more enhanced effect on promoting angiogenesis than the Blank group (Fig. 10J–L).

To validate the mechanism of SMM@Gel in cell-cell interactions, we designed an experiment as illustrated in Fig. 11A: hydrogels or IL-4 were co-cultured with mouse macrophages (Raw264.7) in cell culture inserts for 24 h, and the supernatants were subsequently co-cultured with mouse microvascular endothelial cells (Bend.3) and cardiomyocytes (H9C2) for 3 days, respectively. Compared with the control group, immunofluorescence and Western blot (WB) assays revealed that STAT6/VEGF expression in macrophages was significantly upregulated in the SMM@Gel group (Fig. 11B and C; Fig. S11A). Additionally, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) showed that the concentration of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the culture medium was also elevated (Fig. S11D). These results confirmed the hypothesis that the SMM@Gel hydrogel promotes VEGF expression by activating M2-type macrophages. MEK1/2 represents one of the downstream factors of VEGF-induced proliferation. Immunofluorescence results demonstrated significantly increased phosphorylation levels of MEK1/2 and Erk1/2 in Bend.3 cells cultured with macrophage secretions from the SMM@Gel-treated group (Fig. 11D and E; Fig. S11B), suggesting that the VEGF-MAPK pathway activated Bend.3 cell proliferation. For cardiomyocytes, immunofluorescence signals of cTnT and α-actinin were significantly enhanced in H9C2 cells co-cultured with macrophage secretions from the SMM@Gel-treated group (Fig. 11F and G; Fig. S11C). The above data indicate that SMM@Gel may promote endothelial cell proliferation and enhance cardiomyocyte actin and troponin expression by facilitating M2-type macrophage polarization and VEGF secretion, suggesting that its driven angiogenesis and cardiomyocyte protection may be mediated through inflammatory regulation (Fig. 11H). Experiments have shown that after implanting the hydrogel with excellent immunomodulatory properties, the Sa@mPDA-Man nanoparticles release danshensu, activate the immune system, dynamically regulate the host myocardial immune microenvironment, and enhance the myocardial protective effect and the protection against ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) under physiological conditions. Under the action of danshensu, the signal is transmitted to the intracellular domain of macrophages, resulting in tyrosine phosphorylation and activation of the receptor JAK1. JAK1 activates the STAT6 docking site and leads to tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT6, which finally enters the nucleus, binds to DNA, and regulates transcription. Ultimately, M2 macrophages release VEGF, promote the upregulation of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 in vascular endothelial cells, and finally lead to angiogenesis. Meanwhile, in the immunofluorescence staining of cardiomyocytes, it can be found that the expressions of cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and α-actinin increase, demonstrating that it has multiple cardioprotective effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cardiac fibrosis, and anti-cardiac hypertrophy.

This study demonstrates the excellent biocompatibility and cardioprotective ability of hydrogels, alongside innovations in enhancing mechanical/adhesive properties in liquid states, integrating an intelligent drug release system, and developing a nano-sustained-release platform (SMM@Gel) for macrophage-targeted delivery of sodium danshensu—enabling on-demand drug release/degradation over ≥7 days post-myocardial infarction (MI). Mechanistic investigations reveal that danshensu activates the JAK-STAT pathway to upregulate VEGF, promoting angiogenesis and myocardial protection. The injectable design further facilitates minimally invasive treatment via intrapericardial injection, a strategy with proven local delivery efficiency but limited clinical validation [39,40]. Together, these findings establish a novel, minimally invasive and continuous therapeutic approach for MI. However, several limitations should be acknowledged: the use of young (6-week-old) rat models failed to recapitulate the age-related inflammatory microenvironment typical of elderly MI patients, and future studies should validate the hydrogel's efficacy in aging animal models, the reliance on Sprague-Dawley rats—whose inherent cardiac regenerative capacity exceeds that of humans—requires caution in extrapolating results to clinical settings and subsequent validation in large-animal models (e.g., pigs) or humanized organ-on-a-chip systems are critical, and while SMM@Gel was shown to modulate macrophage polarization the study did not elucidate its potential interaction with epigenetic mechanisms (e.g., DNA methylation) requiring further investigations to deepen understanding of its therapeutic mechanisms. These limitations highlight directions for future research to enhance the clinical applicability and mechanistic clarity of this innovative cardiac patch technology.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a PT-SCSal-Exogels release system with good elasticity and wet adhesion by solvent exchange and salting-out treatments and loaded Sa@mPDA-Man in it, which resulted in an intelligent cardiac patch, SMM@Gel. It was verified that the SMM@Gel showed desirable cytocompatibility in vitro blood compatibility vitro and good histocompatibility after long-term subcutaneous implantation. In addition, SMM@Gel induced macrophage M2 polarization and sustained drug targeting to the infarct site to improve MI prognosis. In vivo, SMM@Gel was able to attenuate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in a rat infarction model significantly, and it could promote in situ regeneration of myocardial tissue in rats. SMM@Gel has significant positive significance for intervening in myocardial repair and ventricular remodeling after the onset of MI, and it effectively prevents and even reverses adverse effects, such as heart failure, and it shows great promise for clinical application.
