A spinal USP7-Bach1 positive feedback loop drives NOX4-mediated ferroptosis in neuropathic pain
Authors: Bo Jiao, Shiyang Liu, Kaiwen Zhang, Wencui Zhang, Xueqin Cao, Xianwei Zhang, Jiaoli Sun, Hui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain is a chronic condition caused by somatosensory system lesions, characterized by persistent hypersensitivity and limited therapeutic options. While oxidative stress and ferroptosis contribute to pain progression, the upstream regulators that orchestrate these redox events remain largely undefined. Here, we identify the transcription factor Bach1 as a key regulator of ferroptosis in the spinal cord. Following peripheral nerve injury, Bach1 expression was markedly upregulated in spinal neurons. Bach1 directly bound to the NOX4 promoter and enhanced its transcription, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and lipid peroxidation. We further found that the deubiquitinase USP7 interacted with Bach1 and promoted its stability through deubiquitination. In turn, Bach1 transcriptionally upregulated USP7, forming a positive feedback loop that sustained redox dysregulation and pain hypersensitivity. Inhibition of the USP7–Bach1–NOX4 axis attenuated ferroptosis and alleviated neuropathic pain. Together, these findings delineate a USP7–Bach1–NOX4 signaling axis that links post-translational stabilization to transcriptional activation in spinal ferroptosis and identify potential therapeutic targets for neuropathic pain.Keywords: Neuropathic pain, Ferroptosis, Bach1, USP7, NOX4, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Neuropathic pain, a chronic condition arising from somatosensory nervous system injury or dysfunction, affects 7–10% of the population and imposes a substantial clinical and societal burden [1]. Current therapies, including opioids, antidepressants, and anticonvulsants, often yield inadequate relief and are plagued by side effects, tolerance, and addiction risk [2,3]. This profound therapeutic impasse underscores the critical need to elucidate novel pathogenic mechanisms underlying neuropathic pain.

Accumulating evidence extends the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain beyond neuronal hyperexcitability and neuroinflammation to include sustained redox imbalance and associated neuronal demise [[4], [5], [6]]. In particular, ferroptosis—an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death—has emerged as a critical mechanism contributing to spinal cord pathology in chronic pain [7,8]. Key hallmarks of ferroptosis, including glutathione depletion, elevated lipid peroxides, and distinct mitochondrial alterations, are evident in neuropathic pain models, and its pharmacological inhibition robustly alleviates pain hypersensitivity [9,10]. However, the specific transcriptional programs that are activated to execute ferroptosis in this context, and how they are regulated at the post-translational level, remain major knowledge gaps. In particular, identifying the key transcription factors that orchestrate the pro-ferroptosis gene expression profile, and the critical downstream effector genes they control, is essential for understanding and therapeutically targeting this cell death pathway in neuropathic pain.

The transcription factor BTB and CNC homology 1 (Bach1) is a central component of the Cap ‘n’ Collar basic leucine zipper (CNC-bZip) family and a key regulator of cellular responses to oxidative stress [11]. It modulates gene expression by binding to antioxidant response elements (AREs) or Maf recognition elements (MAREs), often in competition with the activator NRF2, thereby influencing a network of redox-related genes [12]. This pivotal role is underscored by findings that Bach1 depletion or inhibition confers protection against oxidative damage in models of neurodegenerative disease and cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury [13,14]. Notably, Bach1 can also influence the transcription of pro-oxidant enzymes to modulate oxidative stress and ferroptosis, as demonstrated in models of ischemic stroke and cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [15,16].

As a constitutively active enzyme, NOX4 serves as a primary source of sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the nervous system and has been implicated in spinal oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [[17], [18], [19]]. Recent studies have broadened the role of NOX4 in diverse pain states. For instance, NOX4-mediated epigenetic modification of TRPV1 contributes to chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathic pain [20]. Its involvement in endoplasmic reticulum stress has also been linked to the development of morphine tolerance [21]. Crucially, single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis identified a selective loss of a specific NOX4+ GABAergic neuronal subpopulation in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis during chronic migraine progression, highlighting NOX4 as a conserved driver of inhibitory circuit dysfunction [22]. Although NOX4 plays a key role in ROS production and lipid peroxidation, the relationship between Bach1 and NOX4 in neuropathic pain remains unclear.

The stability and activity of transcription factors like Bach1 are often tightly controlled by post-translational modifications, particularly ubiquitination [23]. Ubiquitin-specific protease 7 (USP7) is a key deubiquitinase that stabilizes diverse substrates. Beyond its roles in cancer and development [24,25], USP7 has recently emerged as a multifunctional regulator in neurodegeneration. In Alzheimer's disease (AD) models, USP7 has been implicated in maintaining ubiquitin homeostasis and autophagic flux [26]. More recent evidence suggests that pharmacological inhibition of USP7 attenuates oxidative stress and reduces ferroptotic vulnerability in AD-related neurotoxicity, potentially through modulation of GPX4 stability and redox balance [27]. Similarly, USP7 inhibition alleviates oxidative stress–associated injury in spinal cord injury and cerebral ischemia–reperfusion models [28,29]. These studies collectively support a broader role for USP7 in neuronal stress adaptation and highlight its emerging translational relevance in CNS disorders [28,29]. Notably, Bach1 can transcriptionally upregulate USP7 in human embryonic stem cells [24]. Previous studies have also shown that Bach1 protein stability can be regulated by deubiquitinases in cancer contexts [30,31], highlighting the general principle that Bach1 abundance can be modulated post-translationally. This suggests a potential reciprocal regulatory relationship. However, whether USP7 stabilizes Bach1 in neurons, and whether such regulation orchestrates Bach1-driven oxidative stress and ferroptosis during neuropathic pain, remains elusive. Defining a USP7-Bach1 regulatory axis would therefore reveal a previously unrecognized post-translational gatekeeper linking ubiquitin signaling to redox imbalance, neuronal ferroptosis, and chronic pain hypersensitivity.

In this study, we employed a spared nerve injury (SNI) mouse model to investigate the expression, interaction, and functional role of USP7 and Bach1 in the spinal cord. We found that USP7 deubiquitinates and stabilizes Bach1, while Bach1 reciprocally activates USP7, forming a well-defined positive feedback loop. Furthermore, Bach1 directly binds to the promoter of NOX4, leading to increased NOX4 expression and enhanced ROS generation, which drives ferroptosis and contributes to pain hypersensitivity. Inhibition of USP7 or Bach1 effectively reversed these effects. Collectively, our findings identify the USP7-Bach1-NOX4 axis as a novel redox-regulated pathway that drives ferroptosis and neuropathic pain, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for pain management.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China. Mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures (No.TJH-202409015) were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology and conformed to National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and ARRIVE Guidelines for Reporting Animal Research.

Establishment of neuropathic pain model

The SNI model of neuropathic pain was established as previously described [32]. Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia (2%), the left sciatic nerve was exposed, and the common peroneal and tibial branches were tightly ligated with 5–0 silk and transected, leaving the sural nerve intact. Sham-operated mice underwent the same exposure without ligation or transection.

Drug and viral administration

In in vivo experiments, the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 (10 mg/kg, i.p., Hy-100579) [7], the Bach1 inhibitor Bach1-IN-1 (also known as HPPE, 10 mg/kg, i.g., HY-153040, MCE, China), the NOX4 inhibitor GLX351322 (5 mg/kg, i.p., HY-100111, MCE, China), and the USP7 inhibitor P005091 (15 mg/kg, i.p., HY-15667, MCE, China) were administered once daily for 5 consecutive days. The GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 (5 μg, HY‐100218A) was administered via intrathecal injection [9].

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was designed according to previous studies [33,34] and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The sequence is provided in Table S1. Intrathecal siRNA targeting Bach1, NOX4, or USP7 (2 μg in 10 μL PBS) was administered once daily for 3 consecutive days starting on post-operative Day 7. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blot and qRT-PCR of spinal dorsal horn tissues.

For gain-of-function experiments, pAAV-CAG-USP7-3xFlag-P2A-EGFP-polyA (AAV-USP7), pAAV-CAG-Bach1-3xFlag-P2A-EGFP-polyA (AAV-Bach1), or pAAV-CAG-MCS-3xFlag-P2A-EGFP-polyA (AAV-Vector) were intrathecally injected via lumbar puncture under brief isoflurane anesthesia (1 × 1012 v.g./ml, 10 μL per mouse). A 21-day interval was maintained following AAV injection to ensure stable and maximal transgene expression within the spinal cord dorsal horn prior to the initiation of inhibitor or siRNA treatment, as previous studies indicate that AAV-mediated protein expression typically peaks around 3 weeks post-infection [35]. Viral expression was confirmed by EGFP fluorescence in the spinal dorsal horn.

Behavioral assessment

Mechanical allodynia was evaluated by measuring the paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) using the “Up and Down” method with a series of von Frey filaments [36]. Mice were acclimated individually in transparent chambers on a wire-mesh floor for 30 min prior to testing. Filaments of ascending forces (0.02 - 2.0 g) were applied vertically to the plantar surface of the left hind paw until slight bending was observed. Each stimulus was maintained for approximately 1 s. A positive response was defined as rapid paw withdrawal, shaking, or licking. Following a positive response, the next weaker filament was applied after a 5 min interval; after a negative response, the next stronger filament was used. The PWT was determined as the minimal filament force (in grams) that elicited a consistent positive response.

Thermal nociception was assessed using the Hargreaves plantar test apparatus. Mice were placed individually in compartments on a transparent glass plate and allowed to acclimate for 30 min. A movable radiant heat source was focused on the plantar surface of the left hind paw, and the paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was automatically recorded. Each paw was tested three times with intervals of at least 5 min between trials, and the mean PWL was calculated from the three measurements.

Western blot analysis

Spinal cord tissues or cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-USP7 (1:1000, ab108931, Abcam), anti-Bach1 (1:1000, DF8317, Affinity), anti-NOX4 (1:1000, 14347-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-GPX4 (1:1000, A11243, Abclonal), anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, A25302, Abclonal), anti-4-HNE (1:1000, 68538-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-ACSL4 (1:5000, 22401-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-NRF2 (1:2000, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-β-actin (1:5000, AC038, Abclonal). Blots were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5000, AS014, Abclonal), visualized using Super Lumia ECL Plus HRP Substrate Kit (K22030, Abbkine) and scanned by the ChemiDoc XRS System (Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). The intensity of protein bands was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and normalized to the corresponding β-actin loading control.

Biochemical assays for ferroptosis

Ferric ion levels were measured using a tissue iron analysis kit (A039-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China) following the manufacturer's protocols. The levels of ROS, malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced glutathione (GSH) were detected according to the manufacture's instructions using reactive oxygen species assay kit (E004-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China), lipid peroxidation MDA assay kits (S0131, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime Biotechnology, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The L4–L6 segments of the spinal cord were rapidly dissected, post-fixed in 4% PFA at room temperature, then dehydrated and embedded in paraffin blocks. Consecutive 4-μm thick paraffin sections were cut and mounted on slides for immunostaining. Prior to staining, these sections underwent deparaffinization, hydration, and antigen retrieval in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave method. Spinal cord sections were incubated with antibodies against 4-HNE (1:100, 68538-1-Ig, Proteintech), USP7 (1:100, ab108931, Abcam), Bach1 (1:100, DF8317, Affinity), NeuN (1:50, ab104224, Abcam), Iba1 (1:100, ET1705-78, Huabio), and GFAP (1:100, 3670, Cell Signaling Technology). After PBS washes, secondary antibodies were applied (SA00013-8, SA00013-5, SA00013-2, Proteintech), and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BX51, OLYMPUS, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Spinal cord tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan). Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated as a hallmark of ferroptosis.

Cell culture and transfection

Mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The expression plasmid His-USP7 or Flag-Bach1 was constructed by cloning the coding sequence of human USP7 or Bach1 into the pcDNA3.1 vector. The Bach1-targeting siRNA (si-Bach1) was designed and synthesized based on previous study [37], and its sequence is listed in Table S1. Cells were transfected with USP7 or Bach1 plasmids or si-Bach1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Bach1-IN-1 (5 μM) was added 6 h post-transfection where indicated.

For rescue experiments, cells were divided into: vehicle control (Ctrl), USP7 overexpression (OE-USP7), and OE-USP7 + Bach1-IN-1. To evaluate the effect of Bach1 on USP7 expression, cells were transfected with a Bach1 overexpression plasmid (OE-Bach1), Bach1-targeting siRNA (si-Bach1), or negative control (Ctrl). The mRNA levels, protein expression, ferroptosis markers, and ROS levels were evaluated 48 h after transfection or treatment.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, HY-K0301, MCE, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, N2a cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then transfected or treated as indicated and cultured for 24, 36, and 48 h. At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well containing 100 μL of culture medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using Infinite M200 PRO (TECAN).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The mRNA levels were quantified by real-time PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system.Relative gene expression was quantified using gene-specific primers (sequences listed in Table S2), normalized to β-actin, and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Measurement of ROS and lipid peroxidation

To evaluate ROS production, 10 μm frozen spinal cord sections were incubated with 10 μM dihydroethidium (DHE) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired from randomly selected fields under a fluorescence microscope. For lipid peroxidation analysis, cells were incubated with 5 μM C11-BODIPY 581/591 for 30 min at 37 °C. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of oxidized C11-BODIPY was measured to quantify the level of cellular lipid peroxidation.

Measurement of ATP levels

Spinal cord ATP content was quantified using a bioluminescence assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, tissue supernatants were mixed with the luciferase reaction buffer, and the luminescence was recorded using a multimode plate reader. The final ATP concentrations were normalized to the total protein weight, expressed as μmol/g protein.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For animal tissue endogenous Co-IP, spinal dorsal horn tissues from SNI or control mice were homogenized and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of total protein lysates were incubated with anti-USP7 antibody or control IgG overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G beads for an additional 4 h. After extensive washing, the bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-Bach1 antibody. For exogenous Co-IP, lysates from N2a cells transfected with FLAG-Bach1 and His-USP7 were incubated with anti-FLAG or anti-His antibodies and Protein A/G beads overnight at 4 °C. After washing, bound proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Ubiquitination assay and protein stability assay

For ubiquitination assays, cells were co-transfected with FLAG-Bach1, HA-Ub, and His-USP7 (or empty vector control), treated with MG132 (10 μM, 6 h), and lysed under denaturing conditions (1% SDS). Anti-FLAG immunoprecipitates were analyzed with anti-HA antibodies to detect ubiquitinated Bach1. Cycloheximide (CHX, 100 μg/mL) chase assays were used to assess Bach1 protein stability at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h after CHX treatment.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP-qPCR was performed in N2a cells using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (9003, Cell Signaling Technology). Putative Bach1-binding motifs in the USP7 and NOX4 promoter regions were identified using the Jasper database. DNA-protein complexes from crosslinked cells were immunoprecipitated using an anti-Bach1 antibody or control IgG. Quantitative PCR was performed on purified DNA using primers flanking the predicted Bach1-binding sites in the USP7 and NOX4 promoters (sequences listed in Table S3).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase reporter assays were performed to assess transcriptional activation of USP7 and NOX4 by Bach1. Wild-type or mutant fragments of the USP7 and NOX4 promoters were cloned into pGL3-basic. N2a cells were co-transfected with the indicated reporter plasmids together with a Bach1 expression vector (or empty vector control) and a Renilla luciferase control plasmid. Firefly luciferase activity was measured 24-48 h after transfection and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0) and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For behavioral experiments involving multiple time points (PWT and PWL), statistical significance was determined using two-way repeated-measures (RM) ANOVA with group and time as independent factors. For biochemical assays and Western blot quantifications involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was employed. For all ANOVA models, Bonferroni's post-hoc test was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests.

For behavioral assessments, the sample size ranged from n = 6 to n = 9 animals per group, as specified in the respective figure legends. Specifically, a cohort of n = 9 was utilized for key foundational and rescue experiments to enhance statistical power, whereas n = 6 was employed for other behavioral and molecular/biochemical assays. In vitro mechanistic studies were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical parameters, including exact P-values, are provided in the Supplementary Tables S4–S11.

Results

SNI induces neuropathic pain and ferroptosis in the spinal cord, which is rescued by FER-1

From day 3 to day 14 following SNI surgery, mice exhibited a significant reduction in PWT and PWL, indicating the development of mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. S1A–C). Importantly, intraperitoneal administration of the canonical ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 significantly attenuated both mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in SNI mice (Fig. S2A, Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis revealed that SNI markedly increased the expression of the lipid peroxidation-associated proteins 4-HNE and ACSL4, while decreasing the ferroptosis suppressors SLC7A11 and GPX4 in the spinal cord compared with Sham controls. FER-1 treatment effectively suppressed 4-HNE and ACSL4 expression and restored SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels (Fig. 1B, D-G), indicating reversal of ferroptotic alterations. Moreover, 4-HNE was predominantly colocalized with the neuronal marker NeuN in the spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 1C). Consistently, compared with sham controls, SNI mice exhibited significantly elevated levels of Fe2+ and MDA (Fig. 1H and I), along with a decreased GSH content in spinal cord tissues (Fig. S2B). These alterations were markedly reversed by FER-1 administration, further supporting the involvement of iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images exhibited classical ferroptosis-like mitochondrial abnormalities in spinal neurons of SNI mice, including shrunken mitochondria with increased membrane electron density and a reduction or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae. Conversely, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 significantly mitigated these morphological abnormalities, preserving mitochondrial integrity. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the percentage of shrunken mitochondria was significantly elevated following SNI but was markedly restored by FER-1 administration (Fig. 1J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SNI induces ferroptosis in the spinal neurons and that pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis alleviates both molecular hallmarks of ferroptosis and pain hypersensitivity, supporting a causal role of ferroptosis in neuropathic pain.

Bach1 drives spinal ferroptosis and neuropathic pain following SNI

To determine whether Bach1 contributes to SNI-induced ferroptosis and pain hypersensitivity, we first examined its temporal expression profile in the spinal cord. Western blot analysis revealed a significant upregulation of Bach1 protein at 3, 7, and 14 days after SNI compared with Sham controls (Fig. 2A), indicating sustained upregulation of Bach1 during pain development. To delineate the cellular localization of Bach1 expression in the spinal dorsal horn, we performed double immunofluorescence staining. The results demonstrated that Bach1 was predominantly colocalized with the neuronal marker NeuN, with minimal colocalization observed with the astrocyte marker GFAP or the microglial marker Iba-1 (Fig. 2B). We next evaluated whether pharmacological inhibition of Bach1 alleviates established neuropathic pain. Mice received intragastric administration of the Bach1 inhibitor Bach1-IN-1 from day 7 to day 11 after SNI (Fig. S3A). A significant dose-dependent effect was observed, with analgesic efficacy detected beginning at 10 mg/kg (Fig. S3B). Behavioral assessments performed during this period showed that Bach1-IN-1 significantly increased the PWT and PWL compared to the SNI + Vehicle group, indicating an attenuation of mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis revealed that Bach1-IN-1 treatment effectively downregulated the expression of Bach1 and elevated NRF2 expression (Fig. S3C). Importantly, inhibition of Bach1 restored the levels of the ferroptosis-related proteins GPX4 and SLC7A11 in the spinal cord of SNI mice (Fig. 2D–G). Consistent with these molecular changes, treatment with Bach1-IN-1 significantly reduced the SNI-induced elevations in ROS, Fe2+, and MDA levels, and restored the content of GSH in the spinal cord (Fig. 2H–I, Fig. S3D–E). To further validate the role of Bach1 in this process, we performed genetic knockdown using intrathecal siRNA administration from day 7 to day 9 after SNI, and behavioral testing was conducted on day 11 (Fig. S4A). Mice treated with siBach1 exhibited significantly increased PWT and PWL compared with SNI + siNC controls (Fig. 2J). Western blot analysis confirmed efficient reduction of Bach1 without altering NRF2 protein levels (Fig. 2N, Fig. S4B). Notably, siBach1 significantly reversed the SNI-induced dysregulation of 4-HNE, ACSL4, SLC7A11, and GPX4, and attenuated ROS accumulation in the spinal cord (Fig. 2K–Q). Consistent with these changes, siBach1 treatment mitigated Fe2+ and MDA accumulation and restored GSH content (Fig. 2R–T). TEM analysis further demonstrated that siBach1 largely ameliorated the SNI-induced mitochondrial damage in ferroptosis (Fig. 2U). Collectively, these findings indicate that inhibition of Bach1 alleviates neuropathic pain and mitigates ferroptosis in the spinal cord of SNI mice.

NOX4 is required for Bach1-driven ferroptosis in the spinal cord

Ferroptosis requires not only depletion of antioxidant capacity but also strong pro-oxidant pressure to initiate lipid peroxidation. Given that NADPH oxidases (NOXs) are major cellular sources of ROS, we focused on two key isoforms—NOX4 and NOX2—that are abundantly expressed in the nervous system and associated with oxidative stress in neuropathic pain [18,38,39]. We aimed to investigate whether Bach1 specifically regulates the expression of either pro-oxidant enzyme. We found that mRNA and protein levels of NOX4 were significantly increased in the spinal cord of SNI mice compared to sham controls, and this increase was reversed by Bach1-IN-1 treatment (Fig. S5A, Fig. 3A). Moreover, NOX4 levels were markedly reduced by intrathecal administration of siBach1 (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the mRNA expression of NOX2 was elevated after SNI but was not affected by Bach1 inhibition, indicating a specific regulatory role of Bach1 on NOX4 (Fig. S5B). We next investigated whether NOX4 is required for pain hypersensitivity and ferroptosis after SNI. Dose-dependent administration of the NOX4 inhibitor GLX351322 significantly reversed SNI-induced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. S6A–C), confirming NOX4 as a key downstream mediator of neuropathic pain. Concomitantly, this treatment reduced spinal cord levels of Fe2+ and MDA, and restored the protein expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 and the GSH content, indicating a reversal of ferroptosis (Fig. S6D–G).

To further corroborate these pharmacological findings and investigate the causal link between NOX4 and ferroptosis-mediated pain, we employed a genetic approach using siRNA-mediated knockdown (Fig. S7). Furthermore, intrathecal injection of siNOX4 significantly alleviated mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia compared with SNI + siNC mice (Fig. 3C). Importantly, co-administration of the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 abolished the analgesic effects of siNOX4, restoring pain hypersensitivity, indicating that NOX4-mediated behavioral effects depend on ferroptosis signaling. At the molecular level, siNOX4 markedly reduced NOX4 protein expression and reversed the SNI-induced downregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11, while decreasing ACSL4 and 4-HNE levels (Fig. 3D–I). These protective effects were reversed by RSL3 treatment. Consistently, siNOX4 significantly reversed the SNI-induced accumulation of Fe2+ and MDA, and restored the SNI-depleted GSH content in the spinal cord, whereas RSL3 reinstated these ferroptotic alterations (Fig. 3J–L). TEM further demonstrated that siNOX4 alleviated SNI-induced ferroptotic mitochondrial morphology, characterized by condensed mitochondria with disrupted cristae, and that RSL3 restored these ultrastructural features (Fig. 3M).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NOX4 is a critical downstream effector of Bach1 and that NOX4-driven pain hypersensitivity is dependent on ferroptosis signaling in vivo.

Luciferase reporter assays in N2a cells demonstrated that Bach1 overexpression significantly enhanced NOX4 promoter activity, whereas mutation of the predicted Bach1-binding motif abolished this effect (Fig. 4B). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed the direct binding of Bach1 to this specific promoter region (Fig. 4C), suggesting Bach1 as a direct transcriptional activator of NOX4.

To investigate the functional necessity of NOX4 in Bach1-mediated effects in vivo, we performed a rescue experiment in naïve mice (Fig. S8A). Intrathecal injection of AAV-Bach1 led to robust EGFP expression in the spinal dorsal horn, confirming successful viral transduction (Fig. 4D). Mice overexpressing Bach1 (AAV-Bach1) exhibited markedly reduced PWT and PWL compared with AAV-Vector control mice, indicating the induction of pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 4E). Co-administration of the NOX4 inhibitor GLX351322 significantly attenuated this Bach1-induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity. At the molecular level, Bach1 overexpression increased NOX4 protein and decreased the ferroptosis suppressors SLC7A11 and GPX4, effects that were reversed by GLX351322 treatment (Fig. 4F–J). Consistently, GLX351322 also reversed the hallmarks of ferroptosis induced by AAV-Bach1, which include elevated levels of ROS, Fe2+, and MDA, and reduced GSH content (Fig. 4K–N). Additionally, TEM revealed that AAV-Bach1 induced mitochondrial changes characteristic of ferroptosis, which were ameliorated by GLX351322 treatment (Fig. S8B).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Bach1 directly transcriptionally activates NOX4, which in turn drives ferroptosis and contributes to pain hypersensitivity in the spinal cord.

Inhibition of USP7 reduces Bach1 expression, ferroptosis and neuropathic pain

Having established Bach1 as a key driver of NOX4-mediated ferroptosis and neuropathic pain, we next sought to identify upstream regulators that control Bach1 expression or stability in this context. Given that USP7 is a known deubiquitinase implicated in stabilizing various transcription factors, we explored whether USP7 might regulate Bach1 protein levels, thereby modulating the ferroptosis pathway and pain hypersensitivity. We first confirmed that USP7 protein expression was significantly upregulated in the spinal cord of SNI mice compared to sham controls (Fig. 5A). Double immunostaining demonstrated that USP7 was predominantly colocalized with the neuronal marker NeuN, with minimal colocalization with the microglial marker Iba1 or the astrocytic marker GFAP (Fig. 5B), suggesting that USP7 acts primarily in spinal neurons. To assess whether USP7 contributes to neuropathic pain, SNI mice with established hypersensitivity were treated intraperitoneally with the USP7 inhibitor P005091. Dose–response analysis revealed a dose-dependent effect, with significant reversal of hypersensitivity beginning at 15 mg/kg (Fig. S9A–B). Behavioral tests showed that P005091 administration significantly increased both the PWT and PWL compared with the SNI + Vehicle group, indicating a marked alleviation of mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis revealed that P005091 treatment not only downregulated USP7 but also decreased the expression of Bach1 and NOX4 in the spinal cord of SNI mice. Concurrently, P005091 treatment upregulated the expression of the ferroptosis inhibitors GPX4 and SLC7A11 (Fig. 5D–I). Biochemically, P005091 treatment restored the content of GSH and reduced the SNI-induced elevations in Fe2+ and MDA levels in spinal cord tissues (Fig. 5J–L). Ultrastructural examination by TEM showed that P005091 treatment ameliorated the mitochondrial damage in ferroptosis observed in SNI mice (Fig. 5M). Collectively, these findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of USP7 alleviates neuropathic pain, downregulates the Bach1/NOX4 axis and reduces ferroptosis in spinal neurons.

We next investigated whether USP7 directly regulates Bach1 protein stability. Double immunofluorescence staining showed that USP7 and Bach1 were highly co-localized in the spinal dorsal horn of SNI mice (Fig. 6A), suggesting a close spatial association in vivo. To confirm a physical interaction, exogenous Co-IP in N2a cells showed that FLAG-tagged Bach1 specifically co-precipitated with His-tagged USP7, and vice versa (Fig. 6B and C). Moreover, endogenous Co-IP in mouse spinal tissue demonstrated that USP7 immunoprecipitates contained detectable Bach1 protein (Fig. 6D), indicating that the two proteins form a complex under physiological conditions. Given that USP7 is a deubiquitinating enzyme, we next examined whether it regulates Bach1 ubiquitination. Denaturing ubiquitination assays showed that USP7 overexpression markedly reduced Bach1 polyubiquitination, whereas cells transfected with empty vector exhibited extensive HA-Ub–labeled Bach1 (Fig. 6E). Consistently, cycloheximide chase analysis revealed that USP7 significantly prolonged the half-life of Bach1 protein (Fig. 6F), indicating that USP7 stabilizes Bach1 by preventing its proteasomal degradation. Conversely, CHX chase analysis demonstrated that Bach1 overexpression did not significantly alter the protein stability or half-life of USP7 (Fig. S10A–B).

To determine whether USP7 is required for Bach1-driven ferroptosis in vivo, we performed genetic depletion experiments in SNI mice (Fig. S11A). Efficient viral transduction was confirmed by EGFP fluorescence in the spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 6G). Compared with SNI + AAV-Vector + siNC mice, intrathecal administration of siUSP7 significantly reduced USP7 mRNA levels (Fig. S11B). Importantly, siUSP7 markedly alleviated SNI-induced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, as evidenced by increased PWT and PWL (Fig. 6H). However, in mice overexpressing Bach1 (SNI + AAV-Bach1+siUSP7), the protective effects of USP7 depletion on pain behaviors were significantly reversed, indicating that the analgesic effect of USP7 knockdown depends on Bach1.

At the molecular level, siUSP7 reduced USP7, Bach1, and NOX4 expression and restored the ferroptosis suppressors GPX4 and SLC7A11, while decreasing ACSL4 and 4-HNE levels (Fig. 6I–Q). Notably, Bach1 overexpression reinstated these pro-ferroptotic alterations despite USP7 depletion. Biochemical analyses further showed that siUSP7 significantly decreased Fe2+ accumulation and MDA levels and restored GSH content, whereas Bach1 reintroduction largely reversed these effects (Fig. 6R–T). Moreover, spinal ATP levels, which were markedly reduced following SNI, were significantly restored by USP7 knockdown and again decreased upon Bach1 reintroduction (Fig. 6U), indicating recovery of mitochondrial function. Ultrastructural examination demonstrated that siUSP7 ameliorated ferroptosis-like mitochondrial abnormalities induced by SNI, while Bach1 overexpression restored mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae loss (Fig. 6V).

Collectively, these results indicate that USP7 directly interacts with and stabilizes Bach1 and is functionally required for Bach1-mediated NOX4 activation, ferroptosis, and pain hypersensitivity in vivo.

USP7 overexpression induces Bach1-dependent ferroptosis and pain hypersensitivity in naïve mice

To further validate the functional linkage between USP7 and Bach1 in vivo, we overexpressed USP7 in the spinal cords of naïve mice through intrathecal injection of AAV9-USP7 (Fig. S12A). Robust EGFP fluorescence in the spinal dorsal horn confirmed efficient viral transduction (Fig. 7A). Behavioral tests showed that mice injected with AAV-USP7 exhibited a significant decrease in both the PWT and PWL compared with AAV-Vector controls, indicating the development of pain hypersensitivity under physiological conditions (Fig. 7B). Notably, quantitative PCR analysis revealed that USP7 overexpression did not alter Bach1 mRNA expression levels (Fig. 7C). In contrast, western blot analysis confirmed successful USP7 overexpression and, importantly, a concomitant elevation in Bach1 protein levels (Fig. 7D–F). To test the functional dependence on Bach1, mice were treated intragastrically with the Bach1 inhibitor Bach1-IN-1 for five consecutive days following AAV-mediated gene expression (Fig. S12B). Bach1-IN-1 treatment significantly reversed the reductions in PWT and PWL induced by USP7 overexpression (Fig. 7G and H). At the molecular level, Bach1 inhibition restored the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, reversing the increased NOX4 and the decreased levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 caused by AAV-USP7 (Fig. 7I–L). Moreover, AAV-USP7 elevated Fe2+ and MDA levels while reducing GSH content in the spinal cord, and all these ferroptosis markers were rescued by Bach1-IN-1 treatment (Fig. 7M–O). Finally, TEM showed that USP7 overexpression induced mitochondrial damage in ferroptosis, which was ameliorated by Bach1-IN-1 (Fig. S12C). Together, these findings show that USP7 overexpression induces pain hypersensitivity and ferroptosis, effects that are mediated by the Bach1/NOX4 signaling axis.

Previous studies have shown that Bach1 can transcriptionally activate USP7 in other cellular contexts [17]. Given our findings that USP7 stabilizes Bach1 to promote ferroptosis, we next explored whether Bach1 reciprocally regulates USP7 expression to form a feedback loop. RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis showed that OE-Bach1 significantly upregulated, while si-Bach1 downregulated, both USP7 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8L–O). Bioinformatic analysis using JASPAR revealed two putative Bach1-binding sites (MAREs) within the USP7 promoter, located at −1756 bp (Site 1) and −1159 bp (Site 2) relative to the transcription start site (Fig. 8P). Sequence alignment indicated that Site 1 (5′-AGGCTGAGTCAGGAG-3′) closely resembles the consensus Bach1-binding motif (Fig. 8Q). Furthermore, cross-species sequence alignment confirmed that these MARE motifs, particularly Site 1, exhibit high evolutionary conservation across various vertebrates (Fig. S13), suggesting their functional importance in regulating USP7 transcription. Therefore, we prioritized Site 1 for site-directed mutagenesis. To assess the functional relevance of this interaction, luciferase reporter assays were performed using a precisely designed mutational strategy (Fig. 8S). Overexpression of Bach1 significantly transactivated the wild-type USP7 promoter, whereas mutation of the core binding sequences completely abolished this induction (Fig. 8T). Consistently, ChIP-qPCR analysis confirmed the direct binding of Bach1 to this promoter region in vivo (Fig. 8R).

Collectively, these results suggest that USP7 promotes ferroptosis in neuronal cells in a Bach1-dependent manner. Conversely, Bach1 transcriptionally activates USP7 expression, thereby forming a reciprocal activation loop that may promote Bach1 signaling and ferroptosis in the spinal cord under neuropathic pain conditions.

Discussion

Neuropathic pain remains a major clinical challenge due to its multifactorial pathogenesis and limited therapeutic options. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized USP7–Bach1–NOX4 signaling axis as a key driver of spinal ferroptosis and pain hypersensitivity. Our findings demonstrate that the transcription factor Bach1 acts as a central executor of ferroptosis signaling by directly activating NOX4, thereby triggering excessive ROS generation and lipid peroxidation. The deubiquitinase USP7 stabilizes Bach1 through deubiquitination, ensuring sustained Bach1 activity and downstream oxidative stress. Strikingly, Bach1 reciprocally enhances USP7 transcription, establishing a feedforward amplification that perpetuates its own signaling and ferroptosis drive. Disruption of this circuit at multiple levels—by inhibition of USP7, Bach1, or NOX4—effectively alleviated ferroptosis and neuropathic pain in vivo. Together, these results uncover a novel redox regulatory feedback loop that integrates transcriptional activation and post-translational stabilization, providing new mechanistic insight into the persistence of neuropathic pain and identifying actionable therapeutic targets within the USP7–Bach1–NOX4 axis.

Building upon this framework, our results first established that Bach1 is upregulated in spinal neurons following peripheral nerve injury and plays a critical role in promoting neuropathic pain and ferroptosis. Mechanistically, Bach1 acts as a key initiator of the ferroptosis cascade by directly transactivating the NOX4 gene, thereby establishing a sustained pro-oxidant state. The resulting NOX4-derived ROS overproduction triggers a ferroptosis execution program, which includes the depletion of core defensive proteins (GPX4 and SLC7A11), iron overload, glutathione exhaustion, and excessive lipid peroxidation. This direct transcriptional control of NOX4 by Bach1 is further supported by independent evidence in ischemic stroke, in which pharmacological inhibition of Bach1 by p-coumaric acid attenuates neuronal damage via suppression of NOX4 expression and ROS production [16]. These parallel findings highlight the Bach1–NOX4 axis as a conserved transcriptional mechanism driving redox imbalance across neurological disorders. In our model, inhibition of NOX4 significantly attenuated the pain and ferroptosis phenotypes induced by Bach1 overexpression, confirming NOX4 as an essential downstream executor of Bach1 in spinal neurons.

Although Bach1 is canonically known as a transcriptional repressor, its regulatory polarity is remarkably plastic and context-dependent. Beyond its classical competition with NRF2, Bach1 possesses the inherent biochemical capacity to recruit activation complexes, such as the MLL/SET1 methyltransferase complex, to establish an active chromatin landscape [40]. Besides, in an ischemic stroke model, Bach1 similarly promotes NOX4 transcription via direct promoter binding [16]. These findings demonstrate that Bach1 possesses intrinsic transcriptional activator potential.

An important consideration concerns the selective engagement of NOX4 rather than other NOX family members. Unlike NOX2, which is stimulus-dependent and typically associated with acute inflammatory ROS bursts, NOX4 is constitutively active and generates sustained ROS production. Such persistent oxidative output is well suited to drive chronic lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic vulnerability. Consistently, although NOX2 expression increased after SNI, it was not regulated by Bach1 inhibition, whereas NOX4 was both robustly upregulated and directly transcriptionally controlled by Bach1. These findings indicate that Bach1 selectively programs a NOX4-dependent oxidative state to sustain ferroptosis in neuropathic pain. This aligns with the latest evidence in SNI models showing that the PKCα/NOX4 signaling pathway triggers mitochondria-associated ferroptosis, specifically leading to the exhaustion of GABAergic interneurons [9]. Our study complements this by identifying the upstream transcriptional regulator, Bach1, that sustains this NOX4-driven pro-ferroptotic environment.

Notably, Bach1-IN-1 (HPPE), the pharmacological inhibitor used in this study, has been reported to activate NRF2 signaling in addition to inhibiting Bach1 [41]. Consistently, NRF2 protein levels increased following Bach1-IN-1 treatment. However, genetic knockdown of Bach1 did not alter NRF2 expression yet still alleviated pain hypersensitivity and ferroptosis markers. These findings suggest that suppression of the Bach1–NOX4 pro-oxidant axis is sufficient to mitigate ferroptosis and neuropathic pain, although NRF2 activation may provide additional antioxidant support.

Having identified Bach1 as a core executor, we sought to understand the mechanism underlying its pathological accumulation. USP7 emerged as a critical upstream regulator. We demonstrate that USP7 binds to, deubiquitinates, and stabilizes Bach1 protein. This post-translational stabilization is crucial, ensuring that Bach1 transcriptional activity is sustained. Although other deubiquitinases have been reported to regulate Bach1 in cancer [30,31], our work identifies USP7 as a novel neuronal stabilizer of Bach1 in chronic pain. Together with emerging evidence that USP7 inhibition attenuates oxidative stress and ferroptotic injury in neurodegenerative and acute CNS injury models [[27], [28], [29]], our findings extend the functional scope of USP7 by identifying a defined transcriptional-post-translational feedback loop that sustains ferroptosis in chronic neuropathic pain. This mechanistic clarification strengthens the rationale for targeting USP7 in redox-driven neurological disorders. Importantly, we further reveal that Bach1 transcriptionally upregulates USP7, forming a reciprocal activation. This self-reinforcing circuit provides a plausible molecular explanation for pain chronification, whereby sustained redox imbalance and ferroptotic sensitivity are maintained even after the initial injury stimulus subsides.

From a translational perspective, targeting the USP7-Bach1 axis effectively reverses established neuropathic pain, indicating its contribution to the maintenance of chronic hypersensitivity. Mechanistically, this approach differs from conventional analgesics by acting at a redox-regulatory hub that drives neuronal ferroptosis and persistent oxidative stress. Traditional analgesic strategies primarily target neuronal excitability or inflammatory signaling, such as voltage-gated sodium channels, calcium channels, or pro-inflammatory cytokines. This mechanistic distinction suggests that targeting the USP7–Bach1–NOX4 axis may complement existing therapies while reducing reliance on opioids or NSAIDs. Although definitive CNS pharmacokinetic data and direct evidence of blood–brain barrier penetration are currently lacking, systemic P005091 administration in our model reversed both pain hypersensitivity and spinal ferroptosis markers, consistent with previous reports showing modulation of epigenetic changes in the paraventricular thalamus [42]. Future efforts should prioritize CNS-targeted pharmacokinetic evaluation and optimization of USP7 inhibitors to facilitate clinical translation and potential repurposing for refractory neuropathic pain.

Despite these insights, several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, only male mice were used in this study. Given the documented sex differences in pain processing and redox signaling, future work should determine whether the USP7–Bach1–NOX4 axis operates similarly in females, potentially by including both sexes in subsequent experimental cohorts and analyzing data with sex as a biological variable. Second, although our behavioral and molecular data strongly support a causal role of this axis, direct genetic evidence would further strengthen the causal conclusions. Future studies employing conditional, inducible knockout mouse models (e.g., neuronal-specific Bach1 or USP7 knockouts) subjected to SNI would provide definitive genetic validation of this pathway's necessity. Third, although we focused on NOX4 and USP7 based on prior mechanistic rationale, future research utilizing unbiased transcriptomic approaches could identify additional Bach1 target genes, thereby expanding our understanding of its broader regulatory network in neuropathic pain. Lastly, to better define the cell-type specificity of this axis, future investigations could extend our mechanistic findings from N2a neuronal cells to more physiologically relevant models, such as primary spinal neuronal cultures or iPSC-derived human neurons, allowing for validation of the feedback loop and testing of therapeutics in a human context.

Conclusion

This study identifies the USP7–Bach1–NOX4 signaling axis as a central driver of ferroptosis and neuropathic pain, offering a novel mechanistic insight into redox regulation in chronic pain. These findings provide new therapeutic targets for disease-modifying treatments, highlighting a promising path forward in the development of more effective, mechanism-based interventions for neuropathic pain.
