AT1R autoantibody impedes fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis by inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling pathway during late gestation
Authors: Jingwei Bian, Wang Wang, Pengli Wang, Lina Bai, Chunyu He, Mingming Yue, Xiang Cheng, Huirong Liu, Suli Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFetal hepatic glycogen is essential for intrauterine development and postnatal metabolic adaptation. This study aimed to explore the impact of intrauterine exposure to angiotensin II type 1 receptor autoantibodies (AT1-AA) on fetal hepatic glycogen homeostasis and underlying mechanisms.MethodsAT1-AA-positive pregnant rat models were established by intravenous administration of AT1-AA on gestational days 13 and 15, with angiotensin II- and L-NAME-treated groups serving as controls. Serum levels of AT1-AA in pregnant rats were quantified using ELISA. Fetal hepatic glycogen content was evaluated through PAS staining and an anthrone-sulfuric acid assay kit. On gestational day 18, fetal livers were harvested for RNA sequencing. Alterations in signaling molecules in hepatocytes were analyzed by Western blot.ResultsUnlike the angiotensin II- and L-NAME-treated groups, which similarly induced placental ischemia and growth restriction, intrauterine exposure of AT1-AA uniquely reduced fetal hepatic glycogen content at gestational day 18. This reduction was attributed to impaired glycogen synthesis, as characterized by decreased GYS2 expression and glycogen synthase activity. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that suppression of the PI3K/AKT pathway was the predominant mechanism involved. Both in vivo and in vitro studies confirmed that AT1-AA-mediated PI3K/AKT inhibition occurs through AT1R activation. Either stimulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway or blockade of AT1R significantly restored glycogen synthesis.ConclusionsOur study identifies AT1-AA as a unique maternal factor that selectively impairs fetal hepatic glycogen storage via AT1R-mediated suppression of PI3K/AKT pathway.

Background

Fetal hepatic glycogen is essential for supporting intrauterine development, maintaining normal organ function, and enabling postnatal metabolic adaptation [1, 2]. Inadequate glycogen content is associated with adverse outcomes, including neonatal hypoglycemia, neurodevelopmental impairments, and an increased risk of adult-onset diseases such as glucose intolerance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying early risk factors that impair hepatic glycogen and elucidating the molecular mechanisms are critical for developing strategies for early detection and intervention strategies.

Maternal factors including nutrition, stress, and exposure to harmful stimuli during pregnancy can significantly impair fetal hepatic glycogen content [5–7]. Preeclampsia (PE), a pregnancy-related disorder linked to placental dysfunction, induces various intrauterine stressors such as maternal hypertension, placental vasoconstriction, and the release of pathogenic factors [8, 9]. Among these stressors, angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) autoantibody (AT1-AA), an IgG autoantibody detected in up to 50% of PE patients [10], plays a critical role by inducing placental dysfunction through vasoconstriction, cellular apoptosis, and impaired perfusion [11]. AT1-AA is an agonistic autoantibody that acts by specifically recognizing the second extracellular loop of AT1R (AT1R-ECII) [12, 13]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that AT1-AA can cross the placental barrier, leading to intrauterine growth restriction and an increased risk of adult-onset metabolic diseases in offspring [14, 15]. Notably, AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats exhibited fetal hypoglycemia during the third trimester [16], suggesting a potential disruption of glycogen metabolism. However, the direct impact of AT1-AA on fetal hepatic glycogen content and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Hepatic glycogen content is determined by the dynamic balance between glycogen synthesis and decomposition. Glycogen synthesis is primarily catalyzed by glycogen synthase (GS), which converts glucose into glycogen. GS has two main isoforms: GYS1 (muscle type) and GYS2 (liver type). Conversely, glycogen decomposition is mainly driven by glycogen phosphorylase (GP), which breaks down glycogen into glucose-1-phosphate. GP is represented by three major isoforms: PYGM (muscle type), PYGL (liver type), and PYGB (brain type) [17, 18]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether AT1-AA directly disrupts the balance between glycogen synthesis and decomposition, thereby affecting glycogen content.

In this study, we discovered that AT1-AA specifically impairs glycogen synthesis, rather than acting through the increased general IgG levels or vasoconstrictive effect, leading to reduced hepatic glycogen content in the fetuses of AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats during late gestation. Mechanistically, AT1-AA inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through activation of AT1R. Blocking AT1R or activating the PI3K/AKT pathway reversed AT1-AA-induced suppression of glycogen synthesis. Collectively, our results reveal that AT1-AA, as an independent maternal risk factor, impaired fetal hepatic glycogen content during embryonic development, providing novel intervention targets for maintaining fetal glycogen homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of AT1-AA

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (approval number: AEEI-2015-050). The AT1-AA-negative male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were provided by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (license no. SCXK[Beijing] 2012-0001), and actively immunized with the AT1R-ECII peptide for 8 weeks. At the end of the immunization period, sera from immunized rats were collected, and AT1-AA was purified using the Hi Trap™ Protein G HP Kit (GE Healthcare, 17-0404-01). Quantification of AT1-AA was performed with the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific™, 23225). The purity of AT1-AA was verified by SDS-PAGE, and its biological activity was determined using a vascular ring assay. Meanwhile, AT1-AA-negative IgG was purified from the sera of healthy male SD rats.

The construction of the animal model

A total of 30 pregnant rats were included in this study and randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 per group): the Saline group, AT1-AA-positive IgG (AT1-AA) group, AT1-AA-negative IgG (IgG(-)) group, Ang II group and Nω-Nitro-L-arginine methyl ester hydrochloride (L-NAME) group. The AT1-AA-positive pregnant rat model was established as described in previous publication [16, 19]. Simultaneously, the Saline group received intravenous injection of saline (0.5 mL/rat) on gestational days 13 and 15; the IgG(-) group received intravenous injection with AT1-AA-negative IgG (20 µg/g); the Ang II group was infused via an osmotic minipump (1007D, Alzet) with Ang II (50 ng/kg/min, A9525, Sigma-Aldrich); and the L-NAME group received daily subcutaneous injection of L-NAME (50 mg/kg/d, N5751, Sigma-Aldrich). Blood pressure and serum AT1-AA levels in pregnant rats were dynamically monitored on gestational days 12, 14, 16 and 18. Fetal weight and fetal liver weight were measured on gestational day 18.

Detection of AT1-AA level in sera from pregnant rats by ELISA

Due to the high sequence homology between rat AT1R-ECII (165-191aa: I-H-R-N-V-Y-F-I-E-N-T-N-I-T-V-C-A-F–H-Y-E-S-R-N-S-T-L) and human AT1R-ECII(165-191aa: I-H-R-N-V-F-F-I-E-N-T-N-I-T-V-C-A-F-H-Y-E-S-Q-N-S-T-L), a peptide corresponding to human AT1R-ECII was synthesized by GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. Serum AT1-AA levels in pregnant rats from all five groups were detected by ELISA according to an established protocol. The P/N ratio was calculated as follows: (sample OD—blank OD)/(negative control OD—blank OD). Based on predefined thresholds, samples were classified as AT1-AA-positive (P/N ≥ 2.1) or negative (P/N ≤ 1.5).

PAS staining

Fetal livers from all five groups were collected to prepare paraffin sections. Oxidant solution was applied to the tissue and incubated at room temperature for 8 min. Then the sections were incubated with Schiff reagent in the dark for 20 min. After washing with tap water for 10 min, hematoxylin staining and hydrochloric acid differentiation were performed. Sections were then dehydrated through a gradient alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with glycerol.

Detection of fetal hepatic glycogen

Glycogen content was measured using a commercial kit according to the following procedure: approximately 0.1 g of fetal liver tissue was mixed with 0.375 mL of extraction buffer, boiled for 20 min with vortexing every 5 min, cooled, and diluted to 2.5 mL with distilled water. Blank wells (60 µL distilled water + 240 µL reagent II), standard wells (60 µL reagent I + 240 µL reagent II), and sample wells (60 µL glycogen extract + 240 µL reagent II) were prepared, mixed, boiled for 10 min, cooled, and 200 µL of each mixture was transferred to a microplate. Absorbance (A1, A2, A3) was measured at 620 nm. Glycogen content was calculated using the formula: Glycogen (mg/mg prot) = 0.111 × (A3 – A1) / (A2 – A1) / Cpr, where Cpr represents the sample protein concentration.

RNA sequencing

On the 18th day of the pregnancy, rats in Saline and AT1-AA groups were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and euthanized. Fetal livers were collected for gene detection using the BGISEQ platform. After screening for differential genes, signaling pathway enrichment was performed via KEGG PATHWAY.

Cell culture

The human hepatoma cell line (HepG2) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in MEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% nonessential amino acids. Cells were stimulated with AT1-AA (1 × 10–8 mol/L), IgG (1 × 10–8 mol/L), L-NAME (1 × 10–8 mol/L), Ang II (1 × 10–8 mol/L), PBS, 740 Y-P (6 × 10–6 mol/L, HY-P0175, MCE), Telmisartan (1 × 10–7 mol/L, T8949, Sigma-Aldrich), and Valsartan (1 × 10–7 mol/L, SML0142, Sigma-Aldrich). Small interfering RNAs targeting human AT1R (si-AT1R) were designed and synthesized by RiboBio (China). Transfection was performed using RiboFECT™ CP Reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from fetal livers across all five groups and HepG2 cell lines and quantified by Western blot. The following antibodies were used: Anti-GYS2 (1:2000, 22371-1-AP, Proteintech), Anti-PYGL (1:500, 15851-1-AP, Proteintech), Anti-AT1R (1:1000, 124734, Abcam), Anti-PI3K (1:500, 67071-1-AP, Proteintech), Anti-p-AKT (1:500, bs-0876, Bioss), Anti-AKT (1:1000, 9272, CST), anti-β-actin (1:1000, Immunoway, YT0099).

Statistics

Investigators were blinded to group allocation and rats/samples were randomized into different groups with similar sample sizes. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The unpaired independent Student’s t test was utilized to compare two groups with normal distribution. One-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA were used to compare multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The fetal liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly lower in the AT1-AA group on gestational day 18 (Fig. 1D). Importantly, to further elucidate the specific effect of AT1-AA on fetal hepatic glycogen, we concurrently established an AT1-AA-negative IgG (IgG(-)) group by injecting IgG from healthy rats, along with L-NAME and Ang II groups. Since AT1-AA is an IgG-class autoantibody, the IgG(-) group served as a negative control to rule out nonspecific effects due to elevated IgG levels [20]. The results showed that IgG(-) did not increase blood pressure or AT1-AA levels and only caused a slight—though not statistically significant—reduction in fetal hepatic glycogen. L-NAME, a vasoconstrictor commonly used to establish preeclampsia models [21, 22], was used to exclude any glycogen reduction resulting from vasoconstriction. Results indicated that L-NAME significantly increased blood pressure but did not elevate AT1-AA levels in pregnant rats or decrease hepatic glycogen of fetuses. We also compared our findings with those from the endogenous AT1R agonist Ang II. Ang II markedly raised maternal blood pressure and impaired fetal development; however, in contrast to AT1-AA, it increased fetal hepatic glycogen content. Together, these results further support the specificity of AT1-AA in reducing fetal hepatic glycogen.

AT1-AA reduced fetal hepatic glycogen content by inhibiting glycogen synthesis rather than promoting its decomposition

A decrease in hepatic glycogen content may result from either reduced glycogen synthesis or increased glycogen decomposition [18, 23]. We therefore evaluated the effect of AT1-AA on both processes. Given that glycogen synthase GYS2 is the key enzyme responsible for hepatic glycogen synthesis, and glycogen phosphorylase PYGL mediates its breakdown, we examined the expression of GYS2 and PYGL in fetal livers on gestational day 18. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the Saline group, GYS2 expression was significantly decreased in the AT1-AA group and IgG(-) group but markedly increased in the Ang II group. In contrast, PYGL expression showed no obvious differences among most groups, although an increase was observed in the L-NAME group compared with the Saline group (Fig. 2A–C). We further assessed the activity of glycogen synthase (GS) and glycogen phosphorylase (GP) in fetal livers. Similarly with the findings regarding GYS2 and PYGL expression, compared to Saline group, there was a significant decrease in GS activity in AT1-AA group and L-NAME group, while it was markedly increased in the Ang II group. The activity of glycogen phosphorylase exhibited no obvious changes among the different groups (Fig. 2D, E). The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was downregulated in the fetal liver of AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats during late gestation

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying AT1-AA-induced reduction of fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis to identify differentially expressed genes in the fetal liver between the Saline group and AT1-AA-positive group (Fig. 3A). On gestational day 18, RNA-seq analysis identified 145 significantly up-regulated and 475 down-regulated genes in the AT1-AA group (Fig. 3B). KEGG enrichment analysis of the down-regulated gene revealed significant involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 3C), which is known to play a central role in regulating hepatic glycogen metabolism through activation of glycogen synthase [24, 25]. To test this hypothesis, we examined changes in PI3K/AKT signaling in the fetal liver samples (Fig. 3D). Western blot analysis showed that PI3K/AKT signaling was significantly inhibited in the AT1-AA group compared with the Saline group, as indicated by reduced PI3K expression and decreased AKT phosphorylation. In contrast, the Ang II group showed enhanced PI3K/AKT signaling (Fig. 3E–G). These findings demonstrate that during late gestation, downregulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway occurs specifically in the fetal livers of AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats and not in those exposed to Ang II, supporting the specificity of AT1-AA-induced metabolic disruption.

AT1-AA inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling and glycogen synthesis in HepG2 cells

AT1-AA can be transferred from mother to fetuses across the placenta [15, 26]. To investigate whether AT1-AA directly downregulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and impairs glycogen synthesis, we treated HepG2 cells with AT1-AA (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the animal model results, AT1-AA stimulation significantly reduced PI3K expression and AKT phosphorylation. In contrast, Ang II markedly increased both PI3K expression and AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 4B–D). Regarding glycogen synthesis, unlike the promotive effect of Ang II, AT1-AA significantly inhibited the expression of glycogen synthase GYS2, glycogen synthase activity, and cellular glycogen content (Fig. 4F–H). Glycogen synthesis was inhibited by AT1-AA through suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

The results showed that pre-incubation with 740 Y-P significantly abolished the inhibitory effect of AT1-AA on the PI3K expression and AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 5B–D). Moreover, 740 Y-P pretreatment rescued the decreases in GYS2 expression, glycogen synthase activity, and cellular glycogen content (Fig. 5E–G). To test this, we used two clinically relevant AT1R blockers, Telmisartan and Valsartan, to inhibit AT1R activation (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis showed that both Telmisartan and Valsartan effectively reversed the suppressive effects of AT1-AA on PI3K/AKT signaling (Fig. 6B–D), glycogen synthase GYS2 expression (Fig. 6E), and glycogen synthase activity (Fig. 6F). Ultimately, both compounds restored the decreased cellular glycogen content induced by AT1-AA (Fig. 6G). Discussion

Fetal hepatic glycogen content plays a crucial role in both intrauterine development and adult-onset health outcomes. Here, we have identified an early maternal risk factor for impairing fetal hepatic glycogen content—AT1-AA. We found that fetuses of AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats exhibited reduced hepatic glycogen content during late gestation, primarily due to impaired glycogen synthesis. Mechanistically, AT1-AA specifically inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through activation of AT1R, thereby disrupting fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis and ultimately leading to decreased hepatic glycogen content (Fig. 8).Fig. 8AT1-AA impeded fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis by inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling pathway during late gestation. A large body of evidence has demonstrated that adverse maternal conditions during pregnancy, such as gestational obesity, malnutrition, and exposure to pharmacological agents, can disrupt hepatic glycogen metabolism in offspring during early life [3, 5–7, 27]. Our previous study revealed that offspring of AT1-AA-positive pregnant rats are prone to develop metabolic syndrome by week 48, suggesting that AT1-AA acts as a significant maternal risk factor [15]. These findings prompted us to investigate the early developmental window during which AT1-AA induces alterations in hepatic glycogen content. AT1-AA is present in pregnant women with preeclampsia during the second trimester, a period when the fetal liver has already formed and glucose metabolism begins to develop, gradually optimizing until the third trimester [8, 28]. Consequently, the third trimester represents a critical window for metabolic development. In this study, we found that fetal rats from AT1-AA-positive pregnancies exhibited decreased hepatic glycogen content during the third trimester. These results not only clarify the impact of AT1-AA on fetal liver metabolism during the embryonic stage but also further underscore its role as a significant adverse stimulus during pregnancy, with profound implications for intrauterine development and adult-onset health outcomes.

AT1-AA is an IgG-class autoantibody capable of crossing the placental barrier [15]. To delineate the specific effect of AT1-AA on fetal liver glycogen, we concurrently established control groups including a general IgG group, an Ang II group, and an L-NAME group to rule out potential confounding effects mediated by non-specific immunoglobulins, placental vasoconstriction, or inadequate placental perfusion. Recent studies have shown that elevated IgG levels promote inflammation and accelerate aging [29]. Additionally, IgG accumulation in adipose tissue has been linked to insulin resistance and macrophage infiltration [30]. Since maternal IgG is capable of crossing the placenta, it may act as a maternal risk factor that influences fetal intrauterine development [20]. In our study, we found that the increase of maternal IgG slightly reduces fetal liver weight, hepatic glycogen content, and the expression of GYS2 and PI3K in vivo. However, the impacts of AT1-AA-negative IgG were milder than those observed with AT1-AA, indicating a distinct and more potent mechanism of action for AT1-AA. Notably, these IgG-associated effects were not observed in HepG2 cells, suggesting that maternal IgG may not act directly on hepatocytes. Instead, it may exert its influence through indirect pathways, such as affecting adipose tissue or promoting inflammation [29, 30]. These results suggest that maternal IgG accumulation also represents an early risk factor for impaired fetal hepatic glycogen storage, although the precise mechanism requires further investigation.

In contrast to general IgG, AT1-AA exhibits unique pathogenic properties. AT1-AA exerts an agonist-like effect by binding to the AT1R, leading to vasoconstriction and inadequate placental perfusion [12, 31]. These effects align with key pathological features of preeclampsia, as demonstrated by studies showing that uterine vascular ligation or the administration of vasoconstrictors can replicate preeclamptic symptoms, such as hypertension, proteinuria, and fetal growth restriction [32]. To determine whether the AT1-AA-induced reduction in fetal hepatic glycogen was merely a consequence of vasoconstriction and impaired placental perfusion, we included L-NAME and Ang II treatment groups as additional controls. L-NAME is a well-known vasoconstrictor (nitric oxide synthase inhibitor) used to establish preeclampsia models [21]. Our study showed that L-NAME significantly elevated maternal blood pressure and reduced fetal liver weight; however, it did not alter AT1-AA levels or decrease hepatic glycogen content in fetuses. Similarly, Ang II markedly raised blood pressure and impeded fetal development, but it also failed to reduce fetal hepatic glycogen. Collectively, these results suggest that the reduction in fetal hepatic glycogen induced by AT1-AA is not simply due to vasoconstriction or inadequate placental perfusion. Instead, AT1-AA likely impairs fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis through a distinct, receptor-mediated signaling mechanism, underscoring its unique pathological role compared to that of general IgG and other vasoconstrictor agents.

To clarify the receptor pathway through which AT1-AA mediates its effects, we utilized clinically applicable AT1 receptor blockers (ARBs) for reversal experiments. To minimize potential bias arising from any single pharmacological agent, two distinct ARBs—Telmisartan and Valsartan—were selected. Due to the known teratogenic effects associated with ARBs, all experiments were conducted in vitro. Both Telmisartan and Valsartan significantly reversed the AT1-AA-induced reduction in glycogen content in HepG2 cells and the suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. To exclude the possibility of off-target effects of ARBs unrelated to AT1R, we employed siRNA interference to directly knockdown AT1R expression. The results showed that siRNA-AT1R similarly reversed the inhibitory effect of AT1-AA on glycogen synthesis in hepatocytes, indicating that the impairment of hepatic glycogen synthesis by AT1-AA is primarily mediated through AT1R. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether other receptor or non-receptor pathways are involved. Furthermore, among the differentially expressed signaling pathways identified via RNA-seq, in addition to PI3K/AKT, other pathways related to glycogen metabolism—such as AMPK and MAPK—were also detected. Their potential roles under AT1-AA exposure require further investigation.

Notably, although both AT1-AA and Ang II function as agonistic ligands of the AT1R, they elicit distinct downstream signaling responses and pathological effects upon receptor activation. In our study, AT1-AA inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, whereas Ang II promoted its activation. Functionally, AT1-AA impaired fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis, leading to reduced hepatic glycogen content, while Ang II had the opposite effect. Other studies have underscored the differential actions of AT1-AA and Ang II. For example, AT1-AA induces a sustained increase in intracellular calcium levels, whereas Ang II only triggers a transient increase [31, 33]. Similarly, AT1-AA prolongs vasoconstrictive responses, while Ang II causes transient vasoconstriction [31]. These functional differences likely stem from their distinct binding sites on AT1R. Ang II interacts with charged amino acid residues in the second and third extracellular loops of AT1R [34], whereas AT1-AA binds to a unique epitope comprising 26 amino acid residues within the second extracellular loop [35]. Additionally, studies using gene-deficient preeclampsia rat models have revealed that AT1-AA selectively targets a six-amino acid fragment within the second extracellular loop of AT1R [36]. These ligand-specific binding properties likely induce distinct conformational changes in AT1R, leading to divergent downstream signaling pathways and pathological outcomes. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the differential effects of AT1-AA and Ang II on AT1R signal transduction remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further investigation.

This study has several limitations that are worth considering and exploring further. First, although clear effects of AT1-AA on hepatic glycogen were observed in both animal and cellular models, clinical data are currently lacking to confirm how hepatic glucose metabolism is altered in the offspring of AT1-AA-positive preeclamptic patients, and what the subsequent health outcomes might be. Second, according to the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) theory [37], early-life metabolic reprogramming can exert long-term health influences. Whether the reduction in fetal liver glycogen resulting from prenatal AT1-AA exposure increases the offspring’s susceptibility to metabolic disorders in adulthood requires long-term dynamic observation. Furthermore, it would be valuable to investigate whether the timing of AT1-AA exposure leads to differential effects on offspring. Third, although indicators such as maternal blood pressure, fetal weight, and liver weight were used as indirect proxies for placental perfusion, future studies would benefit from direct monitoring of placental blood flow and oxygenation in pregnant rats to better control for confounding variables. Fourth, although living conditions and environmental stressors were standardized across experimental groups to ensure comparability, it remains unclear whether AT1-AA-induced changes in the maternal internal environment, such as inflammatory responses, contribute to alterations in offspring hepatic glycogen. Fifth, in vivo reversal experiments were not performed in this study due to the ongoing challenge of clearing AT1-AA during pregnancy. Currently available strategies, including ARBs, dominant epitope-mimicking peptides targeting the AT1 receptor’s second extracellular loop [38], the aptamer BC007 [39], and immunoadsorption techniques [40], have not yet been translated into clinical use for preeclampsia, highlighting an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

In summary, this study revealed the specific effects of AT1-AA on hepatic glycogen metabolism in fetal rats and uncovered the mechanism by which AT1-AA impairs fetal hepatic glycogen synthesis through inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These findings identify AT1-AA-induced metabolic reprogramming as a novel developmental origin of adult metabolic disorders. Monitoring maternal serum AT1-AA levels could allow early identification of high-risk pregnancies, enabling personalized nutritional and metabolic interventions. Furthermore, blocking the interaction between AT1-AA and AT1R or activating the PI3K/AKT pathway may represent promising strategies for managing preeclampsia and improving fetal metabolic outcomes.
