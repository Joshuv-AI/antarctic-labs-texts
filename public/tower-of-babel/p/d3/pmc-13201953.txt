Use of patient-specific 3D reconstruction models for en bloc resection of primary thoracic vertebral sarcomas without formal spondylectomy: illustrative cases
Authors: Vivek Sanker, Aaditya Dravid, Rebekka Miller, Chelsea Park, Maria Jose Cavagnaro, Ikchan Jeon, Corinna Zygourakis, Atman Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEn bloc resection remains the treatment of choice for primary vertebral column sarcomas but often necessitates total spondylectomy, a procedure associated with high morbidity. The use of patient-specific 3D reconstruction models in combination with intraoperative neuronavigation provides a strategy to achieve oncological resection goals while minimizing resection of uninvolved bone and reducing complication rates.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present 2 cases of thoracic vertebral sarcomas successfully resected en bloc using patient-specific 3D models and intraoperative neuronavigation without performing formal spondylectomy. Postoperative imaging confirmed gross-total resection, and both patients showed durable symptom relief with no evidence of recurrence on follow-up.LESSONSIntegrating 3D modeling and neuronavigation enables tailored en bloc resections of thoracic vertebral sarcomas. This approach preserves structural stability and avoids spondylectomy in select cases, supporting improved oncological and functional outcomes. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25672 Keywords: 3D reconstruction, neuronavigation, chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, thoracic vertebral tumors, en bloc resection, case reportsABBREVIATIONS: AR = augmented reality, SRS = stereotactic radiosurgery

The most common primary malignancies of the vertebral column include osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma.1 Chondrosarcomas of the spine, however, are rare, representing 7%–15% of all primary bony spinal tumors and up to 25% of primary malignant spinal tumors.2 The thoracic spine is the most frequently involved site, followed by the cervical and lumbar regions. Patients typically present with localized pain and, less commonly, a palpable mass.3 Osteosarcomas of the spine are even less common, accounting for only 3%–5% of all osteosarcomas, and most often arise in the sacrum or lumbar spine.

These tumors present significant challenges due to their resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy.3,4 En bloc resection is considered as the treatment of choice but is associated with high risk owing to the tumor’s proximity to vital structures, including nerve roots, spinal cord, and major blood vessels.

En bloc resection with negative margins offers the best chance for durable local control, but conventional techniques often require total spondylectomy, which increases surgical complexity and complication risk. The advent of high-resolution imaging and advanced surgical planning platforms allows for individualized operative strategies. Three-dimensional reconstruction models derived from CT and MRI can provide precise visualization of tumor boundaries and adjacent neurovascular structures. When integrated with intraoperative neuronavigation, these tools enhance surgical accuracy, allowing preservation of healthy bone and avoidance of formal spondylectomy while achieving oncological goals.5,6

The purpose of this article is to demonstrate how 3D reconstruction of the patient’s anatomy can be used to enhance patient outcomes and lower recurrence rates. This enables the surgeons to navigate the patient’s image-based anatomy during the procedure, which eventually improves accuracy in locating and excising the tumor with less damage to nearby structures. We present 2 cases of primary thoracic vertebral sarcomas resected en bloc using patient-specific 3D modeling and neuronavigation to guide osteotomies and soft tissue dissection.

Planning Workflow

Preoperative CT and MRI were imported into the Surgical Theater SyncAR Spine software. CT provided bony architecture, while MRI delineated soft tissue and tumor margins. Manual segmentation was used to label tumor, nerve roots, blood vessels, and vertebral anatomy. Color-coded 3D renderings were analyzed by the surgical team to delineate dissection margins and optimize osteotomy site planning. Intraoperatively, an O-arm CT platform was registered with the preoperative 3D model and integrated into the StealthStation (Medtronic) neuronavigation platform.

Stealth navigation uses a stereoscopic infrared camera to track reflective spheres attached to the patient and surgical instruments, allowing real-time 3D localization of anatomy during surgery. These markers are registered to preoperative imaging, enabling accurate co-registration and display of the probe’s position within the reconstructed surgical field (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). Registration accuracy is achieved by locally fusing intraoperative and preoperative 2D and 3D scans.

The navigation-enabled model was used throughout exposure, osteotomy, and resection steps to ensure accurate and safe tumor removal.

The Surgical Theater SyncAR Spine software imports the DICOM files, which are then aligned in a fusion step. Following fusion verification, the spinal architecture depiction from the CT image is modified to improve visual clarity. MR and CT imaging are utilized to manually segment the sarcoma along with surrounding soft tissues, including adjacent organs, muscles, nerves, and vasculature, on a patient-specific basis.7 CT imaging is used to render the intensity threshold of the bony structure.

The procedure of manual segmentation involves defining the anatomical structure by manual painting and/or outline delineation.8 There was no automatic segmentation involved. Different colors are used to enhance the sarcoma and surrounding soft tissue (sarcoma in green). The final model, which incorporates relevant data from the many imaging modalities and presents a single comprehensive model, is displayed in a virtual 3D model. In order to visualize the impact of tumor removal, comparative 3D models were also created using postoperative imaging (postoperative component).

Illustrative Case

Case 1

A 28-year-old male was referred with more than 6 months of right-sided rib pain. Imaging revealed an approximately 3.8 × 8.7 × 8.0–cm contrast-enhancing mass spanning from T6 to T9 in the paraspinal region with right T7–8 foraminal extension (Fig. 1). Biopsy confirmed findings consistent with a chondrosarcoma. The patient elected to proceed with en bloc resection as this had the highest likelihood of long-term local control.

Sagittal (A) and axial (B) T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MR images showing an enhancing mass spanning from T6 to T9 and extending into the right T8–9 neuroforamen. Sagittal (C) and axial (D) CT scans of the chest demonstrating a right paraspinous mass arising from the right eighth rib.

To better visualize the interaction between the tumor and surrounding structures, preoperative MR and thin-slice CT images were acquired (Fig. 1). The Surgical Theater SyncAR Spine software was used to render the spinal anatomy overlay after the CT and MR images were imported. Specifically, the CT scans were used to manually segment the tumor and nerve roots (which are shown in bright green and purple, respectively, in Fig. 2).

Lateral (upper) and posterior (lower) views of the 3D reconstruction models of the tumor utilizing preoperative thin-slice CT and MRI, showing spinal anatomy, in particular, extension and segmentation of the sarcoma (green) and blood vessels (red).

The surgery was planned in two stages. Stage 1 involved posterior instrumentation (T3–5, T10–11), laminectomy (T6–9), and osteotomies to disconnect the tumor from the posterior column. Stage 2 included anterior dissection via thoracotomy, and final tumor release guided by neuronavigation.

Stage 1

A subperiosteal dissection was performed of the T3–11 spinous processes, laminae, pars, and transverse processes. After that, a 3D space registration was produced by integrating the SyncAR Spine system with an intraoperative CT scan (Medtronic O-arm). The 3D model can be navigated intraoperatively owing to the fusion of the intraoperative CT scan with preoperative imaging. The primary surgeon’s side of the bed was where the SyncAR Spine monitor was placed, and it was also visible on the hanging display. A multidimensional representation of the tumor and surrounding anatomy was made possible by the SyncAR Spine, which shows a virtual probe with real-time navigation of soft tissue renderings from preoperative imaging while the Stealth navigates the intraoperative CT. This helped with intraoperative dissection and hardware placement.

Using the neuronavigation system, we placed pedicle screws at T3, T4, T5, T10, and T11 bilaterally, followed by laminectomies at T6, T7, T8, and T9. On the right side, which was the side of the tumor, the T6–9 transverse processes, pedicles, and rib heads were removed, taking care to not enter the tumor capsule. Then the nerve roots were ligated. A partial right-sided corpectomy was performed at T7–9 to further disconnect the tumor from the spinal column. After confirming that the tumor had been completely disconnected posteriorly, the posterior fusion was then completed with placement and fixation of the rods, followed by wound closure (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D).

Stage 2

The patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position and lung isolation was achieved. The second stage was initiated by the thoracic surgeon through an incision over the rib, with entry into the chest via the sixth intercostal space anteriorly. The seventh, eighth, and ninth ribs were divided from the tumor and subsequently resected. The previously accessed area from the first stage was encountered. The tumor was then meticulously dissected from the mediastinum and mobilized away from the aorta. The azygos vein was divided both above and below the level of the mass. On completion of this dissection, and with the aid of neuronavigation, the neurosurgeon proceeded with resection of the remaining tumor adherent to the T7–9 vertebral bodies. The tumor was already noted to be disconnected from the T6 vertebral body. Once completely mobilized, the tumor was removed en bloc and submitted for pathological evaluation.

A corpectomy and discectomy were then performed at T7–9, followed by placement of a titanium cage and anterior thoracic buttress plate at T6–10 (Video 1). Chest wall reconstruction and closure was then performed by the thoracic surgeon. Pathological analysis was consistent with WHO grade 2 chondrosarcoma and showed negative margins.

Postoperative Course

Postoperatively (Fig. 3C and D), the patient remained neurologically intact. At his 5-year follow-up (Fig. 4A and B), he reported persistent right-sided thoracic pain; however, this represented > 90% improvement compared with his preoperative symptoms. Surveillance imaging has continued to demonstrate stability without evidence of tumor recurrence (Fig. 3).

Case 2

A 21-year-old female presented for right-sided rib and back pain with noted paresthesia and pain in the right T6–8 dermatomes. Imaging showed a 7.2 × 5.9 × 6.8–cm mass arising from the posterior aspect of the right seventh rib with extension into the right T6–7 and T7–8 foramen with involvement of the right T6 and T7 nerve roots (Fig. 4). A CT-guided biopsy and pathological analysis was consistent with a high-grade osteosarcoma. After a multidisciplinary discussion, it was recommended that she undergo a staged en bloc resection followed by chemotherapy.

Preoperative MR images and thin-slice CT scans were obtained to delineate the relationships between the soft tissues, bony structures, and tumor. To generate 3D renderings of the spinal architecture, sarcoma, and feeder vessels (shown in white, green, and yellow, respectively, in Supplementary Fig. 2), the CT and MRI datasets were integrated into the Surgical Theater SyncAR Spine software.

A similar two-stage approach was used. Posterior exposure and osteotomy were guided by the 3D model and navigation, preserving tumor capsula integrity. Anterior release was completed via thoracotomy, enabling en bloc removal without spondylectomy. Reconstruction included anterior decortication and posterior allograft.

Stage 1

Subperiosteal dissection of the T3–11 spinous processes, laminae, pars, and transverse processes was performed. After obtaining an intraoperative CT scan and integrating it with preoperative imaging, a neuronavigation system was established that provided a patient-specific 3D depiction of the soft tissue renderings. The SyncAR Spine monitor displaying the navigated 3D model was positioned at the bedside and on hanging displays as described in the first case, which helped significantly during the resection and use of instrumentation.

With the assistance of neuronavigation, pedicle screws were placed at T3–5 bilaterally, T6–8 on the left, and T9–11 bilaterally. Next, laminectomies and facetectomies were performed on the right of T6, T7, and T8, followed by ligation of the right T6 and T7 nerve roots. Osteotomy cuts were then performed with the assistance of neuronavigation to disarticulate the T6–8 vertebral body at the costovertebral joint followed by wedge osteotomies along the T6–8 right lateral vertebral bodies. (Supplementary Fig. 3) This allowed for exposure and dissection of the entire tumor from the spine. The dorsal bony surfaces of T3–11 were decorticated, and then cadaveric bone was placed over the decorticated area for allograft. This was followed by rod placement and wound closure (Video 2).

Stage 2

The second stage involved both thoracic surgery and neurosurgery for the anterior resection of the tumor. The thoracic surgeon first performed a lateral thoracotomy and excision of the right sixth to eighth ribs. Following the thoracotomy, the neurosurgeon carefully dissected the anterior portion of the tumor away from the bony attachments along the T6–8 vertebral bodies, pedicles, and soft tissue. The entire tumor was then removed en bloc and sent for pathological evaluation. The T6–8 vertebral bodies were then decorticated to obtain better margins for tumor control (Fig. 5). The patient then underwent chest wall reconstruction and closure by the thoracic surgeon. Pathological analysis confirmed a diagnosis of high-grade osteosarcoma. Postoperative imaging showed complete tumor resection. After surgery, the patient underwent 6 weeks of chemotherapy followed by radiation therapy.

A: Tumor exposure following mediastinal dissection and rib resection. B: En bloc resected tumor specimen. C: Lateral view of the T6–8 vertebral bodies after partial corpectomy and tumor removal.

Postoperative Course

Postoperatively (Supplementary Fig. 4A–D) she had anticipated right-sided thoracic numbness in the distribution of the sacrificed nerve roots. Her most recent surveillance imaging obtained 2 years postoperatively was stable without evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

For the management of thoracic sarcomas, en bloc resection with a wide margin is favored as previous studies have shown an increased risk of locoregional recurrence with intralesional tumor excision.9–16 Achieving en bloc resection with negative margins in thoracic vertebral sarcomas traditionally requires total spondylectomy. However, this carries substantial risk.14–16

Complication rates can be as high as 82% in en bloc resection of these tumors, which is consistent with previous literature.16–22 Complications such as large-vessel injury-induced blood loss can be mitigated by using a combined anterior and posterior approach to provide more space for tumor resection.16

Beyond spondylectomy, there are other therapeutic options. Deutsch et al. used a minimally invasive partial corpectomy technique that resulted in a reduced blood loss of 227 ml.25 In comparison to the separation surgery technique described by Cofano et al. and Nasser et al., there is a stark decrease in blood loss.23,24 A different method by Xionget al. involved splitting the paraspinal muscles, but that led to 1.28 L of blood loss.26

In recent years, less invasive surgery plus postoperative stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) has been proposed. The rationale for these approaches is to minimize blood loss while improving patient outcome. A recent study by Husain et al. found an 87.6% mean tumor control rate with SRS.27 Laufer et al. found that SRS plus separation surgery yielded a mean control rate of 83.7.28

The integration of augmented reality (AR) and 3D reconstruction introduces a paradigm shift in surgical planning. Boretto et al. used AR to develop a method for direct intraoperative reconstruction of the liver, which proved beneficial for surgical navigation without interrupting surgical workflow.29 Within neurosurgery, the implementation of AR and mixed reality allows neurosurgeons to visualize and overlay salient anatomical information, aiding surgical workflow and outcomes.30 However, standard practice is to use neuronavigation, projecting 3D imaging data onto 2D displays, and asking the surgeon to orientate themselves. This removes a surgeons focus off their field.31,32

Our cases demonstrate that integrating patient-specific 3D modeling and intraoperative navigation allows for anatomically tailored resections, minimizing morbidity while preserving oncological outcomes.

Observations

In our experience, the use of 3D reconstruction imaging has been highly beneficial in facilitating neural structure preservation and achieving en bloc resection with negative margins. In the cases described above, this technology enabled detailed preoperative visualization, improved intraoperative spatial orientation, and guided precise osteotomies. By avoiding total vertebral body resection, we were able to preserve some mechanical stability and minimize the need for extensive reconstruction.

As current literature on the use of 3D reconstruction in thoracic sarcoma patients is in its infancy, our experience marks a significant step forward in this field, on which research can be built and leveraged to improve patient care by utilizing this technology.

Limitations

We reported 2 cases utilizing 3D reconstruction models with two surgeons taking part in this study. There is a need for higher-quality imaging, manual segmentation expertise, and specialized equipment. Broader validation across larger cohorts is required to establish generalizability.

Lessons

Three-dimensional models have quite a steep learning curve and may create technological barriers; however, patient-specific 3D models and neuronavigation can guide en bloc resections of thoracic vertebral sarcomas without formal spondylectomy.

This approach reduces morbidity and preserves function while maintaining oncological efficacy.

Cost is a limiting factor not only for implementation but also for maintenance and repair. Nevertheless, with continued advances in AR and 3D modeling, integration of this technology into patient care may enhance efficiency and provide additional support for surgeons managing complex cases.
