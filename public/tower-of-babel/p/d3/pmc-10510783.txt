Association of Neighborhood Economic Status and Race With Developing Pelvic Inflammatory Disease After Sexually Transmitted Infections
Authors: Runzhi Wang, Kathryn A. Carson, Saumya S. Sao, Jenell S. Coleman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Racial disparities in pelvic inflammatory disease diagnoses are mitigated by neighborhood socioeconomic status.

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is a serious consequence of persistent or untreated sexually transmitted infections (STIs).1 It can cause long-term reproductive adverse sequelae, including tubal factor infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, and recurrent episodes of PID.2–5 In the United States, the cost of treating PID is high, with direct expenses for acute PID and its long-term effects estimated to be nearly $2 billion in 1998 dollars.6 The average lifetime cost per person for PID is $2,150 and may increase significantly if major complications occur.7 According to a study by the CDC in 2017, the prevalence of self-reported PID was 4.4% among sexually experienced reproductive-aged women in the United States, which translates to about 2.5 million women with a PID diagnosis at some point in their lives.8 This estimated number of PID cases may be even higher because PID often goes undiagnosed.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends annual screening of sexually active women aged 24 years or younger,9 which is a key strategy to reduce PID. However, disparities in screening and infection rates remain. In one emergency department study, Black adolescents and young adult women had nearly four times the odds of STI testing compared with other racial groups, despite the presence of STI-related complaints among all patients.10 Another clinic-based study of female adolescents and young adults with STI symptoms revealed that the odds of health care professionals testing Black or Latina adolescents and young adults was 2–3 times that of White adolescents and young adults.11 In addition, the prevalence of PID has been reported to be consistently higher among Black women than White women in the United States.12 For instance, one study reported that about 1 in 88 non-Hispanic Black women but 1 in 300 non-Hispanic White women experienced chlamydia-associated PID.13 However, race is not a proxy for genetics or susceptibility to disease but is a social construct representing differences in access to social and economic resources.14–16 Therefore, it is racism that is the fundamental driver of social determinants of health (eg, neighborhood, health care access, economic stability)17 and health inequities.18,19 Investigators have increasingly acknowledged the association of social determinants of health with the increased risk of disease and poorer health outcomes. For example, one study showed that health insurance coverage and health-seeking behaviors were associated with the risk of chlamydial infection among young adults independently of race.20 Still, many classic textbooks and high-quality journal articles continue to report race as a risk factor for STIs and PID,21–24 and there is a paucity of data describing how social determinants of health may be associated with PID. Therefore, we sought to examine how race was associated with PID after considering neighborhood-level socioeconomic status (SES) among female adolescents and young adults who had a documented STI.

METHODS

The Johns Hopkins School of Medicine IRB deemed the study exempt and acknowledged the study. We used Maryland statewide hospital claims data collected by the Health Services Cost Review Commission that included variables to track admissions of the same patient across settings of care (inpatient and outpatient) and across acute care hospitals to construct our retrospective cohort. Female adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 years who had at least one of three STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis) between July 2012 to March 2015 were identified with International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision and Tenth Revision diagnosis codes (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D323). We selected this age range because this group has the highest prevalence of STIs and PID in the United States.25 A participant entered the cohort on the earliest date of an STI diagnosis code, which we define as the STI diagnosis date. Participants with a PID diagnosis before the STI diagnosis date were excluded from the analysis. In these data sets, time is recorded in quarters (eg, quarter 1, January–March) and years without an exact date; therefore, time for our cohort was considered a discrete variable. Data cleaning and analyses were done with JavaScript and Stata.

Race documented in the hospital claims data was categorized into nine groups: White, Black or African American, Asian, American Indian and Alaska Native, other, two or more races, Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islander, decline to answer, and unknown. Because the White and Black racial categories composed more than 96% of the cohort, data from other racial categories or those with missing data (0.4%) were excluded from further analysis. Because more than 95% of our study population did not self-identify as Hispanic, we did not further combine race with ethnicity. The participant’s five-digit residential ZIP code tabulation area was linked to median income data from the American Community Survey.26 After exploring the distribution of the median household income and its association with PID, we dichotomized median household income at $40,000 to represent meaningful changes in risk, similar to another study evaluating the effect of SES on health outcomes.27 For sensitivity analysis, we used the poverty rate (percentage of people living below the federal poverty level) from the American Community Survey as another measure of neighborhood SES. Higher neighborhood poverty was defined as living in an area with more than 15% of individuals living below the federal poverty level.27

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of a PID diagnosis (incident PID) after STI diagnosis during follow-up. Each participant was followed up until a PID diagnosis or for 3 years after the first STI diagnosis, at which time the participant was censored. We made an assumption that all STI events and PID cases for each participant in our cohort were captured in the hospital claims data.

Covariates included in the analysis were age, number of STI events during follow-up, and insurance type (ie, Medicaid, commercial and military, or self-pay). Age was presented as a categorical variable in 5-year strata in the claims data set. Therefore, in our analysis, age was dichotomized into two groups: 15–19 and 20–24 years. For each participant, the number of STI events during the follow-up was identified by the total number of diagnosis codes for the three STIs. Diagnosis of the same STI occurring within 3 months was considered one case because the data set reported quarters and not exact dates. Diagnosis of more than one STI during the same visit was regarded as one STI event. The number of different STI types was calculated from the types of STIs that participants had during the follow-up period. We selected three common STIs associated with PID (chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis); therefore, the maximum number of STI types a participant could have during the follow-up was three (chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis).

Demographic and clinical characteristics were categorized, summarized as frequency and percentage, and compared across the two racial groups (Black, White) with the use of χ2 tests. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted by race and neighborhood SES for 3 years after study entry, and log-rank tests were used to compare the curves. Because time was observed in grouped form, complementary log–log models were used to estimate the hazard of PID after STI. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated for PID when Black race was compared with White race. We started with an unadjusted model containing only race (model 1) and then adjusted for age, insurance type, number of STI types, and number of STI events (model 2). In the final model, neighborhood SES (measured by neighborhood median household income) was added to model 2. Interaction terms were added to the final model to test whether neighborhood SES would modify the association of race with incident PID after STI. However, the interaction terms were not significant, and data were not shown. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by plotting log–log Kaplan–Meier survival estimates against time stratified by each variable. With direct visualization, the curves were reasonably parallel. In addition, we constructed models with neighborhood poverty level as the measure of SES for the sensitivity analysis.

RESULTS

The cohort included 2,873 female adolescents and young adults in Maryland with at least one STI between July 2012 and March 2015. Of them, 88.5% were of Black race, and 32.8% were 15–19 years old. The median follow-up was 36 months, and 607 (21.1% of the cohort) had a PID diagnosis. Other characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. In univariate analysis, compared with their White counterparts, Black female adolescents and young adults were significantly more likely to live in areas with low SES according to measures of both median household income and poverty rate. In addition, Black female adolescents and young adults were more likely to have more than one STI event and higher PID incidence (21.8% vs 16.1%, P=.019) than White female adolescents and young adults.

The unadjusted association between race and risk of PID was estimated. In model 1, the hazard of PID among Black adolescents and young adults was 1.40 times the risk among White participants (95% CI 1.06–1.85). In model 2, after adjustment for age, insurance type, number of STI events, and number of STI types during follow-up, the HR was similar (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.04–1.83). However, in model 3, once we adjusted for the neighborhood median household income, the HR for Black race compared with White race decreased to 1.25 and was no longer statistically significant (Table 2). Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis using poverty rate as the neighborhood SES measure. In the fully adjusted model, higher neighborhood poverty was associated with a 46% increased risk of PID after STIs (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.24–1.73), and no difference was observed between Black and White participants (HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.93–1.65).

DISCUSSION

This study is one of the first to explore the association between select social determinants of health and race with PID after a documented STI. Our results indicate that, after age, insurance type, number of STI events, and number of STI types were controlled for, Black race was associated with an increased risk of PID. However, when neighborhood SES was considered, the racial difference was no longer significant. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using the poverty rate as an alternative measure of SES confirmed that the relationship between SES and PID remained consistent, and race was no longer significant when the poverty level was considered. Contrary to other reports,20 we did not find an association between health insurance coverage and PID. Maryland expanded Medicaid eligibility in 2014 to include those with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level28; therefore, more than 75% of our population had health insurance and access to care. Eliminating the barrier of insurance coverage allows an evaluation of other factors that may be associated with PID. As we and others have shown,24 low SES is one of several social determinants of health that can affect a PID diagnosis.

Social determinants of health influence the well-being and health of communities and can be wellness promoting (eg, stable, affordable housing, economic opportunities) or detrimental. Besides PID, differential exposure to social determinants of health has been linked to racial and ethnic disparities in conditions such as asthma,29 childhood obesity,30 and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) viral suppression.31 Detrimental social determinants of health that lead to health inequities include limited access to transportation, exposure to environmental pollution, inadequate education, discrimination, and harmful advertisements.32 Many social, political, and economic policies on the national and regional levels drive social determinants of health, which have harmed the Black population. This is how structural racism, which includes inequitable laws, policies, infrastructure, and education, affects health and has led to more Black people living in low-socioeconomic-status neighborhoods than White people33 and residential segregation.34 Despite a slight decrease in segregation rates, Black people in the United States remain the most segregated subpopulation.35 This segregation has been linked to high rates of STI among the Black community, which low health care quality, sexual networks, and health care utilization may partially explain.36–38 Physician shortages, less experienced physicians, inadequate access to critical care services, fewer specialty referrals, and inadequate access to pharmacies for STI partner therapy are higher in segregated neighborhoods.39–42 Segregated sexual networks and selection of sexual partners (eg, number, timing, concurrency) can sustain and restrict STIs within certain racial or ethnic groups.36,43 In addition, structural racism has led to policies contributing to high Black male incarceration rates.44,45 Furthermore, structural exclusion of neighborhoods from opportunity structures based on racial composition of residents can contribute to limited community resources, inadequate social support, and strengthened social stigma or medical mistrust,46 all of which can lead to poor health care utilization and delayed treatment for STIs.47–49

Our data also revealed that neighborhood SES differed significantly by race, and its association with PID differed within racial categories. Only Black adolescents and young adults with low neighborhood-level SES were associated with PID, whereas White adolescents and young adults with low SES were not. Studies have shown that the higher burden of infection not only is attributable to increased prevalence alone but also may be exacerbated by clinician implicit bias.11,50 Implicit attitudes are thoughts and feelings existing outside of conscious awareness that may influence clinician behavior and treatment choices.51 Negative implicit attitudes about people of color may contribute to disproportionate screening in Black adolescents and young adults10,11 and, thus, the detection of early asymptomatic infection. Because PID is a clinical diagnosis, this bias can lead to overdiagnosis of PID in certain socially disadvantaged groups such as Black people and people with low SES.

Our study also highlights the increased risk of PID progression among adolescents and young adults with more than one STI event. Compared with female adolescents and young adults who had only one STI episode (the initial STI event), the risk of PID was increased by an additional STI episode, which is consistent with previous findings. A large retrospective study in the UK reported that repeated chlamydia infections increase the risk of PID, ectopic pregnancy, and female infertility.52 Other studies have also observed a dose–response relationship between the number of chlamydia diagnoses and the risk of PID.53,54 Along with our findings, these studies illustrated the importance of optimal treatment for the patient and sexual partner(s) and 3-month STI retesting to reduce incident PID.

It is important to note that our findings contrast with those from a previous PID risk study using NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) data.8 The NHANES study reported no significant differences in the prevalence of lifetime PID between White and Black women and by socioeconomic factors. However, the NHANES data relied on self-reported PID, and the study population consisted of women aged 18–44 years, whereas our study focused on a younger population who had confirmed STI and PID diagnoses based on International Classification of Diseases codes. These differences may account for the differing results between the two studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, we assumed that the population was stable and that all STI or PID events were captured in the hospital claims data. However, relocation to a different residential ZIP code could have occurred during the follow-up period, and some STI or PID events could have been diagnosed out of the state or in clinics that are not associated with a hospital and may not have been captured. Further research that prospectively follows adolescent girls from the time of their first STI event and collects rich data on social determinants of health would overcome this limitation. Second, it is unknown how race was collected in our data source (ie, whether race was self-reported or assigned). Furthermore, our analysis was limited to two racial groups. A larger population sample would be needed for adequate power to investigate the association of STI and PID across different racial groups, providing broader and more robust evidence for these relationships. Third, the number of White women with neighborhood-level SES less than $40,000 represented only 2% of total PID cases, which may have limited the power to detect differences. Fourth, there are other non–STI-related causes of PID (eg, aerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis) that we did not capture. Finally, as a result of the limitations of the data set, we used insurance type to represent individual-level SES and household income in the residential area to represent neighborhood-level SES. However, other social determinants of health such as education access and quality, health care access and quality, history of trauma or violence, and community context may have contributed to PID outcomes. The use of data containing Census tract data, which allows linkage to other social determinants of health measures such as the social vulnerability and area deprivation indices, can improve the evaluation of the effect of the overall social determinants on PID development.

The results of our study demonstrate that neighborhood SES attenuated the racial disparity in PID diagnosis after STI. Health care professionals should acknowledge the effect of social determinants of health and examine their own biases. Our results support the literature that emphasizes the need to reduce and ultimately eliminate disparate social conditions that can give rise to sexual health inequities.55,56 Previous research has extensively explored the effect of individual behaviors and race on sexual health outcomes. However, the next step in sexual health equity requires a shift toward addressing systemic barriers and structures that perpetuate racial health disparities. It is imperative to address structural racism by transforming systems, policies, environments,57 and practices to eliminate disparities and to ensure sexual health equity for all.
