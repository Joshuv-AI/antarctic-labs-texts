Unraveling the effects of shading on sensory quality, non-volatile metabolites and volatile flavor compounds of Longjing tea
Authors: Xiaojun Niu, Cun Ao, Peixian Bai, Hongli Li, Yun Zhao, Haitao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShading improves tea quality, yet the effects of different shading materials on metabolites of Longjing tea remain unclear. This study compared conventional cultivation (CK) with transparent plastic film (TPF) and black shading net (BSN) treatments in Longjing tea. BSN significantly enhanced appearance, liquor color, taste, and aroma, while TPF improved only aroma. Metabolomics showed that BSN increased the contents of certain amino acids and terpenoids but decreased catechins and flavonoids. GC–MS analyses revealed that, while BSN- and TPF-treated Longjing tea displayed greater diversity of volatile compounds, only a few key aroma compounds showed marked increases in concentration. Combined analysis of OAV values and concentrations revealed that (E)-2-hexenyl hexanoate, hexyl hexanoate, cis-3-hexenyl n-valerate, 3-carene, octanal, hexanal, nerolidol, and phenylethyl alcohol played a crucial role in enhancing the aroma quality of shade-treated tea. These findings clarify how shading regulates tea metabolites, supporting targeted cultivation for flavor-oriented production of Longjing tea.Keywords: Shading, Longjing tea, Sensory quality, Metabolism, Volatile compounds

Introduction

As a globally popular beverage, green tea is celebrated for its unique flavor, aroma, color, and potential health benefits. The quality of green tea is primarily driven by the composition of its secondary metabolites, which directly determine its color, flavor, and taste (Zeng et al., 2019). These metabolites exhibit considerable variation among different cultivars and are profoundly shaped by environmental factors, with light and temperature being particularly pivotal (Liu et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2022). Shading is a key agronomic practice in tea production that enhances tea quality by modifying light intensity and temperature, which in turn regulates the physiological and biochemical processes of the tea plant (Chen et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2011; Ye et al., 2023). There are two primary forms of shading in tea cultivation: cover shading and ecological shading. Studies have shown that ecological shading can improve biodiversity (Chen et al., 2019), stabilize the community structure in tea plantations, and enhance the overall quality of tea leaves (Fu et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2022). However, cover shading has become the most widely adopted shading method in tea plantations, owing to its advantages of low cost, simple operation and diverse specifications. In commercial production, it is also the predominant approach, typically using shade nets and plastic greenhouses.

Previous research studies have shown that moderate shading (using shade nets) stimulates the accumulation of chlorophyll and amino acids in young tea shoots while simultaneously reducing tea polyphenol levels, thereby facilitating the quality of the green tea (Ku et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2012; Ji et al., 2018; Sano et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2021; Yamashita et al., 2021). However, research has mainly focused on how shading affects non-volatile metabolites in tea leaves, while the influence of shading on volatile metabolites and the underlying mechanisms driven by shading conditions remain less studied. Aroma is a critical factor influencing the character and quality of tea (Yang et al., 2013). The formation of aroma is the result of the tea leaves defensive response to various stresses experienced during the growth (pre-harvest) and processing (post-harvest) phases (Zeng et al., 2019). Shading acts as a stressor on the tea growth environment, altering enzymatic activities and metabolic pathways, thereby inducing variations in volatile compounds. While previous studies have established that shading enhances certain classes of volatile compounds (Liu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2012), its specific effects on the aroma-active compound profile of Longjing tea under different covering materials, and the resulting impact on sensory attributes, require further investigation. Longjing tea is one of the world's most renowned premium green teas, regarded as China's national tea, with its unique aroma being a key factor in its widespread acclaim (Wang et al., 2020). Therefore, exploring the influences of different shading materials on both non-volatile and volatile compounds in Longjing tea carries important implications for improving its flavor quality.

In recent years, metabolomics has been widely applied to explore the physiology of tea plants (Dai et al., 2015), identify key taste and aroma compounds in tea (Dai et al., 2017; Ku et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2018), and clarify the biosynthetic pathways of metabolites that regulate quality (Ji et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). Furthermore, headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) has emerged as the predominant analytical technique for the detection and identification of volatile compounds in tea (Wang et al., 2020). This study integrated sensory evaluation, metabolomics, HS-SPME-GC–MS, and odor activity value (OAV) calculation to comprehensively assess how different shading treatments affect the sensory quality and chemical composition (both non-volatile and volatile compounds) of Longjing tea. The research aims to analyze the effects of different shading treatments on the sensory quality and flavor characteristics of Longjing tea, providing a theoretical basis for the precise cultivation of high-quality shaded tea gardens and the production of tea with specific flavors.

Materials and methods

Tea plant cultivation and experimental design

The shading experiments were conducted at the experimental base of Pingyao, the Tea Institute, Hangzhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The study used a new tea cultivar, ‘Lifeng’, which is suitable for producing Longjing tea. The experimental design followed our previously used methodology (Niu et al., 2025). The detailed steps are as follows: The 9 rows of Camellia sinensis (L.) cv. Li Feng tea plants with 10-years-old from tea field (15 m long and 1.5 m wide for each row, 0.33 m between plants within the row) were selected for the treatments. The shade period was from April 12 to 17 in 2023 when the new shoots had grown to one bud or one bud and one leaf. Tea plants were covered by either a BSN (black shade netting, shade rate 85%) or a layer of TPF (transparent plastic PE stretch film, 0.1-mm thickness), both installed 0.5 m above the surface of the plant canopy. Tea plants without any covering served as controls (CK). Each treatment was replicated three times.

Tea processing and sensory evaluation

The Longjing tea was processed using an automatic roasting machine for flat tea (6CCB-100ZD, Hengfeng Technology Development Co., Ltd., Shaoxing, China). After four hours of withering, the fresh tea leaves were stir-fried for 110 circles at a temperature of 210 °C. After cooling, the tea leaves were stir-fried again for 90 circles at a temperature of 160 °C. The leaves were then machine-dried at 80 °C in a tea dryer (6CHX-9D, Haoyu Machinery Ltd., Lishui, China) until their moisture content was ∼5%.

The sensory evaluation of the tea samples was performed by five experienced tea experts, who were trained and authenticated by professional organizations. The characteristics and quality scores were described according to the criteria in GB/T 23776–2018 (Chinese Standards-Methodology of sensory evaluation of tea). Precisely 3 g of a tea sample was infused with 150 mL of freshly boiled water, and filtered after brewing for 4 min. The appearance of dry tea, brew color, aroma, taste and infused leaf of green teas were then assessed by the quality scores using a 100-point quantitative evaluation system. The final quality scores were derived through calculating the means of the scores given by the individual panelists in their independent assessments.

UPLC-Q-MS/ MS for metabolomic

Metabolite extraction from tea samples was performed as follows: approximately 100 mg of sample was homogenized with 800 μL of methanol: water (4:1, v/v) using a Wonbio-96c cryogenic grinder (Wanbo Biotechnology, Shanghai) at 50 Hz for 6 min (−10 °C). The homogenate was subjected to ultrasonic extraction (40 kHz, 30 min, 5 °C), incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged (13,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Thermo UHPLC system equipped with an ACQUITY BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water-acetonitrile (2:98, v/v; solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient program was: 0–0.5 min, 2% B; 0.5–7.5 min, 2–35% B; 7.5–13 min, 35–95% B; 13–14.4 min, 95% B; 14.4–14.5 min, 95–2% B; 14.5–16 min, 2% B. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min with a 3 μL injection volume.

Metabolomics data were processed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www. cloud.majorbio.com, accessed on 8 October 2025). Significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) were identified based on VIP >1 from OPLS-DA model and p < 0.05 from Student's t-test. Six biological replicates were analyzed per treatment.

Extraction and analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds were extracted by the headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) method with a divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) coating fiber (50/30 μm, 2 cm; Supelco, Inc., Bellefonte, PA, USA). The detailed steps are as follows: 0.5 g of tea powder was weighed and transferred into a 20 mL brown headspace flask, and then 5 mL of boiling deionized water and 10 μL of ethyl decanoate (8.64 mg/L) were added to the flask. After 10 min equilibration at 50 °C with magnetic stirring (200 rpm), the fiber was exposed to the headspace for 40 min, followed by thermal desorption in the GC inlet (250 °C, 5 min).

The GC–MS analysis of volatiles was performed using an Agilent system consisting of an 8890-gas chromatograph and a 5977C mass spectrometer (Santa Clara, CA, USA). A HP-INNOWAX capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm, 2.5 μm, J & W, Folsom, CA, USA) was used for the separation of the volatile compounds. The injector temperature was set at 250 °C, and the spitless injection method was employed. Initially, the oven temperature was set at 50 °C for 5 min, then the temperature was increased to 180 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min. The temperature of 200 °C was maintained for 1 min, then raised to 240 °C at a speed of 10 °C/min. The temperature of 240 °C was held for 8 min. Ultra-pure helium (99.999%) was employed as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.6 mL/min with a 4-min solvent delay. MS parameters: EI mode at 70 eV (source 230 °C); scan range: m/z 50–550. Volatile compounds were identified by spectral matching against the NIST 20 mass spectral reference database (match factor > 80). Retention indices (RI) were determined using n-alkanes (C₆-C₂₅) retention times (RT) via linear interpolation. Quantification employed ethyl decanoate-normalized peak area ratios. The principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS–DA) of volatile profiles were performed in SIMCA 13.0 software (Sartorius AG, Germany). All analyses were conducted in triplicate.

The relative odor activity value (ROAV) was calculated as the ratio of the concentration of each volatile compound to its corresponding odor threshold in water. The odor thresholds of volatile compounds in water were referenced to data from the literature. (Guo et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2015).

Statistical analysis

The measured concentrations of volatile compounds were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and the result with a p-value <0.05 was considered differentiated significantly.

Results and discussion

Effect of shading on the appearance and sensory quality of Longjing tea product

To investigate the effects of shading on tea plants, both BSN and TPF treatments were carried out on tea plants in plantation. As shown in Fig. 1A, significant changes were observed in the appearance of the tea between the different treatments. Compared to the control, BSN-shaded tea leaves exhibited a darker and greener color, which can be attributed to the increased chlorophyll content that helps the leaves capture more photons under low light and thus compensates for the reduced light intensity (Lee et al., 2013). Some recent studies have found that the greening of shaded leaves is due to the increase in chlorophyll content and the development of chloroplasts in the leaves (Hu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2018; Yamashita et al., 2021). Additionally, our previous study demonstrated that tea leaves shaded by BSN exhibited a significant increase in chlorophyll a and b content, while those shaded by TPF showed no notable changes (Niu et al., 2025). This difference is fundamentally attributed to how the distinct shading treatments alter the microecological environment of the tea garden—increasing the proportion of scattered radiation, improving light quality, and thereby affecting chlorophyll levels (Elango et al., 2023). This also explains why Longjing tea shaded by BSN appears dark green in color, whereas TPF-shaded Longjing tea tends to show a yellowish-green appearance.

The results of the sensory evaluation showed that the BSN treatment had a significant effect on all the sensory factors, especially the appearance of the dry tea, brew color, aroma and taste. Compared to the control group, the quality of the shade-grown (BSN) tea exhibited significant improvements, characterized by a green-bright brew color (Fig. 1B), fresh aroma, and enhanced umami flavor. At the same time, there was a significant reduction in bitter and astringent flavors, with sensory scores for each attribute exceeding those of the control (Table 1). In addition, the results of this study showed that there was no significant improvement in the quality of TPF-treated tea compared to the control, except for aroma. These findings align with previous reports on ecological shading for green tea quality improvement (Liu et al., 2025). Overall, the results indicate that the BSN shading treatment effectively improves both the appearance and aroma of Longjing tea, while also significantly reducing bitterness and astringency, resulting in an overall enhancement of its quality. In contrast, the TPF shading treatment only resulted in notable changes in appearance and aroma quality.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolomic profiling identified 1558 non-volatile metabolites, encompassing 328 flavonoids, 200 terpenoids, 160 lipids, 128 carbohydrates, 109 phenolic acids, 98 amino acids, 55 organic acids, and other compounds (Table S1). PCA revealed clear separation among the three treatment groups, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 45.30% and 20.40% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2A), indicating substantial shading-induced metabolic shifts. The tight clustering of QC samples confirmed analytical stability and reproducibility. Based on OPLS-DA (VIP ≥ 1, |FC| ≥ 1, P < 0.05), 513 and 484 significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) were identified in BSN and TPF treatments, respectively, compared to control. Among these, 243 SCMs were upregulated and 270 downregulated in CK vs. BSN, while 301 were upregulated and 183 downregulated in CK vs. TPF (Fig. 2B).

Further visualize the changes in the abundance of key metabolites through heat maps (Fig. 2C). Amino acids are crucial determinants of tea quality, significantly correlated with aroma and flavor development (Dai et al., 2017), with catechins, as key flavonoid compounds, contributing critically to its astringency and bitterness (Wang et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 2C, most amino acids—such as γ-glutamylphenylalanine, L-aspartic acid, l-glutamine, L-norleucine, and L-arginine—were present at significantly higher levels in BSN- and TPF-treated tea samples than in the control (CK). In contrast, the BSN treatment notably reduced the contents of catechins (e.g., gallocatechins and epicatechin gallate) and most flavonoids in Longjing tea samples. These results align with our earlier quantitative analysis of the biochemical components in tea leaves under different shading treatments (Niu et al., 2025). This likely explains why shaded Longjing tea received higher scores for freshness and cleanness with significantly reduced bitterness and astringency in the sensory evaluation. Moreover, terpenoids are key constituents of tea aroma, and their composition and abundance are influenced by multiple factors, including external environment and tea cultivar (Zhao et al., 2022). Consistent with this, we observed a significant increase in terpenoid content following BSN treatment. It is noteworthy that compared with the control (CK), the abundances of acorusnol and cofaryloside decreased significantly in the BSN-treated samples but increased markedly in the TPF-treated samples, which may be attributable to the distinct properties of the two shading materials. These terpenoid changes are likely to collectively modulate the aroma characteristics of tea. Overall, the BSN treatment had a more pronounced impact on the non-volatile metabolites in Longjing tea than the TPF treatment.

Detected volatile compounds in Longjing tea by GC–MS

Volatile compounds in differently treated Longjing tea samples were analyzed using HS–SPME–GC–MS. A total of 133 volatiles were identified across all samples, with 120 detected in CK, 127 in BSN, and 128 in TPF. These compounds comprised 20 alcohols, 18 aldehydes, 23 alkanes, 33 esters, 14 ketones, 7 heterocycles, and 18 other compounds (Fig. 3, Table S2). PCA performed on the content data of the 133 volatiles revealed clear separation among the different treatments (Fig. 4A), indicating significant differences in their volatile profiles.

In this study, while more volatile compounds were detected in BSN- and TPF-treated green tea samples compared to the control, most showed no significant concentration increase. Only a limited set of volatiles—including hexanal, phenylethyl alcohol, nerolidol, 2-hexenal, heptane, methylene chloride, (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol benzoate, hexanoic acid hexyl ester, 2-methylpropanoic acid hexyl ester, (Z)-hexanoic acid-2-hexenyl ester, 3-octen-2-one, and (E, E)-3,5-octadien-2-one—increased significantly under BSN-shading. Overall, the volatile profiles identified under different shading regimes align with those previously associated with the aroma of Longjing tea (Wang et al., 2020). Studies have shown that shading treatments (dark treatment) significantly enhance the levels of volatile compounds in tea leaves, particularly volatile phenylacetones/benzonoids (Yang et al., 2012; Zeng et al., 2019). The study further revealed that the increase in these compounds under shading conditions was attributed to elevated levels of their common biosynthetic precursor, L-phenylalanine. Interestingly, in this study shading significantly increased phenylpropanoid/benzene-derived compounds (e.g., phenylethyl alcohol) in tea samples, but in our previous studies, shading treatment did not simultaneously increase the content of their common precursor L-phenylalanine (Niu et al., 2025). Li et al. (2025). found that both shading treatments and inherent cultivar differences can influence the chemical composition of tea. For instance, the ‘Longjing 43’ cultivar exhibited a significant decrease in L-phenylalanine after shading, while no significant difference was observed in the ‘Yabukita-shade’ treated tea samples. Previous studies have found that the aroma compounds such as benzaldehyde, benzyl alcohol and methyl benzoate in tea derived from phenylpyruvic acid (Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the shading-induced accumulation of volatile phenylacetone/benzene compounds in tea plants remain to be elucidated.

Previous studies have demonstrated that plastic film covering significantly regulates the synthesis, composition, and release of aroma substances in tea plants by altering the light, temperature, and humidity microenvironment of tea gardens, as well as modulating carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Zhao, Tong, Tu and Luo (2000) reported that combining greenhouse covering with organic fertilizer application could significantly increase the content of glycosidically-bound alcoholic aroma precursors and the total amount of alcoholic aroma compounds in fresh tea leaves, however, excessively high temperatures inside the greenhouse may inhibit the activity of related enzymes. Further research by Tang et al. (2014) showed that the application of light-converting film covering combined with CO₂ enrichment in the greenhouse could enhance the levels of sweet aroma constituents (e.g., soluble sugars and β-linalool) in tea shoots. Although this treatment resulted in a slight decrease in amino acid and caffeine contents, it did not exert an adverse effect on the overall quality of tea. In this study, compared with the control, the variety of aroma compounds in TPF-treated Longjing tea samples increased; however, the concentrations of most aroma compounds did not show a significant rise. Notably, nerolidol and 4,7-dimethyl-undecane showed a marked increase specifically in TPF-treated samples (Table S2). Studies have suggested that nerolidol and linalool are key compounds contributing to the characteristic fresh and floral aroma of green tea (Shi et al., 2022). Sensory evaluation results (Table 1) from this study also revealed that the aroma quality of Longjing tea under TPF treatment was significantly improved. The enhancement of aroma quality in TPF-treated Longjing tea can be attributed, on the one hand, to the increased amino acid content induced by TPF covering, and on the other hand, to the elevated levels of specific aroma compounds including nerolidol. Nevertheless, the overall quality of Longjing tea treated with BSN shading was still superior to that of the TPF-treated group.

Candidate differential volatile compounds in tea with different shading treatments

To further ascertain the contribution of each volatile to the classification, the Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) model was established to classify volatiles from different treatments. As shown in Fig. S1A, the PLS-DA model displayed a good of fit, CK, BSN, and TPF samples were well discriminated. The cross-validation analysis of the above PLS-DA model showed that this model was reliable (Fig. S1B, R2 = 0.943 Q2 = 0.381). Volatiles with variable importance in projection (VIP) scores exceeding 1.0 were selected as the threshold to identify key volatiles contributing critically to sample differentiation in the PLS-DA models (Liland, 2011; Oliveira et al., 2022). This study identified 28 candidate differential volatile compounds using PLS-DA models with a VIP score threshold >1.0. As shown in Fig. 4B, these compounds comprised 10 esters, 7 alcohols, 6 aldehydes, 3 alkanes, 1 ketone, and 1 unclassified compound. The top 10 ranked differential volatiles included (Z)-3-hexenyl hexanoate, (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol acetate, linalool, dimethyl sulfide, 3,7-dimethyl-1,5,7-octatrien-3-ol, hexanal, cis-3-hexenyl isobutyrate, 4-(Methoxycarbonyl)-cyclohexanone, methyl salicylate, 2-methyl-butanal. Whether these candidates are key contributors to the overall aroma of shaded tea remains to be further analyzed.

ROAV calculations for volatile compounds in tea

The contribution of individual volatiles to the aroma of tea is determined by their ROAV, calculated as the ratio of concentration to odor threshold in water (Wang et al., 2020). Volatile compounds with a ROAV greater than 1 are frequently identified as key aroma-active compounds, and are considered to have made significant contributions to aroma characteristics. Table S3 give the statistics of ROAV values of 28 candidate differential volatile compounds. Among these, six volatile compounds were excluded from ROAV calculation due to unavailable odor thresholds in water reported in the literature. Five volatile compounds exhibited ROAV values <1, while the remaining compounds showed ROAV values >1. Among the analyzed compounds, the concentrations of (E)-2-hexenyl hexanoate, hexanoic acid hexyl ester, n-valeric acid cis-3-hexenyl ester, 3-carene, octanal, hexanal, nerolidol and phenylethyl alcohol exhibited a significant increase after BSN-shading treatment. Notably, the concentrations of (Z)-3-hexenyl hexanoate, linalool, trans-linalool oxide, and methyl salicylate showed an upward trend following shading treatment, though these increases were not statistically significant. Interestingly, previous studies on Lushan Yunwu tea have also found that the levels of aroma compounds such as cis-3-hexenyl hexanoate, nerolidol and linalool increase significantly after ecological shading (Liu et al., 2025). These results indicate that the influence of different shading methods (cover shading or ecological shading) on tea aroma compounds is consistent in certain aspects. Future studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the impact of light environment on Longjing tea quality, thereby providing a theoretical basis for quality enhancement.

Studies have shown that volatiles derived from fatty acids, such as (E)-2-hexenyl hexanoate, hexanoic acid hexyl ester, n-valeric acid cis-3-hexenyl ester, octanal, and hexanal, typically exhibit odors characterized as green, fresh, fruity, fatty, or woody (Ho et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020). The volatile terpenoids and aromatic alcohols—such as linalool, linalool oxide, methyl salicylate, nerolidol, and phenylethyl alcohol—generally exhibit floral-like, clean, or pleasant fragrances (Guo et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). In this study, compared with the control, the aroma of Longjing tea after shading treatment showed more fresh and floral characteristics (Table 1). Thus, the improvement in the aroma quality of Longjing tea can be attributed to the impact of different shading treatments on the composition and concentration of several key volatile constituents in the tea leaves. Additionally, in the analysis of non-volatile compounds, we found that shading treatments increased the amino acid content in Longjing tea samples while reducing the levels of catechins and most flavonoids. Our previous research has shown that shading treatments significantly upregulate the expression of genes related to theanine synthesis and transport in tea plants, leading to the accumulation of theanine and other amino acids. Moreover, we found that shading can facilitate the interaction between light signaling components and transcription factors, whose coordinated regulation ultimately induces changes in a range of metabolites within the tea plant (Niu et al., 2025). In summary, the improvement in the overall sensory quality of Longjing tea under different shading treatments results from the combined effects of changes in both volatile and non-volatile metabolites within the fresh tea shoots. Consequently, the application of short-term covering shading holds practical value for improving the flavor and fragrance of Longjing tea, offering a viable strategy for quality optimization.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates how shading treatments modulate the quality of Longjing tea by reshaping its non-volatile and volatile metabolites profile. We found that shading treatments, particularly BSN, significantly improved the appearance, taste, and aroma of Longjing tea. This enhancement is primarily due to the increase in non-volatile compounds such as amino acids and terpenoids, a decrease in catechins and flavonoids, and an elevation in the levels of several key volatile compounds. Critical aroma compounds—including (E)-2-hexenyl hexanoate, hexanoic acid hexyl ester, n-valeric acid cis-3-hexenyl ester, 3-carene, octanal, hexanal, nerolidol and phenylethyl alcohol identified as the key compounds contributing to the characteristic aroma of shaded group. Longjing tea subjected to shading treatment developed more pronounced floral notes and exhibited significantly reduced bitterness and astringency. These results deepen our understanding of light-mediated aroma formation and offer a metabolic basis for flavor-oriented cultivation of Longjing tea.
