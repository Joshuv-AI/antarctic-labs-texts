Trends in pre-pregnancy diabetes and gestational diabetes by U.S. State, 2016–2024
Authors: Tanvi Nayak, Havisha Pedamallu, Nilay S. Shah, Kartik K. Venkatesh, Xiaoning Huang, William A. Grobman, Sadiya S. Khan, Natalie A. Cameron
Published: 2026

Introduction

Diabetes during pregnancy, including both pre-pregnancy and gestational diabetes (GDM), affects approximately 10% of pregnant individuals and is associated with higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preeclampsia, preterm birth, and macrosomia [1]. Over the long-term, individuals with prior GDM have up to a 10-fold higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus and a 2-times higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease [2,3]. Prior data have demonstrated substantial geographic variation in the rates of pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM by U.S. state between 2016 and 2021 [4]. To guide contemporary public health policies to improve maternal health outcomes, we describe up-to-date patterns of change in pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM between 2016 and 2024 by U.S. census regions and states.

Methods

This was a serial, cross-sectional analysis of maternal health data from the National Center for Health Statistics Natality Files. We included data from nulliparous pregnant individuals aged 15–44 years old with singleton, live births between 2016 and 2024. Diagnoses of pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM across the U.S. were reported using a check box on birth certificates by the medical attendant present at birth. We calculated the age-standardized prevalence and average-annual percent change (AAPC) in pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM by US census region (Northeast, Midwest, West, and South), and US state from 2016–2024. Inflection points indicating years in which there were changes in trends were identified using the model of best fit determined by Joinpoint software [5].

Results

Of 12,617,106 nulliparous individuals with live births between 2016 and 2024, 11.2% were 35 years old or older, 22.8% identified as Hispanic, 53.3% as non-Hispanic White, 13.1% as non-Hispanic Black, 7.7% as non-Hispanic Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander and 0.6% as non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native. Most individuals gave birth in the South (40.0%), followed by the West (23.7%), Midwest (19.8%), and Northeast (16.6%).

In 2024, the prevalence of pre-pregnancy diabetes per 100 live births in the U.S. was 1.2 (1.2, 1.3) in the Midwest, 1.1 (1.1, 1.1) in the South, 1.0 (0.9, 1.0) in the Northeast, and 0.9 (0.9, 1.0) in the West. From 2016 to 2024, the prevalence increased significantly in each U.S. census region, with the highest rate of change observed among the Western States (AAPC 4.6%/year [3.6, 5.6]) although all confidence intervals for AAPCs overlapped. No inflection points in national or regional trends were identified. Within US states in 2024, prevalence of pre-pregnancy diabetes ranged from 0.8 (0.5, 1.1) in Alaska to 2.0 (1.7, 2.3) in New Mexico (Fig. 1). The prevalence was significantly higher in 2024 compared with 2016 in 26 US states. The models that best fit the trend data included an inflection point for Hawaii (2021) and Florida (2022), after which there were accelerating rates of change in pre-diabetes prevalence. In Hawaii, the APC was −6.6 %/year (−1.8, 6.4) from 2016 – 2021 and 38.8%/year (9.0 – 76.3) from 2021–2024; in Florida the APC was 2.2%/ year (0.1, 4.3) from 2016 – 2022 and 10.5 %/year (−0.5, 22.9) from 2022–2024. Conversely, in Virginia and Wisconsin decelerating rates of change in pre-pregnancy diabetes were noted. In 2024, the prevalence of GDM was highest in the Midwest (7.8 [7.7, 7.9]), followed by the West (7.3 [7.2, 7.4]), Northeast (7.1 [7.0, 7.3]), and South (6.4 [6.4, 6.5]). Between 2016 and 2024, the overall prevalence increased in all U.S. census regions. An inflection point was noted in each US region between 2020 and 2021 after which the rate of change in GDM prevalence decelerated. Among US states, prevalence of GDM ranged from 4.6 (3.7, 5.3) in the District of Columbia to 11.7 (10.5, 13.0) in Alaska in 2024 (Fig. 1). Prevalence of GDM was significantly higher in 2024 compared with 2016 in 47 US states. Inflection points were noted in the best-fit models in the following states and years: California (2021), Georgia (2021), Illinois (2020), Indiana (2020), Kansas (2020), Maine (2020), Massachusetts (2021), Missouri (2020), Montana (2019), New Jersey (2020), New Mexico (2019), Ohio (2020), Oklahoma (2018), Utah (2021), and Vermont (2021). Notably, New Mexico was the only state in which rates of GDM accelerated between 2019 and 2024, while the remaining states demonstrated deceleration in their rates after their respective inflection points.

Discussion

The prevalence of pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM increased significantly across U.S. census regions between 2016 and 2024. There was substantial variation in prevalence by U.S. region and state, with the highest prevalence for both observed in the Midwest.

This study builds upon prior research demonstrating increasing rates and regional variation in pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM [4]. We newly identify inflection points in trends in pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM prevalence across states using the most up-to-date national data. These data revealed trends in individual US states, including accelerating rates of pre-pregnancy diabetes in Hawaii and Florida, as well as accelerating rates of GDM in New Mexico. Many states had inflection points between 2019–2021, during the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have played in a role in access to care. Identifying these state-based differences can allow state public health departments to tailor interventions to prevent and manage pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM.

This study is strengthened by use of national level data including all live births in the U.S. Limitations include potential underreporting of diagnoses of pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM and from birth certificates. However, birth certificate data have been found to have moderate sensitivity (70.5%) and high specificity (99.3%) for diagnoses of GDM, and there is no reason to believe reporting has changed over time [6]. In addition, data were not available on lifestyle behaviors that may influence diabetes prevalence. Future studies are needed to explore the social, environmental, and policy-level factors that contribute to state-level differences in diabetes prevalence in US regions and states.

Substantial variation in the prevalence of pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM have persisted among US regions and states. Additional work is needed to identify factors contributing to state-level differences in pre-pregnancy diabetes and GDM prevalence to guide state-based policy changes that promote maternal cardiometabolic health equity across US regions.
