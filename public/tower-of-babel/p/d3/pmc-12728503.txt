An Analysis of the Factors Influencing Forehead Soft-Tissue Thickness: An Ultrasound Study
Authors: Jun Zhuang, Li Yuan, Zhiyao Lin, Tongtong Wu, Xueshang Su, Liya Jiang, Ying Jia, Jintian Hu, Hongli Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA detailed understanding of the anatomy of the soft tissue layers of the forehead can help guide clinical treatment, but to date there is a lack of large-scale and comprehensive research in this area.ObjectivesThe aim of the authors of this study is to evaluate the effects of age, gender, BMI, and other factors on the thickness of the soft tissue layers of the forehead.MethodsA total of 238 volunteers were examined using ultrasonography, and the thickness of each forehead layer was measured. The effects of age, gender, BMI, and region on the thickness of each layer of the forehead were statistically analyzed using an analysis of variance.ResultsIn men, the thickness of the skin, superficial subcutaneous tissue (SST), and frontalis muscle were significantly greater than those in women (skin: 0.95 vs 0.73 mm; SST: 0.75 vs 0.67 mm; frontalis muscle: 0.35 vs 0.25 mm), whereas the thickness of the deep subcutaneous tissue (DST) was significantly thicker in women than in men (1.26 vs 1.42 mm). An increased BMI was associated with increased thickness of the skin, SST, and DST. In addition, as age increases, the thickness of SST also increases.ConclusionsFactors, such as gender, age, BMI, and the left vs right sides, all affect the thickness of each forehead tissue layer. These findings can provide a treatment reference for the forehead region.Level of Evidence: 3 (Therapeutic)

The forehead has several anatomical layers mainly composed of the skin, subcutaneous tissue, frontalis muscle, fat tissue behind the frontalis muscle, and periosteum, which together produce facial expressions.1-3 Frontal wrinkles are a typical sign of forehead aging; currently, the mainstream view is that the formation of forehead wrinkles is related to frontalis muscle movement, skin aging, and collagen loss.3-5 However, no research has clarified the specific relationship between aging and the anatomical layers of the forehead. At present, the treatment of forehead wrinkles mainly involves the injection of botulinum toxin or filling with hyaluronic acid; however, increasing our understanding of the frontal anatomy could help us provide better treatments.2,6-9 Anatomical research was the mainstream method of exploring the frontal anatomy in the past, but owing to the difficulty in obtaining specimens and the high requirements for operators, such research is difficult to perform on a large scale.6,10-12

In contrast, ultrasonic examination is more suitable for measuring the thickness of forehead tissues in a high number of samples. In previous studies, ultrasound has been utilized to assess and measure the tissue thickness of the forehead layers. Many researchers, such as Choi et al, Phumyoo et al, Kehrer et al, and Lee et al, have begun to explore the anatomical structure of facial and neck tissues through ultrasonography.9,13-15 However, these studies often focus on local layers of the forehead (such as the frontalis and skin) and do not measure the thickness of all tissues in the forehead.16,17 Additionally, these studies included a small number of patients, making it difficult to assess the impact of different factors on the various layers of forehead. In this study, we will include more participants and conduct comprehensive measurements of the frontal tissue to deepen our understanding of frontal tissue and clarify the influencing factors of frontal tissue. Understanding the anatomical structure of the forehead can help us develop more personalized treatment plans for patients, such as clarifying the injection range and levels of botulinum toxin and hyaluronic acid, determining whether the injection dose of botulinum toxin should be adjusted according to factors such as gender and age in different populations, and understanding the factors of forehead aging, thereby helping us improve the effectiveness and safety of forehead wrinkle treatment.

In this study, we sought to perform comprehensive measurements of the forehead tissue layers in a larger cohort of patients than has previously been reported, to deepen our understanding of the forehead anatomy and to help determine whether any individual variables (eg, age and gender) influence the soft tissue layers of the forehead.

METHODS

Participant Selection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Plastic Surgery Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences Plastic Surgery Hospital (Beijing, China), the patients gave informed consent, and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki were followed. All participants were informed and agreed to this study. This study recruited patients through in-hospital promotion. The inclusion criteria for this study were voluntary participation in the research and the ability to cooperate with ultrasound examinations. The exclusion criteria for this study were: a history of any previous forehead treatments (such as fat grafting, hyaluronic acid filler injections, botulinum toxin therapy, or implant placement), congenital malformations (such as cleft lip and palate, microtia, facial nerve paralysis, craniofacial deformities, etc), and inability to cooperate with ultrasound examinations.

Ultrasonic Examination

To evenly divide the forehead into distinct regions, we utilized the superior border of the eyebrow arch and the frontal lobe eminence, along with their midline, as horizontal references. Vertical lines were drawn corresponding to the inner canthus, pupil, and outer canthus. This method partitioned the forehead into a total of 24 areas (12 on each side, designated as L1-12 and R1-12), the thickness of each forehead layer was measured in these 24 areas (Figure 1) using the Diagnostic Ultrasound Scanner (GE LOGIQ-E9, Wauwatosa, WI). A single radiologist (with subspecialty training in ultrasound) performed all examinations using a 15 MHz frequency ultrasound probe. In this study, we divided the forehead above the frontal bone into 6 layers using high-frequency ultrasound (Figure 2). During the measurement process, we instructed the participants to lie flat, close their eyes, and relax their forehead. The ultrasound probe was placed horizontally in the middle of each area to record the ultrasound images. In addition, we applied a large amount of gel to the forehead during the measurement to reduce the measurement error caused by the compression of the ultrasonic probe on the forehead. To identify the location of the lateral edge of the frontalis muscle, we utilized a previously reported technique to measure the distance between its lateral edge and the superior temporal line (STL; Figure 3).9

Research Design

The patients were divided into 4 groups based on BMI (weight [kg]/(height [m]2): Group I: BMI <18.5 kg/m2, Group II: 18.5 to 23.9 kg/m2, and Group III: 24.0 to 27.9 kg/m2, Group IV: BMI ≥28 kg/m2. In addition, we divided the patients into 6 groups based on age: 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 to 49, 50 to 59, and 60 to 69 years.

Statistical Analysis

Because of the large sample size, we utilized analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the impact of factors, such as gender, age, and BMI on forehead tissue. In addition to exploring the effects of different regions on the thickness of the forehead soft tissue layers, when exploring the effects of other factors (eg, BMI, age, and gender) on forehead tissues, we took the average of the 24 regions to perform additional statistical assessments. To control the overall Type I error rate, the author of this study utilized the Bonferroni correction method for multiple comparisons (assessing the effects of age and BMI on forehead). In addition, we constructed univariate linear regression models as well as multivariate linear regression models to assess the effects of age and BMI on forehead thickness. R2 > 0.5 is considered to indicate good predictive performance of the linear regression model. All calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). P ≤ .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The Layer of the Forehead

Based on ultrasonic detection, we divided the forehead into the following layers for measurement: skin layer, superficial subcutaneous tissue (SST) layer, DST layer, suprafrontal fascia, frontalis muscle, and connective tissue behind the frontalis muscle (CTFM) layer (Figure 2).

The Impact of Gender on Forehead Soft Tissue Thickness

In the measurement of male and female foreheads, we found that male skin, SST, frontalis muscle, and CTFM were significantly thicker than female (skin: 0.96 vs 0.73 mm, P < .001; SST: 0.75 vs 0.67 mm, P < .001; frontalis muscle: 0.35 vs 0.25 mm, P < .001; CTFM: 0.56 vs 0.38 mm, P < .001), whereas the thickness of the DST was significantly thicker in women than in men (1.26 vs 1.42 mm, P = .011; Figure 4).

The Impact of Age on Forehead Soft Tissue Thickness

Through univariate linear regression analysis, we found that there was no significant relationship between skin thickness and age (P = .391). Age was significantly positively correlated with SST thickness (β = .031, P < .001), but there was no significant relationship between age and DST thickness (P = .349). There was also no significant relationship between age and frontalis muscle thickness (P = .124), whereas age showed a significant negative correlation with CTFM thickness (β = −.011, P = .034). In multiple linear regression analysis, skin thickness was significantly positively correlated with age (β = .047, P = .003), and the positive correlation between age and SST thickness was even stronger (β = .061, P < .001). Age showed a significant positive correlation with DST thickness (β = .315, P < .001), and there was no significant relationship between age and frontalis muscle thickness (P = .351). Finally, age was significantly positively correlated with CTFM thickness (β = .047, P < .001; Tables 2, 3). However, linear regression analysis found no association between age and other levels (skin, DST, frontalis muscle, and CTFM; Figure 5, Supplemental Table 1). Because the coefficient of determination (R2) in this study is <0.5, we divided the patients into different age groups and conducted a secondary analysis using ANOVA. One patient was over 70 years old and, therefore, was excluded from the age group analysis, resulting in an analysis cohort of 237 patients. The values of SST, thickness of the 6 groups of 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 to 49, 50 to 59, and 60 to 69 years old, were 0.61, 0.70, 0.68, 0.72, 0.75, and 0.78 mm (P < .001), respectively.

The Impact of BMI on Forehead Soft Tissue Thickness

Through univariate linear regression analysis, we found that BMI was significantly positively correlated with skin thickness (β = .044, P = .002), significantly positively correlated with SST thickness (β = .076, P < .001), and significantly positively correlated with DST thickness (β = .279, P < .001). There was no significant relationship between BMI and frontalis muscle thickness (P = .149), but BMI was significantly positively correlated with CTFM thickness (β = .031, P = .005). In multiple linear regression analysis, BMI showed no significant relationship with skin thickness (P = .71). BMI was significantly positively correlated with SST thickness (β = .019, P < .001) and significantly negatively correlated with DST thickness (β = −.044, P = .01). There was no significant relationship between BMI and frontalis muscle thickness (P = .293), and BMI was significantly negatively correlated with CTFM thickness (β = −.020, P < .001) (Tables 2, 3). Because the coefficient of determination (R2) in this study is <0.5, we divided the patients into different BMI groups and conducted a secondary analysis using ANOVA. With an increase in BMI, the thickness of the skin, SST, and DST increased (Figure 6, Supplemental Table 2). We divided the participants into 4 BMI groups. The average thickness of the skin in all areas of these 4 groups (I-IV) was 0.77, 0.80, 0.83, and 0.91 mm, respectively (P = .016). The average thickness of the SST in all areas of the forehead of the 4 groups was 0.60, 0.67, 0.76, and 0.82 mm, respectively (P < .001). The average thickness of the DST in all areas of the forehead in the 4 groups was 1.07, 1.22, 1.49, and 1.88 mm, respectively (P < .001). The average thickness of the CTFM in all areas of the 4 groups was 0.42, 0.43, 0.47, and 0.51 mm, respectively (P = .039). The thickness of the frontalis muscles in all areas of the 4 groups was 0.28, 0.28, 0.30, and 0.30 mm (P = .536), respectively.

Comparison of Tissue Layer Thickness Between the Left and Right Foreheads

We detected the frontalis muscle boundary in Plane 1 (Figure 1); we found that 14 (6.1%) of the left lateral borders of the frontalis muscles were within the STL (4.4 mm on average), 22 (9.6%) were on the STL, and 192 (84.2%) were beyond the STL (12.3 mm on average). Nineteen (8.3%) of the right frontalis muscles were within the superior temporal line (4.5 mm on average), 17 (7.5%) were on the superior temporal line, and 192 (84.2%) were beyond the superior temporal line (11.1 mm on average). Among the 228 volunteers, 128 (56.1%) had a greater distance between the lateral border of the left frontalis muscle and the STL than that of the right, 95 (41.7%) had a greater distance between the lateral border of the right frontalis muscle and the STL than that of the left, and this distance did not differ in 5 (2.2%).

The upper edge of the frontalis muscles of some individuals can be detected at L/R1/2/3/4/5/6. Because of the fact that the upper edge of the frontalis muscles of some patients exceeded the hairline, the authors of this study only measured the upper edge of the frontalis muscles of patients whose measurement area could determine the upper edge of the frontalis muscles. In the measurement of the upper edge of the frontalis muscle of 95 volunteers, we found that the upper edge of the right frontalis muscle was higher in 34 volunteers at L/R123, that of the left and right frontalis muscles was equal in 2, and that of the left frontalis muscle was higher in 59. In Area 4/5/6, 26 volunteers had a higher upper edge of the right frontalis muscle, 1 had the same upper edge of the left and right frontalis muscles, and 68 had a higher upper edge of the left frontalis muscle.

There were certain differences in the thicknesses of various areas within the same plane, and the thickness differences of each area are shown in Figure 7. For the left and right tissues, the thickness of the frontalis muscle at L6 was significantly thicker than that at R6 (0.33 vs 0.27 mm, P < .001), and the CTFM at L6, L8, L9, and L12 was also significantly thicker than that at R6, R8, R9, and R12 (P = .024, P < .001, P = .007, P < .001, respectively). There were no significant differences between the left and right foreheads in the other regions.

DISCUSSION

Ultrasonography is a noninvasive examination method that is widely utilized in clinical practice.18-21 The use of high-frequency ultrasound can help us identify subcutaneous soft tissue and muscle structures in the patient's face and neck. Ingallina and Bravo have found that the anatomical layers of the forehead can be clearly displayed using ultrasonography and that the anatomical layers of the forehead can be evaluated using ultrasonography and autopsies.2,22 We did not measure the thickness of the fascia because they were too thin. In addition, researchers have found through anatomical research and computed tomography that there are 3 compartments in the superficial and middle layers of the forehead, respectively.6 Unfortunately, in this study, we found that these compartments were difficult to identify using high-frequency ultrasound; thus, we did not describe them. In this study, we divided the forehead above the frontal bone into 6 layers using high-frequency ultrasound (Figure 7). Our results revealed that the thickness of the forehead layer is affected by gender, age, BMI, and region.

Frontal wrinkles are a sign of aging. Previous studies have suggested that skin thickness decreases with age.23,24 Some scholars have also suggested that the thickness of the dermis will increase significantly before the age of 20 years and will gradually become thinner afterwards.25,26 In a previous study, the authors investigated the factors that affect the histological thickness of the forehead and found that it is not affected by age.2 However, in this study, we discovered that when the measurement area is confined to the forehead, only the thickness of the SST increases with age, particularly after the age of 40. In contrast, the thickness of other layers of forehead tissue does not significantly change with age, a finding that has not been reported in previous studies. This insight may help us uncover the true causes of forehead aging, thereby enabling us to address the root of forehead aging in treatments.

In our study, an increase in BMI was associated with an increase in thickness of the skin, SST, CTFM, and DST. The thickening of the SST, CTFM, and DST is consistent with the nutritional status of facial soft tissue and the human body in previous studies, but there was no significant difference in the thickness of the frontalis muscle in various BMI groups.2 In clinical treatment, for patients with higher BMI values, we can appropriately increase the depth of injection therapy to make the injection level more accurate. In addition, for patients with a lower BMI, the tissue above the frontalis muscle is thinner, and when using fillers to treat the static frontal striae, the dose of subcutaneous fillers may need to be increased, whereas the dose of subcutaneous fillers may need to be reduced for patients with a higher BMI to prevent frontal swelling.

Bravo et al and Choi et al found that there was no significant difference in frontalis muscle thickness between men and women.2,13 However, in our study, we found that male skin, SST, frontalis muscle, and CTFM were significantly thicker than those of women, whereas the DST was significantly greater in women than in men. Having thicker frontalis muscles may explain why forehead wrinkles are more common in men. Therefore, when using botulinum toxin to treat frontal striae, increasing the dosage of botulinum toxin in male patients may have a more obvious effect. Male skin being significantly thicker than female skin is consistent with previous research.2,13,24,25,27 In addition, the thickness of the frontalis muscle in the left part of the region was significantly thicker than that of the frontalis muscle on the right side, which may be related to the fact that more people show right eyebrow tail ptosis. Moreover, the left frontalis muscle was significantly thicker at L6 than at R6. We speculate that this may be related to increased activity of the left frontalis muscle. Additionally, in the CTFM, we found that Plane 1 (the area between Line 1 and Line 2) was significantly thicker than Plane 2 (the area between Line 2 and Line 3) and Plane 3 (the areas above Line 3). We speculate that this may be because of the fact that the tissues in Plane 1 are closer to the periorbital tissues, resulting in more active tissue movement.

Based on these results, we believe that using high-frequency ultrasound can help us understand the anatomical structure of the forehead and its influencing factors. Understanding the anatomy of the forehead can help guide clinical treatment. The main minimally invasive cosmetic treatment methods for the forehead include injection of botulinum toxin, filling with hyaluronic acid, the application of polydioxanone (PDO) threads, and micro-focused ultrasound. In order to avoid the injection of fillers into the supratrochlear and supraorbital vessels, the current mainstream view suggests that the injection level of frontal fillers should mainly be above the periosteum rather than at superficial frontal regions. Fixing the PDO line between the dermis and frontalis muscles can achieve the best lifting effect. Larger muscles often require additional doses of botulinum toxin to achieve the same effect.2,28 In this study, we investigated the effects of factors such as age, BMI, and gender on the frontal anatomy. Understanding the thickness of different anatomical layers of the forehead can help us choose the appropriate treatment depth.

Limitations

Firstly, it only included Chinese participants and recruited them within the same hospital, which may lead to recruitment bias. Therefore, the representativeness of the research results may be limited by regional populations. Secondly, as this study included adolescents as young as 10 years old, comparing their frontal parameters directly with adults may have an impact on the interpretation of the results. On a technical level, because of the small size of the blood vessels and nerve structures in the forehead, it is difficult to clearly identify them during ultrasound examination. Therefore, in this study, the authors did not measure their anatomic course, and the data in this study were all measured by the same ultrasound specialist, which may result in measurement bias. In addition, because of the influence of the hairline, the authors of this study did not provide a precise definition of the outer edge of the outermost region, which may lead to measurement bias. What's more, the research design did not fully consider the potential impact of confounding factors such as sunlight exposure intensity, dominant hand, and pathological obesity on the measurement of forehead tissue. Last but not least, there is a risk of measurement error with a single radiologist taking measurements, given that small differences in marker placement with thin tissues can greatly impact the results.

CONCLUSIONS

In this cohort of 238 Chinese volunteers, the skin, SST, frontalis muscle, and CTFM of males were significantly thicker than those of females, whereas the thickness of the DST was significantly greater in women. The skin, SST, and DST thickened with an increase in BMI, and the SST thickened significantly with age. The left frontalis muscle and CTFM were thicker in some areas than the right frontalis muscle. By measuring the thickness of each level of the forehead, we can better understand the factors influencing the thickness of the forehead and provide information for follow-up treatment.
