Auto-arrange buildings in urban planning with DQN
Authors: Panzhong Lin, Guofeng Shi, Chenchun Hu, Jie Zhang, Yanjia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we propose a two-stage Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework for the automatic arrangement of buildings in urban planning, with a focus on satisfying critical regulatory constraints: sunshine duration and inter-building distance. To overcome the computational bottleneck of high-fidelity sunshine simulation during DRL training, we first introduce a Convolutional Auto-Encoder (CAE) model that serves as a fast and accurate surrogate for predicting sunshine distribution across a building layout. This CAE model, trained on data generated from Autodesk Revit, achieves near real-time prediction (∼0.015 s per layout) with an average error of less than 3 min, enabling its seamless integration into the iterative RL loop. Subsequently, we formulate the sequential placement of buildings as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and employ a Deep Q-Network (DQN) to learn the optimal placement policy. The DQN agent interacts with a planning environment, where its actions (moving a building in discrete directions) are rewarded based on the satisfaction of distance constraints and the avoidance of low-sunshine zones identified by the CAE. Our integrated CAE-DQN system automates the generation of compliant building layouts. Experimental evaluation demonstrates that our method significantly outperforms baseline approaches (Random Placement, Greedy Heuristic, and Genetic Algorithm) in terms of success rate (100%), convergence speed, and layout compactness. The proposed framework not only ensures hard constraint satisfaction but also provides a practical and scalable tool for rapid prototype generation in the conceptual design phase of urban planning.Keywords: Auto-arrange buildings, Sunshine duration and distance requirements, Generative model, Reinforcement learningSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, urbanization is occurring in many places around the world1. Especially in China, people from rural areas are immigrating to urban cities to enjoy a better life. Numerous high-rise buildings have been established and thus increase the intensity in mega cities such as Beijing2. Unfortunately, China has a large population and the price of land is rising significantly, building layout tends to be compact: buildings in cities such as Shanghai, Beijing etc. are higher and the space between buildings is becoming narrower. Requirements of large space and adequate sunshine often conflict with the needs of increasing building density due to the land usage efficiency. Chinese national and local governments of different cities have formulated regulations ensuring the minimum number of hours of sunshine duration on a certain area of a land as well as the permitted minimum distance between buildings, which needs to be satisfied when planning building layout on a given piece of land3.

In this work, we consider building layout planning as a process of arranging buildings to proper positions while satisfying sunshine duration and distance requirements. Currently, designers plan building layout with CAD software such as Tianzhen, Autodesk Revit, Grasshopper4 etc. Satisfactory results require expert knowledge and numerous trial-error experiments. We aim at automating the design process, where we train a reinforcement learning model to learn from the trial-error experiments through properly designed reward functions. After training, the model would be able to arrange buildings automatically. In literature, building layout planning can be affected by factors such as distance between buildings (Erell et al., 2010), ventilation5, air quality and humidity6, solar radiation and sunshine duration7etc. In this work, we consider only the sunshine duration and distance requirements, which are the hard requirements need to be satisfied in urban planning in China89.,

While prior studies have applied optimization algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms, simulated annealing) to urban layout problems, and a few have begun exploring Reinforcement Learning (RL) in related design domains, the sequential decision-making nature of building placement under hard regulatory constraints remains under-explored in an RL framework. Existing RL applications in urban contexts often focus on traffic flow or land use classification, rather than fine-grained, constraint-satisfying geometric arrangement. Our work distinctively formulates the building auto-arrangement as a sequential decision process solved via Deep Q-Networks (DQN), integrated with a fast generative model (CAE) for constraint evaluation. This two-stage approach—fast sunshine prediction followed by RL-based sequential placement—represents a novel integration aimed at practical design assistance.

To incorporate sunshine duration computation into the training process of reinforcement learning, we first propose a generative model to directly predict sunshine duration for a given building layout with low computational cost and high accuracy, instead of the complex modelling and solving process in CAD software used by current designers. After that, we present an automatic tool capable of arranging buildings satisfying sunshine duration and distance constraints based on reinforcement learning. We believe this tool can greatly assist designers to create new ideas and process large amount of building layouts planning in their daily work.

The proposed auto-arrangement tool is primarily positioned for conceptual massing design and preliminary compliance checking in urban residential planning. At this stage, designers need to rapidly generate multiple layout alternatives that satisfy fundamental regulatory constraints (such as sunshine and fire separation) as a basis for further detailed design. Our tool automates this trial-and-error process, producing compliant building layout prototypes that can assist designers in scheme comparison and feasibility analysis.

We assess the effectiveness of our proposed method through experiments. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to present a reinforcement learning framework to learn the policy of arranging buildings satisfying sunshine duration and distance requirements.

Related works

Xu4, use urban block type and street width as input, define the percentage of acceptable universal thermal comfort index (UTCI) range as the objective and propose to use genetic algorithm to optimize and generate an urban block by maximizing the percentage of acceptable UTCI at urban block level and street level. Chen et al.10, combine simulated annealing algorithm with a microclimate model to determine the optimal layout that provides the most comfortable environment. Hong and Lin11, utilize the Simulation Platform for Outdoor Thermal Environment (SPOTE) to investigate the optimal building layout patterns and tree arrangements, resulting in improved outdoor wind conditions and thermal comfort at the pedestrian level. Hsieh12, use alternative layout planning scenarios based on the factors such as surface temperature of land and buildings, air temperature, wind distribution etc. to assess the influence of building arrangement, Ooka et al.13, examine the optimal tree placement for designing a comfortable outdoor environment using a multi-objective genetic algorithm combined with simulations of convection, radiation, and conduction. Hong and Lin2, use SPOTE to study the thermal environment and pedestrian thermal comfort of the occupants in the open space with different patterns of the building and green space. Yang and Lu14, optimize the sunshine environment by adjusting the building orientation, organization of building layout, architecture of buildings and design strategies. Groenewegen et al.15, propose to create a reasonable urban layout at a high-level, where the input are city size, location and historic background. The resulting layouts consist of different kinds of city districts which are arranged using constraints derived from established models of urban land use. Yang et al.16, present a framework for generating street networks and parcel layouts, which is based on hierarchical domain splitting using two splitting types: streamline-based splitting and template-based splitting.

Most of the work in literature uses optimization methods to solve building layout problem. However, the problem can be treated as sequential decision process: designers need to decide where to place different buildings at different time step. Environment changes as more buildings are placed and the policy of arranging buildings need to be learned through interacting with the changing environment. Recently, reinforcement learning has shown promise in sequential design tasks. However, its application to automatic building layout under precise regulatory constraints remains novel. Our work bridges this gap by formulating the arrangement as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and employing DQN to learn the placement policy.

Background and problem definition

Sunshine duration requirements

The Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the People’s Republic of China (MOHURD) has published a series of national standards, such as Standard for Urban Residential Areas Planning and Design (GB 50180 − 2018) and Standard for Assessment Parameters of Sunlight on Building (GB/T 50947 − 2014). With these standards, the MOHURD has clearly prescribed requirements for the standard of available sunlight for buildings design and urban planning. In clause 4.0.9 of Standard for Urban Residential Areas Planning and Design (GB 50180 − 2018), the national planning regulation in China, the position for sunshine assessment is windowsill at ground floor (or the external façade with 0.9 m height above the indoor floor).

In this work, according to the Table 2 of Xu and Yuan17, we take the Beijing city on the Great Cold Day as the study case, which requires that the sunshine duration be no less than 2 h per day. Moreover, since Beijing located in the northern hemisphere, south side of a building could get much more sunshine than the north side in winter. Most of the time, designers would prefer to set direction of windowsill on the first floor to the south so as to obtain more sunshine in winter. Therefore, we assume that windowsills of a building on the ground floor are set to the south as default in this work.

Building distance requirements

In China, there are many regulations on the distance requirements of buildings. Requirements vary depending on the location and situation. In this work, we adhere to the “Code for Fire Protection Design of Buildings” [GB50016-2014(2018)]: the minimum distance between two high-rise buildings should be no less than 13 m.

Mathematical formulation of constraints

Let Lbe a given land parcel. For any building Bi placed at position pi with dimensions wi, li, hi, we define:

Sunshine Constraint: For the designated windowsill point qi (south-facing, ground floor) of Bi, the total sunshine duration S(qi) on the Great Cold Day must satisfy S(qi)≥Tmin, where Tmin=120 min.

Sunshine Constraint: For the designated windowsill point qi (south-facing, ground floor) of Bi, the total sunshine duration S(qi) on the Great Cold Day must satisfy S(qi)≥Tmin, where Tmin=120 min.

Distance Constraint: For any two distinct buildings Bi and Bj, the shortest 2D horizontal distance between their footprints, d(Bi, Bj), must satisfy d(Bi, Bj)≥Dmin, where Dmin=13 m.

Distance Constraint: For any two distinct buildings Bi and Bj, the shortest 2D horizontal distance between their footprints, d(Bi, Bj), must satisfy d(Bi, Bj)≥Dmin, where Dmin=13 m.

Additionally, all buildings must be placed within the boundaries of L, i.e., pi∈L.

The target

Given a set of n buildings B={B1,B2,…,Bn}, each with known dimensions, and a piece of land L, the target is to find an arrangement P={p1,p2,…,pn}such that all sunshine and distance constraints are satisfied simultaneously (Fig. 1).

Arrangement of buildings on a given piece of land.

This study focuses on two core hard constraints—sunshine duration and building spacing—while excluding other common planning factors such as floor area ratio (FAR), building setback, and orientation. This simplification allows the model to rapidly generate compliant layouts at the conceptual design stage, but may affect the volumetric efficiency and morphological diversity of the solutions. In practice, the output of our tool can serve as a base layout that designers can further refine to meet a broader set of design objectives.

Generative model for sunshine duration prediction

Generative models such as variational auto-encoders18 are often used to learn a function to approximate the distribution of the training data. In this work, we propose to use convolution auto-encoder (CAE) to map the input data: building layout (Fig. 4, Left) to the output data: sunshine duration (Fig. 4, Right).

(1) The CAE network architecture: The CAE model is a fully convolution network19, 20, 21. The network model architecture as shown in Fig. 5. Input to the network is a building layout image (Fig. 4, Left) while the output is the corresponding sunshine duration image (Fig. 4, Right). The model is composed of an encoder and a decoder connected by skip connections21. It connects feature maps of shallow layers with those of deep layers. (2) Encoder: After the impressive performance of AlexNet22 at the LSVRC2012 classification contest, deep Residual Network23 is widely considered one of the most revolutionary advancements in the computer vision and deep learning fields in recent years. According to the universal approximation theorem, given enough capacity, a feedforward network with a single layer is sufficient to represent any function. However, the layer might be massive and the network is prone to overfitting the data. Therefore, there is a common trend in the research community that the network architecture needs to go deeper. Increasing network depth does not work by simply stacking layers together. Deep networks are hard to train because of the notorious vanishing gradient problem - as the gradient is back-propagated to earlier layers, repeated multiplication may make the gradient infinitively small. As a result, as the network goes deeper, its performance gets saturated or even starts degrading rapidly. ResNet enables the training of hundreds, or even thousands, of layers while maintaining impressive performance. The core idea of ResNet is introducing a so-called “identity or projection shortcut connection” that skips one or more layers, as shown in the Fig. 7. Detailed explanation of every operation of each layer can be found in23.

Shortcut for ResNet.

Our encoder uses the Resnet-34 as the backbone network. We down-sample the feature map with convolution operations setting the stride is 2, which effectively increases the receptive field of the encoder and facilitates the extraction of global features. However, details of features continue to lose with the increasing of globality of the features, as well as weakening the spatial correlation between the features20. To retain more feature details, we add layers of atrous convolution (A. Conv)24 to the Resnet-34 encoder model. Atrous convolution increases the receptive field by setting the dilatation rate24,25 of the convolution kernel. Also, during the convolution, the feature maps are zero-padded to maintain its size. Therefore, size of the feature maps is unchanged but its receptive field increases. In this study, the atrous convolution dilatation rate is 2. All of kernel sizes, number of channels, and strides are shown in the Fig. 5.

(3) Decoder: The right part of Fig. 5 is a decoder network, which aims at restructuring the down-sampling features to be the same size as the one of the input. It consists of convolutional layers and transposed convolutional (T. Conv)26 layers. The kernel in the initial layers has a size of 3 × 3 with a stride of 1, whereas in the later layers, the kernel is 4 × 4 with a stride of 2. The transposed convolutional layers perform an inverse convolution operation by up-sampling the feature maps, the size of which will be doubled. After all the convolution and transposed convolution operations, the final output size of the decoder part should be the same as the input size of the encoder part.

Error analysis in regulatory context and impact on RL

Although the average prediction error is around 3 min, the error distribution in regions near the regulatory threshold (e.g., 2 h) remains within ± 2 min. Considering that actual sunshine assessment typically allows a certain margin for measurement and calculation tolerance (e.g., ± 5 min), the prediction accuracy of our model is sufficient for preliminary compliance judgment and is unlikely to cause misjudgment due to minor errors. To assess the impact of CAE prediction errors on the RL agent’s decisions, we conducted an ablation study. We compared the final building layouts generated by our full pipeline (using CAE-predicted sunshine) against layouts generated using ground-truth sunshine values from Revit simulations (as an oracle). The results showed that in over 95% of test cases, the same final compliant layout was reached. In the remaining cases, minor positional differences (< 5% of land width) were observed, but all layouts still satisfied both constraints. This demonstrates the robustness of our sequential RL policy to the small, unbiased errors introduced by the CAE.

Integrating the CAE surrogate model into the RL training loop reduces the time for a single sunshine evaluation from ~ 1.5 s (Revit simulation) to ~ 0.015 s (CAE inference). For a typical RL training run involving millions of state evaluations, this translates to a reduction in total training time from an estimated several weeks (using direct simulation) to under 48 h, making the approach practically feasible.

In short, the CAE model enables to predict sunshine duration with high accuracy but is 100 times faster than the method currently used. In the next section, we will use it to compute the sunshine duration in the training process of the reinforcement learning model.

This section describes our main approach for auto-arranging buildings. The main idea is to auto-arrange buildings to proper positions predicted by the reinforcement learning model while satisfying sunshine duration and distance requirements.

DQN.

(4) Auto-arrangement with DQN: We formulate the building arrangement as a sequential decision-making problem under uncertainty, where the agent must decide where to place the next building given the current partial layout. This is naturally modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Deep Q-Network (DQN) is chosen for several reasons: (i) It is well-suited for problems with discrete action spaces (four movement directions), (ii) it can handle the high-dimensional state representation (building positions on a grid) through the use of function approximation (neural networks), and (iii) its off-policy nature allows for efficient experience replay, which is crucial for learning from sparse rewards in a complex environment. While policy gradient methods are an alternative, DQN offers a stable and sample-efficient approach for our discrete action setting.

The decision process is partially observable and the agent receives observations o from the set of observations O. When both sunshine and distance constraints are involved, the model follows a “sunshine-first” principle embedded in the state representation: the sunshine affection area (where duration <2 h) is computed first and forms a “forbidden zone” for the south-facing windowsill. The agent learns to avoid placing the building where its windowsill falls in this zone. If a potential position satisfies sunshine but violates distance, the reward function provides a negative signal, guiding the agent to explore other actions. This design ensures that the policy prioritizes the hard sunshine constraint while optimizing for distance.

Under the state st, mis the minimum distance between the building to be placed and the buildings already been placed or the boundaries, σ is the distance requirement. The reward function encourages minimum distance satisfaction, discourages distance that is too large and punish those that are negative e.g. m < 0 (buildings overlap with each other or overlap with boundaries).

The distance requirement σ = 13 m in our reward function is based on Chinese fire protection codes for high-rise buildings. The function structure is general: σ can be adjusted to match spacing regulations in other countries or regions; the time threshold in the sunshine affection area calculation can also be modified to suit sunshine standards in different climatic zones. This makes the framework adaptable to different regulatory contexts. Furthermore, to test generalization across land shapes and sizes, we trained the model on a dataset with rectangular lands of varying proportions and tested it on irregularly shaped parcels (simulated by masking parts of a rectangular land). The agent successfully adapted by treating masked areas as obstacles, demonstrating promising generalization capability. Scaling to significantly larger areas (e.g., entire blocks) would require a hierarchical approach or a shift to multi-agent RL, which is an important direction for future work.

As it is shown in the Fig. 1, buildings are placed successively at a time: sunshine duration affection area is computed first so that the bounding box of the building to be placed is obtained (Sect. 5). The bounding box is then used to compute the minimum distance, which decides the value of the reward function. Parameters of the evaluation Q and target Q network are updated based on the value of the reward function. The process is repeated until all buildings are placed with the sunshine duration and distance requirements satisfied.

Experimental evaluation and analysis

(e) Experiments: We conduct experiments on 120 distinct land parcels derived from our dataset. These are split into 80 training lands and 40 held-out test lands to evaluate generalization. The DQN hyperparameters are: discount factor γ = 0.95, learning rate α = 0.001, batch size for experience replay = 64. Training proceeds for 200 episodes per land on the training set. We take the buildings and the corresponding land information from the dataset used in Sect. 4, where the size of the land ranges from [300,300] to [600, 600] and number of buildings ranges from 5 to 12. These information are used as the input and the target is to auto-place these buildings by training a reinforcement learning model. The configuration for computation is the same as the one used for training the CAE model (Sect. 4.1).

The current method is evaluated on land parcels up to 600 × 600 units with up to 12 buildings, which is suitable for residential block planning. Scalability to larger, more complex urban scenarios (e.g., city districts) presents challenges in state-space size and training time. Potential pathways include: (i) Hierarchical RL: Coarsely planning building clusters first, then detailing within clusters. (ii) Multi-agent RL: Treating each building as an agent in a cooperative game. (iii) Transfer Learning: Pre-training on smaller parcels and fine-tuning on larger ones. As shown in our generalization test with irregular shapes, the core methodology is adaptable. Future work will explicitly address scaling through these advanced RL techniques and by incorporating more diverse training data encompassing various urban forms and regulations.

Study limitations

While the proposed method demonstrates promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged:

Simplified Constraint Set: The current model focuses exclusively on sunshine duration and fire safety distance, which are critical but not the only factors in urban planning. Important considerations such as Floor Area Ratio (FAR), building setback regulations, wind ventilation, noise, view corridors, and infrastructure access are not integrated. This simplification limits the practical applicability of generated layouts as final designs, positioning our tool primarily as a precursor for conceptual brainstorming and preliminary compliance screening.

Geographic and Climatic Specificity: The CAE model for sunshine prediction is trained on data generated specifically for Beijing on the Great Cold Day. While the model structure is transferable, its accuracy for cities at significantly different latitudes, with distinct atmospheric conditions, or under different regulatory sunshine thresholds (e.g., 3 h in some regions) requires further validation and potentially retraining with localized data.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presented an integrated CAE-DQN framework for the automatic arrangement of buildings under stringent sunshine and distance constraints. The key innovation lies in the decoupling of the computationally expensive sunshine evaluation via a fast and accurate CAE surrogate model, which makes the subsequent sequential decision-making process tractable for DRL.

Our main contribution is a novel, integrated framework that combines a fast, data-driven sunshine predictor (CAE) with a sequential decision-making agent (DQN) to solve the building auto-arrangement problem under hard regulatory constraints. This approach addresses a gap in applying RL to fine-grained geometric planning. The CAE achieves a 100x speedup over traditional simulation, making RL training feasible, and the DQN agent learns a policy that outperforms standard baselines in success rate and efficiency. The framework is designed for the conceptual design stage, providing planners with rapid, compliant layout prototypes, thereby enhancing productivity and exploration of design alternatives.

This work leads to several perspectives. First, we consider only the sunshine duration and distance requirements when arranging buildings. Requirements like outdoor air flow ventilation, orientation of buildings on the indoor lighting etc. have not been considered. They should be considered so that the final arrangement of buildings can mostly satisfy living standard. Second, our results are readily usable for automatic production and it would be interesting to explore a collaborative scenario where both human and machine creatives can be joint together in the design process.

Future work will focus on: (1) Incorporating Additional Constraints: Integrating soft constraints (e.g., view, privacy) and multi-objective optimization (e.g., balancing sunshine with density). (2) Enhanced Human-in-the-Loop Interaction: Developing an interactive interface where designers can adjust constraints, set preferences, and iteratively refine AI-generated layouts. (3) Advanced RL for Scalability: Exploring hierarchical RL, multi-agent RL, or graph-based representations to handle larger-scale urban planning problems. (4) Cross-City Generalization: Training and evaluating the model on datasets from cities with different climates and regulations to build a more robust and universally applicable tool.
