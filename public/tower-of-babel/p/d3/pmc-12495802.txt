Comparative chloroplast genomes of Sophora species: identification of variable DNA markers and phylogenetic relationships within the genus
Authors: Na Duan, Dafu Ru, Bingbing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroudSophora L. is a medicinally significant genus within Papilionoideae (Fabaceae), renowned for its medicinal, ecological, and economic importance. However, taxonomic and phylogenetic uncertainties persist within this genus, especially in China, due to limited genetic data and the lack of effective molecular markers. This study aimed to address these limitations by sequencing and analyzing chloroplast (cp.) genomes from six Sophora species endemic to China, thereby enriching genetic resources for future research.ResultHere, we reported and characterized six chloroplast (cp.) genomes of Sophora species (Sophora xanthantha, Sophora davidii, Sophora moocroftiana, Sophora tonkinensis, Sophora velutina, and Sophora velutina var. cavaleriei) from China, sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform. Comparative analyses of these newly generated cp. genomes, alongside previously published Sophora cp. genomes, revealed that the Sophora cp. genomes exhibited a size range of 151,270 − 154,378 bp (excluding S. japonica and S. xanthantha). We observed expansions of the inverted repeat (IR) regions in Sophora, leading to the translocation of rpl2 into the large single-copy (LSC) region, pseudogenization of ycf1, and absence of trnH in some species. Comparative genome analysis revealed a ~ 24-kb inversion between trnC-GCA and trnF-GAA within the genus, a phenomenon first observed to involve at least three rearrangements. Furthermore, we identified three valuable molecular markers (trnT-UGU - trnL-UAA, psbE - petL, and rps11 - rpl36) that are suitable for phylogenetic inference within Sophora.ConclusionsPhylogenetic analyses based on the chloroplast genomes robustly supported the monophyly of sect. Pseudosophora. Importantly, our phylogenetic results suggested that S. japonica and S. xanthantha should not be classified within the genus Sophora. These findings contribute significantly to the chloroplast genomic data of Sophora and provide valuable genetic resources and molecular markers for resolving taxonomic ambiguities and advancing future phylogenetic, evolutionary, and taxonomic investigations of Sophora.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07398-6.

Background

Plant chloroplasts (cp.) are important organelles for photosynthesis and carbon fixation [1, 2]. The cp. genome in angiosperms typically exhibits a circular DNA molecule ranging from 115 to 165 kb in length and consists of one large single-copy (LSC) region, one small single-copy (SSC) region, and two copies of inverted repeat (IR) region [3–5]. This circular DNA molecule could provide useful information in plant systematics due to its maternal inheritance and high conservation in gene content and genome structure in most angiosperms [6]. Traditional methods to sequence the cp. genome rely on time-consuming and costly organelle isolation, PCR, and Sanger sequencing [7]. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies, it is now more practical and less expensive to obtain cp. genome sequences, advancing cp-based phylogenetics to phylogenomics [8, 9]. Meanwhile, there is increasing awareness of plastome rearrangements even in photosynthetic angiosperms that maintain a quadripartite structure, as seen in Orobanchaceae [10], Oleaceae [11], Campanulaceae [12, 13], and Geraniaceae [14, 15].

Fabaceae (also known as Leguminosae) is the third-largest angiosperm family after daisies (Asteraceae) and orchids (Orchidaceae) across global ecosystems, with ~ 765 genera and over 19,500 species [16]. Plastomes in this family range from 120 to 180 kb (LSC: 70–113 kb; SSC: 13–60 kb; IR: 23–42 kb) in length, with considerable variation due to expansion or contraction of the IR region, or loss of the inverted repeat, or inversions [17, 18]. For example, the tribe Ingeae exhibits IR expansion of around 13 kb toward the SSC, and some smaller plastomes that have lost the IR are known as IRLC (inverted repeat-lacking clade). The tribes Swartzieae and Sophoreae within the subfamily Papilionoideae were found to have a 50-kb inversion in the LSC region [19]. Additionally, 23-kb, 24-kb, or 36-kb inversions have also been described in various genera of the Genistoids clade [20–23].

The genus Sophora L., belonging to the tribe Sophoreae of the genistoids clade within the subfamily Papilionoideae, is a medicinally important genus comprising approximately 50–70 species, primarily distributed in tropical and temperate regions [24, 25]. Beyond its medicinal value, exemplified by its active components’ antitumor, anti-inflammatory, anti-arrhythmic, antibacterial, antitoxic, and immune-modulating properties, Sophora species also hold significant ecological and economic importance, contributing to biological nitrogen fixation, soil conservation, and ecosystem restoration [26, 27]. Morphologically, the genus is highly diverse, encompassing shrubs, subshrubs, deciduous or evergreen trees, and perennial herbs. Traditional classification and identification within the genus Sophora once relied heavily on its morphological characteristics, such as leaf morphology, petal color, and hypanthium structure [28, 29]. However, these morphological characteristics are closely related to the growth environment, rendering many Sophora species morphologically similar and challenging to distinguish. The complexity of these morphological characters has made it challenging to understand Sophora taxonomy and evolution. This inherent taxonomic ambiguity not only impedes scientific progress but also carries practical implications, potentially limiting industrial development and endangering public safety due to the confusing use of Sophora medicinal materials. To overcome these long-standing taxonomic ambiguities and the limitations of morphological approaches, molecular phylogenetic studies have provided crucial initial insights into Sophora systematics. Early molecular investigations, primarily utilizing nuclear ribosomal ITS regions and various chloroplast DNA markers (matK, psbA-trnH, and trnL-F), first revealed the polyphyletic nature of Sophora sensu lato, indicating that species traditionally placed within Sophora should be reclassified into distinct genera [30]. Despite these significant advancements, molecular studies have faced limitations in fully resolving deeper relationships and providing comprehensive phylogenetic insights. For instance, even when chloroplast and nuclear evidence generally align, low bootstrapping support is often observed for various phylogenetic nodes, suggesting complex evolutionary processes like hybridization and introgression [31]. Crucially, for Sophora species in Asia, a major center of diversity and distribution, comprehensive phylogenetic relationships remain poorly resolved [30]. Consequently, a robust and comprehensive backbone phylogeny for the genus Sophora is yet to be fully constructed, primarily due to the persistent lack of sufficient high-resolution genetic resources, particularly whole chloroplast genomes. The robust resolution of these relationships, achievable with comprehensive genomic data, is paramount for guiding Sophora taxonomy, systematics, species conservation, and sustainable resource utilization, as well as for identifying reliable genetic markers for the Sophora industry.

In this study, we reported six Sophora whole-chloroplast genomes from China, and combined these data with three previously published Sophora plastomes to produce a comprehensive analysis of 9 species. Including genomic structures and characters, identification of molecular markers, and phylogenetic inference. Our aims of this study were: i) to explore the expansion or contraction of the IR, or inversions in the single-copy regions; ii) to identify molecular markers for future phylogenetic inference within the genus Sophora; and iii) to elucidate the molecular evolution and phylogenetic relationships of Sophora species based on their chloroplast genomes.

Results

General features analysis

The cp. genomes of the nine Sophora species, including their sizes, GC content, gene numbers, and other characteristics, are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. All sampled plastomes of Sophora exhibit a typical quadripartite structure and conserved gene content (Fig. 1). Specifically, the cp. genomes of the six newly sequenced Sophora species ranged in length from 151,270 bp (S. moocroftiana) to 172,376 bp (S. xanthantha), with GC content ranging from 35.60% to 36.60% (Supplementary Table S1). Across all nine Sophora species, the LSC regions ranged from 84,221 bp (S. alopecuroides) to 88,983 bp (S. japonica) and accounted for 49.83% − 56.67% of the total length. The SSC regions varied between 12,595 bp (S. xanthantha) and 19,058 bp (S. japonica), comprising 7.3% − 12.00% of the total length. The IR regions ranged from 23,539 bp (S. davidii) to 36,936 bp (S. xanthantha) and accounted for 15.53% − 21.43% of the total length.

In total, the cp genomes of the nine Sophora species contained 126–135 genes, consisting of 79–90 protein-coding, 36–38 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 18 genes were duplicated, including 4 rRNA genes, and 14 other genes (rpl2, rpl23, rps7, rps12, rps19, trnA-UGC, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnM-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnR-ACG, trnV-GAC, ndhB, and ycf2). Furthermore, 17 genes contained introns, including 11 protein-coding genes (rpl2, rpl16, rps12, rpoC1, ndhA, ndhB, petB, petD, atpF, clpP, and ycf3) and 6 tRNAs genes (trnA-UGC, trnI-GAU, trnK-UUU, trnL-UAA, trnT-GGU, and trnV-UAC) (Supplementary Table S2).

Repeat sequence analysis

In this study, we identified a total of 597 complex repeats across the Sophora cp. genomes, including 448 interspersed repeats (178 forward, 45 reverse, 214 palindrome, and 11 complementary) and 149 tandem repeats (Fig. 2A). Complementary repeats were not detected in S. flavescens, but palindrome repeats were most abundant in this species, with 30 instances (Fig. 2A). For interspersed repeats, sequence lengths were Mainly concentrated in the 30–80 bp range, and regardless of whether they were forward or palindromic, very few repeat sequences exceeded 80 bp. As for tandem repeats, approximately half of them ranged from 15 to 35 bp, with some exceeding 100 bp in length, particularly in S. xanthantha, where one reached 149 bp. This specific repeat is located between the genes trnK-UUU and rps16. These tandem repeats were mainly distributed in the non-coding regions of LSC and SSC.

The simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in the cp. genomes of the nine Sophora species were analyzed, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. The total number of SSRs found in the cp. genome of these species ranged from 98 to 171 (Fig. 2B). The majority were mononucleotide repeats (67%), followed by dinucleotides (19%) and tetranucleotide (7%), while other SSR types constituted a lower proportion (Fig. 2C). Mononucleotide repeats may play a more important role in genetic variation than other types of SSRs. SSRs were mainly composed of single nucleotide repeats, especially (A)n and (T)n, and their lengths were mostly in the 10–20 bp range. Furthermore, SSRs in the chloroplast genome of Sophora species were primarily distributed in the LSC and SSC regions. In addition, analysis of SSR locations revealed that most SSRs were distributed in the non-coding regions of the genome, namely the intergenic and intron regions. These newly detected SSRs will contribute to the development of genetic markers for the Sophora species in future studies.

Comparative genome analysis

We compared the JL (LSC/IR) and JS (IR/SSC) boundary positions of the Sophora species (Fig. 3). The length of the IR regions ranged from 23,539 − 25,886 bp in eight of the nine Sophora species with some contraction, except for S. xanthantha (36,936 bp), which exhibited a significant expansion. For the eight Sophora species, the JLA (IRa-LSC: rpl2 & trnH) and JLB (IRb-LSC: rps19 & rpl2) boundaries showed high similarity in five species (S. japonica, S. alopecuroides, S. flavescens, S. velutina var. cavaleriei and S. velutina), while notable differences were found in three species (S. davidii, S. moocroftiana, and S. tonkinensis). The gene rpl2 crossed the JLA and JLB boundaries and resulted in a pseudo-copy of rpl2 due to the IR contraction. In S. davidii, the gene trnH was relocated to the LSC region near the IRb region. However, it was lost in S. tonkinensis, while in the other six species, trnH was found fully present within the LSC region near the IRa region. For S. xanthantha, the JLA and JLB boundary positions differed completely from the other eight species due to its extensive IR expansion. Due to this IR region expansion, genes such as rpl14, rpl16, rps8, rps3, and rps19 (originally from the LSC region), as well as ycf1 and rps15 (from the SSC region), were relocated to the IR region and became duplicated. At the IRa-SSC border, the ycf1 gene crossed the IRa-SSC border and extended into the IRa region, ranging from 350 bp to 484 bp, in eight Sophora species, excluding S. xanthantha. At the IRb-SSC border, the ndhF gene was fully present within the SSC region in all nine Sophora species. Interestingly enough, in S. flavescens, the ycf1 gene crossed the IRb-SSC border, and the ndhF gene was relocated to the SSC region near the IRa region. This result suggests a complete inversion of the SSC region in S. flavescens.

We compared the cp. genome collinearity of the Sophora species (Fig. 4). The results showed that the genome structure was largely conserved, except in S. xanthantha, S. japonica, and S. flavescens. Subsequently, to further investigate genome rearrangements and variations, we compared the complete plastomes of nine Sophora species and nine other Papilionoideae species using Mauve software. This analysis also indicated that variation in the cp. genome of Sophora species mainly occurred in the LSC and SSC regions. Our Mauve Alignment of the cp. genomes among the 18 Papilionoideae generated 12 locally collinear blocks (LCBs) that represented homologous regions without rearrangement (Fig. 5). The results showed that the gene order of S. xanthantha and S. japonica was similar to that of A. braunii in the LSC region, with no 50-kb inversion observed. For the other seven Sophora species, the 50-kb inversion caused the gene pairs matK & rps16 and accD & rbcL to become neighboring genes in their genomes. Specifically, the matK gene was found upstream of the rbcL gene in the LSC, and the rps16 gene upstream of the accD gene (Fig. 5). Additionally, a notable ~ 24 kb inversion was also observed in these seven Sophora species, and we found this inversion had undergone at least three sequence rearrangements rather than being a simple inversion following the 36-kb inversion. Finally, an approximately 18-kb inversion between ycf1 and ndhF was observed in the SSC region of S. flavescens. Furthermore, we first observed a new inversion (~ 6.7 kb) and one transposition (86 bp, trnS-GCU) distinct from the 36-kb inversion in Ormosia hosiei (Fig. 5).

Divergence hotspots and molecular markers

We calculated the nucleotide diversity Pi (π) values for the seven Sophora species exhibiting good collinearity, excluding S. xanthantha and S. japonica (Fig. 6). The results showed that the Pi values ranged from 0 to 0.04683, with an average of 0.01509. The IR regions showed low nucleotide diversity, indicating that most of the variation in the cp. genome of the seven Sophora species occurred mainly in the LSC and SSC regions (Fig. 6). Nucleotide variations were highest in the intergenic spacer (IGS) regions. Within the IGS regions, trnK-UUU - rbcL, trnT-UGU - trnL-UAA, psbE - petL, rps11 - rpl36, ndhJ - trnC-GCA showed a significantly higher Pi value of > 0.04. The psbE - petL region had the highest divergence value of 0.04683 (Fig. 6). These molecular markers are promising candidate barcode sequences for future phylogenetic inference and population genetics studies of the genus Sophora.

Phylogenetic analysis of Sophora

The phylogenies of the genus Sophora inferred from the two data matrices (CDS and three candidate barcode sequences) and methods (ML and BI) yielded similar topologies (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Figure S1). The phylogenomic analysis based on plastid and barcode sequences generated a strongly and consistently supported (BS = 100, and PP = 1.0) phylogeny with two distinct clades (sect. Pseudosophora and other lineages) within the genus Sophora, excluding S. japonica and S. xanthantha. Three species (S. davidii, S. moocroftiana and S. alopecuroides) of sect. Pseudosophora clustered together to form a single clade, which is consistent with morphological evidence from previous sectional divisions [32]. However, the lineage consisting of sect. Disamaea and sect. Sophora did not cluster into distinct clades, particularly regarding S. xanthantha of sect. Disamaea. Based on the present study, S. japonica and S. xanthantha may not be classified within the genus Sophora because these two species clustered with the outgroup A. braunii.

Discussion

Plastome structure or characteristics of Sophora species

In this study, all plastomes of Sophora revealed a typical quadripartite structure and no IR region was entirely lost. The plastome gene content and gene orientation were conserved, consistent with most reported Papilionoideae species [17]. The cp. genome of most angiosperm species is typically 120–160 kb in size, with this variation often attributed to IR region expansion and contraction [4]. In this study, the plastomes of the nine Sophora species ranged from 151,270 bp to 172,376 bp, exhibiting significant length variation. This variation indicates either substantial IR region expansion in certain species within the genus or raises questions about the current generic assignment of some of these nine species.

The existence of IR expansion and contraction has been reported in various kinds of plants [33, 34]. It is usually more variable in the LSC, while stable in the IR and SSC regions, except in Corydalis [35]. In Sophora, the LSC (~ 84 kb), IR (~ 25 kb), and SSC (~ 18 kb) sizes were relatively stable within the genus, except for S. xanthantha, whose IR region expanded Markedly by approximately 11 kb, while its SSC region contracted significantly by approximately 6 kb. Additionally, the contraction and expansion of the IR boundary could also lead to the gene loss, duplication, or pseudogenization in the cp. genome of angiosperms [36–38]. In this study, due to IR contraction, the trnH gene at the JLA (IRa/LSC) junction was lost in S. tonkinensis, and the ycf1 gene at the JSB (IRb/SSC) junction was found to be pseudogenized in all nine Sophora species. A pseudogene is a type of defective gene, caused by mutation of its sequence during inheritance, resulting in the premature termination of its coding process [39]. Pseudogenes are the evolutionary remnants of functional genes in the genome, providing important information for gene expression, gene regulation, and the history of genome evolution [40]. In this study, the ycf1 gene was also a pseudogene due to the premature appearance of a stop codon in all nine Sophora species, as observed in other angiosperms [41, 42]. Our study aligns with findings that IR contraction and expansion can be useful for studying evolutionary patterns [43].

Most photosynthetic angiosperms have a highly conserved plastome organization, with exceptions such as the Campanulaceae, Fabaceae, and Geraniaceae families that exhibit remarkable and extensive rearrangements [44]. Within the Fabaceae, one of the most remarkable inversions that occurred after the emergence of the family is the 50-kb inversion, which is shared by the tribe Sophoreae [19]. In this study, no 50-kb inversion was found in S. japonica and S. xanthantha, similar to the outgroup of A. braunii. S. japonica has been removed from Sophora and placed in Cladrastis [45], and it appears that S. xanthantha should also be removed from Sophora. The other seven Sophora species in this research shared a ~ 24-kb inversion spanning trnC-GCA to trnF-GAA in the LSC region, which is similar to previously reported [22]. This 24-kb inversion was also observed in the other four genera (Salweenia, Ammopiptanthus, Maackia, Piptanthus). Interestingly, this 24-kb inversion was first observed to have undergone at least three sequence rearrangements due to the inclusion of O. hosiei in our study (Fig. 5). In contrast, without O. hosiei, this inversion might otherwise be considered a simple inversion following the 36-kb inversion in Lupinus, as previously reported [23]. In O. hosiei, we first observed a new inversion (~ 6.7-kb) and one transposition (86 bp, trnS-GCU), distinct from the 36-kb inversion. The transition between O. hosiei to Lupinus luteus involved another ~ 6.7-kb inversion, which included the 86 bp transposition. This 6.7-kb inversion is shared by Sophora and the other four genera within the tribe Sophoreae.

Identification of repeat sequences

The high polymorphism rate of repeat sequences at the species level makes them among the most common molecular markers for phylogenetic and population genetics studies. Long repetitive sequences with a length ≥ 30 bp may promote rearrangement of the chloroplast genome and increase species’ genetic diversity [46]. Repeat sequences may have the effect of promoting chloroplast genome rearrangement and recombination. They are important genetic feature of a genome and are conducive to the study of evolution among species and genomes [47, 48]. SSRs are widely distributed in the chloroplast genome of eukaryotes, and have the advantages of simple structure, relative conservatism, high polymorphism, making them efficient molecular markers widely used in species identification, analysis of genetic differences among individuals, and population evolution studies [49–51]. In total, 98–171 SSRs were found in the cp. genome of Sophora species, including mononucleotide, dinucleotide, trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeats. Of these, mononucleotide repeats, which were rich in (A)n and (T)n, were most abundant. This results are consistent with previous reports [52]. Moreover, SSRs of the cp. genome of Sophora species were primarily distributed in the LSC and SSC regions. As for the tandem repeats, approximately half of them ranged from 15 to 35 bp. Large repeat sequences were primarily distributed in the non-coding regions of the LSC and SSC. Similar to most angiosperm chloroplast genomes, most repeat sequences are located in non-coding regions, which is one of the reasons why non-coding regions are generally more variable than coding regions. These newly detected repeat sequences will contribute to the development of genetic markers for the Sophora species in future studies.

Identification of molecular markers

Given that the whole cp. genome contains abundant mutation sites, it can be directly used as a super barcode for species identification. Alternatively, hypervariable regions can be screened as potential molecular markers for species identification [48]. Researchers proposed using some genes combinations derived from the cp. genome as the core barcodes for land plant identification [53]. Strategies for identifying potential DNA barcodes have been successfully applied to various angiosperm species [54]. However, suitable polymorphic regions need to be identified, especially for genera exhibiting low polymorphism with existing molecular markers in phylogenetic studies. In this study, we identified five polymorphic regions by comparing seven Sophora species using sliding window analysis. The genes trnK-UUU - rbcL, trnT-UGU - trnL-UAA, psbE - petL, rps11 - rpl36, and ndhJ - trnC-GCA (Pi (π) > 0.04) can serve as a candidate barcode sequences. Although the trnK-UUU - rbcL and ndhJ - trnC-GCA in Sophora cp. genomes have a high Pi value mainly due to variation at the JL or JS junctions, this makes it difficult to design universal primers, and thus they are not suitable as barcode genes. To evaluate the ability of selected sequences to identify Sophora species, we constructed ML and BI tree based on three concatenated markers, which showed a tree topology consistent with that of the CDS in Sophora species (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Figure S1). In particular, it should be pointed out that these three molecular markers can be used for constructing single-gene, double- or triple-gene combination trees, whose topology is highly consistent with that of the CDS (Supplementary Figure S1). Therefore, these molecular markers could be useful for phylogenetic and population genetic studies of the genus Sophora. Additionally, the trnK-UUU - rbcL gene crossed the 50-kb inversion in the LSC, making it a useful barcode for taxonomic identification in Sophoreae species.

Phylogenetic relationships of Sophora

Plastomes have been widely used in the phylogenetic analyses of land plants and have proven to provide good resolution among evolutionarily puzzling taxa [44]. In this study, the plastid phylogenomic analysis generated a strongly supported phylogeny with two distinct clades (sect. Pseudosophora and others lineage) and suggested that S. japonica and S. xanthantha should be removed from sophora. For the other seven Sophora species, our findings support the clustering of S. davidii, S. moocroftiana, and S. alopecuroides as a monophyletic clade (sect. Pseudosophora), which was resolved as the first-diverging section of this genus with high support values. For the other clade, S. flavescens of sect. Sophora and three species of sect. Disamaea clustered together with strong support. However, these three species did not cluster as a monophyletic clade, which was not supported by morphological evidence from previous sectional divisions [32, 55]. Therefore, we propose to include more material and evidence in future studies to establish a clearer phylogeny of sect. Disamaea and sect. Sophora. Besides, the plastid phylogeny might only resolve an evolutionary line of matrilineal inheritance and does not necessarily reflect the full coalescent history [17]. Thus, more material and evidence will be needed from future studies to provide a higher resolution of the relationships in sophora.

Conclusions

In this study, we sequenced and annotated six cp. genomes, providing insights into the plastome structure of Sophora species. Comparative genome analysis showed that this genus exhibited extensive rearrangements, including gene losses, gene duplications, gene relocations, pseudogenizations, IR expansions, a ~ 24-kb inversion in the LSC region, and SSC region inversions. We also identified three valuable molecular markers in this genus. These findings contribute significantly to the chloroplast genomic data for Sophora, laying the foundation for future studies on its taxonomy, phylogeny, and evolution as a taxonomically problematic genus. Moreover, this plastid dataset provides new insights into Sophora phylogeny and offers valuable information for broader Papilionoideae phylogeny research, thereby enabling the construction of a more robust phylogeny in future studies.

Materials and methods

Sampling, sequencing, and annotation

Nine Sophora species were included in this study, six of which were reported for the first time, namely: S. xanthantha, S. davidii, S. moocroftiana, S. tonkinensis, S. velutina, S. velutina var cavaleriei. All specimens were formally identified by Dr. Bingbing Liu (Institute of Loess Plateau Research, Shanxi University). Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Institute of Loess Plateau Research. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Fresh leaves of five of the six Sophora species were collected from the field, and fresh leaves of S. velutina were obtained from seedlings germinated from seeds provided by Kunming Institute of Botany. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method [56] and checked by visualization on 1% agarose-gel electrophoresis. The DNA was used to construct 150 bp insert libraries in accordance with the manufacturer’s manual (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and was then used for sequence. The high-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq Platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) at Genewiz in Suzhou, China. The Illumina paired-end data were cleaned with Trimmomatic version 0.36 [57] and then used for the cp. genome de novo assembly using NOVOPlasty version 4.3 [58]. Plastome annotation was performed using Plann version 1.1 [59] and the draft annotation was inspected and Manually corrected in Sequin version 16.0 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). All tRNA genes were further confirmed with the tRNA-SE search server version 1.4 [60]. Physical Maps of the genomes were generated using OGDRAW version 1.3.1 (http://ogdraw.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/).

Repeat sequence analysis

Simple sequence repeats were analyzed using MIcroSAtellite version 2.1 (MISA, https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/index.phps) [61], with parameters set to 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3 for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexa-nucleotides, respectively. Long repetitive sequences, including forward, palindromic, reverse, and complementary repeats, were analyzed using REPuter version 1.1 (https://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer) [62] with a 30 bp minimum repeat size and a Hamming distance of 3. Tandem Repeats Finder version 4.04 (http://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.htm) [63] was used to detect tandem repeats, with parameters set to two for the alignment parameter match and seven for mismatches and indels.

Comparative analyses and identification of highly divergent regions

To better understand the chloroplast genome evolution of Sophora, we downloaded reported chloroplast genome data from some Sophoreae species and some other Papilionoideae species (Supplementary Table S3). Firstly, the general features (including the genome structure, size, GC content and gene content) of the cp. genome in the nine Sophora species were characterized using Geneious version 10.2.6 [64]. Secondly, the cp. genome collinearity analysis of the nine Sophora species was performed using mVISTA (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista/mvista/submit.shtml), with S. alopecuroides as a reference. Some misaligned regions were Manually curated using BioEdit version 7.0.9 (http://www.mbio.ncsu.edu/bioedit/bioedit.html). Thirdly, to detect the breakpoints of inversions in the plastomes of the nine Sophora species and to identify locally collinear blocks (LCBs) in plastomes of all sampled Papilionoideae species, we performed a whole plastome Alignment using Mauve version 2.3.1 [65]. To this end, we used the progressive Mauve algorithm, with both the seed weight and minimum LCB score calculated automatically. To detect the breakpoints of inversions in Sophora species, the plastome of Angylocalyx braunii, which has a typical angiosperm plastome organization, was used as the reference in Mauve alignments. Fourthly, IR expansion/contraction in the nine Sophora species were detected using IRscope version 3.1 [66]. Finally, nucleotide diversity (Pi) was calculated using sliding window analysis (window length = 600 bp, step size = 200 bp, excluding sites with alignment gaps) to detect highly divergent regions (i.e., mutation hotspots) in DnaSP version 6.0 [67]. Variable, parsimony informative, conserved sites of DNA barcode sequences were identified using DnaSP software.

Phylogenetic analyses

To reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships among Sophora and other Sophoreae, we complemented our dataset of six Sophora species with 12 previously published plastomes from other Sophoreae species and some Papilionoideae species (Supplementary Table S3). Angylocalyx braunii Harms (NC_047392) was used as the outgroup in the phylogenetic inference. A total of 75 protein-coding sequences (CDS) (Supplementary Table S3), shared by the cp. genomes of the 18 Papilionoideae species, were selected to construct phylogenetic trees. The CDS Alignments were performed using MaFFT version 7.450 [68] and were manually adjusted where necessary. We used maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods for phylogenetic analyses. The nucleotide substitution model used in phylogenetic analysis was Chosen based on the Akaike information criteria implemented in the jModelTest version 2.1.10 [69]. ML analyses were conducted using RAxML version 8.1.24 [70]. RAxML searches were performed with 500 randomized Maximum parsimony starting trees, and then run again under the same conditions, executing 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap replicates to assess branch support. BI was conducted using MrBayes version 3.2 [71]. Two independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were performed, each with four chains (three heated and one cold) for 30 million generations, sampling every 1000th tree. Each chain started with a random tree, and the first 25% sampled generations were discarded as burn-in to construct a majority-rule consensus tree and to estimate posterior probabilities (PP). Set the parameters as follows: a simple nucleotide model (lset nucmodel = GTR, lset nst = 8), a proportion of invariable sites (lset rates = invgamma). The consensus trees were finally edited using FigTree version 1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/releases/tag/v1.4.4) [72].

To test the value of molecular markers obtained in this study, we designed primers. Three of the five molecular markers (trnT-UGU - trnL-UAA, psbE - petL, and rps11 - rpl36) were successfully designed (Table S4), while the other two did not yield suitable primer design regions. Alignments of the three barcode sequences were performed using MEGA version 6 [73] and manually adjusted where necessary. The methods for phylogenetic analyses were the same as those for the CDS.
