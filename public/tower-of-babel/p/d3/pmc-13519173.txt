Quantification of Infant Distress Across Serial Clubfoot Casting Visits: A Prospective Pilot Study
Authors: Ruth H Jones, Olivia C Tracey, Patrick P Nian, Joshua T Bram, Samuel A Beber, Sarah Lu, Keza R Levine, John S Blanco, Emily R Dodwell, David M Scher, Shevaun M Doyle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Idiopathic clubfoot is typically corrected with the Ponseti method, involving foot manipulation and serial casting followed by possible Achilles tenotomy and bracing. This process causes distress, but the quantification of infant distress during longitudinal casting visits has not been evaluated. The primary aim of this pilot study was to assess infant distress longitudinally across weekly casting sessions among children with clubfeet. Secondary aims included identifying patient characteristics associated with infant distress.Methods:Ten patients with idiopathic clubfoot were prospectively enrolled in this pilot study. Prenatal counseling, family history, baseline parental anxiety, and demographic data were recorded at the initial visit. The patient’s pain response was assessed by the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) and heart rate (HR) at 5 designated casting steps during each visit. The primary outcome measures were NIPS score and HR at each casting step throughout the entire course of casting (ie, longitudinally across casting visits). HR range for each casting step was also recorded at every visit. Additionally, univariable and multivariable analyses were performed to identify predictors of infant distress.Results:There was a significant increase in NIPS across visits at casting steps 1, 2, and 3 (P = .03, P = .01, P = .05) and in HR at each of the 5 casting steps (P < .001, P < .001, P < .001, P < .001, P = .003). With the numbers available, no significant difference in HR range could be detected across visits (P = .51). Multivariable analysis demonstrated that visit number was independently associated with infant distress.Conclusion:This pilot study suggests that infant distress at certain casting steps increases from mild at the first visit to moderate at subsequent visits. The magnitude of heart rate elevation within each visit did not significantly change across visits in this pilot cohort, although whether this reflects infant acclimation or other factors warrants further investigation.Level of Evidence:Level IV, prospective pilot study.Keywords: congenital talipes equinovarus, Ponseti casting, neonatal distress, Neonatal Infant Pain Scale, clubfoot treatment

Introduction

Idiopathic congenital talipes equinovarus (ie, clubfoot) is one of the most common congenital abnormalities of the lower limb, affecting 1 to 2 children per thousand live births globally.1-3 Clubfoot consists of 4 principal components: ankle equinus, hindfoot varus, forefoot adductus, and midfoot cavus. 4 The current standard of treatment is Ponseti casting, which consists of serial manipulation and casting, followed by possible Achilles tenotomy, and then bracing. 5 Although non-invasive, infants often express distress during the manipulation and casting process. However, unlike in children and adults, infantile expression of pain can be challenging for the provider to quantify.

Few studies have sought to quantify infantile distress during clubfoot casting treatment by using a pain score via the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) and heart rate (HR). 6 NIPS is a validated measurement of pain that assesses facial expression, crying, breathing pattern, upper extremity and lower extremity movement, and state of arousal. Studies by Milbrandt et al 7 and Upadhyay et al 8 demonstrate the efficacy of various interventions, including sucrose and milk solution or non-nutritive sucking, during Ponseti treatment to decrease the patient’s pain response, as measured by their NIPS score. However, these studies present aggregate data of patients before, during, and immediately after each cast session. Specifically, patients were not followed through each casting step to examine a potential change in distress. As such, there is a paucity in the literature of evaluating distress during individual casting sessions as well as across serial casting visits.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess infant distress, measured by the NIPS scale and HR, during Ponseti serial casting for clubfoot at different time points during a casting visit and throughout the course of weekly casting visits. Secondary aims were to examine the association between infant distress and potential predictors, including clubfoot severity, baseline parental anxiety, and pre-casting feeding and sleeping characteristics.

Materials and Methods

This was a prospective cohort pilot study that was approved by the Hospital for Special Surgery Institutional Review Board (IRB 2023-0109). Parents of patients with idiopathic clubfoot were approached at their initial casting visit to participate in this study. Patients were included if they were less than 6 months of age at the time of initial casting, were indicated for serial Ponseti method casting, and had no prior attempts at correction. Patients were treated by one of 3 fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons specializing in clubfoot. Planning for Ponseti treatment occurred either at the parents’ prenatal consultation or the patient’s initial consultation after birth. Patients were excluded if they had clubfoot secondary to teratologic, neuropathic, or neuromuscular etiologies. Ten patients were selected as the sample size for this pilot study. This ensured feasibility as well as alignment with prior pilot studies where a sample size of 10 is commonly used for studies targeting large effect sizes.9-11

On the initial visit prior to the first cast, parents/guardians were asked if they had a prenatal clubfoot consult, their number of children, and whether any prior children had clubfoot and required treatment. Baseline parent or guardian anxiety was measured via the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) questionnaire at the initial visit prior to casting. Demographic data of the infant (age, sex, race, ethnicity) were also recorded.

During each casting visit, 2 co-investigators were present to collect the patient’s pain response assessed by NIPS (Supplementary Material 1) and HR. NIPS ranges from 0 to 7 (0-2: none or mild pain; 3-4: mild to moderate pain; 5-7: severe pain). Because the infant’s lower extremities were held during the casting session, investigators assessed the leg components of NIPS by palpating whether the muscles were tense/contracted or relaxed. NIPS was recorded at 5 casting steps: (1) baseline (following cast removal, prior to foot manipulation), (2) initiation of foot manipulation, (3) initiation of soft cotton roll, (4) completion of plaster molding part 1 (up to the knee), and (5) completion of plaster molding part 2 (up to the hip). The patient’s HR, reported as beats per minute (bpm), was monitored on a GE Carescape V100 Vital Signs Monitor (GE Healthcare) throughout the casting with an adhesive oxygen saturation sensor attached to the infant’s hand by the attending orthopaedic surgeon conducting the casting. Minimum and maximum HR during casting was recorded, along with the HR at each of the same 5 casting steps. Other variables collected at each visit included Pirani and Dimeglio scores for each affected foot, parent-reported time since the most recent feeding and sleeping episodes, and parent-reported duration of the most recent sleeping episode. Time since the most recent feeding was recorded as zero if patients began feeding during the visit. For patients with bilateral clubfoot, the provider started each casting visit with the left leg. “Baseline” values for the right leg were acquired following completion of left leg casting.

Primary outcomes included the change in NIPS score and HR over the full treatment period, assessed at each of the 5 casting steps. NIPS score and HR were recorded at each casting step during each visit from the day of the initial cast through the penultimate cast. The final cast, which was placed following the tenotomy, was not included. HR range, defined as maximum HR minus minimum HR for each visit, was also assessed across visits. This number represented the magnitude of heart rate elevation for each visit. Shapiro-Wilk testing was performed to determine normality. Repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to examine changes in the recorded NIPS score and HR across visits. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were used to determine between-visit differences following significant ANOVA results.

Additionally, univariable analyses were performed to identify potential predictors of infant distress, including HR elevation, NIPS and HR at initiation of foot manipulation (casting step 2). Because initiation of manipulation (casting step 2) was identified as the most distressing part of the casting process, it was selected as the step of primary interest. All regression analyses were exploratory in nature and were conducted without multiplicity adjustment; findings should be interpreted accordingly rather than as confirmatory findings.

Variables assessed included age, sex, parental anxiety (higher GAD-7 score used if 2 parents completed GAD-7), visit number, Pirani score, Dimeglio score, time since last fed, time since last sleeping episode, and duration of last sleeping episode. Normality of the distribution variables was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Pearson and Spearman correlations were performed for continuous variables as appropriate, 1-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis testing were performed for ordinal variables as appropriate, and independent samples t tests were used for binary variables based on normality assessment. Multivariable analyses were conducted for age, sex, and predictors of infant distress that met a threshold of P <.20 of significance on univariable analysis. The unstandardized regression coefficient (B) and SE, and 95% CI were reported. Significance was set at an alpha of ≤.05 for all other statistical analyses, which were performed on SPSS (version 23.0, SPSS Inc).

Results

Ten patients were prospectively enrolled and included in this study. The median age at the initial visit was 4 days (range 3-21 days). Eight patients were male. Of the 10 patients, 2 had older siblings with a history of clubfoot. Of the 10 patients’ parents/guardians, 9 had prenatal counseling, and the median parental GAD-7 score was 3 (range 0-16), demonstrating minimal anxiety. The median baseline Pirani and Dimeglio scores (including only the first foot casted for bilateral patients) were 6 (range 4-6) and 17.5 (range 11-20), respectively. Patients had a median of 5 casts before Achilles tenotomy (range 4-6 casts), excluding the cast following tenotomy. All 10 patients underwent tenotomy (Table 1).

Discussion

This pilot study suggests that infant distress, assessed using NIPS obtained at predefined casting steps representing different phases of manipulation during clubfoot casting, increases from mild pain at the initial visit to moderate pain at subsequent visits. In addition, HR at these casting steps, which may be used as an indicator of patient distress, increases from the first visit to subsequent visits. Based on these findings, we propose that (1) infants do experience distress during Ponseti casting and (2) their level of distress increases after the first casting visit but appears to stabilize thereafter.

The observed findings may be a result of the pressure from manipulation of the foot and the inflammation that develops as a result of the anatomical changes in the foot. Although the initial casting visit introduces a novel stressor for the infant, the foot manipulation performed at this stage is relatively limited, focusing primarily on correction of midfoot cavus.12,13 Swelling and inflammation along with tissue adaptations from soft tissue lengthening and bone remodeling that result from cast immobilization occur throughout clubfoot deformity correction. In other words, the foot is immobilized and stretched against the infant’s original alignment, which may cause increasing distress or pain. 14 Additionally, distress at subsequent visits may increase because of residual discomfort from wearing a cast during the previous week, including greater joint stiffness or skin irritation.15-18

NIPS scores increased significantly from one visit to the next at casting step 2, the initiation of manipulation, indicating escalating behavioral distress at the start of the casting process. This suggests that the acute pain experienced at the onset of clubfoot manipulation becomes more intense as casts have been on for longer periods of time. Despite repeated measures ANOVA demonstrating significant variance across visits at casting steps 1 and 3, pairwise comparisons did not further elucidate individual differences among visits, likely because of a sample size with insufficient power to detect such granular differences. Furthermore, the absence of a significant increase in NIPS scores at the final 2 casting steps may be attributable to the fact that the most painful aspect of the procedure, foot manipulation, is completed by the fourth casting step (completion of plaster molding below the knee).

Heart rate, however, increased significantly from the first casting visit to subsequent visits across casting steps 1 through 5, suggesting a divergence between physiological and behavioral indicators of distress during cast application. 19 HR may remain elevated because of ongoing autonomic stress, whereas behavioral responses measured by NIPS could diminish as infants habituate or reach a ceiling in observable pain behaviors. Furthermore, HR range (the magnitude of increase in heart rate) did not increase across casting visits. The observed extreme minimum and maximum values may reflect brief fluctuations during a given visit rather than meaningful changes in overall distress. The stability of HR range across visits could be due to compensatory periods of lower stress during the visit, habituation to discomfort, or physiological adjustment to a new, elevated level of distress. Additionally, it is important to note that although HR values remained within age-appropriate normal ranges (105-205 bpm from ages 0 to 4 weeks; 100-180 bpm from 4 weeks to 1 year), 20 the elevation of HR from the first casting visit to subsequent visits nonetheless reflects a meaningful physiological response to distress.

Univariable analyses revealed several potential predictors of infant distress at initiation of manipulation, including visit number, clubfoot severity, and parental anxiety, which underscore the likely interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic factors contributing to the discomfort experienced by children undergoing clubfoot treatment. 21 Increased visit number was associated with distress as measured by HR. This aligns with results of the primary analysis, which demonstrated the progressive nature of infant discomfort across casting visits. However, visit number was not independently associated with NIPS at initiation of manipulation. This may be due to the previously observed plateau in mean NIPS after the initial increase at the second visit.

Pirani and Dimeglio scores were identified as potential predictors of infant distress. However, the correlations were weak and did not remain significant in multivariable analyses when controlling for other factors. Although one might expect a positive correlation between clubfoot severity and distress, the progression through clubfoot treatment may have a stronger influence on distress levels than clubfoot severity. Furthermore, the observed negative correlation between severity and distress likely reflects the progression through treatment: as deformity severity improves, greater structural change and anatomical adaptation occur, leading to more inflammation and, consequently, increased discomfort during casting. 14 The increase in distress levels over time likely explains the negative correlation observed between severity and distress. It should also be noted that there was little variability in the severity of clubfoot in this small pilot study. This likely contributed to the lack of significant results and ability to draw meaningful conclusions regarding the effect of severity on distress.

Previous literature suggests feeding during clubfoot casting contributes to lowering infant distress.7,8,22 Infant intake of milk or sucrose can serve as non-pharmacologic interventions for pain response of neonates, possibly related to an increase in β-endorphin release or dopamine pathways.7,23 In this study, we evaluated how feeding before casting may impact distress levels and found that recent feeding was not significantly associated with lower distress. The lack of significant effect may be due to the pilot nature of the study, which limited its statistical power. Alternatively, it is possible that feeding before casting does not significantly impact infant distress, and only feeding during the procedure plays a meaningful role in pain management.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the pilot nature of the study and limited statistical power may have impacted the ability to detect meaningful differences in NIPS values and HR across visits at various casting steps. This may also have resulted in an underestimation of the effect of variables included in the multivariable regression analysis. Second, the study analyzed only 10 patients from a single orthopaedic tertiary care hospital treated by 3 fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, limiting the generalizability of these results. Additionally, details of the casting method between orthopaedic surgeons were not documented and this process may not have been standardized. Third, only 3 measures of infant distress were analyzed as outcome variables in univariable and multivariable analyses, including NIPS and HR at initiation of manipulation and HR range. NIPS and HR were selected for their practicality in collection. HR range provided a comprehensive assessment of the visit overall, and initiation of manipulation was identified as the most distressing casting step. Fourth, investigators did not collect specific use of milk or sucrose during the casting procedure as it was outside of the scope of the study. Fifth, the duration of each casting step was not recorded. Because longer steps may elicit greater distress, this unmeasured variable represents a limitation of other variables that was not the analysis. Given these limitations, future research using larger cohorts is needed to confirm this study’s findings, enhance generalizability, and more rigorously examine the relationships between potential predictors of infant distress during clubfoot casting.

Conclusions

No study, to the authors’ knowledge, has investigated how infant distress during casting changes throughout idiopathic clubfoot casting treatment. Our study suggests that infants do experience some distress during clubfoot casting, with discomfort rising from mild pain at the first visit to moderate distress at subsequent visits. After the initial increase between the first and second casts, the magnitude of heart rate elevation within each visit appeared to remain stable across visits in this pilot cohort, suggesting a possible ceiling or adaptive response that warrants further study.

These findings may help parents anticipate the demands of clubfoot treatment and highlight the importance of considering additional pain management strategies to optimize outcomes and enhance the patient experience. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings and to further elucidate the effect of potential predictors of distress.
