Irisin promotes selective changes in hippocampal mitochondrial metabolism in mice
Authors: Ariene S. Fonseca, Marina S. Chichierchio, Mariana G. Chauvet, Guilherme B. de Freitas, Andréa Tosta, Tiago Ferreira de Lima, Marcos A. Formiga-Jr, Felipe C. Ribeiro, Sergio T. Ferreira, Fernanda G. De Felice, Juliana Camacho-Pereira, Mychael V. Lourenco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Irisin, an exercise-induced hormone, exerts neuroprotective actions in neurological disease models, yet its effects on brain mitochondrial metabolism remain unclear. Here, we examined how irisin influences mitochondrial function and dynamics in the mouse hippocampus. Acute irisin exposure boosted ATP-coupled oxygen consumption without altering mitochondrial content or dynamics-related proteins. Furthermore, chronic irisin administration in vivo decreased hippocampal Opa1 and Drp1 levels, two key regulators of mitochondrial remodeling. These findings uncover selective actions of irisin on hippocampal mitochondrial homeostasis and identify prolonged irisin exposure as a potential regulator of mitochondrial dynamics in the brain.

Introduction

Irisin is an exercise-induced hormone produced from proteolytic cleavage of a precursor protein termed fibronectin type III domain containing protein 5 (FNDC5) [1,2]. Irisin was shown to control peripheral metabolism at least in part through modulation of adipose physiology and thermogenesis [1,[3], [4], [5]]. Physical exercise-induced irisin was proposed to activate αVβ5 integrin receptors in the adipose tissue to mediate transcriptional reprogramming towards mitochondrial biogenesis and browning [3].

Brain FNDC5/irisin was shown to be essential for synaptic plasticity and memory [[6], [7], [8]]. We previously reported that brain irisin is reduced in Alzheimer's disease (AD) [4,7,9], a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by impaired mitochondrial dynamics, synaptic function and memory [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. We further demonstrated that irisin mediates the beneficial actions of physical exercise in mouse models of AD [7].

Previous reports identified potential irisin-initiated mechanisms in the brain. Wrann et al. (2013) showed that irisin stimulates neuronal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling [6] and we have demonstrated that irisin engages the cyclic AMP(cAMP)/cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA) signaling pathway in brain explants [7]. Irisin was also reported to reduce amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation in vitro and in vivo [7,14,15] and to prevent Aβ oligomer-induced oxidative stress in hippocampal neurons [16]. However, while irisin was shown to control mitochondrial function in peripheral tissues [1,17], an outstanding question is whether irisin impacts brain metabolism. Here we used ex vivo mouse hippocampal slices and in vivo irisin administration in mice to interrogate whether irisin modifies hippocampal energy metabolism and proteins associated with mitochondrial dynamics.

Methods

Study approval and ethics

All studies have been performed according to national and international ethical regulations. Mouse hippocampal slices were prepared in compliance with international standards. Experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (protocol #051-19).

Ex vivo hippocampal slices

Mouse hippocampal slices were prepared according to established procedures [7,[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Three-month-old male and female C57BL/6J mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation followed by decapitation and their hippocampi were immediately removed. Coronal hippocampal slices (400 μm) were cut, placed on artificial CSF (aCSF; 124 mM NaCl, 4.4 mM KCl, 1 mM Na2HPO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose), and allowed to recover for 60 min. Recombinant irisin (AG-40B-0136) was obtained from Adipogen (San Diego, CA) and resuspended in sterile water, as described [7,16]. For treatments, recombinant irisin (0.025 μM, as per our previous studies [7,16]) was diluted in aCSF, used as vehicle, and added to slices for 3 h.

High-resolution respirometry

Mouse hippocampal slices were treated as described and weighed on a high-precision scale. Slices were placed in a high-resolution Oroboros O2K oxygraph (Innsbruck, Austria), previously calibrated with aCSF at 37 °C, with the following parameters: gain 2, magnet speed at 750 rpm, and polarization voltage at 800 mV. Basal oxygen consumption was assessed considering the glucose present in the medium as the substrate. Additions for the assessment of mitochondrial respiratory states were made following the SUIT (substrates and inhibitors) protocol in the following order: 5 μM carboxyatractyloside (CAT), as an inhibitor of the nucleotide translocator (ANT), and 1 μg/mL oligomycin (OLIGO), an inhibitor of mitochondrial ATP synthase. Carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) was titrated at 0.5 μM as uncoupling agent, 1 mM rotenone as an inhibitor of complex I in the electron transport system, 10 mM succinate as a complex II substrate, 5 mM antimycin A, and 2 mM azide as inhibitors of complex III and IV, respectively, as described [22]. Slices were collected and protein concentration was determined using BCA assays following each respirometry measurement. Oxygen consumption was expressed as O2 flux (pmol/min/mg protein). Basal respiration was determined at the beginning of the experiment, before the addition of any substrate or inhibitor. Oligomycin-inhibited respiration was measured after the addition of oligomycin, which blocks ATP synthase and thereby reflects oxygen consumption not coupled to ATP production. Non-mitochondrial respiration rates, determined after addition of antimycin A and azide, were subtracted from was subtracted from all respiratory parameters. Respiration coupled to ATP synthesis was then calculated as the difference between basal respiration and oligomycin-inhibited respiration. Proton leak was determined by the absolute values after addition of CAT and oligo. Maximal respiratory capacity (MRC) was determined by the maximum absolute value after titration with FCCP. The contribution of complex I to the electron transport system (ETS) was calculated as (FCCP-rotenone). Oxygen flux rates (pmol/min/mg) can be normalized by MRC to designate the flux control ratio (FCR). FCR values are independent of mitochondrial content or cell size and theoretically range between 0.0 and 1.0. Thus, the calculations were made with MRC values as the normalization reference [23].

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and citrate synthase activities

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity was determined from the reduction of pyruvate to lactate, by incubating 0.05 mg/mL of protein in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 8.8, with the addition of 1 mM NADH and reading for 5 min at 340 nm. NADH consumption independent of LDH activity was measured beforehand and considered as the reaction blank. The reaction was then initiated by addition of 1 mM pyruvate, and the NADH consumption dependent on LDH was measured. The Vmax obtained from the blank was subtracted from the Vmax after the addition of pyruvate to obtain the specific activity of LDH [24]. Citrate synthase was measured as described [25,26]. Briefly, hippocampal slices were incubated in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 0.1 mM acetyl-CoA, 0.1 mM 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid), and 0.1% Triton X-100. The assay started with the addition of 0.2 mM oxaloacetate and was monitored at 412 nm for 10 min at 25 °C.

Glucose uptake, lactate and NAD measurements

Glucose uptake in hippocampal slices was quantified using the Glucose Uptake Colorimetric Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK083), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, slices were homogenized in the assay buffer, and glucose uptake was measured through enzymatic phosphorylation of 2-deoxyglucose to 2-deoxyglucose-6-phosphate, which is subsequently oxidized to generate a colorimetric product. Absorbance was read at 412 nm, and glucose uptake rates were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to total protein content. Lactate content was measured using the Lactate Colorimetric Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK064). Tissue homogenates were incubated with lactate oxidase and peroxidase reaction mix to convert lactate into a colorimetric product detectable at 570 nm. Lactate concentrations were normalized by protein levels. NAD and NADH levels were measured using the NAD/NADH Quantification Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK037) according to the manufacturer's instructions and by cycling assay [27]. Slices were homogenized in extraction buffer, centrifuged, and supernatants collected. Total NAD was measured directly, whereas NADH was quantified after heating samples at 60 °C for 30 min to degrade NAD+. Absorbance was then recorded at 450 nm. NAD+ values were obtained by subtracting NADH from total NAD.

Mouse experiments

Three-month-old male and female C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the animal facility at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro and were housed in groups of 4-5 mice per cage, with food and water ad libitum, in rooms with controlled temperature and humidity on a 12 h light/dark cycle. Irisin (2 μg/kg) or vehicle (saline) was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) once a day for 7 days (N = 8 mice/group). Mice were euthanized 24 h after the last i.p. injection and hippocampi were collected.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from the hippocampus using the SV Total RNA Isolation kit (Promega, CA), following manufacturer instructions. Purity of extracted RNA were checked by the 260/280 absorbance ratio. Only preparations with 260/280 nm optical density ratios higher than 1.8 were used for the reported experiments. RNA concentrations were determined by absorption at 260 nm. For qRT-PCR, 0.5 μg total RNA was used for complementary DNA synthesis using high capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Quantitative expression analysis of target genes was performed on a 7500 system with the Power SYBR Green kit in 15 μL reaction volumes, as described [7,21,[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Actb (gene corresponding to β-actin) was used as an endogenous reference for data normalization. Cycle threshold values were used to calculate gene expression fold changes using the 2−ΔΔCt method [32]. Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunoblotting

Proteins were extracted by homogeneization of hippocampal slices in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) containing 1x Halt protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Pierce) and were resolved on 4-20% polyacrylamide pre-cast gels (BioRad) with Tris/glycine/SDS buffer run at 150 V for 60 min at room temperature. The gel (30 μg total protein/lane) was electroblotted onto Hybond ECL nitrocellulose using a buffer consisting of 25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% (v/v) methanol, pH 8.3, at 300 mA for 90 min at 4 °C. Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at RT, as described [19,[33], [34], [35]]. Primary antibodies (described in Supplementary Table 2) were diluted in blocking solution and incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit fluorescent IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000) for 60 min, membranes were washed and then scanned in an Odyssey detector. Optical density determination for quantification was performed on Fiji [36].

Statistics

Data are expressed as means ± SEM and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (La Jolla, CA). Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to statistical comparisons. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were analyzed using two-tailed Student's t-test or Welch's test. For comparisons between two groups deviating from normality, Wilcoxon matched-pairs rank test was used. Sample sizes and p-values for each experiment are indicated in each figure.

Results

Irisin does not impact glucose uptake, lactate, or NAD+ content in mouse hippocampal slices

We initially used mouse hippocampal slices to determine whether irisin would impact glucose uptake and lactate metabolism. We found that a 3-h treatment with irisin did not modify glucose uptake, as assessed by 2-deoxyglucose content (Fig. 1A). Irisin selectively increases ATP-coupled respiration in mouse hippocampal slices

We next investigated the effects of irisin on hippocampal mitochondrial metabolism. We performed high-resolution respirometry in mouse hippocampal slices, and found basal mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates were similar to values previously reported [22]. We did not detect significant differences in basal mitochondrial oxygen consumption between irisin and vehicle groups (Fig. 2A). Whereas no changes were observed in oxygen consumption resulting from complex I activity (Fig. 2B), irisin selectively triggered succinate-induced oxygen consumption (Fig. 2C). No changes were found in basal respiration (Fig. 2A), maximal (Fig. 2D) or spare capacities (Fig. 2E) upon irisin treatment. Interestingly, irisin increased ATP-coupled oxygen consumption (Fig. 2F) in the absence of significant effects on proton leak (Fig. 2G). Discussion

Physical exercise has revealed to be an effective lifestyle intervention to promote brain health [4,[37], [38], [39]], yet the molecular pathways linking exercise to improved neuronal function remain incompletely understood. Irisin, an exercise-induced hormone with documented neuroprotective actions [4,7,16], has emerged as a promising mediator of these benefits. Although irisin has been implicated in modulating mitochondria in the adipose tissue and skeletal muscle [1,40,41], whether it shapes mitochondrial energy metabolism in the brain has remained unclear. Here, we provide initial evidence that irisin exerts selective mitochondrial effects in the hippocampus, a brain region particularly involved in memory.

A central finding of this work is that acute irisin exposure enhances ATP-coupled oxygen consumption in hippocampal slices, in the absence of major changes in glucose uptake, lactate metabolism, NAD+/NADH balance, or mitochondrial content. These results delineate a specific mitochondrial effect triggered by irisin in the short term. It suggests that irisin may fine tune mitochondrial function, in contrast with the uncoupling thermogenic profile found in adipocytes [1].

The increase in succinate-supported respiration suggests that irisin may selectively enhance complex II-linked mitochondrial activity or even boost succinate-mediated signaling [42] in the hippocampus. Together with the increase in ATP-coupled respiration, and in the absence of changes in proton leak or maximal respiratory capacity, these findings suggest improved respiratory coupling efficiency rather than global increases in mitochondrial function.

Chronic irisin administration in vivo reduced hippocampal OPA1 and DRP1 protein levels, indicating that prolonged irisin exposure may impact the molecular machinery governing mitochondrial morphology. OPA1 is a dynamin-related GTPase that mediates inner mitochondrial membrane fusion and maintains cristae architecture [43], whereas DRP1 is recruited to mitochondria to drive fission [44], together coordinating mitochondrial network organization, bioenergetic efficiency, and quality control. Changes in Opa1 and Drp1, two core regulators of mitochondrial dynamics [45], may reflect compensatory remodeling processes that emerge only after sustained irisin signaling. Although the direction and functional implications of these changes require further investigation, the results suggest that irisin influences mitochondrial homeostasis in the hippocampus.

Previous studies have linked FNDC5/irisin to mitochondrial quality control and dynamics in several tissues. For example, Ge et al. reported that FNDC5 deficiency disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis after traumatic brain injury through a SIRT3-dependent mechanism and increased OPA1 acetylation without altering OPA1/DRP1 transcript levels [46]. Our findings do not necessarily conflict with those observations, as the studies differ substantially in experimental context. Whereas Ge et al. used an in vivo injury model with FNDC5 loss-of-function approaches [46], our study examined the effects of acute recombinant irisin exposure in ex vivo hippocampal slices and prolonged irisin administration in vivo. Under these conditions, irisin selectively increased ATP-coupled respiration without robust acute changes in mitochondrial dynamics-associated proteins, while prolonged exposure reduced OPA1 and DRP1 abundance in the hippocampus. Of note, we found that irisin reduced OPA1 protein content in the absence of altered mRNA levels, suggesting translational regulation or increased turnover in the mouse hippocampus. Changes in OPA1 could reflect to mitochondrial remodeling in the brain.

Together, these findings suggest that irisin may exert temporally distinct effects on mitochondrial physiology, with acute modulation of bioenergetic function preceding more sustained remodeling of mitochondrial dynamics pathways. Because mitochondrial fusion and fission are tightly linked to synaptic maintenance and neuronal resilience [47], such adaptations may contribute to the neuroprotective actions previously associated with exercise-related FNDC5/irisin signaling.

We and others have reported that irisin triggers both acute and more long lasting signaling effects [6,7,14,16,17]. Our ex vivo experimental design considered a 3-h timeframe in which irisin has shown beneficial effects against impaired synaptic plasticity in AD models [7]. Conversely, our in vivo experimental approach focused on more long-lasting effects of irisin, also previously reported in our previous studies [7]. The distinct effects observed after acute versus prolonged irisin exposure may reflect time-dependent regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis. Proteins such as OPA1 and DRP1 are strongly regulated by post-translational modifications, including acetylation and phosphorylation [45], which may precede detectable changes in total protein abundance. Thus, the absence of acute changes in OPA1 or DRP1 despite increased ATP-coupled respiration suggests that early metabolic effects of irisin may occur independently of overt mitochondrial remodeling, whereas prolonged exposure may engage compensatory adaptations in mitochondrial dynamics pathways.

Together, our results indicate that irisin selectively modulates mitochondrial homeostasis, without changing other metabolic parameters in the hippocampus and underscore the importance of investigating exercise-related signals to fully capture their metabolic and structural consequences. Limitations of this study include the absence of mitochondrial morphology data, which could further inform on irisin-induced mitochondrial remodeling, and the lack of cell-type specific information that would pinpoint the key cell types targeted by irisin signaling.

Because we did not directly assess mitochondrial morphology, the structural consequences of reduced OPA1 and DRP1 abundance remain speculative. One possibility is that reduced OPA1 could limit inner membrane fusion or alter cristae organization, whereas reduced DRP1 could decrease mitochondrial fission, together shifting the balance of mitochondrial dynamics toward a less fragmented and more stable mitochondrial network. Such an interpretation is consistent with a compensatory remodeling model, but future studies using mitochondrial imaging, ultrastructural analysis, and direct measurements of fusion/fission events will be required to determine whether irisin alters mitochondrial morphology in hippocampal neurons or glial cells.

Importantly, sex differences have been reported in terms of the effects of physical exercise [48]. In the present study, we used male and female mice, and investigating sex differences was not within the scope. Nonetheless, future studies may interrogate whether irisin trigger neuroprotective effects in a sex-dependent manner. Further studies will also be essential to determine whether irisin controls glucose and oxidative metabolism in vivo, how irisin-driven mitochondrial adjustments shape brain function, and which cell types contribute significantly to irisin-mediated mitochondrial adaptations.
