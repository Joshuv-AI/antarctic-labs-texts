Unraveling the distinctive features of sour shrimp paste: A comparative analysis with conventional shrimp paste based on aroma and microbiota profiles
Authors: Weixi Yang, Jiahui Shi, Yuxiang Gu, Yibin Lan, Changqing Duan, Yanbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSour shrimp paste (SSP) is a traditional fermented food produced by co-fermenting shrimp and grains. It is characterized by a unique sour and fruity aroma that contrasts with the fishy notes of conventional shrimp paste (CSP). GC–MS analysis revealed that SSP contains significantly higher levels of esters (656.50–4717.62 mg/kg) and alcohols (98.30–538.11 mg/kg), which are 2–10 times higher than in CSP. CSP has more heterocyclic compounds. SSP was notably enriched in branched-chain esters, ethyl butanoate, and phenyl esters, many of which are derived from amino acid metabolism. Microbial profiling indicated higher abundances of Wallemia, lactic acid bacteria, and yeasts in SSP. Correlation analysis further identified Ligilobacillus, Vagococcus, Peptoniphilus, Metschnikowia, Wallemia, and Saccharomyces as key microbiota strongly associated with esters, alcohols, and acids. These results systematically clarify the biochemical and microbial basis underlying the unique sensory qualities of SSP.Keywords: Sour shrimp paste, Traditional fermented foods, Sensory analysis, Metabolomics, Microbial community

Introduction

Shrimp paste, a traditional fermented food in Asian regions, is typically produced through the salt fermentation of small shrimp, developing a characteristic salty taste and umami-rich flavor profile (Hu et al., 2024). In southwest China; a sour fermented variant of shrimp paste; known as Xiasuan or sour shrimp paste (SSP); has gained popularity. Unlike conventional shrimp paste (CSP); it undergoes prolonged co-fermentation with grains (also known as acidic fermentation); a traditional method also used in other Asian fermented foods like Suanyu (sour fish) and Suanrou (sour meat) (Zhong et al., 2021). The production of SSP involves a two-stage fermentation process (Fig. 1). In the initial phase; fresh shrimp undergo conventional fermentation techniques to form the base shrimp paste. This primary product is then blended with rice wine lees (fermented glutinous rice) and chili powder; followed by a secondary fermentation period of two months to develop its distinctive flavor (Song et al., 2024). All raw materials-including shrimp; rice wine lees; and chili powder—are fully incorporated into the final product and are entirely edible; resulting in no solid waste generation and high raw material utilization. The key distinction between SSP and CSP lies in the incorporation of starch-rich ingredients for co-fermentation in SSP. The mechanism of starch-protein co-fermentation systems is based on the synergistic interaction between carbohydrates and animal protein. In addition to serving as primary fermentation substrates; carbohydrates modulate localized microenvironments through their physical structures (e.g.; starch barriers); thereby influencing microbial communities and fermentation products (Okeke et al., 2021). In actual production, notable differences exist between SSP and CSP, particularly in terms of aroma characteristics. However, systematic comparative studies on the flavor profiles of these two types of shrimp paste remain insufficient.

Schematic illustration of the manufacturing processes for conventional shrimp paste (CSP) and sour shrimp paste (SSP).

The aroma of food is a key factor in determining its flavor and significantly impacts consumer preferences. This characteristic is predominantly governed by volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Compared to other fermented seasoning products, shrimp paste exhibits a notably more intricate aroma composition (Hu et al., 2024). To this point; 146 VOCs have been identified and reported in CSP; with the majority originating from four distinct metabolic pathways. Carbohydrate fermentation is central to the flavor development of shrimp paste. It drives the accumulation of key flavor intermediates; such as pyruvate; ethanol; and acetate (Han et al., 2023). Alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, and furans are mainly synthesized via lipid auto-oxidation, the lipoxygenase pathway, and incomplete β-oxidation mediated by microorganisms. Ester production in shrimp paste is accomplished through microbial-mediated esterification and alcoholysis reactions, utilizing alcohols and acids as precursors. The degradation of amino acids gives rise to various aroma compounds, including pyrazines, branched-chain volatile compounds, and sulfur-containing compounds. Microorganisms play a critical role in cleaving the side chains of aromatic amino acids via amino acid lyase, generating phenol, indole, and methanethiol (Seo, You & Baek, 2024). The production of these flavor compounds is reliant on microbes. The composition and interactions of the microbial community are key determinants of the flavor profile. During natural fermentation, the assembly of the microbial community is influenced by the fermentation substrate and environmental factors. The unique fermentation system of SSP may generate a distinct microbial composition compared to CSP; however, these differences remain uncharacterized. Understanding the microbial community structure is crucial for elucidating flavor-forming substrates. While current research has predominantly focused on the flavor profiles and formation mechanisms of CSP, the synergistic effects of microbial succession and biochemical interactions during the secondary fermentation of SSP remain underexplored.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the differences between SSP and CSP in terms of sensory characteristics, volatile compounds, and microbial composition. Using CSP as a control, quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was employed to characterize the sensory profiles of SSP. Headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) coupled with partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to identify the characteristic aroma compounds. Furthermore, microbiome analysis was conducted to explore the microbial basis of flavor formation, elucidating the accumulation mechanisms of key aroma compounds from perspectives of substrate composition and microbial interactions. This study advances the theoretical understanding of flavor formation in SSP while offering important insights into analogous protein-starch co-fermentation processes found in other Asian traditional fermented foods.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

Reference standards of aroma compounds and C6-C24 n-alkanes were purchased from sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Analytical grade sodium chloride was purchased from Beijing chemical works (Beijing, China)

Samples

A total of 15 shrimp paste samples were included in this study, consisting of 15 conventional (CSP) and 5 sour (SSP) variants (Table S1). The CSP samples were prepared from fresh shrimp (Acetes chinensis and Acetes japonicus) using a standardized traditional method (Hu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022), and were obtained from six major production regions in China, encompassing a broad geographical spectrum: Shandong, Liaoning, Hebian, Zhejiang, and Guangdong. The preparation process was as follows: first, the raw shrimp were uniformly mixed with solar salt at a concentration of 10–35% (w/w). Subsequently, the salted mixture was tightly packed into sealed fermentation tanks to create an anaerobic environment for fermentation.

Sour shrimp paste (SSP) was produced from local freshwater shrimp (e.g., Caridina spp. and Palaemonetes sinensis) using a two-stage fermentation process. In the primary stage, the raw shrimp were uniformly mixed with 10% (w/w) solar salt and underwent anaerobic fermentation, a process identical to that used for CSP. In the secondary stage, the fermented base was mixed with 10–20% (w/w) rice wine lees (Laozao) and 5–10% (w/w) chili powder (Song et al., 2024). The final mixture was then sealed in fermentation tanks and fermented at ambient temperature for an additional two months (Fig. 1).

The pH was measured according to the method described by Lv et al. (Lv et al., 2023), using a calibrated digital pH meter after sample homogenization. Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content was analyzed according to the Chinese national standard method (GB 5009.228–2016) using an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Table S1). More details are described in the Supplemental Information.

Sensory and electronic noses analyses

Sensory evaluation

Sensory analyses were performed at controlled room temperature (20 °C). Samples, each accurately weighed at 3.0 g, were placed in 50 mL opaque vials and coded with a three-digit random number. The samples were presented simultaneously to the panels to evaluate the aroma. The sensory panelists for this study were recruited from Beijing Technology and Business University and China Agricultural University. Sensory panelists consisted of 20 individuals (9 males and 11 females, aged 20–35 years). The training for panelists focused on aroma identification and intensity scoring. The screening process consisted of a triangle test, where panelists identified the odd sample out of three (two identical and one different), and an intensity ranking test using standard solutions, where panelists had to identify aroma types and rank their intensities. All participants were informed that they were completely voluntary and free to participate in these subjects, and informed consent was obtained from them in full accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the involvement of human subjects in this study was granted by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Technology and Business University (reference number 2025–127).

The Quantitative Descriptive Analysis (QDA) method was employed to evaluate the aromatic characteristics of the samples (Yang et al., 2023). Panelists attended a total of two sessions (2 h each) over one month. During the first session, all samples were evaluated to generate appropriate aroma terms to describe them. The panel agreed upon seven aroma attributes (fishy, salty, stinky, sour, fruity, spicy, and soy sauce notes). Corresponding reference standards, including commercially available odorants and natural products, were used to define each of these attributes (Table S2). To ensure that each judge was able to accurately identify these aroma characteristics, sensory training was conducted for judges using these standards in session 2. In formal sessions, panelists were asked to evaluate the intensity of seven aroma attributes for all samples, with each of the five samples per session presented in a randomized order and evaluated in duplicate. The intensity of each aroma attribute was scored using an 11-point scale (0: The aroma attribute is not perceptible; 1–3: The aroma attribute is clearly perceptible but at a low level; 4–6: The aroma attribute is easily perceptible at a moderate level; 7–9: The aroma attribute is strong and dominant; 10: The aroma attribute is extremely intense). Panel performance was assessed using PanelCheck software. The repeatability of individual judges was evaluated by examining their agreement in scoring the duplicate samples, alongside analyses of variance for the effects of sample, judge, and presentation.

Electronic noses analysis

The flavor profiles of samples were analyzed using an electronic nose (E-nose) system (Airsense, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with 10 metal-oxide sensors. The samples (5.0 g) were placed in a 20 mL headspace bottle and incubated at 25 °C for 20 min. Then, the headspace gas was extracted with a syringe and injected into the E-nose system. Before the experiment, the sensors were purged with clean air for 100 s, and the total acquisition time was 240 s.

Volatile compounds analysis

Extraction of flavor compounds by HS-SPME

Volatile compounds in shrimp paste were extracted by headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) following a previously established method (Xia et al., 2025). A CTC CombiPAL autosampler (CTC Analytics, Zwingen, Switzerland) with DVB/CAR/PDMS 50/30 μm SPME fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was used for the automatic HS-SPME. The sample was weighed accurately to 3.00 g in a 20 mL sample vial, mixed with 3 mL of saturated NaCl solution and 10 μL of internal standard (2-methyl-3-heptanone), then capped with a PTFE‑silicone septum. After equilibration at 40 °C for 30 min, the SPME fiber was inserted into the headspace of the vial to extract volatile compounds at 40 °C for 30 min. The equilibration and extraction processes were carried out at 500 rpm agitation.

GC–MS analysis

GC–MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph equipped with an HP-INNOWAX column (60 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA), and coupled with an Agilent 5975 mass spectrometry (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The carrier gas was helium with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The SPME fiber was thermally desorbed in the GC injector for 8 min at 250 °C with a 10:1 split mode. The oven temperature was as follows: initially, holding at 40 °C for 5 min; increasing to 250 °C at the rate of 5 °C/min, then held at 250 °C for 5 min. The temperatures of the ion source, transfer line, and quadrupole were 230 °C, 250 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV and in the full scan mode with data collected from m/z 30–350.

The identification of volatile compounds from full scan mode data was achieved by comparing the retention indices (RIs) and mass spectra with those of reference standards. Compounds that lacked a standard were identified by comparing the retention indices (RIs) calculated with a C6-C24 n-alkane series under the same chromatographic conditions and mass spectra with compounds in the NIST 14 mass spectral library. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Microbial macrogenome analysis

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States) and kept at −80 °C before further use.

PCR amplification

The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primer pairs 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R(5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) by T100 Thermal Cycler PCR thermocycler (BIO-RAD, USA). The fungal ITS region (ITS1/ITS2) was amplified using primer pairs ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′) with a T100 Thermal Cycler PCR thermocycler (BIO-RAD, USA). The PCR amplification cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturing at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, and a single extension at 72 °C for 10 min, and ended at 4 °C. The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions and quantified using the Quantus™ fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Illumina sequencing and bioinformatics processing

Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform/NovaSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw gene sequencing reads were demultiplexed, quality-filtered by Fastp version 0.20.0, and merged by FLASH version 1.2.7. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with a 97% similarity cutoff were clustered using UPARSE version 7.1, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed. The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed by the RDP Classifier (version 2.2) against the 16S rRNA database (Silva v138) using a confidence threshold of 0.7 (Zhong et al., 2021).

The raw sequencing reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database, with bacterial 16S rRNA (SRR339976) and fungal ITS (SRR339991) deposited separately under BioProject PRJNA1277469.

Statistical analysis

Partial least-squares regression (PLSR) was performed by SIMCA (version 14.1 from Umetrics, Sweden). One-way ANOVA (p < 0.05, Duncan's multiple range test) and Mann-Whitney U test were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 24 (International Business Machines Corporation, USA). Bioinformatic analysis of the microbiota was carried out using the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Based on the OTUs information, alpha diversity indices, including observed OTUs, Chao1 richness, Shannon index, and coverage, were calculated with Mothur v1.30.1.

Results and discussion

Sensory profiles

Quantitative Descriptive Analysis (QDA) elucidated significant sensory disparities in aroma between CSP and SSP. CSPs were characterized by a more intense fishy, salty, and stink aroma (Fig. 1). Specifically, the fishy attribute of CSP registered a mean score of 6.2, which was substantially higher than that of the SSP (3.4). Moreover, CSPs manifested a robust salty aroma with an intensity of 5.4, surpassing that of SSPs (3.2). On the other hand, SSP displayed unique fruity, sour, and soy sauce aromas. Its fruity aroma had a mean intensity score of 4.2, significantly higher than the 1.0 score of CSP. The mean sour aroma scores were 3.8 for SSP samples, compared with 1.5 for CSP (Fig. 1).

An experimental score of 3 or more in a QDA indicates that the feature can be accurately identified in the sample (Yang et al., 2023). The scores for the three aroma attributes (fruity; sour; and soy sauce) fell on either side of that threshold for different types of samples. These three aroma attributes were recognized and distinctively perceived in SSP but were not perceptible in CSP. It is worth noting that this sour; fruity aroma profile is also found in traditional Asian fermented foods; such as sour fish and sour meat (P. Gao; Jiang; Xu; Yang; & Xia; 2020). The production of these foods involves a method of co-fermentation of meat and grains; which is similar to the method employed in SSP (Zhong et al., 2021). We hypothesize that these distinctive aromas are key sensory signatures given to the product by the meat-grain co-fermentation process.

Volatile compounds

E-nose analysis revealed distinct response patterns between sour shrimp paste (SSP) and conventional shrimp paste (CSP) as shown in the radar plot (Fig. S1). The radar plot showed that SSP displayed significantly higher signal intensities for sensors W1W, W2S, and W5S compared to CSP. These findings demonstrate that the two types of shrimp paste possess distinct volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles. To further elucidate the specific volatile compounds underlying these distinct VOC profiles, GC–MS analysis was performed. A total of 77 volatile compounds were identified across 15 shrimp paste samples. Among these, CSP and SSP contained 64 and 61 aroma compounds, and exhibited 16 and 13 unique volatile compounds, respectively (Table 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) based on aroma compound data revealed that the first two principal components accounted for 58.1% and 11.6% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2B). A clear separation was observed between CSP and SSP. CSP samples from five different geographical origins all clustered within the negative quadrant of PC1, whereas SSP samples were distributed across the positive half of PC1 (Fig. 2B). This pattern demonstrates that differences in fermentation type exerted a stronger influence on volatile profiles than regional production variations. Furthermore, the distribution of sample scores revealed that CSP samples were more tightly clustered compared to SSP, despite originating from multiple regions (Fig. 2B). This greater dispersion among SSP samples suggests weaker process stability during the secondary fermentation stage, leading to higher variability in aroma composition. CSP samples contained a higher abundance and diversity of furans, pyridines, and purazines, while SSP samples were characterized by a richer profile of esters, alcohols, and acids, both in variety and concentration (Table 2, Fig. 2B).

The partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model was employed to classify the shrimp paste samples into two groups: conventional shrimp paste (CSP) and sour shrimp paste (SSP). The score plot revealed a clear separation between these two groups (Fig. 2C). The model identified 45 compounds with variable importance in projection (VIP) scores greater than 1.0 as the most significant discriminators. These key biomarkers included 26 esters, 6 alcohols, and 4 acids, among other compounds. These compounds play a crucial role in the discrimination between SSP and CSP and serve as key determinants of their distinctive aroma characteristics (Fig. 2C).

Esters

Esters represented the most diverse category of volatile compounds in both types of shrimp paste. A total of 28 esters were identified in SSP, with concentrations of 656.5–4717.6 mg/kg, representing 57.7–81.2% of the total volatiles. In contrast, 20 esters were detected in CSP at much lower concentrations, ranging from 0.7 to 73.1 mg/kg in total (Table 2). Esters are usually classified as acetates and ethyl esters: acetates, formed from acetic acid and alcohols, and ethyl esters, formed mainly from ethanol and free fatty acids (Sumby et al., 2010). Compared to CSP; SSP contains a large number of esters; especially ethyl esters; which may be related to the high amount of ethanol produced by the fermentation of glutinous rice (Qian et al., 2023). Ethyl esters formed from short-chain fatty acids; such as ethyl acetate; ethyl isobutanoate; ethyl butanoate; ethyl 2-methylbutanoate; ethyl 3-methylbutanoate; and ethyl propanoate; tend to have a low threshold and contribute mainly to fruity notes such as pineapple; banana; strawberry; cherry; and green apple (Xu; Zhao; Liu; Zhang; & Sun; 2021). Long-chain fatty acid ethyl esters such as ethyl hexadecanoate; ethyl myristate; and ethyl decanoate tend to give a fatty; waxy; and soapy aroma (Xu et al., 2022). In addition; ethyl 3-phenylpropionate adds a floral and sweet aroma. Sulfur-containing esters such as ethyl 3-(methylthio)-propionate contribute to the fruity aroma while providing some distinctive notes of tomato; onion; and meat (Table 2). In other acid-fermented meat products; it has been observed that ester compounds are also relatively abundant. For example; in Suanyu (sour fish); 16 esters were identified (P. Gao et al., 2020); and in Suanrou (sour meat); 44 esters were detected (Zhong et al., 2021). A notable consistency was observed between the present study and prior findings; where acidic fermentation variants consistently exhibited higher quantities and contents of esters relative to mono-fermented fish (approximately 10 esters) and meat products (up to 21 esters) (P. Gao et al., 2025).

Alcohols and acids

Alcohols were identified as another prominent volatile compound in the samples, exhibiting significantly higher concentrations in SSP (98.3–538.1 mg/kg) compared to CSP (1.2–58.3 mg/kg), exceeding the latter by more than tenfold. In fermented foods, alcohols originate primarily from the metabolism of sugars, amino acids, and fatty acids. Specifically, sugar metabolism produces ethanol primarily through the pentose phosphate pathway and glycolysis, while other alcohols mainly derive from amino acid degradation and fatty acid oxidation (Hazelwood et al., 2008). In theory; shrimp paste contains more abundant amino acids and fatty acids than grains; and therefore has greater alcohol-producing potential (R. Gao et al., 2023). However, our findings showed that co-fermentation with grains produced more alcohol than mono-fermentation of shrimp alone (Fig. 2D). This apparent paradox may be due to distinctive microbial activities within the co-fermentation system. Among these alcohols detected, only 1-pentene-3-ol in SSP exceeded its odor threshold, imparting green, radish-like, and fruity notes (Table 2). It is noteworthy, however, that most alcohols did not directly contribute to the aroma due to their concentrations below perceptual thresholds. Instead, they function primarily as key precursors for the formation of other odor-active compounds during fermentation and storage, thereby indirectly shaping the overall flavor profile (Xu et al., 2021). CSP contains minimal acid content (<1 mg/kg), whereas SSP exhibits substantially higher concentrations, ranging from 3.0 mg/kg to 51.7 mg/kg (Table 2). Acids such as acetic acid, butanoic acid, and 2-methylbutanoic acid are known for their pronounced sour, cheese, and vinegar notes, playing a crucial role in shaping the sour profile of fermented shrimp paste.

Aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes and ketones are present in significantly higher concentrations in SSP compared to CSP (Fig. 2D). Aldehydes and ketones serve as key aromatic constituents in processed meat products, often exhibiting low sensory thresholds that can significantly influence and modulate product aroma profiles (P. Gao et al., 2025). The analysis of samples revealed the presence of eight aldehyde and ketone compounds; with hexanal; 3-methylpentanal; and 3-hexanone being more abundant in the SSP; imparting green grass; fruity; and sweet aromas (Gemert, 2011). In SSP; the elevated levels of hexanal and 3-hexanone are primarily derived from the plant-based ingredients; such as chili peppers and glutinous rice; rather than the shrimp itself. The enzymatic lipoxygenase pathway in plants directly generates hexanal from linoleic acid upon tissue disruption (Matsui, 2006). Further, six‐carbon aldehydes can be converted into corresponding six‐carbon ketones under the action of microorganisms, a biotransformation primarily facilitated by microbial enzymes, specifically alcohol dehydrogenases (ADHs) and aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs). Conversely, 2-heptanone and 2-methylbutanal were found in higher concentrations in CSP, contributing to fruity, spicy, nutty, and fatty notes (Table 2). In CSP, high shrimp content provides abundant precursors: free fatty acids (e.g., decanoic acid) for 2-heptanone via microbial β-oxidation, and branched-chain amino acids (e.g., isoleucine) for 2-methylbutanal via Strecker degradation. The richer availability of these shrimp-derived precursors in CSP leads to higher concentrations of these characteristic flavor compounds.

Others

Furan, pyridines, and pyrazines are heterocyclic compounds. Among them, pyridines and pyrazines are nitrogen-containing heterocycles, while furan is an oxygen-containing heterocycle. All three classes of compounds were detected in higher varieties and concentrations in CSP compared to SSP (Table 2, Fig. 2D). Pyrazines and furans, which are formed primarily via the lipid oxidation and Maillard reaction, were found at significantly higher concentrations in CSP. These compounds contribute nutty, earthy, cocoa-like, and burnt notes (A. Sun, Wu, et al., 2022). In contrast; the overall abundance of pyrazines and furans was markedly reduced in SSP. This discrepancy can be attributed to the differential pH requirements of the Maillard reaction; which proceeds more readily under neutral to weakly alkaline conditions. In contrast; the SSP system is characterized by a distinctly acidic environment; with a pH range of 5.07–6.11 (Table S1); thereby suppressing the formation of Maillard-derived pyrazines (Zhang, Cao, Tong and Xu, 2019). Notably; in SSP samples; the incorporation of plant-based ingredients such as glutinous rice and pepper introduces a broader spectrum of flavor-active compounds. These include terpenoids; along with abundant C₆ aldehydes; alcohols; and their derivatives (Skaliter et al., 2022). Representative compounds contributing to this diverse flavor profile include β-myrcene, hexanal, ethyl hexanoate, phenethyl alcohol, and ethyl 3-phenylpropanoate (Table 2). These components enhance the aromatic profile and contribute to the distinctive flavor of SSP.

Dominant microbiota and species

According to the 97% similarity level, all samples' sequences were clustered, and a total of 1894 OTUs of bacteria and 659 OTUs of fungi were obtained. The Shannon, Simpson, Chao, and ACE indices were used to represent the α-diversity of the microbial community in shrimp paste samples (Table S3). Alpha-diversity analysis revealed no significant differences in bacterial communities between SSP and CSP. In contrast, significant differences were observed in fungal diversity, with SSP exhibiting markedly higher ACE and Chao indices compared to CSP (ACE: 143.92 vs 55.33, p < 0.001; Chao: 142.16 vs 54.31, p < 0.001) (Table S3). These elevated fungal richness estimators in SSP suggest a more diverse and abundant fungal community (Zhong et al., 2021). SSP was found to harbor 32 bacterial genera and 60 fungal genera that were completely undetectable in CSP (Fig. 4A). These exclusive microbial groups represented 14.5% and 54.5% of the total bacterial and fungal genera of SSP, respectively, contributing to its distinctive microbial signature. Both the CSP and the SSP undergo sealed fermentation, during which the residual oxygen in the headspace is gradually consumed by aerobic microorganisms. These conditions favor the growth of facultative and strict anaerobes, which together dominate the microbial communities.

Microbial community analysis of SSP and CSP: (A) Genus-level composition across different fermentation types; (B) Differentially abundant microbes between CSP and SSP; (C) Distribution patterns of microbiota among samples.

Beta-diversity analysis, based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and visualized using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), revealed distinct clustering patterns for both bacterial and fungal communities. A significant separation between SSP and CSP was observed along the primary coordinate axes for bacteria (PCo1: 32.45% of variance; PCo2: 17.4%) and for fungi (PCo1: 25.85%; PCo2: 20.06%), indicating pronounced differences in overall microbial structure (Fig. 3CD). PERMANOVA tests confirmed these patterns, with significant effects of group on bacterial (p = 0.001, R2 = 0.83) composition. The greater within-group variation observed in CSP suggests that the geographic origin exerts a non-negligible influence on its microbial composition (Fig. 3CD). These findings highlight that the microbial community structure and diversity are strongly influenced by both fermentation technology and region.

Bacterial species

Notable variations in microbial composition were observed between the two types of shrimp paste. At the phylum level, Bacillota was the most abundant bacterial group in both types of shrimp paste, accounting for 95.39% in SSP and 67.05% in CSP. It was followed by Pseudomonadota, which represented 24.54% of the total bacterial community in CSP, making it the second most abundant phylum in this group (Fig. 3A). In the SSP, at the genus level, Ligilobacillus, Peptoniphilus, and Vagococcus were identified as predominant bacterial species (Fig. 4). Ligilobacillus and Vagococcus are two types of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) that constitute 33.85% and 7.02%, respectively, of the total bacterial population in SSP, compared to only 0.39% and 0.8% in CSP. This high abundance of LAB can rapidly lower the pH of the fermentation system, creating an acidic environment with antibacterial properties (Che et al., 2021; Y. Sun, Hua, et al., 2022). Ligilobacillus; such as Ligilobacillus salivarius; exhibit functional properties and are commonly incorporated into probiotic capsules or powders to modulate intestinal flora equilibrium and enhance immune responses (Yang et al., 2024). Bacteriocins produced by Ligilobacillus demonstrate inhibitory activity against foodborne pathogens like Listeria monocytogenes; making them valuable natural preservatives in the food industry (Yang et al., 2024). Peptoniphilus; a Gram-positive anaerobic coccobacillus; is characterized by its unique metabolic pathway that utilizes tryptone and amino acids as energy sources; primarily generating butyric acid and acetic acid as metabolic end-products (Nzeteu, Trego, Abram & O'Flaherty, 2018). Recent studies suggest that this bacterium may contribute significantly to proteolysis; facilitating the production of both amino acids and short-chain fatty acids through protein degradation (Y. Gao et al., 2021). Functional prediction based on PICRUSt2 further revealed that bacteria in SSP exhibited higher predicted abundances of DNA-directed RNA polymerase (EC 2.7.7.6) and fumarate reductase (EC 1.3.5.4); indicating enhanced transcriptional activity and anaerobic energy metabolism (Fig. S2). Furthermore; a genus-based analysis of variance revealed that Peptostreptococcus; Tissierella; W5030; and Weissella were significantly more abundant in SSP than in CSP. The microbial composition has a high similarity to that of sourdough meat; which also undergoes protein-starch co-fermentation (Lv et al., 2023). These organisms may therefore be responsible for the unique flavor of SSP.

Microbial community composition and beta diversity analysis of SSP and CSP. Bacterial (A) and fungal (B) community composition; Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plots illustrating the structural separation of bacterial (C) and fungal (D) communities between SSP and CSP groups.

Conversely, in CSP, Tetragenococcus was identified as the predominant bacterium, constituting 52.02% of the microbial population, followed by Vibrio at 10.19% (Fig. 4). Tetragenococcus played a pivotal role in the proteolysis and conversion of amino acids during shrimp paste fermentation due to its remarkable salt tolerance (Hu et al., 2024). As a halophilic LAB; Tetragenococcus produces a considerable number of organic acids; such as lactic acid; tartaric acid; and malic acid; which accumulate significantly in the middle of fermentation and become the dominant flora in CSP in the later stages of fermentation (Hu et al., 2024). Previous studies have reported a positive correlation between Tetragenococcus and the formation of furans and pyrazines in shrimp paste; which could explain the higher abundance of these compounds observed in CSP with a predominance of Tetragenococcus in the present study (Hu et al., 2024). Vibrio is a genus of Gram-negative bacteria prevalent in marine and brackish water environments. Primarily carried into the fermentation system by raw shrimp; it constitutes a significantly higher proportion of the microbial population in CSP compared to SSP (0.02%). This difference is likely attributable to the low-pH fermentation environment of SSP; which effectively suppresses Vibrio growth (Sampaio et al., 2022).

Fungal species

The fungal communities of the two types of shrimp paste exhibited considerable divergence at the phylum level. In SSP, Basidiomycota was the most abundant fungal phylum, accounting for 69.06%, followed by Ascomycota (28.85%). In contrast, Ascomycota was the dominant phylum in CSP, representing 47.91% of the fungal community (Fig. 3B). At the genus level, Wallemia was the predominant fungus in SSP, constituting 64.78% of the sample, a significantly higher proportion compared to CSP at 18.54% (Fig. 4). Wallemia is commonly present in foods with high osmotic pressures, particularly in salted, sweetened foods, and dried preserved products (Tian et al., 2022). SSP exhibited elevated levels of yeast fungi, with Saccharomyces and Metschnikowia comprising 4.73% and 5.50% of the sample, respectively, surpassing the levels found in CSP (≤ 05%) (Fig. 4). These microorganisms have strong fermentation characteristics and play a leading role in the alcoholic fermentation (Xu et al., 2021). They use carbohydrates to produce ethanol, concomitantly producing flavor compounds like higher alcohols and esters (Xu et al., 2021). The distinctive co-fermentation approach employed in SSP establishes a unique substrate environment, thereby facilitating the development of a distinct microbial community within the fermentation system. This distinctive microbial composition plays an important role in the flavor formation of SSP.

Functional prediction based on ITS1F sequencing using PICRUSt2 further revealed that the fungal community in SSP exhibited higher predicted abundances of KEGG enzymes involved in energy metabolism (EC 5.3.1.4, EC 3.6.3.14), protein degradation (EC 3.4.25.1, EC 3.1.2.15), and biosynthesis (EC 2.7.7.7, EC 2.7.7.6), along with elevated levels of signal transduction-related enzymes (EC 2.7.1.37 and EC 3.1.3.16), suggesting a stronger metabolic activity and environmental responsiveness in SSP (Fig. S2). Fungi are essential for the formation of flavor in protein-starch co-fermentation, and it has been shown that inhibition of fungi in sour meat fermentation systems greatly reduces the production of flavor compounds such as alcohols, esters, and aldehydes (Zhong et al., 2022).

Microbial-mediated aroma formation analysis

Screening of microorganisms correlated with aroma compounds

The Spearman method was employed to assess the correlation between key microorganisms and characteristic flavor compounds in SSP, including 21 esters, six alcohols, and four acids. The analysis revealed predominantly positive correlations between ten bacterial genera and these flavor compounds (Fig. 5A). Specifically, four bacterial genera exhibited lactic acid bacteria (LAB) metabolic traits: Ligilobacillus, Vagococcus, Carnobacterium, and Periweissella. These LABs possess the capability to metabolize sugars into pyruvate through anaerobic glycolysis, subsequently converting it to lactic acid via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalysis (Che et al., 2021; Y. Sun et al., 2022). Ligilobacillus and Vagococcus predominantly engage in homolactic fermentation, displaying efficient acidogenic properties. In contrast, Periweissella demonstrates heterotactic acid fermentation, leading to the production of lactic acid along with by-products such as ethanol and acetic acid. There is a significant positive correlation between the seven fungi and the characteristic substances (Fig. 5B). Among these, Metschnikowia, Wallemia, and Saccharomyces have high levels in SSP and may therefore play an important role in forming its characteristic flavor.

Metabolic analysis of characteristic aroma compounds

The characteristic aroma compounds in SSP were correlated and classified according to their carbon skeleton structure (Fig. 6). Ethanol was found to be an important precursor for the formation of esters, which mainly accumulate during the alcoholic fermentation of glutinous rice wine. Butyric acid and butanol are the main precursors of flavor compounds such as methyl butanoate, ethyl butanoate, and butyl butanoate in SSP. These are produced primarily via the acetone-butanol fermentation pathway of anaerobic bacteria (e.g., Clostridium, Peptostreptococcus, and Peptoniphilus), which use carbohydrates (such as starch or sugar) as substrates. Butyryl-CoA is generated via the pyruvate pathway and converted into butanol and butyric acid through various microbial metabolic processes. Butanoyl-CoA is reduced to butanal by butyraldehyde dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.1.57) and then to butanol by alcohol dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.1). Butanoic acid can be produced in two ways. One way is the CoA transferase pathway, in which butanoyl-CoA is directly converted to butanoic acid by the alpha subunit of acetate CoA/acetoacetate CoA-transferase (EC 2.8.3.8), and the other is the phosphotransferase pathway. In this pathway, butanoyl-CoA is first catalyzed by phosphotransbutyrylase (EC 2.3.1.19) to form butanoyl-phosphate. This is then converted to butanoic acid by butyrate kinase (EC 2.7.2.7) (Koh et al., 2016). The fermentation of glutinous rice provides the carbohydrates necessary for the formation of butyl esters, while the anaerobic fermentation of SSP involving Clostridium promotes their accumulation.

Classification of characteristic aroma compounds by carbon skeletons and associated biosynthetic pathways.

Branched-chain fatty acids and alcohols are primarily produced through the transamination and decarboxylation of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including L-leucine, L-valine, and L-isoleucine (Fig. 6). Clostridium can metabolize BCAAs through Stickland fermentation, converting amino acids into short-chain fatty acids and alcohols. Potential products include isobutanol and isoamyl alcohol (Koh et al., 2016). Some lactic acid bacteria possess transaminases and decarboxylases that can degrade BCAAs. Through the Ehrlich pathway; yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae indirectly produce branched-chain alcohols via the metabolic intermediates of BCAAs (Koh et al., 2016). Phenylethyl alcohol; phenylacetic acid; and aromatic esters primarily originate from the metabolism of phenylalanine (P. Gao et al., 2025). Saccharomyces cerevisiae produces high levels of phenylethyl alcohol. In other yeast species; such as Kluyveromyces lactis and Hanseniaspora spp.; small amounts of phenylacetic acid and aromatic esters are produced simultaneously (Gobbi et al., 2013). By contrast; bacteria such as Clostridium tend to produce phenylacetic acid via the Stickland reaction under anaerobic conditions. Lactic acid bacteria; however; usually lack phenylalanine deaminase or phenylpyruvate decarboxylase; leading to amino acid metabolism dominated by transamination and the production of branched-chain fatty acids rather than aromatic derivatives (P. Gao et al., 2025). SSP contains various medium- and long-chain fatty acid ethyl esters; the fatty acids of which are mainly derived from the decomposition of triglycerides in shrimp. In summary; the glutinous rice fermentation system provides an abundance of alcohols and yeast microbial communities (Anzian et al., 2024) while shrimp paste is a rich source of amino acids and fatty acids (López-Cervantes et al., 2006; Zhou et al., 2021). During the process of mixed fermentation, metabolic products are exchanged between the microorganisms present in both the glutinous rice and shrimp paste systems. This collaborative effort leads to the formation of distinctive aroma compounds that give SSPs their characteristic odors.

Conclusion and prospects

In this study, the differences in aroma compounds, flavor profiles, and core microbiota have been systematically analyzed between conventional shrimp paste and the distinct variety known as sour shrimp paste. SSP exhibited more intense fruity, sour, and soy sauce aroma characteristics than CSP, which was attributed to its significantly higher concentrations of esters, alcohols, acids, aldehydes, and ketones. The aroma profile of CSP is mainly dominated by salty and fishy notes, and its volatile components are much lower in terms of the number of substances and overall concentration. The predominant bacteria found in SSP were Ligilobacillus, Peptoniphilus, and Vagococcus, while the primary fungi present include Wallemia, Aspergillus, Saccharomyces, and Metschnikowia. SSP, in contrast to CSP, exhibits higher levels of Wallemia, lactic acid bacteria, and yeast. Through correlation analysis, it was observed that Ligilobacillus, Peptoniphilus, Metschnikowia, Wallemia, and Saccharomyces exhibit strong correlations with the production of esters and alcohols. The characteristic aroma compounds of SSP were mainly concentrated in metabolites related to amino acid metabolism, particularly those associated with the branched-chain amino acid and phenylalanine metabolic pathways. The shrimp paste provided a rich amino acid matrix, and the fermented rice system produced a large quantity of alcohol, creating a unique flavor through the action of microbial communities such as yeast, lactic acid bacteria, Peptoniphilus, and Clostridium in the system.

This study focuses on elucidating the differences driven by processing technology. Nevertheless, the potential influence of shrimp raw material variations should not be overlooked. Future research may further explore this aspect to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors shaping the unique flavor and microbial community of sour shrimp paste.
