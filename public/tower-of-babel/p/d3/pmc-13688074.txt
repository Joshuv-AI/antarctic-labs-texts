Impact of RAS/RAF Mutations on Metastatic Treatment Response Following Total Neoadjuvant Therapy in Patients With Stage IV Rectal Cancer
Authors: Tracy R. Fitzsimmons, Z. Bunjo, E. L. Neo, A. Badiei, M. Penniment, S. Selva‐Nayagam, A. Ruszkiewicz, J. Wong, T. Sammour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe underlying biology and genetic status of rectal cancer can guide treatment options. However, there is limited understanding of the impact of underlying mutational status of RAS/RAF on TNT response in metastatic rectal cancer. This study aims to determine treatment response to TNT in patients harboring RAS/RAF mutations.MethodsThis prospective multicenter observational study identified patients diagnosed with synchronous metastatic rectal cancer suitable to receive personalized TNT (pTNT) treated with curative intent between 2019 and 2024. Metastatic treatment response to pTNT was compared between patient groups based on RAS/RAF mutational status. Three‐year disease free (DFS) and overall survival (OS) were compared.ResultsSeventy Stage IV rectal cancer patients were treated with curative intent and 49 had genetic testing available for inclusion. Among them, 27 (55.1%) patients had mutations (mRAS group) and 22 patients (44.9%) were wild‐type (wtRAS group). mRAS patients were older than wtRAS patients (median 65.0 [60.0–71.0] vs. 55.5 years [39.0–69.25] respectively, p = 0.037). Synchronous metastases occurred more commonly in the liver than in the lungs (53.1% vs. 16.3%, p = 0.014). Complete metastatic tumor response to TNT was demonstrated in half as many mRAS as wtRAS patients (22.2% vs. 40.9%, p = 0.158) and disease progression was higher in patients harboring RAS mutations (37.0% vs. 22.7%, p = 0.280), but neither were significant. Differences in 3‐year DFS and OS between the two groups did not reach significance.ConclusionRAS/RAF mutations may negatively impact metastatic tumor response rates in curative Stage IV rectal cancer patients treated with pTNT. Larger scale studies are needed to verify these results.

Introduction

The underlying biologic mutation and genetic status of rectal cancer can guide treatment options due to differential response. Specifically, mutations in KRAS or BRAF genes are predictive of poor treatment response to anti‐epidermal growth factor receptor (anti‐EGFR) and confer worse survival outcomes in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer [1]. RAS/RAF mutations with concurrent mutations in related genes, including PIK3CA, are associated with anti‐EGFR resistance [2]. While these studies have guided the development of targeted therapies, benefits are limited to patients with wild‐type status. Study cohorts are heterogeneous, combining metastatic colon and rectal cancer patients, overlooking the significant differences in primary tumor biology, clinical characteristics, and anatomical considerations between the two cancer types.

Treatment pathways for rectal cancer are distinct from colon cancer, evidenced by the inclusion of total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) into international rectal cancer guidelines [3]. Recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of TNT management pathways demonstrate improved overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), complete response rates, and subsequent organ preservation [4, 5, 6]. However, these trials exclusively focused on Stage II and III rectal cancer, and the utility of TNT in metastatic Stage IV rectal cancer remains understudied by comparison [7].

Curative intent treatment strategies for Stage IV rectal cancer involve management of the primary rectal tumor and distant metastases with a combination of radiotherapy, surgical resection, and systemic chemotherapy. Sequencing is often complicated by disease factors. When the primary rectal tumor is locally advanced, local control may need to be prioritized, potentially leaving metastases untreated for a prolonged period. Alternatively, upfront systemic therapy in induction TNT protocols can result in local disease progression, making treatment sequencing a difficult challenge to navigate.

Optimal management of Stage IV rectal cancer is complicated when underlying oncogene mutations are revealed. Few studies assess impact of RAS/RAF mutational status on treatment response to TNT management of rectal cancer and may be dependent on sequencing [8, 9, 10]. Additionally, like landmark TNT trials, reported survival outcomes are restricted to Stage II and III rectal cancer, highlighting the paucity of data relating to Stage IV disease.

As TNT continues to expand in rectal cancer management and include Stage IV disease, there is a need to ensure that treatment strategies are personalized and disease biology directed. Understanding the significance of mutations in RAS/RAF oncogenes in patients with metastatic rectal cancer may provide optimal treatment strategies for this complex patient group. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the metastatic treatment response to TNT in Stage IV rectal cancer patients harboring RAS/RAF mutations.

Methods

This prospective, multicenter, and observational cohort study was undertaken with ethical approval from the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/15/RAH/186) and St Andrew's Hospital Research and Ethics Committee (#117).

Patient Selection

Between January 2019 and April 2025, patients were prospectively identified from weekly colorectal cancer oncology multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings across three hospitals. Adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) diagnosed with synchronous metastatic rectal cancer suitable to receive TNT with curative intent were included. Clinical staging included pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for local disease and computer tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis for distant disease. MRI liver or positron emission tomography (PET) scan was selectively used to confirm metastatic disease location where indicated. Synchronous metastatic disease was defined as present at diagnosis of the primary rectal tumor according to current international guidelines [11, 12]. Intention to treat metastatic disease was based on referral and discussion at hepatobiliary or thoracic MDT meetings and other subspecialties as appropriate. Curative intent of metastatic nodal disease was determined by site‐specific surgical expertise and experience on an individual basis. Patients diagnosed with Stage I–III rectal cancer with no metastatic disease, those treated with palliative intent, or those who declined treatment were excluded.

Treatment

Patients received personalized TNT (pTNT) with induction or consolidation chemotherapy, with treatment sequencing decided through multidisciplinary consensus [13]. Induction TNT (iTNT) consisting of CAPOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin) every three weeks for 5–6 cycles or FOLFOX (5‐Fluorouracil [5FU], leucovorin, and oxaliplatin) every 2 weeks for eight cycles with or without targeted therapy (bevacizumab, cetuximab, or panitumumab) and followed by LC‐CRT with 50.4 Gy external beam modulated radiation delivered in 25 fractions with concurrent intravenous 5FU or oral capecitabine [13]. Treatment response was assessed halfway through induction chemotherapy (iTNT) with MRI and CT scans, and in cases where local progression was observed, patients were accelerated to chemoradiotherapy or surgery. When local symptom control was required, TNT with consolidation chemotherapy (cTNT) was administered and determined on a case‐by‐case basis. Treatment response was assessed at the completion of TNT administration (within 10 weeks post‐iTNT, or 4 weeks post‐cTNT) with MRI, CT, and flexible sigmoidoscopy and subsequent MDT discussion. Where clinical complete response (cCR) in the primary rectal tumor was observed, patients were offered non‐operative management (NOM) of the rectum with an active surveillance protocol. Where near complete response (nCR) was observed, patients were restaged after a further 3 months in a shared decision between patient and clinician [14]. Surgical resection was recommended for an incomplete response in the primary tumor. Additional management of distant metastases included monitoring, local ablative therapy including stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR), or surgical resection with sequencing based on referral and further discussion at an appropriate MDT.

Follow‐Up

Where patients achieved cCR of the primary rectal tumor after TNT and underwent NOM, an active surveillance protocol included three monthly digital rectal examinations (DRE), flexible sigmoidoscopy, carcinoembryonic antigen testing, and MRI for 12 months, then biannually for 5 years, and annual CT chest/abdomen for 5 years. Colonoscopies were scheduled according to National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) colorectal cancer surveillance guidelines at 1, 6, and 11 years [15]. Post‐operative surveillance was in accordance with NHMRC guidelines [15].

Mutational Analysis

All genetic profiling was undertaken by the Molecular Oncology Laboratory within SA Pathology. DNA was extracted from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded diagnostic biopsy tissue samples, and mutation analysis of target genes undertaken to detect variants within BRAF, RAS (KRAS, NRAS), KIT, EGFR, and PIK genes using the OncoFocus panel V3.0 (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, California, USA). Microsatellite instability and mismatch repair status were determined by a combination of immunohistochemistry and methylation analysis. Patients with mutations in KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, or PIK3CA formed the mRAS group, and patients without mutations formed the wild‐type group (wtRAS).

Data Collection

Prospective data collection included patient demographics and clinical disease parameters. Primary tumor characteristics included TNM staging, tumor height, extramural vascular invasion (EMVI), presence of lateral pelvic lymph nodes, and metastatic disease location [16]. TNT administration was documented, including chemotherapy agent, radiotherapy type and dose, compliance, and toxicity, using a prospectively collected and well‐established database [17, 18].

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was metastatic disease response defined as complete, partial, or progressed based on review of restaging MRI and CT by expert radiologists at MDT discussions informed by response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST) criteria [19]. Secondary study endpoints included DFS and OS. DFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis until last local or distant metastatic regrowth or recurrence not treated with curative intent [20]. Local regrowth was defined as rectal tumor growth visualized on clinical examination (with pathological confirmation as necessary) or medical imaging after achieving a cCR, or conversion from nCR to cCR after completion of TNT and restaging. Local recurrence was defined as presence of rectal tumor after initial R0 (surgical margins ≥ 1 mm on histopathology) surgical resection of the primary tumor. Metastatic regrowth was defined as tumor growth at the same site of distant disease at presentation after either: a complete metastatic response at restaging, metastatic resection with clear surgical margins, a partial metastatic disease response and subsequent R0 resection, or alternative curative treatment (for example SABR). Distant recurrence was defined as metastatic tumor growth occurring at distant sites distinct from the initial site of distant disease present at the time of rectal cancer diagnosis. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis until last follow‐up date or death. Other key endpoints included primary tumor complete response rate and overall complete response rate, defined as the proportion of patients who achieved an overall complete response in the primary rectal tumor together with a complete metastatic response.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.1.0, build 171 (IBM Corp. Armonk, New York, USA). Patient demographics, treatment details, and outcomes were presented as median (range) or mean (standard deviation) dependent on data distribution as determined by Shapiro‐Wilk test. Group comparisons were performed using Mann‐Whitney U test or Student's t‐test dependent on normality of data. Categorical data were reported as frequencies (percentages) with group comparisons performed using Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log‐rank test for group comparisons. Data were censored if patients were lost to follow‐up or alive without progression at the date of last follow‐up (October 4, 2025). Statistical significance was indicated when p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Rectal cancer patients (n = 557) were screened for inclusion (Figure 1). There were 150 Stage IV patients, of whom 80 were treated with palliative intent, leaving 70 suitable for and electing to receive TNT with curative intent. The study included 49 patients who were restaged with genetic testing. The mRAS patient group had 27 patients with KRAS, NRAS, BRAF or PIK3CA genetic mutations (n = 22 [81.5%], n = 3 [11.1%], n = 1 [3.7%], n = 1 [3.7%]) respectively (Table S1). Two patients with KRAS mutations had simultaneous TP53 or PIK3CA genetic mutations, and the patient with a BRAF mutation also had a PIK3CA mutation. The wtRAS group comprised 22 (44.9%) patients who did not have any mutations present.

Treatment and Compliance

Sequencing of TNT administration was similar between groups, with 72% and 74% receiving induction chemotherapy (Table 2). mRAS patients received CAPOX twice as often but less targeted therapy than wtRAS (CAPOX 48.1% vs. 22.7%; targeted therapy 18.5% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.023). Long‐course chemoradiotherapy was administered to 89.8% of all patients. Three mRAS patients did not receive any chemoradiotherapy due to a complete response at both primary rectal and metastatic disease sites after induction chemotherapy (n = 1); rectal tumor obstruction on induction chemotherapy and subsequent primary tumor resection (n = 1); or progression of distant disease and primary rectal tumor obstruction and death. Chemotherapy treatment compliance was similar across groups with 57.1% receiving their planned number of cycles and 98.0% receiving four or more cycles. mRAS patients experienced more Grade 3 toxicity including diarrhea, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, pulmonary symptoms, neutropenia, and colitis, compared to wtRAS patients, but this was not significantly different (25.9% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.075).

Response to Treatment

A complete metastatic tumor response to TNT occurred in half as many mRAS patients as wtRAS patients (22.2% vs. 40.9%, p = 0.158). Disease progression was more frequent in mRAS patients (37.0% vs. 22.7%, p = 0.280; Table 3). Overall, 18 (36.7%) patients exhibited neither complete metastatic response nor disease progression following TNT. Primary tumor cCR was less common in the mRAS group (14.4% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.018). A complete response was observed in both the metastatic and primary tumor sites in 12.2% of the cohort, with no difference between groups (14.8% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.678).

Discussion

Stage IV rectal cancer patients have a high risk of distant failure and stand to benefit from TNT, prioritizing systemic chemotherapy delivery before radical surgery. Adopting TNT in this patient cohort is an understudied area of research and much less is understood regarding the influence of RAS/RAF genetic status on metastatic responses in this context. This study demonstrates the negative impact of mutational RAS/RAF status on metastatic response after pTNT is delivered with curative intent for Stage IV rectal cancer patients. Primary rectal tumor response to TNT was worse in mRAS patients with a trend towards worse survival outcomes.

Few studies exist where a TNT management pathway is implemented as primary treatment for Stage IV rectal cancer, and even fewer describe outcomes with respect to RAS/RAF genetic status [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Meta‐analyses demonstrate RAS/RAF oncogene mutations to be indicative of poor prognosis in metastatic colorectal cancer particularly when considering primary tumor response [1, 2]. Comparisons with these studies are limited, though, because patient cohorts are heterogeneous, with the inclusion of colon and rectal cancer patients with metastases limited to the liver. Dabout et al. (2025) assessed outcomes of patients with metastatic rectal cancer after induction chemotherapy, separating patients with resectable synchronous metastases from those with unresectable disease, and included RAS/RAF status in selected analyses [26]. The chemotherapy regimen included eight planned cycles of FOLFIRINOX with or without the addition of targeted therapy (bevacizumab, cetuximab or panitumumab). Of those with resectable disease, “most” received (chemo)radiotherapy prior to resection of the rectal tumor and the median follow‐up was 40 months. Treatment responses demonstrated a lower complete metastatic tumor response rate in patients with resectable disease compared to ours (10.3% vs. 30.6%) and a comparable rate of progression (27.6% vs. 30.6%) [26]. These responses were coupled with higher metastatic and primary resection rates than in our study, 79% vs. 45.5% and 69% vs. 51.5%, respectively. When patients were stratified based on mutational status, the Dabout et al. study indicated that almost half (47%) of patients with RAS/RAF mutations underwent resection of both metastases and the primary rectal tumor, whereas only 35.3% of these patients in our study had both sites of disease resected. Patients with wild‐type RAS/RAF genotype endured more surgery, with 88% undergoing resection of both primary and metastatic disease compared to only 25% in our study. The median OS of patients with mRAS mutations were similar between their study and ours (39.9 vs. 38.8 months, respectively). The 3‐year OS was 37.9% for patients with resectable disease in the Dabout et al. study with no further description based on genetic status, but this was worse than either mRAS (53.0%) or wtRAS (75.0%) patients in our study.

Differences in treatment response between the two studies are likely explained by variation in chemotherapy protocols and radiotherapy administration. Patients in the Dabout et al. study received a chemotherapy backbone of FOLFIRINOX, with or without targeted therapy. In comparison, our patients primarily received CAPOX‐ or FOLFOX‐based chemotherapy (85.7%, n = 42) without the inclusion of irinotecan. Existing concerns surrounding treatment toxicity in TNT pathways and the known toxicity profile of irinotecan may further limit its inclusion in TNT protocols [27]. This is particularly relevant for Stage IV rectal cancer with evidence of irinotecan‐based chemotherapy potentially impeding liver resection, increasing the risk for postoperative liver failure [27, 28]. The similar median survival of the current study and that of Dabout et al. suggest that the role for irinotecan in TNT chemotherapy regimens warrants further research. Beyond conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, molecular characterization of rectal cancer has allowed development of targeted agents to VEGF or EGFR. Better treatment responses are mostly seen for wtRAS/RAF patients but the impact on survival outcomes remains inconsistent [1, 2, 29]. Patients in our cohort who received targeted therapy were predominantly in the wtRAS group and may have contributed to the survival benefit seen when compared to the mRAS group, albeit not statistically significant. Plausibly, the heterogeneity within the wtRAS group, where not all patients respond to targeted therapies, may also explain this result, but the current study size limits a more in‐depth analysis of identifying such patients [2].

While management strategies for Stage IV rectal cancer focus on metastatic disease, local control of the primary rectal tumor remains vital to evaluate treatment efficacy. The primary rectal tumor is more often locally advanced, with optimal disease control provided by neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy enabling NOM when cCR is observed. Only 56% of patients in the Dabout et al. study received chemoradiotherapy, potentially explaining the low cCR rate in the resectable cohort (3%) and the necessity for primary tumor resection in 69% of patients [26]. Comparatively, one‐third of our patients achieved cCR with significantly less in the mRAS group, a trend consistent with non‐metastatic rectal cancer patients [30]. Despite this, the local regrowth rate of mRAS patients electing NOM was comparable to wtRAS patients (25% vs. 20%), similar to regrowth rates reported in the OPRA study for non‐metastatic LARC patients [31]. The disparities in cCR rates between mRAS and wtRAS patients in our study were also reflected in worse OS for mRAS patients. The benefit of adopting iTNT in this patient cohort, particularly evident for wtRAS patients, provided acceptable local disease control leading to a delay in radical resection of the primary rectal tumor (by opting for NOM), a procedure that carries significant morbidity and quality‐of‐life implications. Further, recent studies show better functional outcomes for patients treated with TNT and highlight the need for patient‐reported outcomes and quality‐of‐life assessments in metastatic rectal cancer patient cohorts [31, 32].

Discussions surrounding quality‐of‐life implications of treatment options are important, particularly for younger patients. This raises the question of whether age‐related differences in tumor biology, including RAS/RAF genetic status, should be considered in treatment decisions. Our study showed that mRAS patients were significantly older than those in the wtRAS group, a trend consistent with published data [33]. While early‐onset metastatic rectal cancer (< 50 years) is associated with better OS, the influence of age in the current study appears limited [34]. This is likely because the median age of both groups was above that typically defined as “early‐onset”. Since metastatic tumor response rates did not differ between groups, age is unlikely to be a confounding factor for the primary endpoint of the current study. However, age plausibly may have contributed to the observed differences in primary tumor response rates. Younger patients demonstrate higher pCR rates compared to their older counterparts after neoadjuvant therapy and surgical resection [30]. In the setting of TNT, however, outcomes are equivocal for age related responses in non‐metastatic rectal cancer patients, and data from metastatic populations is scarce, highlighting an area for further investigation [30, 35].

The combination of systemic chemotherapy and metastatic resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for colorectal cancer, but the sequencing continues to be debated. Evidence is predominantly derived from mixed patient cohorts where left‐sided colon cancer is combined with rectal cancer. Amini et al. demonstrated that the importance of separating the two groups in an assessment of the impact of RAS mutations in colorectal cancer patients with resected liver metastases [36]. Rectal cancer patients with KRAS mutations showed similar survival outcomes to their wild‐type counterparts, but colon cancer patients experienced significantly worse prognoses [36]. Our data showed a non‐significant trend toward different survival outcomes for rectal cancer patients when stratified based on RAS mutational status. Other studies suggest that KRAS mutations are associated with worse survival outcomes in patients who do not undergo metastatic resection but is not an independent predictor [37]. TNT may assist in better selection of patients with potential to benefit from resection and save undue morbidity associated with surgery. Identification of these patients could be particularly helpful in the case of “disappearing liver metastases,” a conundrum often observed after neoadjuvant therapy [27].

This study has some limitations. Variation in chemotherapeutic agents and use of targeted therapies within the cohort may have influenced metastatic treatment response and survival outcomes, although this type of heterogeneity is a component of pragmatic real‐world studies. Age differences between groups may also be a confounding factor in the primary endpoint and survival analyses. Given the small patient cohort, larger studies with more comprehensive analysis may not only verify the current results but provide clarity surrounding the impact of these variables. Additionally, future studies incorporating quality‐of‐life metrics may assist discussions between clinicians and patients in treatment decisions and goals of care.

Overall, the presence of RAS/RAF mutations negatively impacts metastatic treatment response in Stage IV rectal cancer patients treated with TNT with curative intent. RAS/RAF genetic status also significantly influences primary rectal tumor response to TNT where patients harboring mutations respond poorly. While larger‐scale studies are needed to verify these results, TNT is a viable approach for management of Stage IV rectal cancer, and routine genetic testing continues to be important in this group.
