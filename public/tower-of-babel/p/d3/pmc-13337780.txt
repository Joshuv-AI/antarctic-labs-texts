Right Ventricular Dysfunction Score in the Prognostic Evaluation of Patients with Sepsis and Septic Shock
Authors: Sindhuja Kasinathan, Souvik Maitra, Puneet Khanna, Rahul K Anand, Meera Ekka, Bikash Ranjan Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsRight ventricular dysfunction (RVD) in sepsis carries a poor prognosis, and there is heterogeneity in its assessment methods. The RVD score, incorporating multiple domains of RV function, was originally designed for chronic heart failure grading. This study evaluated its ability to predict 28-day all-cause mortality in sepsis and septic shock.Patients and methodsIn this prospective observational cohort, 211 adults with septic shock were enrolled within 48 hours of diagnosis. The RVD score was calculated using five transthoracic echocardiographic parameters: Pulmonary artery (PA) systolic pressure (PASP), RV end-diastolic area/body surface area (RVEDA/BSA), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), tricuspid regurgitation (TR) grade, and inferior vena cava (IVC) collapsibility. One point was assigned to each component if the measurements deviated from predefined cut-offs. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/PASP ratio, a marker of RV-PA coupling, was also computed. Patients were followed for 28-day prognostic outcomes.ResultsAmong 189 analyzed patients, 39.68% were nonsurvivors. An RVD score of >2 and a TAPSE/PASP ratio of ≤0.447 mm/mm Hg predicted 28-day mortality with an area under receiving operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.857 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.801–0.914] and an AUROC of 0.883 (95% CI: 0.829–0.937), respectively. Their performance did not significantly differ (ΔAUROC = 0.026, p = 0.09). In a multivariable logistic regression model, after adjustment for key clinical covariates, RVD score of >2 [adjusted odds ratio (OR): 3.48, 95% CI: 1.37–8.83] remained an independent predictor of mortality.ConclusionRight ventricular dysfunction score of >2, as a composite marker of RVD, demonstrates a robust prognostic utility in sepsis and septic shock. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/PASP of ≤0.447 is a simpler alternative, given its comparable predictive performance for 28-day mortality.Clinical significanceRight ventricular dysfunction score of >2 and TAPSE/PASP of ≤0.447, being prognostic echocardiographic parameters, can be used to indicate the timing of incorporation of various RV-protective measures during the course of management of septic shock.How to cite this articleKasinathan S, Maitra S, Khanna P, Anand RK, Ekka M, Ray BR. Introduction

Right ventricular dysfunction occurs in 40–60% of patients with severe sepsis and septic shock, with this incidence varying depending on the defining criteria and methods of assessment used.1–3 The pathophysiology of RVD in sepsis is multifactorial and has a wide variability in its definitions.4–6 It occurs in a wide spectrum from isolated RV systolic dysfunction causing derangements in dimensional echocardiographic parameters to overt RV failure with features of congestion.6 Direct myocardial injury from circulating cytokines and altered adrenergic receptor signaling contributes to intrinsic contractile dysfunction.4,5 In parallel, extrinsic factors such as altered preload due to aggressive fluid resuscitation during early phases of septic shock, increased afterload due to pulmonary vascular dysfunction, reduced lung compliance, and concomitant LV dysfunction impair RV performance.4,5

The prognostic value of RV dysfunction has been studied in various clinical settings, such as early sepsis, severe sepsis, septic shock, and mechanically ventilated septic shock patients with concomitant acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in both the emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU).7,8 Acute cor pulmonale and pulmonary hypertension in septic patients result in a mortality of 28–30%.9,10 Commonly studied parameters include right ventricular to left ventricular ratio (RV/LV ratio), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV fractional area change (FAC), tricuspid annular peak systolic velocity (RV S’), RV fractional shortening, RV end-diastolic diameter (RVEDD), RV ejection fraction (RVEF), RV global longitudinal strain (RV Str), RV myocardial performance index (RV MPI).1,3,5–7 The measured parameters are dynamic, influenced by the underlying disease, loading conditions, use of fluids and vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, timing of echocardiography, and image quality.1,3,6 Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE)/pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) ratio has been established as a non-invasive marker of right-sided ventriculoarterial coupling, as it correlates well with invasively derived RV systolic elastance-to-arterial elastance ratios.11,12 Previous studies in the septic shock population have reported a TAPSE/PASP cut-off in the range of 0.50 mm/mm Hg for mortality prediction.11

A combination of various such RV parameters has been incorporated into scoring systems like the RV global dysfunction (RVGD) score, although it has been described primarily in cardiac conditions.13 Recently, a novel five-parameter comprehensive RVD score, including PASP, RV end-diastolic area/body surface area (RVEDA/BSA), TAPSE, tricuspid regurgitation (TR) grade, and inferior vena cava (IVC) collapsibility, was studied by Bech-Hanssen et al. to classify hemodynamic profiles in LV failure and was shown to outperform single parameters like TAPSE and RV S’ that are sensitive to dynamic loading conditions.14

Given this background, we sought to extrapolate the utility of the five-parameter RVD scoring system in the sepsis cohort. We aimed to determine the ability of the five-parameter RVD score in predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in sepsis and septic shock. We also aimed to compare its performance to the TAPSE/PASP ratio, a widely studied index of RV-PA coupling.

Patients and Methods

This prospective observational cohort study was performed according to the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IECPG-382/20.07.2023). The study protocol was registered on CTRI (CTRI/2023/08/056086). All patients admitted to the ED or critical care units with sepsis or septic shock (n = 604) over the months (August 2023–November 2024) were assessed for enrollment in the study (Fig. 1). Patients were enrolled after formal written informed consent from the next of kin. All adult patients, according to the Sepsis-3 definition, were enrolled within 48 hours of diagnosis of septic shock.15

Study flowchart

Patients with recent acute coronary syndrome, diagnosed heart failure, significant valvular heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with pulmonary hypertension or cor pulmonale, pulmonary hypertension due to any other cause, pregnancy, poor transthoracic echocardiographic windows, or car-diac dysrhythmias interfering with image acquisition were excluded.

Echocardiographic assessments were performed using a GER Venue Go machine (Mexico) with a 2.5 MHz phased-array probe. All studies were conducted by the primary investigator, who had completed a structured posting in the echocardiography lab, performed over 100 independent echocardiographic measurements under the supervision of a cardiologist, and demonstrated expertise in image acquisition and interpretation.

Demographic details, clinical history, physical examination, acute physiological and chronic health evaluation (APACHE II) score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, hemodynamic data, laboratory investigations, and norepinephrine equivalent dose were recorded.

All transthoracic echocardiography measurements were done according to American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines, as outlined in the Supplementary material (Supplementary File).16 American Society of Echocardiography quality criteria were followed, and further recommendations by Vallabhajoysula et al. for basic and advanced echocardiographic evaluation of myocardial dysfunction in sepsis and septic shock were followed.17

RVD Score Computation Echocardiographic Parameters

At recruitment, within 48 hours of septic shock, the RVD score was calculated using five transthoracic echocardiographic parameters—three continuous (Doppler PASP, RV end-diastolic area indexed to body surface area RVEDA/BSA, TAPSE) and two categorical (TR grade, IVC collapsibility). One point was assigned per component if the value exceeded pre-defined thresholds (Table 1).14 The TAPSE/PASP ratio was also computed.

Additional RV Functional and Dimensional Measurements

Right ventricular/LV ratio, RVEDD, peak TR velocity (TRV max), RV outflow tract velocity-time integral (RVOT VTI), and RV S’ were measured.

LV Systolic and Diastolic Function Measurements

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed using the biplane Simpson's method, and the mitral E/e′ ratio (early diastolic mitral inflow velocity/peak early diastolic longitudinal velocity of the septal aspect of mitral annulus) was calculated.

Derived Measurements

Additional pulmonary hypertension metrics—like the TRV/E/e′ ratio known as (ePLAR)—echocardiographic pulmonary to left atrial ratio, TAPSE/TRV ratio, RV systolic pressure to systolic blood pressure ratio RVSP/SBP ratio, and TRV/RVOT VTI ratio—were calculated.

Patients were followed for prognostic outcomes, with all-cause 28-day mortality recorded as the primary endpoint. Deaths were confirmed through electronic medical records or telephone follow-up. Other prognostic outcomes were as follows: Duration of mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, ICU length of stay, and need for renal replacement therapy (RRT).

Statistical Analysis

Sample Size Calculation

Taking a 31% mortality rate in septic shock with RVD based on previous studies, with a desired level of significance of 5%, and a desired power of 80%, an estimated sample size of approximately 186 participants would be needed to establish an association between RVD score and 28-day mortality in sepsis and septic shock.6

All data were tabulated and analyzed using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Skewed categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared or Fisher's exact test, and skewed continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U test. Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses of the RVD score and TAPSE/PASP ratio were performed for mortality, with optimal cut-offs identified using the Youden index. Receiver operating characteristic curves were compared using the DeLong test. Spearman's correlation assessed the relationship of RVD score and TAPSE/PASP ratio with additional RV echocardiographic indices not contained in the RVD score and with non-mortality clinical outcomes. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of mortality. Clinically relevant non-collinear variables were selected after assessing for correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Only clinically relevant parameters with a VIF of <5 were included in the model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patients were divided according to 28-day mortality (the primary endpoint) into two groups: Survivors and nonsurvivors. The TAPSE/PASP ≤ 0.447 mm/mm Hg ratio had a slightly higher area under receiving operating characteristic curve (AUROC) (0.883) compared to RVD score (AUROC: 0.857), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (ΔAUROC = 0.026, SE = 0.015, 95% CI: −0.004–0.056, p = 0.093).

Isolated RV dysfunction, defined as RVD score > 2 with preserved LVEF, was observed in 40 patients (21.1%). Combined RV and LV dysfunction (RVD > 2 and LVEF ≤ 40%) was present in 45 patients (23.8%), while isolated LV dysfunction (LVEF ≤40% without RVD >2) was seen in 35 patients (18.5%). A significant association was observed between RVD score > 2 and LVEF ≤ 40%: Among patients with an RVD score >2, 39.2% (29/74) had an LVEF ≤ 40%, compared to only 3.5% (4/115) in those with an RVD score ≤ 2.

Right ventricular dysfunction score showed moderate to strong correlations with markers of RV size (RVEDD, RV/LV ratio, RVEDA), function (RV S′, RVOT VTI), and afterload (TRV max, TAPSE/PASP). The TAPSE/PASP ratio also displayed moderate to strong correlations with several of these parameters. Right ventricular dysfunction score and TAPSE/PASP ratio correlated well with mitral E/e’. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of 28-day mortality. The model comprised the following clinically relevant variables: Age, SOFA score, norepinephrine equivalent dose, LVEF, TRV/E/e′ ratio, RVD score (RVD >2 vs ≤2), and presence of ARDS (Table 3).

Right ventricular dysfunction score > 2 emerged as an independent predictor of 28-day mortality, with an adjusted OR of 3.48 (95% CI: 1.37–8.83). Age, LVEF, and the presence of ARDS were also identified as independent predictors in the multivariable model. The model demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance, with an AUROC of 0.918. Good overall model fit was confirmed by a Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic of 7.175 (p > 0.05), and a highly significant likelihood ratio test (Wald χ2 = 51.467, df = 7, p < 0.0001). The Nagelkerke R2 indicated that the model explained 63.1% of the variance in mortality.

Subgroup Analysis

The cut-off RVD score >2 predicted mortality in the subgroups with ARDS and without ARDS, with an AUROC of 0.846 and 0.838, respectively. Their respective sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV are given in Supplementary Figure 2.

The cut-off of the TAPSE/PASP ratio to predict mortality among those with ARDS was ≤0.59 mm/mm Hg with an AUROC of 0.889.

The total RVD score displayed a moderate positive correlation with duration of mechanical ventilation (ρ = 0.396) and number of RRT sessions (ρ = 0.375), and a moderate negative correlation with ventilator-free days (ρ = −0.469) and vasopressor-free days (ρ = −0.351), all statistically significant (p < 0.0001). No significant correlation was observed with ICU length of stay (ρ = 0.069, p = 0.343).

Discussion

The five-parameter RVD score > 2 and TAPSE/PASP ratio ≤ 0.447 mm/mm Hg demonstrated high discriminative ability to differentiate survivors from nonsurvivors, with no significant difference between the two measures. In our cohort, each component of the RVD score differed significantly between the outcome groups. Right ventricular dysfunction score also correlated well with other indices of RV function.

As a tertiary care referral center, all patients enrolled in the study were in septic shock at the time of echocardiographic assessment, representing a population with high disease severity. Bloodstream and respiratory infections were more frequently observed among nonsurvivors.

The cut-offs RVD score > 2 and a TAPSE/PASP ratio ≤ 0.447 mm/mm Hg show potential to address the lack of consensus on prognostically relevant RVD parameters and thresholds. No prior study has compared the TAPSE/PASP ratio with established RVD scoring systems. A three-parameter RV injury (RVI) score comprising RV systolic dysfunction (TAPSE < 17 mm, FAC < 35%, or S′ < 10 cm/s), RV chamber dilatation (R/LVEDA >2/3), and impaired RV-PA coupling (TAPSE/PASP <0.47) has been described to independently predict 30-day mortality with an additive prognostic effect.18 Although TAPSE and PASP are components of RVD score, TAPSE/PASP, as a ratio, is an integrated physiological parameter represents the appropriateness of matching of RV contractility and RV afterload.11,12 Hence, it may be a simpler alternative to the more elaborate five-component score, as both have shown similar prognostic value in our study. Unlike the RVI score and several other studies, where RVD could be defined using any one of multiple parameters (TAPSE, FAC, or S′), the five-parameter RVD score did not allow interchangeable criteria across its domains.3,18,19 Right ventricular dysfunction score also assigned equal weight to each component of RV assessment, bringing in more uniformity in prognostication. Our identified TAPSE/PASP ratio cut-off for the entire study population of <0.447 mm/mm Hg falls within a similar range to values reported in previous studies.10–12 In septic shock, RV function is influenced by a complex interplay of contractility, afterload, volume resuscitation, PEEP, and vasopressor use.11,12 Right ventricular dysfunction score-based risk stratification may guide RV-protective strategies such as early proning and limiting excessive fluids, PEEP, and vasopressors.

In the ARDS subgroup, RVD > 2 had a higher sensitivity and specificity than the entire study population in predicting mortality. This is expected as patients with concurrent ARDS with poor lung compliance develop hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, and the use of higher PEEP adversely affects both the static and pulsatile components of RV afterload.9,12 The acute cor pulmonale risk score has been described, which highlights the interactive effects of ARDS-related lung mechanics and right heart function.20

Each of the components of the RVD score differed significantly between outcome groups in our study and has been recognized to have individual prognostic value. Various studies have established the prognostic significance of RV function parameters like RV S’, TRVmax, and RV FAC.18,21,22 In our study, the RVD score showed good correlation with various additional RV function parameters that are not included in the score. Among the parameters included in the RVD score, TAPSE, representing the RV systolic function, remains the most extensively studied parameter, though its reported cut-offs to independently predict mortality varied from 16 to 21 mm.1,18,23–25 A meta-analysis of six studies emphasized the prognostic significance of TAPSE in comparison with other measures like RV MPI, RV FAC, RV S′, and RVStr, while another study by Viellard-Baron et al. showed that a reduction in TAPSE did not correlate with RV dilatation, and it failed to detect RVD in isolation.1,5,23 Such inconsistencies underscore the limitations of relying on a single parameter and support the rationale for incorporating multiple echocardiographic measures spanning structural and functional domains of RV function. Another multi-parametric four-point RVD score incorporating FAC, TAPSE, central venous pressure (CVP), and TR grade has been described for prognostication in acute heart failure, where a score ≥ 2 was associated with a higher risk of a composite endpoint, including all-cause mortality, and performed better than TAPSE or FAC alone.26 Compared with the five-point RVD score used in our study, it uses a different set of parameters, although the domains representing RV function remain similar.26

The composite five-parameter RVD score did not explicitly distinguish between RVD and overt RV failure, although two of its components, IVC collapsibility < 50% and RVEDA/BSA ratio, have been shown to be associated with the development of overt RVF by Zhang et al.27,28 The TR grade was used as a qualitative parameter to compute the composite RVD score. The TR acts as an amplifying mechanism where RV dilatation and tricuspid annular enlargement lead to worsening regurgitation and create a vicious cycle of progressive RV failure, and this explains the higher proportion of nonsurvivors having a grade II TR.2,7,27 Significant elevation in Doppler PASP calculated from TRV max and RAP can be explained by mixed pre- and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension due to the combined effects of elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), diastolic dysfunction, and elevated left-sided filling pressures.9,10,26,28,29

Right ventricular dysfunction score > 2 was independently associated with 28-day mortality (adjusted OR: 3.48) after adjusting for key covariates like LVEF and ARDS. Due to the strong negative correlation between RVD score and TAPSE/PASP, RV S’, RVOT VTI, and the strong positive correlation between RVD score and RV basal diameter, RV/LV ratio, and TRV max, these parameters were not included in the model. The echocardiographic pulmonary to left atrial ratio (ePLAR), an index to differentiate pre-capillary from post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, was included in the model as it captures both LV diastolic function and RV function, but did not show an independent association with mortality.30 The three-parameter RVI score by Zhang et al. showed a higher mortality risk with a hazard ratio of 7.17 for an RVI score of 3.18 A meta-analysis of 10 studies reported the pooled ORs for short-term (2.42) and long-term mortality (2.26), where RVD was defined by decreased RV systolic motion, elevated RV:LV ratio or reduced RVEF.1 Lanspa et al. reported an OR of 3.4 using FAC < 35% and TAPSE < 16 mm to define RVD.7 Although definitions of RVD varied across studies, there has been a consistent independent association of RVD with short-term mortality, with an OR exceeding 2.18,31,32

Presence of ARDS and LVEF also emerged as independent predictors of mortality. The independent prognostic significance of ARDS and higher sensitivity/specificity for RVD score cut-off to predict mortality is supported by its biological plausibility, and it is shown that mortality may reach up to 50% when ARDS coexists with RVD.9 Reduced LVEF was significantly more prevalent in patients with more severe RVD, while combined LV systolic dysfunction and RVD was present in only 23.8% of patients. It has been shown that RV dilatation may worsen LV diastolic function despite preserved LV systolic function.6 The impact of RVD on LV diastolic function was reflected by the correlation patterns between E/e′ and RVD score as well as TAPSE/PASP ratio.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Direction

This single-center observational study reflects real-world clinical data through inclusive eligibility criteria. Both novel and established echocardiographic measurements of RV, LV function, and pulmonary hypertension metrics have been compared at a standardized time point during early septic shock. This phase, during early septic shock, remains crucial due to the hemodynamic effects of resuscitation. The identification of RVD score > 2 as an independent predictor of mortality reinforces the robustness of this cut-off, which was initially derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Limitations

However, several limitations exist. In this study, approximately 25% of the eligible patients were excluded due to poor transthoracic echocardiographic windows, thereby introducing a potential selection bias. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) would have been a better assessment tool for RV function. Survival analyses were not feasible due to unavailable time-of-death data. We instead focused on binary outcomes, relevant to routine clinical decision-making. Echocardiography was performed only once, precluding evaluation of dynamic changes. We estimated CVP using IVC diameter rather than direct measurements. Acute respiratory distress syndrome subgroups were not pre-defined as the study was not powered for multiple subgroup analyses. We also did not perform separate analyses comparing isolated RVD with RVD accompanied by concurrent LV dysfunction. However, this reflects the pragmatic nature of the study, enhancing its generalizability.

Future studies could validate the identified RVD score and TAPSE/PASP cut-offs in larger, multicenter cohorts. Serial echocardiography could explore trajectories of RVD score over time in response to targeted interventions and may enhance its utility in guiding individualized management.

Conclusion

Right ventricular dysfunction score comprehensively captures multiple components of sepsis-associated RV dysfunction that occur due to interactive pathophysiological processes. The five-parameter RVD score displayed prognostic utility with high discriminatory ability in predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in sepsis and septic shock. The TAPSE/PASP ratio exhibited equivalent predictive performance and provides a more practical alternative for bedside application. Upon adjustment for key clinical covariates, including ARDS and LV ejection fraction, RVD score >2 continued to independently predict mortality.

Clinical Significance

Repeated assessments of RV function using RVD score and TAPSE/PASP ratio can be additional parameters to track hemodynamic and cardiovascular changes in response to therapy and the disease trajectory itself. Right ventricular dysfunction score > 2 and TAPSE/PASP ratio ≤ 0.447 mm/mm Hg can be used to aid in guiding the timing of various RV-protective strategies, like judicious fluid administration, fluid removal, and early prone ventilation in a diverse group of patients with septic shock with various etiologies and phenotypes.
