Free Water Elevation in Alzheimer’s Disease: Influence of Amyloid-Beta Deposition Beyond Brain Cell Loss
Authors: Jieun Park, Min Jeong Kwon, Sungman Jo, Jun Sung Kim, Hae In Kim, Yejung Kim, Ji Won Han, Ki Woong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate whether amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposition in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) increases free water (FW) before cell loss, we examined regional Aβ levels, volume, and FW of the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial and inferior temporal cortex (MITC) across AD stages.MethodsAβ, volume, and FW in PCC and MITC were compared between normal cognition (NC), preclinical AD, prodromal AD, and dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (DEMAD) groups. Multiple linear regression was used to analyze the effect of Aβ and volume on FW.ResultsContinuous increases were observed in the regional standardized uptake value ratio of 18F-Florbetaben (SUVR) and regional volume loss in all regions of interest. Also, there was a consistent rise in the MITC FW. However, in PCC volume, only DEMAD showed a significant decrease. In the context of multiple linear regression, the study found that both SUVR and volume were significant predictors of FW in the MITC. However, in PCC, only SUVR was able to predict FW without considering volume.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that after Aβ deposition in AD, an increase in FW can occur not only due to brain cell loss but also through other Aβ-induced mechanisms, even in cases where brain cell loss has not yet occurred.Keywords: Alzheimer’s Disease, Diffusion Tensor Imaging, Nerve Degeneration, Amyloid Beta-Protein, Inflammation, Free Water

INTRODUCTION

Free water (FW) is a neuroimaging marker calculated from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) that indicates unrestricted diffusing water in the extracellular space with an isotropic tensor.1 The original purpose of this marker was to correct for partial volume effects and reduce cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) contamination in conventional DTI metrics including fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD).2 Later, it was discovered that FW values are significantly elevated in various psychiatric and neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD).3,4 In AD, an increase in FW has been observed with disease progression5,6,7 and found to be associated with various cognitive function.8 Additionally, it is known to result from extracellular processes triggered by AD pathologies, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau deposition.4,6,9

However, it is uncertain whether FW can increase without brain cell loss. AD is a prevalent neurodegenerative disease characterized by substantial loss of brain cells.10 Therefore, the increase in FW in AD can be considered as a consequence of extracellular space expansion resulting from brain cell loss. In fact, previous studies have often attributed increased FW to brain atrophy or brain cell loss.7,11,12 However, Ofori et al.6 found that the increase in FW is only associated with Aβ deposition and not with phosphorylated tau deposition. In AD, tau pathology occurs after the Aβ deposition and indicates neurodegeneration.13 This result suggests that there are Aβ-induced mechanisms, leading to an increase in FW without the brain cell loss.

Furthermore, the descriptions of the changes in FW in relation to brain cell loss were inconsistent across studies. Montal et al.7 found that in AD, FW changed reciprocally with cortical thickness in the superior parietal, medial temporal, and inferior temporal regions. In these regions, FW decreased as cortical thickness increased in the early preclinical stage of AD, and thereafter, FW increased as cortical thickness decreased. Since all participants in the AD stages of this research exhibited significant Aβ deposition, it may be inferred that changes in FW in AD could be influenced by macrostructural changes that occur after Aβ deposition, such as alterations in brain volume or cortical thickness. However, another study showed that brain FW may increase due to other factors even in the absence of macrostructural changes. Ofori et al.6 reported a significant difference in hippocampal FW between individuals with normal cognition and those with early mild cognitive impairment (MCI). However, there was no significant difference in hippocampal volume between the two groups.

Taken together, these studies suggest the possibility that FW may also increase during the state of Aβ deposition, before brain cell loss occurs. To demonstrate this, we compared regional Aβ deposition, volume, and FW in the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and the medial and inferior temporal cortex (MITC) across all stages of AD. The reason for choosing these two regions is that the PCC is the first region to show Aβ deposition but shows little volume change even in advanced AD,14,15 whereas the MITC shows both Aβ deposition and volume loss early on.14,16

METHODS

Participant selection and description

We enrolled 483 older adults: 182 Aβ-negative individuals with normal cognition, 46 Aβ-positive individuals with normal cognition, 106 Aβ-positive individuals with MCI due to AD, and 149 Aβ-positive individuals with dementia due to AD. All participants were free of major physical, neurological, or psychiatric disorders other than AD that could affect their cognition. They were enrolled from two sources: 304 from the individuals attending the dementia clinic at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH), and 179 from the individuals enrolled in the Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD).17 The KLOSCAD is a nationwide, population-based, prospective cohort study designed to understand cognitive aging and dementia in the elderly population. In the KLOSCAD, 6,818 community-dwelling Koreans aged 60 years or older were randomly sampled from 30 villages or towns across South Korea and were followed every 2 years from November 2010 to October 2020.

Diagnostic assessment

All participants underwent the same standardized diagnostic assessment. Geriatric neuropsychiatrists administered a standardized face-to-face diagnostic interview, physical and neurological examinations, and laboratory tests to each participant using the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Packet (CERAD-K) Clinical Assessment Battery18 and the Korean version of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview.19 Neuropsychologists or trained research nurses administered the CERAD-K Neuropsychological Assessment Battery which consists of the following neuropsychological tests: Verbal Fluency Test, 15-item Boston Naming Test, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Word List Memory Test, Constructional Praxis Test, Word List Recall Test, Word List Recognition Test, Constructional Recall Test, Trail Making Test A/B, Digit Span Test, and Frontal Assessment Battery.20 Research nurses asked participants to self-administer the Korean version of the Geriatric Depression Scale to assess depressive symptoms.21

A panel of four geriatric psychiatrists then determined the diagnosis and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) at the consensus diagnostic conference. We diagnosed MCI according to the consensus criteria of the International Working Group on MCI,22 dementia and other Axis I major psychiatric disorders according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition diagnostic criteria,23 and probable/possible AD according to the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Diseases and Stroke/Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria.24

We classified participants into four diagnostic groups: cognitively normal (CN) as all neuropsychological tests scored above −1.5 standard deviation for the age-, sex-, and education-adjusted norms for older Korean adults, with a CDR of 0, and Aβ-negative, preclinical Alzheimer’s disease (PREAD) as all neuropsychological test scored above −1.5 standard deviation for the age-, sex-, and education-adjusted norms for older Korean adults, with a CDR of 0 or 0.5 but Aβ-positive, prodromal Alzheimer’s disease (PROAD) as Aβ-positive MCI with a CDR of 0 or 0.5, and dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (DEMAD) as Aβ-positive AD with a CDR of 0.5 or higher.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) acquisition and preprocessing

All participants underwent MRI at the SNUBH using a Philips Achieva 3T MR scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Eindohovenm, Netherlands). We acquired three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted MR images, DTI, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The T1-weighted image was acquired using the following protocol: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 8.2 ms/4.6 ms, acquisition matrix = 175 × 240 × 240 resulting in a voxel size of 1.0 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm3, slice thickness = 1 mm, flip angle = 8°. DTI is a mathematical model applied to diffusion-weighted imaging data that can analyze the 3D shape of diffusion and measure the diffusion and directionality of water molecules.25 The DTI image was acquired using the following protocol: 15 directions, b-value of 600, one b0 image with two-dimensional acquisition, TR/TE = 5,160 ms/48.9 ms or 7,333 ms/65 ms, acquisition matrix = 112 × 112 × 75 mm3 resulting in a voxel size of 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm3, slice thickness = 2 mm, flip angle = 90°. FLAIR images were acquired using the following protocol: TR/TE = 9,900 ms/125 ms, acquisition matrix = 256 × 301 × 50 mm3 resulting in a voxel size of 0.5 × 0.5 × 3.0 mm3 with no gap between slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, flip angle = 90°.

The DICOM format of the T1 images was converted to the Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIFTI) format using MRIcron software (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricron), and then resliced into 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 isotropic voxels using mri_convert in Freesurfer version 6.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). After that, we ran recon-all in Freesurfer version 6.0 to automatically segment the whole brain structure.26 The recon-all procedure consists of three steps. In the first step, motion correction, non-uniform intensity normalization, and skull stripping are performed. In the second step, full volumetric labeling with automatic topology fixation is applied. Finally, spherical mapping and cortical parcellation are performed.

For white matter hyperintensity (WMH) segmentation, we spatially normalized the resliced T1 NIFTI images of each participant to the MNI 152 space and applied a 12-parameter affine transformation to correct for inter-individual differences in brain size and shape.27 We converted the DICOM format of the FLAIR images to High Dynamic Range Raster Image format images using the MRIcron software and performed bias correction using the segment tool of the Statistical Parametric Mapping software version 8 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK).28 We then applied the transformation parameters calculated from the T1 image normalization step to the bias-corrected and T1-coregistered FLAIR images to obtain spatially MNI-normalized FLAIR images.

The DICOM format of the DTI images was also converted to NIFTI format using MRIcroGL software (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl). After that, we extracted the b0 image from the NIFTI format DTI images using fslroi, a function of FMRIB Software Library (FSL) (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/FSL), and created a binary brain mask using the Brain Extraction Tool in FSL. To correct for head motion and eddy current distortion, we performed eddy correction using eddy in FSL.

Estimation of regional volume and FW

Segmented masks for PCC, medial temporal cortex (MTC), and inferior temporal cortex (ITC) were generated from the preprocessed T1-weighted images. The masks corresponding to the MTC and ITC were then MITCs mask using fslmaths in FSL. It should be noted that the PCC has been recognized as one of the brain regions where volume loss in this region is relatively less pronounced, although Aβ deposition occurs in early stages of AD. In contrast, the MITC serve as representative regions where both volume loss and Aβ deposition coincide in early stages of AD.14,15,16,29

We calculated the volumes of these regions of interest (ROIs) in both hemispheres using FreeSurfer version 6.0.30 The volumes of all ROIs were normalized by dividing by the total intracranial volume (ICV) to adjust for individual variations in intracranial size.31

FA and FW maps were calculated using MarkVCID1, MRI Free Water Kit Protocol (v10.30.19) (https://markvcid.partners.org/consortium-protocols-resources).32 In principle, DTI images contain both tissue and FW compartments within each voxel.1,2 By separating the freely diffusing extracellular water, which has a fixed diffusivity of 3 × 10−3 mm2/s (the diffusion coefficient of FW at body temperature), it is possible to obtain separate maps for the FW-corrected tissue compartment map (FAt, MDt, etc.) and FW. Estimation of WMH volume

We segmented WMH from bias-corrected FLAIR using in-house MATLAB code (MATLAB and statistics Toolbox Release 2014a, MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA), which was custom written.33 We then calculated the total WMH volume and normalized it by dividing by the total ICV to adjust for individual intracranial size variation.

Amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) acquisition and quantification of amyloid burden

All participants underwent 18F-florbetaben PET scanning with a Discovery STE scanner (General Electric Medical Systems). We injected 8.1 mCi (300 MBq) of 18F-florbetaben (Neuraceq) as a single intravenous bolus (6 s/mL) in a total volume of up to 10 mL. Four 5-minute dynamic frames were then acquired to provide 20 minutes of PET after a 90-minute uptake period.

To obtain global cerebral Aβ burden, we used PETsurfer34 to apply motion and partial volume correction, co-register MRI and PET images, and calculate the standardized uptake value ratio of 18F-Florbetaben (SUVR) by averaging the standardized uptake value of 18F-Florbetaben (SUV) of the five main cortical regions (frontal lobe, parietal lobe, temporal lobe and anterior and PCC) and dividing it by the SUV of the whole cerebellum.35 Regional SUVRs of ROIs were calculated by dividing the regional SUV by the whole cerebellum SUV. We defined Aβ-positive as a global SUVR of 0.96 or higher.36

Statistical analysis

We compared continuous and categorical variables using one-way analysis of variance and χ2 test, respectively, with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons.

In each ROI, we compared mean regional SUVR, volume, and FW between diagnostic groups using analysis of covariance adjusted for age, sex and total cerebral WMH volume with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons. Covariates are known to influence brain FW.37

To investigate how Aβ deposition and volume loss in each ROI impact the increase of FW, we performed multiple linear regression analyses with a forward selection of variables in participants with PREAD, PROAD or DEMAD. In these multiple linear regression analyses, we computed each regional FW value as a dependent variable, each regional SUVR and volume as independent variables, and age, sex, and total cerebral WMH volume as covariates. In these multiple linear regression analyses, we used z-scores of regional SUVR, volume, and FW. Furthermore, due to the larger absolute value of the beta for volume in MITC compared to SUVR, we conducted a multiple linear regression analysis and coefficient of determination (R2) analysis in two steps to confirm that the volume of MITC has a greater effect on MITC FW. In step 1, only SUVR was used as an independent variable. In step 2, both SUVR and volume were used as independent variables. The change in R2 and significance levels between these two models was examined.

A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows version 22.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

All of the participants gave written informed consent themselves or through their legal guardians. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (B-2108-700-301, B-0912-089-010).

As shown in Table 2, regional Aβ deposition, volume loss, and FW may continuously increase with AD progression from the early stages of AD. As shown in Table 3, the amount of regional Aβ deposition emerged as a significant predictor of PCC FW in both hemispheres, whereas regional volume did not. In multiple linear regression analysis, regional SUVR was selected as a significant predictor of FW in PCC, explaining 14.6% and 13.3% of the total variance of FW in left and right hemispheres, respectively (β = 0.172, P = 0.002; β = 0.201, P < 0.001). These R2 values represented a significant increase when compared to the R2 values of the Step 1 regression models which included only SUVR as an independent variable (P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Increase in FW has been found in the gray matter of AD patients,6,7 but the cause of the increase remains ambiguous. This study demonstrated that after Aβ deposition occurs, FW can increase not only due to brain cell loss, but also due to Aβ deposition before cell loss takes place. In MITC, where both Aβ deposition and volume loss are prominent in AD, the increase in FW was followed by volume loss (Fig. 2). This increase was found to be associated with both Aβ deposition and volume loss (Table 3). On the other hand, in PCC, where Aβ deposition occurs prominently in AD while volume loss does not, the increase in FW was observed at an earlier stage than the volume loss (Fig. 1). It was found to be only associated with Aβ deposition and not with volume loss (Table 3). These results suggest that in AD, increases in FW associated with Aβ deposition may not be solely occur due to extracellular space expansion caused by cell loss, but may also be due to other independent mechanisms.

Research on gray matter FW in AD is limited, but the available studies consistently support our findings. Montal et al.7 reported that the increase in FW in the PCC and MITC began in the later stage of PREAD. During this stage, an increase in MD, a DTI measure of extracellular water content, was also observed in the PCC and MITC. Other studies have similarly demonstrated a significant increase in MD in the MITC and PCC with AD progression.38,39 These studies and the current study suggest that gray matter FW in MITC and PCC may begin to increase from the PREAD stage and become evident in the PROAD stage (Table 2).

However, the present study showed that the reasons for FW increase in these two ROIs differed (Table 3). In many previous studies, Aβ deposition was prominent while volume loss was not prominent in the PCC of AD patients.14,15,29 These results are consistent with the current study (Table 2). Therefore, it can be suggested that the PCC can indicate a statement where only Aβ deposition exists without brain cell loss. In this context, FW increased in earlier AD stage than the occurrence of volume loss (Table 2), and this increase in FW was specifically associated with Aβ deposition, but not volume loss (Table 3). According to Ofori et al.,6 hippocampal FW increased in the early MCI stage without volume loss. The levels of FW were only associated with the amount of Aβ deposition, indicated by florbetapir PET (AV45) and Aβ1-42 CSF, but not with the amount of phosphorylated or total tau, indicated by p-tau and t-tau CSF. This suggests that FW may increase in association with Aβ deposition without volume loss.

Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated a significant increase in FW over time in AD, even after controlling for volume, and have suggested neuroinflammation as a potential contributing factor.4,5 Additionally, another study showed a significant correlation between FW and proinflammatory cytokines, highlighting FW as a highly sensitive and physiologically relevant measure of inflammation.40 Neuroinflammation results from the chronic activation of microglia stimulated by Aβ.41,42 When activated, microglia release cytotoxic materials into the extracellular space, including proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, leading to breakdown and increased permeability of the blood-brain barrier. This allows various immune cells to enter the brain, prolonging the inflammatory process, and ultimately resulting in a secondary effect of cellular damage that affects the surrounding tissue. It can affect the microstructural integrity in the brain, such as oligodendrocytes, the myelin sheaths. These processes are likely to occur before volume loss but may impact the water content of the extracellular space.43 In addition, materials released into the extracellular space due to neuroinflammation could induce excessive osmotic pressure, resulting in movement of water from the intracellular to extracellular space.3 In summary, FW can increase due to neuroinflammation activated by Aβ deposition. Through these mechanisms, it can be said that FW in the PCC could increase solely due to Aβ deposition without the presence of volume loss.

However, Montal et al.7 demonstrated that FW may decrease in the very early state of PREAD (stage 1). At this stage of AD, Aβ begins to accumulate, but tau does not. The decrease in FW may be attributed to mechanisms such as neuronal swelling, glial swelling, and recruitment, which occur in the initial phase of neuroinflammation. In this phase of neuroinflammation, activated microglia undergo thickening, and lead to the recruitment of other microglia through the secretion of proinflammatory signals.44 This process leads to a reduction in extracellular space, resulting in a decrease in FW. In acute inflammatory responses, these processes would normally be completed after antigen removal. However, in diseases such as AD, this process becomes chronic.41,44 When inflammation reaches a chronic stage, cytotoxic materials are abundantly secreted by microglia, and progressively damage the microstructure of neurons. This damage, in turn, leads to relatively unimpeded water diffusion in the extracellular space, resulting in an increase in FW. In the current study, no decrease in FW was observed in the PREAD group (Table 2), which may be due to the fact that the majority of participants in the PREAD group had already reached a chronic stage of neuroinflammation or had concomitant tau deposition after Aβ deposition, indicating ongoing neurodegenerative processes. Future studies should further investigate the preclinical stage with direct measurements of neuroinflammation and tau using direct indicators.

In MITC, FW showed a significant association with both Aβ deposition and volume (Table 3). MITC is a region where both volume loss14 and Aβ deposition16 are prominent in AD, which is consistent with the results of the current study (Table 2). Therefore, in the current study, it can be suggested that the MITC represents a condition where both Aβ deposition and brain cell loss exist. In the multiple linear regression of the current study, beta value of volume is greater than that of Aβ deposition in the regression model and the R2 value significantly increased in the Step 2 model (Table 3). These results suggest that the increase in MITC FW may be more influenced by the brain cell loss. Unlike PCC, MITC has been reported to accumulate tau pathology in the relatively early stages of AD.45,46 Another study indicated that in the later stage of PREAD (stage 2/3), characterized by both Aβ and tau deposition, increases in FW were followed by a decrease in cortical thickness.7 Tau pathology is known to occur after Aβ pathology,13 and the synergistic effect of Aβ and tau leads to the breakdown of the axonal microtubule structure, resulting in neurodegeneration.47,48 This process involves the expansion of the extracellular space, leading to an increase in FW. Indeed, it has been reported that the atrophic lesion is replaced by CSF, and FW is the most sensitive neuroimaging measure capable of detecting the lesional tissue.49,50 From these studies, it can be inferred that in AD, brain cell loss following amyloid accumulation leads to an increase in FW.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a cross-sectional study and thus the differences between the individuals at different stages of AD do not directly indicate the changes associated with the progression of AD. Second, this study did not directly measure neuroinflammation. Therefore, FW increase due to Aβ deposition without atrophy does not necessarily indicate FW increase due to neuroinflammation associated with Aβ deposition. Third, this study did not measure tau pathology. Therefore, we could not classify stage 1 PREAD and stage 2/3 PREAD in our PREAD group, which may have confounded the level of FW. Fourth, the sample size of PREAD group was relatively small compared to other groups.

In conclusion, after Aβ deposition in AD, an increase in FW can occur not only due to brain cell loss but also through other Aβ-induced mechanisms, such as neuroinflammation, even in instances where brain cell loss has not yet taken place.
