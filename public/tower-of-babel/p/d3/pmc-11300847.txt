Disentangling drivers of mudflat intertidal DOM chemodiversity using ecological models
Authors: Kai Ma, Yueyue Li, Wen Song, Jiayin Zhou, Xia Liu, Mengqi Wang, Xiaofan Gong, Linlin Wang, Qichao Tu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroorganisms consume and transform dissolved organic matter (DOM) into various forms. However, it remains unclear whether the ecological patterns and drivers of DOM chemodiversity are analogous to those of microbial communities. Here, a large-scale investigation is conducted along the Chinese coasts to resolve the intrinsic linkages among the complex intertidal DOM pools, microbial communities and environmental heterogeneity. The abundance of DOM molecular formulae best fits log-normal distribution and follows Taylor’s Law. Distance-decay relationships are observed for labile molecular formulae, while latitudinal diversity gradients are noted for recalcitrant molecular formulae. Latitudinal patterns are also observed for DOM molecular features. Negative cohesion, bacterial diversity, and molecular traits are the main drivers of DOM chemodiversity. Stochasticity analyses demonstrate that determinism dominantly shapes the DOM compositional variations. This study unveils the intrinsic mechanisms underlying the intertidal DOM chemodiversity and microbial communities from ecological perspectives, deepening our understanding of microbially driven chemical ecology.Subject terms: Biogeography, Microbial ecology, Molecular ecology

Introduction

One of the central goals in ecology is to comprehend the composition, diversity, and distribution of biological entities across various temporal and spatial scales, as well as the underlying maintenance mechanisms1,2. Dissolved organic matter (DOM), closely linked with microorganisms3,4, is a pivotal component in the global carbon cycle and serves as a critical nourishment source for heterotrophic microorganisms5,6. This intricate mixture of organic compounds comprises a vast array of molecules and is omnipresent in the Earth’s biosphere. In the ocean, an immense quantity of DOM molecules contributes to approximately 660 Pg of carbon7 and encompasses a broad spectrum of organic matter classes, including lipids, proteins, lignin, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons8,9. Based on their putative degradability by microbes and molecular lability10–12, DOM is commonly categorized into two primary groups: labile and recalcitrant DOM (LDOM and RDOM), with the latter being further divided into semi-labile, semi-refractory, refractory, and ultra-refractory fractions13. Recently, effort has been made to investigate the porewater/extracted DOM pools in soils and sediments2,14,15, and demonstrated that the molecular diversity (hereafter chemodiversity akin to the biodiversity) and composition of DOM pools are attributed to molecular properties16,17, hydrological factors18, climate variations2, and microbial dynamics6,15.

Despite the acquisition of valuable insights, there is still much to be explored regarding the intricate DOM pools in natural ecosystems. For example, being closely related with microorganisms, it remains unclear whether the ecological models obeyed by microbial communities are conveyed to DOM pools. These well-recognized ecological models include, but are not limited to: species abundance distribution (SAD)19, distance-decay relationship (DDR)20, and latitudinal diversity gradient (LDG)21,22. Notably, these ecological patterns were initially discovered in plant and animal communities, but have since been observed in diverse ecosystems across Earth’s biomes1,23,24. As one of the most fundamental patterns in diversity theory, SAD posits that natural biological communities are composed of a few abundant and many rare species (or taxa when referring to microbes)19. Out of various models, the lognormal distribution is the most suitable for predicting the dynamics of the rare biosphere, especially for microbial communities25. A global survey across micro- and macro-organisms revealed a unified power-law distribution that describes the relationship between the number of individuals and their commonness or rarity24. DDR and LDG are two spatial patterns commonly observed in biological communities. Of these, DDR illustrates the pattern that biological communities become increasingly heterogeneous with increasing geographic distance20, while LDG describes the gradient pattern of community diversity with increasing latitude21,26. Investigating whether DOM compounds in natural ecosystems adhere to these well-established ecological models can offer not only insights into understanding the complex relationship with microorganisms but also novel perspectives on the enduring question of whether universal ecological laws govern the diversity and distribution of biological entities24,27,28, including the non-living DOM pools.

Besides the observable patterns of DOM chemodiversity, another critical issue is the driving mechanisms underneath it. Prior studies have demonstrated that microorganisms predominantly contribute to the generation of RDOM in natural ecosystems4,7,13. Microbial consortia foster DOM chemodiversity by breaking down complex molecules into smaller ones or transform them into different forms and release these compounds into the surrounding environment4,14. Meanwhile, these compounds reciprocally influence the composition, phenotype, and physiochemical properties of microbial communities29,30. DOM chemodiversity and microbial diversity are often positively associated, jointly influencing ecosystem functions31. As investigations into the DOM dynamics expand across temporal and spatial scales, it becomes imperative to explore the underlying mechanisms that drive the compositional variations of DOM chemodiversity, thereby enhancing our predictive capability for DOM in various ecosystems. To achieve this, multiple approaches have been developed to disentangle the drivers underneath DOM chemodiversity, including multivariate statistics3,18, transformation-weighted characteristics dendrograms (TWCD)32, null models32,33, DOM partitioning frameworks12, energy-diversity-trait integrative analysis10, and neutral models34. Methodologically, these studies also propose that DOM pools can be regarded as analogs to biological communities, thereby allowing for the application of ecological models and the expansion of statistical approaches to investigate DOM chemodiversity, consequently leading to significant advancements in our understanding of DOM ecology.

In this study, we aimed to uncover the intrinsic linkages among the complex DOM pools, microbial communities and environmental heterogeneity via large-scale sampling effort in the mudflat intertidal wetlands along the Chinese coasts. The intertidal wetlands are critical ecological ecotones connecting the marine and terrestrial ecosystems, performing vital functions in various biogeochemical cycles, especially the very complex carbon cycling8,9. The absence of plant communities in the mudflat wetlands maximumly exposes the interactive DOM-microbe relationship, reducing influences from plants and other enriched macro-organisms. The following questions were mainly addressed: (i) How do the chemodiversity and composition of DOM pools vary in the mudflat intertidal zones? (ii) Do intertidal DOM pools follow similar ecological patterns (e.g., SAD, DDR, and LDG) as those of microbes? Or are the ecological patterns followed by microbes conveyable to DOM pools? (iii) Which mechanism mainly drives the compositional variations of DOM pools? Is it similar to that of microbes? Considering the important roles as decomposers that fungi play, both bacteria and fungi were included in this study. As previous studies have demonstrated close relationship between DOM and microbes3–6,35, we expected that DOM pools follow similar ecological patterns as those of microbial communities, especially bacteria, the end-user of organic matter in the food web. However, owing to the accumulative property and strong dispersal capacity of DOM molecules in the environment, especially RDOM, we expected weaker spatial turnovers of DOM than those of microbes. The results well supported our expectations and demonstrated that deterministic processes mainly governed the compositional variations of both DOM and microbes in the intertidal zones. This study enhances our understanding of the ecological patterns and underlying mechanisms of DOM chemodiversity, suggesting that there may exist universal ecological models explaining the patterns of both living and non-living biological entities in the Earth’s biosphere.

Results and Discussion

Overall diversity of intertidal DOM and microbial communities

A total of 135 mudflat intertidal sediment samples were collected from 12 coastal regions, spanning from the southmost (SanYa) to the northmost (DanDong) intertidal zones along the coastline of China (Fig. 1A). The annual mean temperature and precipitation at these sites ranged from 9.53 °C to 25.59 °C and 705 mm to 1839 mm, respectively. The moisture content of the samples varied from 20.19% to 53.03%, whereas pH and salinity ranged from 7.59 to 8.66 and 1.6‰ to 7.3‰, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). To avoid potential freshwater interference, riverine inputs were carefully avoided from the sampling sites. For each sampling site, 5 samples were subject to DOM extraction and analyses. The corresponding microbial communities were also analyzed together with DOM profiles. Following calibration and filtration, a total of 20,980 molecular formulae were detected, which were further classified into ten different groups (Fig. 1B). Though differ substantially, the abundances of different DOM groups were quantified by relative intensities in order to comply with microbial profiles, with the top five most abundant being lipid-like (32.14%), aliphatic-like (22.88%), lignin-like (17.83%), condensed aromatic-like (6.69%), and polyphenol-like (5.92%). Notably, although different DOM groups come with different lability, true labile compounds in natural ecosystems are consumed within minutes or hours and are therefore usually not capturable13. Consequently, most of the molecular formulae captured by solid-phase extraction method are refractory fractions. Therefore, the “presumptive partitioning” method we employed mainly differentiated relatively labile and recalcitrant molecular formulae10–12. For microbial communities, raw reads were subjected to quality control, filtering, chimera removal, and rarefaction, resulting in 5,765,245 and 5,280,390 high-quality sequences for bacteria and fungi, respectively. Taxonomic assignment of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) showed 89 phyla for bacteria and 17 for fungi. The top 8 phyla accounted for 62.79% and 98.48% of the total relative abundance for bacteria and fungi, respectively. The most abundant phyla for bacteria were Proteobacteria (21.95%), Bacteroidetes (6.92%), and Firmicutes (6.85%) (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 2), whereas Ascomycota (33.99%), Basidiomycota (16.64%), and Chytridiomycota (15.54%) were the most abundant for fungi (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 2). ANOSIM analysis showed significant among-site differences in DOM composition (R = 0.233, P < 0.001) at the group level as well as bacterial (R = 0.706, P < 0.001) and fungal (R = 0.537, P < 0.001) communities at the phylum level (Supplementary Table 2), suggesting compositional variations of both DOM formulae and microbial communities along the latitude.

DOM followed similar ecological patterns as microbes

We first examined the abundance patterns of DOM pools and microbial communities. SAD models, which describe the distributions of commonness and rarity among species, play a pivotal role in understanding the mechanisms maintaining biological communities, making it a fundamental aspect in diversity analysis23. Owing to the vague definition of the microbial species36, the term taxa usually represented by ASVs/OTUs is commonly used in microbial ecology. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that the majority of microbial communities are composed of a limited number of abundant/core microbial taxa and a large number of rare/satellite taxa19, and adhere to lognormal distributions25,28. By generalizing the species concept to DOM molecular formulae, we examined whether DOM formulae exhibited a comparable abundance distribution pattern as observed for bacterial and fungal communities. By fitting multiple abundance distribution models with DOM molecular formulae and microbial ASVs, the results revealed that the lognormal model demonstrated the best predictive power for DOM formulae (R2 = 0.79, 91.67% samples), bacterial communities (R2 = 0.86, 62.12% samples), and fungal communities (R2 = 0.77, 85.93% samples) (Fig. 2). This suggested that lognormal distribution not only describes the distribution of microbial communities19,25,28, but also of DOM pools, that contain substrates, intermediates, and products of microbial metabolism6,9,12,15,34. Moreover, we observed a consistent linear relationship between the mean square and variance of the abundance distributions for DOM formulae, bacterial, and fungal ASVs (Supplementary Fig. 1), demonstrating that the coefficients of abundance fluctuation for both DOM pools and microbial communities were constant. Such linear relationship followed the Taylor’s Law, a well-recognized empirical pattern in ecology that describes dependent relationship between the mean and variance of the abundance distribution across species28,37, indicating foreseeable abundance fluctuations for DOM formulae. Furthermore, a significant power law relationship was observed between the diversity and intensity sum (or the individual numbers when referring to microbes,N) of the DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities. The richness, dominance, and rarity all significantly increased with increasing N, whereas the evenness gradually decreased. The ratio of DOM richness (λ = 0.415, P < 0.001) was comparatively lower than that of bacteria (λ = 0.604, P < 0.001) and fungi (λ = 0.459, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2A), while the ratios of DOM evenness, dominance, and rarity fell between that of bacteria and fungi (Supplementary Fig. 2B-D). These results suggest a resemblance in the abundance patterns between DOM formulae and microbial ASVs.

Second, we investigated whether DOM also followed similar DDR patterns as microbes, a well-recognized ecological pattern describing decreased community similarity with increasing geographic distance20. Significant DDR patterns were observed for DOM pools (β = −0.048, P < 0.001) and bacterial communities (β = −0.256, P < 0.001), but not for fungal communities (β = −0.008, P = 0.693) (Fig. 3A–C). As expected, weaker DDR pattern was observed for DOM than bacteria, potentially due to the cosmopolitan and accumulative properties of DOM molecules38. Dispersal, via which individuals move from one place to another, results in more homogenized communities, thus leads to weaker DDR. For accumulation, RDOM formulae, which can be considered as the final form of DOM, are likely to accumulate in the environment7,13, whereas labile ones are usually easily degraded and not accumulated, resulting in more similar DOM compositions. Notably, the true lability of DOM is also related with environmental conditions, microbial communities, and enzymatic capacity7. The “presumptive partitioning” of LDOM and RDOM based on molecular formulae here provided overall spatial pattern differences between labile and recalcitrant groups. Additionally, the actual DOM chemodiversity in complex ecosystem is usually underestimated due to indistinguishable isomeric compounds39–41. Consequently, weak DDR patterns were observed for DOM. DOM molecular formulae, bacterial and fungal ASVs were then classified into core and satellite groups based on the occurrence frequency, as different groups may contribute differentially to the observed spatial scaling patterns42. As a result, 849 core molecular formulae were identified for DOM pools (Supplementary Fig. 3). Looking specifically, the DDR pattern for DOM was mainly driven by core molecular formulae (β = −0.061, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A), but by satellite taxa (β = −0.375, P < 0.001) for bacterial communities (Fig. 3B). We then investigated the DDR patterns at the group (DOM) and phylum (microbes) levels. High DDR turnover was observed for some of the less abundant DOM groups, such as polyphenol-like (β = −0.117, P < 0.001), condensed aromatic-like (β = −0.106, P < 0.01), and protein-like (β = −0.084, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 4A), suggesting strong spatial regionalism of these relatively rare molecular groups. Similarly, significant DDR was observed for some microbial phyla with lower relative abundance, such as bacterial phyla including Bacteroidetes (β = −0.362, P < 0.001) and Actinobacteria (β = −0.358, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 4B), and fungal phyla including Fungi_phy_Incertae_sedis (β = −0.111, P < 0.001) and Chytridiomycota (β = −0.058, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Additionally, DOM was also categorized as labile and recalcitrant, as well as low and high DBE based on molecular reactivity12. As a result, stronger DDR turnover was observed for labile (β = −0.057, P < 0.001) and low DBE groups (β = −0.050, P < 0.001) than recalcitrant and high DBE groups (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). These suggested that different DOM groups and microbial phyla exhibited different spatial distributions.

Third, we investigated whether DOM and microbes followed similar latitudinal diversity patterns. Notably, the well-recognized LDG pattern, which describes the tendency pattern of more species toward the equator21, is not always followed by microbes22,26. We therefore extended LDG to examine the more general latitudinal diversity patterns. As a result, the diversity of DOM (s = −43.91, P < 0.01) and bacteria (s = −16.11, P < 0.001) decreased with latitude, conforming with the LDG pattern, while the diversity of fungi (s = 16.02, P < 0.001) increased with latitude, opposing the LDG pattern. For both DOM pools and microbial communities, satellite molecular formulae/taxa were mainly responsible for the observed LDG patterns (Fig. 3D–F). Notably, the chemodiversity of DOM formulae decreased more rapidly with latitude than bacterial ASVs. For DOM groups, aliphatic-like (s = −18.3, P < 0.01) and lignin-like (s = −14.2, P < 0.001) groups, which were among the most diverse groups and accounted for 51.53% of the DOM chemodiversity, were found with steeper LDG patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4D). For microbial phyla, the Proteobacteria (s = −8.33, P < 0.001) and Ascomycota (s = 5.97, P < 0.001), respectively accounting for 48.46% and 31.89% of bacterial and fungal communities, had the strongest LDG patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4 E and F). In contrast to the DDR pattern, labile and low DBE molecular formulae had weaker LDG patterns than recalcitrant and high DBE molecular formulae, which mainly contributed to the LDG patterns of DOM (Supplementary Fig. 5C and D).

These results revealed several intriguing insights into DOM ecology. First, the abundance distributions of DOM formulae were highly similar to those of microbial ASVs, following lognormal distribution and Taylor’s Law25,28. This suggested that there may exist universal ecological laws governing the diversity and distribution of both living and non-living biological entities23,24. Second, the spatial patterns (i.e., DDR and LDG) of DOM bore greater resemblance to those of bacteria than to fungi. Though not directly, this suggested that the DOM chemodiversity was more likely driven by bacteria than by fungi, as also indicated by several recent studies3,35,43. Third, we observed different spatial patterns for different types of DOM formulae, such as labile vs. recalcitrant and low vs. high DBE. Such differences shall be attributed to the different properties of DOM formulae11,12,16,17, as well as the different preference of local microbial communities in utilizing DOM, i.e., microorganisms have different affinities for different DOM groups. On the other hand, recalcitrant DOM formulae comprise a variety of molecules synthesized by macro- and microorganisms with complex structures that are difficult to degrade13,44, resulting in weaker DDR turnover. Notably, a large fraction of environmental DOM formulae are recalcitrant, and can have an age of hundreds to thousands of years13. The accumulation and dispersal capacity of these old RDOM may affect the performance of ecological models, which could be another reason for the relatively weak patterns observed for DOM formulae. While the residence time of solid-phase extracted DOM spanning from hours to millennia may have limited ecological models to capture the rapid responding relationship between microbial communities and DOM, the observed patterns of DOM chemodiversity along environmental gradients similar to those of microbial communities nevertheless indicated a close relationship between DOM and microbial communities that manifested itself in clear chemical and biological signatures.

DOM features also followed latitudinal patterns

Besides the observed pattern of increasing DOM chemodiversity with decreasing latitude, latitudinal patterns were also found for DOM features such as intensity, transformations, and other molecular traits. First, we screened 630 key molecular formulae based on variations of the relative intensity and occurrence frequency (see Methods for more details), which were considered to be environmentally and ecologically/biologically important for their positive and negative associations between molecular intensity and latitude. Of these, 544 also belonged to the core molecular formulae (Fig. 4A). The Kendrick mass defect (KMD) values were calculated for six homologous series of the key molecular formulae. As a result, the KMD-CH2 values divided the key molecular formulae into 71 series and revealed that negatively associated molecular formulae had greater KMD values (0.203 ± 0.003 vs. 0.153 ± 0.005) and mass (347.81 ± 2.72 vs. 319.75 ± 4.52) than positively associated molecular formulae (Fig. 4B). This suggested that within the subset of DOM formulae associated with latitude, molecular formulae with larger mass and KMD values were more likely to accumulate at low latitudes. Conversely, the DOM formulae with smaller mass and KMD tended to accumulate at high latitudes. A similar phenomenon was also observed for KMD-H2 (Supplementary Fig. 6A). KMD-CO2 divided the key molecular formulae into 36 series, of which 35 were negatively associated molecular formulae (Fig. 4C), indicating that the abundance of key DOM formulae containing -CO2 groups decreased with increasing latitude. A similar pattern was also observed for molecular formulae containing -O2, -OH, and -CHO (Supplementary Fig. 6B-D), suggesting that DOM formulae at low latitudes had larger mass than those at high latitudes.

Second, out of an extensive set of over 1,000 putative transformations, a notable subset of 243 transformations exhibited significant increase in turnover with decreasing latitude (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Notably, the transformation turnover plunged approximately at 50 Da transformation mass (Fig. 4D). While low mass transformation pathways had steeper latitude-occurrence patterns, their mean squared errors (300 to 3500) were higher when compared to the large mass transformation pathways (0 to 200), indicating that low mass transformations might be co-regulated by multiple different factors. The top three pathways with the largest transformation turnover were hydrogenation/dehydrogenation (H2), C_1H_2O1, and single carbon addition/loss (C1) (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Finally, significant linear correlations could be observed between several important molecular traits and latitude (Fig. 4E–H). The weighted mean of three molecular traits, including O/C, polarity index (PI), and nominal oxidation state of carbon (NOSC), decreased with latitude, while Gibbs free energy (GFE) increased with latitude, suggesting that high latitudes tended to accumulate DOM formulae with relatively high carbon and low oxygen (Fig. 4E). The decrease in NOSC along increasing latitudes implied that thermodynamically, the reduction of organic matter was more favored at high latitudes than low latitudes (Fig. 4G).

Ecologically, several reasons/theories may explain the latitudinal patterns of DOM chemodiversity and features. First, organic matter is mainly produced by biological communities in natural ecosystems3,4,6,45. The high biodiversity toward the equator (i.e., low latitude) is expected to result in high diversity of organic matter46, some of which is convertible to DOM via biotic/abiotic processes or is DOM itself2,6,18. Particularly important among these are photosynthetic organisms, which can release up to 50% of DOM into the environment47,48. Second, RDOM, which is structurally complex and less degradable, tends to accumulate in the environment32,38, and harbors higher chemodiversity than LDOM44,49, as also observed in this study (11146 vs. 9834). Third, LDOM, as is defined13, can be easily degraded by microorganisms under favorable abiotic and biotic conditions7,18,50. The high availability of LDOM at low latitudes as a result of high primary productivity leads microorganisms to preferentially consume LDOM, leaving the less degradable DOM to accumulate. Finally, the metabolic theory of ecology predicts that the high temperature at low latitudes drives accelerated rates of ecosystem metabolism, energy flow, and nutrition turnover rate51. The delicious LDOM at lower latitudes serves as a significant energy source for microorganisms to sustain accelerated metabolic processes. This results in a high incidence and abundance of complex pools of RDOM compounds. Conversely, the limited availability of LDOM at high latitudes and the relatively lower metabolic rates enables microorganisms to consume RDOM. This was also evidenced by the more pronounced molecular transformations mass below 50 Da at low latitudes (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 7A), and the decreased molecular polarity and carbon oxidation of DOM formulae at high latitudes observed in this study.

Cascading DOM chemodiversity with abiotic and biotic factors

We then investigated the abiotic and biotic factors that potentially drove the spatial patterns and compositional variation of DOM chemodiversity across the intertidal zones. First, the contributions of climate, environment, microbial diversity, molecular dispersal, traits, and interactions to the DOM chemodiversity were assessed using structural equation modeling (SEM). By extracting the first two principal components from the climates, environments, and molecular traits, 85.2% of the variance in DOM chemodiversity could be explained (Fig. 5). Negative cohesion (the strength of negative intermolecular correlations) (est = 0.714, P < 0.001), bacterial diversity (est = 0.435, P < 0.001), molecular trait PC1 (which was significantly positively correlated with DBE and NOSC) (est = 0.411, P < 0.001), climate PC1 (est = −0.333, P < 0.05), and dispersal (est = 0.325, P < 0.001) were strongly associated with DOM chemodiversity. Latitude modulated climate PC1 (est = 0.985, P < 0.001), molecular dispersal (est = −0.448, P < 0.001), and bacterial (est = −0.348, P < 0.001) and fungal (est = 0.559, P < 0.001) diversity. Molecular dispersal was strongly negatively regulated by climate PC2 (est = −0.658, P < 0.001), which was significantly associated with longitude. Bacteria, but not fungi, significantly influenced DOM chemodiversity (est = 0.435, P < 0.001). Molecular trait PC1 and latitude influenced the tendency of interactions among DOM formulae, resulting in a greater impact of negative cohesion (est = 0.714, P < 0.001) compared to positive cohesion (est = −0.214, P < 0.001) on DOM chemodiversity. These results demonstrated that the DOM chemodiversity in mudflat intertidal zones was jointly modulated by both abiotic and biotic factors2,4,18,52, and negative cohesion played a profound role in modulating DOM chemodiversity. Comparatively, bacteria were more important than fungi in driving the DOM chemodiversity in mudflat intertidal zones, consistent with several recent studies35,43. This is also consistent with the role that fungi mainly play as decomposers in natural ecosystems, breaking down large and complex dissolvable organic matter into smaller ones via complex enzymatic systems53,54, whereas the relatively simple bacterial enzymatic systems drive them to consume less complex DOM as the nutrient source45,55.

Ecological processes mediating the variations of DOM and microbes

Multiple ecological processes, both deterministic and stochastic, simultaneously mediate the compositional variations of biological communities56,57 and DOM pools32–34. To investigate whether DOM and microbes were subjected to similar community assembly processes, the ecological processes mediating the compositional variations of DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities were comparatively analyzed using multiple approaches. First, variation partitioning analyses (VPA) revealed that deterministic factors (e.g., environmental and climatic factors) mainly explained the compositional variation of DOM pools (Fig. 6A), bacterial (Fig. 6B), and fungal (Fig. 6C) communities. Second, stochasticity analyses were conducted using both taxonomic- and phylogenetic-based null models. The taxonomic null model demonstrated stochastic ratios below 0.5 for DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities (Fig. 6D), indicating deterministic processes may have dominated the community assembly of DOM and microbes. Using phylogenetic-based null models, the relative contributions of five ecological processes were quantified byβNTI-coupled RCbray indices, with selection (variable and homogeneous selection) and dispersal limitation comprising the major processes mediating DOM pools (80.51%), bacterial (96.43%), and fungal (82.33%) communities (Fig. 6E). Further, the impacts of ecological processes on different groups of DOM were also assessed using the two null models. As a result, the top three DOM groups were all under strong deterministic controls (Fig. 6 F and G). Finally, comparisons of habitat niche breadth and dispersal among DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities indicated that DOM pools exhibited the widest niche breadth (Fig. 6H) and dispersal capability (Fig. 6I), while fungi was the narrowest. Moreover, when examining the habitat niche breadth and dispersal capability of DOM groups (Supplementary Fig. 8), it was observed that the highly abundant lipid-like, aliphatic-like, and lignin-like groups exhibited higher dispersal rates and strong correlations. These findings suggested that the compositional variation of DOM pools was similarly governed by deterministic factors as microbes, and dominant groups were mainly responsible for the patterns.

A wealth of research has highlighted the interplay of deterministic and stochastic processes in shaping microbial community assembly56–59. In contrast, the underlying mechanisms governing the compositional variations of DOM pools remain inadequately characterized, except for a few recent studies10,33,34. In river corridor ecosystems, inconsistency has been observed between the assembly dynamics of DOM and microbial communities32. Such discrepancy suggests that the regulation of DOM assembly extends beyond the influence of the microbial community, emphasizing the involvement of additional factors, such as climatic2, hydrological18, and anthropogenic8,44. Additionally, different ecosystems, the resolution of obtained DOM and microbial data, and even analytical methods may also impose such discrepancy. Consequently, there is a pressing need to discern and dissect the intricate processes governing DOM assembly across different ecosystems. Given the similarities in SAD and spatial scale patterns, such as DDR and LDG, observed between DOM pools and microbial communities in this study, we hypothesized that DOM, being closely linked to microbial metabolism, shall exhibit similar community assembly processes as microbes. Consistent with our hypothesis, deterministic processes were found to predominantly shape the assembly of DOM, bacterial, and fungal communities in intertidal mudflats along the Chinese coasts, as cross-verified by multiple lines of evidence. The SEM revealed that negative cohesion, reflecting co-exclusive relationships between DOM formulae, played a major role in mediating DOM chemodiversity. Comparatively, stronger association was observed between bacterial diversity and DOM chemodiversity than that between fungi and DOM. In addition, DOM pools exhibited broader habitat niche breadth and stronger dispersal ability compared to bacteria and fungi, consistent with the non-living property and molecule-level size of DOM60,61. At the group level, lipid-like, aliphatic-like, and lignin-like molecular formulae exerted the greatest influence on DOM assembly. A similar phenomenon has also been observed for microbes that a few groups dominated community assembly62. These results collectively demonstrated close relationships between DOM and microbes5,6,12, and that bacteria are more important than fungi in mediating DOM chemodiversity29,43,46. Notably, compared to the more widely applied 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, the sampling of fungal communities may have been somewhat biased due to the current ITS2-based amplicon approach that certain community members and functional clades may have been undersampled63. However, such technical limitation can hardly be conquered, and the effects on the obtained results shall be limited64.

In conclusion, this study comparatively examined the composition, diversity patterns, spatial patterns, and ecological mechanisms governing DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities in the mudflat intertidal zones along the Chinese coasts. DOM exhibited high similarity in abundance distribution and spatial patterns with microbes, especially bacteria. Latitudinal patterns were not only observed for DOM chemodiversity, but also the features that DOM formulae carried. Negative cohesion, bacterial diversity, and molecular trait were identified as the dominant factors shaping DOM chemodiversity. Multiple approaches consistently demonstrated that deterministic processes governed the assembly of intertidal mudflat DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities. Compared with previous studies showing statistical associations between microbes (e.g., OTUs or ASVs) and DOM formulae5,10,29,33, this study explored the intrinsic ecological patterns and driving mechanisms of DOM pools and linked them with microbes in the intertidal sediments. Critically, multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that well-recognized ecological laws not only govern the diversity and distribution of biological communities1,23,27, but also non-living biological entities such as DOM. These findings provide crucial insights into the ecological mechanisms underlying DOM dynamics and deepen our understanding of microbially driven chemical ecology.

Methods

Site description and sample collection

The coastline of China spans approximately 18,000 km from south to north, covering tropical, subtropical, and temperate zones8. The intertidal zone, a biodiverse habitat located at the border between marine and terrestrial environments, was selected to investigate the linkages among the complex DOM pools, microbial communities, and environment. Intertidal samples were collected from twelve sites from the southmost to the northmost intertidal zones in China (Fig. 1A). And nineteen bioclimatic variables for the 12 sites were shown in Supplementary Table 165. To avoid potential freshwater interference, the sampling regions were carefully selected being away from riverine inputs. To minimize temperature differences at the time of sampling, intertidal samples were collected from south (SanYa, April, 2021) to north (DanDong, June, 2021). For each sediment sample, five surface sediment cores (~15 cm depth) were collected as the tide receded to expose the mudflat. The samples were mixed, and then immediately placed in an ice box for transportation to the laboratory. After freeze-drying, the samples were sieved through a 2 mm filter to remove large debris before being stored at −80 °C for further processing.

Physicochemical parameters and climatic factors

Consistent procedures as in our previous study were used to measure eleven physicochemical parameters43, including dissolved organic carbon (DOC), temperature, moisture content (MC), pH, salinity, ammonia‑nitrogen (NH4+-N), nitrite‑nitrogen (NO2--N), nitrate‑nitrogen (NO3--N), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and water-soluble sulfates (SO42-). Specifically, the DOC of the sediment samples was obtained by extracting 3 g of dry soil powder with 30 mL of deionized water and shaking at 150 x g for 2 h, after which the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 x g for 20 minutes66. Then DOC was quantified with a TOC-L instrument (Shimadzu, Japan), after extracting one gram of supernatant with a pre-combusted (450 °C, 4 h) glass dropper (see Supplementary Methods 1 for more details). The remaining supernatant underwent solid-phase extraction of DOM.

FT-ICR-MS analysis

The instrumentation and parameters utilized in this study for mass spectrometry analysis were consistent with our recent research43. In brief, each supernatant sample was filtered through a 0.7 µm glass fiber Whatman GF/F filter and subsequently passed through an activated styrene-divinylbenzene copolymer cartridge (Agilent Bond Elut PPL, 100 mg)67. Solid-phase extraction was performed at a controlled flow rate of 6 s · drop−1, the PPL cartridges were dried with ultrapure nitrogen and eluted with 6 mL of methanol before being concentrated to 1 mL. The mass spectra were acquired using a 15T SolariX Ultra FT-ICR mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The 200 μL of the solution was injected into the ESI source in negative ionization mode at a flow rate of 120 μL h−1. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio (s/n), 300 broadband scans of 4 M words were completed. The assignment and summary of molecular formula were completed using Formularity (v1.0.8476)68 based on the following criteria: (1) elemental combination of C1-60H1-120O1-30N0-3S0-2, (2) minimal number of non-oxygenated heteroatoms, (3) homologous inspection, (4) alignment tolerance <1 ppm and (5) s/n ≥ 4 and FtmsAnalysis (v1.0.0) (arguments:--mass_range 200 800--OC_range 0 1.2--HC_range 0 2.4--groups CH2 CO2 H2 O2 OH CHO--trans_db)69, respectively.

Seventeen molecular traits, including DBE70, AImod71, H/C, and O/C etc, were calculated (Supplementary Table 3) and intensity-weighted means were taken as the molecular traits of the samples. Additionally, several indices were calculated, including molecular lability boundary (MLBL)11, degradation index (IDEG)72, as well as the ratio of carboxyl-rich alicyclic molecules (CRAM)49, and island of stability (IOS)73 molecules (Supplementary Table 3). Based on the element ratio and AImod, DOM formulae were classified into ten groups74,75, including lipid-like, protein-like, amino sugar-like, carbohydrate-like, aliphatic-like, condensed aromatic-like, polyphenol-like, highly unsaturated and phenolic-like, lignin-like, and tannin-like (Supplementary Table 4), which were further classified into different regions by principal component analysis (PCA) (Supplementary Fig. 9). The similarity analysis of the composition among different sampling locations is performed by the function ‘anosim’ in the R package “vegan”76. To facilitate subsequent data processing, the function ‘normScale’ (arguments: floor = 200, multiple = 9.8e-5, intFmt = TRUE) was utilized to scale the molecular intensity according to a specific ratio (9.8e-5). The function ‘min_error’ was applied to determine the option multiple to minimize the changes in community dissimilarity before and after scaling43 (see Supplementary Methods 2 for more details).

DNA extraction, sequencing, and data processing

Total DNA was extracted from sediment samples and underwent quality assessment (see Supplementary Methods 3 for more details). Amplification of the 16 S V3 ~ V4 region in bacteria and the ITS2 region in fungi was performed using 338 F/806 R (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’ and 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)77,78 and gITS-7F/ITS-4R (5’-GTGARTCATCGARTCTTTG-3’ and 5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’)64,79 primer pairs, respectively. Library preparation was conducted, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd, generating 250 bp paired-end reads.

The R package “DADA2” pipeline80 was utilized for processing the raw data of 16S rRNA and ITS sequences. Taxonomic classification of bacterial ASVs employed the native RDP training set81 and the SILVA v138 training set82, while the UNITE (11.29.2022) training set83 was employed for fungal ASVs classification. Taxonomic assignment was conducted using a confidence cutoff of 0.5.

Molecular formulae and ASVs partitions

Before partitioning the molecular formulae and ASVs, each sample was rarefied to the same size using the function ‘rarefy_vt’. The core and satellite molecular formulae/taxa were defined based on the occurrence frequency of molecular formulae or ASVs84,85. DOM formulae were classified into putatively labile and recalcitrant categories based on H/C ratios greater than or less than 1.511. The function ‘DBE_thresholds’ was used to determine the DBE thresholds that delineate molecular formulae with high and low DBE, and we found that DBE equal to 6 was the most appropriate boundary (Supplementary Fig. 10). The key molecular formulae associated with the latitudinal gradient were screened by the function ‘keyMole’. KMD analysis was employed to find combinations of molecular formulae in the same series with the same KMD (see Supplementary Methods 4 for more details).

We applied the function ‘fitsad’ from the R package “sads”86 to extract eigenvalues for each sample under diverse model contexts. The function ‘sim_sad’ from the R package “mobsim”87 was then used to generate predicted species abundances based on these eigenvalues. SEM of abiotic and biotic effects on chemodiversity

The structural equation model (SEM) of DOM chemodiversity was performed using the function ‘sem’ in the R package “lavaan”88, following predefined models with Spearman’s correlation. PCA was conducted separately on bioclimatic, environmental parameters, and molecular traits to mitigate the impact of collinearity among multiple abiotic factors on model interpretation. Given the relatively smaller size of DOM compared to bacteria and fungi, we hypothesized that DOM’s dispersal capacity might contribute to its chemodiversity32,43. Moreover, this study also accounted for the impact of intermolecular correlations on chemodiversity by utilizing cohesion89. Various metrics, such as R-squared, RMSEA, CFI, and P-value, were employed to evaluate the model’s goodness of fit (see Supplementary Methods 5 for more details).

Quantifying ecological mechanisms for DOM and microbes

To explore the relative contribution of deterministic and stochastic processes, variance partitioning analysis (VPA)90, both taxonomic-58, and phylogenetic-based91 null model analyses were conducted. The function ‘VPA_dbmem’ was employed to quantify the contributions of environmental, spatial, and bioclimatic variations inβ-diversity across the metacommunity. Stochastic ratios for the DOM pools, bacterial, and fungal communities were estimated using the function ‘nullStoc’ (arguments:null_model = “shuffle”, reps = 1000). Specifically, proximity between DOM formulae was assessed using the transformation-weighted characteristics dendrogram (TWCD)32, which fuses the number of major atoms (C, H, O) and molecular transformation relationships. The molecular characteristics dendrogram (MCD) and transformation-based dendrogram (TD) that solely focus on molecular characteristics and transformations individually, were not employed in this study. Bacterial phylogenetic trees were constructed using FastTree92, following prior alignment and trimming steps using mafft93 and trimAl94, respectively. Given the substantial variability of the ITS2 region in fungi, the referenced phylogenetic tree construction method known as ghost-tree was adopted95. Using the R functions ‘bNTI’ and ‘Raup_Crick_Abundance’91, theβNTI-coupled RCbray indices classify ecological processes into five categories, including variable selection (VaS), homogeneous selection (HoS), homogenizing dispersal (HD), dispersal limitation (DL), and undominated processes (UP, such as drift). Also, the function ‘commDispersal’ was employed to calculate the dispersal of DOM formulae and microbial ASVs among multiple sites (Supplementary Methods 5). Furthermore, ecological niche widths for DOM formulae and microbial ASVs were calculated using the function ‘niche.width’ from the R package “spaa”96 (see Supplementary Methods 6 for more details).
