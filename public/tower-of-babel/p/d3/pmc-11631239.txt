A case report of right‐sided heart failure secondary to JAK2‐mutation Loeffler endocarditis
Authors: Xi Liu, Limin Liu, Zhiguo Yan, Lin Sun, Menghuai Ma, Yi Xiang, Xiang Cai, Xiaoqi Wang, Wenhui Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLoeffler's endocarditis (LE) is the cardiac manifestation of hypereosinophilic syndrome. We present a case of LE in a 45‐year‐old female, resulting in diffuse endothelial fibrosis and severe right‐sided heart failure. The patient was admitted with dyspnoea and oedema, with haematology revealing an absolute eosinophil count of 20.9 × 109. Imaging showed near‐complete obliteration of the right ventricular apical and formation of thromboses. Endomyocardial biopsy indicated diffuse fibrous hyperplasia of the endocardium with fibrinous thrombi rich in eosinophils. Molecular and cytogenetic analyses of bone marrow cells showed no signs of FIP1L1‐PDGFRA fusion, PDGFRB mutation, abnormal myeloid maturation, or a lymphoproliferative disorder. Flow cytometry indicated no clonality, ruling out chronic eosinophilic leukaemia. Gene mutation screening discovered a p.L583_A586delinesS mutation in the JAK2 gene. Following treatment with ruxolitinib, the patient's eosinophil levels normalized, but unfortunately, the damage to the heart was irreversible. The patient was hospitalized multiple times due to right heart failure and resistance to diuretics. After thorough discussions with the medical team, it was determined that a heart transplantation would be the most effective treatment. Following the surgery, the patient successfully navigated the postoperative critical period with the support of an intra‐aortic balloon pump (IABP), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and ventilator‐assisted ventilation but subsequently developed an acquired Intensive care unit‐acquired weakness (ICU‐AW) and a depressive state. Fortunately, the patient gradually recovered from these complications.Keywords: Eestrictive cardiomyopathy, Heart failure, Hypereosinophilic cardiomyopathy, Hypereosinophilic syndrome, Loeffler endocarditis

Introduction

Loeffler endocarditis (LE) is the cardiac manifestation of hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES), which is a rare systemic disease characterized by the sustained production of eosinophils. Cardiac involvement is observed in about 50% of HES cases and is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates. Patients are often diagnosed in the advanced stages of the disease and have a mortality rate of 35–50% within 2 years. 1 Typically, both ventricles are involved, showing endocardial thickening of the inflow regions and ventricular apices. 2 In this report, we present the case of a female patient with LE who had congestive cardiac failure with predominant features of right‐sided volume overload.

Case Report

A 45‐year‐old female patient complained of gradually increasing shortness of breath, leg swelling, and difficulty breathing at night, which persisted for 9 months. Prior to this, she had also developed a rash on her waist and legs and felt like small insects were crawling beneath her skin. She denied experiencing fever, travel, or drug allergies. During a physical examination, elevated jugular venous pressure and significant bilateral lower extremity pitting oedema were observed.

The laboratory test results showed a significantly high NTproBNP level of 6,768 pg/mL. The haematology report revealed an absolute eosinophil count of 20.9 × 109 (normal range: 0.02 ~ 0.52 × 109). The stool examination for ova and parasites, as well as the HIV and Strongyloides serologies, all came back negative. The electrocardiogram showed a rightward deviation of the electrical axis, flat T‐wave in leads I and aVL, and T‐wave inversion in leads V1–V3 (Figure 1 ). The transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed enlarged atria and right ventricle (RV), reduced RV function, RV apex obliteration, and thrombus. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was preserved, but there was thickened endocardium, diastolic dysfunction, short posterior mitral valve leaflet, and moderate to severe regurgitation. There was also severe regurgitation in the tricuspid valve (Figure 2 ). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) confirmed the dilation of both atria. The RV was hypertrophic, particularly in the apical region, with obliteration of the RV apex, and had low diastolic volume (RV end‐diastolic volume index 64 mL/m2) and ejection fraction (42.2%). There was a preserved LV ejection fraction (52.2%) with average LV volume and wall motion. However, the left ventricular endocardium was significantly thickened without outflow tract obstruction. Strips of diffuse delayed gadolinium enhancement were observed in the RV and LV apical, and mural thrombosis was formed in the left ventricular cavity (Figure 3 ). CT scan showed no coronary artery stenosis.

The endomyocardial biopsy of the right ventricle showed fibrous hyperplasia of the endocardium with fibrinous thrombi that were rich in eosinophils (Figure 4 ). Immunohistochemical results showed Fibrinogen to be positive, LCA to be negative, CD34 to be positive, Vimentin to be positive, and Myoglobin to be negative. The Masson stain was positive. The bone marrow biopsy indicated the presence of cells from all phases of the granulocytic lineage, with a predominance of cells in the middle and young stages, along with an increase in eosinophils. Furthermore, the spinal marrow chromosome karyotyping showed no abnormalities or rearrangement in the PDGFRA gene. The molecular and cytogenetic analyses of bone marrow cells were negative for FIP1L1‐PDGFRA fusion or PDGFRB, ruling out chronic eosinophilic leukaemia. Gene mutation screening for blood system diseases revealed mutations in the JAK2 and TET2 genes. Specifically, a p.L583_A586delinesS mutation was found in the JAK2 gene, representing an amino acid deletion insertion mutation. These findings led to the diagnosis of idiopathic eosinophilia and LE.

The patient was given furosemide, spironolactone, and nesiritide for congestive heart failure. They were also prescribed oral prednisone for LE. Upon discharge, the patient received a prescription for daily 50 mg of prednisone, torasemide 40 mg, rivaroxaban 15 mg, spironolactone 20 mg, and bisoprolol 1.25 mg. The patient's symptoms significantly improved following this treatment. Even after 2 months of steroid therapy, there was no decrease in the eosinophil count. The haematologist recommended switching to ruxolitinib 10 mg to manage HES and gradually normalize the eosinophil count.

After 6 months, the patient experienced a recurrence of pleural and abdominal effusions, as well as swelling in both legs, which led to repeated hospitalizations. Thoracentesis drainage was performed at this time, and tests showed a fluid leak in the pleural area. The patient was prescribed high doses of diuretics, including torasemide 120 mg, bumetanide 2 mg, and tolvaptan 15 mg daily. Subsequent assessments with TTE and CMR showed resolution of ventricular thrombi with anticoagulation but no improvement in cardiac function. A cardiopulmonary exercise test showed that the patient's maximum oxygen consumption was significantly impaired at 10.6 mL/kg/min. Following this, the patient underwent a cardiac catheterization, which revealed that her right ventricular (RV) pressure was 32/16 mmHg, pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) was 22/12 mmHg, and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) was 5 mmHg.

We have had multiple interdisciplinary discussions about this patient. If a valve replacement was performed, the patient would need to have the mitral and tricuspid valves replaced. However, choosing the replacement valve material was difficult. Mechanical valves are prone to pannus ingrowth and thrombosis, leading to valve dysfunction, while bioprosthetic valves are susceptible to valvular destruction. Endomyocardial stripping does not completely relieve ventricular diastolic dysfunction and is highly invasive. This posed a challenge in meeting the expectations of both the surgeon and the patient. After several discussions among doctors and with the consent of the patient and her family, the decision to proceed with a heart transplant was ultimately made.

She underwent a heart transplant in March 2024, 2 years after being diagnosed with LE. The examination of the removed heart revealed significant endocardial fibrosis in both ventricles but no significant cellular infiltrates. Postoperatively the patient survived the critical postoperative period with the support of IABP, CRRT, and ventilator‐assisted breathing. Subsequently, the patient developed ICU‐AW and depression but gradually recovered. The immunosuppressive regimen was adjusted based on the TBNK immunoassay results, which included prednisone 10 mg at 8:00, cyclosporine A 100 mg at 9:00 and 75 mg at 21:00, mycophenolate mofetil 0.5 g at 9:00 and 21:00, and ruxolitinib 5 mg at 11:00 and 23:00.

Discussion

LE is a group of disorders characterized by the infiltration of the heart with eosinophils, leading to various complications such as valve lesions, thromboembolism, and heart failure. The development of LE involves necrosis, thrombosis, and fibrosis. The progressive time from mild symptoms to restrictive cardiomyopathy with decompensation varies from 2–3 weeks to 10 years. The most common cause of Loeffler endocarditis is hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES), and the diagnosis of HES is based on three criteria: (1) a peripheral blood eosinophil count >1.5 × 109/L for longer than 6 months, (2) exclusion of other causes for eosinophilia, including eosinophilic leukaemia, allergic diseases, drugs, sarcoidosis, connective tissue disorders, and parasitic infections, and (3) signs or symptoms of organ involvement by eosinophilic infiltration. 3 If HES is suspected, other causes of eosinophilia such as reactive eosinophilia, lymphocyte‐variant hypereosinophilia, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia, WHO‐defined myeloid malignancies associated with eosinophilia, eosinophilia‐associated myeloproliferative neoplasms, or acute myeloid leukaemia/lymphoblastic leukaemia with rearrangements of PDGFRA, PDGFRB, or FGFR1 or with PCM1‐JAK2 should be excluded. 3

The diagnostic workup includes laboratory testing, ECG, visualized morphological evaluation, and myocardial biopsy. Elevated levels of brain natriuretic peptides may be observed. ECG findings may include atrioventricular blocks, bundle branch blocks, abnormal Q‐waves, poor R‐wave progression, low voltage, and T‐wave changes. Chest radiography may show pleural effusion, pulmonary oedema, and cardiomegaly. Echocardiography reveals biatrial enlargement, bicuspid and tricuspid regurgitation, biventricular endocardial thickening, ventricular thrombosis, and restrictive diastolic dysfunction. 4 CMR morphologic features include apical obliteration of the ventricle associated with enlarged atriums. The typical late gadolinium enhancement pattern is the ‘double V’ sign, which includes thickened enhanced endomyocardial layers and overlying apical thrombosis. T2‐weighted imaging can also be instrumental in identifying inflammatory changes and edema. 5 In cases where non‐invasive imaging and clinical suspicion do not align, an endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) may be conducted to identify the distinct histologic pattern of LE. 6 The characteristic histologic findings of LE include interstitial infiltrates with significant eosinophils and other immune cells, myocyte necrosis and oedema, endocardial thrombosis containing eosinophils, and endocardial fibrotic thickening without inflammatory infiltrates in the late‐stage. 7

The treatment for LE depends on the disease stage and the degree of fibrosis. The main goals of therapy are to reduce hypereosinophilia, prevent further organ damage, and avoid the formation of blood clots. The first‐line treatment is corticosteroids, but some patients may not respond to them. 8 Other medications that can be used include interferon‐alpha, hydroxyurea, imatinib, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, mepolizumab, or other immunomodulatory drugs. 3 Imatinib is effective in patients with FIP1L1‐PDGRFA gene fusion, leading to hematologic and molecular remission. Small‐molecule inhibitors, such as the JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor ruxolitinib, should be considered for patients with JAK2 and FLT3 fusion tyrosine kinases to achieve complete hematologic remissions and cytogenetic responses. 9 , 10 Individuals at risk of thromboembolism need to receive adequate anticoagulant therapy. In this case, both TTE and CMR indicated that the patient had a left ventricular thrombus and an elevated D‐dimer, necessitating anticoagulation. However, due to the patient's refusal to undergo regular monitoring of INR and experiencing heavy menstrual bleeding, a decision was made to use rivaroxaban (15 mg/d) for anticoagulation. After 6 months of anticoagulation, a repeat CMR showed no ventricular thrombus, and D‐dimer levels decreased to normal. Despite the successful outcome, and in consideration of the patient's high risk of thrombosis, anticoagulation therapy was continued until heart transplantation. No bleeding events were observed during treatment with oral NOAC, and it was noted that anticoagulation in this patient was effective and safe. Additionally, there were no comparative studies on the use of different types of anticoagulants in such patients.

As heart failure progresses, treatment options become limited. Patients may gradually develop resistance to standard drug therapy. For patients with late‐stage heart disease, endomyocardial decortication with valve repair or replacement can relieve symptoms, especially in subjects with advanced heart failure and severe valvular regurgitation, offering the chance of long‐term survival. 11 , 12 Bioprosthetic devices are generally preferred over mechanical ones due to the lower incidence of valve thrombosis. 13 In some cases, a total artificial heart has been implanted as a bridge to a heart transplant or as a direct heart transplantation. 14 , 15

LE is a rare and severe condition that can lead to high mortality and morbidity rates. In this case report, we describe the diagnosis and management of a patient who developed heart failure due to LE and eventually underwent heart transplantation. It is crucial to eradicate hypereosinophilia before it causes irreversible damage. Unfortunately, most patients present at later stages due to the initial asymptomatic nature of the disease. Early recognition, comprehensive laboratory tests, multimodality imaging diagnostic workup, and early initiation of treatment can slow down the progression of the disease and promote remission. If restrictive cardiomyopathy progresses, endomyocardial decortication, valve replacement, or transplantation may be considered.
