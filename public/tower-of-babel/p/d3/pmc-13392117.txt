Experimental, numerical, and ARX model–based prediction of axial buckling capacity in stainless steel cylindrical shells
Authors: Özer Zeybek, Ali İhsan Çelik, Yasin Onuralp Özkılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the structural behavior of thin-walled cylindrical shells fabricated from stainless-steel pipe under uniform axial compressive loading. Pursuant to this goal, a combined experimental and FE parametric study was performed to develop more generalized design insights for long stainless-steel shell structures. Seventeen specimens with different diameters, thicknesses, and lengths were tested to characterize load–displacement behavior and failure modes. Digital image correlation (DIC) was employed to assess the initial geometric imperfections of the shells, which were then introduced to the numerical models. A nonlinear finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted employing ANSYS software to validate these models against the experimental results. The FE model closely matched the experimental results when considering the peak axial loads of the six tested pipes, with FEA/Test ratios ranging from 0.998 to 1.013 (a difference of approximately − 0.20% to + 1.3%) and an average absolute percentage error of about 0.61%. Based on the validated models, a parametric study using finite element analysis was conducted to evaluate the effects of the radius-to-thickness and height-to-diameter ratios on buckling capacity. Finally, an autoregressive with exogenous inputs (ARX) model was developed as a design-oriented predictor to support the preliminary estimation of nonlinear buckling capacity within the experimentally validated parameter space under uniform axial compression. The proposed capacity model accounts for imperfection amplitude effects and facilitates the evaluation of long stainless-steel shell structures during the design phase.Keywords: Stainless-steel pipe, Shell buckling, Numerical modelling, FEA, Digital image correlation (DIC), ARX modelSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Thin walled cylinders are essential structural components of vessels under different loading conditions such as internal or external pressure in civil, marine and aeronautical applications 1–8.Thin-walled cylindrical components have a wide range of applications in liquid storage and transportation, granular storage, stationary and mobile tanks, underwater hulls, and pressureless chambers 9,10. Thanks to their advantages such as lightness, high load-bearing capacity, and material efficiency, thin cylindrical shell structures are known to be utilized in a large number of applications as critical structural elements, in many engineering disciplines 11. Compared to traditional carbon steels, stainless steels have superior corrosion resistance because they contain at least 10.5% chromium, which promotes the formation of a chromium-rich passive oxide film on the surface, and according to the EN 10,088 standard series, stainless steels contain no more than 1.2% carbon 12. The chromium element contained reacts with the oxygen and moisture in the environment and forms a chromoxide layer on the surface that provides resistance against corrosion, and can even repair itself 13. In addition, it has high resistance to high and low temperatures, chemicals and to mechanical loads. Stainless steel is also easy to form, weld, recycle and easy to clean. Stainless steel has important uses such as in construction materials and power plants. Furthermore, stainless steel alloys have low proportional limits and broad strain hardening abilities. Since there is no obvious yield plateau as in carbon steels, an equivalent yield stress (traditionally 0.2% proof stress) is employed to identify the strength of the material in structural design 13,14. While stainless steel initially costs more than structural carbon steel, it has great corrosion resistance and requires less maintenance and inspection. Because of this reason, it may be a better choice when we look at the life cycle 15. In addition to their excellent durability, stainless steels are increasingly preferred in structural applications requiring long life and architectural value due to their attractive appearance, superior mechanical performance, and high ductility 16. Stainless steel is a high-cost material, and failure to take certain characteristics into account naturally means that the results obtained will be less efficient and less cost-effective than those that could be achieved using more accurate and appropriate methods 17. To use stainless steel more efficiently, a better understanding of the structural behavior of this material is of great importance. Structural research projects conducted worldwide have made significant contributions to the more effective use of stainless steel in the construction and design fields. This has also had a noticeable impact on the development of construction and design guidelines 18. The nonlinear stress–strain properties of stainless-steel result in a slightly different structural response than for structural carbon steel. Nonlinear behavior can cause a decrease or increase in capacity relative to an equivalent component with material response in different ranges 19. Stainless steel stands out as a sustainable building material thanks to its high corrosion resistance and long service life; these properties significantly reduce maintenance requirements for buildings, resulting in considerable cost savings 19,20. The advancement of contemporary materials, design theories and construction technology has promoted the evolution of steel structures and also brought new challenges to the construction and structural engineering industry in the form of environmental protection and economic requirements 21.

Nonetheless, cylindrical shell structures are vulnerable to instability risks since they facilitate the construction of extremely thin-walled structures through their effective geometric design. Buckling is a significant problem for all types of thin structures, regardless of the material used. The capacity to resist buckling stands as the pivotal constraint and primary focus in advancing such thin-walled cylinders 9. Recently, there has been an important emphasis on the impact of nonlinear effects on the structural behavior of the systems. This underscores the inadequacy of linear-elastic analysis in predicting the critical collapse load. Given the advancements in current technology, there is a demand for conducting nonlinear stability analysis on thin circular cylindrical shell structures under external pressure 18,22. Timoshenko and Gere 23 proposed theoretical formulations for calculating the bifurcation (critical) buckling load of cylindrical shells of perfect geometric form under uniform axial compressive loads, based on classical elastic buckling theory. Furthermore, analytical solutions have been proposed by many researchers 24,25 to determine the critical buckling load of circular cylindrical shells with initial geometric imperfections 26,27.

One of the important factors that distinguish stainless steel from conventional steels is the characteristic properties of its stress–strain curve. In traditional steel design, however, it is commonly considered that the stress–strain behavior is typically represented by a bilinear relationship 17. The instability behavior of thin-walled cylindrical elements varies depending on their length/diameter (H/D) ratio. Theoretical and experimental studies reveal different results regarding the distribution of buckling behavior under axial pressure 28. These differences are due to the initial geometry of the elements, non-uniform ends and non-uniform loadings 1,29–33.

Both linear and nonlinear analyses were applied to assess the accuracy of the numerical analyses performed in the ANSYS program for some of the cylindrical shell models presented in the study by Han and colleagues. Comparison of the results obtained revealed that both types of analysis were able to simulate structural responses to a sufficient level 34. An experimental program was carried out to evaluate the structural behavior and section responses of circular hollow sections made of cold-formed stainless steel subjected to axial loading. The experimental results were verified by FEA analysis 16. It has been observed that the created FE models accurately reflect the basic experimental results and successfully mimic the complete load-deformation curves of test specimens and the deformed damage shapes after ultimate failure.

Compared to our earlier research 35,36 on stainless-steel pipe shells—primarily focused on short-to-moderate shell lengths and associated imperfection conditions—this new study focuses on longer cylindrical shells that are subjected to uniform axial compression. Nevertheless, experimentally validated evidence specifically addressing the length-dependent axial buckling response of thin-walled stainless-steel pipe shells remains comparatively limited, particularly for longer shell configurations. To complement our earlier work, 35,36 this study extends the validated geometry and material nonlinear analyses with imperfections (GMNIA) framework to longer shells and combines DIC-measured imperfections with a design-oriented autoregressive with exogenous inputs (ARX) surrogate for preliminary buckling capacity prediction. The experimental program includes smooth stainless-steel pipes with lengths of 400, 500, and 600 mm, and the resulting peak and post-peak responses are used to validate the nonlinear FE models. Based on this validated numerical platform, a parametric study is conducted to quantify the influence of key geometric parameters—the shell length H, outer diameter D (or radius R = D/2), and wall thickness t—together with imperfection amplitude, on the buckling capacity of long stainless-steel shell structures.

Materials and methods

Laboratory testing of samples

Diameters of 76 mm, 89 mm, 101 mm and 114 mm, thickness of 1.5 mm, 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm and heights of 400 mm, 500 mm and 600 mm were selected. The experimental setup is detailed in Fig. 2. Axial loading procedures were performed using a servo-controlled hydraulic testing machine with a capacity of 500 kN. Cylindrical shell specimens were loaded at a constant rate of 1 kN/s to maintain static stability. During the test, load and displacement data were recorded simultaneously by the data acquisition system. Furthermore, initial imperfections arising from the production of the specimens were mapped with high accuracy using the DIC technique.

Numerical study

The finite element software ANSYS was utilized in the study. In the first stage, the numerical models were verified with test results. Next, an FE-based parametric investigation was conducted to examine different shell dimensions. Considering these FEA results, a data-driven based estimation was proposed to obtain the buckling strength of long stainless-steel cylindrical shell structures subjected to uniform axial compression.

The constitutive behavior of stainless steel was determined through tensile tests by transforming the engineering stress–strain data into true stress–true strain measurements. Using the converted data, the stainless steel behavior was modeled in ANSYS with multi-linear kinematic hardening plasticity (TB,KINH), defined by specific true stress–plastic strain points. The equilibrium path was determined using the arc-length method, terminating when the axial displacement of a monitored end node was reached. Outputs were recorded at each successful increment.

In the FEA simulations, the cylindrical shell was discretized using the quadratic 8-node SHELL93 element, which supports large-deformation and material nonlinear analyses and provides an efficient representation of thin-walled shell behavior. The formulation includes stress stiffening, making it suitable for GMNIA simulations of axially compressed cylindrical shells. Following mesh-convergence studies, the element size was selected in the range of 2–8 mm, depending on the specimen geometry.

Initial imperfection generally occurs during the fabrication process and it has a strong impact on the instability behavior of shell structures 37–39. DIC measurements provided the initial imperfection amplitudes for the FE calculations. Initial geometric imperfections were quantified from Digital Image Correlation (DIC) by extracting the maximum initial out-of-plane deviation, w0, on the shell surface (Fig. 4) and expressing it in normalized form as w0/t (e.g., w0 ≈ 3.6 mm and w0 / t ≈ 2.4 for the 76 × 1.5 mm specimen). To introduce these imperfections into the FE model, an eigenmode-affine approach was adopted, as commonly used in GMNIA of cylindrical shells: a linear buckling analysis was first performed to obtain the relevant buckling mode shape, and the imperfect geometry was generated by superimposing this mode onto the perfect mesh and scaling it so that the maximum radial deviation matches the DIC-measured amplitude. This procedure follows standard shell-buckling practice and design guidance (e.g., EN 1993–1-6 40) while anchoring imperfection severity directly to the experimentally measured defect level.

Results and discussion

Laboratory test results

All specimens were loaded to failure. While the test specimens exhibited varying buckling modes, the deformation predominantly occurred near the bottom edges or shifted toward the center. The edges of the cylinder test specimens experienced the typical elephant foot buckling shown in Fig. 5. The obtained damage modes of the all test samples have been presented in Fig. 6. Furthermore, axial load values were plotted against axial displacement (Fig. 7). For brevity, only the specimens with H = 400 mm are shown here. Within the studied range, changing H has a limited impact on the peak axial capacity compared with the dominant effects of D/t and material strength. Under uniform axial compression, the behavior of the specimens is primarily governed by local shell buckling rather than global Euler-type buckling. According to classical shell theory, for sufficiently long cylinders the elastic axial buckling stress mainly scales with t/R (including the elastic modulus E and Poisson’s ratio ν) and depends only weakly on length once the local buckling wavelength can form41,42. Accordingly, increasing H within the tested H/D range primarily affects the buckling pattern and post-peak response, whereas the peak load is predominantly controlled by D/t, imperfections, and boundary conditions.

Figure 6 clearly indicates that the governing parameter controlling the buckling response of stainless steel cylindrical shells is the D/t ratio. For the thick-walled specimen (89 × 2.5) characterized by a relatively low D/t ratio, buckling initiated at a later stage. The failure mechanism was dominated by a pronounced elephant foot deformation localized near the end regions, followed by progressive damage propagation toward the mid-height of the shell. As the D/t ratio increased (76 × 1.5 and 89 × 1.5), the instability mode evolved into a more complex configuration. In these specimens, local circumferential buckling waves interacted with axial crushing mechanisms, while the characteristic elephant foot deformation remained evident at the boundaries. For the most slender specimens with the highest D/t ratios (101 × 1.5 and 114 × 1.5), the buckling pattern became more axisymmetric and predominantly localized at the shell ends. Mid-height deformations were significantly reduced and, in practical terms, negligible. The failure mode in these cases was governed almost entirely by the elephant foot mechanism. Although the finite element models successfully reproduced the overall behavioral trends, they slightly underestimated the experimentally observed damage intensity. This discrepancy is attributed to the pronounced sensitivity of high D/t shells and geometric irregularities, which act as strong buckling initiators. Overall, the experimental campaign demonstrates that the prevailing failure mechanism across all configurations was elephant foot buckling, with its severity and spatial distribution strongly governed by the D/t ratio.

The curves in the graph in Fig. 7 initially exhibit high stiffness in the linear elastic region and then show a distinct plastic deformation region after the yield point. The load–displacement curves of the tested specimens with a height of H = 400 mm clearly demonstrate the impacts of changes in diameter and wall thickness on the load-carrying capacity and failure behavior. As the D/t ratio increases, the shells become more prone to buckling, and failure occurs earlier and more abruptly. Increasing thickness, on the other hand, increased the plastic deformation capacity before failure and made the failure more ductile. In particular, the 76 × 1.5 and 89 × 1.5, the 101 × 1.5 and 114 × 1.5 specimens exhibited similar failure modes, indicating the combined effect of H/D and D/t ratios on the failure mechanism. The results support the findings of Rasmussen & Hancock (1993) and Gardner (2005) on thin-walled stainless steel pressure elements 43,44.

Numerical results

The numerical models were first developed for verification purposes. Axial load values were plotted against axial displacement (end shortening) for each test specimen. For the test specimen, the comparisons of these quantities obtained from FEA and experiments have been shown in Fig. 8. To evaluate the accuracy of the numerical simulations, the peak axial capacities from FEA were compared with the experimental results for the six reference pipes. The FE model predicts peak loads of 105.80 kN vs. 106.01 kN (pipe 76 × 1.5), 130.69 kN vs. 129.96 kN (pipe 89 × 1.5), 208.81 kN vs. 207.49 kN (pipe 89 × 2.0), 237.51 kN vs. 234.39 kN (pipe 89 × 2.5), 149.17 kN vs. 148.04 kN (pipe 101 × 1.5), and 133.63 kN vs. 133.86 kN (pipe 114 × 1.5) for FEA and experiments, respectively. The normalized FEA/Test ratios range from 0.998 to 1.013, corresponding to a difference of approximately − 0.20% to + 1.3%, with a mean absolute percentage error of about 0.61%. These findings show that the developed nonlinear FE models accurately replicate the experimental peak capacity throughout for the examined pipe structures. Minor discrepancies mainly occur in the post-peak region, where the numerical responses show slightly more gradual strength degradation. This may be due to the idealized representation of imperfections and the challenge of highly localized collapse in thin-walled shells.

Axial load and displacement response for specimens with H of 400 mm.

The experimental program was necessarily limited to a finite set of specimens due to manufacturing and testing constraints. To further examine different sizes of cylindrical shells, an FE analysis was performed. To investigate a broader geometric domain relevant to practical stainless-steel shell components and to systematically quantify sensitivity trends, a parametric GMNIA study was performed by extending the geometric ranges of D/t (or R/t) and H/D beyond the tested specimens. In this way, the transition from shorter to longer shell behavior was assessed as H/D increased. Furthermore, imperfection sensitivity was evaluated over a wider slenderness spectrum. For clarity, the results are presented by explicitly distinguishing the experimentally tested/validated domain from the extended parametric domain; predictions obtained outside the tested range are identified as extrapolative and are interpreted as numerical trend indicators. The proposed ARX predictor is therefore recommended for use within the experimentally validated parameter space. Furthermore, since it provides a transparent and computationally efficient surrogate for preliminary capacity prediction, an autoregressive model with exogenous inputs (ARX) was adopted. The ARX formulation offers an interpretable mapping between the predicted capacity and physically meaningful inputs, including geometric parameters and imperfection amplitude and can be implemented with low computational cost. Predictive performance was evaluated through a validation process outlined in this section, and the model is designed for use within the experimentally validated parameter space. To enhance readability, the complete ARX coefficient listings are included in the Appendix, while the main text emphasizes the model structure and performance metrics.

GMNIA were performed for the parametric combinations of geometric slenderness and imperfection amplitude, and the corresponding peak axial buckling stress (σ(cr)) was extracted as the response variable. An ARX model was then employed for preliminary prediction of the critical buckling stress. The ARX identification was conducted separately for a = 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0, corresponding to different construction quality levels and associated imperfection amplitudes. The resulting models exhibit relatively high polynomial orders (up to z − 41 and z − 42), reflecting the use of ARX as a data-driven surrogate to approximate nonlinear geometry–strength interactions rather than as a physical dynamic model. The order of the polynomial (e.g., z − 41) indicates the number of coefficients (lags or basis terms) used to represent the relationship between the input variables and the output response. Accordingly, a z – 41 model corresponds to a polynomial containing 41 coefficient terms. Geometry-related inputs dominate the identified polynomials, while the modulus input (E = 200 GPa) appears as a low-magnitude baseline term due to its constant value across the dataset. The proposed model for the shell buckling strength is applicable for the range of tank geometries discussed earlier. Specifically, it is valid for H/D ratios between 4.0 and 12.0 and for eigenmode-affine imperfections corresponding to the three construction quality levels previously defined.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively investigated the structural behavior of thin-walled cylindrical shells made of stainless steel under axial loading using experimental, numerical, and predictive (ARX model) methods. Based on the findings, the following major conclusions were reached.

The tests showed that medium and long shell structures (400 mm, 500 mm, and 600 mm in length) with D ranging from 76 to 114 mm exhibited length-independent behavior in terms of load-carrying capacity, contrary to expectations. This situation proves that the structures are dominated by local elastic–plastic buckling modes (especially “elephant foot” and similar types of modes concentrated at the ends of the specimens) rather than global buckling.

This situation proves that the structures are dominated by local elastic–plastic buckling modes (especially “elephant foot” and similar types of modes concentrated at the ends of the specimens) rather than global buckling.

The developed FEA showed high agreement with experimental data. The integration of initial geometric defects measured by the DIC method into the model minimized the error margin in buckling load predictions and confirmed the reliability of the model as a tool for engineering design.

ARX (autoregressive with external input) models, applied for the first time in this study, have proven superior to traditional regression methods in predicting nonlinear buckling capacity. Despite the variability in the data set, high correlation coefficients of the model demonstrate that it offers a fast and effective alternative for the design of complex shell structures.

For engineering applications, it is recommended that D/t and material yield strength be taken as the primary criteria in the design of medium-length stainless steel shells, rather than the H/R ratio. Furthermore, it is recommended that DIC-based defect mapping be established as a standard protocol in aerospace and storage tank designs requiring high precision.

Future research may focus on the effect of different stainless steel alloy types (e.g., duplex steels) and dynamic/cyclic loading conditions on buckling. Furthermore, combining the ARX model with hybrid artificial intelligence algorithms will enable the development of universal design formulas across much larger datasets.

This study has concentrated on thin-walled stainless-steel shells within the specified ranges of D/t, H/D, and imperfection amplitude. Predictions outside these ranges are extrapolative and should be regarded as numerical trend indicators that necessitate additional validation. Consequently, the proposed model ought to be applied with caution and corroborated prior to implementation in design.
