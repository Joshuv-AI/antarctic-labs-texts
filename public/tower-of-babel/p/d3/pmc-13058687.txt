Characterization of Pulmonary Dysfunction in Systemic Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis Using Xenon and Proton MRI
Authors: William J. Garrison, Matthew M. Willmering, Bilal I. Masokano, Shipra Rai, Antoinette Wannes Daou, David J. Roach, Esi M. Morgan, Jason C. Woods, Zackary I. Cleveland, Emrah Gecili, R. Paul Guillerman, Andrew H. Schapiro, Grant S. Schulert, Alexei A. Grom, Christopher T. Towe, Laura L. Walkup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSystemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis‐associated lung disease (SJIA‐LD) is increasingly recognized and associated with potentially life‐threatening complications. Diagnosis is challenging as SJIA‐LD is complex and frequently presents with subtle or no respiratory symptoms, necessitating CT imaging or other tests to detect budding dysfunction. We hypothesized that xenon and proton lung MRI could identify SJIA‐LD‐associated structural changes and functional deficits.MethodsThirty‐one participants aged 5–15 (16 healthy, 7 SJIA without diagnosed lung disease [SJIA‐non‐LD], 8 SJIA‐LD) underwent MRI for this cross‐sectional study. Participants underwent xenon ventilation MRI (14 healthy/6 SJIA‐non‐LD/7 SJIA‐LD), xenon gas‐exchange MRI (12 healthy/3 SJIA‐non‐LD/5 SJIA‐LD), and/or proton ultrashort echo time (UTE) MRI (12 healthy/5 SJIA‐non‐LD/7 SJIA‐LD). Ventilation defect percentage (VDP) and ratios of xenon signal in alveolar membrane (Membrane), red blood cells (RBC), and gas were calculated from xenon MR images. CTs and PFTs were obtained from participant medical records where available for comparison.ResultsRBC/Membrane differed significantly (p = 0.014) between healthy individuals and SJIA‐LD patients, and corresponded with DLCO results in a limited sample of participants with both measurements. VDP did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.30), but was abnormally high in 3/7 SJIA‐LD patients. SJIA‐LD pattern findings in UTE MRI were detected in 4/7 SJIA‐LD patients and in no healthy or SJIA‐non‐LD participants (p < 0.001).ConclusionXenon and proton lung MRI can identify SJIA‐LD‐associated pulmonary abnormalities, with RBC/Membrane showing particular promise for characterization of pulmonary diffusion limitation in SJIA‐LD. Future studies will evaluate lung MRI for SJIA‐LD phenotyping and longitudinal monitoring of SJIA‐associated lung function changes.

Introduction

Systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (SJIA) is a pediatric rheumatic disease that comprises roughly 10% of juvenile idiopathic arthritis, with an incidence of approximately 0.4–0.8 cases per 100,000 children [1]. SJIA is characterized by fever and arthritis, accompanied by rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and/or serositis [2]. Over the last 2 decades, SJIA‐associated lung disease (SJIA‐LD) has been recognized at increasing rates among SJIA patients and is associated with 5‐year mortality following diagnosis greater than 5% [3, 4, 5, 6]. Prominent risk factors for SJIA‐LD include SJIA diagnosis at younger ages, particularly the age of 2 or younger; history of recurrent macrophage activation syndrome (MAS); and adverse reactions to medications including IL‐1 and/or IL‐6 inhibitors [6, 7]. SJIA‐LD is complex and varies in phenotype between individuals, with one or more of interstitial lung disease (ILD), pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), endogenous lipoid pneumonia, and/or pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) typically observed in patients with SJIA‐LD. Onset of clinically discernible SJIA‐LD tends to occur 1–2 years after SJIA diagnosis, and patients of all SJIA‐LD phenotypes frequently present with subtle or no respiratory symptoms at the time of SJIA‐LD diagnosis [8, 9].

Non‐invasive pulmonary imaging methods are appealing to screen SJIA patients at high risk to develop SJIA‐LD and to characterize treatment response in those diagnosed with SJIA‐LD. Thoracic high‐resolution computed tomography (CT) is currently considered the most sensitive and specific modality to diagnose SJIA‐LD [4, 5, 8] and has been favored to date for assessing response to potential SJIA‐LD treatments [10, 11]. The most prevalent CT findings in patients with SJIA‐LD include peripheral/peribronchovascular consolidation or interlobular septal thickening in an anterior upper lobe and basilar distribution, ground‐glass opacities, and thoracic lymphadenopathy [4, 8]. However, while CT produces structural thoracic images with high spatial resolution and can detect structural abnormalities associated with SJIA‐LD, it provides only limited information on regional lung function. Additionally, CT exposes patients to ionizing radiation, reducing its appeal for serial imaging of SJIA‐LD burden and treatment response, especially in asymptomatic children [12]. Thus, there is a pressing need to develop non‐ionizing imaging techniques to screen SJIA patients for developing lung disease [13].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can deliver a wide array of spatially‐resolved measurements of pulmonary health. High‐quality proton MR images of lung anatomy can be obtained using respiratory‐gated radial ultrashort echo time (UTE) pulse sequences, overcoming somewhat the low water density and short T2* of lung parenchyma [14, 15, 16]. Proton UTE MRI holds promise as a non‐ionizing alternative to CT for lung structural imaging. Xenon ventilation MRI can visualize ventilated and non‐ventilated lung regions by using inhaled hyperpolarized xenon gas as a contrast agent [17, 18, 19, 20], and in some cases can detect early functional changes in pediatric pulmonary disease in patients without functional deficits on pulmonary function tests (PFTs) or who are unable to reliably perform PFTs [21, 22]. Additionally, xenon gas‐exchange MRI leverages the distinct resonant frequencies of 129Xe in three separate compartments of the lung microenvironment—within alveolar air spaces, dissolved in alveolar membrane and blood plasma, and affiliated with the hemoglobin in red blood cells (RBC)—to quantify and visualize regional xenon gas exchange. Increased membrane uptake and decreased RBC transfer as measured by xenon gas‐exchange MRI are increasingly recognized as common features of interstitial lung disease in both adults and children [19, 23, 24].

The goal of this cross‐sectional study was to perform proton UTE MRI and xenon MRI in a cohort of children with diagnosed SJIA‐LD. The secondary goal was to compare MRI characteristics in SJIA‐LD children to healthy controls and patients with SJIA without diagnosed lung disease, and to compare MRI outcomes with CT and PFT outcomes across these three participant groups. Our hypothesis was that proton UTE MRI and xenon MRI can identify structural and functional changes in the lung associated with SJIA‐LD, including functional deficits that are difficult or impossible to detect via other methods.

Methods

Participants

All experiments described in this work were approved by the Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center institutional review board (IRB 2014–5279) and were performed according to the standards outlined in the US Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects. Xenon MRI studies were performed under a Federal Drug Administration investigational new drug application (IND 123577). Informed written consent was obtained from the parent/guardian of each participant prior to study enrollment. Eligible volunteer participants were identified and recruited through the Division of Rheumatology and Division of Pulmonary Medicine at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center. Screening criteria for participants included age ≥5 years and ability to cooperate with instructions and reliably carry out required breathing maneuvers. Evidence in participant medical records of a clinical diagnosis of SJIA by a treating rheumatologist was required for inclusion in the SJIA group.

Hyperpolarized Xenon Dose Preparation and Administration

Isotopically‐enriched xenon gas (≥ 83% 129Xe) was polarized to approximately 25% via spin‐exchange optical pumping using a continuous‐flow 129Xe hyperpolarizer (HPX Hyperpolarizer 9820A; Polarean, Durham, NC). For xenon calibration scans, a 250‐mL xenon dose was dispensed into an inert fluoropolymer bag for inhalation (Tedlar plastic bag; Jensen Inert Products, Coral Springs, FL). For each xenon imaging scan, a xenon dose consisting of one‐sixth of predicted participant total lung capacity based on sex and height [25] was dispensed. Immediately prior to each xenon scan, the hyperpolarized xenon dose was inhaled by the participant from approximately functional residual capacity. Participants then held their breath for the entirety of the xenon scan (< 15 s). For low‐resolution proton breath‐hold anatomical images, a dose bag of room air of identical volume to that of the corresponding xenon dose bag was prepared and administered immediately prior to the start of the proton scan.

MRI Acquisition

MRI was performed on a 3 Tesla (T) whole‐body scanner (Achieva; Philips Healthcare, Best, NL). Xenon images were acquired using a custom‐built dual loop coil [26] or a quadrature vest coil (Clinical MR Solutions, Brookfield, WI) tuned to the Larmor frequency of 129Xe at 3 T. Low‐resolution proton MR images acquired for xenon lung mask generation were acquired using the scanner's built‐in body coil. High‐resolution free‐breathing proton UTE MR images were acquired using a 32‐channel cardiac coil (Philips Healthcare). Xenon calibration scans were performed prior to xenon ventilation and gas‐exchange MRI to determine the 129Xe central frequency, appropriate xenon MRI flip angle correction factor, and optimal TE for xenon gas‐exchange MRI [27].

Xenon ventilation MRI was acquired using a multi‐slice 2D axial slice‐selective sequence; 12–20 slices with slice thickness = 15 mm were acquired. Accompanying multi‐slice 2D axial proton MR images were acquired in a separate breath‐hold; 9–14 slices with slice thickness = 15 mm were acquired. 3D radial xenon gas‐exchange MRI was acquired using previously‐published pulse sequence methods [19, 27, 28]. Accompanying low‐resolution 3D radial UTE proton MR images were acquired in a separate breath‐hold. Free‐breathing high‐resolution proton UTE MRI was acquired using a respiratory‐triggered 3D stack‐of‐stars sequence. The duration of the free‐breathing UTE MRI acquisition varied based on participant size and breathing regularity, but generally fell between 6 and 8 min and was less than 10 min in all cases. Pulse sequence parameters for all MRI scan types listed above are given in Table S1.

Xenon MR Image Reconstruction and Analysis

Xenon ventilation images, corresponding proton anatomical images, and proton UTE images were reconstructed on the MRI scanner. Coil inhomogeneity and surface coil flare artifacts in free‐breathing proton UTE images were corrected using the constant level appearance (CLEAR) correction method included in the scanner software. All other MR image reconstructions and analyses were performed in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA) using workflows based on open‐source software packages [29, 30]. Ventilation defects were identified in each bias‐corrected, manually‐segmented xenon ventilation image by applying a threshold equal to 60% of the mean xenon signal in the image in question [31, 32, 33]. Ventilation defect percentage (VDP) was then defined as the percentage of segmented voxels in the bias‐corrected xenon image with signal below the above‐defined threshold. The upper limit of normal (ULN) value for VDP was set at 4.5% based on age‐adjusted calculations presented previously [34], and individuals with VDP greater than 4.5% were considered to have abnormal ventilation.

Xenon gas‐exchange images for each of three 129Xe compartments (gas, alveolar membrane/blood plasma [Membrane], red blood cell [RBC]) and corresponding low‐resolution proton images were reconstructed via non‐Cartesian reconstruction with iterative density compensation using existing pipelines [19, 29], after excluding the first 60 radial projections from each of the gas‐ and dissolved‐phase acquisitions. One‐point Dixon decomposition was used to separate the dissolved‐phase image into membrane and RBC components [28, 35]. Xenon gas‐exchange images and corresponding low‐resolution proton images were co‐registered, and lung masks derived from the proton images were applied to the xenon gas‐exchange images. The resulting masked xenon gas, membrane, and RBC images were divided by one another to produce the following self‐normalized, spatially‐resolved readouts of pulmonary xenon gas transfer: Membrane/Gas, RBC/Gas, and RBC/Membrane [27].

CT and PFT Collection

CT images acquired fewer than 18 months before or after MR imaging were collected from participant medical records when available. PFTs were attempted during MRI visits or were collected from participant medical records if clinical PFTs had been performed fewer than 6 months before or after MR imaging. Specific PFT readouts used for this study included forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV1/FVC, and diffusing capacity of lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO).

Evaluation of Proton UTE MR and CT Images

Proton UTE MR and CT images were independently reviewed and scored for pulmonary findings by two pediatric radiologists (R. P. G. and A. H. S.) with 26 and 8 years of post‐fellowship experience, respectively. MR images were scored on a scale from 1 to 4, with 1 signifying that images were of nondiagnostic quality; 2 signifying that images were adequate for gross findings (consolidation/confluent atelectasis or marked interstitial thickening); 3 signifying that images were adequate for both gross findings and more subtle diffuse lung disease findings of ground‐glass opacities, mild interstitial thickening, or small nodules/cysts; and 4 signifying that images were of comparable quality to CT. Lung MRI findings were classified into the following categories: interstitial thickening, consolidation/confluent atelectasis, other, and none. CT and MR images were assigned a score of “yes,” “no,” or “indeterminate” based on whether an SJIA‐LD pattern was visible [4, 8].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical tests were two‐sided (α = 0.05). Continuous variables were summarized as median (IQR), and no less than five data points within a participant group were required for that group to be included in a statistical test for a given continuous variable. Fisher's exact test on a 3 × 3 contingency table was used to assess associations between participant groups (healthy, SJIA‐non‐LD, SJIA‐LD) and reader‐determined SJIA‐LD patterns on proton UTE MR images [36]. Post‐hoc pairwise group comparisons were then performed using Fisher's exact tests on 2 × 3 contingency tables, with Bonferroni correction applied to p‐values from these tests. VDP and PFT results (FEV1 %‐predicted, FVC %‐predicted, and FEV1/FVC) were compared across the three participant groups using Kruskal‐Wallis tests [37]. Due to small sample size in the SJIA‐non‐LD group for gas‐exchange data (three participants), the SJIA‐non‐LD group was excluded from gas‐exchange statistical comparisons across participant groups. Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests were therefore used to compare Membrane/Gas, RBC/Gas, and RBC/Membrane between the healthy and SJIA‐LD groups [38]. Spearman correlation was used to assess associations between PFT readouts and VDP for all available samples across all participant groups.

Results

Demographics

Thirty‐one participants (15 with diagnosed SJIA, 16 healthy) were enrolled and underwent MRI for this study between May 2018 and October 2021. Eight participants with SJIA had been diagnosed with SJIA‐LD by a treating rheumatologist and/or pulmonologist prior to MRI using a screening algorithm for SJIA patients previously published by clinicians at our medical center [5], and the remaining seven participants with SJIA (hereafter referred to as SJIA‐non‐LD) had not. For SJIA‐LD participants, the average time between clinical SJIA‐LD diagnosis and the MRI study visit was 2.3 years.

Xenon ventilation MRI was successfully performed in 27 participants (14 healthy, 6 SJIA‐non‐LD, 7 SJIA‐LD), xenon gas‐exchange MRI was successfully performed in 20 participants (12 healthy, 3 SJIA‐non‐LD, 5 SJIA‐LD), and free‐breathing proton UTE MRI was successfully performed in 24 participants (12 healthy, 5 SJIA‐non‐LD, 7 SJIA‐LD). CT images acquired fewer than 18 months before or after MR imaging were available in five participants (two SJIA‐non‐LD, three SJIA‐LD). Acceptable and repeatable FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC results according to American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines were available in 21 participants (10 healthy, 5 SJIA‐non‐LD, 6 SJIA‐LD). Additionally, acceptable FEV1 (but not FVC or FEV1/FVC) was available in three participants (one healthy, aged 9; two SJIA‐LD, aged 7 and 8), per ATS criteria. DLCO was available in five participants (two SJIA‐non‐LD, three SJIA‐LD). Failure to obtain repeatable spirometry results was attributed to poor participant compliance in all cases, likely due to participant age as all three participants who produced acceptable but not repeatable spirometry results were under the age of 10. Median (IQR) RBC/Membrane ratios for the healthy, SJIA‐non‐LD, and SJIA‐LD groups were 0.49 (0.43–0.54), 0.46 (0.40–0.52), and 0.38 (0.37–0.43), respectively (Figure 5b; Table 1). Of the five participants with available DLCO, three SJIA‐LD participants had xenon gas‐exchange MRI, and two SJIA‐non‐LD participants did not. DLCO %‐predicted was between 59% and 66% for the three SJIA‐LD participants (Figure 5), and was 106% and 112% for the two SJIA‐non‐LD participants.

Discussion

Markers of lung function derived from xenon MRI revealed differences between healthy individuals and SJIA patients. RBC/Membrane measured by xenon gas‐exchange MRI was significantly lower in the SJIA‐LD group than in the healthy group, and low RBC/Membrane was observed in one of the three SJIA‐non‐LD patients who performed xenon gas‐exchange MRI. DLCO results for comparison with RBC/Membrane were limited, but all three SJIA‐LD patients for whom both measurements were available had DLCO %‐predicted less than 70% and RBC/Membrane well below the median in healthy individuals in the study, and both SJIA‐non‐LD patients with DLCO had DLCO %‐predicted of greater than 100%. Key proposed criteria for SJIA‐LD diagnosis include radiographic and/or histopathological findings consistent with one or more of PAH, ILD, and/or PAP [8]. Previous studies have found low xenon MRI‐derived RBC/Membrane in adults with PAH [39] and ILD [23, 24] relative to healthy controls. The results shown in this work suggest that RBC/Membrane from xenon gas‐exchange MRI may play a useful role in detecting and monitoring regional interstitial processes and associated pulmonary diffusion limitation in SJIA‐LD.

While median differences in VDP between the healthy, SJIA‐non‐LD, and SJIA‐LD groups were small, three out of seven SJIA‐LD patients (43%) displayed VDP above 6%, exceeding the 4.5% upper limit of normal VDP for healthy children. Of these patients, FEV1 was low in two individuals but normal in a third. These preliminary results suggest xenon MRI may be useful for detection and monitoring of ventilation deficits in SJIA‐LD patients who appear normal on PFTs. It is reasonable to expect that ventilation deficits may appear on xenon ventilation MRI in some SJIA patients, given that peribronchovascular thickening and associated airway narrowing may be observed on CT images in SJIA‐LD patients [8].

Gross SJIA‐LD pattern findings from proton UTE MR images were detected in four out of seven SJIA‐LD patients (57%), and in no healthy or SJIA‐non‐LD participants. While CT images were only available in limited cases and only for SJIA patients, the presence or absence of gross SJIA‐LD pattern findings matched between proton UTE MR images and CT images in all available cases. These initial results suggest that proton UTE MRI warrants further investigation as an alternative to CT for detection and serial monitoring of SJIA‐LD‐associated gross structural abnormalities. While the image quality of proton UTE MR images in this work precluded detection or characterization of more subtle SJIA‐LD pathological features observable by CT, ongoing technical development in structural proton lung MRI may soon facilitate this as well [40].

This study was limited by the low number of participants and incomplete data sets for some participants. Enrollment was constrained by the rarity of the condition, as the number of SJIA patients seen clinically at our institution is about 50–75 per year. Xenon gas‐exchange MRI was available in only 20 of the 31 participants, and three of the seven enrolled SJIA‐non‐LD participants, preventing inclusion of the SJIA‐non‐LD group in groupwise gas‐exchange MRI statistical comparisons. Gas‐exchange MRI was attempted unsuccessfully in two of the 11 participants in question (a 7‐year‐old SJIA‐LD patient and a 5‐year‐old healthy participant), with both failures attributed to poor breath‐hold compliance. The remaining participants either did not receive gas‐exchange MRI due to a variety of study design reasons (five participants) or received gas‐exchange MRI using a different acquisition sequence that precluded direct comparison with the other gas‐exchange MRI scans (four participants).

Due to the cross‐sectional and exploratory nature of this study, we did not assess progressive changes in lung function prior to and following SJIA‐LD diagnosis, and comparisons to clinical outcomes from PFTs and CT images were largely limited to available retrospective data in participants’ electronic medical records. CT images and DLCO were only sparsely available in our participant group, reducing our ability to compare CT images and DLCO with MR images and MRI‐based readouts. Future prospective, longitudinal studies will focus on developing MRI‐based outcomes for early assessment and monitoring of SJIA‐LD in young children.

In this study, we assessed MRI‐based readouts of lung structure and function as potential markers of lung disease burden in SJIA. We found significantly reduced RBC/Membrane in SJIA‐LD patients relative to healthy individuals, suggesting that xenon gas‐exchange MRI is sensitive to altered lung microstructure in SJIA‐LD. We did not find a statistically significant groupwise difference in VDP, but noted that VDP was elevated above the upper limit of normal in select SJIA‐LD patients in our study. Additionally, we found close correspondence in a limited sample between gross SJIA‐LD pattern findings on proton UTE MRI and CT. Given these results, as well as the difficulties associated with obtaining quality PFTs and risks associated with CT in children diagnosed with SJIA at young ages, further examination of MRI for serial monitoring of lung function changes in SJIA is well warranted.
