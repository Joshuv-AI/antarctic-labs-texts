YOLO11-guided swin transformer for molar occlusion classification
Authors: Mohamed Hosny, Bayan Abusafia, Ibrahim A Elgendy, Samia Allaoua Chelloug, Mohammed Muthanna, Yiqiang Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolar occlusion identification is a fundamental component of orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. Conventional assessment using intraoral photographs relies on manual visual inspection, making it time-consuming and susceptible to inter-observer variability. Moreover, existing deep learning (DL)-based approaches either analyze the entire image without explicit anatomical localization or depend on manually defined regions of interest, thereby limiting clinical applicability. Accordingly, this study proposes a DL framework for molar occlusion classification. The proposed framework blends YOLO11-based anatomical localization with a Swin Transformer (Swin-T) for occlusion recognition. YOLO11 performs automatic instance segmentation to identify the molar region and eliminate non-diagnostic background content. The resulting prediction-guided molar crops are then processed by Swin-T, which learns discriminative representations of posterior occlusal relationships through hierarchical shifted-window attention and multiscale feature modeling. A dataset of 1101 intraoral images spanning five occlusion classes was used. The localization stage achieved a Dice coefficient of 96.82%. The developed framework attained classification accuracy of 98.17%, outperforming expert orthodontist assessment. Furthermore, the proposed framework outperformed well-established models, including ResNet50V2, DenseNet201, MobileNetV3-Large, EfficientNetV2-S, and vision transformer (ViT-B/16). Experimental results demonstrated perfect recall for Half Class II, Class III, and Half Class III, while maintaining strong predictive performance for Class I and Class II. Localization and explainability analyses confirmed that the framework consistently attended to clinically relevant posterior occlusal structures, affirming the anatomical plausibility of its predictions. These findings provide a promising foundation for automated molar occlusion assessment and suggest potential for future integration of DL-driven decision support into digital orthodontic workflows.Keywords: Molar occlusion classification, Molar localization, YOLO11, Swin transformer, Orthodontic decision supportSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Orthodontics is a focused area of dental practice dedicated to the recognition, early intervention, and comprehensive treatment of structural deviations in the dentition and craniofacial region, including the correction of malocclusion and the alignment of displaced teeth1. A fundamental pillar of orthodontic assessment is the classification of molar relationships using Angle’s classification system. This framework utilizes the anteroposterior relationship between the maxillary and mandibular first molars as a primary diagnostic reference. The maxillary first molar is designated as the key to occlusion by serving as the anchor for establishing arch relationships2. In this system, patients are classified into three primary categories based on their molar relationships. Class I is diagnosed by the harmonious interdigitation of the maxillary first molar mesiobuccal cusp within the mandibular first molar buccal groove. Class II is defined by a retruded mandibular first molar position relative to the maxillary first molar. Class III is characterized by a protruded mandibular first molar position relative to the maxillary first molar. Molar relationship classification serves as a critical diagnostic step that guides orthodontic treatment planning. By quantifying the discrepancy between a patient presenting malocclusion and an idealized functional state, this analysis guides subsequent skeletal and dental evaluations3. Beyond its diagnostic role, molar relationship assessment remains an important reference throughout the orthodontic care pathway. Accurate identification of molar relationships contributes to treatment planning, facilitates communication among clinicians, and supports the monitoring of occlusal changes during treatment. Furthermore, post-treatment molar relationships are widely regarded as important indicators for evaluating treatment success, functional occlusal stability, and long-term orthodontic outcomes. Although molar classification alone is insufficient to determine the complete therapeutic strategy, it provides a standardized and clinically meaningful framework for evaluating sagittal occlusal relationships before, during, and after orthodontic intervention. Therefore, reliable assessment of molar relationships remains essential not only for diagnosis and treatment planning but also for evaluating treatment success and long-term occlusal stability in orthodontic practice3,4.

Furthermore, molar relationships may be assessed using several diagnostic modalities, including direct clinical examination, plaster study casts, digital dental models, and standardized intraoral photographs5. Among these approaches, lateral intraoral photographic records have become widely used in both clinical practice and orthodontic research because they provide a practical, non-invasive, and readily accessible means of documenting occlusal relationships6–8. Previous studies have demonstrated that standardized two-dimensional intraoral photographs can provide reliable information for evaluating sagittal molar relationships and may serve as a valid alternative to study casts for orthodontic assessment and screening purposes7. The diagnostic reliability of photographic records, however, depends on adherence to standardized imaging protocols, including appropriate patient positioning, adequate illumination, correct camera angulation, and clear visualization of the posterior occlusal region8. Consequently, standardized intraoral photographs represent a clinically valid source for automated molar relationship assessment and provide a strong foundation for the development of image-based artificial intelligence systems for orthodontic diagnosis5.

Orthodontists have conventionally performed occlusal evaluation through expert manual examination of plaster study casts and standardized two-dimensional intraoral photographs5. Although the identification of molar relationships from a single record can often be performed rapidly by experienced clinicians, the assessment of large numbers of patient records and the maintenance of consistent diagnostic judgments across cases remain challenging. This process requires careful visual interpretation and is susceptible to inter-examiner variability, particularly in borderline or transitional occlusal relationships9. Additionally, diagnostic performance often depends on observer interpretation, thereby increasing susceptibility to inter-examiner inconsistency. For example, Jackson et al.8 reported that orthodontists achieved approximately 79.9% accuracy when classifying molar relationships using ideal standardized intraoral photographs, with diagnostic performance declining significantly as camera angulation deviated from the optimal position. These findings highlight the challenges associated with achieving consistent assessments from photographic records alone. Moreover, variations in photographic quality, illumination, image acquisition angle, and operator-related factors can affect diagnostic consistency and reproducibility. These challenges become more pronounced in high-volume clinical settings, where large numbers of records must be reviewed efficiently10,11. Accordingly, the need for robust automated diagnostic approaches is on the rise. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into orthodontics has rapidly expanded the diagnostic landscape, augmenting manual assessments to reduce clinical burden12–14. Recent scoping reviews have highlighted the growing adoption of AI for malocclusion diagnosis, orthodontic image analysis, and clinical decision support, while also emphasizing the need for robust validation and clinically interpretable models15. Deep learning (DL) systems have demonstrated strong performance across a wide spectrum of diagnostic orthodontic tasks, including automated cephalometric landmark detection16,17, tooth segmentation18, and the detection of impacted teeth from panoramic radiographs19,20. These advances highlight the capability of AI-based models to analyze dental images and facilitate automated diagnostic and decision-support workflows21,22. Furthermore, recent studies have increasingly incorporated explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques to improve model transparency and clinical trustworthiness in dental diagnostic systems23. Nevertheless, their adoption remains uneven across orthodontic applications. Most existing systems emphasize skeletal evaluation and treatment simulation, whereas occlusal classification continues to receive comparatively limited attention24,25.

Where research into intraoral image-based Angle’s classification does exist, it faces significant challenges despite recent advances in multi-stage classification frameworks and anatomically guided approaches26. For instance, Grano de Oro et al.27 employed ResNet architecture using original images without prior cropping. The absence of anatomical localization increases the risk that models focus on irrelevant background features rather than clinically meaningful occlusal landmarks. Similarly, the framework proposed by Zhang et al.28 did not incorporate an explicit bounding-box-based localization stage for precise extraction of the molar region of interest. This lack of region-specific attention increases the risk that these models rely on spurious correlations rather than clinical features. Furthermore, the data sources used in previous studies often fail to capture the complexity of the clinical environment. Juneja et al.29 utilized a dataset of dental images rendered as 2D projections from Stereolithography files. However, these representations are extracted from intraoral scans. Therefore, they do not reflect the real-world variability inherent in routine intraoral clinical photography, such as lighting inconsistencies, saliva artifacts, and patient positioning. Despite that previous research highlights AI potential in occlusal classification, these early models often rely on static architectures. Such designs can falter in real-world clinical settings, particularly when faced with varying image orientations and extraneous visual noise. To address these vulnerabilities, this study introduces a multi-stage pipeline. Our cascaded diagnostic pipeline is designed to mimic clinical reasoning through a two-stage modular workflow. In the first stage, a you only look once (YOLO)-based segmentation network automatically localizes the diagnostically relevant molar region, generating mask-aware delineations that effectively suppress non-essential background structures like retractors, lips, and gingival tissue. The resulting prediction-guided crops are then processed by a swin transformer (Swin-T) classifier, which utilizes hierarchical attention-based representation learning to capture the spatial relationships and subtle morphological cues essential for posterior occlusal recognition. This architecture directs the model toward clinically decisive regions, improving interpretability and anatomical plausibility while mitigating the risk of shortcut learning from irrelevant image features. Notably, the framework is trained and evaluated on automatically localized crops rather than idealized manual extracts. Furthermore, the system extends beyond simple Angle’s classification to include intermediate half-unit relationships (e.g., Half Class II and Half Class III), addressing the common clinical need for more precise diagnostic resolution in transitional cases30. Related works

Accurate molar occlusion classification is essential for determining malocclusion severity and guiding orthodontic treatment. Although the angle system remains the traditional standard, traditional methods relying on physical examinations and plaster models are often time-intensive and susceptible to subjective bias. This has led to a growing interest in AI-based frameworks for the efficient analysis of intraoral data, with AI emerging as a transformative tool that enables enhanced diagnostic speed and precision through advanced landmark detection. For instance, Swaity et al.20 developed a DL-based convolutional neural network (CNN) model for the automated segmentation of maxillary impacted canines in CBCT scans. This model achieved high clinical accuracy, demonstrated by a dice similarity coefficient of 0.95 and a minimal Hausdorff Distance of 0.27 mm. Although minor manual refinements were sometimes needed in crowded apical regions, the model achieved high accuracy, with a precision of 97% and a recall of 93%. Additionally, it significantly reduced the time required for 3D model generation, improving the efficiency of orthodontic treatment planning and surgical simulations. Similarly, Küçük et al.31 introduced a hybrid AI-based framework for the automated detection of impacted teeth in panoramic radiographs. The proposed approach combined the object detection capabilities of YOLO with a real-time detection model, while a Bayesian-optimized weighted boxes fusion algorithm was employed to integrate their predictions. Meanwhile, Kartbak et al.22 investigated the effectiveness of several deep learning models, including DenseNet, Inception, and ResNet, for classifying intraoral photographs according to conventional cephalometric parameters. The results revealed considerable variation in performance across models and classification tasks, with accuracies ranging from 33.33% to 99.0%. Nevertheless, the study demonstrated that intraoral photographs possess sufficient morphological information to facilitate AI-based classification, thereby underscoring their potential as a non-invasive alternative to lateral cephalometric radiographs for preliminary orthodontic assessment.

Similar to the enumerated efforts, Tizno et al.32 introduced a multi-stage AI framework for automated tooth detection and segmentation. Their pipeline combined YOLO-based arch and tooth localization with the segment anything model (SAM) for zero-shot tooth segmentation. They demonstrated the effectiveness of anatomical localization and staged processing for orthodontic image analysis. However, the framework was designed for tooth detection and segmentation rather than occlusal relationship assessment. Consequently, this work did not address the classification of molar relationships and the interpretation of sagittal occlusal patterns. Talaat et al.33 introduced a YOLO-driven convolutional framework for the detection of six orthodontic malocclusion traits. They achieved classification accuracy of 99.99% using five standardized intraoral views collected from 700 individuals. However, that study did not address molar relationship assessment, as its emphasis was placed primarily on anterior occlusal characteristics and dental arch irregularities. Consequently, its utility appears more suitable for screening-oriented applications than for full orthodontic evaluation, where sagittal molar relationships are diagnostically important. Despite promising advances in AI-based anomaly detection and cephalometric interpretation, the classification of molar occlusion remains underexplored. Concurrently, recent research efforts have focused on developing benchmark datasets to facilitate automated malocclusion analysis. Specifically, Xue et al.34 introduced a large-scale benchmark comprising multi-view oral photographs annotated for malocclusion assessment. This study evaluated several models within a comparative framework, including CNN-, Transformer-, and graph-based architectures, thereby highlighting the growing interest in AI-driven orthodontic image analysis and the potential of deep learning for supporting clinical diagnosis. However, one of the drawbacks is that the study primarily focused on dataset development and benchmark evaluation rather than the targeted classification of molar relationships. Moreover, it did not incorporate anatomical localization to isolate relevant posterior occlusal regions.

Furthermore, beyond the aforementioned AI-based applications, research has focused on formalizing the clinical integration of intraoral photography into diagnostic workflows. For example, Göranson et al.7 demonstrated that intraoral photography constitutes a reliable and valid alternative to three-dimensional dental casts for assessing orthodontic treatment needs in adolescent populations. Specifically, with the aid of established indices such as the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), the Index of Complexity, Outcome and Need (ICON), and the Dental Aesthetic Index (DAI), the study revealed moderate to excellent agreement in assessing sagittal occlusal relationships. These findings indicate that high-quality two-dimensional lateral photographs are sufficient for malocclusion screening, potentially obviating the need for digital impressions during preliminary assessments. Meanwhile, Jackson8 investigated the diagnostic accuracy of 192 orthodontists using standardized intraoral images. Although the participants achieved accuracy rates of 79.9% for molar classification and 51.3% for canine classification, the results were highly dependent on image quality and camera angulation. Additionally, the observed variability among clinicians underscores the need for AI-driven approaches to enhance the consistency and objectivity of occlusal assessment. In addition, Bardideh et al.35 developed an AI-driven framework based on YOLOv8-Seg and Detectron2 that achieved 93.1% accuracy in dental occlusion classification. While the system showed strong agreement with clinical specialists in evaluating molar and canine relationships, its reliance on extensive manual annotation and lack of explainability remain notable limitations. Moreover, Grano de Oro et al.27 employed ResNet-based architectures to classify orthodontic Angle classes from uncropped intraoral photographs. In addition, automated data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization improved performance by 5–10%, while Grad-CAM was employed to provide visual explanations of the model’s predictions. Despite these contributions, the absence of anatomical localization or cropping remained a significant limitation, as it allowed non-clinical peripheral information to influence the model, potentially reducing its focus on clinically meaningful occlusal characteristics.

Recently, Abusafia et al.5 proposed a weakly supervised DL framework for molar occlusion classification. Their model integrated DenseNet201 with multiscale feature refinement, attention-based modules, and handcrafted feature fusion to enhance classification performance. In addition, WSDAN-based attention cropping and dropping enabled the network to focus on discriminative occlusal regions without requiring manual annotations. However, the absence of an explicit anatomical localization stage and prediction-guided molar cropping prior to classification may have reduced region specificity and increased susceptibility to irrelevant background information. Further, Zhang et al.28 developed a DL framework for the automated classification of dental occlusion. However, the framework utilized global image representations and lacked a bounding-box localization mechanism to isolate clinically relevant molar regions, potentially limiting its focus on diagnostically important features. Meanwhile, Kavousinejad et al.26 proposed a multi-stage deep learning framework for automated Angle’s classification from intraoral photographs. Their pipeline consisted of an occlusion-side classifier, side-specific molar localization models, and a ResNet50-based Angle classification network. Furthermore, Grad-CAM visualizations were employed to improve model interpretability by highlighting clinically relevant molar regions. Despite these strengths, the framework relied on bounding-box localization and was limited to conventional three-class Angle classification, which may restrict the ability to capture intermediate occlusal relationships. Existing approaches have notable limitations. Processing the entire image without explicit anatomical focus is common in previous studies. Additionally, reliance on manually prepared regions of interest persists. Furthermore, many methods are restricted to conventional three-class Angle classification. As a result, they may inadequately represent transitional occlusal relationships encountered in clinical practice. This study addresses these limitations by introducing a cascaded DL framework that integrates YOLO11-based instance segmentation for automatic molar localization with Swin-T for posterior occlusal recognition. By transitioning from idealized, manually extracted crops to an automated localization and classification pipeline, this research provides a more realistic deployment setting for five-class molar occlusion analysis, aiming to standardize digital orthodontic workflows with superior precision.

Methodology

This section illustrates the methodology of the developed molar occlusion classification system. The framework is designed as a two-stage pipeline. In the first stage, a YOLO11-based segmentation model localizes the diagnostically relevant molar region and generates class-aware instance masks. In the second stage, the segmented molar region is fed to Swin-T classifier for occlusion classification. Figure 1 shows the structure of the proposed framework. Dataset

This study is based on a multi-center dataset of 1,101 intraoral photographs. These images were collected from the Department of Orthodontics, School of Stomatology, First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China. Data was originated from patients with complete permanent dentition who attended the clinic between 2022 and 2025. An initial review of 861 patient records was conducted using predefined eligibility criteria. Following that, 1,101 images were deemed suitable for analysis. Among the study cohort, 240 patients submitted bilateral buccal photographs, while the remaining 621 provided only one image each. All images were independently classified by four experienced orthodontists. The labeling process employed a modified Angle-based classification comprising five categories, namely Half Class II, Half Class III, Class I, Class II, and Class III1,36. Class I depicts the mesiobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar coinciding with the buccal groove of the mandibular first molar. Class II represents a complete cusp-width mesial displacement of the maxillary first molar relative to the mandibular counterpart. Half Class II denotes an edge-to-edge cusp relationship. Class III indicates a complete cusp-width distal displacement of the maxillary molar, with Half Class III capturing an intermediate half-cusp posterior relation. We included half-step classes to reflect transitional occlusal configurations. In cases of disagreement among the four orthodontists, the final label was determined through a masked consensus review to minimize bias. Disagreements occurred infrequently, affecting approximately 4.5% of the images. Orthodontists re-evaluated the discrepant images independently without knowledge of the initial labels or each other’s judgments. The label agreed upon by a minimum of three orthodontists following discussion was adopted as the ground truth. The annotation process demonstrated strong consistency, yielding an inter-rater Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.94.

Buccal photographs were acquired with the patient’s dentition in maximum intercuspation (MIP), from both the right and left perspectives, with the camera oriented perpendicular to the buccal tooth surfaces and centered on the canine-premolar region. Each image encompassed the area from the distal aspect of the first molars to at least the dental midline. In line with prior findings8,37, intraoral mirrors were intentionally avoided as their use can hinder perpendicular image capture. Images that clearly display the molar relationship under adequate illumination, appropriate angulation, and satisfactory sharpness were included. Images exhibiting optical distortion, retained primary teeth, and missing first permanent molars were excluded. The final dataset incorporated variability in device type, lighting conditions, and image quality. Representative examples of the five classes are shown in Fig. 2. Data partitioning was performed at the patient level to ensure that images belonging to the same individual were assigned to only one subset. Using this strategy, 882 images from 689 patients were allocated to the training set, while 219 images from 172 patients were used for testing. The class-wise distribution was as follows: Class I comprised 350 training and 87 test images; Class II, 55 training and 15 test images; Class III, 101 training and 26 test images; Half Class II, 209 training and 50 test images; and Half Class III, 167 training and 41 test images. This class distribution reflects the natural prevalence of occlusal categories. All records were fully de-identified to protect patient confidentiality. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Dentistry at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients.

Mask generation and annotation protocol

Binary masks were generated using the Roboflow annotation platform. The same panel of four experienced orthodontists and consensus-review procedure, used for class labeling, was employed during mask generation. The protocol involved manually delineating polygonal boundaries to encapsulate the primary region of interest, specifically focusing on the maxillary and mandibular first molars. While non-diagnostic elements, such as cheek retractors and oral mucosa, were strictly excluded, the masks were extended to include proximal landmarks, including the second premolars and second molars. This contextual inclusion ensures that the model evaluates the first molar relationship relative to the surrounding dentition rather than in isolation.

Left and right buccal photographs were treated as individual samples and processed independently by the proposed pipeline. This design allows the proposed model to learn molar relationships from the naturally occurring anatomical variations present in both views.

Proposed framework

The proposed framework adopts a cascaded localization-classification architecture that reflects the clinical workflow. First, the posterior molar region is localized. Then, the localized region is analyzed to determine the occlusion class. Algorithm 1 summarizes the developed workflow. The following subsections describe each component in detail.

YOLO11-based molar localization

The first stage automatically localizes the diagnostically relevant molar region. This step is pivotal, as buccal intraoral images contain non-diagnostic content (e.g., retractors, gingiva, artifacts) that may distract the classifier. Accordingly, the model first locates the posterior structure, then identifies its occlusion class. Herein, YOLO11 is employed for instance segmentation38. This step provides a refined spatial representation, enabling accurate crop generation and focused classification. Yolo11n-seg variant was exploited because it offers an effective balance between computational efficiency and localization precision. For training, each binary molar mask was converted into a YOLO11-compatible polygon annotation. The external contour of the annotated molar region was extracted and converted into the standard YOLO11 segmentation format. To maintain a one-object-per-image formulation, only contours above a minimum area threshold were retained. When multiple valid contours existed, only the largest contour was preserved. This design is consistent with the dataset structure, in which each image contains a single diagnostically dominant molar relationship.

The model was initialized with pretrained segmentation weights and fine-tuned using an input resolution of 640 × 640 pixels for 100 epochs. During inference, multiple candidate instances may be produced. Since the classifier requires a single region of interest, the highest-confidence prediction was retained. This strategy ensures structural consistency between the localization and classification stages while suppressing lower-confidence detections that correspond to irrelevant structures. The predicted segmentation mask served as the primary localization cue, as it provides the most precise estimate of the molar support region. Localization performance was evaluated using overlap-based measures. Specifically, the predicted mask was compared with the ground-truth mask using the intersection over union (IoU) and Dice coefficient. In addition, detection rate and class consistency were examined to assess the reliability of the dominant predicted instance. Different confidence thresholds were applied depending on the role of the segmentation output. A confidence threshold of 0.25 was used during segmentation evaluation to ensure reliable localization assessment. A lower threshold of 0.05 was employed during crop generation to retain diagnostically plausible molar regions for the classification stage. This design enables the segmentation model to function not only as a localization module but also as an adaptive anatomical proposal mechanism for classification.

This stage links anatomical localization to final occlusion recognition. It transforms the detected posterior region into a standardized input for the Swin-T model. This ensured that both training and testing reflected realistic deployment conditions. The predicted segmentation mask served as the localization cue. This prediction-guided transformation generated a crop dataset for classification. Accordingly, the proposed framework offers a closer approximation to realistic operational settings.

Swin transformer for occlusion classification

Clinically relevant evidence for molar occlusion arises from cusp alignment, groove correspondence, posterior intercuspation, and the mesiodistal position of the first molars. Accordingly, the proposed framework employs Swin-T as the final classifier. Its hierarchical attention mechanism is well suited to modeling both local morphology and broader posterior relationships39. Unlike conventional vision transformers, Swin-T performs attention within local windows. It progressively expands contextual coverage through shifted-window interaction and hierarchical feature extraction. This design is suitable for molar classification, where diagnostically meaningful cues are both local and relational. The Swin-T model was fine-tuned on the prediction-guided molar crop dataset. Results and discussion

Performance analysis

Localization analysis was conduced to confirm the robustness of the segmentation stage. The Yolo11 model achieved a mean IoU of 93.92% and Dice coefficient of 96.82%. The mean centroid deviation was only 2.45 pixels, demonstrating highly accurate spatial localization of the molar region. Besides, the localization stage achieved a detection rate of 100%, with all test images successfully localized. Fallback processing was not required for any sample. All 882 training images and 219 testing images were successfully processed. Robustness analysis showed that 100% of predictions achieved IoU greater than 0.50, 99.09% exceeded IoU of 0.75, and 90.87% exceeded IoU of 0.90. Additionally, Fig. 3 characterizes the localization behavior of the YOLO11 model. It shows the distribution of image-level IoU and Dice scores on the test set. This analysis is important because mean values alone may conceal whether performance is uniformly strong or arises from a mix of very strong and weak cases. Both overlap metrics are strongly concentrated in the high-score region. Most cases cluster near the upper end of the scale. The Dice distribution shifts slightly right of the IoU distribution. This is expected, as Dice is generally less conservative than IoU for the same mask pair. Importantly, both histograms show a pronounced accumulation near the upper range. Thus, the segmentation model achieves high-quality anatomical delineation for most posterior molar regions, not just for a limited subset of easy cases. A small left-sided tail remains, corresponding to a minority of comparatively difficult samples. These lower-overlap cases are relatively rare but methodologically informative. These results demonstrate that the developed localization stage provides highly accurate and consistent anatomical delineation of the diagnostically relevant molar region. Also, the principal challenge of the localization stage lies in a limited subset of images affected by transitional occlusal geometry, less distinct posterior boundaries, and increased visual ambiguity. Therefore, YOLO11 is not merely accurate on average, but also stable across most test images. Comparative analysis of localization and classification architectures

Table 3 compares the localization performance of the evaluated segmentation models. YOLO11n-seg achieved the highest mean IoU of 93.92% and mean Dice coefficient of 96.82%. Besides, it obtained boundary IoU of 43.20%, whereas the remaining models achieved values between 13.11% and 19.43%. This signifies that the proposed YOLO11n-seg produced considerably more accurate delineation of molar contours and anatomical boundaries. Additionally, precise boundary localization is paramount in because the generated segmentation masks are subsequently used for crop generation and background suppression. Consequently, boundary accuracy directly influences the quality of the region presented to the classifier. Furthermore, YOLO11n-seg demonstrated excellent localization robustness. It achieved a detection rate of 100%, while maintaining the highest proportion of highly accurate predictions. Specifically, 99.09% of predictions exceeded IoU threshold of 0.75 and 90.87% exceeded IoU threshold of 0.90. Therefore, YOLO11n-seg consistently produced highly reliable localization outputs across a wide range of samples. YOLOv8n-seg achieved comparable performance in terms of mean IoU and Dice coefficient, suggesting that both models generated similar region-level overlap. However, YOLOv8n-seg suffers from substantially lower boundary IoU. Although YOLOv8n-seg was effective at identifying the approximate molar region, it was less accurate in defining the exact anatomical boundaries. A similar trend was observed for YOLO26n-seg. Despite that YOLO26n-seg yielded the lowest centroid deviation, indicating accurate localization of the molar center, lower overlap metrics, reduced detection rate, and considerably lower boundary IoU suggest less reliable contour delineation. Mask R-CNN with a ResNet50-feature pyramid network backbone and U-Net with a ResNet34 encoder exhibited lower performance across most evaluation metrics. Although both methods achieved perfect detection rates, they produced less accurate boundaries and larger centroid deviations than the YOLO-based models. Furthermore, the proportion of highly accurate predictions decreased substantially, particularly for IoU values greater than 0.90. This behavior is likely related to the underlying segmentation paradigms. U-Net performs semantic segmentation and therefore focuses primarily on pixel-wise region delineation without an explicit mechanism for instance-level localization. In contrast, Mask R-CNN employs a two-stage instance segmentation strategy based on region proposal generation followed by mask refinement. While effective for general object segmentation, this multi-stage process may introduce localization inaccuracies near fine anatomical boundaries. On the other hand, the YOLO-based models perform localization and mask prediction within a unified one-stage optimization framework, enabling more precise delineation of posterior molar contours. Since the proposed framework relies on the generated masks for crop extraction and background suppression, accurate boundary definition is pivotal. Consequently, the superior boundary IoU achieved by YOLO11n-seg translates directly into more reliable anatomical localization and higher-quality inputs for the classification stage.

Comparison with prior studies

Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of applying DL for molar occlusion classification29,35. Juneja et al.29 developed classification models using two-dimensional images rendered from STL files. Despite that such data provide a controlled experimental environment, they do not capture real-world orthodontic variability. This includes differences in illumination, image quality, patient positioning, and soft-tissue visibility. Furthermore, their framework relied on conventional image-classification architectures. Their framework did not incorporate an explicit anatomical localization stage. This may increase sensitivity to irrelevant background information and reduce robustness to image variability. Bardideh et al.35 employed object detection and segmentation models on real intraoral photographs. However, their framework primarily emphasized automated classification performance. It provided limited discussion regarding model interpretability and the impact of localization uncertainty on classification. In addition, they did not explicitly evaluate classification performance under a prediction-guided crop generation strategy. Recently, Abusafia et al.5 proposed an explainable weakly supervised DL framework for molar occlusion classification. Their model used DenseNet201, multiscale feature refinement, handcrafted feature fusion, and weakly supervised data augmentation (WSDA) strategy. This study demonstrated that attention-guided learning can successfully identify occlusal regions without requiring region-level annotations. They achieved accuracy of 95.43% on the same five-class classification task. This performance disparity indicates that anatomical localization and prediction-guided crop generation confer supplementary discriminative information beyond that available from raw intraoral images. Furthermore, the WSDA strategy introduces additional computational complexity due to the generation and processing of multiple attention-guided image augmentations during training. This overhead becomes increasingly pronounced for larger datasets. In this study, YOLO11 model isolates the diagnostically relevant molar region and Swin-T performs occlusion classification. By decoupling anatomical localization from semantic classification, the proposed framework reduces the influence of non-diagnostic background structures. This design reflects a realistic deployment scenario and improves classification performance. Besides, the proposed model is developed using routine intraoral photographs acquired under realistic clinical conditions. Additionally, the two-stage YOLO11-Swin-T architecture reduces the influence of non-diagnostic structures. Also, it improves robustness to background variability. Moreover, the classification stage does not rely on manually prepared regions of interest. As a result, the reported performance reflects a realistic end-to-end scenario. Finally, the proposed framework supports clinically meaningful five-class classification. It incorporates transitional categories, namely Half Class II and Half Class III. These categories are frequently encountered in orthodontic practice but are often overlooked in previous studies.

Comparison with orthodontist performance

The performance of the proposed framework was compared with that of an experienced orthodontist with more than seven years of clinical practice. Evaluation was conducted on the same independent test set using per-image assessment. The expert achieved perfect classification for Class II, Class III, and Half Class II. However, Half Class III was classified with recall of 97.6%, with one image misclassified as Class I. Class I represented the most challenging category for the expert, with 72 of 87 images correctly classified, corresponding to recall of 82.8%. Most of the errors occurred near transitional boundaries, where Class I cases were assigned to Half Class II or Half Class III. The proposed model demonstrated highly competitive performance across the five classes. For Class I, the model correctly classified 84 of 87 images with recall of 96.6%. For Class II, the model achieved recall of 93.3%, with a single image misclassified as Half Class II. For Class III and Half Class II, both the model and the expert achieved perfect recall of 100%. For Half Class III, the proposed framework correctly classified all 41 samples, achieving recall of 100%. These results indicate that the proposed framework provides highly consistent performance across both canonical and transitional occlusal categories. The largest improvement over expert assessment was observed for Class I, where the model substantially reduced confusion with neighboring transitional classes. Therefore, the proposed framework can help reduce diagnostic variability in borderline occlusal relationships while serving as a reliable decision-support tool for orthodontic assessment. McNemar’s test indicated that the proposed YOLO11-Swin-T framework significantly outperformed the expert assessment (, p=0.0095). Specifically, the proposed framework correctly classified 15 cases that were misclassified by the expert, whereas the expert correctly classified three cases that were misclassified by the model. This supports the reliability of the proposed system for automated molar occlusion classification.

Implications

This study revelations bear profound weight for clinical orthodontics, hospital-based digital dentistry, and the unfolding arc of intelligent oral healthcare systems. The proposed system offers a practical incantation for automating five-class molar occlusion assessment from routine intraoral photographs. Within hospitals and specialized orthodontic centers, the proposed system can be integrated into existing digital workflows as a near-real-time decision-support tool for molar occlusion classification. Requiring neither specialized equipment nor manual annotation, the system converts routine intraoral photographs into standardized occlusal classification outputs. This would reduce observer variance and accelerate early case triage (i.e., an invaluable boon in high-volume clinics). It does not offer replacement of clinical judgment, but a rapid second opinion to democratize access to expert-level screening, especially in peripheral realms where mastery dwells not. In multi-stage hospital rites, such as pediatric units, outpatient halls, and maxillofacial chambers, the system can automatically flag Class I, Class II, Class III, and half-cusp enigmas for priority review. This triage function proves vital for growth-sensitive interventions, enabling timely referrals and sharper interdisciplinary stewardship. Furthermore, the localization-classification architecture reflects the clinical diagnostic workflow by first localizing the posterior molar region and subsequently determining the occlusal classification. The staged architecture of the proposed framework improves deployment realism by enabling model learning under routine clinical imaging conditions rather than relying on manually cropped inputs. From a longitudinal perspective, the system can support consistent interpretation of serial patient records across different phases of treatment. Although a single class label cannot fully represent the complexity of occlusal change over time, it can provide structured and standardized metadata that facilitate auditing, retrospective analysis, and the gradual development of clinically useful orthodontic databases. In educational contexts, the framework may serve as a valuable training aid by offering an anatomy-informed reference for junior clinicians. The proposed system may be applicable to other dental diagnostic tasks beyond molar occlusion assessment. Nevertheless, clinical translation requires careful evaluation. Meaningful adoption will depend on prospective validation, calibration across different imaging devices and lighting environments as well as effective integration into real-world clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study introduced a DL-based framework for automated molar occlusion classification from routine intraoral photographs. The proposed architecture combines explicit anatomical localization with transformer-based classification through a two-stage YOLO11-Swin-T pipeline. In the first stage, YOLO11 automatically localizes the diagnostically relevant posterior molar region and generates segmentation masks that support prediction-guided crop extraction and background suppression. This localization step reduces the influence of non-diagnostic structures, including retractors, soft tissue, illumination variability, and adjacent dentition. In the second stage, the localized molar crops are analyzed using Swin-T. The localization stage achieved a mean IoU of 93.92%, Dice coefficient of 96.82%, and detection rate of 100%. Additionally, comparative experiments showed that YOLO11n-seg outperformed localization architectures, including YOLOv8n-seg, YOLO26n-seg, U-Net, and Mask R-CNN, particularly in terms of boundary delineation and localization robustness. The proposed framework achieved accuracy of 98.17% on the five-class molar occlusion classification task. Swin-T outperformed longstanding CNN and transformer backbones when trained on the localized molar crops. This confirms the suitability of hierarchical shifted-window attention for modeling both local dental morphology and broader inter-arch spatial relationships. Furthermore, the proposed framework significantly outperformed direct full-image classification approaches and achieved superior performance compared with expert orthodontist assessment. Moreover, GradCAM++ visualizations demonstrated that the model consistently focused on clinically relevant posterior occlusal structures. The remaining errors were concentrated in transitional and borderline categories, where class boundaries are inherently subtle. Therefore, the proposed framework has the potential to serve as a computer-aided orthodontic assessment tool that can be integrated into digital orthodontic workflows and chair-side clinical decision-support systems.

Despite the promising performance achieved by the proposed framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study is retrospective in nature and relies on previously collected intraoral photographs. Therefore, prospective validation in routine clinical settings is required. Furthermore, external validation on completely independent datasets was not performed. Although the model demonstrated high performance on a multi-center dataset, future studies should evaluate the framework using larger external cohorts. Moreover, future work will focus on developing lightweight deployment variants, clinician-based evaluation of the generated attention maps, and quantitative landmark-level validation.
