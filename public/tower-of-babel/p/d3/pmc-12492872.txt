Return to work for people with chronic health conditions after medical or vocational rehabilitation during the COVID-19 pandemic: a scoping review
Authors: Nadine Sänger, Jan Mathis Elling, Christian Hetzel, Betje Schwarz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis review aimed to identify factors that acted as facilitators or barriers for returning to work (RTW) for people with chronic conditions following medical or vocational rehabilitation during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA scoping review was conducted on PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCOHost and Epistemonikos. Additional articles were identified via Google Scholar and citation tracking. All retrieved reports were screened, narratively reported and consolidated into a model aligned with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF). This model illustrates how the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced RTW for people with chronic health conditions.ResultsThe search yielded n = 1,720 hits. After removing duplicates (n = 807) and screening for eligibility, n = 57 articles met the inclusion criteria. Further articles were identified via Google Scholar (n = 23) and citation tracking (n = 18), resulting in a total number of n = 98 included reports. n = 3 articles explicitly examined RTW during the pandemic. Further articles addressed medical rehabilitation (n = 39), occupational health management (n = 21), work ability and labor market (n = 17), health services (n = 10) and vocational rehabilitation (n = 7). Most reports were published in scientific journals (83%). A variety of possible barriers and facilitators of RTW during the pandemic were identified, clustered according to the ICF components and integrated into a corresponding model.ConclusionRTW during the COVID-19 pandemic has not been extensively studied yet. However, several facilitators (e.g., flexibility, remote work, time for recovery) but also barriers (e.g., therapy interruptions, increased stress, risk of infection) for RTW of people with chronic health conditions were identified. Despite mixed outcomes, these findings provide a broad overview of how the pandemic likely impacted RTW processes. Further research is needed to directly assess its effects on RTW outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24769-8.Keywords: Return to work, Rehabilitation, COVID-19, Chronic health conditions, Scoping review

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic had a profound impact on healthcare systems worldwide, as the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) posed a significant health risk, especially for vulnerable individuals such as people with chronic health conditions [1]. In addition to the risk of infection, healthcare accessibility was limited due to safety measurements, staff shortages, and reduced service capacities [2]. These restrictions did not only affect hospitals and general healthcare but also medical and vocational rehabilitation services [3, 4].

In Germany, medical rehabilitation facilities of the German Pension Insurance are responsible for restoring or strengthening patients’ health and work ability following hospital treatments (e.g. after a disc surgery, a cardiac event or a stroke) or in the case of chronic health issues. The goal of these multi-professional services, which last three to five weeks and can be provided either as inpatient or outpatient care, is to maintain earning capacity and prevent permanent work disability or, in other words, to support individuals in returning to or remaining in work. For individuals whose chronic condition prevents them from returning to their usual work environment or reentering the common labor market, vocational rehabilitation offers the possibility to gain qualifications in new fields through specialized training programs, more precisely retraining or support for reintegration, with a duration up to two years [5]. Both arms of rehabilitation faced challenges during the pandemic. Studies have shown that the capacities and therapy services were reduced, but also the number of requests for rehabilitation dropped [6, 7]. For medical rehabilitation in Germany, a decrease of 14.5% could be attributed to the pandemic [6].

Besides healthcare, the pandemic had a substantial impact on the labor market. Lockdowns, safety measures leading to business closures, and the broader economic strain collectively contributed to this situation. In Germany, the unemployment rate increased in 2020, less job offers were available and approximately 20% of all employers had to impose short-time working, which means reduced working hours [8–10]. The economic burden reached its peak in the summer of 2020 and started to recover after the second lockdown in winter. However, the number of unemployed people remained elevated until 2021 before it returned to pre-pandemic levels [9].

Given these circumstances, the pandemic affected various aspects of life and work, especially for people with chronic health conditions. This study focused on the primary goal of medical and vocational rehabilitation: to support rehabilitees in RTW upon completion of the rehabilitation program. To explore how the challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced RTW, the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides a valuable conceptual framework [11]. Following the ICF’s biopsychosocial model, a person’s level of functioning and participation is the result of a dynamic interaction between the underlying health condition (with its effects on body functions and structures), environmental factors, and personal factors. Previous studies have used the ICF framework or underlying biopsychosocial model to investigate RTW [12, 13]. Although work participation is by definition part of the ICF component ‘functioning and participation’, we align with existing approaches that consider the components of the model as a whole to draw conclusions about the RTW process.

Previous studies reported that work ability, the situation on the labor market, the health status, and further ICF components are important predictors for RTW after rehabilitation [14–17]. The aforementioned risks to health, barriers in rehabilitation services, and challenges in the labor market during the pandemic can be assigned to the ICF components, giving examples on how the pandemic might have affected the RTW process and success of people with chronic conditions.

The present work is part of a larger mixed-methods study initiated and funded by the German Pension Insurance, which aimed to investigate whether and how the success of RTW after medical or vocational rehabilitation was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, and, based on these findings, to learn from the experiences of the pandemic and to derive measures and recommendations for future action. As a first step, this scoping review aimed to examine the existing literature in order to answer the research question: ‘What barriers and facilitators to RTW emerged for people with chronic health conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic?’ Besides the review, interviews and focus group discussions with rehabilitees, rehabilitation professionals, and health care managers in companies were conducted. Additionally, administrative data of the German Pension Insurance were analyzed. This article focuses on the results of the scoping review. Further findings will be published separately.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted, following the methodology of the Joanna Briggs University [18] and considering the PRISMA-ScR-checklist [19]. Based on the categories of the ICF, which—as outlined in the introduction—is already used to conceptualize RTW processes, all identified barriers and facilitators were collected and organized alongside this framework. The eligibility criteria were defined by using the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework [20]. The population included adults with chronic conditions related to major rehabilitation indications such as oncology, orthopedics, psychiatry, cardiology, neurology, or addiction disorders. Articles focusing on Long-Covid/Post-Covid-19 were excluded (criterion E1). For the concept, a broad term was used to identify all kind of reports in the field of RTW as well as occupational, vocational, and medical rehabilitation. An article was considered for eligibility if it addressed components of the ICF that could be linked to RTW (criterion E2). The context required a clear connection to the COVID-19 pandemic (criterion E3). All types of studies and reports, also grey literature, were included, if a full text was available (no letter to the editor or conference abstracts; criterion E4). Also, articles had to be published no earlier than 2020 (criterion E5) and in German or English (criterion E6). Exclusion criteria were defined as the inverse of the eligibility criteria. Further reasons for exclusion, for example no access to full text or later appearing duplicates, were summarized with E7. To develop the search strategy, a pre-search was conducted based on the defined criteria and some phrases were added or deleted accordingly. The terms were discussed within the research team. The final search strategies can be found in the additional files [S1–S4]. The systematic search was conducted in April 2024 on PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCOHost and Epistemonikos, encompassing all type of literature and evidence levels. The obtained results were screened in three steps from one reviewer. Additional searches with five search terms were performed on Google Scholar. These terms were predominantly formulated in German to better capture publications from German institutions and authorities, which were especially relevant given the project’s national funding background [S5]. For each term, the first 20 result pages (200 reports) were screened, and “related articles” were checked for suitable publications. Additional relevant articles were identified through citation tracking between April till July 2024. Eligible articles were extracted into a standardized sheet. The extraction sheet, tested with the first ten references, captured details such as author, title, main subject, country of reference, article type, source type, aims, methods, and main results. The distribution of the articles main subject was summarized to illustrate how the topic has been researched to date. Results were reported narratively and were categorized in alignment to the ICF framework. All gathered information was consolidated into a simplified model, illustrating how the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced RTW for people with chronic health conditions.

Results

Search results

The systematic search yielded n = 1.720 hits. After the removal of duplicates, n = 913 articles were assessed for suitability. Of these, a total of n = 57 articles were included. Through additional search methods, n = 41 further articles were identified (Google Scholar: n = 23; citation tracking/manual search: n = 18). This resulted in a final number of n = 98 articles included in this review (see detailed flowchart in Fig. 1).

PRISMA-flowchart of the systematic and additional search [21]

n = 3 reports were identified that directly addressed RTW in the context of the pandemic. Another n = 8 articles, which mainly focused on other topics, mentioned RTW as a minor aspect. Medical rehabilitation was the most frequently assessed subject (n = 39), followed by occupational health management (n = 21) and work ability and labor market (n = 17). Further topics were health and health services for the target population in general (n = 10) and vocational rehabilitation (n = 7; see Fig. 2).

Included n = 98 articles according to their referring topic (single allocation)

Most of the included articles (n = 39) referred to or were published in Germany, which can be attributed to the additional search (see methods). Other countries with more than one publication were the United States (n = 12), Australia (n = 7), United Kingdom (n = 7), Canada (n = 6), China, Italy, and the Netherlands (each n = 3), and South Africa and Taiwan (each n = 2). The distribution of studies by country is visualized in Fig. 3.

The distribution of studies by country

Nearly 83% of all articles were published in scientific journals, while a further 8% were part of published collective works. Of all the publications, only 9% were classified as grey literature, predominantly comprising study reports issued by national institutions in Germany (7 out of 9).

When classified by study type, more than half (n = 56) used quantitative methods, while n = 13 used qualitative approaches, and n = 5 applied mixed methods. Reviews were conducted in n = 11 articles, of which n = 3 performed a meta-analysis. The remaining were categorized as simple reports (n = 11) or opinion papers (n = 2).

Return to work barriers and facilitators

Only a few reports directly addressed RTW. However, several facilitators and barriers regarding returning to work during the pandemic were identified through assigning them to ICF components. Therefore, all identified factors were categorized accordingly as functioning and disability related factors, activities and participation, and environmental factors, separated into workplace, labor market and rehabilitation settings (medical, vocational), and personal factors. A more precise categorization along the framework was considered, but discarded, as no added value resulted due to overlaps.

Functioning and disability related factors

As outlined in the introduction, SARS-CoV-2 posed an elevated risk for people with chronic health conditions. Included studies indicated that these challenges were particularly difficult for people with pre-existing mental health issues and could have led to worsening or new development of symptoms [22–24]. According to several reports, at-risk individuals experienced heightened concerns about their health, felt more stressed, and had to deal with mental health comorbidities [23, 25–28]. Also, some studies indicated that the general health status of people with chronic conditions was compromised in some cases because capacities in healthcare facilities were reduced, operations were postponed, and rehabilitation stays delayed. Therefore, some reports assumed unmet rehabilitation needs [29–32]. Many patients disrupted their regular appointments, treatments, or therapies, and thus risked a deterioration of their health [29, 31, 33–35].

To maintain health care services and minimize the risk of infection, online therapies—primarily for mental health issues—were offered as a substitute in some cases. However, these were generally perceived as less satisfactory by both patients and therapists [36]. Simultaneously, the introduction of online consultations and the expansion of remote work also offered advantages. These measures improved access to and organization of medical appointments, such as follow-up or routine care, by increasing flexibility and reducing the need for commuting [29, 37]. Some reports indicated that such changed processes required greater competences and health literacy for the effective management of chronic illnesses during the pandemic [38, 39]. Despite that, there were indications that the altered conditions during the pandemic allowed for more time for recovery, health, and self-care, which seemed to be an enabling factor to stay at work for people with chronic health conditions [25].

Activities and participation

Activities and participation were significantly impacted and restricted. Several studies reported that less contact to friends, family, and colleagues—due to remote working or physical distancing—led to isolation [e.g. 40, 41]. Vulnerable groups were particularly affected, as the fear of a COVID-19 infection was observed to amplify feelings of isolation [27, 42]. Isolation and uncertainty related to the pandemic were reported to be associated with a decreased well-being in general, which appeared as a further hindering factor of RTW [43]. Also, restrictions regarding hobbies and outdoor activities were mentioned to result in reduced physical activity, which, depending on the condition, increased the risk of health deterioration [40, 44–47]. Several publications stated how the lack of social interactions reduced participation in everyday life, emphasizing its impact on individuals with mental health conditions and unemployed individuals [48–52]. Simultaneously, other studies indicated that some affected individuals perceived a decreased social demand, likely resulting from lockdowns and contact restrictions, which was associated with reduced stress levels [43, 44, 53].

Additional barriers were reported for people with disabilities. For those with hearing impairments, difficulties appeared from communication when masks obscured lip-reading. For people with visual disabilities, social distancing seemed to cause the greatest discomfort [54].

Environmental factors—workplace

At the workplace, several publications stated how occupational health management shifted its focus and resources from conventional topics like nutrition, physical activities, or ergonomics to infection control and safety measures [50, 55–57]. To meet the contact restriction requirements, remote working was widely expanded and companies implemented various strategies like physical distancing or consistent teams [57–60]. Some organizations introduced programs to support employees in adaption to the changed conditions, e.g. digital courses on mindfulness or healthy remote working practices [59, 61]. As authors assumed, these changes will possibly have an influence on occupational health management in the future as well [62]. However, despite positive feedback, acceptance and usage of these programs were reported to be low [63–67]. Maintaining communication was indicated as critical for employees who valued staying connected with colleagues, receiving updates, and resolving uncertainties. To address this, some organizations implemented consultation hours, regular online meetings, or informal communication channels [68–71]. For workers in sectors without remote working options, especially in the health sector, increased stress and fear of infection was observed [58, 67]. Due to this strained situation, a demand for more practical support appeared in these roles to provide relief in daily life, e.g. quiet rooms or free meals [67, 72]. Clear processes and strong leadership were also observed as helpful in these contexts [64, 73–75].

For employees working from home, positive and negative influences on healthy working were reported. According to some studies, especially people with chronic health conditions could benefit from less challenges with commuting and easier scheduling of medical appointments [29, 37]. Therefore, they were enabled to create a healthier work-life balance and subsequently re-enter work with less stress and organizational effort [43]. According to a study focusing on after care following orthopedic procedures during the pandemic, these benefits could have even contributed to shorter sick leave durations [76]. Simultaneously, barriers were indicated for remote working people through the risk of blurring between work and private life, digital stress, and increased burden in case of caring for children or relatives [40, 41, 53, 57].

When reintegrating to the workplace during the pandemic, it was noted that well-structured processes, transparent communication, and a sense of safety and value within the team were beneficial for both returning employees and their colleagues [43, 77]. On the contrary, hindering factors for RTW during the pandemic were observed if reintegration services and support through managers were unavailable, or when managers were unable to adequately monitor and assist returning employees. This was attributable to increased workloads, limited capacities, or physical distancing due to the pandemic [43, 51, 78]. Several publications added that changed working conditions due to pandemic restrictions and a higher workload led to fear of RTW and not being able to deal with the increased demands [27, 43, 77].

Environmental factors—labor market

Some studies reported job losses caused by the strained labor market, along with greater difficulties in securing new employment due to the economic downturn [35, 79]. Simultaneously, there is some evidence that people with disabilities or chronic illnesses were more likely to keep their job during the pandemic. According to different studies, the unemployment rate rose less for these group or was reported as normalizing more quickly [80–83]. In return, employees with a higher risk of infection were often assigned for remote work or placed on paid or unpaid leave to ensure their safety [51, 84, 85]. Multiple studies reported that this situation left vulnerable persons with a higher uncertainty, worries, and feeling of exclusion in relation to their workplace [22, 53, 86, 87]. The actual experience of job loss, short-time work, or unpaid leave was associated with mental stress, a negative impact on wellbeing and on quality of life, particularly for vulnerable people [44, 52, 57, 68, 88]. According to the literature, re-entering the labor market was more complicated because of less available positions, difficulties in accessing decision-makers, reliance on online processes, and decreased motivation [49, 68].

Environmental factors—medical rehabilitation

Multiple findings in the literature highlighted how medical rehabilitation was affected during the pandemic. During the first lockdown, some facilities had to shut down completely or did not accept new arrivals for a while [47, 89–91]. Several publications reported the necessity of safety measures, e.g., wearing a face mask, testing, and physical distancing [e.g. 3, 92, 93]. As a result of distancing and converting some premises to COVID-19 stations, the capacities for patients got reduced. At the same time, the request for rehabilitation decreased and more people did not show up to their scheduled stay, so that in total fewer patients attended medical rehabilitation. According to the literature, this could have led to unmet needs, worsening of health conditions, and longer periods of suffering [6, 30, 32–34, 51, 78, 94, 95]. Studies highlighted that the number of rehabilitative treatments, which were elective and non-urgent, declined more than post-hospital rehabilitation [6, 92]. During rehabilitation, identified reports showed how offered therapies and activities were limited in number and characteristics due to safety restrictions and staff shortage [90, 96, 97]. The availability of RTW counselling at the rehabilitation facilities was limited in some cases [89]. In addition, several publications reported that activities for leisure time were cancelled, smaller groups were established for group therapies, and in some cases, single therapy sessions were reduced [3, 29, 78, 92]. After rehabilitation, it was more challenging to secure a place in aftercare, so that a decline of participants was observed [29, 78, 92, 96]. Beside the rehabilitees, studies also reported the burden of clinical staff due to greater workload, new processes, and high levels of sick leave caused by the pandemic [3, 58, 98].

Despite these challenges, some benefits were noted as well. Smaller group sizes were well-received by most patients [3, 78]. Moreover, there is some evidence that rehabilitees felt safe and less stressed in stationary care [26, 99]. With regards to health outcomes, studies showed that physical health indicators and patient satisfaction remained consistent throughout the pandemic [26, 100, 101]. With the support of telerehabilitation, several studies indicated the potential to offer benefits for both health outcomes and organizational efficiency [102–108]. However, multiple barriers were reported as well, especially the lack of social interaction, difficulties in ensuring patient safety, the need for self-assessment of health status, limited digital literacy and access to equipment, and difficulties in recognizing and analyzing new symptoms [37, 109–112].

Environmental factors—vocational rehabilitation

Due to safety measures, the literature stated how most facilities had to transition their teaching to online courses [4, 51, 113–115]. Several barriers were reported in connection with this change. Many rehabilitees lacked the necessary digital skills or access to technical equipment [4, 49, 51, 115]. Additionally, publications highlighted how the increased demands for self-reliance and organizational skills created further challenges, particularly for individuals who also had child or other family care responsibilities [7, 113]. The online courses often led to feelings of loneliness or isolation, exacerbating the risk of mental health issues [51, 115]. Authors stated that this was particularly concerning as vocational rehabilitees often belong to vulnerable groups requiring substantial mental and social support, beyond acquiring new skills [7, 113]. Further barriers were associated with internships, which are a key component of vocational rehabilitation. Following several publications, due to the economic strain of the pandemic—along with short-time work, safety measures, and remote work policies—many companies were less willing or able to offer internships. As a result, rehabilitees gained less practical experience, making it more difficult to secure employment afterward [7, 49, 51, 115]. Overall, uncertainty among rehabilitees, higher dropout rates, fewer new enrollments, and delays in completing programs were reported [7, 51, 68, 78].

Several facilitators were identified to help mitigate these challenges. First of all, especially communication between rehabilitees and rehabilitation facilities was mentioned to successfully provide support through times of isolation [4, 7, 68]. Furthermore, online classes seemed to offer certain advantages, such as greater flexibility and less commuting [7, 51, 116]. Due to the pandemic and the resulting compulsion to establish an online infrastructure for classes, vocational rehabilitation experienced a digital transformation which survived the COVID-19 phase and will probably be a key point of future rehabilitation [4, 7, 113, 116].

Personal factors

According to several publications, people with chronic health conditions who experienced fear of COVID-19 infection reported higher stress levels, avoided public transport, canceled appointments, and expressed concern about RTW [25, 27, 42, 58, 60]. Additionally, general anxiety was found to have increased during the pandemic and was identified as a predictor for discontinuing rehabilitation [22, 117]. Safety measurements were able to reduce these fears [3, 68].

During the pandemic, reports emphasized the importance of digital competencies which became crucial across various domains, including work, accessing information, scheduling appointments, and maintaining social connections [e.g. 7, 51, 110]. Some publications added, that patients were required to manage their health care and everyday lives with greater autonomy because it was harder to access necessary health care, to participate in society, and to stay active at the labor market [e.g. 39, 41, 53]. To address these increased demands, a higher resilience was stated as necessary to master the requirements and adapt to the new situation [23].

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to examine facilitators and barriers which may have influenced the RTW process of people with chronic health conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic after a rehabilitation measure.

While RTW during the pandemic has not yet been thoroughly examined and only a small number of publications addressed the topic, it was possible to cluster more factors by considering the dimensions of the ICF. Most information was available regarding medical rehabilitation and occupational health management. Even though grey literature was consulted, most included reports were scientific papers.

A model was drafted to summarize the identified factors influencing RTW after medical or vocational rehabilitation during the pandemic. This model reinforces the assumption that, based on the mechanisms of the ICF, the pandemic could have significantly affected the RTW process and its success. Given the lack of extensive research on RTW during the pandemic, this review offers valuable insights that can serve as a foundation for future studies.

Due to the pandemic, several barriers appeared which could have led to decreased health. First of all, people with chronic health conditions belong to the at-risk group for a COVID-19 infection, which had consequences for their participation, increased fear, and posed a risk to their health. Another critical factor identified was the role of self-management and health literacy, which influence an individual’s ability to effectively manage their condition. The pandemic led to complex processes and required vulnerable individuals to remain informed and proactive. In cases where these competencies were insufficient, decreased well-being and neglected health care were observed. In general, decreased well-being and mental health due to the stress and uncertainty of the pandemic was a widespread problem, especially for people with pre-existing mental illnesses. Taking into consideration that subjective health is a predictor for RTW, these challenges could have led to barriers for returning employees [13, 14].

Medical rehabilitation treated less rehabilitees during the pandemic because of cancellations or postponing. Furthermore, we identified indications of longer waiting times and reduced therapy offers. According to the literature, this led to longer periods of suffering and worsening of conditions in some cases. Moreover, the health conditions could have also been affected because of less physical activity, bad nutrition, or disrupted medical treatments during the pandemic. Several studies reported that a higher symptom severity and lower general health negatively influenced the RTW probability [13, 118]. Moreover, according to the ICF framework, health status is a key predictor for work ability [12]. Considering these interrelationships, a more challenging RTW during the pandemic seems possible.

Despite these challenges for people with chronic conditions, some studies also stated that no negative effects on health outcomes after rehabilitation were observed. This suggests that medical rehabilitation facilities adapted effectively to maintain therapy quality and ensure successful outcomes. Smaller group sizes, for instance, allowed for greater individual attention, which may have benefited rehabilitees. Also, the usage of telerehabilitation was observed to have positive effects. Most studies reported a better or at least comparable health outcome than with common treatments. This result is consistent with present evidence [119–121]. In Germany, telerehabilitation has only been used in rehabilitation aftercare or to provide additional information during a stay in a facility. Most positive results were reported from the US.

While medical rehabilitation proved to be reasonably resilient during the pandemic, vocational rehabilitation faced distinct challenges, particularly due to the digital transformation and reduced practical opportunities. The hurried shift to online schooling, insufficient technical infrastructure, and missing digital competence were often stated as barriers. Close contact was a key factor for overcoming these obstacles. A further problem occurring from the pandemic was the absence of practice transfer because of fewer internships and job opportunities afterwards. The strained labor market and increased unemployment rates, which are crucial for successful RTW, further compounded the challenges [17]. On the other hand, the review identified some benefits for people with chronic health conditions at the labor market, as they had a higher chance to stay at their job in relation to healthy employees. Reasons for this could be dismissal protection or social selection. Therefore, no clearly positive or negative impact can be assumed. However, started due to the pandemic, the necessary digital transformation is still ongoing and has the potential to enrich vocational rehabilitation in the future, provided that the stated barriers are considered [114].

In total, the drafted model highlights numerous barriers to RTW, but also underscores the importance of facilitators. Hints from the literature, such as a lower unemployment rates for vulnerable groups, stable health outcomes for rehabilitation, positive aspects of remote working, and less pressure in the everyday life, could have even led to benefits for some groups. However, these outcomes were not uniformly observed. A good example of this is the effect of lockdown restrictions. While some studies highlighted the detrimental effects of lockdowns on mental health, others reported benefits such as reduced social pressures. Proofs for both conflicting aspects can also be found in existing literature [122, 123]. The divergence suggests that individual circumstances and resources play a significant role in determining outcome.

With our collected information and considering the suspected reasons for these contradictions, it seems that some aspects made the difference of how a person overcame the pandemic without a negative effect on health and work ability: resilience, health literacy and social support. We assume that if people with chronic health conditions were able to remain mentally stable, manage their disease throughout the complex processes, were able to organize their work-life-balance, and felt less isolated through social support—from friends, family but also in relation to work and medical demands—it was possible to overcome the challenges. Unfortunately, these characteristics are often concerned for people with chronic illnesses, as they perceive less well-being and social support, especially during the pandemic [124–126]. By considering these differing aspects, it appeared that the ability to adapt to the changed conditions was heavily influenced by the individual resources and coping mechanisms available to each person.

Beside these competencies and personal factors, fewer available re-integration management programs at work could have influenced the success of RTW. Studies detached from the pandemic already reported that an established re-entry management can support RTW [127]. In Germany, according to Social Code Book IX, § 167, a person who was on sick-leave more than six weeks a year has a claim for workplace integration management programs. Reported figures on how many companies comply with legal requirements and offer integration management vary. However, observations in Germany suggest that only about a quarter to half of all eligible employees make use of this service [128, 129]. First of all, this states that, regardless of the pandemic, the requirements for RTW programs have not been implemented across the board. On top of these already unmet needs, as stated in this review, some of the services got disrupted during the pandemic, so that even less employees got supported during their re-entry after sickness. Therefore, it can be assumed, that more people had an insufficient RTW process or reduced success in returning to work during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Limitations

The results of this review are limited at some points. First, a scoping review serves primarily to provide an overview of a topic and does not generate robust evidence. The aim was to check existing literature and get an idea of already reported facilitators or barriers, which could have affected RTW processes and success. Despite the broad search and manual additions, the review does not claim completeness. Secondly, in accordance with the method of scoping reviews, also grey literature got included in the synthesis. Even though most of the included articles were studies from scientific journals or at least scientific reports from national organizations, grey literature without peer review was included as well and no risk of bias was assessed, so that no clear evidence can be assumed. Thirdly, although the findings were discussed jointly by two researchers, the screening and data extraction were conducted by only one individual due to limited resources. This may have increased the risk of selection or extraction bias in the inclusion of studies and data interpretation. And lastly, due to the funding of the German Pension Insurance, there was a strong focus on national literature and not all kind of aspects can be transferred to the international context. Neither the burden through the pandemic nor the rehabilitation system can be easily compared. The results and the drafted model, based on the ICF framework, therefore are to be used with caution. The collected information of this review will be used as a background and knowledge base for the planned qualitative and quantitative analysis during the project, which will provide additional insight.

Conclusion

Even though RTW during the COVID-19 pandemic after medical or vocational rehabilitation has not yet been extensively studied, some facilitators and barriers were identified. By arranging them according to the ICF-based model, a potential impact on RTW processes for people with chronic health conditions seems to be plausible. Personal competencies and resources, such as health literacy, digital competence, self-care skills, and social support, were important even before the pandemic, but they appeared to gain increased relevance under these special conditions. Therefore, this study suggests that if patients were able to manage their condition independently and receive support, they could still successfully undergo rehabilitation and re-enter the workforce. However, vulnerable groups maintained more challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic and obtained more obstacles while managing their rehabilitation and RTW. Nevertheless, some potential benefits were observed in relation to rehabilitation, work, the labor market, and social demands. Some of these benefits appeared to arise from digital applications, and if considered in the future, they could offer opportunities for enhancing rehabilitation and inclusion.

This review will be used as a knowledge base for future research. Further planned quantitative and qualitative analysis will be consulted to obtain a clearer picture and to proof, if RTW quotes got negatively affected or if the identified benefits, health literacy, good structures, and support were able to even out the circumstances.
