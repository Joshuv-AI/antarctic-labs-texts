Exploring the causal relationship between gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammation-related proteins and coronary artery calcification: A multi-omics Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Lin Ma, Mingyue Xiao, Shuya Cai, Yanchun Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coronary artery calcification (CAC) is an established indicator of atherosclerosis and the incidence of cardiovascular disease. Currently, there is no effective pharmacological intervention to reverse CAC. The gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammation-related proteins have all been implicated in the mechanisms underlying CAC. However, the causal relationships and specific mechanisms connecting these factors remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate the causal relationships among the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, immune cells, and inflammation-related proteins with the risk of CAC using Mendelian randomization (MR) methods. A bidirectional, 2-sample univariate MR analysis was conducted using data from a genome-wide association study to explore potential causal relationships among 4 exposure factors and the incidence of CAC. The inverse-variance-weighted approach, complemented by a series of sensitivity analyses, was employed to strengthen the conclusions. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). The MR results indicated that 10 types of gut microbiota, 60 circulating metabolites, 21 immune cells, and 6 inflammation-related proteins were either negatively or positively associated with CAC. Reverse MR analysis found that there was no bidirectional causal relationship. The MR analysis method was employed to investigate the pathogenesis of CAC from a genetic perspective. Due to the challenges associated with clinical trials, analyzing the influence of risk factors on outcomes through comprehensive genetic data has emerged as a significant trend in disease research.

1. Introduction

Despite continued improvements to treatment strategies and outcomes, coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for 20% of all deaths before the age of 65 years in 2020, primarily due to atherosclerosis.[1] The extent of coronary artery calcification (CAC), a highly specific indicator of atherosclerosis, has been correlated with an increased incidence of cardiovascular events.[2,3] Assessment of calcium deposits within the coronary arteries is an established measure of the risk of CAD.[4]

Although commonly employed to explore causal relationships, observational studies are limited by inferences of confounding temporal environment, dietary, and lifestyle factors underlying the complex interactions between exposures and outcomes. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a genetic epidemiological approach that employs genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV) of exposure to establish causal links between exposures and outcomes.[5] The random distribution of genotypes from parent to offspring ensures that any resulting causality is unaffected by confounders and reverse causation. With the proliferation of databases, MR has emerged as a prominent methodology to identify risk factors related to cardiovascular disease (CVD).

The aim of this comprehensive 2-sample MR study was to clarify causal links of CAC with 412 gut microbiota, 1400 circulating metabolites, 731 immune cells, and 91 inflammation-related proteins.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Experimental design

As illustrated in Figure 1, various factors causally related to CAC-related traits were identified by referencing publicly available data from a genome-wide association study (GWAS). Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the requirements for ethical approval and patient consent were waived.

2.2. Data source

All data of the gut microbial taxa were retrieved from a large-scale GWAS meta-analysis of 7738 individuals of European origin (Northern Netherlands) participating in the Dutch Microbiome Project (https://dutchmicrobiomeproject.molgeniscloud.org/). The study was designed to control for confounding factors affecting gut colonization and to identify gut microbes by birdshot macro genomic sequencing of fecal samples. This is the most recent and comprehensive species-level GWAS of gut microbial composition and function to date, encompassing 207 microbial taxa (5 phyla, 10 orders, 13 orders, 26 families, 48 genera, 105 species) and 205 pathways correlated to microbial composition and function.[6]

We utilized the most recent and comprehensive GWAS dataset currently available for the human metabolome, which included 1091 blood metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios of 8299 participants and approximately 15.4 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with 1400 biomarkers from the Canadian Longitudinal Study of Aging cohort.[7]

The various immune cell subtypes were derived from the summary statistics of a GWAS conducted by Orru et al[8] of 731 immune phenotypes, including absolute cell counts (n = 118), median fluorescence intensity (n = 389), morphological parameters (n = 32), and relative cell counts (n = 192), of 3757 individuals from the Sardinian founder population assessed by flow cytometry.

Data for 91 inflammation-related proteins, derived from the summary statistics of a GWAS conducted by Zhao et al[9] of the number trait loci of 14,824 proteins via the Olink Target platform.

Data for the CAC analysis were retrieved from the Open GWAS study published by Kavousi et al,[3] the largest multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis to date, which included 26,909 individuals of European ancestry and 8867 individuals of African ancestry. The details of the genome-wide association studies involved in this Mendelian randomization study are presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q545.

2.3. Filtering of IVs

The forward MR analysis included SNPs that were significantly associated with exposure. To maximize the number of IVs, the probability (P) value was set to 1 × 10‐5.[10–13] To ensure that selected SNPs were relatively independent from each other, the linkage disequilibrium threshold was set at r2 < 0.001 with a window size of 10,000 kb (using the 1000 genomes reference panel). The F statistic for each SNP was beta2/SE2.[14] The strength between the selected IVs and exposure was assessed. In general, F > 10 is considered to reduce bias due to weak instrumentation.[15] Genetic proxies for CAC were set at genome-wide significant levels and independent of each other when CAC was used as an exposure (P < 5 × 10‐8; linkage disequilibrium r2 < 0.001; window size = 10,000 kb).[3]

2.4. MR analysis

A bidirectional, 2-sample univariate MR approach was employed to evaluate the causal impact of the 4 exposure factors on CAC. In order to obtain valid causal relationships, it was necessary that the screened IVs were: strongly associated with the exposure factors; had influenced the outcomes only through exposure; and independent of the confounders (Fig. 1).[16] The inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) approach was employed to assess the causal effect of exposure on outcome. The weighted median, weighted model, MR-Egger, ConMix, and PAPS were employed to assess the robustness of the IVW results under various hypotheses.

2.5. Sensitivity analyses

A range of sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify putative causalities obtained by bidirectional MR. First, heterogeneity within the IVs was assessed using Cochran Q statistic for IVW and MR-Egger analyses. Second, the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier global test was utilized to detect horizontal pleiotropy. Third, MR-Egger regression was used to assess the mean pleiotropic effects of all genetic variants. Evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was indicated when MR-Egger intercepts for all associations differed from zero (P < .05). All analyses were executed using R software (version 4.3.1; https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/old/4.3.1/). The IVW, weighted median, and MR-Egger regression methodologies were implemented through the “TwoSampleMR” software package (version 0.5.7; https://github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR).

3. Results

3.1. Causal relationships of the gut microbiota with CAC

To explore the causal relationship of the gut microbiota with CAC, an in-depth analysis of 207 gut microbiota was conducted using a strict criterion of P < 1 × 10‐5. First, 2 bacterial taxa associated with a reduced risk of CAC were identified, which included Bilophila wadsworthia (P = .0046; odds ratio [OR] = 0.78) and Clostridium asparagiforme (P = .0092; OR = 0.92), while 8 bacterial species were associated with an increased risk of CAC, which included Burkholderiales bacterium_1_1_47 (P = .0003; OR = 1.17) and Ruminococcus callidus (P = .0277; OR = 1.18). Preliminary analyses found no heterogeneity or pleiotropy (P > .05) in the above relationships. Reverse MR analysis was performed to determine whether CAC affects the composition of the gut microbiota. However, the results did not support the existence of bidirectional causality. These findings provide preliminary evidence of a causal relationship of specific gut microbiota with the risk of CAC, thereby improving our understanding of the role of the gut microbiota in CVD, while providing new perspectives for future prevention and treatment strategies. However, larger and more diverse studies are needed to clarify the specific mechanisms employed by these bacteria in CAC. The complete results are provided in Figure 2. The detailed causal relationship results between gut microbiota and CAC are presented in Tables S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q545.

3.2. Causal relationships of circulating metabolites with CAC

In total, 36 metabolites were negatively associated with CAC, which included bilirubin (P = .271; OR = 0.94) and 2-aminobutyrate (P = .0131; OR = 0.85), while 24 metabolites were positively correlated with CAC, including isoleucine (P = .0002; OR = 1.28) and phenylacetate (P = .0007; OR = 1.22). The detailed causal relationship results between circulating metabolites and CAC presented in Tables S4 and S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q545.

3.3. Causal relationships of immune cells with CAC

MR analysis identified 10 types of immune cells negatively correlated with CAC, which included CD4 regulatory T cells (P = .0325; OR = 0.94), and 11 cell types positively associated with CAC, including CD11c + human leukocyte antigen—DR isotype++ monocytes (P = .0091; OR = 1.07) and myeloid dendritic cells (P = .0157; OR = 1.06). Reverse MR analysis to detect reverse causality found no heterogeneity or pleiotropy (P > .05). The complete results are shown in Figure 3. 3.4. Causal relationships of inflammation-related proteins with CAC

In total, 5 types of inflammation-related proteins were negatively correlated with CAC, including AXIN1 (P = .0336; OR = 0.82), while interleukin-10 receptor subunit alpha (P = .0093; OR = 1.23) was associated with increased risk of CAC. However, analysis of heterogeneity indicated that there was heterogeneity for the IVs included in the analysis of fractalkine levels (P < .05, Cochran Q-test for both IVW and MR-Egger). 4. Discussion

CAC is a pathological manifestation of atherosclerosis with a complex multifactorial pathogenesis involving multiple biological processes and molecular events, including abnormal lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells, calcium and phosphorus deposition, mineralization, and vascular remodeling. Limited by the challenges associated with clinical trials, analysis of the impact of risk factors on outcomes through extensive genetic data has emerged as a prominent trend in disease research. As an innovative approach in epidemiology, MR is increasingly recognized as a valuable statistical method across various diseases.

4.1. Gut microbiota and CAC

Dysregulation of the gut microbiota has been implicated in the etiology of CVD, including CAC. Abnormalities in the composition of the gut microbiota can result in an elevated inflammatory response, altered oxidative stress, and abnormal metabolite accumulation, all of which contribute to the development of CAC.[17] The gut microbiota can influence cardiovascular health, including the development of CAC, through a number of different mechanisms.[18] First, the gut microbiota is involved in the digestion and metabolism of food, producing a variety of metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and trimethylamine oxide. A number of studies have demonstrated an association between trimethylamine oxide levels and an elevated risk of CVD, potentially via the promotion of atherosclerosis and calcification.[19] Second, disruption to the intestinal flora may result in impaired intestinal barrier function and increased intestinal permeability, thereby allowing bacterial products and endotoxins to enter the circulation and trigger a systemic inflammatory response.[20–22] Furthermore, the gut microbiota has been demonstrated to significantly influence the development and functionality of the host immune system. An imbalance in the gut microbiota may result in dysregulation of the immune system, which may increase the risk of CVD.[22] Additionally, the gut microbiota affects the absorption and metabolism of nutrients, including lipids, minerals, and glucose, which may consequently impact calcification of the blood vessel wall.[23] The gut microbiota, including members of the genus Bilophila, plays a significant role in the development and progression of atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and atherosclerotic CVD, while members of the genus Ruminococcus are reportedly associated with CVD.[24–26] Members of the order Burkholderiales are present at high levels in atherosclerotic plaque samples.[27] As a promising strategy for treatment of CVD, leveraging big data to understand the specific effects of different bacterial genera has paved the way for targeted interventions aimed at promoting the proportions of beneficial intestinal flora and enhancing the stability of the gut ecosystem.

4.2. Circulating metabolites and CAC

A range of metabolic pathways and metabolites are significantly attenuated following control of conventional CVD risk factors, indicating that the levels and homeostasis of circulating metabolites are closely linked to CVD. The levels and homeostasis of circulating cholesterol and lipid metabolites have been implicated in the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and subsequent CAC.[28] In addition to direct deposition on the arterial wall, some metabolites can directly or indirectly affect the AMPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, which play pivotal roles in regulating vascular smooth muscle cell differentiation and arterial wall growth.[29] Some metabolites have been implicated in oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. Patients with benign hyperbilirubinemia, who may benefit from a slight elevation in bilirubin levels, exhibit a reduced risk of CVD.[30] Conversely, isoleucine has been associated with an increased risk of CVD.[31] In the human liver, the combination of phenylacetate and glutamine produce phenylacetylglutamine, which can mediate cellular responses through G protein-coupled receptors, participate in platelet activation, and promote thrombosis, thereby playing a significant role in the development of CVD.[32]

4.3. Immune cells and CAC

The development of CAC is influenced by the interplay between the immune and inflammatory responses. The involvement of immune cells, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes, underscores the correlation between inflammation and CAD.[1] T cells are activated during plaque deposition and CD8 cells expand with disease progresses via single-cell cloning.[33] Particular T cells undergo distinctive expansion within coronary plaques, which may signify a potential pathway exploited by antigens to facilitate the development of atherosclerosis.[33] CD4 + T cell subsets secrete specific inflammatory mediators that exacerbate inflammation and contribute to apoptosis of endothelial cells, ultimately leading to the formation of arterial plaques.[1] In addition to overproduction of inflammatory factors, CD8 + T cells exhibit cytotoxic properties that further intensify plaque development or rupture.[1] B1a lymphocytes secrete natural IgM, which reduces the necrotic core of atherosclerotic lesions.[34] Activated or memory T cell subsets (such as CD25++ CD45RA + CD4, CD28‐ CD25 ++ CD8+), which are frequently associated with an activated state of the immune response, may potentially play a catalytic role in atherosclerosis and the development of CAC.[35] HLA DR is a T cell subset associated with CAD and HLA DR++ monocytes are usually associated with activation status.[35] The inflammatory response is frequently linked to CVD. Myeloid dendritic cells, which can recognize and present antigens, are expressed in both the normal and atherosclerotic aorta, playing a pivotal role in modulating the progression of inflammation.[36]

4.4. Inflammation-related proteins and CAC

Chronic inflammation plays a crucial role in promoting the development of atherosclerosis and calcification. The inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1β has been demonstrated to damage endothelial cells by increasing the levels of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokines in the arterial intima, thereby exacerbating inflammation of the arterial vessel wall and promoting the development of atherosclerosis.[28] Local inflammation can increase the density of microvessels surrounding the coronary arteries, ultimately resulting in heightened vulnerability.[37] Cardiac fibrosis is a significant pathological process in CVD, often closely associated with alterations in cardiac structure and function. MicroRNAs, such as miR-124-3p, in the plasma exosomes of patients with atrial fibrillation can modulate expression of AXIN1, subsequently influencing activity of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This mechanism plays a crucial role in both atrial fibrillation and fibrosis.[38] Meanwhile, AXIN1 can enhance the antiviral immune response after viral infection.[39] Interleukin-10 receptor subunit alpha has been identified as a risk factor for CAD, consistent with our findings.[40]

The majority of clinical drugs for treatment of CAC concentrate on adjunctive statin therapy. However, there is currently no effective pharmacological treatment to reverse vascular calcification. Conventional surgical options, such as percutaneous coronary intervention, are unsuitable for removal of coronary calcification due to the potential interference of the calcification site with the placement of the traversed device and stent, which may result in an increased incidence of trapping, perforation, and distal embolization.[41] Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is indicated for severe coronary artery stenosis that cannot be controlled by pharmacological or interventional therapy.[42] CABG can achieve significantly greater improvement of angina than optimal pharmacological treatment and percutaneous coronary intervention.[42] However, conventional CABG is associated with increased risks of stroke due to embolus dislodgement during the procedure, postoperative hemorrhage, systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by the bypass device, and myocardial ischemia.[43] Hence, a novel revascularization technique is needed without the necessity of cardiopulmonary bypass to avoid the increased risks of intraoperative myocardial ischemia and myocardial infarction.[43] A number of innovative surgical techniques have been developed in recent years, including rotational atherosclerotic resection, which can effectively reduce calcification. However, as with any surgical procedure, there are potential intraoperative complications, including intraoperative myocardial infarction, entrapment, and perforation.[44] Endovascular lithotripsy is a safe and convenient technique based on the principle of acoustic pressure wave impact for the treatment of severely calcified plaques.[45]

5. Conclusions

Despite the above potential links, the relationships of the gut microbiota, human circulating metabolites, immune cells, inflammation-related proteins with CAC remain controversial. Future research is needed to further explore the impact of these 4 factors on the development of CAC as potential therapeutic targets.

Bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis using genome-wide data was employed to investigate the impacts of 4 risk factors on the development of CAC. The findings indicate that these specific factors can influence the pathogenesis of CAC, thereby offering novel insights for potential diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
