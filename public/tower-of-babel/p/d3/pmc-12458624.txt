IPDMA – RADICAL – NSCLC: individual participant data meta-analysis and systematic review of radical local therapy for oligometastatic NSCLC
Authors: Krishna Tiwari, Muhammad Aaqib Shamim, Isha Yadav, Rakesh Dodiya, Ajay Kumar Kondeti, Naveen Dutt, Parmod Kumar, Sonali Kar, Vikas Tiwari, Pradeep Dwivedi, Surjit Singh, Shoban Babu Varthya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe role of radical local therapy in oligometastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is rapidly evolving and has shown mixed results. We assessed the effect of add-on radical local therapy versus systemic therapy alone on overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS) and safety in oligometastatic NSCLC.MethodsIn this systematic review and individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis, we screened PubMed, Embase, Scopus and CENTRAL until 20 April 2025 for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) answering our research question. We retrieved IPD from survival curves of published reports and used one-stage IPD meta-analysis. We also estimated the more clinically intuitive restricted mean survival time difference (RMSTD). We assessed between-study heterogeneity using the median HR (MHR). We assessed risk of bias using the Risk of Bias 2 tool and rated the evidence certainty using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations framework.FindingsWe screened 1004 records to include ten RCTs (mostly at a low risk of bias) with 752 participants (338 males), predominantly using radiotherapy as radical local therapy. Add-on radical local therapy improved OS by 38% [HR: 0.62, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.76; high certainty of evidence]. Between-trial heterogeneity does not affect the results, only leading to 3% difference [MHR 1.03]. Add-on radical local therapy leads to longer OS by 0.47 month (0.21–0.72), 2.18 months (0.74–3.63), 4.20 months (1.95–6.45) and 6.65 months (4.05–9.24) over 1, 2, 3 and 4 years. Add-on radical local therapy possibly improved PFS by 40% (HR: 0.60, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.80; low certainty of evidence). Radical local therapy was well tolerated with no major safety concerns.InterpretationAdd-on radical local therapy—chiefly radiotherapy—is beneficial in oligometastatic NSCLC. Inconsistent reporting of safety limited quantitative synthesis. Future studies may address the role of surgery as radical local therapy, and the role of programmed death ligand 1 expression.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024576829.Keywords: Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer, Lung Cancer Chemotherapy, Lung Cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading contributor to cancer mortality.1 More than 85% of all lung cancer cases are comprised of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).2 Two-thirds of such cases present with advanced disease.3 Metastatic NSCLC is associated with a very poor prognosis, and local therapy is reserved for palliation.4

However, metastatic disease is not a homogenous entity, and a quarter to half of advanced NSCLC cases are oligometastatic.3 On an apparent continuum ranging from localised well-controlled disease to poly-metastatic widespread disease, there is a distinct place for the oligometastatic disease entity.5 Here, the tumour biology does not have full-blown metastatic virulence.6 This reduces the tumour growth and distant seeding and also makes it more amenable to disease control by radical local treatment.7

In oligometastatic NSCLC, incorporating definitive local therapy along with systemic treatment has demonstrated improved survival outcomes.8 9 Previously, radical treatment primarily involved surgery, but it now encompasses both surgery and radiotherapy.10 Radiotherapy is non-invasive and works in synergy with immunotherapy.11 Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is increasingly becoming the standard approach for managing patients with oligometastatic NSCLC.12

Most of the systematic reviews have included both retrospective studies along with randomised controlled trials (RCTs).13,15 Moreover, they have not included the recent RCTs.8 16 Certainty of evidence is not assessed in most of these systematic reviews. As per our knowledge, there is no individual patient data meta-analysis (IPDMA) yet to show the evidence of add-on radical local therapy in oligometastatic NSCLC. Individual patient data (IPD), as opposed to aggregate data, enhances the quality of the analysis17 and is considered the gold standard for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.18

In time-to-event outcomes, HRs are often used. The precision of HR is influenced by the number of observed events rather than by the duration of patient exposure. Therefore, in trials where event rates are low (as in our condition), achieving precise HR estimates may require large sample sizes. A greater limitation with HR is that in time-to-event outcomes, the impact of interventions fluctuates over time, even when covariates are balanced.19 When relative effect varies with time, the proportional hazard (PH) assumption may be violated, and this is seen in around 19% of the studies.20 Here again, HR is not appropriate.19

Another way to assess treatment effects in time-to-event outcomes is by comparing the difference in restricted mean survival times (RMSTD).21 In the estimation of RMSTD, it is not necessary for the PH assumption to hold true and is reliable in estimating survival difference when the PH assumption is violated.22 Moreover, RMSTD provides a more clinically interpretable summary by estimating the difference in survival time as months, thereby helping patients along with physicians make more informed and responsible decisions in patients with cancer.23 RMSTD is a valuable tool for the design and analysis of clinical studies due to its robustness, interpretability and effectiveness.24

For calculating the more robust and interpretable RMSTD, individual participant data are required, further highlighting the importance of IPDMAs. Therefore, we conducted this IPDMA to provide updated, robust and interpretable estimates on comparison of add-on radical local therapy vs standard therapy alone in oligometastatic NSCLC.

Methods

Study design and reporting

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) of individual participant data (IPD) published in eligible studies. Using IPD instead of aggregate data for SRMA has several advantages,25,27 including assessing survival measures at varying follow-up lengths and studying time-dependent variation in intervention effect.19 We registered this study at PROSPERO (CRD42024576829) and reported the study according to the IPD extension of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-IPD).28

Selection criteria

We assessed the efficacy and safety of add-on radical local therapy in oligometastatic NSCLC. Thus, we included studies based on the following criteria: Population—patients with oligometastatic NSCLC; Intervention—radical local therapy in addition to systemic therapy; Comparator—systemic therapy alone; Primary outcome—overall survival (OS); Secondary outcomes—progression-free survival (PFS) and safety; and Study design—RCTs reporting survival curves. There are several RCTs and therefore, we excluded non-randomised studies of interventions or observational studies.

Search and data extraction

The search strategy included synonyms and MeSH terms for the research question components. It was peer-reviewed by a co-author according to the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies 2015 Guideline (online supplemental appendix). PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and Scopus were searched until 6 August 2024, which was further updated on 20 April 2025. Clinical trial registries and expert opinion were considered to identify related studies. Studies available in languages other than English were not included. IY and RD determined the eligibility of studies, and in case of any conflict, the issue was resolved by AKK.

We used WebPlotDigitizer to plot the co-ordinates from the survival curves published in the eligible studies.29 Next, we used a modified iterative Kaplan-Meier algorithm to reconstruct the IPD using the plotted points.30 This builds on the widely used31,33 method proposed by Guyot et al34 and improves the estimation of censored observations.30 We further optimised it to allow non-uniform censoring by using the extracted co-ordinates to report censoring timepoints. The data extraction spreadsheet contained standard fields pertaining to the research question, and the data were extracted by MAS and KT. It was further validated by ND and PK.

Risk of bias, publication bias, and evidence certainty assessment

Risk of bias was assessed with the Risk of Bias 2 tool,35 by SS and SBV, and any discrepancy was resolved by PD. We did not formally test for publication bias for OS because we had less than 10 studies and used contour-enhanced trim-and-fill funnel plots with Egger’s regression for PFS where we had 10 estimates.18 Certainty in the obtained evidence is assessed as per the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (Core GRADE) framework.36

Statistical analysis

Hazard Ratio (HR)

We performed a one-stage IPDMA instead of a two-stage process as it claims superior convergence.37 38 Moreover, this method adjusts for both within-trial and between-trial heterogeneity in one go.39 We used a random-effects model due to the anticipated clinical heterogeneity.40 41 We used a Cox model with log-normal frailty to adjust for varying baseline hazards between different trials as a result of unmeasured covariates.

Heterogeneity

Different studies may exhibit heterogeneity and clustering due to varying baseline hazards and unmeasured covariates. To estimate the variance, we used the median HR (MHR) which is recommended for multilevel survival (or time-to-event) analysis.42 MHR provides the median of the relative difference in hazards between different trials. Therefore, this expresses the variation in the same measure as the outcome, thereby facilitating comparison with the intervention effect. This aids clinical interpretation of the variance. For example, in our study, the MHR expresses the median change in HR of OS (or PFS) for add-on radical local therapy vs systemic therapy that may be due to the different contexts in the eligible trials.

Restricted Mean Survival Time Difference (RMSTD)

Though HR is routinely used for time-to-event outcomes, it has some limitations.22 43 Instead, RMSTD is touted as an effective alternative.22 43 RMSTD expresses the gain (or loss) in the event-free survival with the intervention compared with the control, at varying lengths of follow-up. Therefore, compared with HR, RMSTD has several advantages including more clinically intuitive interpretation,44 higher statistical efficiency, time-dependent effect estimation and reliability even in case the assumption of proportionality of hazards is violated, as is commonly encountered.45 To ensure the robustness of our results, we used three alternate methods for sensitivity analyses. We have detailed the statistical methods in the supplementary.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding two studies, one which included patients with oligoprogression,16 and another that only included patients with central nervous system (CNS) metastases.46 After this sensitivity analysis for clinical reasons, we conducted another sensitivity analysis for methodological reasons based on different methods of calculating the RMSTD. We performed another sensitivity analysis using the conventional meta-analytic approach of pooling summary estimates, that is, reported HRs.

Patient and public involvement

This systematic review and meta-analysis was guided by clinical expertise from oncology and pulmonary medicine specialists whose day-to-day work and experience with patients helped played a key role in shaping the research questions, selection of outcome measures and interpretation of findings. Although patients were not directly engaged in the research process, the study was designed with a focus on addressing clinically relevant questions that align with patient priorities. The results will be disseminated through clinical and academic channels with the aim of improving patient care and outcomes.

Results

Study characteristics and risk of bias assessment

We retrieved 1004 records and finally included 10 studies (figure 1).89 16 46,52 We excluded these studies for some reasons including single-arm study design, preference trial design and ongoing/incomplete studies. Overall, 752 participants were included, with 380 in the add-on radical local treatment group and 372 in the systemic therapy group. Radical local therapy interventions include a variety of approaches such as SBRT (stereotactic body radiotherapy), traditional hypo-fractionated radiotherapy, microwave ablation49 and surgery. Overall survival (OS)

Add-on radical local therapy improved OS by 38% (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.76; 6 RCTs with 476 participants; high certainty of evidence) (figure 2A, table 2). Between-trial heterogeneity only led to a median of 3% variation in the HR (median HR: 1.03), much less than the estimated 38% reduction in the hazard explained by the intervention (ie, add-on radical local therapy).

Progression-free survival (PFS)

Add-on radical local therapy possibly improved PFS by 40% (HR: 0.60, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.80; 9 RCTs with 685 participants; low certainty of evidence) (figure 2B). Between-trial heterogeneity led to a median of 43% variation in the HR (median HR: 1.43), which is slightly more than the estimated 40% reduction in the hazard explained by the intervention (ie, add-on radical local therapy).

We found evidence for non-proportionality of hazards in several trials (online supplemental appendix), further necessitating RMSTD calculation. It led to longer progression-free survival by 1.83 months (0.98 to 2.68) over 1 year. It led to increase by 3.43 months (1.22 to 5.65) over 2 years and by 4.08 months (0.28 to 7.87) over 3 years (figure 3B). The funnel plot is fairly symmetrical (Egger’s regression: p=0.29) (online supplemental appendix).

Safety

Most of the studies have concluded that radical local therapy is well tolerated with few serious adverse events.9 47 48 Skin rash, pruritus, nausea and pneumonitis occurrences are most common adverse events.8 9 There was no significant difference between radical local therapy and systemic therapy groups in safety terms.8 Few studies like Shan et al found more haemoptysis and pneumothorax with radical local therapy.8 16 49 Gomez et al reported more number of death with systemic therapy.47 Lim et al found more progression to brain metastasis with systemic therapy.46

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding three studies, two which included patients with oligoprogression,16 52 and another that only included patients with CNS metastases.46 The results remained consistent with the estimate for OS changing from HR 0.62 (0.50 to 0.76) to HR 0.47 (0.36 to 0.61). RMSTD for OS at a follow-up period of 4 years changed from 6.65 months (4.05 to 9.24) to 9.79 months (6.53 to 13.06). Similarly, PFS changed from HR 0.60 (0.45 to 0.80) to 0.54 (0.41 to 0.72). The RMSTD for PFS at a follow-up period of 3 years changed from 4.08 months (0.28 to 7.87) to 4.98 months (1.51 to 8.44).

In the primary analysis, RMSTD for OS at a follow-up period of 4 years was estimated at 6.65 months (4.05 to 9.24). The result remained consistent in sensitivity analyses for different methods of pooling the results, ranging from 6.66 months (3.72 to 9.6) to 7.66 months (2.81 to 12.51). The results remained consistent for all the assessed timepoints for both OS and PFS (online supplemental appendix). A conventional meta-analysis using reported HR showed similar estimates with OS changing from HR 0.62 (0.50 to 0.76) to HR 0.63 (0.42 to 0.93), and PFS changing from HR 0.60 (0.45 to 0.80) to 0.51 (0.33 to 0.79). RMSTD could not be computed for the conventional meta-analysis.

Subgroup analysis could not be performed based on intervention type because of diverse intervention subtypes with one study assessing surgery or radiotherapy as required, and other studies assessing varying doses and regimens of radiotherapy.8 48 49 Most of the studies did not furnish data adequately for the different subgroups for our use case. And exploratory analyses were either not done or not reliable due to a smaller number of participants in different categories. This limited our ability to conduct subgroup analysis. However, since some studies did report exploratory analyses, and some used dynamic or stratified randomisation based on other prognostic variables,9 16 47 we have narratively summarised the results of exploratory analyses from these studies in table 1.

Discussion

We found that add-on radical local therapy improved OS by 38% compared with systemic therapy alone in oligometastatic NSCLC. It led to longer OS by a half of a month over 1 year, increasing to 6.65 months over 4 years. It also possibly improved PFS by 40% and led to longer PFS by 1.83 months over 1 year, increasing to 4.08 months over 3 years. There was no major difference in serious adverse events between the two groups.

Radical local therapy for oligometastatic lung cancer commonly involves two modalities, surgery and radiotherapy. However, most studies have used radiotherapy which has historically served as a palliative modality in metastatic NSCLC through diverse immunologic effects in modulation of the tumour microenvironment.53 Radiotherapy is great option for the treatment of oligometastatic disease as it is a non-invasive method delivering ablative doses of external beam radiotherapy to the target tumour while minimising dose to the surrounding normal tissues.54 In patients with oligometastatic NSCLC, ionising radiation also acts by producing greater antigen presentation for better immune system recognition.55 Radical local therapy acts by dual action through increasing the sensitivity of the tumour cells to chemotherapy and arresting the spread of distant metastases.56 Radical local therapy is preferable at the time of progression rather than early on, as it may also inhibit the metastasised tumour burden.

In previous systematic reviews, assessing add-on radical local therapy in oligometastatic NSCLC, OS and PFS has been reported separately for both radical local therapy and systemic therapy.14 15 However, they were not compared between the interventions. And subtracting the estimates in both groups does not provide the exact difference. Moreover, this does not yield the CI, and hence, we cannot judge the precision of difference. We have instead provided the restricted difference in both OS and PFS at several timepoints.

Some systematic reviews did compare the two groups directly. However, they have included both RCTs and retrospective studies.13 15 Li et al found no difference, reporting an imprecise HR of 1.61 (95% CI 0.86 to 3.03).57 In contrast, Petrelli et al observed 56% and 58% reduction in the hazards for OS and PFS.58 Our study aligns with Petrelli et al, showing an improvement in both OS and PFS (38% and 40% respectively). The variation in results compared with previous studies could be explained by the inclusion of both retrospective studies and RCTs. It is reported that RCTs can better assess the survival among cancer patients.59 This previous systematic review included two RCTs.58 Our study, which includes the most recent and maximum number (10) of RCTs, likely reflects this improved credibility of results. Most of the studies have expressed PFS and OS as HR and, as discussed earlier, the PH assumption does not always hold true.14 58 60 61 This was also highlighted in our study as this assumption was violated in at least one trial included to assess OS, and multiple trials included to assess PFS. Thus, HR may not be the appropriate measure to assess intervention effect here, and we decided to use RMSTD instead.

We could assess the survival time benefit (as RMSTD) for OS up to 4 years, and for PFS up to 3 years. The reason is that we prioritised robustness of analysis over misleading data. In the data for PFS, the survival curve shows that only 5% of the initial participants were progression-free at the end of 3 years, and hence, very limited patients contributed additional data for the fourth year. This contrasts with 34% and 17% of the participants (in the experimental and control arms, respectively) surviving by 3 years, and thereby providing additional data for OS at 4 years. Therefore, since the statistical model assumptions were not met for RMSTD of PFS at 4 years, we did not compute this data. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for OS and PFS in oligometastatic NSCLC is not available in the published literature. The only related studies discussed MCID for outcomes like quality of life.62,65 Nevertheless, even minor improvements in critical direct outcomes like survival/mortality are considered important. Hence, an effect of this magnitude—increase in 6 months of overall survival over 4 years, and an increase in 4 months of progression-free survival over 3 years—can be considered to be clinically important.

Most meta-analyses have reported adverse events narratively, as the available data from individual studies are incompatible for pooling, making it difficult to reach a more definitive conclusion.66 67 Previous reviews have shown that radical local therapy is well tolerated.68 Similarly, in our study, adverse events were largely comparable between radical local therapy and standard care, and radical local therapy is well tolerated among most of the patients. However, chemotherapy is linked to a higher rate of tumour progression at the original site and distant spread, due to which patients were switched to radical local therapy group.47 48 This further reinforces our findings, highlighting a more favourable risk-benefit ratio for the radical local therapy group.

Radical local therapy has shown promising role in other oligometastatic cancers too. In prostate cancer, the role of radical local therapy was earlier only in palliative care, but recent studies demonstrate its role in oligometastatic cancer patients. Progression-free survival is also found to be increased significantly with radiotherapy.69 In oligometastatic liver cancer, SBRT has shown an increased local control rate of up to 96%, though there is no definitive evidence on overall survival.70

Our study includes 10 RCTs, much than any other meta-analysis (MA), and did not combine RCTs with retrospective studies. Studies have shown that RMSTD is more robust, clinically interpretable and typically yields more conservative estimates than those derived from HRs, making it a more reliable basis for conclusions.21 There are IPD meta-analyses on various aspects of chemotherapy in NSCLC.71,73 However, no IPD meta-analyses are currently available to explore the role of add-on radical local therapy in oligometastatic NSCLC.

A limitation is that we could not retrieve data on covariates. We had contacted the trial investigators but could not obtain this information. Subgroup analyses could not be performed, but nevertheless, the low median HR shows that between-study heterogeneity was not an issue and the results were favourable regardless. We did not assess OS and PFS at 5 years because statistical assumptions were not met due to less evidence at these timepoints. Hence, we preferred to avoid misestimation and reported until the 3–4-year timepoint where the statistical model assumptions were met. Further, some advantages of IPD like including covariates could not be incorporated because we could not obtain the data from the trial investigators. Nevertheless, other advantages, like more precise and interpretable estimates, were obtained. We complemented the IPD approach with a standard MA on reported HR. Formal tests for publication bias could not be conducted, but we did not find any evidence of reporting bias.

Although radical local therapy showed significant change in overall and progression-free survival, most of the studies are about radiotherapy even though radiotherapy was introduced later in local therapy. Surgery remains an area for further exploration in oligometastatic NSCLC studies, as there is limited evidence supporting its role in radical local therapy, with most evidence coming from early stage NSCLC.74 75 Additionally, studies have indicated that patients in the programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) negative group benefit more from radical local therapy, suggesting that future RCTs should focus on PD-L1 status to further investigate this potential covariate, opening the scope for personalised therapy. Moreover, complete results from ongoing studies may further improve the certainty in the evidence.76

This systematic review and individual participant data meta-analysis shows add-on radical local therapy—chiefly radiotherapy with ablative techniques—improves overall survival and possibly improves progression-free survival compared with systemic therapy alone, without major safety concerns. Future studies may address the treatment outcomes of these local therapies in combination with PD-L1 expression-based therapies. Future research could investigate the role of surgery as a form of radical local therapy and compare it with radiotherapy to provide clearer evidence for selecting the most appropriate radical local therapy.
