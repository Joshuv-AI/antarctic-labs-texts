Exploring predictors of interaction among low-birth-weight infants and their caregivers: a machine learning–based random forest approach
Authors: Qihui Wang, Wenying Gao, Yi Duan, Zijin Ren, Ying Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuality caregiver-infant interaction is crucial for infant growth, health, and development. Traditional methods for evaluating the quality of caregiver-infant interaction have predominantly relied on rating scales or observational techniques. However, rating scales are prone to inaccuracies, while observational techniques are resource-intensive. The utilization of easily collected medical records in conjunction with machine learning techniques offers a promising and viable strategy for accurate and efficient assessment of caregiver-infant interaction quality.MethodsThis study was conducted at a follow-up outpatient clinic at two tertiary maternal and infant health centers located in Shanghai, China. 68 caregivers and their 3-15-month-old infants were videotaped for 3–5 min during playing interactions in non-threatening environment. Two trained experts utilized the Infant CARE-Index (ICI) procedure to assess whether the caregivers were sensitive or not in a dyadic context. This served as the gold standard. Predictors were collected through Health Information Systems (HIS) and questionnaires, which included accessible features such as demographic information, parental coping ability, infant neuropsychological development, maternal depression, parent-infant interaction, and infant temperament. Four classification models with fivefold cross-validation and grid search hyperparameter tuning techniques were employed to yield prediction metrics. Interpretable analyses were conducted to explain the results.ResultsThe score of sensitive caregiver-infant interaction was 6.34 ± 2.62. The Random Forest model gave the best accuracy (83.85%±6.93%). Convergent findings identified infant age, care skills of infants, mother age, infant temperament-regulatory capacity, birth weight, positive coping, health-care-knowledge-of-infants, type of caregiver, MABIS-bonding issues, ASQ-Fine Motor as the strongest predictors of interaction sensitivity between infants and their caregiver.ConclusionsThe proposed method presents a promising and efficient approach that synergistically combines rating scales and artificial technology to detect important features of caregiver-infant interactions. This novel approach holds several implications for the development of automatic computational assessment tools in the field of nursing studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05080-8.

Background

Caregiver-infant interaction refers to the reciprocal relationship and communication between a caregiver and infant, including verbal and nonverbal cues, physical contact, and emotional expression. This interaction is critical for the infant’s development, as it provides the foundation for the infant’s learning, behaviors, and physical and mental health [1]. Sensitive caregiver-infant interactions are characterized by the caregiver’s ability to perceive and appropriately respond to an infant’s cues in a timely manner. They include talking and singing to the infant, playing games, and providing physical comfort when the infant is upset. Less sensitive interactions, on the other hand, can involve harsh or neglectful behaviors, such as ignoring the infant’s cries or responding in an angry or dismissive manner [2]. These interactions are not isolated behaviors but are influenced by a complex interplay of factors including infant characteristics, parental behaviors, relationship dynamics, and broader psychosocial conditions.

Parents may struggle to detect and respond to their infant’s cues, especially when those cues are not clearly exhibited. This challenge is most pronounced in the case of very low-birth-weight infants (< 1500 g; VLBW), whose biological immaturity often leads to reduced activity and responsiveness, making it difficult for caregivers to interpret and address their needs [3]. Consequently, both low-birth-weight infants (< 2500 g; LBW) and VLBW infants may receive less sensitive and more intrusive caregiving from parents, who are often highly stressed [4]. Parents reported more difficulty in engaging with their high-risk infants, attributing to the complexities of interpreting their subtle and atypical behaviors [5].

The Centre of Perinatal Excellence (COPE) reported that influencing factors in caregiver-infant interaction include infant factors, maternal factors, relationship factors, and psychosocial risk factors [6]. According to a systematic review that included 28 papers [7], maternal factors include maternal age, maternal education, mode of delivery, and maternal perception of how they were parented. Many of these factors also hold for father [8]. Infant factors include birth age, sex, and infant physiological development factors. Relationship factors included the mother-infant relationship. Psychosocial factors include those like Socioeconomic status [9] and postpartum depression. Most of these factors can be collected by the Hospital Information System (HIS). Other factors, such as parents coping ability, postpartum depression, caregiver-infant relationship, infant temperament, and infant neuropsychological development, can be collected through instruments such as questionnaires and scales.

The main assessment tools for caregiver-infant interaction include parent-report measures, direct observation, and video-based assessments [10]. The video observation method is a standardised assessment tool for parent-infant interaction. Video-based assessments involve recording caregiver-infant interactions and then coding the videos using a standardized system developed by experts.

Despite their usefulness, these assessment tools also have limitations. Parent-reported measures may be subject to biases and may not accurately reflect the true nature of the caregiver-infant relationship. Direct observation by caregiver-infant interaction experts is more objective, but they can be time-consuming and may not fully capture the complexity of caregiver-infant interactions in everyday life. Additionally, the use of video-based assessments requires specialized equipment and training to administer and interpret the results, which is time-consuming and laborious. Compared with video data, questionnaire and scale data offer the advantage of saving time and manpower in assessments, making them suitable for large-scale screening. However, they also have drawbacks, such as being more susceptible to confounding factors. Therefore, it is helpful to create a prediction model that uses medical records and rating scales combined with machine learning. This model could then predict results obtained from observation tools, potentially replacing video assessment and facilitating large-scale screening. Moreover, it offers the added benefit of being more objective than parent-reported measures, enhancing the accuracy of parent-infant interaction risk assessment.

Some research groups have implemented machine learning-based prediction models for caregiver-infant interaction. LECLèRE [11] in 2016 and Paraskevi [12] in 2022 have both explored this area using video analysis. LECLèRE reported a mother-infant interaction measurement tool using video-based 3D reconstruction, extracting 2D + 3D motion features from interaction videos and employing a support vector machine to classify interactions, showing promising results. Similarly, Paraskevi proposed a computer-based micro-interaction analysis program, which also utilized interaction videos alongside mental functioning questionnaires. However, both studies faced significant challenges. The 3D reconstruction technique used by LECLèRE may cause social anxiety when used in unfamiliar environments due to the invasive nature of RGB-D camera data acquisition. Meanwhile, Paraskevi’s behavior code-based system, though innovative, has shown considerably less accuracy compared to expert observational techniques [2]. To address these limitations and enhance the accuracy of interaction assessments, several measures could be considered. Recording interaction videos in non-threatening environments like the home-setting, using easily accessible devices such as cellphones, and adhering to expert observational techniques as the gold standard can mitigate these issues. Additionally, incorporating more clinical measurements relevant to mother-infant interactions and utilizing robust machine learning models like Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine, and Random Forest may further improve prediction models.

This study aimed to construct a predictive model for caregiver-infant interaction quality assessment, utilizing feature variables that can be collected at 3–15 months of corrected age from rating scales and the HIS.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used a video dataset of caregiver-infant interaction from a one-year follow-up project with a prospective longitudinal design. The aim of the longitudinal study was to explore the synergistic effects of caregiver-infant interaction and infant temperament on the growth and development of low-birth-weight infants discharged from neonatal intensive care units (NICU). Data were collected at two tertiary maternal and infant health centers located in Shanghai, China.

This study included low-birth-weight infants (birth weight < 2500 g; birth age < 37 weeks gestation) and their caregivers between November 2021 and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: Low-birth-weight infants discharged from the NICU; caregivers are native speakers of Chinese; willingness to participate in this study; and ability to cooperate well. The exclusion criteria were as follows: caregivers with language disorders and audiovisual disorders; low-birth-weight infants with severe congenital diseases or malformations; those who have incomplete basic information in the in-hospital information system and cannot be contacted. We ensured the protection of patient privacy by obtaining informed consent from the primary caregiver and maintaining the confidentiality of questionnaire responses and video interaction quality throughout the study.

Measures and instruments

Sociodemographic and clinical measures

Perinatal data related to low-birth-weight infants were collected from HIS, including birth age, birth weight, sex, twin, heart disease, and length of stay in the NICU. Maternal perinatal and sociodemographic variables, such as maternal age, educational background, and delivery mode, were also collected from the medical records. Monthly household income and family housing area were collected by a consultant.

Caregiver-infant interaction

Caregiver-infant interaction was measured by the Infant CARE-Index (ICI), which was constructed by Crittenden in 1981 and updated in 2005 [13]. Caregiver-infant interactions from birth to approximately two years of age are assessed based on a brief, 3 to 5-minute videotaped play interaction. The ICI assesses caregivers’ sensitivity, control, and unresponsiveness on a 0–14 scale by looking at seven aspects of interaction behavior, such as facial expression, vocal expression, position, emotional expression, rhythm of communication, control of activities, and developmental appropriateness of activities. We classified the video data into two groups based on their sensitivity scores: the insensitive group (score < 7) and the sensitive group (score ≥ 7). On the Adult Sensitivity Scale, a subscale of the ICI, a score of 5–6 indicates a need for basic parenting guidance aimed at improving general caregiving skills. A score of 3–4 suggests a more substantial need for parental intervention, which involves targeted support to address specific deficits in parent-child interactions. Lastly, a score of 0–2 indicates a critical situation where psychotherapy with the parent is recommended, focusing on deep-seated issues affecting the parent’s ability to provide care. The Adult Sensitivity Scale offers various benefits that professionals can utilize in different environments, and it is suitable for a wide range of ages. Additionally, the scale demonstrates reliable inter-rater agreement, which generally averages around 83% but can exceed this value under optimal conditions.

To ensure the authenticity of the interaction, the location was chosen to be the home of the low-birth-weight infants. Before the video shooting, one person within the subject group was professionally trained in the ICI. We used a uniform instructional language to inform the parents of the purpose, significance, and process of this study, clarify the position and attitude, and gain their trust. The parents signed an informed consent form. Afterwards, the first author instructed the mothers to complete a 3 to 5-minute interactive video filmed at home, with the filming device being a smartphone. The prescribed picture was filmed horizontally, at a distance where the movements and faces of caregiver-infant dyads could be seen. The interactive content was daily play activities between the mother and infant at home. The video data were collected in 3–6, 6–9, 9–12, and 12–15 months of corrected age. A total of 68 videos were collected in total. In order to avoid influencing the results of the prospective longitudinal study, the results of the interaction video evaluation were provided at the end of the study.

Two qualified researchers with reliable licenses independently evaluated the quality of caregiver-infant interactions in a binary context using the ICI video assessment tool. The researchers took approximately 15 to 20 min to code each video. If there were inconsistent results between these two researchers the third researcher from the International Association for the Study of Attachment (IASA) determined the final evaluation. 35% of the videos were assessed by two experts, with a 90% agreement between the scores of both experts.

Parents coping ability

Caregivers completed the coping ability questionnaire for discharged preterm infants [14], which includes 40 items and 8 dimensions (Care skills, parental role adaptation, parent-infant relationship, family functioning, positive coping, negative coping, external support, and knowledge of health care for infants). Each item’s score is put together, and higher scores indicate better coping capacity. Cronbach’s α for the whole questionnaire was 0.916, and it was 0.724 ∼ 0.906 for each dimension.

Maternal postpartum depression

Mothers estimated their level of depression symptoms by completing the Chinese version of the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale (EPDS) [15], which consists of ten items rated using a Likert response format (0 = never 3 = always). Higher EPDS scores imply more severe postpartum depressive symptoms when the scores for each item are put together. Generally, a cutoff score of 10 is used to screen for symptoms of postpartum depression. The EPDS had a high reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s = 0.89).

Mother-infant relationship

Mothers filled in the Mother and Baby Interaction Scale (MABIS) to measure the mother-infant relationship, which contains 10 items. It evaluates four dimensions: bonding issues, worries about caretaking, regulation and routine, and sensitivity and separation. This self-reported five-point Likert scale was developed by Marilyn Hackney [16] in 1996. The scale was translated into Chinese and cross-culturally adapted by the whole study group which includes two candidates for a master of science in nursing, three clinical nursing specialists, a professor studying pediatric nursing, and a lecturer of psychology. The Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.76.

Infant temperament

Caregiver reported infant temperament by using the 37-item Infant Behavior Questionnaire-Revised Very Short Form (IBQ-R VSF). Three temperament domains are called surgency (high impulsivity, intense pleasure and novelty seeking, high activity level), negative emotionality (mood instability, anger, dysregulated negative emotions), and regulatory capacity (ability to maintain attention, resist distraction, and refrain from a behavior as a situation necessitates). On a seven-point Likert scale (1 = never ∼ 7 = always), caregivers were asked to rate how frequently their infant engaged in certain behaviors over the course of the previous week. If the caregivers did not see the infant in the situation, they might select “not applicable”. The range of Cronbach’s α for each dimension was 0.60 ∼ 0.90, with a mean Cronbach’s α of 0.75 for the three dimensions [17].

Neuropsychological development

Caregiver provided Infants’ neuropsychological development indicators by answering the Ages and Stages Questionnaire-Chinese Edition (ASQ-C), which consists of 30 items covering five domains: Communication (CM), Gross Motor (GM), Fine Motor (FM), Problem Solving (CG), and Personal-Social (PS). In the questionnaires, each “yes” response was worth 10 points, while “sometimes” and “not yet” were worth 5 and 0 points, respectively. The sum scores for each domain were compared to the national normative cutoff threshold for China. The ASQ-C provided the opportunity to conduct large-scale studies related to child neurodevelopment in China [18], and the scale had a Cronbach’s α of 0.8 in Chinese infants and a sensitivity of 87.5% and a specificity of 84.4%.

Statistical analyses

For comparing the predictive variables between sensitive interaction and insensitive interaction groups, the collected data were analyzed using descriptive statistics with IBM SPSS Version 26.0. The machine learning algorithm was implemented in Python 3.9.12 (Python Software Foundation, USA) with Scikit-learn (Packt Publishing, UK). The time required for model training was less than 2 min using the CPU (i9-13900 K).

Machine learning algorithm

Our goal was to develop a machine learning model that uses easily collected structured data to efficiently and accurately assess the quality of mother-infant interactions and provide interpretable analyses. This technology has the potential to become an AI-driven tool for intelligent assessment of mother-infant interactions in the future.

For model construction, we employed four machine learning classifiers, including logistic regression, decision tree, support vector machine, and random forest. These models are known for their interpretability and extensive use in the medical field. We first performed data cleaning to ensure high data quality, involving steps such as data encoding, outlier removal, and data normalization. We then conducted fivefold cross-validation for each classifier, using grid search to find the optimal parameters. Random forest demonstrated the best performance, so we performed a detailed interpretability analysis on this model, including Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) feature importance analysis and visualization of the decision process. This may aid in practical understanding.

Development of the machine learning model

The exploratory data analysis indicated the need for complex feature combinations and modeling for interaction quality prediction. The correlation coefficient heatmap is shown in Supplementary file 1. All features had a moderate or weak correlation to the interaction sensitivity level, indicating that no single rating scale or physiological measurement was sufficient to explain interaction sensitivity.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used for data compression and visualization by finding the principal components of the data. Here, we performed PCA and extracted the first two dimensions, which helped reduce data dimensionality while retaining important information. This visualization allowed us to gain insights into the classification difficulty level in this study, which could guide our selection of appropriate classification models. The PCA visualization is shown in Supplementary file 2, where we found that there was no clear and simple classification boundary between the two groups. This suggested that simpler classification models, like linear regression or logistic regression, might not be suitable in this case.

Firstly, during the data cleaning process, we performed data normalization on all continuous variables to ensure uniform scaling (0 to 1). For categorical variables, we encoded them from 0 to N-1, where N represents the number of categories. The interaction sensitivity and insensitivity classes were relatively balanced, so we did not apply any data imbalance processing. To fully utilize the dataset, we applied 5-fold cross-validation on all 68 data points to train the model. To identify the best hyperparameters and prevent model overfitting, we conducted a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. The final hyperparameters were determined by selecting the ones that yielded the highest accuracy value. The detailed hyperparameter search interval can be found in our open-source code provided in the Supplementary file 3 materials.

The evaluation metrics used included accuracy, F1 score, precision, recall, and Area Under the Curve (AUC). The model that achieved the best accuracy was selected as the final models through a combination of five-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. Subsequently, we made predictions on the whole dataset and plotted the AUC curve and classification confusion matrix, respectively. To estimate the contribution of each feature variable to the predictive capacity, we calculated SHAP feature importance based on Gini impurity and sorted them in descending order.

Comparison of different models

We compared four different classifiers (Table 2). Using 5-fold cross-validation, each performance metric (including accuracy, AUC, F1 score, precision, and recall) was output five times. Table 2 shows the mean and standard deviation for each metric. The Random Forest classifier achieved a high average accuracy (83.85%±6.93%). This is consistent with our data exploration findings, indicating that the data lacks clear linear separable boundaries, and more complex models may be more suitable. Discussion

Our study explored the predictors of sensitive caregiver-infant interactions, employing machine learning techniques for their assessment. After conducting a descriptive analysis, we implemented four classification models with fivefold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. The Random Forest model outperformed the others, achieving an average accuracy of 83.85% ± 6.93% and an AUC of 97.61%, underscoring its effectiveness in predicting interaction sensitivity. The key findings of the study are as follows: (1) the mean sensitivity scale score for caregivers of low-birth-weight infants was 6.34 ± 2.62; (2) caregiver factors—such as caregiving skills, healthcare knowledge, maternal age, positive coping, and bonding issues—had a strong influence on interaction quality; (3) fathers exhibited higher sensitivity compared to mothers, while grandparents demonstrated lower sensitivity, with variations observed across different caregiver types; and (4) infant-related factors, including age, temperament, birth weight, and motor skills, significantly impacted interaction quality.

The results show that the mean sensitivity scale score of caregivers for low-birth-weight infants was 6.34 ± 2.62, a level comparable to those observed in similar studies where mothers were induced to ruminate under dysphoric or non-dysphoric conditions following the still-face situation [19]. This finding suggests that the interaction sensitivity level of caregivers for low-birth-weight infants may be concerning, highlighting the need to understand and promote positive interactions between caregivers and these infants. Four classification models were implemented using fivefold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. Among them, the Random Forest (RF) model demonstrated the best performance in predicting interaction sensitivity, with an average accuracy of 83.85% ± 6.93%. Additionally, ROC curve analysis further supported the model’s high predictive performance, with an AUC value of 97.61%. Random Forest, as a predictive tool, possesses significant advantages and potential. Firstly, the Random Forest model serves as an effective approach for complex data classification tasks. It is a non-linear modeling tool that exhibits excellent robustness when handling various types of variables, such as continuous, count, and ordinal variables. Secondly, the Random Forest algorithm is capable of considering multiple dimensions and diverse influencing factors comprehensively. It provides importance scores for these factors, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy. Additionally, the Random Forest model can identify high-risk populations in conventional survey questionnaires, potentially enabling early detection, recognition, and intervention without imposing additional financial and time burdens on caregivers. This can be achieved through the simple administration of questionnaires to identify individuals at high risk.

The Random Forest model identified the top 10 features with SHAP value that influence caregiver-infant interaction sensitivity levels. Among them, caregiver factors such as care skills of infants, health-care-knowledge-of-infants, maternal / mother’s age, positive coping, and bonding issues were found to strongly impact the quality of interactions. Caregivers with knowledge and skills in early healthcare and daily caregiving were better able to meet the needs of infants and provide a stable environment [20, 21]. Additionally, maternal age also played a role, with older mothers often better equipped to handle the challenges of parenting [22]. Then, Positive coping means that caregivers are confident in dealing with many difficult aspects of caring. Evidence from various studies suggests that positive coping strategies can enhance parenting adaptation and role transition, while also increasing parental warmth and responsiveness [23–25]. Furthermore, positive parent-infant bonding fostered emotional support and security, enhancing interactions [26, 27].

The type of caregiver also played a role in the quality of caregiver-infant interaction. Fathers exhibited higher levels of interaction sensitivity compared to mothers, engaging in more exploratory and playful activities with their infants [28]. Grandparent caregivers, on the other hand, demonstrated lower levels of interaction sensitivity, potentially related to age, health conditions, and differences in infant-rearing beliefs [29]. It should be noted that the context-specific nature of caregiver sensitivity and supportiveness may vary across different caregiver types and should be assessed accordingly. Additionally, recent research suggests that increasing grandparents’ sensitivity can enhance the positive impact within the family environment [30].

In addition to caregiver factors, the study also identified several infant-related factors that influence the quality of caregiver-infant interactions. These include infant age, temperament-regulatory capacity, birth weight, and ASQ-Fine Motor. Infant age can influence caregiver sensitivity, which tends to increase as the infant becomes more capable of expressing their needs. A study shows that mothers and infants become increasingly coordinated in their gaze behaviors as the infants grow older [31]. In terms of infant temperament, regulatory capacity was found to significantly impact caregiver-infant interactions. Similar studies have also demonstrated a reciprocal relationship between parental behavior and infant regulation [32, 33]. Interestingly, the quality of interactions improves as birth weight decreases. This could be because the average weight of LBW infants in this study is around 2000 g, which is relatively stable compared to extremely low birth weight (ELBW) infants [34]. Infants with relatively higher birth weights are more likely to be primarily cared for by grandparents, whereas smaller LBW infants are usually the primary responsibility of their parents, who provide more focused attention and systematic, phased interactions [35]. And if the infant is more fragile, doctors would ask the family for more follow-up visits. Furthermore, infants with lower ASQ-Fine Motor scores may experience developmental delays that hinder their ability to send appropriate signals to caregivers. Consequently, caregivers might overlook these signals, leading to lower sensitive interaction scores [1, 36].

Understanding these factors is crucial for designing targeted interventions. Providing comprehensive education programs during pregnancy and postpartum can significantly enhance caregivers’ skills in early healthcare and daily caregiving. For example, self-compiled CDs and manuals for first-time mothers can increase their confidence and competence in managing parenting challenges [37, 38]. Additionally, platforms for caregiver communication and support can help alleviate stress and loneliness [39]. Providing feedback and guidance on emotional connection during daily interactions can improve caregiver-infant interactions [40, 41]. Interventions targeting infant and environmental factors are also essential. Early interventions on motor development through the use of toys can be beneficial [42] and caregiver-mediated coregulation supports infants in responding adaptively to their environment [43].

The identification of significant predictors, such as Care of Infant Skills, Health-care-knowledge-of-infants, infant age, and maternal age, emphasizes the multidimensional nature of caregiver-infant interactions. These findings underscore the importance of considering various factors when assessing and understanding the quality of caregiver-infant interactions. The utilization of machine learning techniques, particularly the Random Forest algorithm, offers advantages in terms of accuracy, interpretability, and scalability. The ability to analyze medical record data, integrate it with rating scales, and provide efficient assessment methods holds great promise for the field of caregiver-infant interaction.

Furthermore, this study highlights the strengths and limitations of the research. Our innovative approach involves a research design that ensures the collection of authentic caregiver-infant interaction data within a home environment. The use of the well-established video observation tool, the ICI, as the gold standard for data classification significantly enhances the validity of our findings. Additionally, we employ powerful machine learning models to achieve interpretable results. However, our current model development phase includes only maternal sociodemographic variables. It will be essential to incorporate data from other caregivers, such as fathers’ depression and relationship scores, and to expand the Parents Coping Ability questionnaire to include more data from grandparents and fathers. The relatively small sample size and the specific setting in which the data were collected may also limit the generalizability of the results. Future research should aim to replicate the findings with larger and more diverse samples. In this study, we did not strictly define the primary caregiver as the individual who spent the most time with the infant. Instead, we included those who were actively involved in infant care and occupied most of the day or night as primary caregivers. Future research could benefit from further refining the definition of primary caregivers to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the proposed methodology combining rating scales, accessible features, and the Random Forest algorithm presents a promising approach to assessing and understanding caregiver-infant interactions. The findings from this study have implications for improving early screening, intervention, and the development of automatic computational assessment tools in the field of caregiver-infant interaction. Future research should focus on replication studies, validation in diverse populations, and the integration of technology to enhance the scalability and accessibility of the assessment process.
