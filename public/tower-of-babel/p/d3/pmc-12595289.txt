Computer‐Aided Engineering Process for the Development of a Bread Baking Biomass Oven
Authors: Delmer Gómez‐Heleria, Antonio Pola, José Núñez, Carlos Alberto García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of this study was to design and assemble an efficient oven for bread baking in rural communities, where access to conventional energy sources is limited or costly. The technological development of the prototype was supported by computer‐aided design (CAD), computer‐aided manufacturing (CAM), computational fluid dynamics (CFD), and finite element analysis (FEA), enabling an approach that aligns with the specific needs of rural contexts. The design features a combustion chamber and a cooking chamber optimized for even heat distribution. Results showed that the assembled oven achieved suitable baking temperatures across different fire power levels. Tests demonstrated good heat retention and uniform temperature distribution, validating the proposed design. Based on the temperature distribution measured in the baking chamber, the proposed technology can maintain temperatures below 473.15 K, unlike traditional ovens that reach values above 700 K. At the chimney exit, the oven exhibited an oxygen concentration close to zero, in contrast to the significantly higher levels observed in conventional brick bread ovens, indicating that the proposed design achieves more complete combustion. The results confirm that this biomass oven could offer a more efficient technology for rural areas.Keywords: biomass oven, bread baking, combustion, CFD, FEA

Introduction

Baking is a widely practiced cooking method that involves using heat to transform raw ingredients into food. Ovens play a very important role in the cooking process (Chhanwal et al. 2012). Understanding the interactions between heat transfer, moisture release, and chemical reactions is crucial for achieving uniform and high‐quality baking results. The literature indicates that simulations enhance our understanding of the thermal behavior of these devices.

Gas‐fired ovens for the baking process have been studied. According to Khatir et al. (2012), the use of CFD models enables the prediction of flow and temperature fields within an oven, making it possible to analyze how velocity depends on the behavior of the incoming jets. Yi et al. (2017) developed a CFD model to evaluate the thermal efficiency of a conveyor belt oven used in continuous product manufacturing. The simulations accurately predicted the maximum conveyor belt speed and informed design improvements, such as lowering the nozzle height to reduce the product cycle time. In a similar study, Zhou and Therdthai (2018) investigated the impact of the load on the airflow and temperature distribution, and also evaluated key bread quality parameters. In another study, Wang et al. (2023) found that the flame diameter plays a significant role in reducing cooking time. In recent times, Mansour et al. (2024) calculated the heat transfer coefficients affecting the bread in a tunnel‐type oven.

Certain studies examine (Chhanwal et al. 2010; Pinelli and Suman 2017; Reddy et al. 2023) the thermal performance of electric baking ovens. Electric ovens typically have simple geometries, such as cubic or cylindrical shapes. Efforts have focused on improving the heat distribution, with CFD simulations, in these devices to enhance bread baking. In this context, Chhanwal et al. (2011, 2012) proposed an oven design with heating coils positioned beneath the bread trays. In another study, Ciarmiello and Morrone (2016) reported an oven with a heating bed, featuring a geometry like that of a conventional wood oven. Subsequently, the effect of forced air convection in a rotary bread oven was investigated (Pinelli and Suman 2017). In this regard, Park et al. (2018) examined the effect of adding holes near the fan to enhance forced convection. The study found that the oven's thermal performance improved when the holes were positioned either at the top left and bottom right sides or at the top right and bottom left sides. Recently, Reddy et al. (2023) determined that using convex dome heat sources, compared to U‐shaped heat sources, enhances baking temperatures, resulting in better browning of the bread.

In the field of biomass ovens research, Manhiça et al. (2012a, 2012b) proposed a furnace with two chambers: a combustion chamber and a baking chamber. Their study demonstrated that incorporating forced convection in the combustion chamber reduces firewood consumption compared to natural convection. Numerical simulations offer valuable insights into the contributions of various heat transfer mechanisms in different geometries of traditional biomass ovens made of bricks, cement, stone, and mud (Sadripour et al. 2018 and Falciano et al. 2022). However, solar technology has also been applied to bread baking. In fact, Aragaw and Adem (2022) designed a parabolic solar collector for this purpose.

In the literature, most studies have focused on electric and gas‐fired ovens, with few papers investigating biomass ovens. The aim of this work is to contribute to the design of biomass ovens with simulation strategies. This work focuses on presenting design strategies for ovens that account for internal volumes, corresponding to a biomass oven with a combustion chamber and a baking chamber. Both chambers are separated by a solid plate supported by legs. The study includes fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations to analyze the thermal behavior of the oven, finite element analysis (FEA) to assess the casing supports, and stability tests to evaluate the structural integrity.

Methodology

The methodology of this study outlines a four‐stage process for the development and implementation of a biomass oven system. It begins with identifying user needs through site visits, surveys, and data filtering to extract relevant information. This is followed by the computer‐aided engineering (CAE) phase, which includes convection and combustion CFD simulations, FEA structural analysis, and data interpretation to inform design improvements. The third stage focuses on assembly, starting with a 3D CAD model, followed by 3D printing and the construction of a full‐scale prototype. The final stage involves on‐site implementation, including site preparation, oven installation, user training, and experimental validation of the baking chamber under real‐world conditions (see Figure 1).

Identification of the Population's Needs

In rural communities across Mexico, bread is traditionally baked in wood‐fired ovens made of stone, brick, cement or mud. These ovens lack controlled combustion, causing soot from the burning firewood to accumulate on the oven's outer walls and the walls of nearby homes. This build‐up is a clear sign of potential health risks. In the rural community of Pichátaro, Michoacán (Figure 2a), a survey found that 16 families bake bread. Of these, only two use LP gas ovens, while the rest rely on traditional wood‐fired ovens (Figure 2b). To address this energy demand, it is essential to develop biomass‐based technology that fosters local economic growth while reducing health risks for those using these ovens (Núñez‐Montiel et al. 2024). In this context, the present study focused on developing a biomass stove using CAE tools.

Geometry Creation

For the design of an oven, similar methodologies used in other technologies (cook stoves) were followed (Gómez Heleria et al. 2024, 2023). This process involves proposing a fluid volume and a solid volume that represent the internal volume of the technology. Figure 3a shows the internal volume composed of a gas mixture volume corresponding to the combustion chamber, an air volume corresponding to the baking chamber, and a solid volume that separates both areas. The combustion chamber has a trapezoidal tube in which the firewood is supplied, and air enters for combustion. It also has a 0.865‐meter chimney where the gases exit. To maintain the uniform air temperature inside the baking chamber, the front wall (where the door is located) and the left wall (where the air inlets and outlets are positioned) do not come into contact with the combustion chamber. Additionally, two rectangular ducts were incorporated into the baking chamber to ensure proper air mixing. To maintain a temperature below 473.15 K in the baking chamber and prevent the generation of carcinogenic substances in bread (Ahrné et al. 2007; Friedman 2015; Nguyen et al. 2016), the angles of the ducts, located at the baking chamber, were varied from 30° to 90° relative to the left wall, resulting in a total of four cases (Figure 3b). The baking chamber with ducts inclined at 30° relative to the left wall of the zy‐plane is referred to as Case 1 (C1). The configuration with a 45° inclination is designated as Case 2 (C2), while Case 3 (C3) has a 60°inclination, and Case 4 (C4) features a 90° inclination.

(a) Internal volume of bread baking oven and (b) study cases for the different injection angles in the baking chamber.

A computer‐assisted design was created with Autodesk Inventor 2023 (Figure 4). The CAD design consists of: (1) a structural support with legs that holds all the components, (2) an external casing that insulates the chambers with a metal wall and a ceramic fiber wall, (3) a door with a thermometer, 4. a profile arrangement to support the door, (5) a primary box for loading the chambers (combustion and cooking), and (6) a secondary box containing the baking chamber, with a capacity of 10 commercial trays of 65 cm × 45 cm. This capacity allows for the baking of 20 breads, each measuring 25 cm × 30 cm.

Isometric views: (a) completed and (b) without external casing.

CFD Model Assumptions and Solver Settings

The forced convection test was conducted considering that hot air at 1073.15 K enters the combustion chamber (primary inlet), and a stagnation pressure of zero was assumed for the exit at the chimney (primary outlet). A convective heat transfer coefficient of 15 W/m2 K was considered for the chimney (Galindo et al. 2025; Prapas 2013). Other solid walls did not have energy losses and were non‐slip. For air entering and exiting the baking chamber (secondary inlet and outlet), inlet and outlet pressures were considered, respectively. To model the operating conditions of the biomass oven, boundary conditions were imposed to solve the main transport equations. Since the oven is covered with multiple layers of metal and insulation, all solid surfaces were assumed to be adiabatic with no‐slip. For the numerical combustion analysis, an oxygen mass fraction of 0.23 was specified as the inlet condition for the primary inlet of the combustion chamber. (Kalla et al. 2015) were imposed. Wood volatiles at 1073.15 K (Kumar et al. 2013), with a mass flow rate of 1.498 × 10−4 kg/s (Borraz et al. 2022; Kalla et al. 2015), were supplied to the injector. For the numerical solution of the equations, numerical schemes were employed. The coupling between pressure and velocity was carried out using the SIMPLE scheme. To properly model the changes in density as a function of temperature, weighted body forces were applied. Turbulence was modeled using the realizable k ‐ ϵ model. The finite rate model was used to solve the species equation. Sudden temperature changes caused by reactions were stabilized using the stiff‐chemistry solver. Additionally, second‐order upwind schemes were used to stabilize the numerical solution for density, momentum, species, energy, DOM, turbulent kinetic energy, and turbulent dissipation rate.

FEA Model Assumptions

The deformation and stresses on the structural support, element containing the legs, of the oven were evaluated. ANSYS Discovery 2024 was used for this study. The equations of the deformation model were solved considering the oven's mass (257.95 kg). However, this mass does not include the structural support mass. Consequently, a force of 2530.54 N, directed by gravity, was applied. The legs were modeled with fixed supports. The square profiles on the legs are C‐150. For this study, different gauges of this profile were used (C20, C18, and C14). For the legs, all four were considered as C‐200, 14‐gauge profiles. The material for these profiles is composed of A36‐Steel. Additionally, CFD results indicated that the floor temperature of the casing is 321.15 K. This temperature was applied to the square profiles. Other regions were considered to be at ambient temperature, with convective losses of 10 W/m2K.

Computational Meshing

The mesh for the CFD study was generated using ANSYS‐Meshing 2024. The technique involved dividing the interior volume (Vi) into 687 blocks (Núñez et al. 2020). These blocks were meshed based on the max face size (Mfs). The max face size (Mfs) enables control over the number of cells (ef) by considering the volume of the domain (Fluent 2013).

The number of nodes in regions of interest within the domain was adjusted using the refinement factor (rf) (Gómez Heleria et al. 2023; Roache 1998). Values of rf=rm3 and rf=1/rm3 were applied. Otherwise, the meshes for the FEA study were generated using the default tools in ANSYS Discovery 2024. The computational domain only considers the support structure of the oven (Figure 4), composed of nine volumes. The mesh used for the FEA study consists of 14,740 cells, named medium mesh of FEA study (Mfm) (Figure 5b). The regions with the highest magnitudes of deformation and Von Mises stresses were refined to ensure the accuracy of the numerical solution in those areas. Other mesh sizes were generated using the default fidelity settings in ANSYS Discovery.

Experimental Details

The experimental setup consisted of a Keysight DAQ970A data acquisition system and three Keysight 34307A type‐J thermocouples. The thermocouples were positioned at the top, middle, and bottom of the baking chamber to measure temperature. The experimental tests were conducted in the field while the device was operating with all trays loaded with bread. Data was recorded every 5 s for 1.3055 h, and the steady‐state period from this test was extracted for comparison with the steady‐state CFD simulations (Motyl et al. 2020 and Gómez‐Heleria et al. 2023).

Force Convection Test

The thermal behavior of the oven was determined at different firepowers (Fp), using the average temperature inside the baking chamber (Tcc), the thermal efficiency defined as the rate of energy transferred from the injector to the walls of the baking chamber (η) and volumetric isocontours inside the baking chamber. These parameters were applied to different air injection configurations in the baking chamber. Figure 6 shows that as Fp increases, Tcc remains constant. For different angles of air entry and exit in the baking chamber, it is shown that the C3 case (60° angle) has the lowest Tcc. The C1 case (30° angle) has the highest temperature, since most of the air entering and leaving the baking chamber flows primarily near the left wall. As for η, it is observed to decrease as Fp increases. Tcc and η depend on the air distribution within the baking chamber, which facilitates effective temperature distribution. As shown in Figure 6c, the injection C3 and C4 (90° angle) cases provide optimal temperature distribution, followed by C2 and C1 cases. Baking bread should be done at temperatures below 473.15 K to avoid high concentrations of acrylamide (Ahrné et al. 2007; Nguyen et al. 2016). In this respect, the C3 case is best suited to this need, as it operates at temperatures below 473.15 K, ensures good temperature distribution, and achieves high thermal efficiency. Consequently, this case was considered for the subsequent tests, as this configuration ensures a more homogeneous baking of the bread.

Buckling Test

The deformation (δm) and maximum Von Mises stresses (σm) were analyzed in the structural support to evaluate its performance. Tests were conducted on different square profile gauges used at the base of the support legs (Figure 7a). The results showed that deflections for all evaluated gauges remained below 1 mm, indicating acceptable dimensional stability under the applied loading conditions. Deformation decreased progressively from C20 to C14, reflecting increased structural stiffness as the profile thickness increased. A critical consideration is the margin between the maximum von Mises stresses and the yield strength of A36 steel equal to 152 MPa (Avallone et al. 2006). For all analyzed gauges, the stresses remained well below the yield strength, indicating a significant safety margin. This margin ensures that under standard loading conditions, the structure operates far from the plastic deformation threshold, enhancing its reliability and longevity. However, under rustic use conditions characterized by rough handling, environmental exposure, or dynamic loads, the durability of the support may be affected by repeated stress cycles or potential impacts. Although the stresses remain within the elastic limit, prolonged exposure to fluctuating or unexpected loads could result in material fatigue or localized stress concentrations. To mitigate these risks, the selection of the C14 gauge ensures not only stiffness but also resilience against such conditions. Figure 7b displays the deformation isocontours, illustrating the distribution of stresses and displacements in the structural support for legs with C14 gauge. The greatest deformation occurs in the central regions of the square profile, where the oven base rests, particularly at points subjected to higher stress concentrations or loading effects. In contrast, the least deformation is observed at the base and lower sections of the legs, which experience minimal displacement due to their support role and the rigidity of the square profile. Overall, the deformation distribution transitions smoothly from the top to the base of the legs, reflecting efficient load distribution and structural stiffness across most of the frame.

Combustion Numerical Results

Thermal performance was also evaluated using numerical combustion simulations. Specifically, Figure 8a shows the distribution of emissions (products in Equation 8) within the combustion chamber (gas mixture volume). As oxygen enters the combustion chamber, combustion begins in the injector region, where methane reactants, carbon monoxide, acetylene, and ethylene react with oxygen. These reactions lead to the formation of carbon dioxide and water, which accumulate around the baking chamber (air volume) before being expelled through the chimney. Consequently, the oxygen concentration in the injector region decreases as it is consumed upon reaching the base of the baking chamber. At the chimney exit, the oven exhibited an oxygen concentration of 2.24 × 10−3%, compared to 13.52% reported for a traditional bread oven made of bricks (Sadripour et al. 2018). This suggests that the oven proposed in this study achieves a more complete combustion. On the other hand, Figure 8b illustrates the distribution of turbulent kinetic energy (k) and temperature (T) in the combustion chamber using volumetric isocontours, while the temperature and velocity magnitude (U) distributions in the baking chamber are depicted with streamlines. These results reveal that both k and temperature exhibit higher magnitudes in the injector region, where the chemical reactions occur, indicating that the generation of combustion products is highly turbulent. Additionally, numerous turbulent structures are formed around the baking chamber, promoting effective mixing of the emissions and enhancing the temperature distribution within the combustion chamber (Dalbehera et al. 2022; Pundle et al. 2019). As a result, energy is transferred uniformly from the combustion chamber to the baking chamber, ensuring homogeneous air velocity and temperature distribution in the baking chamber—conditions necessary for the uniform baking of bread. The numerical combustion results indicate that the temperature inside the baking chamber does not exceed 473.15 K, corroborating the findings from the forced‐convection tests.

(b) Volumetric isocontours of turbulent kinetic energy (k) within the combustion chamber combined with temperature streamlines inside the baking chamber; and volumetric isocontours of temperature (T) in the combustion chamber combined with velocity (U) streamlines inside the baking chamber.

Biomass Oven Manufacture

The structural stability of the oven was evaluated through the construction of a scaled‐down prototype at 1/8 scale. This prototype was fabricated using 3D printers, specifically the ENDER‐3 V2 and DREMEL 3D20, which allow for precise replication of the oven's design. Figure 9a,b present multiple perspectives of the fabricated oven model, highlighting its key structural features. Stability tests were conducted to assess the integrity of the structure, both with the oven door closed and open. The results confirmed that the design is robust, with no evidence of collapse under these conditions. In the final stage, the oven was built at full scale for implementation in a rural community (Figure 9c), with the goal of providing a more controlled cooking experience than a traditional oven.

CFD Validation

Field temperature measurements inside the baking chamber are shown in Figure 10. The air temperature increases until it stabilizes at approximately 0.9 h, after which a quasi‐steady state is maintained for 24 min. The average measured air temperature was 447.572 ± 14.985, while CFD simulations predicted 465.624 K for convection and 447.276 K for combustion. Based on the temperature distribution obtained from both measurements and simulations, the proposed technology can maintain temperatures below 473.15 K, in contrast to traditional furnaces, where temperatures reach 709.15 ± 6 K (Falciano et al. 2022). Social and cultural barriers within the community limited the extent of experimental evaluation of the oven, as further testing would have disrupted the daily activities of those who depend on it for their livelihood. The results presented do not correspond to WBT or baking tests; rather, the test focused on measuring the thermal behavior of the baking chamber with a bread load, with the sole objective of verifying that the operating temperature remains within the design limits.

Conclusions

The design and development of the biomass bread baking oven, based on CFD and FEA simulations, have proven to be stable and safe for use in rural communities. The simulations identified the optimal air injection angle (60°), which achieves a uniform temperature distribution in the baking chamber, prevents the formation of acrylamide, and ensures homogeneous baking. Structural tests validated the stability of the support with C14 profiles, highlighting its strength and durability under typical loading conditions. The fabrication of a scaled prototype confirmed the robustness of the design, while field tests in Michoacán demonstrated its capacity to evenly bake 20 loaves across 10 trays, adapting to local cultural and functional needs. This design can contribute to sustainable development by reducing emissions and improving energy efficiency, offering an option for rural communities. In the future, experiments will be conducted to evaluate this technology and compare it with traditional clay ovens to determine its environmental impact and its social adoption. The present work was focused on developing and validating a biomass oven prototype using CAD, CAM, CFD, and FEA tools; however, it did not explore advanced optimization or predictive approaches. Machine learning techniques could be applied to address these limitations for the development of biomass ovens or rural cooking systems.
