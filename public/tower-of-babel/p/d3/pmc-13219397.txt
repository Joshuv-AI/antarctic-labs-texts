A large Chinese dataset of ten-category semantic relations with developmental performance in children and adolescents
Authors: Jiayi Fu, Chuqi Liu, Shuaihui Mou, Liang Shi, Liang Zhang, Xiaojing Peng, Tong Li, Huinan Hu, Mingxue Fu, Gui Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSemantic relations are fundamental to human conceptual knowledge and underpin cognitive processes such as categorization, analogical reasoning, and language comprehension. Despite extensive research on semantic relations in English, a systematic Chinese dataset aligned with established taxonomies remains unavailable, limiting cross-linguistic and developmental research. Here, we present a large-scale Chinese semantic relation dataset based on the ten-category taxonomy proposed by Bejar et al., comprising 1,000 typical word pairs that were behaviorally validated. Initially, 3,758 candidate word pairs were generated by 32 college students and subsequently validated via a semantic relation judgment task with 5,898 participants aged 9–18 years. The final dataset includes relation labels, concreteness ratings for 2,295 unique words, and behavioral measures across developmental stages. Developmental analyses revealed significant age-related increases in accuracy for most semantic relations, with performance in most relations approaching a plateau at ages 15–17. This dataset provides validated materials and developmental performance patterns for investigating semantic cognition in Chinese-speaking populations, with applications in cognitive psychology, developmental science, computational linguistics, and natural language processing.

Background & Summary

Semantic relations are a fundamental component of human conceptual knowledge1,2. Rather than being represented solely by the meanings of individual words, semantic knowledge is structured through relations that link concepts (e.g., tiger–animal, rose–love, fold–clothes). These relations support fundamental cognitive operations such as categorization, analogy, inference, and discourse comprehension, and are critical for applications in education, psycholinguistics, and artificial intelligence.

Multiple taxonomies of semantic relations have been proposed by psychologists and linguists3–6. Among these, the taxonomy proposed by Bejar et al.5 has been widely adopted in cognitive neuroscience and artificial intelligence research7,8. This framework defines ten distinct types of relations, including two hierarchical relations (class-inclusion and whole-part) and eight non-hierarchical relations (similar, contrast, attribute, non-attribute, symbolic, cause, case, and time-space). Building on this taxonomy, Jurgens et al.7 compiled a large-scale English word-pair dataset that has served as a foundational resource in psychology, cognitive neuroscience, and computer science7–12. Despite the utility of existing English resources, a comparable Chinese dataset systematically aligned with the framework proposed by Bejar et al. remains unavailable. This gap limits cross-linguistic investigations of semantic cognition and constrains the development of Chinese natural language processing applications.

Research on cognitive development has demonstrated that the ability to process semantic relations is not innate but emerges gradually across development13–21. Studies have shown that infants exhibit sensitivity to simple relational structures13,14, and children begin to reliably distinguish between relations such as similarity and contrast during early childhood15–17. By adolescence and early adulthood, individuals can perform complex semantic relation judgments and show substantial improvement in analogical reasoning tasks18–21. However, most existing developmental studies have focused on a limited set of relations, such as similarity, contrast, and class-inclusion. Consequently, the developmental trajectories of other fundamental relations (e.g., attribute, symbolic) remain largely unexplored. Whether different types of semantic relations follow similar or distinct developmental patterns remains an open question. A large dataset covering multiple relation types across age groups would enable systematic investigation of these questions and provide insight into the formation of conceptual knowledge systems.

To address these gaps, we present a large Chinese semantic relation dataset based on Bejar et al.‘s taxonomy5, accompanied by developmental measures from children and adolescents. The dataset comprises 1,000 typical Chinese word pairs spanning all ten semantic relation types. Word pairs were initially generated by 32 college students and then validated through a semantic relation judgment task administered to 5,898 participants aged 9–18 years. In addition to relation category labels, we provide word concreteness ratings for all unique words in the dataset, with each word classified as abstract or concrete. Behavioral measures derived from the judgment task enable researchers to quantify developmental trajectories of semantic relation processing across this age range. Notably, accuracy for most semantic relations increased with age, with performance in most relations reaching a plateau at ages 15–17.

In summary, this dataset addresses a critical gap in Chinese language resources for semantic relation research. At the same time, its scope should be interpreted in light of the sampled age range. The current word pair materials and task paradigm were developed for this age range, and their extension to younger children will require age-appropriate task adaptation and further validation. Within this scope, the dataset supports applications across multiple disciplines. First, the age-stratified behavioral results provide a foundation for developmental and cognitive neuroscience studies, enabling researchers to investigate how semantic relations are acquired across development and to examine the neural substrates of relational reasoning using validated experimental materials. Second, systematic alignment with Bejar et al.‘s taxonomy enables direct cross-linguistic comparisons with established English datasets, supporting investigations of universal versus language-specific mechanisms in semantic processing. Third, the dataset provides fine-grained semantic relation classifications, along with human performance patterns and concreteness ratings, that can be used to examine Chinese semantic relation recognition models and the relational reasoning capabilities of large language models. For example, researchers can use the word pairs as test items and evaluate whether a model correctly assigns each pair to its semantic relation category. Model performance can then be compared across relation types and across concrete versus abstract conditions, and further evaluated against the age-related performance patterns observed in the dataset. By integrating psychometric validation with developmental evidence, this dataset provides a useful resource for research on Chinese semantic relation processing across cognitive science and computational applications.

Methods

Participants

This study involved three distinct participant samples recruited for the different phases of dataset construction.

Word-pair generation task

Thirty-two college students (4 males; mean age = 21.73 years, SD = 2.22) were recruited offline from Beijing Normal University to generate candidate word pairs for the semantic relation dataset. Participants were randomly assigned to one or more of eleven semantic relation categories and were required to demonstrate their comprehension of the target relation before proceeding with the generation task.

Concreteness rating task

Fifty-three college students (9 males; mean age = 20.52 years, SD = 2.22) were recruited offline from Beijing Normal University to provide concreteness ratings for the words in the generated word pairs. Five participants were excluded due to inadequate comprehension of the task instructions, yielding a final sample of 48 raters.

Semantic relation judgment task

A total of 6,306 middle school and high school students (3,448 males; mean age = 13.53 years, SD = 2.23) were recruited through collaborating schools and completed one of two web-based semantic relation judgment tasks on school computers under teacher supervision. After excluding participants with poor task performance (see Data Analysis for details), 5,898 participants were retained for subsequent analyses. Of these, 3,318 students (1,774 males; mean age = 12.77 years, SD = 2.28) completed Set A, and 2,580 students (1,401 males; mean age = 14.55 years, SD = 1.66) completed Set B.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the State Key Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience and Learning at Beijing Normal University (approval number: IRB_A_0001_2021002). All research procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. For the adult participants in the word-pair generation and concreteness rating tasks, written informed consent was obtained directly from the participants. For the participants under 18 years of age in the semantic relation judgment task, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, and oral assent was obtained from the participants themselves. The consent procedures covered participation in the study and the use and public release of de-identified data for research and publication purposes. To protect participant privacy, no names or other direct personal identifiers were included in the released dataset. All participants were represented only by anonymized user IDs.

Procedure

Word-pair generation

To construct a Chinese semantic relation dataset aligned with the taxonomy proposed by Bejar et al.5, eleven online questionnaires were designed, corresponding to eleven semantic relation types, with the time-space relation separated into time and space subcategories. Each questionnaire began with four example word pairs, including two concrete pairs and two abstract pairs (e.g., apple–fruit, dog–animal, Buddhism–religion, angry–mood in the questionnaire for the class-inclusion relation), along with explanations distinguishing concrete from abstract word pairs. The classification of a word pair as concrete or abstract was based on the concreteness of the first word in the pair, because the word pairs were constructed as ordered relational pairs rather than unordered word combinations. In this design, the two word positions were treated as functionally distinct, with the first word serving as the focal term and the second word providing the interpretive reference or relational context22,23. Concrete word pairs were not collected for the contrast relation, and abstract word pairs were not collected for the symbolic relation. This decision reflects the nature of these relations: contrast relations typically involve abstract concepts with clear antonyms (e.g., joy–sadness), whereas symbolic relations typically involve concrete objects representing abstract meanings (e.g., rose–love) (Table 1). In total, 20 subcategories of word pairs were generated.

Participants were randomly assigned to generate word pairs for one semantic relation. They were asked to explain the relation of the examples to the experimenters online, and only participants who demonstrated a clear understanding of the target relation and task requirements were invited to generate word pairs. Each participant was instructed to generate at least 50 word pairs for each relation without time constraints. For each of the 20 categories, the experiment was terminated once 200 unique word pairs had been generated. Two experimenters (C.L. and S.M.) manually checked all the word pairs and applied quantitative criteria for initial screening. Word pairs were removed if either word had a familiarity rating below 3.0 according to a published familiarity database24. To reduce redundancy among candidate materials, relation similarity between word pairs was estimated using the Tencent AI Lab Chinese word2vec embeddings. For any two word pairs A:B and C:D, relational similarity was defined as the cosine similarity between vectors vec(B) - vec(A) and vec(D) - vec(C). Pairs were treated as highly similar if the cosine similarity exceeded 0.90, in which case one redundant pair was removed. For words not covered by the familiarity database or without available word vectors, decisions were made by consensus between the two experimenters. Pairs were also removed when the two experimenters failed to reach consensus.

Concreteness ratings

To obtain fine-grained measures of word concreteness, additional participants were recruited to rate the concreteness of the first word in each word pair. The words were divided into three groups, and participants were randomly assigned to complete the ratings for one group. Each group of words was rated by at least 20 participants. Five participants completed two groups, and one participant completed all three groups. An additional five participants were recruited but were excluded from the analysis due to inadequate comprehension of the task instructions.

Before rating, participants were shown the following instructions: “If a word refers to a concept that can be seen, heard, smelled, tasted or touched, such a thing is concrete. Conversely, if a word refers to a concept that cannot be or is difficult to experience directly with the senses, such a thing is abstract.” Illustrative examples of concrete and abstract concepts were provided, including nouns, adjectives, and verbs. Only participants who demonstrated a correct understanding of the instructions were permitted to proceed with the rating task.

Each word was presented with its corresponding part of speech and an example sentence (e.g., “Betray, verb: He betrayed his friend”). Participants were asked to rate each word on a 7-point scale (0 = completely impossible to experience through the senses, 6 = highly possible) in response to the prompt: “To what extent do you think it is possible to experience and imagine this concept through your five senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch)?” We collected 20 ratings for each word, as this number has been shown to be sufficiently reliable25. Previous studies defined words with high imageability or high concreteness (>500, on a scale of 100–700) of the MRC Psycholinguistic Database as concrete words and words with low imageability or low concreteness (<450) as abstract words26–28. In the present study using a 0–6 scale, we defined words with concreteness scores equal to or higher than 4 as concrete words, and all others as abstract words.

Semantic relation judgment task

Participants completed the semantic relation judgment tasks on school computers in supervised group sessions, facilitated by one or two teachers. Participants first learned the definition and typical examples of each semantic relation. They then practiced this task by identifying two pairs that exhibited the target relation from a set of four pairs, with feedback provided after each response. During the formal experiment, participants judged the relation of a presented word pair by selecting one from five candidate semantic relations. Note that the ten relations were divided into two groups to avoid presenting too many options in one task. To keep the task challenging, the relations that were confusable were grouped together. The first group (Set A) comprised similar, contrast, attribute, non-attribute and symbolic relations, whereas set B comprised class-inclusion, whole-part, cause, case and time-space relations. Because Set B was more difficult, it was administered only to middle and high school students (age range: 12–18 years). Both concrete and abstract word pairs were included in each set. Within each task set, trials were sampled randomly from the five relation categories rather than being strictly counterbalanced across categories for each participant. Participants completed 45 trials for Set A. For Set B, participants completed either 45 trials (788 participants) or 60 trials (1,745 participants). No participant completed both sets. Some participants finished the same task twice (337 participants) or more times (19 participants) with mostly different word pairs (due to randomization). In the rare instances where a participant encountered the same word pair more than once, only the response from the first encounter was retained for analysis.

Data analysis

Preprocessing and item selection

Participant-level accuracy was first calculated for each task. For the purpose of item selection, participants with accuracy ≤ 0.40 were excluded. Item-level accuracy for each word pair was then computed as the mean proportion of correct responses across the remaining participants. For each subcategory, the 50 word pairs with the highest accuracy were selected as typical exemplars. For the time-space relation, word pairs were selected separately from the time and space subcategories.

Developmental trajectory analysis

For analyses based on the final dataset, participants with overall task accuracy at or below chance level (≤ 0.20) in the original semantic relation judgment task were excluded. Subsequent analyses were restricted to responses to the final 1,000 selected word pairs. The age range of participants was 9–18 years for Set A and 12–18 years for Set B. Due to limited sample sizes at the extreme ages, developmental analyses focused on ages 10–17 for Set A and 12–17 for Set B. Mean accuracy was calculated for each semantic relation at each age. To characterize developmental trajectories, both linear and nonlinear (quadratic) regression models were fitted to the relationship between age and accuracy for each relation. Model fit was evaluated using adjusted R-squared, with the Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion used as complementary indices of relative fit. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using the false discovery rate (FDR) method29.

Data Records

The dataset30 is publicly available on the Open Science Framework (OSF) repository (10.17605/OSF.IO/P4VWT). All data are organized into five folders. The “Materials” folder contains all word-pair stimuli used in this study. The “Concreteness” folder provides word concreteness ratings. The “Behavioral” folder includes raw and processed behavioral data from the semantic relation judgment task. The “Validation” folder contains results from all reliability analyses. The “Code” folder includes all analysis scripts used for data processing and validation. Detailed descriptions of each folder are provided below.

Materials folder

The complete set of candidate word pairs generated during the initial construction phase is provided in “word_pair_ori.csv”. The final set of typical word pairs, selected based on item-level judgment accuracy (top 50 pairs per subcategory), is provided in two files corresponding to the two task sets: “word_pair_A.csv” (Set A covering similar, contrast, attribute, non-attribute, and symbolic relations) and “word_pair_B.csv” (Set B covering class-inclusion, whole-part, cause, case, and time-space relations). Each file includes the word pair, semantic relation category, and concreteness classification (concrete or abstract).

Concreteness folder

Raw concreteness ratings collected from three rater groups are provided in “Concreteness_A.csv”, “Concreteness_B.csv”, and “Concreteness_C.csv”. Aggregated concreteness norms are provided in “word_concreteness.csv”, which includes for each word, the mean concreteness score and the derived concreteness classification (concrete or abstract) based on the threshold specified in the Methods section. Demographic information for the raters is provided in “Concreteness_subject_info.xlsx”.

Behavioral folder

Raw response data from all 6,306 initially recruited participants are provided in “dataA_ori.xlsx” (Set A) and “dataB_ori.xlsx” (Set B). Each file contains trial-level data with the following variables: participant identifier, gender, age, word pair, target relation category, concreteness classification of the first word (concrete or abstract), participant response, response accuracy, and response time. After applying exclusion criteria (see Methods), data from the final sample of 5,898 participants are provided in “dataA_final.xlsx” (Set A) and “dataB_final.xlsx” (Set B).

Validation folder

Item-based split-half reliability coefficients (Spearman–Brown corrected) for each of the ten semantic relation categories are provided in “split_half_results.csv”. Inter-rater reliability results for concreteness ratings are provided in “concreteness_reliability_results.csv”. An independent concreteness norm dataset from the MELD-SCH database31, used for the convergent validity analysis, is provided in “Xu2020_database.xlsx”.

Code folder

The R Markdown file “Relation_judgement.Rmd” contains code for data processing, accuracy calculation, and developmental trajectory analyses. The Jupyter Notebook “reliability_analysis.ipynb” contains Python code for computing split-half reliability coefficients. The R Markdown file “concreteness_validation.Rmd” contains code for assessing inter-rater reliability and convergent validity of concreteness ratings.

Data Overview

The initial word-pair generation task yielded 4,222 candidate word pairs across 20 subcategories. After manual review by two experimenters, 3,758 word pairs were retained, with at least 150 pairs per subcategory. In the subsequent concreteness rating task, participants provided ratings for the first word in each of these 3,758 word pairs, yielding 2,295 unique words. For the semantic relation judgment task, after data exclusion (see Data Analysis for details), the final dataset comprised 1,000 word pairs containing 1,535 unique words. Due to the random trial sampling procedure, the number of valid judgments contributing to each retained word pair was not identical. The mean number of valid judgments per word pair was 135.25 ± 30.78, ranging from 50 to 238.

Due to the limited number of participants aged 9 and 18, analyses were restricted to participants aged 10 to 17 years for Set A and 12 to 17 years for Set B. These developmental trajectories are consistent with prior research on the maturation of relational reasoning. For instance, Whitaker et al.19 demonstrated that analogical development from ages 6 to 19 is best characterized by quadratic models, reflecting steep gains during childhood that stabilize by late adolescence. Similarly, Nippold18 found that performance on complex verbal analogies improved steadily from age 10 to 14 before stabilizing or slightly declining by age 16 to 17. Despite differences in task paradigms and linguistic contexts, the present dataset showed a broadly similar age-related nonlinear pattern in semantic relation processing. This convergence with prior studies provides evidence for the construct validity of the current dataset.

Internal consistency

To assess the reliability of the semantic relation norms, we quantified the internal consistency of each relation category using item-based split-half reliability32,33 with Spearman–Brown correction34,35. For each semantic relation category, word pairs were randomly partitioned into two halves, and the Pearson correlation between participants’ mean accuracy scores on the two halves was computed. To ensure stable estimates, participants were required to have responded to at least three word pairs in each half to be included in a given iteration. This random split-half procedure was repeated 1,000 times for each relation category. The final reliability coefficient was calculated as the mean across all iterations.

Split-half reliabilities ranged from 0.556 to 0.772 across the ten semantic relation categories (Table 4). The highest reliability was observed for case relations (r = 0.772, 95% CI [0.734, 0.806]) and time-space relations (r = 0.768, 95% CI [0.746, 0.789]). Lower reliability was observed for symbolic relations (r = 0.556, 95% CI [0.419, 0.664]) and contrast relations (r = 0.559, 95% CI [0.325, 0.724]). The relatively lower reliability of these categories may partly reflect their smaller numbers of word pairs.

Validation of concreteness ratings

Inter-rater reliability

We assessed the inter-rater reliability of the concreteness ratings using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Specifically, we calculated ICC(2,k) using a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, which treats both words (items) and raters as random effects drawn from larger populations36. This approach is appropriate when the goal is to generalize findings to other potential raters and when the final scores represent averages across multiple raters37.

Given that words were divided into three groups, with participants randomly assigned to rate one or more groups (see Methods), ICC(2,k) values were computed separately for each group. As shown in Fig. 2a, all three groups demonstrated high inter-rater reliability: Group A (ICC = 0.961, 95% CI [0.952, 0.968]), Group B (ICC = 0.954, 95% CI [0.947, 0.961]), and Group C (ICC = 0.901, 95% CI [0.880, 0.919]). The consistently high ICC values across all groups indicate strong agreement among raters and support the reliability of the aggregated concreteness norms.

Convergent validity

To assess convergent validity, we compared the present concreteness ratings with an independent set of concreteness norms for two-character Chinese words from the MELD-SCH database31. The MELD-SCH database uses a 1–5 scale where lower values indicate greater concreteness, whereas the present study employed a 0–6 scale where higher values indicate greater concreteness. To facilitate comparison, we reverse-scored the MELD-SCH ratings (mapping 1–5 to 5–1) before computing correlations.

In total, 1,334 words overlapped between the two datasets. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, concreteness ratings from the present study showed a strong positive correlation with the transformed MELD-SCH ratings (Pearson r = 0.820, p < 0.001). This association supports the convergent validity of the present concreteness norms, indicating that our ratings capture the same underlying construct as previously established measures.
