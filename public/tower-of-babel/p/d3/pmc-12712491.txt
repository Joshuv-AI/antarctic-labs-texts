Tibial Infratubercle Anterior Closing-Wedge Slope-Reducing Osteotomy With Combined Revision Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Ricardo Abreu, Alexis Aboulafia, Jorg Dickschas, Matthieu Ollivier, Alfred Mansour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElevated posterior tibial slope (PTS) has been identified as a risk factor for failure of primary and revision anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. Anterior closing-wedge slope-reducing high tibial osteotomy (ACW-SRO) has been shown to decrease PTS, decrease ACL graft strain, and decrease static anterior tibial translation. One approach is an infratubercle ACW-SRO, which preserves the tibial tubercle and extensor mechanism, allows for concurrent procedures, and reduces complications related to patellar height and/or tunnel placement. The purpose of this study is to present technical and planning tips that are critical to executing a successful infratubercle ACW-SRO. Preoperative planning, precise execution, and plate fixation are critical to avoid complications such as hinge fracture, neurovascular injury, and nonunion. This technique is a safe and effective option for patients with elevated PTS and failed ACL reconstruction, although further studies are needed to assess long-term outcomes.

Elevated posterior tibial slope (PTS), defined as 12° or more, is a lower-extremity sagittal deformity that has been implicated in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) graft strain and retear.1, 2, 3 Anterior closing-wedge slope-reducing high tibial osteotomy (ACW-SRO) has become increasingly used for the correction of PTS in the setting of failed ACL reconstruction (ACLR).1 We present tips and tricks for successful completion of infratubercle ACW-SRO.

Indications and Contraindications

The main indication for this procedure is PTS of 12° or more in a patient with a failed ACLR.1,3,4 Other findings include severe anterior instability, as evidenced by high-grade Lachman and pivot-shift (3b) results on physical examination.5 Contraindications for infratubercle ACW-SRO include genu recurvatum with significant knee hyperextension (>10°), posterior cruciate ligament deficiency, significant genu varus malalignment (>3°) with medial-compartment compromise, and/or end-stage tibiofemoral osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 3-4).1,6, 7, 8

Preoperative Radiologic Planning

PTS is measured along both the medial and lateral tibial plateaus on calibrated full-length lateral (FLL) tibial radiographs according to the technique described by Dean et al.9 (Fig 1). We prefer FLL radiographs because of the variability in measurements between standard knee and FLL radiographs.10, 11, 12 The amount of necessary PTS correction is calculated according to the correction desired on the FLL radiographic image, with a target reduced slope angle of 6° to 8°.2,13,14 An essential tip is that one should not assume that 1 mm of resection correlates with 1° of correction. Careful planning is required to avoid overcorrection, given that a PTS less than 5° may increase stress on the posterior cruciate ligament.6,15, 16, 17

(B) Magnified view of A, created for comparison with fluoroscopic images intraoperatively. The green lines measure the posterior tibial slope, the angle and measured planned wedge resection.

Coronal-plane deformity is assessed by measuring overall mechanical axis alignment on full-length anteroposterior radiographs.18 Magnetic resonance imaging is also critical for preoperative planning to identify vascular anatomy and minimize complications, as the rate of aberrant anterior tibial artery takeoff is 3% (Fig 2).19

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine on a radiolucent operating table. Examination under anesthesia is conducted to identify any ligamentous instability and to evaluate the range of motion. The C-arm is positioned on the surgical side. It is important to ensure that the target of the fluoroscopic image is in the middle of the x-ray beam to avoid inaccuracies due to the parallax effect.20

Surgical Approach

A complete diagnostic arthroscopy is first completed. Associated procedures, including revision ACLR, meniscal and cartilage procedures, and extra-articular procedures, are performed prior to the start of the osteotomy. The ACL tunnel is prepared, and the graft can be secured on the femoral side without being secured on the tibial side. If the hamstring or patellar tendon is used for the ACL graft, the skin incision made for graft harvest is used as an entry point for the ACW-SRO. If another graft is chosen, a new incision is made over the anterior aspect of the tibia, starting from the proximal tip of the tibial tubercle and extending 5 to 7 cm distally. Full-thickness subcutaneous flaps are elevated, and the inferior fibers of the patellar tendon insertion are identified as the proximal point for the osteotomy. The thick periosteum at this junction point is elevated and reflected distally for later re-approximation over the anterior aspect of the osteotomy site.

The anteromedial soft tissue and the medial collateral ligament are then elevated with a Cobb elevator only along the planned course of the osteotomy, leaving the soft-tissue sleeve attached proximally and distally. The anterior compartment is elevated in a subperiosteal fashion along the Gerdy tubercle toward the tip of the fibular head.

Osteotomy

Once the anterior cortex of the tibia is exposed, an electrocautery device is used to mark the measured distance, which will result in the planned degree of resection. A large-diameter wire (3.0-mm K-wire) tends to minimally deflect (is stiffer), despite the oblique trajectory, compared with smaller-diameter wires and is used as a saw guide. The wire is advanced to the posterior tibial cortical flare. A second K-wire is advanced inferior to the measured area and positioned such that the wires would hypothetically converge just posterior to the posterior tibia cortex. This allows the saw blade to follow the wire path and converge within the tibia just anterior to the posterior tibial cortical flare, leaving a hinge (Fig 3).

Lateral fluoroscopic image of proximal tibia with 2 K-wires representing proposed osteotomy site and convergence just posterior to tibial cortex.

After appropriate pin placement, the fluoroscopic profile of the bone saw is used to confirm that parallel osteotomy cuts are being created. The cuts are parallel to the articular surface in the coronal plane to minimize inadvertent asymmetrical closure and potentially increased varus deformity (Fig 4A). When the saw profile is at its thinnest, as seen on a lateral fluoroscopy image, the blade is correctly aligned (Fig 4B). The cuts are performed, and the wedge is removed. A broad osteotome and curettes are used to complete the medial- and lateral-most margins of the osteotomy site to allow adequate collapse of the osteotomy along the hinge point. If not removed, the corners behave like a structural I-beam and resist bending and compression of the osteotomy, which increases the risk of fracture propagation (Fig 5). The knee is then hyperextended to collapse the osteotomy site and allow for compression (Fig 6).

Fixation

A provisional interfragmentary pin is placed, and an anteromedial compression plate is then applied. The plate is positioned with the new ACL tunnel in mind. The plate can partially cover the tunnel (if drilled prior to osteotomy) as long as the graft does not extend beyond the aperture of the tunnel and the tensioning sutures can pass freely under the plate. The plate is secured proximally with locking screws. A nonlocking screw is then placed using a compressive hole distal to the osteotomy site to compress it. The interfragmentary pin can be exchanged with a 4.5-mm cortical screw, which further stabilizes the tubercle, and distal locking screws to complete the construct (Fig 7). An osteotome is used to bevel the anterior distal tibial edge to prevent prominence. Autograft from the bone wedge can be placed along the osteotomy edges. The anterior compartment is closed. The periosteum over the osteotomy site is restored anteriorly and attached to the inferior portion of the tibial attachment of the patellar tendon. After osteotomy fixation, final tensioning of the tibial side of the ACL graft, as well as concomitant repairs, can be performed and the graft can be tied to a cancellous tibial screw post. The wounds are then closed in layered fashion. A sterile dressing and hinged knee brace are placed.

Postoperative Care

A 4-week period of toe-touch weight-bearing, followed by partial weight-bearing, is used to protect the tibial tubercle. At 6 weeks, the patient may progress to full weight-bearing if able to do so without pain or limping, unless added protection is required per protocol (meniscus repair or transplant, osteochondral allograft transplant, and so on). Range of motion is limited to 0° to 90° for the initial 2 weeks to protect the anterior skin incision. For the first 6 weeks, active knee extension is prohibited to protect the tibial tubercle from fracture. Multimodal pain medication is prescribed postoperatively. The patient receives deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis with aspirin, 81 mg twice daily for 4 weeks, and vitamin D3 supplementation, 50,000 IU weekly, for 6 weeks to promote bone healing and bone quality.21,22

Discussion

Elevated PTS is a significant risk factor contributing to the failure of ACLR, and various slope-reducing osteotomy techniques have shown promising outcomes.1,7,8,23 We prefer the infratubercle approach because of its previously mentioned advantages, including sparing of the extensor mechanism, availability of additional bone stock at the infratubercle area, and predictable and measured cuts. This osteotomy technique is achieved by performing cuts below the tibial tubercle to avoid complications with the extensor mechanism or changes in patellar height—a concern with supra-tuberosity approaches.24 Additionally, sufficient bone stock is preserved proximal to the osteotomy, eliminating the need for re-drilling ACL tunnels. Our technique also accommodates concomitant procedures, such as meniscal transplant or root repair, without encroaching on the trough or tunnels. We perform planned osteotomy cuts with a hinge based at the posterior tibial cortical flare. This theoretically mitigates intra-articular fracture propagation compared with more proximal osteotomy trajectories. From a mechanical perspective, this approach differs from conventional slope-correcting osteotomies. Closing the osteotomy gap in full knee extension allows greater tolerance for axial force.13 Our experience reveals no intraoperative or postoperative hinge fractures among patients treated with ACW-SRO, aligning with other studies that report no posterior hinge fractures.24

This technique carries potential risks, including nonunion of the osteotomy site, creating unintended varus deformity, as well as posterior hinge fractures. These risks can be minimized using precise planning, meticulous pin placement, accurate saw trajectory, and rigid fixation devices rather than screws or staples.

The infratubercle anterior closing-wedge slope-reducing osteotomy is a reliable and effective method in the context of failed ACLR with elevated PTS. Its compatibility with single-stage procedures and favorable safety profile make it a valuable technique. However, additional large-scale studies are essential to evaluate long-term outcomes and optimize this approach further.
