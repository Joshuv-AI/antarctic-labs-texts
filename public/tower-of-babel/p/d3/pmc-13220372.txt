Kaempferol-based nanocarriers as a potential therapeutic strategy against drug-resistant lung cancer
Authors: Pouya Goleij, Mohammad Amin Khazeei Tabari, Danaé S. Larsen, Mahboube Hadipour, Aryan Rezaee, Mohadeseh Poudineh, Maria Daglia, Tian Li, Haroon Khan, Le Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is the predominant cause of cancer-related deaths, with chemoresistance posing a significant challenge for effective treatment. Drug resistance in lung cancer cells involves various cellular and molecular mechanisms, including the upregulation of DNA repair pathways, activation of secondary proto-oncogenes and alterations in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis and the tumor microenvironment. Recent advances in nanotechnology have paved the way for innovative approaches to overcome chemoresistance, particularly through the utilization of nanocarriers for targeted drug delivery. When encapsulated within nanocarriers, kaempferol’s therapeutic potential is significantly enhanced, offering improved bioavailability, targeted delivery and reduced systemic toxicity. Kaempferol-based nanoparticles, including liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers, have shown substantial efficacy for combating chemoresistance in lung cancer models. These nanoparticles May enhance the intracellular delivery of kaempferol, potentially leading to the inhibition of key signaling pathways implicated in chemoresistance. Additionally, kaempferol-based nanocarriers have been shown to sensitize cancer cells to conventional chemotherapeutic agents, reducing drug efflux and enhancing apoptosis. The synergistic effects of kaempferol in combination with chemotherapy drugs, including cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil, further underscore its potential in overcoming resistance mechanisms. Moreover, the use of hyaluronic acid-modified, kaempferol-based nanoparticles has demonstrated enhanced targeting of lung cancer cells, inhibition of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and suppression of tumor invasion and metastasis.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Lung cancer is currently one of the major causes of cancer-related death [1]. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide, with approximately 2.5 million new cases each year, constituting 12.4% of all cancer diagnoses. Additionally, it was the primary cause of cancer deaths, resulting in approximately 1.8 million fatalities and accounting for 18.7% of all cancer-related deaths worldwide [2]. Lung cancer primarily occurs in two forms, the most common being non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with the other, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) being known for its greater aggressiveness. Additionally, there is a rarer type of lung cancer, known as carcinoid tumours [3–5]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the efficacy of standard chemotherapy can often be hampered by the occurrence of chemoresistance, leading to treatment failure and disease progression [6]. In this context, there is an urgency to finding novel treatment modalities that can overcome chemoresistance and enhance patient outcomes [7].

Kaempferol, a naturally occurring flavonoid present in various plants, contains a number of functional groups that can increase its biochemical interactions in biological systems. It is classed as a strong antioxidant because of its hydroxyl groups, which provide the ability to scavenge free radicals and, therefore, decrease oxidative stress [8]. Kaempferol has attracted considerable interest due to its anticancer effects, such as inducing apoptosis, arresting the cell cycle, and preventing metastasis [9]. Despite these benefits, it has seen limited clinical use due to having low bioavailability, its fast metabolism, and its limited solubility [10]. Nanotechnology provides a promising solution to these issues by improving the delivery and effectiveness of chemotherapeutic drugs [11, 12]. Nanocarriers, including liposomes, nanoparticles, and micelles, have transformed drug delivery by enabling targeted and controlled release of therapeutic compounds [13, 14]. These nanocarriers can encapsulate kaempferol, safeguarding it from degradation, increasing its solubility, and facilitating its preferential accumulation within tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and active targeting. The EPR effect significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicine, thus improving targeted delivery of drugs to cancer cells while reducing systemic toxicity. This forms a strategic advantage in treating solid tumors, in which conventional therapies often fail to achieve adequate drug concentrations [15]. Active targeting strategies have also been explored as a form of complementary approach. By functionalizing nanoparticles with specific ligands, such as antibodies or peptides, nanomedicines can be delivered to cells expressing excess receptors. These ligands enable cellular internalization and uptake, thereby improving therapeutic effectiveness. Key ligands being explored include those that target angiogenesis-related proteins and other tumor-related receptors [16]. Additionally, modifying the surface of nanocarriers can enhance their specificity for cancer cells, thereby mitigating off-target effects and decreasing overall toxicity [17]. This literature review investigates the creation and analysis of kaempferol-based nanocarriers to tackle chemoresistant lung cancers. Utilizing the benefits of nanotechnology, this method aims to improve kaempferol’s therapeutic effectiveness, offering a potentially promising solution for overcoming chemoresistance during lung cancer therapy.

Method

The aim of the present study is to provide an overview of the current evidence regarding the use of kaempferol-based nanoparticles as a therapeutic approach for lung cancer. A search of electronic databases such as PubMed, google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science based on the keywords ‘lung cancer’, ‘Kaempferol-based nanoparticles’, and ‘chemoresistance’ was undertaken to retrieve all papers, (predominately from 2020), that assessed the effects of Kaempferol-based nanoparticles on lung cancer.

Key molecular pathways involved in lung cancer drug resistance

Lung cancer is the predominant cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with a mortality rate exacerbated by chemoresistance [18, 19]. This phenomenon, where cancer cells become resistant to chemotherapy drugs, results in failed treatments and relapses. The mechanisms behind chemoresistance in lung cancer are complex, involving a range of cellular and molecular processes [20]. Intrinsic chemoresistance is present in cancer cells even before the patient receives any drug or therapy. Tumors exhibiting this type of resistance have phenotypes which are already unresponsive to chemotherapy, prior to any exposure to chemotherapeutic agents [21]. The KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway plays a key role in maintaining redox balance and metabolic stability. The transcription factor NFE2L2 controls the expression of antioxidants, growth factors, detoxifying enzymes, and drug efflux transporters. KEAP1, the cytoplasmic adaptor protein associated with Cullin3 (Cul3)-loaded E3-ligase, regulates NFE2L2 activity. In lung cancer, common mutations of KEAP1 and NFE2L2 impact their interaction, or result in the overexpression of NFE2L2 [22]. These mutations are present in 33% of lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) and 22% of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cases [18, 23]. Studies on NSCLC in animal models have shown that the deletion of KEAP1 and mutations in NFE2L2 induce cancer cell proliferation and provide these cells with a survival advantage [24].

Pre-clinical models of LUAD and LSCC have found an association between KEAP1 deletion and chemoresistance [22]. First-line chemotherapy in individuals with NSCLC who have mutations of KEAP1, NFE2L2, or CUL3, tends to result in shorter times to treatment failure (TTF) and overall survival (OS) [25]. KRAS mutations, often observed in NSCLC, may also contribute to resistance through SIRT1-mediated mechanisms [26].

Acquired drug resistance in cancer treatment occurs when cancer cells, initially responsive to chemotherapy or targeted therapy, evolve and become resistant over time. This resistance results in diminished treatment efficacy and can lead to further disease progression [27]. The development of acquired drug resistance is complex and multifaceted, often involving genetic, epigenetic, and microenvironmental changes. It is characterized by the emergence of cancer cells that are resistant to medication, and a decline in the efficacy of anti-cancer treatments [28]. Contributing factors include mutations in drug targets [29], activation of secondary proto-oncogenes [30], epigenetic modifications such as methylation and acetylation, alterations in microRNA (miRNA) expression [31, 32], disruptions in cell cycle regulation and checkpoints [33], impaired apoptosis [34], and changes in DNA repair mechanisms [35]. Research has identified various acquired mechanisms driving chemotherapy resistance in lung cancer. Key among these are the upregulation of DNA repair pathways, specifically nucleotide excision repair (NER) as well as homologous recombination (HR), which contribute significantly to resistance against platinum-loaded treatments [36]. In particular, the ERCC1 protein is correlated with resistance to cisplatin [37]. Additionally, ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transport proteins, including MRP1/ABCC1 and MRP3/ABCC3, exacerbate resistance by pumping drugs out of cells [38]. The presence of efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) in lung cell cancer was demonstrated by a study that assessed the impact of cigarette smoke extract (CSE) on P-gp activity in human lung-derived A549/P-gp cells [39]. P-gp is an efflux drug pump that is predominantly ATP-dependent and functions to actively remove a wide array of chemotherapeutic drugs from cells, thereby preventing drug accumulation, cytotoxicity, and drug resistance [40]. Overexpression of glutathione S-transferases, including GSTP1 and GSTA1, further enhances drug detoxification, promoting resistance [41]. Resistance is also driven by activated signaling pathways involving epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt), and Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [42], alongside antiapoptotic proteins including Bcl-2 [43]. Targeting these pathways, as well as inhibiting ATM and Bcl-2, may help restore sensitivity to treatment [44]. Elevated WNT/β-catenin signaling has been shown to drive resistance to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors in lung cancer [45, 46]. Using a PKC inhibitor to block WNT5A can decrease cell migration and invasion in lung cancer cells [47]. Additionally, increased expression of TWIST1, stimulated by WNT signaling, is associated with drug resistance, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) as well as metastasis. Targeting of the WNT pathway might offer a solution to overcoming this resistance [48]. Moreover, miRNAs influence drug resistance; for instance, miR-1 can enhance sensitivity to cisplatin [49], whereas other miRNAs such as miR-106a and miR-31 promote resistance by downregulating essential proteins. Addressing these miRNA alterations and resistance mechanisms could potentially improve the effectiveness of treatments and help manage resistance [50] (Table 1).

Current strategies to overcome chemoresistance in lung cancer

Chemoresistance in lung cancer remains a significant challenge, but ongoing research has led to several promising strategies to combat it [51]. Targeted therapy focuses on specific genetic mutations or alterations that contribute to tumor growth [52, 53]. For example, in many cases of lung cancer, there are mutations present in the EGFR gene [54]. To address these mutations, drugs such as Osimertinib have been developed to inhibit EGFR directly. However, the use of Osimertinib to treat NSCLCs has been hindered by chemoresistance [55]. Likewise, cancers with alterations in the ALK gene can be treated with ALK inhibitors such as alectinib and brigatinib. By targeting these genetic alterations specifically, targeted therapies can provide a more beneficial therapeutic approach with reduced adverse effects when compared to conventional chemotherapy [56]. Recent advances in nanotechnology have resulted in the creation of advanced nanoscale drug delivery systems that are both targeted and multifunctional[57]. These cutting-edge solutions improve the precision of drug delivery to tumors, helping to overcome drug resistance. For instance, magnetic nanoparticles can specifically target tumors, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity [11]. Park et al. sought to tackle multi drug resistant (MDR) tumors through nanodrugs with improved intracellular drug internalization. They achieved this by covalently attaching precise nano-sized carbon nanotubes to doxorubicin (DOX). The resulting carbon nanotube-conjugated DOX (CNT-DOX) persisted longer within MDR tumor cells, leading to mitochondrial damage, reduced ATP production, and increased therapeutic efficacy. Their study demonstrated that H69AR lung cancer cells, a DOX-resistant line, didn’t trigger drug resistance mechanisms against these nanodrugs. Ultimately, nanodrugs of a precise size, combined with DOX, successfully overcame MDR in tumors by selectively accumulating in tumor cells and preventing ATP production [58]. Paclitaxel, commonly used in various cancers, requires Cremophor EL (CrEL) and ethanol to enhance solubility due to its hydrophobicity, but CrEL can cause adverse reactions. To avoid these, paclitaxel albumin nanoparticle formulation (nab-paclitaxel) was developed, with enhanced performance and tolerance in NSCLC patients. It has been approved in the U.S. for treating advanced NSCLC [59]. Furthermore, photodynamic therapy (PDT) has become an effective treatment approach, with new methods using nanoparticles to deliver siRNAs that can inhibit genes associated with drug resistance. The application of biocompatible materials and RNA interference (RNAi) technologies also shows considerable promise [60]. Kaempferol enhances the effectiveness of PDT, mainly by acting as a photosensitizer. Due to its antioxidant properties, Kaempferol can absorb light and transfer energy to molecular oxygen, thus generating singlet oxygen, the major cytotoxic species responsible for tumor cell death during PDT [61]. Cancer immunotherapy stands out as an appealing treatment option compared to traditional methods like chemotherapy, targeted therapy and radiation therapy (RT), due to its potential for promising clinical outcomes [62]. Various immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), adoptive T-cell therapy, cytokine modulators, and cancer vaccines are FDA (The United States Food and Drug Administration)-approved for appropriate cancers [63]. ICIs targeting immune checkpoint proteins (ICPs) have shown stable responses, long-term survival benefits and elevated progression-free survival (PFS) in NSCLC patients [64]. At present, they see use in initial treatment, either independently or in conjunction with surgery, chemotherapy and radiation therapy. Approved ICIs for NSCLC include nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab and durvalumab, which inhibit PD-1 in T cells and inhibit PD-L in tumor cells, thus restoring antitumor immunity [65]. However, resistance to ICIs, either primary or acquired, is common. This resistance is associated with reduced PD-L1 and MHC protein expression, lower effector T cell levels and increased numbers of immune suppressor cells, especially in SCLC patients [66].

Combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy, radiation, or targeted therapies is the most effective strategy to combat drug resistance. These therapies work on different cells and pathways, so their combined use can enhance therapeutic effects without increasing the intensity of side effects. Numerous clinical trials are ongoing to investigate new treatment regimens, both as standalone therapies and in combination with chemotherapy and molecular targeted treatment, such as first-, second-, and third-generation EGFR-TKIs and ICIs [67, 68] (Fig. 1).

Kaempferol: a potential therapeutic agent in cancer anticancer mechanisms of Kaempferol

Kaempferol exhibits both antioxidant and anti-neoplastic effects. It has been shown to diminish cell proliferation and significantly reduce vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, which is a key indicator of angiogenesis in ovarian cancer cells. This compound impedes angiogenesis by interfering with the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-Nuclear factor-kappa B (NFκB)-cMyc-p21-VEGF signaling pathway [69]. Moreover, kaempferol influences cell cycle regulation, which is crucial for cellular homeostasis. It inhibits cell proliferation by modulating cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)−1 and cyclin B, which are involved in the transition from the G2 phase to the M phase, as well as regulating tumor suppressor genes like p53 and polo-like kinase (PLK)−1 in cancer cells [70]. Observation of Kaempferol reducing metastasis in U-2 OS human osteosarcoma cells by targeting the ERK-p38- c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase (JNK) and AP-1 signaling pathways has also been evidenced [71]. Additionally, kaempferol inhibits Src kinase, which affects MAPK activity, thereby impacting cell cycle regulation as well as angiogenesis through suppression of UVB-induced cyclooxygenase (COX)−2 [70].

Kaempferol reduces the mobility of breast cancer cells by halting cell division at the G2/M stage in MDA-MB-453 breast cancer cells. Similar findings were observed in triple-negative breast cancer cells. Researchers noted a remarkable decline in cell numbers during the G1 phase, dropping from 85.48 to 51.35% after kaempferol treatment, while the proportion of cells in the G2 phase rose to 37.5% from 9.27%. These results indicate that kaempferol can halt the cell cycle, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation [72]. The anti-neoplastic effects of kaempferol involve several mechanisms. These include triggering apoptosis, interfering with cell cycle progression, activating chloride channels in response to calcium, and inhibiting Anoctamin 1 (ANO1). Kaempferol also targets oncogenic protein kinase FA, which is influenced by proline and involved in inhibiting COX-2. Additionally, it suppresses glyoxalase 1, impacts neoplastic transformation and progression, blocks fatty acid synthase activity, modulates DNA methylation and downregulates DNMT3b, enhances the formation of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and induces the tumor suppressor PTEN protein [73]. Exposure of SCC-1483 cells to varying concentrations of kaempferol leads to dose-dependent apoptosis. This apoptotic effect was further validated by testing other cell lines, including SCC-QLL1 and SCC-25, which showed that kaempferol stimulates apoptosis in various malignant cell lines. An assay for caspase-3 function demonstrated that the apoptosis induced by kaempferol is dependent on caspase-3. Additionally, kaempferol markedly reduces the migratory and invasive capacities of SCC4 cells in a dose-dependent manner. At the highest tested concentration (100 µM), kaempferol inhibited MMP-2 enzyme function by up to 53% and lowered TIMP-2 protein levels. These findings were corroborated by mRNA expression analysis, demonstrating that kaempferol markedly downregulates MMP-2 mRNA expression in a concentration dependent manner [74]. Kaempferol directly facilitates the nuclear translocation of androgen receptors in androgen-dependent prostate cancer LNCaP cells. Moreover, kaempferol markedly boosts the interaction of androgen receptors in the PSA promoter region in vivo and concurrently enhances the transcriptional activity of these receptors. In contrast, kaempferol pretreatment notably reduces the negative effects induced by dihydrotestosterone, including the transcriptional activity of androgen receptors, cell proliferation in BPH-1, LNCaP, WPMY-1 and PSA and AR gene expression. Furthermore, kaempferol improves pathological conditions in BPH rats and lowers the prostate index. Upon administration of kaempferol, a substantial decrease in elevated serum T levels was observed in rats with benign prostatic hyperplasia. These findings indicate that kaempferol possesses androgenic-like properties and functions as a selective modulator of androgen receptors, potentially influencing androgen-related benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) growth [75]. Notably, kaempferol has been demonstrated to suppress glioma cell growth and migration when used in kaempferol-based nanoemulsions and mucoadhesive nanoemulsions (KPF-MNE). This flavonoid reduces the activity of superoxide dismutase and lowers thioredoxin levels, while simultaneously elevating the concentrations of several factors: cleaved caspase-3 and − 8, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, Bcl-2, anti-apoptotic proteins such as XIAP and survivin, as well as various chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines (including interleukin-6 and − 8). Additionally, kaempferol leads to mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization and a prompt decline in phosphorylation of ERK and AKT [76] (Fig. 2).

Challenges in Kaempferol therapy: bioavailability issues

Despite kaempferol’s promising therapeutic profile, a Major challenge in leveraging kaempferol effectively for cancer therapy is its inherently low bioavailability, which studies have documented at approximately 2% [77]. This limitation is pivotal to understanding and overcoming the obstacles in translating kaempferol from bench to bedside in oncology. Kaempferol exhibits limited solubility in water, which hampers its dissolution and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract when administered orally [72]. This reduced solubility significantly decreases the amount of kaempferol that reaches systemic circulation and thus it is unable to effectively and quickly destroy cancer cells. Once absorbed, kaempferol undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism in the liver. It is conjugated into various metabolites such as sulfates and glucuronides, reducing the concentration of the active parent compound in the bloodstream. This metabolic processing leads to a short half-life and reduced therapeutic efficacy [78]. Kaempferol also faces challenges with cellular permeability, impacting its absorption efficiency.

Efficacy of Kaempferol in lung cancer: focus on molecular mechanisms

Kaempferol has been shown to significantly inhibit the proliferation of NSCLC cells and induce autophagy, leading to increased cell death. Notably, Met expression was markedly downregulated at both the protein and mRNA level. Concurrently, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway was also inhibited. Furthermore, overexpression of Met significantly reversed the impact of kaempferol on NSCLC cell viability and autophagy. These findings, along with the involvement of the associated pathways, were further validated using a xenograft model [79]. Kaempferol has also been found to inhibit the Nrf2 signaling pathway in NSCLC cells by decreasing Nrf2 mRNA levels, which leads to apoptosis. This suppression of Nrf2 signaling is achieved without involving its usual inhibitor, Keap1. Furthermore, kaempferol treatment results in a significant reduction in cancer cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis in NSCLC cells. This dual effect highlights that kaempferol is able to provide treatment through the Nrf2 pathway, and is able to directly influence cell viability [80]. Further research has investigated the impact of kaempferol on lung cancer models implanted in animals. This included in vivo studies analyzing the composition of Gut bacteria using 16 S rDNA sequencing and evaluating its effects on immune cells. Kaempferol was found to effectively inhibit the growth of the xenograft lung cancer model. This inhibitory effect may be attributed to T cell, NK cell, and other immune cell activation, a process influenced by the gut microbiota [81]. Another study used a melting ultrasonic and electrostatic adsorption technique to create two types of hyaluronic acid (HA)-modified, kaempferol-based nanostructured lipid carriers (HA-KA-NLCs). The researchers assessed the antitumor efficacy of these carriers on A549 cells. The results demonstrate that HA-KA-NLCs had notable in vitro antitumor properties, including the inhibition of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Additionally, they promoted apoptosis while increasing the cellular uptake of A549 cells. HA-KA-NLCs were also found to activate signaling pathways associated with EMT and to regulate the levels of Vimentin, E-cadherin and N-cadherin in A549 cells. These results indicate that HA-KA-NLCs are a promising and efficient vehicle for delivering targeted treatment in cancer therapy [82].

Junctional adhesion molecule-like protein (JAML) has been recognized as an oncogenic factor and may provide a novel therapeutic target for lung adenocarcinoma. Decreases in JAML have been observed to reduce lung adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while also hindering EMT by disrupting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Moreover, JAML knockdown diminished the effectiveness of kaempferol in inhibiting lung adenocarcinoma cells. This indicates that kaempferol’s anticancer activity is likely mediated through its targeting of JAML [83].

Approaches to overcome the bioavailability limitations of Kaempferol

Various research has focused on the bioavailability limitations of kaempferol by exploring innovative drug delivery methods. One strategy involves the use of nanotechnology-loaded formulations. Preeti et al. (2023) [84] emphasized the development of nanoformulations to enhance kaempferol’s solubility and structural stability, to potentially improve its therapeutic efficacy and bioavailability. Carriers such as Poloxamer 407 have been reported by Colombo et al. (2019) [85] as effective in increasing kaempferol’s water solubility by up to 4000 times, which also led to a notable improvement in its dissolution rate and overall bioavailability. Another approach involved kaempferol nanosuspensions, produced using high-pressure homogenization. The nanosuspension of kaempferol demonstrateda notably reduced particle size compared to pure kaempferol, thereby improving its absorption and absolute bioavailability, with an observed increase from 13.03 to 38.17% [86]. PEGylation is the attachment of polyethylene glycol chains to drugs to increase solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Studies have shown that a PEGylated kaempferol formulation can enhance anti-tumor efficacy and reduce tumor size [87]. D-α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) plays a role as an emulsifier and as a p-glycoprotein inhibitor, and is important for drug absorption and increased bioavailability. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that a nanosuspension using D-α-tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) as a stabilizing agent not only improved bioavailability but also prolonged the drug’s antitumor activity with minimal toxicity. This formulation was particularly effective in enhancing kaempferol’s therapeutic potential in cancer treatment, showcasing its ability to increase intracellular drug retention and antitumor efficacy [88].

Types of nanocarriers for Kaempferol delivery

Nanotechnology holds great promise in revolutionizing drug delivery systems, enhancing therapeutic effects, and reducing side effects.​ Through new designs and engineering processes, nanoparticles can assist in precise targeting and delivery of drugs to intended sites, overcoming major drawbacks of conventional routes of drug delivery [89]. Several techniques have been established for the effective nanoformulation of kaempferol for increased bioavailability. One widely used method is solvent evaporation, in which kaempferol is dissolved in a suitable organic solvent and evaporated to form nanoparticles. This technique allows encapsulation of kaempferol into nanoparticle carriers, which can enhance its solubility and stability [90]. Nanoemulsions are another new formulation strategy, where kaempferol is solubilized as nano-sized droplets in an emulsifying agent, leading to enhanced solubility and uptake in biological systems [91].

Lipid nanoparticles

Lipid-based nanoparticles (LNPs) have shown potential as effective carriers for delivering hydrophobic drugs such as kaempferol [92]. These nanocarriers offer several advantages including enhanced bioavailability, increased drug loading capacity, improved stability, and water solubility, facilitating their scale-up for commercial production. Nanoliposomes, a specific type of LNP, exhibit high drug loading capacity and versatile administration routes, enabling controlled drug release [93]. LNPs possess a lipid matrix and are categorized into solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs). While SLNs have disadvantages in terms of drug leakage and low loading capacity, NLCs have overcome these challenges. Nanoliposomes, composed of phospholipid bilayers, can encapsulate a wide range of molecules due to their amphiphilic nature, allowing for the incorporation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds [93].

Huang et al. demonstrated the antioxidant capacity of kaempferol-based Liposomes by investigating their scavenging ability of the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical. Scavenging efficiency increased proportionally with kaempferol concentration, reaching a Maximum of 67.07% at 5% w/w. Additionally, the liposomal formulation exhibited protective effects against lipid peroxidation induced by a Fe3+/ascorbate system. However, a concentration-dependent effect on antioxidant activity was observed, with optimal inhibition of Lipid peroxidation at 3% w/w kaempferol. Higher concentrations (5% w/w) reduced antioxidant efficacy, possibly due to destabilizing effects on the liposomal bilayer [94].

Landi-Librandi et al. investigated the antioxidant properties of kaempferol-encapsulated liposomes in the context of neutrophil oxidative metabolism inhibition. The liposomal formulations, composed of phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol with or without oleyl ether (OET), exhibited varying degrees of radical scavenging activity, with IC50 values ranging from 9.03 to 35.35 µM for SPC: CHOL Liposomes and 4.79 to 24.41 µM for SPC: CHOL-OET liposomes. Notably, neither liposomal formulation demonstrated cytotoxicity toward human neutrophils [95] (Table 2). SLNs have been in use since the 1990 s as a variation of traditional delivery systems like liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles [96, 97]. SLNs have drawn significant interest in drug delivery applications because of their capacity to accommodate large quantities of drugs, and their ability to control drug release and protect the drug from degradation. These nanoparticles are formed by dispersing solid lipids in a water-based surfactant solution [97]. Aghazadeh et al. [98] investigated NLCs as a delivery system for kaempferol to augment its anticancer efficacy on MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells. Characterization of the formulated Kaempferol-based NLCs revealed optimal physicochemical properties. In vitro studies demonstrated a significant enhancement in cytotoxicity and induction of apoptosis by kaempferol when delivered via NLCs compared to the free drug state. Furthermore, kaempferol-based NLCs in combination with paclitaxel exhibited a synergistic antiproliferative effect. These findings suggest that NLC-mediated delivery of kaempferol may serve as a novel strategy for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of breast cancer treatments (Table 2).

Polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles (NPs) show promise for drug delivery due to their low toxicity, biodegradability, stability and a minimal inflammatory response [99]. The efficacy of polymeric NPs as nanocarriers is contingent upon a careful selection of polymer composition and the specific cargo being delivered [100, 101]. Biopolymers, composed of amino acids and nucleic acids, constitute the predominant class of polymeric nanocarriers owing to their inherent non-toxicity and biodegradability. A particular subset of polymeric NPs, micelles, have garnered significant attention in drug delivery applications. These structures consist of amphiphilic block copolymers. PEG-PLA micelles, in particular, have been widely experimented on as drug carriers due to their capacity to extend circulation time and improve anti-tumor efficacy [102]. Similarly, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs have demonstrated promising potential as drug delivery vehicles, characterized by their targeted delivery capabilities and controlled release profiles [103]. Kazmi et al. [104] developed kaempferol-based NPs using hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMC-AS) and Kollicoat MaE 30 DP as polymeric carriers via the quasi-emulsion solvent diffusion technique. Their optimized formulation was assessed for its hepatoprotective efficacy in a rat model of cadmium chloride-induced hepatocellular carcinoma. The fabricated NPs exhibited nanoscale dimensions, low polydispersity index and substantial drug loading capacity, coupled with a controlled release profile. Administration of KFP-NPs (50 mg/kg) orally for six weeks significantly attenuated serum levels of liver function markers (ALT, AST, TBiL) and lipid peroxidation (MDA) while concurrently elevating antioxidant enzymes (GST, GSH, SOD, CAT). Furthermore, KFP-NPs markedly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and NF-κB expression at the same time as upregulating the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway. Comparative analysis revealed that KFP encapsulated within NPs demonstrated superior hepatoprotective efficacy compared to free KFP. Collectively, these results indicate the ability of KFP-loaded NPs as a promising therapeutic strategy for oxidative stress-mediated injury (Table 2).

Gold nanoparticles

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have emerged as promising platforms for controlled drug delivery, enhancing bioavailability and mitigating adverse effects associated with conventional therapeutic approaches. Kaempferol can form conjugates with small gold nanoclusters (AuNCs). The effectiveness of synthesized Kaempferol-AuNCs as an anticancer treatment was tested on A549 lung cancer cells. These Kaempferol-AuNCs primarily targeted and caused damage to the cancer cell nuclei. The nanoclusters showed decreased toxicity towards normal human cells while exhibiting increased toxicity against A549 lung cancer cells, signifying their potential for the targeted delivery of anticancer drugs [53]. Consequently, there has been a growing interest in developing cost-effective and biocompatible green synthesis routes for AuNPs [105]. Several studies have explored the use of microorganisms and plant extracts for AuNP biosynthesis [106]. Previous research has reported the synthesis of AuNPs using an aqueous extract of Cissus quadrangularis (CQE) [107]. Given the presence of kaempferol within CQE, it is hypothesized that this flavonoid may contribute to the reduction of Au (III) ions to Au (0) nanoparticles. Furthermore, the anticancer properties of AuNPs synthesized from and utilizing plant extracts has been demonstrated, with studies reporting cytotoxicity in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells using broccoli extract [108]. Kaempferol-conjugated gold nanoclusters have demonstrated the capacity to target and eradicate cancer cell nuclei. Nanoformulations incorporating kaempferol have exhibited significant anticancer properties, characterized by reduced cell viability [106, 109], induction of apoptosis [98], nuclear damage [109], and increased LDH leakage. Furthermore, PEGylated AuNPs-DOX@kaempferol exhibited pronounced in vivo anticancer efficacy, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume [110]. These findings collectively support the potential of kaempferol-conjugated gold nanoclusters as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment (Table 2).

Advantages of Kaempferol-based nanocarriers over free Kaempferol

Nanocarriers such as nanosuspensions, nanoemulsions, and nanoparticles have been employed to improve the solubility and bioavailability of kaempferol. Nanocarrier systems can mitigate the potential toxicity associated with kaempferol by enabling targeted delivery to cancer cells, thereby minimizing exposure to healthy tissues. Kaempferol-coated nanosilvers have been synthesized and shown to induce cytotoxic effects in HepG2 cells with minimal impact on normal cells [111]. The therapeutic index, defined as the ratio between the toxic dose and the therapeutic dose of a drug, is a critical parameter in cancer treatment. Nanocarrier-mediated delivery of kaempferol has been shown to enhance its anticancer efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity, thereby improving its therapeutic index. In a study involving 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, kaempferol nanosuspensions exhibited a tumor inhibition rate of approximately 68.9% with no significant toxicity, indicating a favorable therapeutic index [88].

Limitations of nanocarriers

Nanocarriers have several benefits, including increased drug solubility, targeted distribution, and longer lifetime within circulation. However, despite their potential, major obstacles remain in terms of scalability, stability, and toxicity, which may restrict their applications in clinical settings. Thus, recognizing these limits is crucial for successfully integrating nanocarrier technologies into treatment procedures. As previously noted, the characteristics of nanocarrier materials may be altered across the transition from laboratory-scale syntheses to large-scale manufacturing processes, influencing metrics such as particle size and dispersion [112, 113]. Furthermore, typical nanocarrier synthesis methods may not be suited for large-scale manufacturing due to their complexity and expense. For example, procedures including high-pressure homogenization or solvent evaporation, which are common at the lab scale, encounter challenges when trying to sustain homogeneous particle size and distribution at the commercial scale. This variability has an impact on the consistency of drug loading and release patterns, which are crucial for patient treatment results [114]. Nanocarrier stability is another key issue in real-world applicationse. Stability problems include physical and chemical stability during storage and after delivery of the medication. These stability issues are worsened under physiological circumstances, where they might interact with biological fluids, causing fast disintegration and altered pharmacokinetics of the entrapped medicines. Furthermore, environmental conditions such as pH and temperature might compromise the integrity of the nanocarriers. The long-term mechanical and chemical durability of polymeric nanoparticles requires further investigation as they deteriorate when exposed to enzymes and different pH levels [92].

Biocompatibility is a significant factor when creating nanocarriers since they might cause harmful interactions in biological systems. Some studies have also revealed how the features of nanoparticles, including, size, surface charge, and composition determine their degrees of toxicity in vivo [115].

Effects of kaempferol-based nanocarriers in overcoming chemoresistance in lung cancer

Polymeric nanoparticles offer several benefits, including improved bioavailability, water solubility and efficient delivery of poorly water soluble compounds [116]. Govindaraju et al. [109] synthesized gold nanoclusters conjugated with kaempferol (K-AuNCs) and investigated their anticancer potential using A549 lung cancer cells. Their study evaluated the impact of K-AuNCs on cell proliferation, migration, and morphology. The synthesized K-AuNCs fluoresced strongly red in colour and were approximately 1–3 nm in diameter. Characterization using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed kaempferol was present, while the fluorescent properties were attributed to the gold nanocluster core. K-AuNCs demonstrated selective cytotoxicity towards A549 lung cancer cells, with minimal toxicity to normal human kidney (HK-2) cells. These nanoclusters induced morphological changes in cancer cells, including nuclear damage, as observed through bio-atomic force microscopy. Colony formation assays confirmed the anti-proliferative effects of K-AuNCs, while cell migration assays revealed their ability to inhibit cancer cell movement. These results indicate that K-AuNCs possess promising anticancer properties. The conjugation of kaempferol to gold nanoclusters enhanced the selectivity and efficacy of the drug delivery system. Moreover, the strong fluorescence of K-AuNCs suggests their potential for bioimaging applications. Another study on A549 cells assessed the anti-cancer effects of kaempferol combined solid lipid nanoparticles (KA-SLN). In this experiment morphological changes and the apoptosis level were evaluated. Compared to the control group, KA-SLN showed better anti-cancer effects, tumor cell apoptosis, tumor cell proliferation, migration, and colony formation. Moreover, controlled release of the drug was promoted after treatment with KA-SLN, while simultaneously reducing the toxicity of Kaempferol [117].

Ma et al. [118] developed NLCs loaded with kaempferol and coated with hyaluronic acid (HA-KA-NLCs) to target and treat lung cancer cells (A549). These nanoparticles were characterized and found to be safe for drug delivery. The drug was effectively encapsulated within the spherical NLCs. In vitro assessments revealed that HA-KA-NLCs exhibited pronounced antitumor activity against A549 cells, characterized by prevention of proliferation, migration, and invasion, as well as stimulation of apoptosis. Mechanistically, these formulations were found to modulate the EMT signaling pathway, as evidenced by alterations in the expression of key EMT markers. Collectively, these findings suggest that HA-KA-NLCs possess significant potential as a targeted therapeutic strategy for NSCLC and warrant further investigation for clinical translation (Table 3) (Fig. 3).

Future perspective

In order to translate these promising findings into clinically effective treatments, further research is required to address issues related to cost, scalability and long-term safety. While the potential represented by kaempferol-based nanomedicines is evident, significant hurdles remain, and more clinical and animal model studies are required. Addressing these challenges through ongoing research and development is crucial to harness the full therapeutic potential of kaempferol in the fight against lung cancer.

Conclusion

The escalating incidence of lung cancer, characterized by its aggressive nature and resistance to conventional therapies, underscores an urgent need for novel treatment strategies. A promising avenue involves the exploration of natural compounds, such as flavonoids, for their potential anticancer properties. Kaempferol, a flavonoid abundant in various fruits and vegetables, has emerged as a compelling candidate due to its demonstrated antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic and antimetastatic activities. The clinical introduction of kaempferol-based nanomedicines faces numerous regulatory and translational challenges, including complex preclinical safety, clinical safety and efficacy testing, quality control measures, and development of standardized protocols. To improve the therapeutic efficacy of kaempferol, researchers have focused on developing innovative drug delivery systems. Nanocarriers such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and gold nanoparticles have shown potential in addressing the challenges posed by kaempferol’s poor water solubility and bioavailability. These nanoformulations have shown improved drug delivery, which may enhance anticancer effects in preclinical lung cancer models.
