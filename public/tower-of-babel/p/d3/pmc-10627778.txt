Gynecological concerns and vaginal practices and exposures among transfeminine individuals who have undergone vaginoplasty
Authors: Jason Hallarn, Greta R Bauer, Emery Potter, Hannah Wilcox, Jacy Newfeld, Yonah Krakowsky, Jacques Ravel, Jessica L Prodger
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaginoplasty is a gender-affirming surgery that is medically necessary for some transfeminine individuals. Little research exists describing vaginal health after the initial recovery from surgery, and evidence-based guidelines for vaginal care practices are unavailable.AimThe study sought to describe self-reported gynecological concerns and vaginal care practices among transfeminine persons who have undergone vaginoplasty.MethodsA total of 60 transfeminine participants 18+ years of age, living in Canada, and who had undergone vaginoplasty at least 1 year prior were recruited through social media, community groups, healthcare provider referrals, and study recontact. Participants completed a cross-sectional, online questionnaire detailing demographics, gynecological concerns, and genital practices and exposures. Hierarchical clustering was used to group participants based on behavioral practices and exposures. Associations between clusters and gynecological concerns were assessed.OutcomesOutcomes included self-reported gynecological concerns within the past year, recent vulvar or vaginal symptoms (past 30 days), and behavioral practices/exposures, including douching with varied products and dilating.ResultsParticipants reported a variety of concerns in the past year, including urinary tract infection (13%) and internal hair regrowth (23%). More than half (57%) had experienced at least 1 recent vaginal symptom, most commonly malodor (27%) and vaginal bleeding (21%). Of participants, 48% were dilating weekly and 52% reported douching in the past 30 days. Four distinct clusters of vaginal practices/exposures were identified: limited exposures; dilating, no douching; dilating and douching; and diverse exposures. No significant associations between cluster membership and gynecological concerns were identified, though cluster membership was significantly associated with surgical center (P = .03). Open-text write-ins provided descriptions of symptoms and symptom management strategies.Clinical ImplicationsThe results provide insight for clinicians on common patient-reported gynecological concerns and current vaginal care practices and exposures, including symptom management strategies.Strengths and LimitationsThis was the first study to investigate vaginal health and genital practices/exposures among a community sample of transfeminine individuals. As participants self-enrolled for a detailed survey and swab collection, individuals experiencing concerns were likely overrepresented.ConclusionTransfeminine individuals reported a range of gynecological concerns outside of the surgical healing period. Genital practices/exposures varied across clusters, but no clear associations between clusters and symptoms were identified; instead, practice/exposure clusters were dependent on where the individual underwent vaginoplasty. There is a need for evidence to inform diagnostics, treatments, and vaginal care guidelines to support vaginal health.Keywords: vaginoplasty, vulvoplasty, neovagina, transgender persons, gender-affirming surgery, gynecology, health behavior, public health, epidemiology

Introduction

Transfeminine individuals (ie, transgender women or gender-diverse persons assigned male at birth) may undergo vaginoplasty, the gender-affirming surgical creation of a vaginal canal with vulvoplasty. Penile inversion vaginoplasty is the most common surgical method and is considered the gold standard technique.1-3 Full-depth penile inversion vaginoplasty involves the use of penile and scrotal tissue to create a vaginal canal with sufficient depth and width for desired penetration (if any). Some patients opt to undergo vulvoplasty only, in which a vulva is created either without, or with a very shallow, vaginal canal.

In 2009 to 2010 in Ontario, Canada, it was estimated that 15% of trans women had undergone vaginoplasty.4 This proportion has likely increased, as public provincial health insurance now covers vaginoplasties across Canada. Three surgical centers perform gender-affirming vaginoplasties, in Montreal, Toronto, and Vancouver.

Most research to date has focused on surgical complications, short-term outcomes, and postsurgical satisfaction. Estimates of postsurgical satisfaction have been consistently high, with a recent meta-analysis estimating 91% overall satisfaction.3 Surgical regrets, estimated at 2%,3 were low in comparison with surgical regret and dissatisfaction for other major surgeries such as total knee arthroplasty (~20%) or total hip arthroplasty (5%-13%).5-7 Surgical complications were relatively uncommon, with estimates of 1% for fistula, 11% for stenosis and/or strictures, 4% for tissue necrosis, and 3% for prolapse.3

Limited data exist describing long-term outcomes, gynecological concerns, vaginal care practices, or genital exposures (eg, products, sexual fluids) after vaginoplasty. Existing data indicate that some transfeminine individuals experience symptoms similar to those associated with bacterial vaginosis in the natal vagina (eg, discharge, malodor).8-10 However, clinical diagnostics and treatments based on the natal vagina are often ineffective.10,11

Due to a lack of evidence-based guidelines, vaginal care recommendations vary between surgical centers and healthcare providers. Dilating is a necessary practice to prevent vaginal stenosis, and most surgical centers recommend dilating weekly once a patient is 1 year postsurgery if they are not having regular receptive sex. Application of water-based lubricant to the dilator is recommended to facilitate dilation. However, some transfeminine individuals report challenges with dilating,8 and lubricant use may be associated with vaginal symptoms, as it has been shown to influence natal vaginal tissue health in cisgender women.12-14 While douching is associated with inflammation and negative health outcomes for the natal vagina,15,16 some healthcare providers recommend douching for neovaginal care. Some recommend douching daily, while others recommend douching only after dilation or penetrative sex; many recommend douching with water, while others recommend alternative douching solutions such as soap, saline, vinegar, or iodine solutions.17

The primary objective of this study was to describe self-reported gynecological concerns and practices/exposures related to vaginal health among transfeminine persons in Canada who were at least 1 year postvaginoplasty. The secondary objectives were to identify potential clusters of individuals with similar vaginal exposures and assess associations between those clusters and gynecological concerns.

Methods

Data source and sample

TransBiota is a study investigating the genital microenvironments of trans persons who have received gender-affirming medical care. Eligible transfeminine participants were Canadian residents 18 years of age or older who had undergone vaginoplasty at least 1 year prior. Research ethics board approval was obtained from Western University, and all participants were screened for eligibility and provided written informed consent. Participants were recruited online through social media and community groups, healthcare provider referral, and recontact of consenting Trans PULSE Canada18 participants. Participants were mailed a study kit with an instruction booklet and materials for vaginal sample self-collection. Once a week for 3 weeks, participants completed an online questionnaire and self-collected vaginal swab samples. This study analyzed data from the first questionnaire of 60 transfeminine participants.

Measures

All measures were self-reported. Demographic variables included gender identity, age, ethnoracial background, and province of residence. Surgical variables included year of surgery, surgeon or surgical center, surgical complications, concerns since vaginoplasty, and postoperative care practices within 30 days of surgery. For gynecological variables, participants were asked whether they had ever received a gynecological exam postsurgery, and whether it was for postoperative follow-up, a specific concern, or a routine checkup. Concerns since vaginoplasty included concerns with healing, problems with scar tissue, hypergranulation, bloody discharge, internal vaginal hair, urinary tract infection (UTI), or other concerns. Recent (defined as within the past 30 days) vaginal symptoms included odor, discharge, itching or burning, pain, or bleeding. For those currently receiving estrogen therapy, the number of years receiving estrogen hormone therapy was computed as the years from the date of starting estrogen and the date of questionnaire completion.

Genital practices and exposures were included in the survey based on plausible mechanisms for affecting the vaginal microenvironment, with a 30-day time frame used for all measures; systemic exposures were included in which vaginal microenvironment effects might be expected (eg, oral antibiotics). Practices/exposures included douching frequency, douching substance, dilating frequency, receptive sexual activity, lubricant use, hygienic practices, and local or systemic medications/treatments. Douching frequency and dilating frequency were each coded into 3 categories: none, less than weekly, and at least weekly. Hygienic practices included washing exterior genitals with soap and the use of hygienic wipes and were coded as binary variables (has done at least once vs not). Participants indicated whether they had taken pre- or probiotics, antibiotics, or steroid medications and the administration route. Each medication variable was coded as “yes” if the participant indicated oral or local vaginal use. Participants were asked if they had used any “other” hygienic practice; as some indicated use of antifungal treatment externally or vaginally, a corresponding binary variable was created.

Two open-text questionnaire items were included at the end of the survey: “What improvements would you like to see made to gynecological care as a result of this study or future studies?” and “Is there anything else you would like us to know?”

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted separately for those with full-depth vaginoplasty and those with vulvoplasty only. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering of genital practices/exposures among those with full-depth vaginoplasty was performed in R Version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing)19 using the hcut function from the factoextra package.20 AGNES was specified as the clustering function, Ward’s D2 method as the clustering algorithm, and Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity metric. Solutions with 3 to 7 clusters were assessed. Within-cluster sums of squares and average silhouette metrics were used to assess clusters. No clear elbow point or peak was identified, so dendrograms were assessed and interpretability was considered for solutions.21-23 The 4-cluster solution was ultimately chosen based on cluster separation and interpretability. Participants were assigned to their respective clusters and the data were exported to SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute),24 in which descriptive and analytic statistical analyses were performed. Frequencies, proportions, and exact 95% confidence intervals were computed. Kruskal-Wallis and Fisher’s exact tests were performed to assess associations between clusters and continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Responses to 2 open-text questions were summarized to describe unique experiences and perspectives relating to postsurgical symptoms and behaviors.

Surgical experiences and gynecological concerns

Years since vaginoplasty ranged from 1 to 19, with 59% of participants having had surgery within the prior 3 years. Participants underwent vaginoplasty in Montreal (n = 48), Toronto (n = 7), and Thailand (n = 1). Most did not report any surgical complications (85%). Revision surgeries for complications were for rectovaginal fistula (n = 1) and graft loss (n = 1), with regional skin grafts taken from the thigh in both cases.

Of this nonrandom sample of participants, 85% reported having at least 1 concern since their vaginoplasty and 61% reported a past-year concern. The most common concerns since vaginoplasty were healing (50%), hypergranulation (42%), and UTI (30%). Of 25 participants who experienced hypergranulation, 24 were treated with silver nitrate, by a medical practitioner, themselves, or another person. The most common past-year concerns were internal hair (23%), concerns with healing (16%), and UTI (13%). Of participants, 56% had experienced recent vaginal symptoms, the most common of which were odor (27%) and bleeding (21%). Additional surgical experiences and concerns are presented in Table 2.

Medical and genital practice/exposure histories

Almost all participants were currently receiving systemic estrogen hormone therapy (n = 55 of 56), and 48% were also receiving progestin. Most had received a gynecological exam since their vaginoplasty (91%), primarily for postoperative follow-ups, and less commonly for specific concerns or regular check-ups. The only sexually transmitted infection reported was human papillomavirus (n = 2); the human papillomavirus infection site (eg, oral, anal, vaginal) was not specified.

Genital practices/exposures were assessed for the past 30 days. Over two-thirds of participants reported using a dilator (70%), with half of all participants reporting at least weekly use (52%). Nearly half of the sample had douched (48%), with most reporting douching with water at least weekly. A total of 80% of the sample had engaged in receptive vaginal sex (with a partner or solo) at least once since vaginoplasty, 82% of whom had done so recently. The most common forms of receptive vaginal sex were using fingers or hands (48%) or silicone or latex (52%). Four participants had recently engaged in receptive vaginal sex with a partner’s genitals.

Description of genital practice/exposure clusters

Four distinct clusters of individuals, determined by recent genital practices and exposures, were identified. The first cluster (n = 7), limited exposures, included those with few vaginal exposures; no members reported douching, dilating, or having sex. The second cluster (n = 20), labeled dilating, no douching, was defined by no douching but relatively high frequencies of intravaginal insertion (ie, dilating or receptive sex) and lubricant use. The third cluster (n = 14), dilating and douching, was defined by frequent douching with water, frequent dilating, and lubricant exposure. The fourth cluster (n = 15), diverse exposures, was defined by diverse behavioral exposures, including douching with solutions other than water, intravaginal insertion, lubricant use, hygienic wipe use, and probiotic use. There were no statistically significant associations between clusters and recent symptoms or past-year concerns. Cluster membership was associated with the surgical center of participants’ vaginoplasty; almost all who had surgery in Toronto were assigned to the dilating, no douching cluster. Vulvoplasty only

Four participants indicated that they had vulvoplasty only, all conducted in Montreal without surgical complications. Table S1 describes characteristics of these participants. Three of 4 participants reported no recent symptoms, while 1 participant reported odor, pain, and bleeding. Two participants had no concerns since surgery, while 2 reported UTI and concerns with healing, 1 of whom also reported problems with scar tissue. Participants with vulvoplasty only did not provide information on gynecological concerns or behavioral practices in open-text responses.

Discussion

Vaginoplasty is an essential gender-affirming surgery for some transfeminine individuals, with high satisfaction and low regret rates. This study highlights common concerns beyond the surgical healing period and brings attention to areas for future research necessary to support long-term gynecological health in this patient population.

Consistent with existing literature, few surgical complications were reported.3,8 However, results highlight a number of postvaginoplasty concerns, such as internal hair regrowth and hypergranulation tissue. Several participants reported symptoms consistent with microbial dysbiosis among cisgender women, such as odor and discharge. The limited data available suggest that neovaginal microbiota differ considerably compared with that of the natal vagina.11,25 Studies are needed to identify optimal vs potentially harmful microbiota so that evidence-based diagnostics and treatments can be provided. For example, 1 participant reported that they found using vaginally administered probiotics improved their malodor. Probiotic use may be a treatment strategy that warrants further investigation, depending on the role of lactobacilli in the neovagina.26,27

Exploratory clustering analysis revealed 4 broad clusters that summarized the variation in vaginal care practices. Differences in douching practices may be partially explained by differences in recommendations across surgical centers. Specifically, the Toronto surgical center does not currently recommend routine douching, while the Montreal center typically does. Some participants provided a rationale for their douching practices in their write-in responses; 2 respondents limited douching because they found that it was related to malodor, while another reported that douching sometimes helped with malodor. Cluster membership was not associated with gynecological symptoms or concerns; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and presence of intracluster heterogeneity.

While recent receptive vaginal sexual activity, solo or with a partner, was common, only 4 of 56 participants with full-depth vaginoplasty reported receptive vaginal sex with a partner’s genitals. While not accounting for receptive anal sex, this finding is consistent with an Ontario study that reported only 3% and 4% of transfeminine participants had engaged in high-risk past-year receptive genital or anal sex, respectively.28 Together, these findings conflict with existing data reporting high HIV/sexually transmitted infection–related sexual risk among transfeminine populations.29,30 It is likely these differences are partially explained by sampling. While this study and the Ontario study targeted the general population of transfeminine persons, HIV studies have typically targeted high-risk subgroups.31

There were some limitations to this study. While the largest study on this topic to date, the relatively small sample size led to imprecise estimates. Additionally, the convenience sampling method, and the requirement for specimen self-collection, likely overrepresented individuals experiencing vaginal concerns. While we expect this overrepresentation to be less severe compared with clinical samples, the results are likely not generalizable to all transfeminine persons who have undergone vaginoplasty. There may have been some overlap in terms of healing outcomes during the first year postsurgery and past-year concerns for those who had surgery <2 years ago (n = 16). Finally, determination of full-depth vaginoplasty vs vulvoplasty-only procedures relied on participant write-ins; it is possible that some vulvoplasty-only participants were misclassified as having full-depth vaginoplasty.

No individuals who underwent vaginoplasty at the Vancouver surgical center participated in this study. This center began performing gender-affirming vaginoplasties in late 2019, so the number of potential recruitees was small. While the Toronto clinic also began performing vaginoplasties in 2019, practitioners from this clinic were on the study team and involved in study recruitment.

A major strength of this study was the nonclinical sample. Research to date has almost exclusively been conducted through surgical centers, which overrepresents individuals experiencing concerns or complications and influences which complications are reported. Additionally, this is the first study to provide detailed data on genital practices and exposures among transfeminine individuals, including vaginal care practices such as douching and dilating.

Conclusion

Vaginal concerns and symptoms after the initial postsurgical healing period were commonly reported among transfeminine individuals who had undergone vaginoplasty. Vaginal care practices and treatment strategies varied widely, highlighting the need for evidence-based guidelines regarding vaginal care and treatment of gynecological symptoms in this population. Studies are needed to identify etiologic agents of symptoms, so that effective diagnostics and treatments can be provided. Additionally, future studies should aim to determine the role of douching in maintaining vaginal health and treating gynecological symptoms.
