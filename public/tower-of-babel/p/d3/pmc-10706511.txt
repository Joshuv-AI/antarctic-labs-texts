Frequency and risk factors stratification of hypertension among the rural population of Bangladesh
Authors: Goutam Kumar Acherjya, Keya Tarafder, Debasish Dutta, Md. Golam Mortuza, Alok Kumar Sarkar, Nanda Lal Das, Mohammad Ali
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Hypertension is one of the major risk factors of premature morbidity and mortality in our daily clinical practice. Various studies carried out in the urban settings, but there is scarcity of epidemiological data regarding hypertension among the rural people in Bangladesh. Therefore, this current study has been designed to find out the frequency and risk factors stratification of hypertension among the rural people in Jashore, Bangladesh.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study recruited 1812 participants above 18 years attending on national hypertensive week of 2019 in Bagherpara and Keshabpur upazila (subdistrict) health complex in Jashore, Bangladesh. 2020 International Society of Hypertension Global Hypertension Practice Guidelines had been demonstrated to classify hypertension.Results:Out of the total study population, the frequency of hypertension was 20.6% (Grade 1 and Grade 2 hypertensive patients 15.8% and 4.9%, respectively), and high normal blood pressure was 9.0%. The mean age of the study population, Grade 1 hypertensive and Grade 2 hypertensive cohorts were 42 ± 16, 49 ± 15 and 51 ± 14 years, respectively, with a male and female ratio was 1:2. Progressive rise of mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure were noticed with increasing age. Age (P: <0.001), sex (P: 0.004), occupation (P: <0.001), BMI (P: <0.001), family (P: <0.001) and past history (P: <0.001) of hypertension, sedentary life style (P: 0.004), additional salt intake (P: <0.001) and smoking (P: 0.011) were significantly associated with hypertension following bivariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that age after 50 years (AOR = 1.866, 95% CI: 1.210-2.876), positive past history of hypertension (AOR = 3.493, 95% CI: 2.676-4.558), additional salt intake (AOR = 0.591, 95% CI: 0.453-0.770) and obesity (AOR = 3.389, 95% CI: 1.830-6.274) were significantly associated with developing hypertension.Conclusion:High frequency of hypertension was found among the rural population in Bangladesh where presence with a lot of significantly associated risk factors. The data would be helpful for the health policymakers dealing noncommunicable diseases to reach the sustainable goal and mitigate morbidity and mortality of cardiovascular diseases in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Hypertension, one of the major contributors of noncommunicable diseases, constitutes a global public health problem owing to its high prevalence in all the territories.[1] Hypertension is the most prevalent leading cause of cardiovascular disease and premature morbidity and mortality throughout the world. The global burden of hypertension was 31.1% of adults (1.39 billion) in 2010, which would be projected 60% of adults (1.56 billion) in 2025,[2] whereas the overall prevalence of hypertension is dramatically increasing especially in low- and middle-income countries due to changing variation in modifiable risk factors of hypertension including high sodium intake, low potassium intake, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, sedentary lifestyle, urbanization and western pattern feeding practice. Despite of adequate public awareness, adherence to medication, hypertension monitoring and control are notorious issues in the developing countries including Bangladesh.[3]

Now a days, hypertension is one of the major components of chronic noncommunicable diseases which constitutes enormous burden of morbidity and mortality even in case of the younger population. Moreover, hypertension denotes as a most important modifiable risk factor for chronic heart diseases, chronic renal failure, stroke and peripheral vascular diseases both in elderly and younger population, and developed and developing countries.[1,345] Several risk factors potentially act as a spider web of predisposing to hypertension. However, it is previously thought that hypertension is more prevalent among the urban population due to mechanization, sedentary lifestyle and dietary changes. But the prevalence of hypertension is more or less same in the rural population owing to digitalization, urbanization, increasing elderly population and changing life style.[6,7]

In the recent couple of years, the South-East Asian countries including Bangladesh have an experience of increasing burden of hypertension along with cardiovascular diseases.[8] Additionally, hypertension considers as an important health issue in the Asian region affecting more than 35% of the adult population according to the World Health Organization.[9] Therefore, Bangladesh has recently given emphasis on an epidemiological transition from communicable diseases to noncommunicable diseases. Out of the different condition of non-communicable diseases hypertension, the “Silent Killer” acts as a pivotal role of long-term morbidity and mortality. Literally, rapid urbanization, increased aged people, unhealthy feeding practice and dramatic changes in western pattern lifestyle act as robust factors for increasing rate of noncommunicable diseases including hypertension in Bangladesh.[10,11] The overall prevalence of hypertension among the adult people in Bangladesh was reported 26.4% to 40.7% in some recent surveys.[12-14] Most of the study was carried out in the urban population in Bangladesh. Therefore, this is the high time to point focusing the noncommunicable diseases including hypertension among the rural population of Bangladesh. However, the rural population have not yet properly been focused earlier in Bangladesh especially in its South-East region. As there is scarcity of data of hypertension among the rural population in South-west region of Bangladesh, we have designed this cross-sectional study to assess the frequency and risk factors association of hypertension among the rural adult population in two sub-districts of Jashore, Bangladesh.

Materials and Methods

Study design and settings

This is a cross-sectional study conducted during national hypertension week of 2019 in two upazila (subdistrict) health complexes named Bagherpara and Keshobpur in the district of Jashore, Bangladesh. Upazila health complex is treated as primary health care centre in Bangladesh where the rural population attended for seeking their disease management.

Study sample and participants

The attended patients above 18 years aged during national hypertension week, 2019 in Bagherpara and Keshobpur upazila (subdistrict) health complex, Jashore, Bangladesh, were recruited in the cross-sectional study. The patients who had not given written consent, mental illness or chronic disabled patients were excluded from our study.

Study questionnaire and data collection

Modified and validated WHO stepwise approach to chronic disease risk factors surveillance (STEPS) was used as a tool for data collection of this study. A standard questionnaire was developed both in English and native language Bengali. Preformed questionnaire had specially been demonstrated in the fields including sociodemography, physical examination and data related to hypertension. The sociodemography aspect consists of age, sex, occupation, marital status and wealth status indicating by per month family income. Physical examination was carried out to measure height, weight and blood pressure. Physical activity (at least thirty minutes per day for five days in a week considered), using added salt, family history of hypertension, past history of hypertension, smoking history, history of established diabetes were also asked during data collection in relation to hypertension. Data collection was demonstrated by the well-trained data interviewer. Blood pressure of every patient was monitored by the registered physician. Quality controlled and cross check of each data had been performed by every author involving in this study.

Anthropometric and blood pressure measure

The participants were explained details about the purpose and their role in the study. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg by using an electronic weighing scale (Sinbe Model No, SBS 4414, and made in People’s Republic of China) in light clothing without having footwear. Standing height of the participants without footwear was measured by the stadiometer scale to its nearest millimetre. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilogram divided by the square of body height in meter. The participants were classified by BMI (weight in kg/height in meter2) groups as obesity (BMI ≥ 30), overweight (BMI = 25-29.9), healthy weight (BMI = 18.5-24.9) according to the partial update of CG43 London: National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (UK) and National Clinical Guideline Centre, 2014.

Registered physicians were involved to measure blood pressure by using well-validated aneroid sphygmomanometer with standard sized arm cuffs. The enrolled participants were asked for having complete rest for 10 minutes prior to measure blood pressure. These were also cross checked that they had not had taken part in strenuous exercise, tea, coffee or smoking within thirty minutes before checking blood pressure. The participants were allowed to seat comfortably on chair resting arm at the level of their heart during measuring blood pressure. Subsequently two measurements were recorded between two minutes interval. The average of the two recording was considered as ultimate blood pressure of each patient.

Ethical clearance and institution approval

The study had not demonstrated any intervention and drug trial, additionally, there is no ethical approval board existing in our territory. Therefore, we had completed our study seeking institutional approval from Civil Surgeon Office, Jashore, Bangladesh.

Patients and public involvement

We had recruited each participant after acquisition of informed consent. Before enrolment, we had briefing detail about the purpose of the study and their role in the study. This was assured that their data would not be handed over to any third parties. Their data would only be used in the research purpose. But it was also explained that they had not any role in the designing, data collection, analysis and result of the study. The result of the study would be provided by the correspondent author though mail if any of the participants interest to know.

Case definition of hypertension

We used 2020 International Society of Hypertension (ISH) Global Hypertension Practice Guidelines to find the frequency of hypertension in our study. According to the ISH Global Hypertension Practice Guideline, different stages of blood pressure were classified as a) Normal BP: systolic BP < 130 mmHg and diastolic BP < 85 mmHg, b) High-normal BP: systolic BP 130-139 mmHg and/or diastolic BP 85-89 mmHg, c) Grade 1 hypertension: systolic BP 140-159 mmHg and/or diastolic BP 90-99 mmHg, and Grade 2 hypertension: systolic BP ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 100 mmHg. The participants who were consuming antihypertensive medications by registered physicians considered as hypertensive patients in this study.[15]

Risk covariate of hypertension

The followings variables such as age, sex, occupation, marital status, wealth status, smoking, added salt, BMI, physical activity, family history of hypertension and diabetes were used as covariate to analyse the risk factors of hypertension. We had categorized age according to the decade; occupation by farmer, housewife, business, service and student. Positive physical activity was considered who used to walk or exercise at least 30 minutes per day for five days in a week. We had calculated BMI by weight in kilograms divided by height in meter square which was finally categorized by the following groups such as obesity (BMI ≥ 30), overweight (BMI = 25-29.9), healthy weight (BMI = 18.5-24.9) and underweight (BMI ≤ 18.5).

Statistical analysis

Chi-square test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) used to extract P value and logistic regression analysis was employed to evaluate risk factors analysis among the different groups of the study population.

Results

Out of the total study population, the frequency of hypertension was 20.6% where grade 1 and grade 2 hypertensive patients were 15.8% and 4.8%, respectively. Age (P- <0.001), sex (P- <0.004), occupation (P- <0.001), BMI (P- <0.001), positive family history of hypertension (P- <0.001), past history of hypertension (e.g. gestational hypertension) (P- <0.001), diabetes (P- <0.001), physical inactivity (P- <0.001), additional salt intake (P- <0.001) and smoking history (P- <0.011) were significant risk factors of developing hypertension in our study. Frequency of high normal blood pressure was found 9.0% of the study population [Table 1]. Frequency of hypertension was significantly increased with increasing age and BMI which had distributed in the linear curve. Mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure increased with increasing age of the participants [Figures 1-4].

Multivariate regression analysis was performed to explore the risk factors showing with more the age risk of development of HTN is more. In 5th decade, risk of the development of HTN is significantly 1.866 times higher than 3rd decade (P: 0.005) and in 6th decade or more it is 2.106 times higher than 3rd decade (P: 0.001). Other significant risk factors of developing HTN are positive past history of HTN and taking added salt in meal and obesity. Subjects with positive past history of HTN have 3.493 times higher risk of development of HTN (P: >0.001) than normal subjects with no past history of HTN. Similarly, subjects who took added salt in food had 0.591 times higher risk of developing HTN (P: >0.001) than subjects who did not took any added salt in their meal. In this study, over weight subjects had 1.975 times more risk of developing HTN (P: 0.001) than normal subjects. Similarly, obese subjects had significantly 3.389 times higher risk of developing HTN. Surprisingly, no significant risk on developing HTN observed in smokers or ex-smokers, though smokers had 1.073 times higher risk of developing HTN than non-smokers which is not significant (P: 0.730). Also, diabetes had no impact on development of HTN as a risk factor (P: 0.154). No gender impact as a risk factor also observed (P: 0.107). Marital status, positive family history of hypertension, daily physical activity and occupational status had no significant impact as risk factors in development of HTN [Table 2].

Discussion

Hypertension is a “Silent Killer” considered as one of the major risk factors of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Therefore, hypertension is literally associated with stroke, ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, eye disease, chronic kidney disease and peripheral vascular disease.[16-19] Once it was postulated that noncommunicable diseases including hypertension superseded the communicable diseases. It is also now believed that hypertension is prevalent in both developed and underdeveloped countries as well as urban and rural territories in the same region.[1,2]

In this recent study, we found high frequency of hypertension even among the rural population which constituted 20.6% where grade 1 and grade 2 hypertensive patients were accounted as 15.8% and 4.8%, respectively. We also found high frequency of high normal blood pressure as 9.0% of the total participants who are at high risk of developing frank hypertension in the near future. The same prevalence of hypertension (20.0%) was found by the national NCD Risk Factor Survey of Bangladesh conducted by the Bangladesh society of Medicine supported by the Directorate General of Health Services and the WHO Country Office for Bangladesh in 2010.[20] A little bit lower frequency of hypertension (17.9%) was found in another recent study conducted in Bangladesh.[13] Several recent studies conducted in Bangladesh reported higher frequency of hypertension such as 26.4%,[11] 30.0%[12] and 65.0%.[9] Different frequency had been reported in the other parts of the world such as Turkish adult (44.0%),[1] global burden in 2000 (26.4%),[2] global burden in 2020 (31.1%),[3] Asian population (15-35%),[7] Indian population (more than 11%, 39.2%),[6,21] South Asian population (27%).[8] However, merely the rural population of the different countries had shown at the different frequency of hypertension such as 34.1% in Nepal,[22] 31.5% in India[23] and 30.6% in Bangladesh[24] which are higher compared to our study.

We had enrolled our eligible participants above 18 years who attended to our study places during study period through non-purposive sampling method, so there might have some biasness in the different variables including gender issue (male and female ratio 1:2). The significant high frequency of hypertension was reported among our female participants compared to the men and this finding was similar to other studies published in the different parts of the world.[1,8,11-13,20] The global data analysis of hypertension in 2000 expected that hypertension would be more prevalent among the female gender in 2025.[2] Like other studies, this current study reported that increased age is highly associated with increased risk of hypertension among the rural population. Following multivariate logistic regression of risk factors, it was also noticed more frequency of hypertension after the age of 5th decade of life in our study. Therefore, the study reported increased age was significantly associated increased propensity to develop hypertension. Age more than 60 years group (two times) and male gender (nearly one and half times) higher risk of developing hypertension which were consisted with the other studies representing both national and international data.[1,4,8,9,11-13,17,21,22] Another study reported that hypertension and its consequence stroke occurred at younger age group as a part of Asian ethnicity.[7] Female predominant studies of hypertension among the Bangladeshi and Indian population were also recently reported.[11,20] In 2021, one published study named SHASTO baseline survey reported that hypertension had more prevalent among the women like our study.[24] We noticed that positive family history of hypertension had a mild positive impact on development of hypertension consisting with the other study.[1] However, positive family history of NCDs like hypertension was not supported by the recently published study on the rural population of India.[23]

The participants were previously diagnosed as hypertension, but they discontinued antihypertensive medications and now regarded as normotensive enrolled in our study. Interestingly, the presence of positive past history of hypertension had significantly more than three times higher risk of developing hypertension. Monthly income, marital status and diabetes had no positive effect as risk factors on the development of hypertension. Many of the studies reported that diabetes acted as potential risk of hypertension, but we did not find such pobably due to sampling method.[7,11] However, we found that those who were diabetic had slightly higher chance of developing hypertension in our study.

In our study, smokers had higher risk of developing hypertension than nonsmoker and ex-smoker groups though this was not significant following multivariate logistic regression. Another study reported where nonsmoker group were at more risk of developing hypertension.[1] Our study was pertinent with the other study reported from India where smoking was significant after bivariate analysis but not significant following multivariate analysis.[20] Though smoking had a questionable impact on hypertension following multivariate logistic regression analysis possibly due to the sampling or other biasness in our study, the recent study on rural population from India had shown a significant association between smoking and hypertension.[23] In relation to occupation with hypertension, we noticed that it was not significantly associated with hypertension following multivariate logistic regression. However, business man, farmer and housewife were more chance of developing hypertension than student and service holder groups in our study. Following multinomial regression analysis, occupation was not also significantly associated with hypertension in the other study.[20] Although we placed BMI to see the relationship with hypertension, we reported that overweight and obese people were strongly associated with hypertension in both bivariate and multivariate analysis in our study. Overweight and obese people were nearly two times and more than three times respectively higher chance of developing hypertension than underweight and healthy BMI group of people. These findings were similar to the recently published studies from the different countries of the world.[1,3,7,11,20-22] Therefore, the obese group of the population should specially be focused during implementing hypertension prevention program in our country.

Conclusion

In this current study, we noticed high frequency of hypertensive patients even among the rural people in Bangladesh. The potential risk factors of hypertension among the rural people were age, sex, BMI, occupation, family history of hypertension, previous history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, physical inactivity, additional salt intake and smoking. Therefore, as the frequency of hypertension of the rural population is increasing as a similar pattern of the urban area, the policymakers of this country should pay special attention to the rural people alongside the urban residents for primary prevention of hypertension and web of treatment action plan.
