Neuropsychiatric disorders in pulmonary fibrosis: from brain network alterations to inflammatory mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Chengwei Li, Longfei Wang, Fei Wang, Lezhen Wang, Lin Xu, Wenyuan Lyu, Kaiyue Shan, Jianjun Li, Haipeng Zhou, Fei Rong, Xiangyi Kong, Penghui Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary fibrosis encompasses a spectrum of chronic lung diseases characterized by progressive scarring of lung tissue, ultimately leading to respiratory failure. Emerging clinical evidence indicates that pulmonary fibrosis patients frequently experience comorbid neuropsychiatric disorders, including cognitive impairment, anxiety, and depression. These complications significantly impact patients’ quality of life and treatment outcomes. However, the mechanisms underlying this comorbidity remain incompletely understood, and there is a lack of effective interventions. This review summarizes the latest clinical evidence linking pulmonary fibrosis to neuropsychiatric complications and contributes to the understanding of the underlying mechanisms through the lung-brain axis theory. Studies indicate that various pulmonary fibrosis subtypes, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and pneumoconiosis, exhibit high prevalence of neuropsychiatric disorders and abnormal brain networks, indicating a close pathophysiological connection between pulmonary fibrosis and neuropsychiatric complications. At the mechanistic level, accumulating experimental evidence suggests that pulmonary fibrosis may induce central nervous system damage through three interrelated pathways: (1) Systemic dissemination of pulmonary inflammation leads to blood-brain barrier disruption and microglial activation; (2) Chronic hypoxia exacerbates neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction; (3) The inhaled particulate matter enters the brain from the olfactory mucosa through the olfactory bulb or crosses the damaged alveolar capillary barrier, enters the circulation, and then passes through the already damaged blood-brain barrier, inducing local neurotoxicity. Furthermore, we advocate for actively exploring a comprehensive treatment model that shifts from “lung-focused therapy” to “lung–brain co-therapy”. This approach employs multiple interventions—including medication, pulmonary rehabilitation training, and cognitive training—to prevent or delay systemic complications, including neuropsychiatric damage, in pulmonary fibrosis patients. Elucidating the lung-brain axis in pulmonary fibrosis may open new avenues for a more comprehensive understanding, early intervention, and holistic management of this devastating disease.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a chronic, irreversible lung disease characterized by progressive proliferation of fibrous tissue in the pulmonary interstitium and destruction of normal alveolar architecture. The pathological core of PF is abnormal activation of fibroblasts and excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to loss of lung tissue compliance, gas exchange disorder, and eventually progression to respiratory failure [1]. PF can be observed in various clinical conditions, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and secondary PF arising from diverse etiologies such as connective tissue diseases, allergic pneumonias, occupational dust exposure, drug-induced injury, and radiation exposure [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that the overall incidence of interstitial lung diseases (ILD) ranges from 1 to 31.5 per 100,000 person-years, with a prevalence of 6.3 to 71 per 100,000 individuals. This prevalence has been increasing over time, imposing a huge burden on global healthcare systems and represents a major public health challenge worldwide [3–5].

Traditionally, clinical attention in PF has focused on respiratory symptoms— characterized by progressive exertional dyspnea, dry cough, and decreased exercise tolerance—and the resulting hypoxemia, pulmonary hypertension, and right heart failure. However, in recent years, emerging clinical evidence indicates that patients with PF frequently experience comorbid neuropsychiatric disorders, including cognitive impairment, anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances [6, 7]. These neuropsychiatric disorders impair patients’ quality of life and daily functioning, and lead to decreased treatment adherence and increased hospitalization rates. However, this “hidden” disease burden remains underappreciated in clinical practice, and due to the lack of a systematic explanation for the neuropsychiatric disorders in PF, there are no clearly effective treatments or preventive measures.

The most widely accepted theoretical mechanism for this comorbidity is the hypoxia hypothesis. However, it was found that after improving cerebral oxygenation in patients with IPF through pulmonary rehabilitation, although patients’ exercise capacity and physical activity level improved, there was no significant improvement in cognitive scores [8]. These findings suggest that although hypoxia is an important factor, it cannot fully explain the co-occurrence of neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with PF, indicating the potential involvement of other related mechanisms.

In recent years, the proposal of the “lung-brain axis” has provided a novel perspective for understanding this phenomenon [9]. The lung-brain axis refers to a complex bidirectional communication network established between the lungs and the brain via neural, immune, microbial, and hypoxia-related signaling pathways [10]. Accumulating evidence supports the existence of the lung-brain axis, through which inflammatory factors, metabolites, and hormones can influence central nervous system (CNS) function and subsequently affect the cognitive function [9, 11–15]. Introducing the lung-brain axis perspective into clinical and basic research on PF may provide a crucial theoretical key to unraveling the association between PF and neuropsychiatric comorbidities [16, 17].

Overview and classification of pulmonary fibrosis

PF represents a common pathological endpoint of various chronic lung diseases, arising from multiple endogenous and exogenous etiologies [1]. The core pathological mechanism involves a disruption of homeostasis during lung injury and repair, leading to excessive remodeling and aberrant deposition of ECM within the pulmonary parenchyma. This process ultimately results in irreversible structural damage and functional impairment of lung tissue [1, 18]. Clinically, PF manifests as progressive dyspnea, often accompanied by hypoxemia. Notably, “progressive PF” is typically defined by persistent decline in lung function, radiological extension of fibrotic lesions, or worsening clinical symptoms over a 6–12 months period despite poor response to conventional therapies. It serves as a critical determinant of mortality and complications in patients with PF [18, 19]. Importantly, regardless of etiology, progressive PF may exhibit a stronger association with neuropsychiatric disorders compared to non-progressive PF, owing to the accompanying chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammatory response, and greater disease burden [6]. Based on etiology, PF is primarily classified into idiopathic, secondary, and hereditary forms [20]. This section focuses on four subtypes most relevant to lung-brain axis research: IPF, pneumoconiosis, autoimmune disease-associated PF, and viral infection-associated PF.

IPF is the most representative subtype and constitutes one of the most common ILD with the poorest prognosis [21, 22]. IPF is a chronic, progressive, fibrosing interstitial pneumonia of unknown cause, characterized by progressive scarring of lung tissue. Its pathogenesis is closely associated with aging processes, where telomere dysfunction and cellular senescence play critical roles [23, 24]. Histopathologically, IPF features aberrant proliferation, accumulation, and excessive secretion of ECM proteins (e.g., type I/III collagen, fibronectin) by activated lung fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, leading to gradual replacement of normal alveolar structures with dense scar tissue. This results in significantly reduced lung compliance and progressive functional decline, with a median survival of 3–5 years. Currently, pirfenidone and nintedanib can slow IPF progression but cannot reverse established lung damage; lung transplantation remains the most effective treatment but is limited in availability [25, 26]. Pneumoconiosis represents a classic category of occupational PF induced by long-term inhalation of inorganic dusts (e.g., silica, asbestos), primarily including silicosis and asbestosis [27–30]. The fibrotic process is closely linked to persistent dust particle deposition within the lungs, provoking chronic inflammation and aberrant repair responses [16]. Autoimmune disease-associated PF is mainly observed in connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD), such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and systemic lupus erythematosus [31–33]. This form of PF is closely related to autoimmune inflammation and represents a major pulmonary manifestation of systemic autoimmune diseases. Viral infection-associated PF has drawn increasing attention in the context of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2. Its pathogenesis involves virus-induced epithelial injury, cytokine storm, and aberrant repair processes, ultimately leading to excessive ECM deposition and progressive pulmonary scarring [34, 35]. Similar forms of PF have also been reported following other respiratory virus infections, including influenza A virus (H1N1), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus [36].

Neuropsychiatric disorders commonly observed in PF patients include depression, anxiety, and cognitive dysfunction [6, 37]. A final diagnosis of functional disorders can be established by first systematically excluding potential organic etiologies that may present with psychiatric symptoms through a comprehensive approach integrating detailed history taking, mental state and neurological examinations, and adjunctive laboratory tests—including neuroimaging and electroencephalography—and subsequently applying the standardized diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [1, 6, 38]. The development of these disorders is closely linked to chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and disease burden [24, 39]. Existing evidence suggests that systemic and central inflammation play important roles in the pathogenesis of PF-associated neuropsychiatric disorders. Animal models and clinical observations indicate that anti-inflammatory therapy (glucocorticoids) or anti-fibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib) may indirectly improve depressive symptoms by reducing circulating levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)) [40, 41]. Therefore, attenuating inflammatory processes may improve neuropsychiatric outcomes, and combining anti-inflammatory strategies with psychological and behavioral interventions may represent a more optimal approach (Fig. 1).

Epidemiological evidence and brain network substrates of PF-related neuropsychiatric disorders

Epidemiological evidence

The high prevalence of neuropsychiatric comorbidities in patients with PF has been confirmed by a number of epidemiological studies. Among patients with PF, cognitive impairment is a relatively common neuropsychiatric comorbidity. Vastag et al. compared 45 patients with fibrotic ILD and 32 non-ILD participants, finding that the mean Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was lower in the ILD group than in the non-ILD group (23.9 vs. 26.8; p < 0.001), and the occurrence rate of MMSE < 24 was 33.3% and 12.5%, respectively [42]. This conclusion was corroborated in another cross-sectional study [43].

As the most prevalent form of PF, IPF has been the focus of similar studies. Study indicated that the cognitive scores of IPF patients were significantly lower than those of the control group [37, 44]. Notably, even mild IPF patients exhibited decreased cognitive scores, highlighting the need for attention to the prevention and management of cognitive impairment from the early stages of PF [45]. It is also important to recognize that IPF, the most extensively studied form of PF, is strongly associated with aging [46]. Aging itself is a major risk factor for cognitive decline, independent of pulmonary disease [47–49]. Therefore, some of the neuropsychiatric manifestations observed in PF patients may reflect an interaction between disease-specific mechanisms and age-related vulnerability of the brain. Disentangling these contributions requires carefully designed studies with age-matched controls and longitudinal follow-up. A study in patients with asbestosis/silicosis indicated that the prevalence of the mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and cognitive impairment in pneumoconiosis patients (38.7%, and 11.3%) were significantly higher than those of the community referents (29.3%, and 7.2%) [50]. In addition, a meta-analysis indicated that environmental factors such as particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide, aluminum, and solvents are associated with an increased risk of dementia. It is noteworthy that this review mentioned that the association between “silicon” exposure and dementia did not reach statistical significance, but this primarily pertains to general environmental exposure levels [51], and further studies are needed to confirm this association in patients with silicosis. In addition to cognitive impairment, patients with PF also experience anxiety and depression. In a cross-sectional study, the prevalence of depression among patients with ILD was 23%, while the prevalence of anxiety reached 31% [52]. In IPF patients, the prevalence of prolonged anxiety and depression can reach 21% and 14%, respectively [53]. Furthermore, pneumoconiosis patients have also been found to have a significantly increased risk of depression [54]. A cohort study from the Guangzhou Biobank (2014) systematically assessed 1,612 retired workers, providing early large-scale epidemiological evidence in this field. The findings revealed that high-concentration silica exposure was significantly associated with depression symptoms (odds ratio [OR] = 3.12, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.17–8.31) and anxiety symptoms (OR = 5.29, 95% CI 1.76–15.92) [55]. Although this study focused on occupationally exposed individuals rather than confirmed silicosis patients, these findings still provide suggestive evidence for the association between occupational exposure-related PF and anxiety/depression. Available studies indicate that the prevalence of anxiety in patients with PF ranges from approximately 9% to 75%, with the most commonly reported range from 20% to 40%. Similarly, the prevalence of depression shows marked heterogeneity across studies, ranging from 6.7% to 75.2%, with the majority of studies reporting rates between 15% and 40%. The wide variation in reported prevalence of depression and anxiety likely reflects methodological heterogeneity. First, the assessment instruments differ across studies (e.g., the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‑7, the Beck Depression Inventory), which vary in their sensitivity and specificity to screen for depression and anxiety [77–80]. Second, studies vary considerably in disease severity and illness duration, both of which are known to influence mood disorder prevalence [69]. Third, most studies recruited participants from specialty clinics, introducing potential referral bias. Notably, these reported prevalence rates are broadly comparable to those observed in other chronic diseases. For example, in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the reported prevalence of depression ranges from 10% to 80%, and anxiety from 6% to 74% [81]. Among hospitalized patients with heart failure (HF), depression rates range from 13.9% to 77.5% [82–84]; although studies on anxiety are relatively limited, evidence suggests that up to 40% of patients with PF may suffer from severe anxiety [82]. In addition, up to 60% of patients with severe COPD and approximately 43% of patients with HF experience cognitive impairment [12, 85]. Although differences in assessment instruments and population characteristics limit direct comparisons of the prevalence of neuropsychiatric complications between PF and other chronic diseases, these data still underscore that the risk of neuropsychiatric complications in PF patients should not be overlooked. In addition, most of the available data are derived from cross-sectional studies or from the baseline assessments of cohort studies [52, 56, 58, 69, 70, 75], and the studies involved generally have relatively modest sample sizes and do not consistently control for potential confounders such as age, severity of hypoxemia, and cardiovascular comorbidities. Therefore, while these epidemiological data strongly suggest an association between PF and neuropsychiatric disorders, they cannot establish causality.

Brain network substrates

Mohammadi-Nejad et al. utilized imaging and genetic data from over 32,000 participants in the UK Biobank population cohort to confirm a colocalization relationship between the genetic risk locus for IPF (DEPTOR gene) and reduced anterior cingulate cortex thickness alongside widespread white matter microstructural alterations. This supports the hypothesis that genetic risk factors may explain partial comorbidity between IPF and brain disorders [86]. Additionally, a study of brain white matter networks in pneumoconiosis patients revealed that early-stage coal workers with pneumoconiosis may experience disruption in brain structural networks. The properties of this structural network were found to be correlated with clinical indicators and cognitive scores, suggesting that changes in the structural network may contribute to cognitive deficits in coal worker’s pneumoconiosis (CWP) patients. These findings can enhance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment in pneumoconiosis patients [87]. According to a resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study on patients with CWP, compared to healthy controls, the CWP group showed lower amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation signals in the bilateral temporal poles. Additionally, functional connectivity between the bilateral temporal poles and the left superior frontal gyrus as well as the left frontal pole was reduced [88]. The temporal pole plays a key role in memory storage, particularly in cognitive processes such as autobiographical memory and socio-emotional processing [88–90]. Impairment of this brain region may contribute to several neurological conditions, including post-traumatic stress disorder, behavioral dementia, semantic dementia, associative agnosia, epilepsy, and other related disorders [89, 91, 92]. The latest study employed a bidirectional Mendelian randomization approach using resting-state fMRI data from 31,453 individuals in the UK Biobank and genome-wide association studies data for ten respiratory diseases. This study provided genetic evidence suggesting a bidirectional causal relationship between the lungs and the brain. It was found that IPF exerts a causal effect on brain networks, particularly those controlling attention and executive function [93]. This work offers genetic evidence for understanding the lung-brain axis, establishes the lung-brain axis as a potential therapeutic target, and holds profound implications for both respiratory and neurological interventions.

Beyond the structural and functional network alterations directly observed in PF, evidence from other chronic respiratory diseases further supports related neurodegenerative changes. In COPD, studies have revealed widespread gray matter atrophy, particularly in the frontal lobe, limbic system, temporal lobe, and thalamus [94–97]. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) consistently demonstrates widespread white matter microstructural integrity damage, characterized by increased mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, and radial diffusivity in the cingulum, corona radiata, and corpus callosum, as well as damage to key fiber tracts [95, 97–99]. In addition, resting-state fMRI studies highlight disrupted functional networks, especially the default mode network [97, 100]. In patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), voxel-based morphometry revealed that male patients with severe OSA, compared with age-matched healthy controls, exhibited widespread reductions in gray matter concentration. Affected regions included the left gyrus rectus, bilateral superior frontal gyri, left precentral gyrus, bilateral frontomarginal gyri, bilateral anterior cingulate gyri, right insular gyrus, bilateral caudate nuclei, bilateral thalami, bilateral amygdalohippocampal, bilateral inferior temporal gyri, and bilateral quadrangular and biventer lobules in the cerebellum [101]. These lesions and connectivity deficits are closely associated with cognitive impairment. Given that PF shares some similar pathophysiological drivers with other chronic hypoxic lung diseases, whether similar changes occur specifically in PF populations warrants dedicated investigation. These findings suggest a link between PF and brain dysfunction, which may be mediated through the lung–brain axis.

Potential mechanisms of PF-induced neuropsychiatric disorders

Initiation and maintenance of pulmonary inflammation

The pathological process of PF begins with repeated alveolar epithelial injury and subsequent aberrant repair. Taking silica dust exposure as an example, inhaled particles are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages, leading to lysosomal damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. This activates the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, resulting in sustained release of pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-1beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) [102–104]. These cytokines subsequently recruit neutrophils to infiltrate into the alveolar space and exacerbate the early inflammatory response [102, 105, 106]. Other inhaled pollutants, including PM2.5 and metal-containing nanoparticles, can induce pulmonary inflammation through similar oxidative stress (OS) pathways [107–110], suggesting that particles from different sources share a common inflammatory mechanism. Driven by key cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), resting lung fibroblasts are activated and differentiate into myofibroblasts [111–114], which synthesize and secrete ECM components such as collagen and fibronectin in large amounts [115–117]. Concurrently, alveolar epithelial cells initiate epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which further increases the number of myofibroblasts [118–121]. These processes collectively lead to excessive deposition of ECM and gradual replacement of alveolar structure by fibrotic tissue [122, 123]. Notably, fibrotic lung tissue itself acts as a persistent source of inflammatory factors—activated macrophages and fibroblasts continuously release TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, which enter the systemic circulation through the damaged alveolar-capillary barrier and lay the foundation for the subsequent dissemination of systemic inflammation [124–131] (Fig. 2).

However, the initiating events of pulmonary inflammation vary across PF subtypes. In IPF, which lacks a clear exogenous trigger, chronic inflammation is thought to arise primarily from age-related processes such as telomere dysfunction, cellular senescence, gene mutation and other related factors, together with subsequent aberrant repair mechanisms (e.g., autophagy insufficiency), leading to recurrent alveolar epithelial injury. This drives sustained activation of fibroblasts and the release of low-grade inflammatory mediators [23, 24, 86, 132–137] (Fig. 3). In CTD-ILD, by contrast, autoimmune-driven inflammation serves as the primary trigger, with circulating autoantibodies, immune complexes, and autoreactive T cells all contributing to pulmonary and systemic inflammation [24, 138–144]. Despite these distinct etiological triggers, all forms of PF—whether particle-induced, idiopathic, or autoimmune-associated—converge on a common pathway characterized by persistent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) within the lung microenvironment [138, 145, 146]. These mediators enter the systemic circulation, where they can compromise the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and initiate neuroinflammation, thereby constituting a shared mechanism underlying PF-related neuropsychiatric complications across different disease subtypes.

Systemic inflammatory dissemination and blood-brain barrier disruption

Lung-derived inflammatory mediators that enter the circulation not only maintain a chronic, low-grade systemic inflammatory state [7, 147–149], but also act directly on the BBB, a critical portal to the CNS. In IPF, this systemic inflammatory burden is further exacerbated by several disease-specific factors. Senescent cells and PTEN-induced putative kinase 1-deficient alveolar epithelial cells in IPF lungs release pro-inflammatory factors and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), respectively, along with elevated component 1q (C1q) levels and reduced reticulum 3 expression that promotes profibrotic macrophage polarization — all of which increase circulating levels of IL-1β and TNF-α [39, 132, 150–154]. TNF-α and IL-1β can cross the BBB through receptor-mediated endocytosis or direct penetration, a process that itself causes damage to barrier integrity [155–157]. These factors disrupt endothelial cell junctions by downregulating the expression of tight junction proteins, including claudin-5, occludin, and Zonula Occludens-1, thereby significantly increasing BBB permeability [147, 158–160]. The loss of barrier function in turn allows more peripheral inflammatory mediators and immune cells to enter the brain parenchyma, creating a vicious cycle [161, 162]. Besides, inflammatory signals from the lung can also be transmitted directly to the brain through the afferent fibers of the vagus nerve—cytokines released by the resident immune cells in the lung activate the corresponding receptors on the vagal nerve terminals, and action potentials are transmitted through the nucleus tractus solitarius to the broader cortical regions [163–165]. This neural pathway can rapidly convey peripheral inflammatory status to the brain even before the BBB is significantly compromised. Once they enter the brain parenchyma, these inflammatory mediators activate glial cells [161, 166, 167], and the secondary inflammatory factors released by these activated glial cells in turn aggravate the BBB damage [168, 169], thus establishing a pathological cycle involving peripheral inflammation, barrier disruption and glial activation.

Initiation and amplification of neuroinflammation

Inflammatory signals entering the CNS activate microglia. As the resident immune cells of the CNS, microglia are polarized from a resting state to an M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype under the drive of transcription factors such as hypoxia-inducible factor-1alpha (HIF-1α) and nuclear factor kappaB (NF-κB) [147, 170–172], and release a range of neurotoxic mediators: ROS and nitric oxide (NO) directly damage neurons [173–176]; cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β disrupt synaptic plasticity [16, 177, 178]; complement proteins C1q and complement component 3 (C3) initiate the classical complement cascade, with C3 deposited on the surface of neurons and serving as an “eat-me” signal that mediates microglial phagocytosis of synapses [124, 179–181]. Moreover, in IPF, circulating mtDNA also acts as a danger signal that can activate microglia via Toll-like receptor 9, further amplifying neuroinflammation [182–184]. Activated microglia further recruit and activate astrocytes, which release excitotoxic substances such as glutamate that exacerbate neuronal injury [172, 177, 178]. This self-amplifying cycle of neuroinflammation leads to a reduction in synaptic proteins (synaptophysin, postsynaptic density protein 95) and loss of synaptic function, which ultimately manifests as neuronal apoptosis and cognitive decline [16, 185–187]. Beyond the classic PF silicosis model, evidence of microgliosis, astrogliosis and complement activation has been observed in other respiratory diseases such as COVID-19 and COPD, which further confirms that neuroinflammation is a common pathological feature when these diseases involve the CNS [157, 188, 189]. This phenomenon also extends to the bleomycin-induced PF model—the most widely used experimental paradigm in PF research, where Xiong et al. demonstrated that intermittent hypoxia exacerbated depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors in mice, accompanied by hippocampal microglial activation that positively correlated with pulmonary inflammation [190], further supporting the lung-brain axis across PF etiologies without exogenous particles.

Synergistic injury from chronic hypoxia and direct particle neurotoxicity

In addition to inflammatory pathways, chronic hypoxia and direct translocation of particulate matter represent two important, synergistic mechanisms of injury. Gas exchange impairment in PF leads to intermittent or persistent systemic hypoxemia. In IPF, this hypoxemic burden is frequently exacerbated by comorbid OSA, which affects up to 76% of patients and introduces cycles of intermittent hypoxia (IH) [191, 192]. Pre-exposure to IH has been shown to aggravate bleomycin-induced PF in mice, linking OSA to worse pulmonary outcomes [190, 193]. Under hypoxic conditions, the stabilization of HIF-1α triggers multiple pathological cascades: it disrupts neuronal mitochondrial function, exacerbates OS [147, 194, 195], and promotes microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory (M1-like) phenotype [171, 196, 197]. Critically, hypoxia and inflammation form a mutually reinforcing feedback loop: HIF-1α activation promotes NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses, while inflammatory mediators aggravate tissue hypoxia by increasing local oxygen demand and impairing microcirculation [198–201]. The hippocampus, owing to its high metabolic rate and sensitivity to oxygen deprivation, is particularly vulnerable: hypoxia-induced disruption of neuronal energy metabolism directly compromises synaptic plasticity and contributes to cognitive deficits, including memory impairment [16, 157, 202]. Concurrently, experimental studies suggest that submicron particles can bypass conventional physiological barriers to access the CNS via two alternative routes. One is through the olfactory nerve pathway, traveling from the olfactory mucosa via the olfactory bulb into the brain [203–206]. The other is hematogenous: particles cross the damaged alveolar-capillary barrier, enter the circulation, and then traverse the already compromised BBB [16, 107, 110, 207]. Once deposited in brain tissue, silica nanoparticles induce localized OS, activate NF-κB signaling, and upregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 [16, 208–210]. Similarly, exposure to indium tin oxide nanoparticles has been shown to cause brain inflammation, increase glial reactivity, and lead to accumulation of abnormal proteins such as β-amyloid (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau protein [211]. In the mouse model of silicosis, [18F] AV45 positron emission tomography imaging revealed significant Aβ deposition across multiple brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory bulb, with regional Aβ burden demonstrating a positive correlation with the severity of PF. These preliminary findings provide compelling in vivo imaging evidence supporting the hypothesis of direct neurotoxicity induced by inhaled particles, but require validation in further clinical studies [212].

Collectively, these findings indicate that PF converges on microglia-mediated neuroinflammation through three pathways—inflammatory dissemination, hypoxia-induced injury, and particulate matter translocation—ultimately leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal damage, which constitutes the pathological basis for cognitive and emotional decline observed in PF patients (Fig. 4).

Discussion and prospect

The neuropsychiatric complications of PF patients have received increasing attention. Epidemiological data indicate that PF patients with neuropsychiatric disorders have a poor overall prognosis, experiencing further declines in quality of life, poorer treatment adherence, and significantly increased risks of hospitalization and mortality [53, 68]. Specifically, the annual incidence of depression after IPF diagnosis is approximately 32.4 per 1,000 person-years, with a 1.5- to 2.0-fold increased risk of mortality [68] and an approximately 2-fold increased risk of hospitalization [53]. The incidence of cognitive dysfunction, anxiety, and depression in PF patients is relatively high [7, 52]. The underlying mechanism may be related to the lung-brain axis [16, 17]. Local inflammation in the lungs spreads rapidly through the body fluids to become systemic inflammation [213–216]. The released cytokines increase the permeability of the BBB [161, 217], driving the continuous activation of glial cells [167], and releasing excitatory toxic mediators such as ROS, NO, glutamate, and complement proteins [218], ultimately inducing neuronal damage and synaptic dysfunction. During this process, chronic hypoxia acts as a key synergistic factor [147], further exacerbating the disorder of the microenvironment in the brain, constituting the pathological basis of PF-related neuropsychiatric complications.

The prognostic deterioration and mechanistic analysis outlined above indicate that neuropsychiatric complications are not merely “accompanying phenomena” of PF, but rather key factors influencing the survival outcome of patients. However, at present, the treatment for PF combined with neuropsychiatric complications still focuses on the primary pulmonary disease [25, 219, 220], lacking specific intervention measures targeting the systemic pathological process of the lung-brain axis. The treatment strategy for the lung-brain axis must shift from “lung-focused therapy” to “lung–brain co-therapy”. Therefore, future drug research should focus on drugs that have dual effects of anti-pulmonary fibrosis and CNS protection. For example, MMI-0100, which is currently in clinical trials for PF, can bypass the BBB through nasal administration and exert anti-inflammatory effects directly in the CNS [221, 222], indicating a promising application prospect. Additionally, biological agents targeting key inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α are also expected to block the damage cascade transmitted through the “lung-brain axis“ [22, 223]. For non-drug intervention, intermittent hypoxia training (IHT) (fraction of inspiration O₂ = 9–16%, lasting 3–10 min) can activate the HIF-1α pathway to mobilize the endogenous neuroprotective mechanism and enhance the brain’s tolerance to chronic hypoxia [224, 225]. Specifically, we clarify that IHT with low fraction of inspired oxygen (9–16%) carries a risk of exacerbating hypoxemia and is not suitable for PF patients with hypoxemia (peripheral oxygen saturation ≤ 88–90%). If applied, it should be restricted to early-stage, stable PF patients without hypoxemia, under individualized monitoring and clinical supervision [226]. However, when the BBB has been damaged and the glial cells are activated, more direct neuroprotective strategies should be considered, such as using small glial cell activation inhibitors (p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase inhibitors) [227, 228], synaptic plasticity regulators (brain-derived neurotrophic factor mimetics) [229], or BBB stabilizers (sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor antagonists) [230, 231]. Moreover, embedding cognitive training into the pulmonary rehabilitation aerobic training of patients with PF, and leveraging the upregulation of brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) induced by exercise [232], can synergistically promote cognitive function improvement, which is an important non-drug path for the transformation of the “lung–brain co-therapy” concept. It is important to note that several of the aforementioned strategies, including MMI-0100, IHT, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase inhibitors, remain at preclinical or early exploratory stages and lack direct clinical evidence in PF patients. They are presented here as theoretical directions based on mechanistic insights from the lung-brain axis, not as established clinical interventions. In the future, efforts should be made to actively promote multidisciplinary team collaboration to develop individualized comprehensive management plans for PF patients, covering respiratory function, neuropsychiatric health, and quality of life. At the same time, early inflammatory intervention of pulmonary lesions should be strengthened to prevent or delay the occurrence of systemic complications including neuropsychiatric damage (Fig. 5).

This review has several limitations. Firstly, this review encompasses PF of diverse etiologies [21, 233]. While this approach facilitates the identification of shared mechanisms within the lung–brain axis, it should be acknowledged that different subtypes may vary in the intensity of inflammation, rate of disease progression, and extent of systemic involvement, all of which could differentially influence the risk of neuropsychiatric complications. Future studies should directly compare neuropsychiatric outcomes across PF subtypes to determine whether subtype-specific mechanisms exist. Additionally, current research in this field is mostly of cross-sectional design [37, 52], making it difficult to reveal the direct causal relationship between PF and neuropsychiatric complications. To fill this evidence gap, future basic research should focus on constructing animal models of pneumoconiosis that simulate the characteristics of chronic low-level inflammation in humans (such as low-dose long-term intratracheal instillation or inhalation exposure) [16, 234], in order to clarify the temporal sequence and dose-effect relationship between pulmonary lesions and neuro-pathological changes. At the same time, multi-omics techniques can provide direct evidence for the molecular communication mechanism of the “lung-brain axis“ [235]. For example, it has been found that in more severe cases of PF, the expression levels of miR-101 and miR-27b are significantly downregulated and these two miRNAs also show similar changes in Alzheimer disease [236], suggesting that the pathways regulated by these two miRNAs may constitute the common molecular basis of pulmonary-brain comorbidity. This discovery provides an important direction for exploring common biomarkers of pulmonary-brain interaction, and highlights the great potential of using omics methods to reveal systemic pathological mechanisms. In the clinical aspect, prospective, multicenter longitudinal cohort studies should be actively carried out to systematically collect inflammatory and neuro-injury biomarkers in the peripheral blood of patients with PF [such as IL-6, TNF-α, neurofilament light chain, and glial fibrillary acidic protein] [237–240], and combine neuropsychological assessment and multimodal imaging examinations (such as translocator protein 18 kDa positron emission tomography for visualizing neural inflammation, MRI for evaluating the integrity of the BBB) [241, 242], so as to clarify the dynamic evolution patterns of these indicators. By constructing risk prediction models, it is expected to achieve objective quantification and dynamic monitoring of the path from “pulmonary lesions - systemic inflammation - brain imaging changes - cognitive decline”, providing scientific basis for the early identification of brain damage risks.

Conclusion

Neuropsychiatric disorders, including cognitive impairment, anxiety and depression, are common but underappreciated comorbidities of PF. These comorbidities are often clinically manifested as brain network abnormalities, and their pathogenesis involves multiple factors such as chronic hypoxia, direct neurotoxicity, and neuroinflammation. The concept of the lung-brain axis has deepened our understanding of the mechanism of this comorbidity phenomenon, and revealed a new direction for diagnosis and treatment of this clinical problem. It’s necessary to combine traditional pulmonary interventions with targeted strategies to protect the CNS, including neuroprotective medications, cognitive training, and lifestyle changes such as pulmonary rehabilitation and IHT. Future research should aim to identify predictive biomarkers along with mechanistic studies to discover precise molecular targets that ultimately prevent, delay, or mitigate the neuropsychiatric complications of PF.
