Assessment of Blood Profiles and Pain in Sport Horses Undergoing Intrarectal Ozone Therapy
Authors: Thamirys Dias de Luna Paiva, Anna Rita de Melo Lopes, Álvaro José Chávez Silva, Maria Amélia Fernandes Figueiredo, Selene Siqueira da Cunha Nogueira, Sérgio Luiz Gama Nogueira‐Filho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMedical ozone is used in various treatments due to its oxidising properties.ObjectiveWe aimed in this study to evaluate the impact of intrarectal ozone therapy on haematological and biochemical parameters, as well as the pain induced by the procedure in sport horses.Study designIn vivo experiment with 12 Quarter Horses (3 males and 9 females) used as their own controls to assess the effects of ozone therapy.MethodsBlood samples were collected before treatment (Control), 24 h after ozone therapy (24 h post‐treatment), and 7 days after the last day of ozone therapy (7 days post‐treatment). These samples were analysed for haematological and biochemical parameters. Pain assessment, clinical evaluation, and the measurement of heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR) were conducted before and during ozone therapy.ResultsOzone therapy significantly affected haemoglobin (F 2,20 = 5.55; p = 0.012), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentrations (MCHC) (F 2,20 = 16.01; p < 0.001), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity (F 2,20 = 20.49; p < 0.001). Specifically, haemoglobin concentration decreased 24 h after ozone therapy, MCHC levels increased 7 days post‐ozone therapy, and AST activity decreased 7 days post‐ozone therapy. Blood smears taken 24 h post‐treatment revealed red blood cells with membrane deformities, which normalised by 7 days post‐treatment. None of the animals exhibited clinical symptoms, although pain scores increased, although pain scores increased (before: 4.2 ± 0.4; during: 5.3 ± 0.4; F 1,107 = 5.20; p = 0.025). The reduction in haemoglobin was likely due to oxidative damage, which stimulated the production of erythrocytes with higher haemoglobin content. Lower AST levels suggested an absence of liver damage. The increase in pain scores suggests that using lower concentrations or doses in future studies might alleviate discomfort in the animals.Main limitationsThe study's applicability is limited by the small sample size, particularly the number of males, yet the findings provide valuable insights.ConclusionsOverall, the administration of intrarectal ozone therapy over a 7‐day period in horses was associated with favourable haematological modifications, which may suggest an enhancement in systemic tissue oxygenation.Keywords: anti‐inflammatory, cell renewal, horses, oxidative stress, ozone, pain, red blood cells

Introduction

Ozone therapy is an alternative treatment for various health conditions. Ozone is an oxidising gas naturally present in the atmosphere and possesses antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory, and antioxidant‐inducing properties in the body (Alves et al. 2004; Artis et al. 2010; Assenza et al. 2016; Ballardini 2005). Ozone therapy can affect biochemical enzymes in the body in various ways by altering their activity and function due to its oxidising potential (Ballardini 2006). In some cases, ozone therapy can stimulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which help neutralise free radicals and protect cells against oxidative damage (Bhatt et al. 2016). Furthermore, ozone therapy can also inhibit the activity of pro‐inflammatory enzymes, such as cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX), which are involved in the production of inflammatory mediators (Artis et al. 2010; Bocci 2004; Bocci et al. 2009, 2006; Bocci et al. 2011; Bottegaro et al. 2018; Smith et al.2017). However, more research is needed to better understand these effects and determine the best practices for its use.

Due to these effects, ozone therapy in horses has the potential to improve blood circulation, increase tissue oxygenation, stimulate the immune system, and reduce inflammation, without altering the concentrations of key biomarkers of inflammation and cartilage catabolism when administered intra‐articularly (Assenza et al. 2016). Therefore, ozone therapy may become a valuable tool for the recovery of sport horses, which are commonly subjected to muscle and joint injuries (Carvalho et al. 2023). As in humans, ozone therapy in horses involves the administration of ozone through various routes, such as reperfusion of ozonised serum (Casey 2007); subcutaneous injection (Coelho et al. 2015); autohaemotherapy (Ballardini 2006; Dalla Costa et al. 2014; Galie et al. 2018; Gleerup and Lindegaard 2016); and topical intra‐articular application (Assenza et al. 2016). To date, only two studies have rigorously evaluated the intrarectal route of administration using a sample comprising multiple horses (Haddad et al. 2009a; Haddad et al. 2009b).

The intrarectal route is considered relatively less invasive compared to the aforementioned methods (Artis et al. 2010). Additionally, its application is relatively simple, as it does not require materials such as transfusion bags, catheters, or tubing, which increase the risk of contamination and elevate the cost of the procedure (Haddad et al. 2009a). However, ozone therapy in horses remains controversial and is not widely recognised by the conventional veterinary community due to the lack of standardised treatment protocols (Bottegaro et al. 2018). Furthermore, in the reviewed scientific literature, no studies were found that have evaluated the use of intrarectal ozone therapy in sport horses.

The trainers of the horses involved in this study observed that, overall, all animals exhibited swollen joints, with some displaying mild lameness by the end of the competition season. Consequently, given the antioxidant properties of ozone therapy (Tsuzuki et al. 2015), and its capacity to stimulate erythrocyte production (Bocci et al. 2011), we anticipate an increase in haemoglobin levels and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) after completing ozone administration, as compared to the pre‐treatment data. Additionally, since previous studies have documented the analgesic effects of ozone therapy (Bhatt et al. 2016; Bocci 2006; Sciorsci et al. 2020; Vendruscolo et al. 2018), we predict a potential reduction in pain as a result of this treatment. Furthermore, as intrarectal ozone therapy is a relatively new treatment, we aim to evaluate its effects on various haematological and biochemical parameters in sport horses, comparing the results with baseline data gathered before the initiation of therapy. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no investigations have addressed the level of pain potentially induced by intrarectal ozone administration, nor examined erythrocyte alterations in blood smears – both of which constitute central aims of the present study.

Materials and Methods

All procedures employed in the present study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (CEUA) of the Universidade Estadual de Santa Cruz (UESC) (#013/22).

Selection of Animals, Ozone Dose, and Concentration

Twelve Quarter Horses, maintained at a stud farm in the city of Itapé, Bahia, Brazil, were used in this study. The group comprised nine mares and three geldings, aged between 4 and 12 years (Table 1). At the facility, all animals were subjected to the same feeding management, housing, and training regimen. The horses were provided with 4.0 kg of Tifton 85 Bermuda grass hay (88.7% dry matter (DM), 9.3% crude protein and 1.7 Mcal of digestible energy/kg on dry DM basis) and 3.0 kg of concentrate feed (88.0% DM, 12% crude protein and 3.1 Mcal of digestible energy/kg on DM basis), in the early morning and late afternoon. Additionally, they were given 6.0 kg of Bermuda grass hay during the night. All were kept in individual stalls (3.0 m length × 4.0 m width). Each stall was equipped with a drinking fountain (0.2 m long × 0.3 m wide × 0.2 m high) that provided water ad libitum. They exercised daily, including stretching and warming up for 30 min, 5 days a week. Of these 5 days, two included intensive training sessions consisting of 10 min of extended trot, uphill climbing, and 20 min of circling at a gallop, in addition to obstacle courses. All animals regularly participated in barrel racing competitions, and data collection took place 1 month after the end of the competition season, which corresponded to October 2023. Initially, all horses were clinically examined by a veterinarian and deemed healthy.

We performed five applications of ozone therapy, with 48‐h intervals between each session, conducted early in the morning before any routine activities. The order in which the animals received this treatment was randomised for the first application. For the subsequent four applications, this order was maintained to respect the 48‐h interval between each application. For the application of ozone therapy, the animals were individually placed in a restraint chute. Heart rate and respiratory rate were measured with a stethoscope for 1 min before and immediately after ozone (O3) administration. A medicinal ozone generator (Ozonic Vet [Ozonic, São Bernardo do Campo, São Paulo, Brazil]) was used, along with a silicone hose connected to an embryo collection catheter for rectal insufflation. Before the application of ozone, faeces were removed, and the catheter was inserted into the animal's rectum to a depth of approximately 0.10 m. The device was set to a flow rate of 1/8, corresponding to 125 mL/min and a concentration of 30 µg O3/mL. Based on this data and the animals’ weights (Table 1), the duration for which the catheter remained in each animal's rectum was calculated to reach a dose of 60 µg O3/kg. The flow rate, concentration, and duration of ozone therapy application in this study strictly followed the guidelines established by the International Scientific Committee of Ozone Therapy (ISCO3) (International Scientific Committee of Ozone therapy (ISCO3) 2020).

Blood Collection and Sample Processing

Blood samples were collected 3 days before the start of ozone therapy administration (control), 1 day after the fifth and final day of its administration (24 h after ozone therapy), and again 7 days after the last day of ozone therapy (7 days after ozone therapy). The blood samples were collected in the early morning by jugular vein puncture using disposable needles (40 × 12 mm) and syringes (10 mL) and transferred into tubes containing K3 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as an anticoagulant and another tube without anticoagulant. Immediately after collection, blood smears were also prepared, with two slides per animal. All collected samples were stored in an insulated box with recyclable ice and transported to the Clinical Analysis Laboratory at UESC, where they were processed. The morphology of the erythrocytes was evaluated concerning membrane integrity, size, shape, and staining based on the blood smears (Mark et al. 2008).

In the laboratory, the smears were stained using the Rapid Panoptic kit. For the blood samples, an automated blood cell counter (ABX Vet from Horiba) was employed to analyse erythrocytes (10⁶/µL), haemoglobin (g/dL), haematocrit (%), mean corpuscular volume (MCV) (f/L), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) (%), and leucocytes (cells/µL). Using oil immersion microscopy with a 100× objective lens, eosinophils (%), lymphocytes (%), monocytes (%), and basophils (%) were analysed. Platelets (103/µL) were counted from the slide readings. Total plasma proteins (TPP) (g/dL) and fibrinogen were determined via refractometry. Lastly, the following enzymes were evaluated using Bioclin commercial biochemical kits, read by a Bioplus 2000 semi‐automated biochemical analyser: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT).

Pain Assessment

Five‐minute video‐recordings were made both before (control phase) and during the treatment (ozone therapy phase) using a Canon t3i digital camera, which allows high‐definition recording with a resolution of 1080 p. The camera was placed on a tripod 1.0 m away from the animal, at a front and diagonal angle. The videos were subsequently stored on an external hard drive. At the end of the data collection, two 30‐s video clips were produced for each animal. One video clip was created from the footage recorded at the start of the control phase, and the other from the middle of the treatment phase. Then, six static images (photographs) were selected from the video clips recorded in the control phase, and another six photographs from the treatment phase. Both the video clips and the photographs were coded, so the evaluators (as described below) could not identify either the animals or the phase (control or treatment).

Three evaluators, with previous experience in behavioural observation of equines, were invited to analyse the collected images. Before starting the analyses, these evaluators participated in training on the use of pain scales for equines. During the training, they reviewed photographs and video clips of equines in neutral and painful situations, selected from the UESC Veterinary Hospital's archive images. For pain assessment, the evaluators used two scales: Horse Grimace Scale (HGS) for the photographs and Equine Pain Face (EPF) for the video clips (Mustafa 1990; Satué et al. 2014). After the training, the evaluators underwent a concordance test. Individually, they analysed six photographs and six video clips of equines in neutral and painful situations, selected from a published work (Sciorsci et al. 2020). The evaluators showed Kendall's concordance indices of 0.82 (p < 0.023) and 0.86 (p < 0.013) for HGS and EPF, respectively, which was considered satisfactory. Equal quantities of photographs and video clips were then randomly distributed among the three evaluators for individual assessment. During the evaluation, they were unaware of the identity of the animals and the phase (control and ozone therapy) they were analysing. The data were subsequently organised in a spreadsheet for analysis. To gain a comprehensive understanding of pain in the animals, we supplemented pain scales with a clinical evaluation and analysed potential changes in heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR), which may indicate pain or stress and are also utilised for this purpose (Satué et al. 2014). Consequently, we conducted a clinical evaluation after each application of ozone therapy and measured HR and RR before and after each application.

Data Analysis

To compare the mean values of blood sample data, we employed generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs), with a separate model for each variable. Post hoc Tukey's tests were conducted when necessary. In each model, sex (male or female) and the experimental phase (control, 24 after ozone therapy, and 7 days after ozone therapy for blood sample data; or before and immediately after ozone therapy for HR and RR data) and the interaction between sex and experimental phase were considered as fixed factors (independent variables). Additionally, in each model, the identity of the individuals was treated as a random factor, allowing for the control of dependencies in repeated measures. The residuals of each model were graphically checked for normal distribution and homoscedasticity, and a logarithmic transformation was applied to CK and TPP data to satisfy these assumptions.

We also used GLMMs to analyse the pain scores obtained for each scale (HGS and EPF), with a separate model for each scale. Post hoc Tukey's tests were conducted when necessary. In each model, the order of ozone therapy application (1st to 5th application), sex (male and female), and phase (control and ozone therapy), as well as their interactions, were considered as fixed factors. Additionally, the identity of the individuals was included as a random factor in each model to control for dependencies in repeated measures. The normal distribution and homoscedasticity of the residuals of each model were graphically verified and deemed satisfactory. The selection of the model with the best fit to the data (for the HGS and EPF pain scales) was based on the highest coefficient of determination (R 2) and the lowest values for the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The analyses were performed using Minitab 19.1 software (Minitab Inc., State College, PA), with a significance level set at α < 0.05.

Results

There was an effect of the experimental phase on haemoglobin (F 2, 20 = 5.55; p = 0.012), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) (F 2, 20 = 16.01; p < 0.001), and serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity (F 2, 20 = 20.49; p < 0.001). Post hoc tests indicated a reduction (p < 0.05) in the mean haemoglobin concentration in samples collected 24 h after ozone therapy (9.7 ± 0.3), compared to blood samples collected during the control phase (11.3 ± 0.5) and 7 days after ozone therapy (11.6 ± 0.4) (Figure 1). Conversely, there was an increase in the mean MCHC concentration (%) in blood samples collected 7 days after ozone therapy (33.0 ± 0.4), compared to the means recorded during the control phase (32.1 ± 0.5) and 24 h after ozone therapy (32.1 ± 0.6) (p < 0.05) (Figure 2). Additionally, there was a reduction in serum AST activity in samples collected 7 days after ozone therapy (84.0 ± 23.8), compared to the control phase (254 ± 27.8) and 24 h after ozone therapy (275 ± 11) (p < 0.05). Neither sex nor the interaction between sex and experimental phase affected haemoglobin (p > 0.05), MCHC (p > 0.05), or AST (p > 0.05) (Figure 3).

There was also a non‐significant increase in fibrinogen concentration in blood samples collected 7 days after ozone therapy (F 2, 20 = 3.18; p = 0.063) (Table 2). The other parameters of the haemogram, such as erythrocytes, and haematocrit as well as biochemical parameters like creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), were within the expected reference values for hot‐blooded equine species and were not affected by the experimental phase (Table 2). An effect of the experimental phase was observed (F 1, 90 = 9.37; p = 0.003) (Table 2) when the pain of equines was evaluated using the HGS scale, with higher mean pain scores during the application of ozone therapy compared to the control phase (Figure 1). However, the statistical model did not reveal significant effects on mean pain scores due to the other fixed factors (order of application and sex), either in isolation or their interactions (Table 2).

An effect of the experimental phase was also observed (F 1, 330 = 87.94; p < 0.001) (Tables 3, 4) when equine pain was evaluated using the EPF scale, with higher mean pain scores during ozone therapy compared to the control phase (Figure 5). The statistical model also revealed a significant interaction between the order of ozone therapy application and the phase (F 4, 330 = 3.31; p = 0.011) (Table 3). Post hoc tests revealed that the mean pain scores in the control and ozone therapy phases did not differ significantly between the third and fourth ozone therapy applications (Figure 6). The model also revealed a significant interaction between the order of ozone therapy application and the sex of the equines (F 4, 330 = 2.80; p = 0.026). However, post hoc tests did not show significant differences in mean pain scores between males and females according to the application(Figure 7). Additionally, there was no effect on mean pain scores due to the other fixed factors (order of application and sex), either in isolation or their interactions (Table 3). Lower values were obtained for both the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) in the statistical model using the HGS scale compared to the model using the EPF scale. However, the latter presented a higher coefficient of determination (R 2) than the model using the HGS scale (Table 5).

Discussion

The results of this study showed that intrarectal ozone therapy promoted beneficial effects in sport horses. In this study, there was an increase in haemoglobin and MCHC 7 days after the end of ozone administration (7 days post‐ozone therapy) compared to the data collected before the start of treatment (control). This result is similar to that described in a previous study which also tested intrarectal ozone therapy application (Haddad et al. 2009a). These positive results were expected, as ozone therapy is a widely used adjunct therapeutic tool for various conditions in human and veterinary medicine (Ballardini 2005), particularly in horses due to its antioxidant capacity (Gleerup and Lindegaard 2016), osteoarthritis treatment (Assenza et al. 2016), chronic laminitis (Seyam et al. 2018) via intra‐articular routes (Assenza et al. 2016), or autohaemotherapy (Ballardini 2006). Notably, the improvement in the condition of red blood cells 7 days after treatment, as verified in this study, contraindicates its application on the eve of competitions. Therefore, the final application should be administered at least 1 week prior to such events.

The animals in the present study did not exhibit clinical symptoms because of ozone therapy. Additionally, the enzymes evaluating hepatic profiles showed reductions. AST levels were lower at the end of the treatment (7 days post‐ozone therapy). AST typically increases during exercise due to the heightened permeability of the sarcolemma and remains in circulation for 24 h (Siniscalco et al. 2018). This reduction may suggest that 1 week after ozone therapy, there was greater stability of muscle and liver cell membranes. There is a possibility of reduced production of metabolites generated after muscle stress, which would be metabolised by the liver, particularly lactic acid, the primary metabolite produced via the anaerobic pathway when ATP demand is not met by aerobic respiration. This requires further evaluation in future studies.

The beneficial effects of ozone, such as the increase in MCHC and haemoglobin concentrations, along with the decrease in AST, occurred 7 days after the end of its administration. Thus, the effect of ozone therapy is delayed. This result corroborates previous studies suggesting a delayed effect, as it ‘awakens’ the organism to activate cellular metabolism (Bocci 2004). Consequently, ozone therapy can contribute to improved tissue oxygenation and antioxidant capacity, thereby reducing the demand for the anaerobic pathway.

We observed that, after ozone therapy, red blood cells exhibited oxidative damage to their structure. However, in both studies that also used intrarectal ozone therapy in horses did not report such alterations (Haddad et al. 2009a; Haddad et al. 2009b). In contrast, another study has shown the presence of deformities in red blood cells, indicative of oxidative lesions and membrane fragility, following intrarectal oxygen therapy in rabbits (Torcivia and Mcdonnell 2021). Oxidative damage to red blood cells may be associated with lipid peroxidation of the membrane, denaturation of haemoglobin, or a change in the valence state of the iron atom when the cell is exposed to oxidising agents (Mark et al. 2008), such as the ozone (O3) used here. Additionally, rectal insufflation of O3/O2 may exert local and acute systemic effects arising from the absorption of oxidative products derived from the decomposition of O3 into H2O and O2, and their interaction with unsaturated fatty acids of cell membranes or other biomolecules present in the intraluminal contents, resulting in the formation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (Bottegaro et al. 2018; Tsuzuki et al. 2015). In turn, H2O2 promotes the activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway and antioxidant proteins (Ballardini 2005; Bocci et al. 2011; Bottegaro et al. 2018); this paradoxical effect of ozone partially explains the observed results. Thus, even with such lesions, blood variables remained within reference values. Additionally, no alterations were observed in the red blood cells from the smears of samples collected 7 days post‐ozone therapy.

Therefore, the oxidative lesion process was transient and controlled 7 days after the last day of ozone therapy administration due to the stimulation of cell renewal. This result may be significant for the recovery of sport horses. Young erythrocytes are lighter and contain more glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) than older, heavier cells (Bhatt et al. 2016). Furthermore, other potent antioxidant enzymes (catalase, GSH‐Rd, GSH‐Px, GSH‐Tr, and SOD) are naturally present in erythrocytes, which may improve the physical conditioning of sport horses. These enzymes can rapidly eliminate large quantities of oxidants generated during physical exercise, increasing perfusion and tissue oxygenation, along with the increase in haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in the erythrocyte to the tissues (Bocci 2004; Bottegaro et al. 2018; Jaramillo et al. 2020). Therefore, the results obtained in the present study indicate that ozone therapy can enhance the generation of erythrocytes with improved metabolic characteristics due to the adaptive responses promoted. These results corroborate a previous study suggesting that post‐ozone cells are a kind of ‘supercharged erythrocyte’ (Bocci 2004). This type of erythrocyte could correct hypoxia in vascular diseases and muscle hypoxia generated after exercise effort in sport horses.

Ozone, when reacting with the lipid compounds of the red blood cell membrane in human blood, generates lipid oxidation products (LOPs). Some studies have suggested that repeated therapeutic sessions may allow LOPs to reach the bone marrow and subtly activate erythropoietic development (Bhatt et al. 2016; Bocci 2004). This process stimulates the formation of new erythrocytes. However, the mechanism by which this occurs is not fully elucidated. It is reasonable to suppose that oxidised cells, such as those observed in the present study, when replaced, also contribute to this bone marrow activation, as the reduction in haemoglobin causes transient hypoxia that would stimulate erythropoiesis, a hypothesis needing confirmation in future studies through renal assessment. Previous studies that used ozone autohaemotherapy in horses did not report alterations in red blood cell morphology. The animals used in the present study are sport horses that participate in competitions and are subjected to a daily training routine; the oxidative stress caused by exercise may potentially be greater than in animals used in previous studies, accounting for the observed differences.

In the present study, alterations in red blood cell membranes were observed. It is known that damaged cells are removed from circulation, possibly leading to the reduction in haemoglobin levels after ozone therapy (Mark et al. 2008). Additionally, there was a tendency for increased fibrinogen levels. This result may be due to the presence of free radicals or injuries to red blood cells. In horses, this protein signals an inflammatory process and can rise from one to ten times within 24 to 72 h following the induction of inflammation (Trilk et al. 2002). Generally, the safety of ozone is questioned due to its strong oxidising power. However, reports of side effects are scarce and described as dose‐dependent, emphasising the need to consider doses, concentrations, and routes of application (Tsuzuki et al. 2015). Complementarily, it was found that reported side effects were also related to the incorrect use of the technique (Vendruscolo et al. 2018). The results of the present study suggest that ozone can improve indicators of muscle recovery and is safe as it does not cause liver damage. The trainer's report, indicating an observed improvement in performance, is consistent with the findings. However, the data obtained should be considered cautiously due to the small sample size (N = 12). Thus, a study with a larger number of animals, followed by performance tests for horses in recovery competitions, should be conducted to substantiate its application as a beneficial and safe therapy for sport horses. Furthermore, intrarectal ozone therapy yielded more positive responses than haematological ozone therapy, such as increased haemoglobin and MCHC concentrations. Besides these positive results, it should be noted that intrarectal ozone therapy is less invasive than autohaemotherapy. This is because the intrarectal route reduces the risk of systemic blood infection. Additionally, its application is simpler and does not require other materials, such as transfusion bags, catheters, or other equipment that increases the risk of contamination and the costs of the procedure.

Despite the positive results in haematological and biochemical parameters, both scales used in this study to measure pain in horses (Horse Grimace Scale – HGS and Equine Pain Face – EPF) indicated that intrarectal administration of ozone causes pain in the animals. This finding contradicts previous studies that reported the induction of analgesic effects with the use of ozone (Alves et al. 2004; Artis et al. 2010; Assenza et al. 2016; Ballardini 2005). However, none of these studies employed the rectal route. Conversely, in previous studies investigating the intrarectal administration of ozone therapy in horses, the authors did not address the potential analgesic effects associated with this treatment modality (Haddad et al. 2009a; Haddad et al. 2009b).

The pain caused by intrarectal ozone therapy observed in this study can be attributed to the fact that the concentration and dose of ozone used here were higher than those typically applied in clinical practice. In the only study that tested intrarectal ozone therapy in horses, the authors administered 10 ozone applications with increasing concentrations (two applications of 10 µg O3/mL, two applications of 15 µg O3/mL, and six applications of 20 µg O3/mL) (Haddad et al. 2009a). The concentration of 30 µg O3/mL, dose of 60 µg O3/kg, and infusion rate used here were based on commonly used ozone therapy guidelines (Jaramillo et al. 2020). Therefore, future studies need to test lower concentrations, doses, and/or infusion rates of ozone, as intrarectal ozone therapy should not cause pain in the animals.

The model using the HGS scale exhibited lower values for the Corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). However, the model using the EPF scale had a higher coefficient of determination (R 2) compared to the HGS scale. Moreover, the use of the EPF scale allowed for a more detailed analysis of facial and behavioural manifestations associated with pain. For instance, the application of the EPF scale detected significant effects of interactions between the treatment phase and the order of application, as well as between phase and sex, which were not identified by the HGS scale. This difference can be attributed to the fact that while the HGS relies on static photographs, the EPF utilises video clips. This allows for the capture of subtle variations in the facial and behavioural expressions of the horses. Recently, modifications to the HGS scale have been suggested, such as incorporating anatomical knowledge and more comprehensive descriptions of facial action units (FAUs) to improve the accuracy of pain assessment using this scale (Torcivia and Mcdonnell 2021). Using the EPF scale, it was observed that in the fourth application of ozone therapy, there was no significant difference in the pain level assessed in the control phase compared to the treatment application phase. This suggests that the pain caused by the third application of ozone therapy persisted even 48 h after intrarectal ozone insufflation, the interval between treatment applications, which was not observed in the other applications.

The pain detected using both HGS and EPF scales, however, was not reflected in the heart rate (HR) and respiratory rate (RR) of the animals, which are also used as indicators of pain (Satué et al. 2014). It is possible that the lack of changes in both HR and RR due to ozone therapy can be explained by the fact that the animals used in the study participate in sports competitions and are therefore subjected to constant training. Animals in competitions typically exhibit cardiorespiratory conditioning and, consequently, show little variation in HR and RR (Werner et al. 2024).

As previously noted, the data obtained should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size (N = 12). Furthermore, future research should investigate the effects of lower concentrations, doses, and/or infusion rates of ozone, given that intrarectal ozone therapy should not elicit pain responses in the animals. Moreover, subsequent studies should incorporate a control group consisting of horses not engaged in competitive sporting activities, to better elucidate potential treatment effects.

Overall, intrarectal ozone therapy in horses led to an increase in haemoglobin, MCHC, and hyperchromia of erythrocytes after 7 days of application, indicating improved tissue oxygenation. Additionally, intrarectal ozone therapy reduced AST levels, suggesting protection of hepatic and striated muscle. Erythrocytes with membrane deformities, consistent with oxidative lesions caused by the treatment, were also observed. Conversely, the results of this study reveal that ozone therapy via rectal insufflation causes pain in horses at the concentrations, doses, and insufflation rates tested here. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct further studies to test lower concentrations, doses, and/or infusion rates of ozone for this route of administration. These findings suggest the potential therapeutic impact of this clinical approach on the recovery of sport horses. Future investigations and refinement of this therapeutic strategy aimed at improving the athletic performance of horses are required to deepen the understanding of the underlying mechanisms and long‐term adverse effects. Additionally, it is recommended that future studies use the EPF pain scale to evaluate the animals’ reactions to the treatment. This will allow for a detailed analysis of the impact of ozone therapy on the horses' well‐being, providing valuable data to adjust therapeutic strategies and improve clinical protocols.
