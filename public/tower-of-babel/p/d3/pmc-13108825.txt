Variability in Drug–Drug Interaction Identification Across Commonly Used Drug Information Resources: An Analysis of Real world Tertiary Care Prescriptions (Retrospective Cross sectional Study)
Authors: Swastik Verma, Shubham Atal, Bhavya Bhavya, Balakrishnan Sadasivam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:This study aimed to evaluate and compare three popular drug interaction checkers – Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com – in detecting major drug–drug interactions (DDIs) from real-world prescriptions in a tertiary care hospital.Methods:We analyzed 500 randomly selected prescriptions from a hospital database, identifying 1133 unique drug pairs. Each pair was checked for potential interactions using all three platforms. We compared how often DDIs were detected, their severity ratings, and agreement between tools using Cohen’s kappa for concordance.Finding:Polypharmacy, defined as five or more drugs, was present in 23.8% of prescriptions, with an average of 6.3 drugs per patient. Polypharmacy was strongly linked to severe DDIs, showing nearly a threefold increased risk. Drugs.com identified the highest number of DDIs (21%), followed by Lexidrug (19.6%) and Medscape (16.2%). Severity ratings varied, and agreement between the tools was moderate (κ = 0.47–0.50).Conclusion:There is notable variation in detecting and classifying major DDIs among these common tools. Standardizing databases and interaction definitions is important. Meanwhile, clinicians should use multiple checkers alongside their clinical judgment to ensure safer prescribing when managing patients on many drugs.

INTRODUCTION

Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) represent an important and well-recognized challenge in clinical practice, particularly in tertiary care settings where patients are frequently prescribed multiple medications for complex and chronic conditions. DDIs occur when one drug alters the pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics of another, potentially leading to reduced therapeutic efficacy or adverse drug reactions (ADRs).[12] A systematic review reported that while the median prevalence of DDI-related hospital admissions was relatively low, DDIs accounted for a substantial proportion of ADR-related admissions, with a median prevalence of 22.2% (interquartile range: 16.6%–36.0%).[3]

To mitigate DDI-related risks, drug interaction screening tools are widely used as part of clinical decision support. Multiple online and subscription-based platforms, including Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com provides automated alerts regarding potential DDIs and their clinical significance. However, these tools rely on proprietary databases, differing evidence hierarchies, and distinct severity classification frameworks. As a result, the same drug combination may be classified differently across platforms with respect to detection and severity.[45]

In tertiary care hospitals, prescribing complexity characterized by the use of multiple medications, combination therapies, and prophylactic coprescribing further increases the likelihood of encountering DDI alerts. While polypharmacy is a recognized contributor to interaction burden, it represents one of several contextual factors influencing DDI detection.[678]

In the Indian healthcare context, prescribing patterns are shaped by widespread use of branded generics, fixed-dose combinations (FDCs), and commonly co-prescribed supportive medications. These factors may influence how commonly encountered drug combinations are represented within international DDI databases. Despite the routine use of online interaction checkers, there is limited real-world evidence from Indian tertiary care settings examining the degree of concordance or discordance among commonly used DDI screening platforms when applied to actual prescriptions.

Accordingly, this study aims to assess and compare the variability in detection and severity categorization of potential DDIs across three commonly used drug interaction checkers-Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com-using real-world prescription audit data from a tertiary care center. Rather than identifying a single “best” tool, the study seeks to describe and compare how different platforms classify potential DDIs when applied to real-world prescriptions from a tertiary care setting.

METHODS

The study was conducted in the Department of Pharmacology at a tertiary care center in Central India. The primary objective was to assess and compare the performance of three widely used drug interaction checkers – Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com[91011] in identifying major DDIs from drug combinations prescribed in a tertiary outpatient setting and also to check for the agreement or disagreement between the checkers. The primary outcome of the study was the proportion of potential DDIs detected by each interaction checker and their corresponding severity classification. Secondary outcomes included the level of agreement between interaction checkers as assessed by Cohen’s kappa statistic and the association between polypharmacy and the occurrence of clinically significant DDIs. The data for this study were obtained from the prescription audit database of the Pharmacology Department. A total of 4523 prescriptions from this database were reviewed. The prescriptions were reviewed during April 2025. Prescriptions were included if they contained two or more systemically administered drugs and were issued to adult patients (≥18 years) in the outpatient department during the study period. Prescriptions containing at least one identifiable drug name with a clearly documented dosage form were eligible for analysis. Prescriptions were excluded if they contained only a single drug, illegible drug names, incomplete medication information, or nonpharmacological orders alone. Prescriptions consisting exclusively of topical preparations, vaccines, intravenous fluids, herbal products, or nutritional supplements were also excluded from analysis. From these, 500 prescriptions were randomly selected using the randomization function in Microsoft Excel. This random sampling method ensured that the selection was unbiased and representative of the overall prescription database. For each of the 500 selected prescriptions, drug combinations were generated by applying permutation and combination techniques. This allowed for the systematic creation of drug pairs, which were the units of analysis for identifying potential DDIs. The analysis was performed from May to July 2025. Each drug pair was then subjected to analysis using the three chosen drug interaction checkers. Unique drug combination pairs were then generated. The prescriptions with ≥5 drugs prescribed were also identified to assess the prevalence of polypharmacy. The selection of the three drug interaction checkers was based on their well-known usage in common practice and review of previous studies.[4121314] Lexidrug interaction checker (Retrieved May 2025) classified the DDIs in the following five categories: A, no known interaction; B, no action needed; C, monitor therapy; D, consider therapy modification; X, avoid combination.[9] The Medscape tool (Retrieved May 2025) classified the DDIs as follows into four categories: contraindicated, serious–use alternative, monitor closely, and minor. The degree of severity was indicated by: Contraindicated, serious–use alternative monitor closely, and minor.[1012] Drugs.com (Retrieved May 2025) classified DDIs in the following three categories: Major DDIs (Highly clinically significant, Avoid combinations; the risk of the interaction outweighs the benefit), Moderate DDIs (Moderate clinically significant. Usually avoid combinations, use it only under special circumstances), Minor DDIs (Minimally clinically significant. Minimize risk; assess risk and consider an alternative drug, take steps to circumvent the interaction risk and/or institute a monitoring plan).[1112] All the checkers were accessed during May to July 2025. For the purpose of comparison, severity categories were analyzed as reported by each platform without attempting to reclassify or clinically adjudicate individual interactions. For comparative analysis and agreement assessment, interactions categorized as “avoid combination” (Lexidrug X), “serious/use alternative” or “contraindicated” (Medscape), and “major” (Drugs.com) were operationally grouped as clinically significant interactions. After analyzing the unique drug pairs with each checker, the results were compared to assess consistency and discrepancies in the identification of major DDIs. The total number of interactions detected by each tool, as well as the severity classifications, was recorded and compared across the three platforms. Identification of interactions in this study was deemed to reflect database-generated alerts and not imply prescribing error, clinical inappropriateness, or patient harm. The results from the three drug interaction checkers were compiled in a Microsoft Excel file and analyzed to identify both concordant and discordant findings. Cohen’s kappa was employed to assess the level of agreement between the tools, providing a measure of their reliability.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize prescription characteristics and DDI frequencies. The association between polypharmacy (defined as ≥5 drugs per prescription) and the presence of clinically significant DDIs was assessed using the Chi-square test, with relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. Agreement between drug interaction checkers was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel.

RESULTS

A total of 500 prescriptions were analyzed, generating a total of 2020 drug combinations; out of which 1133 unique drug combination pairs were generated. Out of the 500 prescriptions analyzed, 119 (23.8%) were found to have polypharmacy, in which an average of 6.3 drugs were prescribed. Out of these 119 prescriptions with polypharmacy, 18 prescriptions were found to have significant DDIs with at least 1 checker. Chi-square analysis showed a significant association between polypharmacy and the occurrence of significant DDIs (χ² = 12.6, P < 0.001). Prescriptions with polypharmacy had nearly three times higher risk of significant DDIs compared to those without (RR = 2.88; 95% CI: 1.58-5.26).

Figure 1 illustrates the percentage distribution of DDI severity categories for all evaluated drug pairs across the three-interaction checkers – Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com. The comparative analysis of DDI severity across the three checkers revealed that clinically significant interactions constituted a relatively small fraction of total pairs, although their distribution varied between platforms. In Lexidrug, 6.88% of pairs were categorized as “C” (monitor therapy), 1.28% as “B” (no action needed), 2.3% as “D” (consider therapy modification), and 0.4% as “X” (avoid combination). Medscape identified 7.6% of pairs as requiring close monitoring, 0.8% as minor, and 0.5% as serious, warranting alternative therapy. Drugs.com classified 7.72% of pairs as moderate, 2.4% as minor, and 1.63% as major. The majority of the rest of the drug pairs were classified as having no clinically relevant interaction; 88.21%–90.9% across the three checkers.

Figure 2 illustrates the absolute number of DDIs identified in each severity category by Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com, excluding “no interaction” cases. Lexidrug drug interaction checker identified a total of 222 (19.6%) DDIs out of the total drug combinations. These DDIs were further classified as follows: 26 (11.71%) belong to category-B (no action) type; 139 (62.61%) belong to category-C (monitor therapy); 47 (21.17%) belong to category-D (consider therapy modification), and 10 (4.5%) belong to category-X (avoid combination) type of interaction. Medscape drug interaction checker identified a total of 183 (16.2%) possible DDIs out of the total drug combinations evaluated. These were further classified as follows: 17 (9.28%) were of minor type; 154 (84.15%) were to be monitored closely, and 12 (6.55%) to be considered serious or alternative drug use advised. Drugs.com identified a total of 238 (21%) possible DDIs out of the total drug combinations found. These were further classified as follows: 49 (20.58%) were of minor type, 156 (65.55%) were of moderate type, and 33 (13.86%) were of major type.

Out of all three checkers studied, Drugs.com identified the highest number of possible DDIs (21%), followed by Lexidrug (19.6%) and Medscape (16.2%). Among the analyzed drug combinations, several pairs were found to exhibit significant interactions. Figure 3 presents the frequency of the most commonly prescribed drug combinations out of a total of 500 prescriptions. The combination of calcium with cholecalciferol was the most frequently prescribed (n = 31; 6.2%), followed by domperidone with pantoprazole (n = 23; 4.6%) and aceclofenac with paracetamol (n = 22; 4.4%). Pantoprazole with diclofenac (n = 21; 4.2%) and pantoprazole with cholecalciferol (n = 20; 4.0%) were prescribed in similar numbers to amoxicillin with clavulanate (n = 20; 4.0%) and paracetamol with pantoprazole (n = 19; 3.8%).

DISCUSSION

This study primarily examined the variability in detection and severity categorization of potential DDIs across three commonly used interaction checkers – Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com-when applied to real-world prescriptions from a tertiary care setting. Among the 500 prescriptions analyzed, a substantial proportion of drug combinations were flagged as having potential interactions by at least one platform, with Drugs.com, identifying the highest number of interactions, followed by Lexidrug and Medscape. The Cohen’s kappa analysis showed moderate agreement among the three platforms (ranging from 0.47 to 0.5), underscoring that while there is some consistency, disagreement remains notable. This moderate level of concordance is similar to findings in prior research,[16] indicating that while interaction checkers are valuable, no single tool can be considered universally reliable for clinical decision-making. The finding suggests that discrepancies exist between commonly used checkers, highlighting the importance of clinical judgment in interpreting drug interaction data.

Interpretation of flagged DDIs requires careful contextualization. Identification of a clinically significant interaction by a screening tool does not necessarily indicate a prescribing error or contraindicated use. Some of the flagged combinations represent well-established, guideline-directed, or intentionally co-prescribed therapies that require monitoring, dose adjustment, or individualized risk–benefit assessment rather than avoidance. As this study was based on retrospective prescription audit data, interactions were not evaluated for parameters like route of administration, duration of therapy, patient-specific risk factors, or downstream clinical outcomes. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as differences in interaction flagging and severity categorization across tools, rather than direct measures of clinical appropriateness or patient harm.

Such findings align with previous comparative studies, which have also reported inconsistencies in how interaction checkers classify and report DDIs. Kheshti et al. evaluated the comprehensiveness and accuracy of five common DDI software programs by analyzing 40 drug pairs out of 360 prescriptions used and reported inconsistencies not only in the number of interactions detected but also in how they were categorized by severity.[4] Similarly, Fung and colleagues highlighted structural and interpretational differences among commercial drug knowledge bases, which can influence decision-making and, consequently, patient outcomes.[17] A study by Alkhalid et al.[13] examined the drug interaction checkers Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com in Northern Cyprus and found significant differences in their detection of potential DDIs, with Medscape identifying the most interactions and moderate agreement among other checkers.

Differences in checker behavior were particularly evident in severity categorization in our analysis. Drugs.com flagged a greater proportion of interactions as major, suggesting higher sensitivity but also raising the possibility of over-alerting. Lexidrug demonstrated a balanced distribution across “monitor therapy,” “consider therapy modification,” and “avoid combination” categories, reflecting its design as a clinical decision support tool often used within institutional settings. Medscape, while highly accessible and user-friendly, classified fewer interactions as high-risk, which may reduce alert burden but could also lead to under-recognition of potentially important interactions. These findings highlight that each platform embodies trade-offs between sensitivity, specificity, and clinical usability.

The association observed between polypharmacy and the presence of interactions flagged as clinically significant reinforces existing evidence that increasing medication burden elevates the likelihood of DDI alerts. Prescriptions containing five or more drugs demonstrated a substantially higher probability of having interactions identified by at least one checker. While this relationship is well recognized, its demonstration within real-world tertiary care prescriptions emphasizes the practical value of DDI screening in settings where complex regimens are common. There is need for clinicians to use multiple checkers and rely on their clinical judgment for safe prescribing in polypharmacy cases.[13]

Analysis of frequently prescribed drug combinations further illustrates how interaction alerts intersect with everyday clinical practice. Pantoprazole-based combinations and commonly used analgesic, cardiovascular, and psychotropic drug pairs featured prominently in the dataset. Many of these combinations are clinically justified and widely used, yet their recurrent appearance among flagged interactions underscores the importance of systematic review and monitoring rather than automatic avoidance. In such scenarios, inconsistent severity categorization across tools may create uncertainty for prescribers, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to clinical pharmacology support is constrained.

From an Indian healthcare perspective, these findings carry additional relevance. Prescribing patterns characterized by widespread use of branded generics, FDCs, and prophylactic co-prescribing may not be uniformly represented across international DDI databases.[514] Consequently, interaction checkers developed primarily in high-income settings may differ in how they flag commonly encountered combinations in Indian clinical practice. The reliance on freely available tools such as Medscape and Drugs.com further amplifies the importance of understanding their limitations and interpretive variability.

The strengths of this study include the use of real-world prescription audit data and the focus on interaction checkers that are commonly accessed by clinicians in routine practice. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis was restricted to three interaction checkers, and inclusion of additional platforms such as Micromedex or IBM Watson Drug Database could have provided broader comparative insight.[17] More importantly, the study did not assess clinical outcomes associated with flagged interactions, nor did it involve adjudication of alerts by clinicians. The analysis excluded over-the-counter medications, herbal supplements, and undocumented drugs, common in Indian practice, potentially underestimating interaction risk. Furthermore, conducting the study in a single tertiary care hospital in Central India may limit its applicability to other regions with different drug formularies and patient demographics.[18]

Despite these limitations, the study provides practical evidence that commonly used DDI checkers can yield divergent outputs when applied to identical prescriptions. This variability has important implications for clinical decision-making, particularly in high polypharmacy environments such as tertiary care settings. Until greater standardization and transparency in DDI databases are achieved, clinicians should interpret interaction alerts within clinical context and, where feasible, consult multiple information sources rather than relying on a single checker. Such an approach may help balance patient safety with pragmatic prescribing in tertiary care settings.[519]

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates substantial variability in the detection and severity classification of potential DDIs across commonly used interaction checkers when applied to identical real-world prescriptions from a tertiary care setting. Although moderate agreement was observed, clinically significant discrepancies persist, particularly in severity categorization. These findings emphasize that interaction alerts should be interpreted within a clinical context rather than relied upon in isolation. In high-polypharmacy environments, cross-verification across multiple tools combined with clinical judgment may enhance medication safety and reduce alert fatigue. Further efforts toward standardization and transparency in drug interaction databases are warranted to improve consistency in clinical decision support systems.
