Fluorine-free acetonitrile-based electrolytes with anion-reinforced solvation chemistry for sustainable sodium-ion full batteries
Authors: Xu Xu, Jian Chen, Zhuo Yang, Zhiming Zhou, Xiaosa Zhang, Xinhui Zeng, Yun Wan, Xiaomin Chen, Wenxi Kuang, Xiaoyan Shi, Lingling Zhang, Xunzhu Zhou, Shulei Chou, Lin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcetonitrile-based electrolytes have garnered significant attention for sodium-ion batteries due to their high ionic conductivity and favorable oxidation resistance. However, their practical implementation is hindered by an unsatisfactory anti-reduction ability, leading to poor compatibility with hard carbon anode and, consequently, rapid capacity degradation. Herein, we employ a widely used commercial carbonate ester solvent (ethyl methyl carbonate) to modulate the ion-dipole interactions in fluorine-free acetonitrile-based electrolytes. The relatively weak solvation ability of ethyl methyl carbonate facilitates more anions to participate the inner solvation sheath, thereby promoting the formation of robust electrode-electrolyte interface at a wide temperature range. This stabilized interface effectively suppresses continuous electrolyte decomposition, significantly improving the compatibility between fluorine-free acetonitrile-based electrolytes and hard carbon anodes. As a result, the optimized electrolyte enables stable operation of hard carbon ||Prussian blue full cells over a wide temperature range (25-100 °C), demonstrating a capacity retention of 62.5% after 600 cycles at 55 °C and 1.0 C. It is worth noting that Ampere-hour-level hard carbon ||Prussian blue pouch cells deliver consistent electrochemical performance at both 25 °C and 55 °C. This study elucidates the mechanistic role of cosolvent engineering in enhancing the reductive stability of fluorine-free acetonitrile-based electrolytes and provides a viable electrolyte design strategy for their practical implementation in sodium-ion batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Electrochemical energy storage technologies serve as a pivotal approach for achieving efficient utilization of renewable clean energy1–3. Among existing electrochemical energy storage systems, sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) have emerged as promising candidates for large-scale energy storage applications owing to their abundant sodium resources and significant cost advantages4–7. Nevertheless, the relatively large ionic radius of Na+ (1.02 Å vs. 0.76 Å of Li+) induces substantial volume variations in electrode materials during the charge/discharge process, resulting in material pulverization with rapid capacity degradation8–11. An ideal electrolyte can facilitate the formation of a stable electrode-electrolyte interface (EEI) at the surface of the cathode and anode12,13. The stable EEI not only effectively preserves the structural integrity of electrode materials but also suppresses successive electrolyte decomposition, thereby significantly enhancing the overall electrochemical performance of SIBs14–16. Consequently, the development of advanced electrolyte systems is of paramount scientific significance and engineering value for promoting the commercial application of sodium-ion full cells.

Solvents, as an indispensable component of liquid electrolytes for SIBs, significantly influence the components and stability of EEI17,18. To date, various solvents have been employed in SIBs electrolytes, including ethylene carbonate (EC)19, diethyl carbonate (DEC)20,21, propylene carbonate (PC)22,23, triethyl phosphate (TEP)24,25, dimethoxyethane (DME)26,27, dimethyl carbonate (DMC)28, ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC)29, 1,3-dioxolane (DOL)30, acetonitrile (AN), etc. Among these solvents, AN has attracted considerable attention due to its favorable oxidative stability and high salt solubility31. However, its practical application is hindered by undesirable reductive stability, which leads to poor compatibility with anode materials and consequent deterioration of electrochemical performance. Several strategies have been developed to enhance the reductive stability of AN-based electrolytes, such as constructing high-concentration electrolytes32–35 or localized high-concentration electrolytes36, incorporating functional electrolyte additives37, and so on38,39. Nevertheless, these approaches inevitably increase the manufacturing costs of electrolytes, thereby limiting their practical application. Consequently, exploring a facile and low-cost electrolyte design strategy and elucidating its underlying mechanism are of paramount importance for advancing the practical application of AN-based electrolytes in sodium-ion full cells.

In this work, we construct an anion-enhanced solvation structure by introducing conventional ester cosolvents into fluorine-free AN-based electrolytes to regulate the ion-dipole interactions. This distinctive solvation structure enables the synchronous formation of robust inorganic-rich EEI at both cathode and anode, significantly improving the reductive stability of fluorine-free AN-based electrolytes. Furthermore, the positive effect of EMC works well on constructing the anion-reinforced solvation structure and anion-derived EEI at elevated temperature. Therefore, hard carbon (HC)||Prussian blue (PB) full cells with optimized fluorine-free AN-based electrolyte exhibit stable operation over a wide-temperature performance (25-100 °C), maintaining a capacity retention rate of 62.5% after 600 cycles at 55 °C. Notably, validation experiments through Ampere-hour (Ah)-level HC || PB pouch cells further confirm the universal applicability and industrial potential of this electrolyte design strategy in practical applications.

Results

Electrolyte design

The strong coordinating ability of AN facilitates the formation of a solvent-dominated solvation chemistry, which is the intrinsic reason why it fails to construct a stable EEI and further triggers continuous electrolyte decomposition. In general, fluorine-containing components facilitate the formation of a NaF-rich EEI, thereby effectively enhancing the long-term cycling stability of SIBs40,41. However, the inherent drawbacks of fluorine-containing electrolytes have limited their large-scale application: the high toxicity of NaF, the leakage risks of HF and POF3, the harmful side reactions triggered by trace protic impurities, as well as the high cost, all significantly increase the application cost, environmental burden and recycling difficulty of SIBs42,43. In this work, we employ a cosolvent with relatively weak coordination capability to modulate the ion-dipole interactions in fluorine-free NaClO4/AN-based electrolytes, thereby constructing an anion-reinforced solvation chemistry (Fig. 1a). This solvation configuration facilitates the formation of stable EEI at both cathode and anode surfaces, effectively preventing continuous electrolyte decomposition. As a result, the compatibility between AN-based electrolyte and HC anode is significantly improved, enabling their practical application in sodium-ion full batteries.

Commercially available linear carbonate solvents (EMC, DMC and DEC) with relatively weak coordination ability than AN (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1) and cost advantages are ideal cosolvent candidates to modulate ion-dipole interactions44. Compared with DMC and DEC, the hybrid AN-based electrolyte with EMC exhibits the best electrochemical performance (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, EMC is employed as a model cosolvent to validate our electrolyte design strategy. Meanwhile, the intermolecular interactions between Na+ and solvent are calculated based on the density functional theory (DFT). EMC exhibits the smaller binding energy with Na+ than AN counterpart ( -31.87 vs. -33.38 kcal mol-1), indicating its relatively weak coordination capability toward Na+ (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table 2). As shown in Fig. 1d, AN endows a more negative electron affinity energy (EAE) of 24.5 eV, compared with that of 31.0 eV in EMC, indicative of the poor anti-reduction ability of AN solvent45. According to the frontier molecular orbital theory, EMC exhibits a higher lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level (0.07 eV) than AN (-0.32 eV), suggesting a lower tendency for reduction (Fig. 1e). This qualitative trend may contribute to improved interfacial stability between the AN-based electrolyte and the HC anode46,47. Compared with the blank electrolyte 1.0 M NaClO4-AN + 3 wt% VC (NAV), the designed electrolyte 1.0 M NaClO4-AN/EMC (1:1 vol. ratio) + 3 wt% VC (NAEV) electrolyte shows an acceptable ionic conductivity and viscosity (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4 and Table 3), which are much higher than that of the commonly used commercial carbonate electrolyte, 1.0 M NaPF6-EC/EMC + 5 wt% FEC (1:1 vol. ratio). The introduction of EMC also lowers the freezing point of electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, we investigate the effect of salt types on the electrochemical performance of batteries, and the results demonstrate that NaClO4 exhibits the optimal cycling performance (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7).

Solvation configuration

To systematically investigate the solvation structure and coordination environment influenced by EMC cosolvent, spectroscopic characterizations and theoretical calculations are employed. As the Raman spectra shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 8, free AN exhibits the -C≡N characteristic bond at 2251.0 cm-1, which blueshifts to 2262.5 cm-1 as coordinated with Na+ ions48,49. The ratio between free AN and coordinated AN is obtained by comparing their peak areas. The proportion of free AN increases from 76.3% to 83.4% after introducing the EMC solvent, corresponding to the weakened Na+-AN interactions in the NAEV electrolyte. And this trend is also proved by the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 9, with a lower proportion of coordinated AN in NAEV electrolyte39. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is performed to further elucidate the intermolecular interactions in Fig. 2c, where the 1H resonance signal shifts upfield from 2.66 to 2.48 ppm due to the presence of EMC. This shift reflects the altered electronic environment around hydrogen atoms in AN, possibly due to the increased electron cloud density, which is closely related to dipole-dipole interactions between EMC and AN50. Such interactions may lead to a looser arrangement of solvent molecules at the EEI, facilitating easier Na+ access with lower desolvation energy51. In the 23Na NMR spectra (Fig. 2d), a pronounced upfield shift of the Na+ resonance is observed in the NAEV electrolyte (from -7.10 ppm in NAV to -8.60 ppm), indicating the increased shielding effect around the Na+52. In addition, the observed peak broadening in NAEV is attributed to the increased heterogeneity and intensified short-range interactions around Na+, a signature commonly linked to enhanced Na+-anion correlations within the solvation shell53,54. These features collectively support the formation of an anion-enriched local solvation structure in the NAEV electrolyte.

To gain atomic-level insight into the solvation configuration and local coordination environment of Na+, classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 10). Na+ is mainly solvated by AN solvent with a sharp peak of Na-NAN pair in NAV electrolyte, as the snapshots, radial distribution functions and coordination numbers shown in Fig. 2e. While EMC extensively occupies the inner Na+ solvation sheath with high intensity of Na-OEMC pair, with the small distance of 2.40 Å between Na+ and EMC (Fig. 2f), demonstrating the reinforced Na+-EMC interactions in NAEV electrolyte55. At the same time, the Na+-AN and Na+-ClO4- interactions are weakened after introducing EMC cosolvent, as evidenced by the increased distance of Na+-NAN and Na+-OClO4 pair, with the value from 2.52 to 2.56 Å and 2.48 to 2.54 Å, respectively. As summarized in Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 4, EMC cosolvents participate in the inner solvation sheath with a high coordination number of 1.82, which decreases the coordination number of AN from 4.93 to 2.78 and increases the value of coordinated ClO4- from 0.56 to 0.80. Figure 2h exhibits the inner solvation configurations observed from MD simulations, where an increased diversity is presented after introducing the EMC cosolvent. Inset exhibits the corresponding proportion of SSIP (solvent-separated ion pair, free anion), CIP (contact ion pair, a Na+ coordinated with one anion), and AGG (aggregate, a Na+ coordinated with two or more anions), where the incorporation of EMC increases the proportion of anion-involved species (Supplementary Table 5). Furthermore, solvent-dominated configurations account for 60.4% in the NAV electrolyte, whereas anion-dominated configurations exceed 55.9% in the NAEV system (Supplementary Table 6). These emphasize the construction of anion-reinforced solvation structure upon EMC incorporation, which favors the anion-derived solvation chemistry with enhanced interfacial stability56.

Interfacial chemistry

To further investigate the beneficial effects of EMC cosolvents, a series of representative solvation structures were extracted from both NAV and NAEV electrolytes based on MD simulations, followed by detailed theoretical calculations (Supplementary Fig. 11). As shown in Fig. 3a, substituting AN with EMC leads to the higher HOMO energy levels and lower LUMO energy levels, highlighting the preferential decomposition as EEI at both cathode and anode in NAEV electrolyte. Besides, as the ESP illustrated in the inset of Fig. 3a, the negative potential is mainly localized around the N atoms of AN and the O atoms of EMC, while the positive potential is concentrated around the H atoms of alkyl chains in the Na+-solvent complexes. Notably, solvation clusters with EMC exhibit the more positive ESP values, which is favorable for the enhanced desolvation dynamics45. Thereafter, the influence of EMC cosolvent on interfacial stability and electrode performance is further explored. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is employed to characterize the thickness and uniformity of the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) on the HC anode after 100 cycles. As shown in Fig. 3b, a uniform SEI layer with a thickness of ca. 15.5 nm is observed in NAEV electrolyte, in sharp contrast to the rough and ununiform one with a thickness ranging from 23.2 to 49.3 nm in NAV, highlighting the positive effect of EMC cosolvent on suppressing the side reactions.

The composition and structure of SEI are the core factors determining its thickness uniformity, mechanical stability, and ionic transport properties. The decomposition products of solvents and anions in Na+ solvation sheath are key to regulating the composition and structure of the SEI. The EMC-induced anion-reinforced solvation structure can effectively inhibit the reductive decomposition of AN solvent molecules on the anode surface, while significantly facilitating the generation of anion-derived inorganic components in the SEI. To gain deeper insight into the chemical composition and distribution of SEI, depth-profiling X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is employed. N 1s spectra display peaks corresponding to C≡N (~400 eV) and Na3N (~399 eV) in Figs. 3c, d, which originate from the decomposition of AN37. NAEV electrolyte exhibits much lower atomics of N at different etching depth, indicative of the suppressed decomposition of AN. Moreover, as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 12, the proportion of inorganic Na3N within the SEI increases from 42.7% to 64.0% after the cooperation of EMC cosolvent, which endows the rapid Na+ transport across the interphase. In addition, characteristic peaks associated with both organic (ROCO2Na at ~1073 eV) and inorganic components (Na3N at ~1070 eV, Na2O at ~1070 eV, Na2CO3 at ~1071 eV, and NaCl at ~1072 eV) are observed in Na 1s spectra (Fig. 3e, f)57,58. The SEI formed in NAV electrolyte contains more organic species, such as ROCO2Na, whereas the SEI derived from NAEV electrolyte is enriched with inorganic components (Supplementary Fig. 13). This conclusion is further corroborated by the Cl 2p, C 1s and O 1s spectra (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15), as well as the atomic ratio analysis (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 16), collectively indicating the formation of a gradient inorganic-rich SEI in the NAEV electrolyte. Compared with the organic-rich SEI, SEI with a high content of inorganic components generally shows lower solubility in electrolytes and favorable ionic conductivity. Such an inorganic-rich SEI can not only fundamentally block the continuous decomposition of the electrolyte but also inhibit dissolution and reconstruction during cycling by virtue of its high stability, ultimately forming a thin and uniform interfacial layer.

In addition, highly sensitive time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry, TOF-SIMS, is performed for the compositional distribution and chemical structure of the SEI. Insights from the normalized intensity plots, 2D intensity depth distribution in Fig. 3h, D rendering images in Fig. 3i depict the obvious gradient distribution of inorganic species over sputtering time, such as CN-, Na3N-, NaCl-, Na2O- and so on. The higher content of inorganic species contributes to a mechanically robust SEI, which not only suppresses excessive solvent decomposition but also enhances Na+ transport across the SEI, thereby improving the interfacial stability on the HC anode. Thereafter, the structural stability of cycled HC anode is well maintained in NAEV electrolyte as the X-ray diffraction (XRD) displayed in Supplementary Fig. 17. Detailed information of cathode-electrolyte interface (CEI) on PB surface is also detected by TEM, in-depth XPS, TOF-SIMS and XRD, where the corporation of EMC works well on constructing the thin, robust and inorganic-rich interfacial layer (Supplementary Figs. 18–23).

Electrochemical performance

To further evaluate the practical applicability of the designed electrolyte, full cells are assembled with high-mass loading HC anode and PB cathode (Fig. 4a). As the long-term cycling performance and corresponding charge/discharge profiles shown in Figs. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. 24, HC||PB full cell with NAEV electrolyte exhibits significantly enhanced cycling stability, with a high capacity retention of 83.0% after 300 cycles at 1.0 C (1.0 C = 150 mA g-1), outperforming the 77.2% in NAV and 64.4% in commercial carbonate electrolyte, 1.0 M NaPF6-EC/EMC + 5 wt% FEC. Notably, even under a high specific current of 2.0 C, the NAEV-based cell maintains a high capacity of 114.4 mAh g-1 after 100 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 92.8%. While much lower values of 106.8 mAh g-1 and 88.7% are observed in NAV system under the identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 25). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, the NAEV electrolyte exhibits improved rate capability, delivering high discharge capacities of 124.39, 120.45, 117.86, 115.09, 113.34, 111.08, and 108.11 mAh g-1 at current rates of 0.2 C, 0.3 C, 0.5 C, 0.8 C, 1.0 C, 1.5 C, and 2.0 C, respectively. Furthermore, as summarized in Fig. 4d, the NAEV-based system demonstrates lower overpotentials and higher capacity retention than the NAV-based counterpart across different current densities. This improvement is attributed to the optimized solvation structure in the NAEV electrolyte, which facilitates faster desolvation and enhanced Na+ diffusion kinetics. In addition, NAEV electrolyte delivers a significantly higher Coulombic efficiency of 92.8% during the self-discharge tests, compared to 77.0% for the cell with NAV electrolyte, indicating its improved applicability for practical energy storage applications (Fig. 4e). Besides, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveals the markedly decreased impedance after long-term cycling in the NAEV system (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Table 7), which is attributed to the construction of robust anion-derived EEI with suppressed side reactions59.

Moreover, HC||PB pouch cell employing the NAEV electrolyte exhibits stable long-term cycling performance in Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 28. At a specific current of 1.0 C, the pouch cell delivers a high reversible capacity of 290.4 mAh after 500 cycles, corresponding to the capacity retention of 79.3%, along with the high average Coulombic efficiency of 99.8%. In contrast, the counterpart with NAV electrolyte suffers from rapid capacity degradation with low reversible capacity of 90.2 mAh and capacity retention of 23.7% after 200 cycles, which can be ascribed to severe electrolyte decomposition and side reactions (Supplementary Fig. 29). In addition to the improved cycling stability, the NAEV-based pouch cell also demonstrates good rate capability in Fig. 4h. Specially, pouch cell with NAEV electrolyte delivers a high initial discharge capacity of ca. 380 mAh at 0.2 C and achieves high capacity recovery (~370 mAh) after undergoing high-rate cycling, corresponding to a retention of ~96%. Notably, under a high specific current of 1.0 C, the NAEV-based cell still provides a high capacity of 348.4 mAh, compared to 245.8 mAh for the NAV-based cell. As illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 30, 31, pouch cell with NAEV effectively suppresses voltage polarization, further confirming its enhanced charge transfer kinetics. Moreover, the Ah-level HC||PB pouch cell delivers stable cycling stability, confirming the feasibility of the NAEV electrolyte under practical conditions (Figs. 4i, j). The universality of EMC on enhancing the cycling stability is also proved in other common nitrile solvents, as evidenced in Supplementary Figs. 32, 33. These results underscore the favorable interfacial stability enabled by the NAEV system, highlighting its potential for practical applications of sodium-ion batteries.

Varying-temperature properties

Tolerance against seasonal and daily temperature fluctuations puts forward higher requirements for stability and safety of the secondary batteries. The better temperature tolerance of the designed AN-based electrolyte is considered in Fig. 5a. The full cell with NAEV electrolyte exhibits good thermal adaptability across a wide temperature range, with 125.8 vs. 34.9 mAh g-1 at 100 °C. And the noticeable overcharging is effectively suppressed in the designed electrolyte, with a high Coulombic efficiency of 76.0%, much higher than that of 9.9% in NAV electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 34). At high operating temperature of 55 °C, full cell with NAEV delivers better rate performance, exhibiting a high capacity retention of 84% at 3.0 C (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 35, 36). Long-term cycling also confirms the stable stability of the NAEV system (a high capacity retention of 62.5% after 600 cycles, Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 37), whereas the NAV-based cell suffers severe capacity fading and Coulombic efficiency fluctuations during the long-term cycling at 55 °C. Moreover, the NAEV electrolyte also supports stable cycling of the HC||PB pouch cell at 55 °C, as displayed in Supplementary Figs. 38, 39. More importantly, the Ah-level HC||PB pouch cell using NAEV electrolyte demonstrates satisfactory cycling stability at 55 °C (Fig. 5d), with a high capacity of 1047.0 mAh after 100 cycles. In sharp contrast, Ah-level HC||PB pouch cell using NAV electrolyte can’t work under such high operating temperature, as evidenced by the severe side reactions, pronounced gas generation, and cell swelling in Supplementary Fig. 40. This result indicates that the NAEV electrolyte enables the formation of a robust electrode-electrolyte interface across a wide operating temperature range. Our work achieves a lower per-cycle capacity fading rate than other reported sodium-ion full cells, maintaining stable performance at elevated temperatures of 25 and 55 °C (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 8)24,45,60–67. Further increasing the operating temperature to 80 °C, HC||PB full cell with NAV electrolyte hardly works, influenced by the severe side reactions (Supplementary Fig. 41). In sharp contrast, full cell with NAEV remains stable cycling even at 80 °C, delivering the high capacity of 97.07 mAh g-1 and average Coulombic efficiency of 99.3% after 100 cycles. Furthermore, the NAEV electrolyte exhibits stable rate performance even at 80 °C. At a high rate of 3.0 C, it delivers a capacity of 97.2 mAh g-1, maintaining 86.6% of the initial specific capacity (Supplementary Fig. 42). These results collectively highlight that the incorporation of EMC cosolvent markedly enhances the thermal adaptability and electrochemical robustness of the electrolyte system, supporting its applicability in practical high-temperature operation.

Given the improved electrochemical performance of the NAEV electrolyte at elevated temperatures, it is necessary to investigate the intrinsic mechanism underlying its enhanced high-temperature performance. As the variable-temperature Raman spectroscopy shown in Fig. 5f, the bands in the range of 900-960 cm-1 are ascribed to the specific vibrations of ClO4- anions, corresponding to free ClO4- (~920 cm-1) and coordinated ClO4- (contact ion pairs at ~932 cm-1 and aggregates at ~940 cm-1), respectively54. Quantitative analysis of the band area in this region, as presented in Fig. 5g, reveals the relative population of these ion configurations as a function of temperature. The incorporation of EMC substantially reduces the proportion of free ClO4-, with 57.9% to 29.7% at 25 °C and 56.7% to 27.1% at 55 °C, associated with the construction of anion-reinforced solvation structure in NAEV electrolyte. Furthermore, a smaller proportion of free ClO4- is also observed as increasing the temperature, as evidenced by the 57.9% to 56.7% of NAV and 29.7% to 27.1% of NAEV electrolyte, indicative of the transition toward anion-rich solvation structures at elevated temperatures. This temperature-induced solvation reconstruction is further supported by variable-temperature MD simulations in Supplementary Fig. 43. As the radial distribution functions shown in Fig. 5h, smaller distance between Na+ and ClO4- anion is observed as increasing the temperature, with 2.54 Å at 25 °C vs. 2.48 Å at 55 °C in NAEV electrolyte. And this is also proved by the increased coordination number of ClO4- anion as summarized in Fig. 5i, Supplementary Fig. 44 and Table 9, as evidenced by 0.803 at 25 °C to 0.921 at 55 °C. The solubility of interfacial components in the electrolyte increases significantly at elevated temperatures, which poses a severe challenge to maintaining the stability of the EEI. The anion-reinforced solvation structure strengthened under high-temperature conditions can elevate the proportion of inorganic components in the EEI under high-temperature operating conditions. The characterization results from TEM, in-depth XPS clearly demonstrate that the electrode interface using the NAEV electrolyte can form a dense and stable EEI (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Figs. 45–51). This EEI film, enriched with inorganic components, can effectively suppress the repeated dissolution and reconstruction of the EEI during cycling, fundamentally block the continuous decomposition pathway of the electrolyte, and drastically reduce the degree of side reactions between the AN-based electrolyte and the HC anode, thereby improving their compatibility under high-temperature environments. Consequently, the HC||PB full cell exhibits good high-temperature cycling stability and rate capability in the NAEV electrolyte system. From the perspective of solvation structure regulation, this study successfully boosts the high-temperature compatibility between AN-based electrolytes and HC anodes through an interfacical chemistry optimization strategy, providing important theoretical insights and feasible practical approaches for the rational design of electrolytes for wide-temperature SIBs.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate that introducing a conventional carbonate cosolvent into fluorine-free AN-based electrolytes fundamentally alters the solvation environment, giving rise to an anion-enhanced solvation structure. This strategy enables the formation of robust, inorganic-rich SEI, thereby addressing the long-standing challenge of poor reductive stability and interfacial incompatibility of fluorine-free AN-based electrolyte with HC anode. The optimized electrolyte enables stable operation of HC||PB full cells over a wide temperature range (25-100 °C), delivering a high capacity retention of 62.5% after 600 cycles at 55 °C. More importantly, the practical feasibility of this cosolvent engineering approach is further validated in Ah-level HC||PB pouch cells at 25 °C and 55 °C. Our findings establish a mechanistic blueprint for leveraging ion-dipole interactions to construct robust interphases, offering a versatile design paradigm for advancing high-performance and temperature-tolerant SIBs.

Methods

Electrolyte and electrode preparation

Sodium perchlorate (NaClO4, ≥ 99.9%) was purchased from Canrd Technology Co., Ltd. Acetonitrile (AN, > 99.9%, HPLC grade), sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFA, ≥ 99.0%), sodium trifluoromethanesulfonate (NaOTF, ≥ 98%), sodium tetrafluoroborate (NaBF4, ≥ 99.0%), sodium difluorophosphate (NaDFP, ≥ 98.0%), succinonitrile (SN, ≥ 99.0%), and isobutyronitrile (iBN, ≥ 99.2%) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium hexafluorophosphate (NaPF6, ≥ 99.8%), sodium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (NaFSI, ≥ 99.9%), sodium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NaTFSI, ≥ 99.5%), adiponitrile (ADN, ≥ 99.9%), EMC( > 99.98%) and vinylene carbonate (VC, > 99.9%) were supplied by Suzhou Dodo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium difluoro(oxalato)borate (NaDFOB, ≥ 99.0%) was purchased from Zhejiang Yongtai Technology Co., Ltd. Butyronitrile (BN, ≥ 99.5%) was purchased from Energy Chemical Co., Ltd. A conventional carbonate-based electrolyte (1.0 M NaPF6 in EC/EMC with 5 wt% FEC) was purchased from Guangzhou Tinci Materials Technology Co., Ltd. Prussian blue cathode material (NaxFeyMn1-yFe(CN)6) and hard carbon anode material were obtained from Wenzhou NaTech New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. Aluminum foil (15 μm, 99.6%) was purchased from Hubei Fengsheng Electronic Technology Co., Ltd. The conductive additives consisted of 3.5 wt% graphene composite slurry (Shenzhen BTR New Energy Materials Technology Co., Ltd.) and 1.0 wt% carbon nanotube slurry (Harbin Wanxin Graphite Valley Technology Co., Ltd.). Sodium alginate (Feihong Lithium Battery Materials Co., Ltd.) was used as the binder.

The cathode was prepared by mixing 90 wt% Prussian blue powder, 4.5 wt% conductive additives, and 5.5 wt% binder in deionized water to form a slurry. The anode slurry consisted of 93.0 wt% hard carbon, 2.0 wt% conductive additives, and 5.0 wt% binder in deionized water. The slurries were coated onto the current collectors using an automatic coating machine. The mass loading of active materials was ~9.4 mg cm-2 for the cathode and ~6.4 mg cm-2 for the anode.

For coin cell assembly, the electrodes were punched into disks with a diameter of 10 mm using a manual punching machine (MSK-T10, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Company). The Prussian blue and hard carbon electrodes were dried at 200 °C for 2 h on a heated stage inside a glovebox to remove residual moisture. A cellulose separator (EneRol Nanotechnologies, Inc.) was cut into 16 mm diameter disks and dried at 55 °C ± 1°C for 24 h prior to use.

Electrochemical measurements

CR2032-type coin cells were assembled using a single separator and 65 μL of electrolyte. For pouch cells, double-sided electrodes with areal loadings identical to those of the coin cells were stacked to assemble the cells, using the same separator. The assembled pouch cells were vacuum-dried at 24 h in a vacuum oven (DZF-6050, Ningguo Shaying Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.) to remove residual moisture. Subsequently, the cells were transferred into an argon-filled glovebox for tab welding and electrolyte injection. Tab welding was carried out using an ultrasonic spot welder (MSK-800, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Company). The top and side sealing of the pouch cells were performed using a single-station heat sealer (MSK-140, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Company). After electrolyte filling, a vacuum pre-sealing machine (MSK-115A-S, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Company) was used for final vacuum sealing. The glovebox atmosphere was maintained at H2O < 0.01 ppm and O2 < 0.01 ppm.

After assembly, the pouch cells were directly stored in a 55 °C convection oven for 24 h to ensure sufficient wetting of the electrodes by the electrolyte, without any additional formation or hot-pressing process. During electrochemical testing, a custom-designed pouch cell fixture was employed. The fixture consisted of two perforated plates, guide bolts, and silicone gaskets. A top-and-bottom clamping configuration secured with screws was used to ensure good interfacial contact between electrodes; however, no quantitative external pressure was applied.

The electrochemical performance of HC||PB cells was evaluated using a NEWARE battery testing system (CT-4008Tn-5V20mA-164). For cycling tests, the cells were initially activated at 0.1 °C for one cycle, followed by long-term cycling at 1.0 °C. Rate performance was measured directly after rest using preset current protocols without activation. For self-discharge measurements, the cells were activated at 0.1 °C for one cycle, followed by two charge-discharge cycles, then fully charged and rested prior to discharge; the Coulombic efficiency of the final cycle was used to evaluate self-discharge behavior. 1.0 C = 150 mA g-1. The calculations of specific capacity and specific current are based on the active cathode material content (the cathode needs to be heated at 200 °C ± 1 °C for 2 h in the glove box to remove crystal water).

All electrochemical tests were conducted under controlled temperatures. Specifically, 25 °C tests were performed in a NEWARE temperature chamber (MHWX-200, Neware Technology Limited), 55 °C in an environmental chamber (DHTM-27-RT-P-SD, Doaho, Shanghai), and 80 °C in a high-low temperature chamber (RGD-150, Wuxi Surui), all with temperature deviations within ± 0.5 °C. For variable-temperature cycling, the HC||PB full cells were equilibrated at each target temperature for 1 hour prior to cycling at 1.0 C, and the procedure was repeated sequentially across the programmed temperature range. 1.0 C = 150 mA g-1. Pouch cell performance was evaluated using a NEWARE system (CT-4008Q-5V6A-S1) under identical temperature-controlled conditions.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on a CHI760e electrochemical workstation (Chenhua) in potentiostatic mode, with a frequency range of 1.0 MHz to 0.01 Hz and an amplitude of 5 mV.

HC||PB full cells were first activated at 0.1 C in temperature-controlled chambers at 25 °C and 55 °C, followed by 100 cycles at 1.0 C. 1.0 C = 150 mA g-1. After cycling, the cells were held at the end-of-discharge state. Inside an argon-filled glovebox, the PB cathodes and HC anodes were carefully extracted, thoroughly rinsed with DME, and subsequently dried for detailed characterization of the chemical composition and microstructure of the resulting SEI/CEI layers. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a PHI 5000 VP III system (ULVAC-PHI, Japan) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.8 eV). For depth profiling, Ar+ ion sputtering was performed using a relatively low acceleration voltage (2 kV) over a sputtering area of 3×3 mm2. The sputtering was conducted in a stepwise manner to probe the depth-dependent evolution of the SEI composition. High-resolution XPS spectra were calibrated to the adventitious C 1s peak at 284.8 eV and fitted using Avantage software with Shirley background subtraction and mixed Gaussian-Lorentzian peak functions. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Aeris, PANalytical) data were obtained by scanning samples over the 2θ range of 10-80° to assess the structural integrity of the electrode. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100, JEOL Ltd.) was used to observe the surface morphology of the electrodes. TOF-SIMS measurements were conducted with a PHI nano TOF Ⅲ. The samples were detected using a Bi3+ beam (3 kV, 2 nA) with a raster size of 100 µm, sputtering with an Ar+ beam (2 kV, 100 nA, 400×400 μm2) was applied for depth profiling analysis.
