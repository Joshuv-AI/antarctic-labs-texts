Long-term comparative analysis of secukinumab versus TNFα inhibitors in patients with spondyloarthritis: treatment persistence is differentially affected by disease phenotype, obesity and sex
Authors: Irini D Flouri, Argyro Repa, Nestor Avgoustidis, Sofia Pitsigavdaki, Eleni Kalogiannaki, Konstantina Kalligianni-Sofikiti, Evgenia Emmanouilidou, Eleni Marolachaki, George Bertsias, Prodromos Sidiropoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe anti-interleukin 17A agent secukinumab has been approved for the treatment of spondyloarthritis (SpA) patients as an alternative to tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis). Our aim was to compare long-term treatment persistence and relevant effect modifiers, as well as 6-month clinical responses, in SpA patients starting secukinumab versus TNFis in routine clinical care.MethodsThis was a prospective single-center cohort study of consecutive axial SpA (axSpA) or peripheral SpA (perSpA) patients, including psoriatic arthritis (PsA) patients, who started or switched to either secukinumab or TNFis from 2016–2022. Drug persistence and low/minimal disease activity rates were assessed via Kaplan‒Meier curves and multivariable and propensity score-adjusted regression analyses. Confounding and effect modification were tested for several baseline demographic, clinical and laboratory characteristics.ResultsA total of 644 patients (secukinumab: 214, TNFis: 430) were analyzed. Unadjusted 4-year treatment persistence was greater with secukinumab than with TNFis in the total SpA cohort (38.7% versus 30.5%, respectively, log-rank p = 0.004); including the axSpA (37% versus 34.5%, p = 0.044) and perSpA (40% versus 18.2%, p = 0.011) cohorts. Multivariable analysis confirmed a greater risk for discontinuation of TNFis [HR (95% CI): 1.62 (1.23–2.14), p < 0.001] both for inefficacy [1.38 (1.01–1.90)] and safety reasons [2.95 (1.22–7.14)]. Psoriatic arthritis, obesity and female sex significantly modified the differential efficacy-related persistence. The 2-year persistence in obese female psoriatic arthritis patients was 76% with secukinumab versus 39% with TNFis, whereas in nonobese axSpA males, it was 48% versus 78%, respectively. Six-month treatment target achievement rates were comparable between the two agents in axSpA, perSpA, and PsA patients.ConclusionsThe differential persistence of biologic agents commonly used in SpA has been shown in this real-life prospective study. Should the distinct effects of obesity, sex and specific SpA diagnosis on persistence be confirmed in other cohorts, it could optimize clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03664-y.Keywords: Spondyloarthritis, Secukinumab, TNF inhibitors, Axial spondyloarthritis, Psoriatic arthritis, Response, Treatment persistence

Background

Spondyloarthritis (SpA) is a heterogeneous group of diseases, with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) being the main representative. The “Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS)” has developed classification criteria for axSpA and peripheral (perSpA) groups [1, 2]; however, in clinical practice, various SpA manifestations may be present in a single patient often at different times and with varying severity, posing classification hurdles [3]. Patients with SpA have greatly benefited from biologic therapies (bDMARDs), such as TNF inhibitors (TNFis) or anti-IL-17 monoclonal antibodies, and, recently, from targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs). Guidelines for the management of spondyloarthritis and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) were developed by the EULAR and other societies [4, 5]. Nevertheless, owing to the scarcity of head-to-head trials of different b-/ts-DMARDs and the lack of reliable biomarkers of response, it is difficult to prioritize any of the above targeted therapies in terms of efficacy, which represents a significant unmet need for both guidelines and clinical practice.

In observational studies, assessing drug persistence is regarded as a surrogate measure of treatment efficacy and safety while also reflecting patient and physician satisfaction and comorbidities considerations [6, 7]. Although patients with SpA generally have high response rates to TNFis, we and others have reported 5-year persistence rates of the first TNFi ranging between 46 and 60%, with insufficient response being the main reason for discontinuation [8, 9]. Data on the treatment persistence, effectiveness and safety of secukinumab compared with TNFis in real-world studies are scarce.

In the present study, we aimed to compare long-term treatment persistence, efficacy and safety between secukinumab and TNFis in a cohort of patients with SpA treated in real life. We studied patients prospectively and assessed factors contributing to differential persistence, aiming to characterize distinct groups that could benefit from individual drugs.

Methods

Patient cohort and inclusion criteria

The University of Crete Rheumatology Clinic Registry (UCRCR) is an ongoing, single-center, prospective cohort study of patients with inflammatory arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus [9–11]. Adults ≥ 18 years old with inflammatory arthritis are included in UCRCR at the time of initiation of the first b-/tsDMARD and are prospectively followed irrespective of treatment switches for as long as they receive biologic or targeted therapy. For the present study, we analyzed all consecutive patients with axial or peripheral SpA, including PsA, who were starting (or switching to) secukinumab or a TNFi between January 2016 (secukinumab launch date) and December 2022. A clinical diagnosis by the attending rheumatologist was used to select patients, but the fulfillment of the classification criteria was also recorded. We excluded (a) patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-related SpA and (b) patients whose therapy switched from a bio-originator to a biosimilar TNFi or vice versa for nonmedical reasons. Patients were followed until discontinuation of b/tsDMARD therapy, death, loss to follow-up, or 31 July 2023.

Collected variables

At inclusion, sociodemographics, previous treatments and comorbidities were recorded, and the Rheumatic Disease Comorbidity Index (RDCI) was calculated [12]. Disease characteristics (axial, peripheral and extraarticular manifestations), ongoing treatments, disease activity, function and quality of life measures were collected at inclusion, every 3–6 months for the first 2 years and yearly thereafter. All adverse events (based on MedDRA coding) were also recorded during follow-up and characterized as serious (grade 3–5), moderate (grade 2) or mild (grade 1) on the basis of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v.5.

For disease activity evaluation, the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and the Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) [13] were assessed in axSpA patients, and Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) [14] was calculated in patients with peripheral arthritis. Additionally, the percentages of body surface area associated with psoriasis, enthesitis (indicated as the Leeds Enthesitis Index (LEI) and/or 0/1/> 1 entheseal points) and dactylitis counts, which are all used to calculate Minimal Disease Activity (MDA) are captured in patients with PsA [15]. The pain and global Visual Analogue Score (VAS), physician’s global assessment score, Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ), C-reactive protein (CRP) level and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were recorded for all patients.

As this was an observational study, all treatment decisions were made by the attending physician on the basis of clinical assessments, national guidelines and patient preferences. Any withdrawal from treatment was registered prospectively and was classified by the treating physician as due to treatment inefficacy, adverse event(s), or other reasons (patient decision, pregnancy, etc.). All patients provided written informed consent according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the local Institutional Review Board (1348/09.03.2022).

Definitions and study outcomes

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the 4-year persistence of secukinumab therapy compared with that of TNFis and explore potential effect modifiers. The secondary outcomes were (a) to assess the attainment of treatment targets at 6 months of therapy with secukinumab compared with TNFis and (b) to compare the incidence of adverse events between the two treatment groups.

Persistence (percentage of patients remaining on therapy) was calculated as the time between the start date and the date of the first missed dose of the drug, death, or 31–7–2023, whichever came first. Temporary treatment interruptions of < 6 months (e.g., due to adverse events, surgeries, loss of insurance, etc.) were allowed. Disease activity states used to define attainment of the treatment target at 6 (± 2) months were ASDAS-CRP low disease activity (LDA) in axSpA patients and minimal disease activity (MDA) in PsA patients. DAPSA LDA was also analyzed in all perSpA patients. Disease activity indices were calculated both as crude values and adjusted by LUNDEX [16] for treatment retention.

Statistical analysis

Baseline data are presented as numbers (N) and percentages (%) for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. Nonparametric tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum and chi-square tests, as appropriate) were used to compare baseline characteristics. Drug persistence was assessed by Kaplan‒Meier survival curves with log-rank test and Cox proportional hazard models, with follow-up censored at 4 years. For the analysis of the time to discontinuation of treatment due to ineffectiveness or adverse events, discontinuations for other reasons were treated as censored observations. Confounding (defined as a change > 10% of the crude regression coefficient of the univariable model) was tested for sex, age, disease duration since diagnosis, and SpA phenotype (either as axSpA/perSpA or as axSpA (non-PsA)/PsA/perSpA non-PsA), year of therapy start, treatment line (1st/2nd/≥ 3rd), smoking status (ever/never), RDCI score (0 vs. ≥ 1), and (all variables as yes/no): previous inefficacy-related discontinuation of ≥ 1 TNFi, co-administered csDMARDs, presence of peripheral arthritis, psoriasis, obesity (body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2), fibromyalgia (clinical diagnosis), osteoarthritis, education > 12 years (vs. ≤ 12 years), rural residence and employment. The variables were tested one by one, and the final models were adjusted for significant noncollinear (variance inflation factor < 2.5) confounders. Additionally, effect modification was investigated for selected variables on the basis of previous literature and clinical relevance: SpA phenotype, sex, treatment line, previous discontinuation of ≥ 1 TNFi due to inefficacy, presence of obesity and baseline CRP > 0.4 mg/dl (yes/no). Interaction terms between the bDMARD class and these potential effect modifiers were added and tested one by one in the adjusted models. Those with p < 0.05 were considered to indicate significant effect modification, and stratified subgroup analyses were performed.

For the secondary objectives, we performed logistic regression analyses to investigate the association between bDMARD class and attainment (yes/no) of low/minimal disease activity states at 6 (± 2) months. Patients who withdrew from therapy during the first 6 months of follow-up due to treatment inefficacy were included and labeled “non-responders”. To avoid model overfitting, propensity scores (PSs) of patients receiving SEC versus TNFis were estimated by using separate multinomial regression models for patients with axSpA, those with perSpA and those with PsA. The above-described variables used for persistence analysis were incorporated into the models as potential confounders, along with the ASDAS-CRP score at baseline when the PS for axSpA patients was estimated and the baseline DAPSA score when the PS for patients with perSpA was estimated. The logit of each PS was included in the outcome logistic regression models, along with treatment (secukinumab vs. TNFis). Sex and treatment number were also added as additional covariates for a doubly robust approach. Logistic regressions adjusted for bDMARD class, sex, treatment line and disease activity at baseline are presented in addition to propensity score-adjusted regressions.

For adverse events, we calculated incidence rates (IRs) per 100 patient-years of follow-up and estimated incidence rate ratios via Poisson generalized linear regression for outcomes with more than 10 events in total. Adjustments were made for sex, age, disease duration, SpA phenotype (axSpA/PsA/perSpA non-PsA), year of therapy start, treatment line (1st/2nd/≥ 3rd), smoking status, RDCI score (0 vs ≥ 1), co-administered csDMARDs and prednisolone, presence of peripheral arthritis and obesity. All analyses were performed via the statistical software IBM SPSS version 27, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 644 patients were included in the analysis, 214 (33%) on secukinumab and 430 (67%) on TNFis—adalimumab:128 (20%), infliximab:113 (17.5%), golimumab: 90 (14%), etanercept: 55 (8.5%) and certolizumab pegol: 44 (7%). AxSpA was the diagnosis in 62% of patients starting secukinumab and 74% startring TNFis, whereas psoriasis was more common with secukinumab (51% vs 33.5% respectively) (Table 1). Compared with TNFis, secukinumab was more commonly prescribed as a 3rd-line therapy (49% of patients), whereas TNFis were mostly prescribed as 1st-line therapy (41% of patients) (p < 0.001). As expected, more patients with a prior insufficient response to TNFis were prescribed secukinumab (p < 0.001). Compared with TNFis, secukinumab monotherapy was more common (66% vs 58%, p = 0.04). All other patient characteristics at baseline, including extraarticular manifestations (except psoriasis) and sociodemographics, were comparable between the two groups (Table 1).

The recruited patients had a high burden of comorbidities at bDMARD start, with a comparable prevalence between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1). The most common group of comorbid diseases was metabolic/endocrine (64% in both groups), followed by cardiovascular (40% versus 34% in secukinumab and TNFis, respectively).

Baseline disease activity was high, both regarding axial [median (IQR) ASDAS-CRP: 3.3 (2.8–3.8), BASDAI: 6.0 (4.8–7.3)] and peripheral disease [median (IQR) DAPSA: 35 (22-48), DAS28: 4.7 (3.9–5.5)]. Individual disease activity parameters and composite scores were similar between the two treatment groups (Table 1).

Drug persistence analysis

According to the unadjusted analysis, the estimated overall treatment duration was greater with secukinumab than with TNFis [mean (95% CI), 29.9 (26.8–32.9) vs. 24.4 (22.3–26.5) months, respectively]. The 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-year drug persistence rates were 71%, 59.5%, 44.8% and 38.7% respectively with secukinumab and 60.2%, 45.3%, 34% and 30.5% respectively with TNFis (log-rank p = 0.004 for the 4-year comparison) (Fig. 1A). When persistence was analyzed separately according to reasons for discontinuation, the difference was significant only for adverse event discontinuation (p = 0.043) (Figs. 1B and C). According to the unadjusted analysis, secukinumab had greater 4-year persistence than TNFis in both axSpA patients (37% versus 34.5%, log-rank p = 0.044) and perSpA patients (40% versus 18.2%, p = 0.011) (Fig. 1D and E).

Persistence with secukinumab and TNFis in all spondyloarthritis patients for all causes of discontinuation (A) and stratified by reason for discontinuation (B-C) or by classification as axial or peripheral spondyloarthritis (D-E)

In the multivariable analysis adjusted for significant confounders, TNFi administration was a predictor of lower overall persistence [HR (95% CI) = 1.62 (1.23–2.14), p < 0.001], as well as of both inefficacy and safety discontinuation [HR: 1.38 (1.01–1.90) and 2.95 (1.22–7.14), respectively] (Table 2). Interaction analysis suggested no effect modification for safety discontinuations. However, we found that SpA subdiagnosis, sex and the presence of obesity significantly modified efficacy-related persistence with secukinumab versus TNFis (p values for the interaction terms: 0.006, 0.040 and 0.045, respectively). Unadjusted and adjusted subgroup analyses indicated that efficacy-related persistence was greater with secukinumab than with TNFis in the PsA, female and obese patient subgroups, while it was comparable in the axSpA, male and BMI < 30 groups and lower in the peripheral SpA (non-PsA) subgroup. No effect modification was found for treatment line, previous discontinuation of ≥ 1 TNFi due to inefficacy, or baseline CRP > 0.4 mg/dl (yes/no). Figure 2 presents the Kaplan‒Meier plots of the unadjusted analysis of the cohort stratified by significant effect modifiers, while Table 2 summarizes adjusted hazard ratios for treatment discontinuation of TNFis versus secukinumab for different reasons of discontinuation and focuses on the three patient subgroups for inefficacy stops.

Efficacy-related treatment persistence stratified by significant effect modifiers

Further analysis within subgroups was precluded by the low number of patients. However, on the basis of the Kaplan‒Meier analysis, we constructed a matrix of the estimated 2-year efficacy-related persistence with secukinumab versus TNFis, according to SpA subdiagnosis, sex and BMI (Table 3). Women with PsA had greater 2-year efficacy-related persistence when treated with secukinumab (76–79%) than when treated with TNFis (39–48%), irrespective of their BMI (log-rank, p = 0.029 and p = 0.020 in nonobese and obese patients, respectively). Among women with axSpA, obesity seemed to favor secukinumab over TNFis (66% versus 44%, p = 0.189), whereas persistence was similar in nonobese female axSpA patients (52% vs. 56%, respectively, p = 0.716). A tendency for greater efficacy-related persistence was observed with secukinumab than with TNFis in obese males with either diagnosis (75%−89% vs. 68%−72%, respectively, p = 0.143 for the whole group), but the opposite was true for nonobese male patients (TNFis: 78%>−79% versus SECs: 48%−66%, p = 0.068), especially those with axSpA (Table 3).

Treatment response analysis

A total of 293 patients with axial SpA, 116 with perSpA and 129 with PsA, were treated for at least 6 months and had available data to allow for the evaluation of treatment response. The baseline characteristics of these patients (shown in Supplementary Table 2) were comparable between the two treatment groups for most variables and generally followed the pattern of the whole cohort. The median ASDAS-CRP at 6 months of therapy was comparable in patients with axSpA receiving secukinumab or TNFis [median (IQR) ASDAS: 2.87 (2.22–3.83) vs 2.84 (1.99–3.39), p = 0.868, respectively]. Similarly, in patients with peripheral SpA, the DAPSA at 6 months was comparable between the two groups [median (IQR) DAPSA with secukinumab: 21.5 (15.3–39.5) vs. TNFis: 18.6 (11.90–30.1), p = 0.285].

LUNDEX – adjusted proportions of patients who reached the target of at least low disease activity (LDA) had numerical but not statistically significant differences. Thus, among axSpA/perSpA patients, the percentages who achieved ASDAS < 2.1/DAPSA ≤ 14 at 6 months with secukinumab versus TNFis were 20%/23.9% versus 25.8%/22.9%, respectively. When LDA was assessed according to the bDMARD treatment line, it was 34.5%/28.6% versus 34.7%/25% for the 1 st treatment line, 16.7%/18% versus 17%/24% for the 2nd treatment line and 7.4%/21% versus 19.4%/20% for the ≥ 3rd treatment line with secukinumab or TNFis, respectively (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Analysis of the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society 40% (ASAS40) response yielded similar results to the attainment of ASDAS-LDA in axSpA patients, with no significant difference between the two groups (LUNDEX-corrected, 10% versus 12.7% with secukinumab and TNFis, respectively; p = 0.649).

Specifically, for PsA patients, 22.6% of those treated with secukinumab versus 13.2% treated with TNFis (p = 0.159) reached the target of at least minimal disease activity (MDA) at 6 months; the percentages for the 1 st, 2nd and ≥ 3rd treatment lines were 45.5% vs. 25%, 25% vs. 6.9% and 11.5% vs. 11.1%, respectively. Adjusted analyses provided results similar to those of the unadjusted analyses for both main subdiagnoses (Table 4).

Safety analysis

A total of 647 events (serious: 65, moderate: 582) were recorded during follow-up (822 patient-years; secukinumab: 296, TNFis: 526), with mean (SD) durations of 16.0 (16.0) and 17.6 (16.0) months, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Overall, 33.6% and 42.6% of patients in the secukinumab and TNFi groups, respectively, had ≥ 1 AE, and 7.9% and 8.6% of patients, respectively, had ≥ 1 serious AE (SAE).

The incidence rate of all SAEs was 7.9/100 patient-years, with no statistically significant difference in the incidence rate ratio (IRR) between treatments (Supplementary Table 4). Approximately half of all SAEs are serious infections (SIs), mostly of the lower respiratory tract. The risk of SI was approximately threefold lower among patients on secukinumab than among those in the combined TNFi group (adjusted IRR: 0.36 (0.15–0.90), p = 0.030). Half of all SIs with TNFis (13/25, IR: 6.4/100 patient-years) were recorded in patients receiving intravenous infliximab; the IR of SIs with subcutaneously administered TNFis was comparable to that of secukinumab.

Overall, 48 (7.5%) patients stopped therapy because of an AE: 10 patients in the secukinumab group (IR: 3.4/100 patient-years) and 38 patients in the TNFi group (IR: 7.2/100 patient-years). Among the AEs that stopped treatment, 20 (42%) were allergic/injection-site/infusion reactions (SECs: 1 case, TNFis: 19 cases), and 11 (23%) were infections, including 2 cases of tuberculosis, both with TNFis. One patient was diagnosed with ulcerative colitis, and one was diagnosed with Crohn’s disease, both of whom were receiving secukinumab therapy.

Among moderate AEs (incidence rate: 71/100 patient-years), the risk was comparable between patients receiving secukinumab and those receiving TNFis. However, a focused analysis of specific categories of AEs revealed that there was a lower risk of noninfectious total moderate AEs with secukinumab than with TNFis (adjusted IRR = 0.53 (0.34–0.81), p = 0.003), especially moderate cardiovascular AEs (adjusted IRR = 0.21 (0.05–0.95), p = 0.042) (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In this real-world study of SpA patients starting or switching therapy with secukinumab or a TNFi, we observed greater 4-year persistence with secukinumab than with TNFis, both for safety- and efficacy-related discontinuations. We report that clinical subdiagnosis, sex, and the presence of obesity significantly contributed to the differential efficacy-related drug persistence. Furthermore, comparable low/minimal disease activity rates were found between the two treatment groups in axSpA, perSpA and PsA patients, whereas the adjusted comparative safety analysis revealed an overall similar incidence of serious and moderate adverse events. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study comparing the long-term drug persistence of secukinumab with that of TNFis in a cohort of both axial and peripheral SpA patients.

In patients with axSpA, secukinumab had marginally but significantly greater persistence than TNFis did at 4 years (37% versus 34.5%, p = 0.044), and this is the first study of long-term comparative drug persistence data. A few yet well-designed registry studies have presented short-term data regarding comparative persistence for axSpA patients. Glintborg et al. analyzed data from five Nordic registries (DANBIO, ROB-FIN, ICE-BIO, NOR-DMARD and ARTIS/SRQ) and reported comparable 1-year retention rates of secukinumab compared with the combined TNFis group (secukinumab: 56–76% vs. TNFis: 47–78%) [17]. Researchers from the SCQM and KOBIO registries have also investigated 1-year bDMARD persistence in axSpA patients after withdrawal from ≥ 1 TNFi; both studies reported a comparable risk of drug discontinuation for secukinumab and TNFis [18, 19]. A comparison of the above reported data with our first-year data revealed that secukinumab had better persistence overall (72% versus 60%, p = 0.032), whereas the inefficacy-related persistence was comparable (75% versus 73%, with secukinumab and TNFis respectively, p = 0.396).

We generally consider that the favorable persistence of secukinumab found in the total cohort was mostly evident in patients with peripheral disease, which was also supported by the results of the multivariable analysis (Table 2). Indeed, the overall unadjusted persistence in the perSpA patients in our cohort was 40% versus 18.2% with SEC vs TNFis (p = 0.011). Since the majority of patients with perSpA were patients with PsA, we found overall 1-year and 4-year persistence rates in PsA patients with secukinumab of 79.5% and 47%, respectively, which are within the range reported by one recent study from the EuroSpA collaboration [20] and other observational studies [21, 22], which reported 1-year secukinumab persistence estimates between 66 and 91% and a 5-year estimate of 57% [23]. With respect to the comparative results, we found significantly greater persistence with secukinumab than with TNFis [4-year: 47% versus 24% and adjusted HR (95% CI) for TNFis vs. secukinumab efficacy-related treatment discontinuation: 2.35 (1.42–3.89), p < 0.001]. There are no long-term drug persistence data in the literature for comparison with our 4-year results. However, and comparable to our 1 st year data, a greater 1-year persistence of secukinumab (86%) than of adalimumab monotherapy (76%) in treatment-naïve PsA patients was reported in the “EXCEED” trial, the only head-to-head randomized trial published thus far [24]. Furthermore, in a large U.S. healthcare provider database with PsA patients, secukinumab had greater persistence than adalimumab, infliximab, and ustekinumab did, especially when it was used as 2nd-line therapy [25]. In contrast, two large observational studies pooling data from the five Nordic registries outlined above reported no differences in persistence between secukinumab and adalimumab in PsA [26, 27]. In a subanalysis, we compared patients treated with only adalimumab and found that patients treated with secukinumab still had greater persistence (data not shown). Differences in patient characteristics, such as a greater percentage of female patients (64% versus 57%) and higher baseline DAPSA scores in our cohort, could account for this difference. The percentage of patients with obesity was not reported in the studies from Nordic registries but was quite high in our PsA cohort (47.5%) and in the U.S. database study [25], and we found that obesity contributes to the favorable persistence of secukinumab versus TNFis (Tables 2 and 3).

Interestingly patients with non-PsA perSpA had lower efficacy-related persistence on secukinumab than on TNFis, a finding opposite to that seen with perSpA with psoriasis (Table 2). This could be the effect of a better efficacy of secukinumab versus TNFis in skin psoriasis, a finding of several RCTs summarized in a recent systematic review [28]. Regarding response rates of arthritis in our cohort, although response rates (6-month DAPSA-LDA) were found similar between secukinumab and TNFi treatments in the total peripheral SpA patient group, no comparison of treatment response could be made focused in non-PsA perSpA due to the low number of patients.

A number of studies have evaluated the effect of sex on the treatment response to biologic therapies in SpA patients, as summarized in recent reviews and meta-analyses [29, 30]. Registry studies from our group and others have consistently revealed significantly lower persistence with TNFis in women than in men with axSpA and PsA [9, 31, 32], whereas sex-disaggregated data regarding the efficacy and persistence of secukinumab are still scarce and conflicting. A pooled analysis from five phase III studies of secukinumab (MEASURE 1–5) demonstrated high persistence rates for both males (91.9%) and females (86.8%) and no sex differences in treatment response at 52 weeks in patients with active ankylosing spondylitis [33]. Nevertheless, a recent systematic literature review and meta-analysis of 9 randomized controlled trials of bDMARDs in PsA patients revealed that female patients were less likely to reach efficacy endpoints than male patients with TNFis or secukinumab, although persistence with therapy was not studied [29]. To our knowledge, and supporting the importance of the present analysis, comparative persistence data with the two bDMARD classes stratified by sex have not been previously reported for SpA, neither in randomized trials nor in the real-world setting. Interestingly, in our adjusted stratified model and Kaplan‒Meier analyses (Fig. 2D), females had overall greater efficacy-related persistence with secukinumab than with TNFis, especially for PsA in both obese and nonobese individuals (Table 3). With respect to axSpA patients, female sex favored secukinumab efficacy-related persistence over TNFis only in obese patients (at 2 years: 66% versus 44%). These sex-specific findings, if verified in other SpA cohorts, could assist in guiding treatment decisions in everyday clinical practice.

Several previous studies have demonstrated the negative impact of obesity on efficacy outcomes and persistence with biologic therapies, especially TNF inhibitors, in patients with inflammatory arthritis [34, 35]. However, it remains unclear whether poor response to therapy in obese patients is a “class effect” of TNFis or is related to all biologic therapies [36, 37]. Our study is the first to directly compare drug persistence by weight status and suggest that the hazard of inefficacy is greater in obese SpA patients treated with TNFis than in those treated with secukinumab. This finding could be important in the management of SpA patients, given the high prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome in this group of diseases, most notably in PsA [38]. In the CHOICE Study, Nguyen et al. reported that secukinumab had comparable rates of ACR20/50/70 responses in patients with a BMI ≤ 30 or > 30 [39], whereas in a recent systematic literature review, Gialouri et al. reported that, in contrast to TNFis, clinical responses to IL-23 and IL-17A inhibitors did not appear to be influenced by increased BMI in PsA or axSpA patients [37], and smaller observational studies confirmed secukinumab efficacy [40] and persistence [41] in SpA patients with comorbid obesity. The above data corroborate our results, suggesting that a common comorbidity may negatively affect the persistence of a class of bDMARDs over another. The reasons for this differential effect have not been addressed, and more studies with head-to-head comparisons of SpA treatments with different modes of action, accounting for the contribution not only of weight status but also of other patient- or disease-related characteristics and biomarkers, would provide much-needed information for personalized treatment approaches.

Similar to other large European cohorts [17, 42, 43], the proportions of patients in our study attaining treatment targets at 6 months were generally low, especially when 2nd or later treatment lines were considered. The response rates were not significantly different between secukinumab and TNFi treatments, and neither the 6-month ASDAS-CRP LDA in axSpA patients nor the DAPSA-LDA in peripheral SpA patients were predicted by the class of bDMARD used (Table 4). To date, no head-to-head randomized controlled trials have compared the clinical response of secukinumab to that of TNFis in axSpA patients as a primary outcome. Recently, Baraliakos et al., in a randomized trial investigating the comparative effects of secukinumab and adalimumab biosimilars on radiographic progression, reported that exploratory efficacy analysis revealed comparable response rates (ASAS20 and ASAS40) at 104 weeks across the two groups [44]. Comparative efficacy evidence from real-world studies from the aforementioned Nordic registries analysis as well as from two smaller registry studies also revealed comparable clinical efficacy between the two treatments [17–19].

We finally observed greater safety-related persistence with secukinumab than with TNFis, mostly due to more frequent injection-site and infusion reactions with TNFis. In accordance with the EXCEED trial [24] and observational studies using administrative databases [45–47], the serious infection rate in our cohort was similar when secukinumab was compared with subcutaneously administered TNFis. However, the IRR of SI was significantly lower with secukinumab when infliximab was included in the TNFi group, highlighting the potentially greater risk of SI with infliximab than with other TNFis, a finding also reported in observational studies for RA [48] and in a Cochrane review of TNFis used for various indications [49]. In contrast, the only study reporting a greater absolute risk of hospitalized infections with secukinumab (5/100 patient-years) than with adalimumab (2.3/100 patient-years) during the first year of follow-up is one collaborative analysis of Nordic registries [50]. Patient characteristics, differences in comorbidities and healthcare-related factors may contribute to these differences.

The strengths of our study include the direct comparison of treatments with different modes of action in a prospective real-world cohort of well-characterized patients. We used validated treatment response measures and outcomes for both axSpA patients and perSpA/PsA patients and applied statistical approaches to explore the differential efficacy and safety of the two treatments fully, thus providing comprehensive insights into their comparative therapeutic impact on patient populations typically encountered in clinical practice.

The main limitation is its observational nature, which bears the risk of confounding by indications. Multivariable regressions and propensity score models adjusted for several covariates, including sociodemographics, comorbidities, smoking, disease activity and other disease and treatment characteristics, were used; however, the risk of residual confounding remains. Moreover, we considered TNFis as a group and performed only selective post hoc comparisons of secukinumab with a particular TNFi; therefore, we may have missed within-class differences in the five TNFis. Missing data constitute another limitation associated with real-world evidence studies. We performed complete-case analyses since the percentage of missing data was low for the persistence analysis, and the baseline characteristics of patients with available data for disease activity response at 6 months were not significantly different between the two groups and the total cohort (Supplementary Table 2). Finally, the number of patients was not adequate to allow for comparative evaluation of treatment response in specific patient subgroups to corroborate findings of the persistence analyses.

Conclusions

Our data support greater drug persistence with secukinumab than with TNFis in patients with axSpA and perSpA, both with respect to efficacy- and safety-related discontinuations, while the achievement of 6-month treatment targets was comparable. The important impacts of sex, body weight and the SpA phenotype on efficacy-related persistence were identified, and subgroups of patients who may benefit differentially from individual agents were characterized. Confirmation of the above described and other phenotypic characteristics, combined with the use of reliable biomarkers as predictors of therapy response, is mandatory to support personalized therapeutic choices.
