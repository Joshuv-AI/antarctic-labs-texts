Heterozygous CECR2 variants support a distinct neurodevelopmental syndrome with features overlapping cat eye syndrome
Authors: Anushree Acharya, Irma Järvelä, Andrea Hernandez, Yasmin Rajendran, Thashi Bharadwaj, Dana H. Goodloe, Susan M. Hiatt, Jennifer Morrison, Patricia G. Wheeler, Jesse M. Hunter, Rachel Supinger, Scott E. Hickey, Andrea K. Petersen, Kari Magnussen, Marcello Scala, Pasquale Striano, Federico Zara, Juha Leppälä, Suzanne M. Leal, Isabelle Schrauwen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe CECR2 histone acetyl-lysine reader facilitates chromatin remodeling and plays a significant role in neurodevelopment. It resides within the cat eye syndrome (CES) critical region at 22q11.1q11.21. An increased copy number of this region, often as tetrasomy or a supernumerary chromosome, results in CES. The complex chromosomal arrangements and phenotypic variability have hampered the identification of the true cause(s) of CES. Patients and their clinical data were collected from multiple diagnostic and research laboratories. Exome or genome sequencing was performed in affected individuals and, when available, their relatives. Here, we describe six patients with a heterozygous single-nucleotide or small insertion/deletion variant in CECR2 and their clinical features. Variants were either loss-of-function [c.1734dupC:p.(Thr580Hisfs∗98), c.1819C>T:p.(Arg607∗), and c.2281C>T:p.(Arg761∗)] or missense in an intrinsically disordered and potentially critical region at the C terminus of the protein [c.4153C>G:p.(Gln1385Glu), c.4254C>G:p.(Phe1418Leu), and c.4283A>G:p.(Gln1428Arg)]. The main clinical features were delayed growth, relatively small head circumference, developmental delay, and speech issues/delay. Other clinical findings were variable and included intellectual disability; feeding problems; facial dysmorphism; cleft lip/palate; brain, ear, heart, or other organ abnormalities; behavioral abnormalities; and epilepsy. There was significant overlap with common CES features, including ear, heart, and brain abnormalities, intellectual disability, and growth restriction. In conclusion, we characterize a CECR2-associated neurodevelopmental disorder. The consistent molecular and clinical overlap with CES supports CECR2’s role in the CES phenotype. These findings implicate diverse variant types in related neurodevelopmental disorders, underscoring CECR2’s dosage sensitivity and essential function in epigenetic regulation during development.

Main text

Chromatin remodeling complexes play critical roles in development. CECR2, the CECR2 histone acetyl-lysine reader (also called cat eye syndrome critical region protein 2), is one such epigenetic regulator and is implicated in various developmental processes, particularly neural tube formation and brain development. CECR2 forms a CECR2-containing remodeling factor complex (CERF) that can include chromatin remodelers SMARCA1 and SMARCA5, which in turn regulate transcriptional programs critical for neural and systemic development.1 These imitation switch (ISWI) genes encode ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers SNF2L and SNF2H, respectively. Through their role in nucleosome positioning, ISWI complexes facilitate progenitor differentiation, making them critical for developmental trajectories.2 SNF2H and SNF2L exhibit divergent expression patterns during mouse embryonic development, indicating that they play different roles in development. While SNF2L is critical in modulating neural cortical cell differentiation and proliferation,3 SNF2H is necessary for neuroprogenitor expansion and specification of callosal projections in the corpus callosum.4

CECR2 participates in DNA repair pathways, particularly with respect to cancer cell line double-strand breaks (DSBs) via γ-H2AX formation.5,6 However, more recent research shows that it is not required for DSB repair in neurospheres, i.e., three-dimensional cultures derived from embryonic neural stem cells, and therefore CECR2 may not affect neural development through the DSB repair pathway.7

Research indicates that CECR2 is expressed in various mammalian embryonic tissues, including the neuroepithelium and mesenchyme, during critical stages of cranial neural tube development.8,9 In addition, variations in the composition of the CERF complex across tissues suggest tissue-specific roles, particularly in the differentiation and maturation of neural cells.1

In humans, CECR2 has been suggested to be involved in cat eye syndrome (CES) (OMIM: 115470), a rare disorder that presents a very variable and complex clinical phenotype whose main features include coloboma; ear malformations; intellectual disability (ID); developmental delay (DD); cardiac, renal, skeletal, and anal defects; and clefts.10,11 The “classic triad” of CES includes outer ear anomalies (81%), abdominal malformations (44%), and ophthalmologic anomalies (35%). However, heart anomalies (51%) and ID (47%) were found just as often in a larger study.12 The penetrance and severity of these phenotypic features are highly variable. Larger studies have suggested that the classic triad is present only in a minority of affected individuals and can even be absent.12,13,14,15

CES is caused by a partial 22q11.21 tetrasomy in most affected individuals, presented as an extra small supernumerary bi-satellited marker chromosome derived from chromosome 22 (sSMC(22)). However, less common mechanisms, including both intrachromosomal duplications and triplications at 22q11.21 and a ring of chromosome 22, have been reported in patients with CES.12,16 CECR2 is located in the CES critical region (CESCR), defined by cytogenetic abnormalities seen on 22q11.21 and suspected to contribute to the CES phenotype.16,17 Mouse models present with similar ocular, skeletal, renal, and cardiac defects, ranging from mild to severe, including neural tube defects (NTDs) and exencephaly.8,11,18

Overall, current knowledge on CECR2 underscores its multifaceted role as a chromatin remodeler and transcriptional regulator, essential for proper neurodevelopment and prevention of congenital malformations.

We report six families, each with one affected individual with a rare heterozygous variant in CECR2 (GenBank: NM_001290047.2) (Table 1; Figure 1A). Of these, two were confirmed de novo. For four probands, it was not possible to determine whether the variant was de novo since parents were not available for study. For one variant, c.4283A>G (p.Gln1428Arg), of unconfirmed inheritance, an unaffected sibling of the proband did not carry the variant. Short-read next-generation sequencing analyses were conducted across multiple independent research and diagnostic laboratories worldwide, adhering to standard protocols. Sequencing was performed on affected individuals and, when available, their first-degree relatives, such as parents or unaffected siblings (see supplemental notes). Exome sequencing was used for all families except for family 2, whose DNA samples underwent genome sequencing. Low-quality reads were excluded, and the remaining reads were aligned to the human reference genome. Variant calling for single-nucleotide variants and insertions/deletions was carried out using established best-practice workflows.20 Variants were annotated and filtered based on multiple criteria, including minor-allele frequency in public databases such as the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Bravo, and All of Us, as well as genomic location and predicted functional impact.21,22,23 All CECR2 variants identified in this study were annotated using ANNOVAR,24 incorporating databases such as dbNSFPv4 to assess conservation and in silico predictions of pathogenicity.25 Any additional variants of interest identified per family are discussed in the supplemental notes.

Of the observed CECR2 variants, half (3/6; 50%) are missense variants, two are nonsense variants that introduced a premature stop codon, and one is a frameshift variant (Table 1). It is important to note that all missense variants are clustered at the C terminus and within the last 100 amino acids of the protein (positions 1385, 1418, and 1428), while all loss-of-function (LoF), frameshift, and nonsense variants are clustered toward the middle before the bromodomain of the protein (Figure 1B). All LoF variants are predicted to be targeted by nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). None of the observed variants in our cohort were in the bromodomain, coiled-coil domain, AT hook, or DDT motif of the protein (Figure 1B).

The reported variants were either not observed in population databases such as gnomAD, All of Us, and TOPMed Bravo or were present at very low frequencies (Table 1). Variants in our cohort are also highly conserved evolutionarily across species, with genomic evolutionary rate profiling (GERP) scores of >2.6.26 All missense variants were also predicted deleterious by bioinformatics scores such as combined annotation dependent depletion (CADD) scores.27 Notably, CECR2 is highly intolerant to LoF variants in the human population (probability of being LoF intolerant [pLI] = 1; observed/expected [o/e] = 0.19).23

The analysis of six separate affected individuals suggests a clinical phenotypic spectrum associated with CECR2 variants (Table 2). Growth delay (short stature) and relatively narrow head circumference were some of the main features in this cohort. DD, affecting verbal, cognitive, and motor milestones, presented in all six affected individuals. Speech delay and neurocognitive disabilities were also present in all affected individuals. Motor delay and abnormalities, including hypotonia, were noted in 5/6 (83%) of affected individuals. Severity varied among individuals, with subject 1 being the most severely affected—presenting with spastic tetraplegia and never achieving independent ambulation. ID and learning difficulties were consistently observed in those who reached early childhood and older. Gastrointestinal dysmotility/feeding problems were present in 5 affected individuals who were 3–13 years of age, which was listed as feeding problems with or without G-tube dependence, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and recurrent vomiting. Cardiovascular issues were prevalent in 50% of patients, presenting as a variety of abnormalities, including atrial septal defect, ventricular hypertrophy, dysplastic mitral and pulmonary valves, perimembranous ventricular septal defect, bicuspid aortic valve, and ascending aorta dilatation. Behavioral abnormalities were noted in three affected individuals, mainly presenting as autism spectrum disorder or autistic features. We also noted ophthalmologic anomalies or visual disability in three affected individuals, presenting as strabismus or refractive errors (myopia and astigmatism), although coloboma, commonly observed in patients with CES, was not seen in any of our affected individuals. Variable epilepsy or seizure-like activity was detected in three affected individuals, ranging from absence seizures to refractory seizures. Unilateral hearing was present in one affected individual, together with microtia. An enlarged antihelix of the outer ear was noted in another individual. Cleft lip/palate, sleeping abnormalities, and skeletal abnormalities were noted in two individuals each. The latter included fusion of two vertebrae in subject 2 and digit differences with tapered fingers in subject 3 (Table 2). To contextualize the clinical findings observed in our cohort, we compared the phenotypic features of CECR2 neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) with those reported in CES. Several dysmorphic and phenotypic features overlapped between the two conditions. Characteristic facial features of CES are down-slanting palpebral fissures, inner epicanthic folds, hypertelorism, flat nasal bridge, micrognathia, and various abnormalities of the ears and eyes.12 Subject 1, the oldest in our cohort, has facial dysmorphism, such as a bulbous nose, small nares, a wide mouth, thick lips, widely spaced upper incisors, micrognathia, and an enlarged antihelix of the ear. Ocular and auricular dysmorphology seen in our patients is also an overlapping feature with CES. Outer ear malformations such as preauricular pits/tags, other malformations, and hearing loss have been found in patients with CES, along with low-set ears and severe malformations such as microtia. Subject 3 in our study showed microtia and unilateral hearing loss. Subject 1 had enlarged antihelixes of the ears. No preauricular pits/tags were noted in our cohort. Cleft lip/palate, sometimes observed in CES, was also found in two of our patients. Ophthalmologic anomalies in CES mainly consist of coloboma, refractory errors, and strabismus,12 present in subject 1 in the CECR2 cohort. Two other affected individuals (subjects 3 and 4) present with myopia and astigmatism. Iris coloboma is seen in roughly 33% of individuals affected by CES.12 Its absence in our cohort likely reflects a limited sample size or the prominence of other ocular anomalies, as visual dysfunction is still a component of the CECR2 phenotype. Abdominal malformations in CES, such as anal atresia/hypoplasia or other anal anomalies that present in CES, were not noted in this cohort. However, sacral dimples, such as in subject 5, have also been reported in CES.28 In our cohort, multiple cardiac malformations were present in subjects 5 and 6 (Table 2), which were also compatible with the findings in CES. Both disorders, CES and CECR2-related neurodevelopmental syndrome, include a significant incidence of intellectual impairment.12 In our cohort, ID varied from mild to severe. Although ID is typically mild in CES, moderate and severe cases have also been reported.12 Growth delay and narrow head circumference are among the main features in our cohort, which are also commonly reported in CES.12 Both the speech delay and DD seen in our cohort have also been reported in CES but were atypical.16,29 Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUTs) were common in patients with CES and subject 5 of our cohort (left renal agenesis and right ureteropelvic obstruction compatible with genitourinary malformations). Skeletal abnormalities included fusion of two vertebrae in subject 3 and tapered fingers in subject 4. In CES, skeletal abnormalities have been reported but may be sub-clinical and can include fused vertebrae and abnormal limbs.16 Brain malformations were recently reported to be higher (24%) than originally described in CES,12 including mild ventricular dilation, thin corpus callosum, thin anterior pituitary, and ectopic posterior pituitary. An unusual subject with a de novo duplication at 22q11.1–22q13.1, e.g., a trisomy, not tetrasomy, was also shown to have an interhemispheric cyst and corpus callosum agenesis.30 This overlaps with the corpus callosum thinning/agenesis seen in our CECR2 cohort.

Chromosome 22q11 contains multiple region-specific low-copy repeats that predispose it to DNA rearrangements, resulting not only in CES but also in several other genetic disorders, including 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (velocardiofacial syndrome/DiGeorge syndrome; MIM: 192430 and 188400), 22q11.2 distal microdeletion syndrome (MIM: 611867), and 22q11.2 duplication syndrome (MIM: 608363).31 TBX1 is believed to account for most of the observed phenotype in these cases. Both CECR2 and CESCR are located proximally to the critical regions described in these disorders,32,33 showing that these are distinct disorders that closely reside in an area prone to rearrangement. The 22q11.2 deletion/duplication syndromes differ from the CECR2-related disorder in several phenotypic features (i.e., facial dysmorphism, thymic hypoplasia, immunological defects, increased risk of infections, and hypocalcemia).34 These syndromes can present with septal defects, but 22q11.2 deletion is dominated by conotruncal malformations and aortic arch anomalies,34 whereas CECR2 disorder and 22q11.2 duplication syndrome show broader involvement.35 While all of these conditions share nonspecific neurodevelopmental signs (e.g., developmental and speech delay), the 22q11.2 deletion/duplication syndromes differ from CECR2-related NDD in both phenotype and genetic basis. Overall, each patient’s genomic alteration should be carefully evaluated to determine whether it includes or may affect CECR2, as this gene lies outside the critical interval for 22q11.2 deletion and duplication syndromes but does reside within the more proximal CESCR.

CECR2 is located in a 0.3 Mb region, chr22:17799398–18111588 (hg19), of the CESCR.16 This report describes single-nucleotide and small insertion/deletion variants in the CECR2 gene, underlying a syndrome with clinical features that overlap with CES, a well-delineated molecularly diverse chromosomal disorder. In total, six probands were analyzed with rare heterozygous variants in CECR2. Multiple malformations found in this cohort reflect abnormal early embryonic development, leading to aberrancy in neurulation, neural tube formation, and postnatal brain development.5 The study of Cecr2 in mice has revealed strain-specific differences in the expression and penetrance of developmental defects, which may mirror the variability seen in our human subjects comprising diverse ethnicities.11,18

CESCR contains three candidate genes for CES: CECR2, SLC25A18, and ATP6V1E.16,17 Each gene typically has four copies due to tetrasomy of 22q11.1q11.21 in various complex rearranged states, with sSMC(22) being the most frequent cytogenetic abnormality.16 However, in 37% of sSMC(22) cases, mosaicism was detected. Alternatively, ring sSMC and intrachromosomal triplication have also been reported. Molecular cytogenetics showed that copy number is usually tetrasomic (89%), trisomic (7%), or both (4%).16 Complex chromosomal rearrangements, varying copy numbers, mosaicism, and genes in this region have led to mixed phenotypes, hampering the identification of the causative genes. Denavit et al. estimate that 60% of all individuals with CES are difficult to diagnose based on clinical findings alone,13 and only a minority of them present the classic triad.12,13 Additionally, patients with CES with low mosaicism levels are associated with a milder CES phenotype.12,16

CECR2 remains a strong candidate gene and is likely the main contributor to CES, supported both by similar defects seen in mice11 and now also the phenotypic overlap with CECR2-related NDD described in this study. Its expression pattern is consistent with these phenotypes. In adults, CECR2 is most highly expressed in the adult human cerebellum but is also expressed in skeletal muscle and the pituitary gland.36 In the developing human brain cortex, expression is most prominent in glial progenitor cells,37 and during early development, it peaks in the neural tube.38

CECR2-related NDD is characterized by several universal features, including DD, speech issues, growth delay, relatively small head circumference, brain malformations, and varying malformations of several body systems. Some of these features may be directly explained by CECR2’s interactions with ISWI chromatin remodelers. For instance, reduced head growth could result from disrupted interactions with SNF2L, which plays a key role in regulating neural proliferation.3 Similarly, abnormalities of the corpus callosum may arise through impaired function of SNF2H, which is essential for the development of callosal projection neurons in the corpus callosum.4

Genotype-phenotype correlations are difficult to draw from this small cohort of six affected individuals, but both missense and predicted LoF variants appear to be associated with severe clinical outcomes. For example, subject 1 with the variant p.(Gln1428Arg) presents with absence seizures and tetraplegia, and subject 3 with p.(Thr580Hisfs∗98) presents with focal epilepsy and is non-verbal.

Notably, all identified missense variants cluster within 100 amino acids of the C terminus of the protein (Figure 1B), suggesting that this region may be functionally critical. However, this portion of the protein remains structurally uncharacterized, with low-confidence AlphaFold predictions (predicted local distance difference test [pLDDT] < 60), indicating it is an intrinsically disordered region with limited reliability in its predicted structure. Although these disordered regions do not have a stable secondary or tertiary structure under physiological conditions, they can play essential biological roles, such as providing essential flexibility for ligand binding.39 The N-terminal region of CECR2 contains a bromodomain, which binds acetylated histones to help position CECR2 on the chromatin.40 Many chromatin remodelers rely on intrinsically disordered regions for scaffolding interactions, maintaining protein stability, or enabling conformational flexibility.39,41 The clustering of pathogenic missense variants C-terminally may indicate another critical role of this disordered region in CECR2’s function, such as in CERF complex formation, and highlights its pivotal role in ensuring the dynamic nature required for effective chromatin regulation.

Genetic studies have increasingly implicated chromatin remodeling proteins in the etiology of NDDs,2 underscoring their critical role in brain development and gene regulation. Chromatin remodelers regulate DNA accessibility by modifying nucleosome positioning, thereby influencing transcriptional programs essential for neuronal differentiation and synaptic function. Notable examples include SMARCA2 and SMARCB1, core components of the BAF (SWI/SNF) complex, linked to disorders such as Nicolaides-Baraitser and Coffin-Siris syndromes.2 Recently, pathogenic variants in SMARCA1 have also been shown to cause an X-linked NDD42,43 with mild to severe ID/DD, delayed or regressive speech development, macrocephaly, hypotonia, and behavioral difficulties.

This study supports the model that CECR2 is dosage sensitive, as both increased copy number (duplication, triplication, or mosaicism) and LOF variants predicted to undergo NMD disrupt protein levels and cause overlapping developmental abnormalities. In addition, CECR2 is also highly intolerant to LoF variants in population databases. CECR2-related NDD, likely driven by haploinsufficiency and/or dominant-negative effects, and CES, due to increased copy numbers, suggest that CECR2 expression must be tightly regulated during early development. Reciprocal microdeletion and microduplication syndromes sometimes show similar findings,44 indicating a need for tightly controlled dosage regulation in humans.

In conclusion, this study identifies heterozygous CECR2 variants as a cause of an NDD, expanding the phenotypic spectrum associated with CESCRs. The clinical and molecular findings underscore CECR2’s dosage sensitivity and its essential role in early neural development, most likely mediated through chromatin remodeling and dynamic regulation of gene expression.
