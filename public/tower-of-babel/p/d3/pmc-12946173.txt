Fast multi-resolution 3D printing of microfluidics: enabling 2 μm channels and ultra-compact mixers
Authors: Dallin S Miner, Matthew S Viglione, Kent Hooper, Adam T Woolley, Gregory P Nordin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrofluidic devices with ultra-fine features are critical for applications in biomedical diagnostics, chemical analysis, and lab-on-chip systems, but achieving high-resolution negative features with fast print times remains a significant challenge due to limitations in conventional 3D printing techniques. Motivated by the need for rapid fabrication of precise, compact microfluidic structures to enhance performance and miniaturization, we present an efficient multi-resolution 3D printing technique designed to fabricate microfluidic devices with exceptionally high-resolution negative features. For instance, we achieve fully enclosed channels with cross sections as small as 1.9 µm × 2.0 µm, a two-order-of-magnitude reduction in cross-sectional area compared to the 18 µm × 20 µm channels reported in our previous work (Gong et al., Lab Chip 17, 2899, 2017). Our method utilizes a dual-optical-engine approach comprising a Very High Resolution Optical Engine (VHROE) and a Main Optical Engine (MOE), each employing distinct pixel resolutions and LED wavelengths. The VHROE, with a pixel pitch of 0.75 µm and a 365 nm LED, delivers unparalleled resolution, while the MOE, with a pixel pitch of 15 µm and a 405 nm LED, ensures efficient coverage for larger areas up to 38.9 mm × 24.3 mm. Custom ultraviolet (UV) short-pass filters are used to tailor each LED spectrum, optimizing performance for each optical engine. Both engines are mounted on an XY stage to achieve multi-resolution imaging in the XY plane. Depth-wise (Z-axis) multi-resolution is achieved by formulating a photopolymerizable resin incorporating two UV absorbers possessing distinct absorption spectra such that the different light spectra from the VHROE and MOE encounter disparate levels of absorption, resulting in 1/e penetration depths of 2 µm and 20 µm, respectively. This enables true multi-resolution printing in all three dimensions. Our method balances speed and resolution by selectively deploying the VHROE for ultra-fine features and the MOE for bulk structures within a single 3D print. To demonstrate the versatility of this technique, we fabricated intricate microfluidic structures, including a triply-periodic minimal surface (TPMS) with 7 µm pores embedded within a 150 µm × 150 µm cross section enclosed channel, and an ultra-compact microfluidic mixer with a printed volume of only 0.017 mm³ (17 nL) and a print time of 21 minutes. These examples underscore the potential of our multi-resolution 3D printing method for advancing microfluidic device fabrication. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Micro-optics

Introduction

Over the past decade and a half, there has been significant interest in leveraging 3D printing for microfluidic device fabrication, leading to numerous advancements, particularly in the use of Digital Light Processing (DLP) image projection-based 3D printing (DLP-SLA)1–7. While other 3D printing methods, including inkjet8, Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM)9, and Powder Bed Fusion (PBF)10 have been used for microfluidic device fabrication, we have found DLP-SLA to be particularly effective for achieving high resolution microfluidic structures11–14.

A fundamental requirement for successfully printing enclosed microfluidic devices with DLP-SLA is the ability to create high-resolution negative features. This requirement imposes different constraints than other applications, such as 3D printing molds with open features for casting materials like polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) for microfluidics15, or more conventional 3D printing applications14, which often prioritize positive features. Since 2015 our research on the development and use of DLP-SLA for microfluidics has focused on (1) understanding the practical resolution limits of negative features12,13,14, (2) designing both passive (e.g., channels, mixers) and active (e.g., valves, pumps) microfluidic components12,16–20, (3) integrating such components into functional devices18,21–25, and (4) applying bespoke devices to specific applications23,24,26. To this end we have developed a series of custom 3D printers and specialized resins designed to work together, enabling the fabrication of high-resolution negative features for both passive and active structures12,14.

When defining negative features, it is essential to distinguish between resolution in the XY image plane and resolution in the orthogonal (Z) dimension. The key factor for XY resolution is the projected pixel size of the image, while for Z resolution it is the optical penetration depth of the light forming the image. The optical penetration depth, ha, (1/e drop in irradiance) is determined by light attenuation due to absorption from photoinitiator and any added UV absorber in the resin. Some work in the literature attributes Z resolution to the selected layer thickness of the 3D print, but this is incorrect. Instead, the layer thickness should be chosen based on the optical penetration depth (typically between 0.5 ha and ha as shown in our previous work)17. The optical penetration depth in conjunction with layer exposure time determines how deeply light will cause photopolymerization in Z, regardless of layer thickness.

When it comes to XY resolution, there is a distinct tradeoff between resolution and print area for a given Digital Micromirror Device (DMD) used in image formation: the higher the resolution, the smaller the print area. As an example, consider the work of ref.27 in which they constructed a custom 3D printer with a 1920 × 1080 pixel DMD in conjunction with projection optics such that each pixel in the image plane is 1 µm2, which is a very attractive XY resolution. However, the tradeoff is small image area, i.e., 1.92 mm × 1.08 mm. As demonstrated in their paper, larger image areas can be achieved by mounting the optical engine on an XY stage and tiling projected images28 for each 3D print layer. The downside is that this approach significantly increases build time compared to printing with a larger image size that covers the entire build area.

An obvious solution is to employ a multi-resolution strategy in which two different print resolutions are available, with the higher resolution used only where it is needed and the lower resolution for everything else. This can in principle significantly decrease build time depending on how many high resolution regions are needed. An example of this approach is discussed in ref.29, which reports a custom 3D printer with 10 µm high resolution and 30 µm low resolution optical engines (3:1 pixel size ratio). A commercial DLP-based tool that takes this approach is the BMF D1025 3D printer, which offers 10 µm high resolution and 25 µm low resolution optical engines (2.5:1 pixel size ratio). Another commercial example is a scanned laser stereolithography system, the DiLase 3D by Kloe, which offers several combinations of high/low resolution laser beam spot sizes: 2 µm and 5 µm (2.5:1 ratio), 5 µm and 20 µm (4:1 ratio), and 10 µm and 60 µm (6:1 ratio). These strategies are attractive in that they break the resolution/print area tradeoff. However, it is important to note that they are multi-resolution only in XY, and not in Z. This means that a z-resolution tradeoff remains in which ha must be selected to be capable of resolving the smallest features in z, thereby constraining the entire print to be fabricated with that high z resolution even though there may be large regions in which lower z resolution is sufficient. Lack of multi-resolution in Z therefore leads to unnecessarily long print times.

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a DLP-SLA system with true multi-resolution in XY and Z, and demonstrate its utility in fabricating mixed-resolution microfluidic devices. We use a Very High Resolution Optical Engine (VHROE) with a pixel pitch of 0.75 µm and a 365 nm LED for high resolution features in XY and Z, and a Main Optical Engine (MOE) with a pixel pitch of 15 µm and a 405 nm LED for lower resolution features in the rest of the 3D print, providing a notably larger 20:1 ratio in pixel size. Each optical engine has its own custom short pass filter to shape its emission spectrum with respect to the resin absorption, and the resin is designed with two UV absorbers such that the 1/e optical penetration depth is 2 µm for the VHROE and 20 µm for the MOE (10:1 ratio), thereby achieving multi-resolution in Z and enabling the use of 1.5 µm and 15 µm layer thicknesses, respectively. Without the substantial disparity in Z-resolution, leveraging the dramatic XY resolution difference would not be practical. With this system we demonstrate enclosed channels as small as 1.9 µm x 2.0 µm, an ultra-porous triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structure with 7 µm pores embedded in a 150 µm channel, and an extremely small and fast, on-chip pump operated diffusive mixer with 2 nL interior volume taking up only 17 nL of printed volume.

Results and discussion

Multi-resolution 3D printer

The overall multi-resolution concept for a 3D printed volume is shown in Fig. 1a where the gray region denotes material created with the MOE using 15 µm thick layers and the embedded red regions are created with the VHROE using 1.5 µm layers. For each MOE layer in which a VHROE-defined region is embedded, ten VHROE layers are first fabricated followed by one surrounding MOE layer in which the VHROE region is accounted for by leaving that region dark in the MOE image. This process is repeated for every MOE layer in which a VHROE region is embedded, which is illustrated in Fig. S1.

Figure 1b shows a schematic illustration of our custom multi-resolution 3D printer designed to enable embedding very high resolution regions in a much larger volume defined at lower resolution, with a corresponding photograph of the actual system in Fig. 1c. The two optical engines are mounted on an XY stage so that they can be appropriately positioned under the resin tray during 3D printing to project UV images at selected locations. In practice, we position the MOE at the same position for every MOE layer exposure because its print area (38.9 mm × 24.3 mm) is large enough to accommodate most devices. Focus is accomplished by moving the entire upper assembly during printing to a calibrated vertical position that is specific to each optical engine.

The designed difference in absorption of the resin for each optical engine is illustrated in Fig. 1d, which shows the absorption coefficients of NPS and avobenzone in our resin as a function of wavelength based on their individually measured absorption spectra and resin concentrations. Also shown is the normalized emission spectrum of each short pass filtered LED. Note that the 405 nm LED spectrum primarily overlaps the NPS absorption spectrum, which has a small absorption coefficient compared to the 365 nm LED spectrum, which overlaps both the NPS and avobenzone absorption spectra. Integrating each LED emission spectrum over the total absorption spectrum of the resin gives a 10-fold difference in absorption coefficient for light from the 365 nm LED compared to light from the 405 nm LED. The result can be seen in Fig. 1e (405 nm light) and Fig. 1f (365 nm light) where the normalized dose12 is shown as a function of z. As can be seen from the graphs, the optical penetration depth (the z position at which the normalized dose is e-1 = 0.368 of its initial value of 1.0 at z = 0) occurs at z = 20 µm and z = 2 µm, respectively, thereby giving rise to multi-resolution capability in Z.

High resolution

With this resin formulation, Fig. 1g demonstrates the raw XY resolution that can be achieved with the VHROE. The microscope image shows 0.75 µm photopolymerized lines alternating with 0.75 µm spaces. This result confirms that the VHROE can fully resolve features where lines of single activated pixels are separated by lines of single deactivated pixels.

Although this paper primarily focuses on negative features for microfluidic devices, we also characterized positive feature formation for both optical engines. Feature fidelity was evaluated as a function of feature size and layer exposure time using L-shaped lines and arrays of square pillars, with line widths and post sizes ranging from 1 to 8 pixels for each optical engine. Complete datasets are provided in Figs. S2–S11 (MOE) and Figs. S12–S23 (VHROE). Representative results are shown in Fig. 2a, b for the MOE and VHROE, respectively; the MOE lines were designed with a 10:1 aspect ratio, while the VHROE lines had a 2.5:1 aspect ratio. The MOE produces notably sharper features, including the isolated pillar in the upper right of Fig. 2a, whereas achieving comparable edge sharpness becomes more challenging at the higher spatial resolution of the VHROE (Fig. 2b).

This model, designed as a boat, features overhangs, small holes, and intricate details, making it a challenging test for print accuracy. To showcase our 3D printing capabilities, we fabricated two 3DBenchy models in a single print run. Figure 2c shows an SEM image of a 4 mm long 3DBenchy fabricated with the MOE. On top of the smokestack, a 200 µm long 3DBenchy (Fig. 2d) was printed with the VHROE, which is 20 times smaller than the original 3DBenchy. Additional SEM images can be found in Fig. S24. For comparison, the smallest 3DBenchys reported in the literature with similar levels of detail are shown in Figs. S25a and S25b for 2-photon polymerization30 and 1 µm resolution DLP-SLA27, respectively. Note that our VHROE-generated 3DBenchy fidelity is comparable to these benchmarks.

As discussed earlier, microfluidic device fabrication is based on negative feature generation. To test the minimum negative feature size we can generate with the VHROE, we designed a device having small enclosed channels, each 120 µm long. After fabrication, the channel must be flushed of uncured resin to be considered a success. The smallest channel cross section that can be reliably printed and flushed is shown in Fig. 2e, which is 1.9 µm x 2.0 µm in size. Multiple simultaneously-fabricated channels of that size are shown in Fig. 2f. Note that the channel size is an order of magnitude smaller in each dimension than our 2017 result12 shown in Fig. 2g, which is 18 µm x 20 µm. This represents ~100x smaller cross sectional area enabled by the VHROE in conjunction with our multi-absorber custom resin. When used for larger negative features, fabrication with the VHROE leads to significantly smoother sidewalls as shown by comparing Fig. 2g to Fig. 2h. The sloped sidewalls result from polymer shrinkage and concomitant stress relief during fabrication. The SEM images show that the lower and upper channel surface roughness is at most a small fraction of a micron. A top-view microscope image of six cleared 2 µm wide channels is shown in Fig. 2i. Widening of each channel near its ends has been consistently observed in our studies since 2015 when we first used a commercial 3D printer to investigate minimum achievable channel size (60 µm by 108 µm at that time using a custom resin)17. Additional SEM images of fabricated channels and channel arrays are shown in Fig. S26, including a 1.1 µm x 1.7 µm channel. Statistics for two small channel devices with 46 and 30 replicates are shown in Table S1 with channel cross sections of 2.84 µm x 2.28 µm and 2.48 µm x 1.42 µm, respectively, along with standard deviations and yields.

As a demonstration of the utility of high resolution channels, we fabricated small biocage devices, inspired by ref.31, which reported fabricating such devices using a Nanoscribe 2-photon polymerization (2PP) 3D printer with a 25x objective. These biocages were 900 µm tall, featuring an internal diameter of 300 µm and a wall thickness of 20 µm (Fig. S27). The pores in their cages were 5 µm in diameter with 10 µm center-to-center spacing. It took 46 min to fabricate a single device. We used our multi-resolution DLP-SLA technology to fabricate similar devices as shown in Fig. 3a–c and Fig. S28. While our final pore size (7 µm) and spacing (14 µm) were slightly larger than ref.31, all other dimensions were kept the same. We found that due to the repeated stress of pulling the print off the print surface after polymerization of each layer that smaller channels and channel spacing could not be fabricated without compromising the structural stability of the device. As shown in Fig. 3c, we were able to print 8 biocages simultaneously, which took 53 min. Thus, our technology allowed us to closely approach the negative feature size of the 2PP device, with the added benefit of multiplexed fabrication. Applying dimensional analysis with Big O notation, the print time for DLP-SLA technology grows linearly as O(N) where N is the number of layers whereas in 2PP systems the print time exhibits cubic growth as O(N3) where N is the voxel count. The difference comes down to the fact that in DLP-SLA, a complete image (O(N2) where N is the number of pixels in one dimension) is exposed simultaneously in O(1).

Embedded multi-resolution

Although the VHROE provides impressive high resolution, the true benefit of our multi-resolution 3D printing method lies in its full XYZ resolution capabilities. As an illustration, we designed a device with 150 µm x 150 µm channels in a 1.65 mm tall rectangular chip. In each channel, we fabricated a 1440 µm long diamond lattice triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structure. With the exception of the TPMS regions, the rest of the device was sliced into 110 layers, each 15 µm thick. The TPMS regions were sliced into 100 layers, each 1.5 µm thick, with the total being equivalent to ten 15 µm layers, i.e., the height of the channels. Slices for the two layer thicknesses were appropriately interleaved using a custom slicer utility we created for this specific application. In our design, we chose to create a 75 µm thick region on top of the TPMS structure with the VHROE interleaved with five 15 µm MOE layers before transitioning to all subsequent layers being formed with the MOE. This was the equivalent of five low resolution layers above the fine TPMS structures to prevent undesired polymerization due to the larger optical penetration depth of light from the MOE. The total print time was 52 min, of which the MOE layers were printed within 5 min. The remaining 47 min were dedicated to the high resolution areas. A large majority of this time was spent waiting for our vibration isolation table to settle between XY stage movements, pointing to the need to improve the vibration isolation mounting of our multi-resolution 3D printer. With better vibration isolation, we estimate that the total print time would be reduced to 15 minutes.

Figure 3d–f shows several images of fabricated TPMS structures. For example, in Fig. 3d, a TPMS structure is shown in a channel where the subsequent layers above the channel were not printed so that the TPMS structure could more easily be evaluated. In this open-top device, the pore size in the TPMS structure is measured to be 7 µm. Looking closely at the edges of the channel, some overlap between the designed TPMS structure and the surrounding material is visible. This was intentionally created to ensure physical continuity between the VHROE and MOE exposures and to reduce the time needed to align the optical systems. Figure 3e shows an SEM image of a channel cross section when the roof layers above the channel are fabricated. Figure 3f is an alternate view using an optical microscope. In both cases, the TPMS structure is well-formed, which demonstrates the efficacy of fully embedding VHROE regions within MOE-generated layers. Additional TPMS structure images are shown in Fig. S29. The demonstrated ability to embed high-resolution TPMS structures within larger, lower-resolution microfluidic channels highlights their potential for applications such as 3D-printed liquid chromatography columns, where high surface area is critical for efficient molecular separations.

MOE pumps

So far, our attention has been on various passive structures; however, the capacity to produce active components such as valves and pumps is essential for microfluidics, offering enhanced fluid control and allowing for more compact experimental arrangements. We now focus on valve and pump fabrication with the MOE, evaluating our original pump cycle18 and two new pump cycles in anticipation of integrating a MOE pump with a brand-new, highly compact VHROE 3D mixer design.

A pump is composed of two membrane valves and a displacement chamber (DC)14,18, which is a valve-like structure where both fluidic ports are at the edges of its fluid chamber. A membrane valve is schematically illustrated in Fig. 4a–c, and a pump in Fig. 4d. Each valve and DC has a flexible membrane that separates upper and lower cylindrical chambers, with the lower being the fluid chamber and the upper the control or pneumatic chamber. The membrane is designed as a single 15 µm layer that is simply part of the 3D print and therefore comprised of the same material as the rest of the print. Its flexibility derives from its thinness in relation to its diameter and the low Young’s modulus of the 3D printed material (~7–8 MPa)12. By alternating the valve states and actuating the DC in the proper order, flow is achieved.

Fast embedded mixer simulation and design

Passive mixing of two fluids in microfluidic devices poses multiple challenges due to the dominance of viscous forces over inertia, leading to purely laminar flow (low Reynolds number) and mixing dominated by diffusion. Typical approaches to promote mixing rely on microstructures to create stretching and folding of fluid layers or geometry-induced mixing using, for example, serpentine channels, staggered herringbone mixers, and asymmetric ridges to disrupt laminar streamlines and enhance diffusion. Such approaches can require a significant fraction of device volume or relatively long channel lengths (many millimeters) to implement. In contrast, we wanted a mixer that has a short overall length ( ~ 0.5 mm) and occupies very little device volume and therefore costs little in terms of layout impact for microfluidic device applications. An obvious route to achieve this goal is to minimize the diffusion distance required for complete mixing of two fluids12. We illustrate the principles and effects of various approaches with a series of computational fluid dynamics simulations as discussed below. In all cases, the total flow rate was 1 µL/min.

As a baseline, consider the geometry in Fig. 5a with two 30 × 30 µm inlet channels offset in X and Z, and where red and blue represent normalized concentrations of 1.0 and 0.0, respectively. These channels merge smoothly into a single 60 µm-wide mixing channel, which then expands into a 500 µm-wide observation channel. To ensure a direct comparison of mixer geometries, the observation channel remains identical across all simulation geometries. The upper left inset in Fig. 5a shows the concentration distribution in a 2D cross section of the observation channel. Note that mixing is confined to the narrow central region where the two fluid streams are in contact, and is due to diffusion across the fluid stream boundary. The average fluid dwell time in the mixing channel is 25 ms and the corresponding 2D diffusion length is 6.4 µm, which is only 10% of the width of the channel and results in poor mixing as represented in the violin plot in Fig. 5g.

A scale bar is shown in the top right corner of (d). g Violin plots showing concentration distributions for each cross section

A possible approach to enhance mixing is to shorten the required diffusion path length by narrowing the mixing channel. Figure 5b shows a geometry with a mixing channel width of 12 µm and height of 198 µm. The average fluid dwell time is 21 ms and the 2D diffusion length is 6.0 µm (i.e., similar to the geometry in Fig. 5a). Note in the corresponding violin plot that mixing is improved over the baseline geometry, but overall is still poor, with a large variation of ±27.0% around a mean of 50%. By extending the channel length from 198 to 748 µm through the adoption of a serpentine channel design, as depicted in Fig. 5c, the mean fluid dwell time can be increased to 82 ms and the diffusion length to 12 µm. Although the corresponding violin plot indicates a twofold decrease in the concentration distribution for a variation of ±14.4%, the mixing performance remains suboptimal.

An alternative mixing geometry is illustrated in Fig. 5d, where both inlet channels are divided into six narrow channels, each 13 µm wide. These narrow channels are interleaved and merged, allowing individual fluid streams to diffuse with their immediate neighbors to achieve mixing. The simulation visualization and violin plot demonstrate that this approach is somewhat effective except for the outermost fluid streams near the channel walls. This issue is largely overcome by redesigning the mixing channel into a narrow, high-aspect-ratio channel, as shown in Fig. 5e, achieving a concentration distribution with a variation of ±6.2% around a mean of 50%. Further enhancement is achieved by incorporating the serpentine channel design from Fig. 5c, as depicted in Fig. 5f, reducing the concentration variation to only ±2.1%.

As a final note on the optimized geometry of Fig. 5f, 12 interleaved fluid streams are compressed into a 12 µm channel, forming ~1 µm wide fluid sheath pairs with a 2D diffusion time of just 0.6 ms. This rapid mixing, achieved at a low flow rate of 1 µL/min, enables a substantial reduction in mixer volume and a correspondingly small dead volume compared to other approaches reported in the literature.

Fast embedded mixer fabrication and measurement

We designed a microfluidic mixer chip integrating the interleaved mixer geometry of Fig. 5f with an on-chip pump, serpentine channel for flow rate measurements, fluid routing channels, and pneumatic control channels. The mixer component was fabricated with the VHROE to achieve the narrow interleaved channels and serpentine mixing channel. Other components, requiring lower resolution, were fabricated with the MOE. For the device results reported here, we chose to fabricate the high resolution mixer region on top of the MOE-fabricated layers to facilitate more convenient imaging. However, devices with the mixer region embedded within MOE-fabricated layers yielded comparable performance.

A CAD model of the device design is shown in Fig. 6a–c, with microscope images of a fabricated device (red dye filling the fluid channels) in Fig. 6d–g. The on-chip pump is designed to pull fluid through the mixer from two different inlets that are connected to reservoirs. The pump then pushes the mixed fluid through a serpentine channel. Monitoring the position of an air/fluid meniscus in the serpentine channel as a function of time with a video camera permits the measurement of both instantaneous and average flow rate through the device. Also note in Fig. 6b, e, f that the mixer (excluding the observation channel) measures <500 µm in length, which is an exceptionally compact component.

We measured mixing performance using fluorescence in two operating conditions. The first utilized an external syringe pump to steadily draw fluid through the device at a flow rate of 1 µL/min. Fluorescence measurement of the mixer and observation channel are shown in Fig. 7a. A line scan of the fluorescence across the width of the observation channel is shown in Fig. 7b, where the channel spans the horizontal axis (measured in units of camera pixels) from pixels 75 to 315. As shown in Fig. S30, we also investigated mixing performance at higher flow rates, including 10x and 100x the designed flow rate of 1 µL/min. In both cases, the concentration uniformity decreases, with greater departure from ideal performance at higher flow rates. This underscores the importance of tailoring mixer design to the maximum flow rate needed for a particular application.

The second operating condition used the on-chip pump to drive fluid flow, in which case the steady flow of the first operating condition is replaced with pulsatile flow. The volume expelled by the DC during its actuation phase is 4.9 nL, which is 3.6 times the volume of the serpentine mixing channel. For pulsatile flow, fluid passes through the mixing channel only during a fraction of a pump cycle. It is therefore important that the peak flow rate during DC actuation is low enough to ensure adequate mixing during fluid passage through the mixing channel. We therefore selected valve and DC actuation times, τv and τdc, of 20 and 80 ms, respectively, for a pump period of 200 ms and an average flow rate of ~0.8 µL/min. This results in a peak flow rate during DC actuation of ~2 µL/min, which is only a factor of 2 larger than our original mixer design flow rate. Under these conditions, fluorescence measurement of the mixer performance is shown in Fig. 7c, with a line scan in Fig. 7d. The line scan shows a concentration of 50% ± 2%, which is similar to the syringe pump operating condition. We have included a video of the mixer in operation as Supplementary Movie 1.

Methods

Photopolymerizable resin

3D printing was performed using an in-house formulated resin consisting of poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA, Mn 250) monomer with 1% (w/w) Irgacure 819 photoinitiator and 1.85% (w/w) 2-nitrophenyl phenyl sulfide (NPS) and 1.85% (w/w) avobenzone as UV absorbers. This resin has excellent optical clarity to facilitate microscope observation, similar to our previous resin formulations that incorporated either NPS or avobenzone alone12,24. As seen in Fig. 1d, the absorption spectra of the combined UV absorbers extend from a wavelength of ~425 nm into the UV, leaving the material transparent throughout the visible spectrum above this wavelength.

PEGDA and Irgacure 819 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, and NPS and avobenzone from TCI America. Each material was used as received. The molecular structure for each material is shown in Fig. S31.

Custom 3D printer

Our custom 3D printer (Fig. 1) has two optical engines mounted on an XY stage comprised of stacked linear stages from Griffin Motion with 100 mm and 300 mm range of travel, respectively. The MOE is a Visitech LRS-WQ DL LC with an LRS-20 lens assembly which projects a 15.2 µm pixel pitch WQXGA (2560×1600 pixel) image with an area of 38.9 mm × 24.3 mm. The VHROE is a Wintech PRO6500 with custom optics to project a 1080p (1920 × 1080 pixel) image with 0.75 µm pixel pitch (image area: 1.44 mm × 1.20 mm). The Visitech optical engine is a dual LED system (365 nm and 405 nm) and equipped with a custom 410 nm short pass filter (Asahi Spectra) to remove the long wavelength tail of the 405 nm LED. Only the 405 nm LED was used in this work. The Wintech optical engine is equipped with a 365 nm LED and a custom 370 nm short pass filter (Asahi Spectra). The effect of the short pass filter on each optical engine spectrum is shown in Fig. S32, which clearly indicates their effectiveness in eliminating the long wavelength tail of each LED source. Additionally, a Keyence CL3000 Confocal distance sensor is mounted on each light engine lens barrel to enable active focus correction. The print surface at the bottom of the printer’s resin tray is a 5 mil FEP film with a 2 mil silicone adhesive (CS Hyde) on a 75 mm × 100 mm x 1.1 mm glass plate.

An invar truss is used to mount the upper assembly to minimize thermal drift of the 3D printer. The upper assembly is attached to the truss with a vertical motion stage (Griffin Motion) which acts as the focus degree of freedom. The assembly also includes a custom tip-tilt mechanism to enable two more degrees of freedom that are necessary to ensure full calibration (focus-tip-tilt) of projected images into the plane in which each resin layer resides prior to photopolymerization. The rest of the assembly consists of the resin tray with glass window bottom and FEP film, and the build platform and associated vertical linear stage (Griffin Motion) that sets layer thickness during printing.

We have created custom python-based 3D printer control software that runs on a Raspberry Pi single board computer. The software is modular and enables complete control of all aspects of 3D printer hardware. It also generates a web-based user interface through which users can submit and manage print jobs. The user interface also has a manual mode through which 3D printer functions can be directly controlled by a user.

The typical VHROE and MOE layer exposure times used for the work in this paper were 90 ms and 900 ms, respectively, with corresponding irradiances of 160 mW/cm2 and 34 mW/cm2.

Print file format

Devices for 3D printing were designed in OpenSCAD32 and exported in STL file format. They were sliced and packaged into print zip files using custom slicer and JSON print file generator software. The 3D printer control software is designed to use a single zip file to control a full print job. The JSON format is a multi-resolution modified version of our 2021 open source JSON format12,33. Simpler prints used images generated programmatically with python/numpy or manually with GIMP34 and then packaged into a zip file with an appropriate JSON control file.

Electron microscopy and sample preparation

After 3D printing, devices were prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging by mounting on a SEM stub with carbon tape. They were then sputter coated with 80/20 Au/Pd in a Quorum Q150T sputter coater. The samples were imaged using a Thermo Scientific Apreo C LoVac SEM with the following settings: 5 kV acceleration voltage, 0.1 nA beam current, and 100 ns dwell time with 16 integrations. For the channel cross sections, additional pre-processing steps were taken: samples were first cross sectioned with a Boeckeler RMC-MTX Ultramicrotome and then the front surface was polished using a Jeol JIB-19630 Broad Ion Beam Mill.

Optical micrographs

Micrographs were taken with either an Olympus BX60 microscope with a yellow light filter (to reduce polymerization of unflushed devices) or a Keyence VHX-7000 Digital microscope.

Pump design

Our pump design (Fig. 4) is implemented in a MOE-created region and consists of 2 valves and a displacement chamber (DC), each of which is comprised of two stacked cylindrical voids (negative features) separated by a 15 µm thick membrane16,18,19. The valves and DC are each 456 µm (30 pixels) in diameter with lower and upper cylindrical chamber heights of 60 µm (4 layers) and 195 µm (13 layers), respectively. The average pump flow rate was determined by measuring the distance a fluid traveled in a serpentine channel of known volume for a given time during which the pump was actuated. All tests were performed with a valve and DC actuation pressure of 40 psi and unless otherwise noted water (viscosity 1.0 cP) was used as the working fluid. It should be noted that different valve geometries may require different pressures to actuate. A variety of valve and pump timings were tested to find the ideal conditions for our designed pump. These are detailed in the supplemental information Figure S33.

CFD simulation

Mixer devices were designed in OpenSCAD32 and exported as STL files. Meshing of the designs was performed with CFMesh+ (Creative Fields, Ltd). Computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simulations were run with OpenFOAM35 and visualized in ParaView36. The velocity simulation used the icoFoam solver with a timestep of 100 ns and water kinetic viscosity, ν, equal to 1.0 × 10–6 m2/s. Both inlets had a constant uniform flow rate of 0.5 µL/min. All walls had a standard noSlip boundary condition. The diffusion simulation was done using the scalarTransportFoam solver and the icoFoam-generated flow velocity field with a timestep of 30 µs. The two fluid inlets had a uniform normalized analyte concentration of 1 or 0, respectively. The diffusion constant, DT, was 4.2 × 10–10 m2/s to model the diffusion of fluorescein.

Fluorescence imaging and analysis

Experimental mixer characterization was done with 100 µM fluorescein in a TRIS buffer solution as input A and water as input B. Imaging was done using an Olympus IX71 inverted microscope with a TRITC filter set. A device was first filled with fluorescein solution and a set of 200 flat field images were collected over 10 s. Syringe pumps or on-chip pumps were then run until the device reached steady state. A new set of images were collected with the same exposure time and interval. A calibration curve was also collected by imaging a series of fluorescein concentrations and fitting a curve between the measured values and known concentrations, the results of which are shown in Figure S34. Images were then post-processed by performing a flat-field correction and applying the fluorescence calibration.

Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a multi-resolution 3D printing technique that achieves true multi-resolution fabrication in both XY and Z dimensions, significantly advancing the capabilities of DLP-SLA for microfluidic device fabrication. By integrating a Very High Resolution Optical Engine (VHROE) with a 0.75 µm pixel pitch and a 365 nm LED, alongside a Main Optical Engine (MOE) with a 15 µm pixel pitch and a 405 nm LED, we have demonstrated a 20:1 XY resolution ratio and a 10:1 Z resolution ratio through tailored resin formulation with dual UV absorbers. This approach enables the fabrication of ultra-fine negative features, such as enclosed channels with cross-sections as small as 1.9 µm × 2.0 µm, a triply-periodic minimal surface with 7 µm pores fully embedded in a microfluidic channel, and an ultra-compact microfluidic mixer with a printed volume of just 0.017 mm³ or 17 nL. These results highlight the versatility of our method in producing complex, high-resolution microfluidic structures. By balancing speed and resolution through selective deployment of the VHROE and MOE, our technique overcomes the traditional trade-offs between resolution and print area, as well as the limitations of single-resolution Z-axis printing. This multi-resolution DLP-SLA system opens new possibilities for rapid prototyping and production of advanced microfluidic devices, with potential applications in biomedical diagnostics, chemical synthesis, separations, and lab-on-a-chip technologies. Future work will focus on further optimizing resin formulations, developing integrated liquid chromatography columns, and exploring additional active microfluidic components to enhance device functionality.
