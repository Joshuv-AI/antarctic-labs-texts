A systematic comparison of methodologies for the estimation of the serial interval
Authors: Lanlan Yu, Yunyi Cai, Gui-Quan Sun, Jiancheng Lv, Quan-Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Serial interval (SI) is a crucial indicator for characterizing the course of epidemic transmission and provides guidance in designing the intervention strategy for preventing epidemic spreading. Many approaches have been developed to estimate the serial intervals, while their effectiveness evaluations are rarely exhibited due to the unavailable of the real SI values. Here, we developed the data-driven based transmission model to simulate the “realistic” epidemics and kept the entire micro-transmission chain to obtain the real SI values for comparison. We firstly found the direct statistic of serial interval obtained from the infector-infectee pairs within households can serve as an accurate representative of the entire population. The households monitored during the early and peak phases of an epidemic provide precise estimations, while the serial interval would be underestimated if the households are collected during the decreasing phase of epidemic. This study reveals that both the ML method using the contact tracing data and the BY method imputing with the household follow up data outperform that the TM method which employing the aggregated data during the early and decreasing phases of epidemic, while the TM method provides a more precise estimation of mean value of SI during the peak phase of epidemic. Our study also presents that sampling 100 households would be enough to give an accurate estimation of SI value. Other sensitiveness on both the epidemic transmission parameters and the noise in the date of symptom onset demonstrate the robustness of the above results. This study provides a systematic comparison of SI estimation methods and gives the public health authority the evidence when they are using the relevant SI estimating methods to inform the policy.

Introduction

The serial interval (SI), defined as the difference between the time of symptom onset of secondary case and of their primary infector (Ali et al., 2020; Nishiura et al., 2020; Tuite et al., 2010; Vink et al., 2014), is a crucial indicator for understanding the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases (Dhiman et al., 2024; S. W. Park et al., 2024). SI is instrumental to guide public health response efforts, including the design of contact tracing guidelines, a time window for monitoring symptoms in contacts of identified cases (Paquet et al., 2006), and an indicator for evaluating the effectiveness of control interventions (Ali et al., 2020, WHO Ebola Response Team et al., 2014). SI also plays a key role in understanding transmission patterns (Nash et al., 2023). However, estimating SI can be challenging as the infector-infectee pair (i.e., who infects whom) can rarely be observed directly and is usually based on the mere knowledge of the presence of an epidemiological link. Moreover, SI vary across contexts due to differences in implemented public health interventions, human behavior, and social mixing patterns, thus differences in SI can be observed also during different phases on an epidemic (Madewell et al., 2023; M. Park et al., 2020; Rai et al., 2021; Vink et al., 2014). Given the importance of SI and the challenges in estimating its distribution, a set of approaches have been introduced to estimate SI from empirical epidemiological data (Chen et al., 2022; Forsberg White & Pagano, 2008; S. W. Park et al., 2021; te Beest et al., 2013; Vink et al., 2014).

Different approaches for the estimation of SI have been proposed, each one relying on a set of assumption and type of available data. When contact tracing data is available, statistical methods have been developed to identify possible infector-infectee pairs and estimating the distribution of SI by exploring the likelihood of the reconstructed transmission pairs (Chen et al., 2022; Du et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; S. W. Park et al., 2021; te Beest et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2020). Lessler et al. (Lessler et al., 2009) developed a systematic approach to identify transmission pairs and estimated the SI distribution by fitting parametric models, such as the Weibull and log-normal distributions. Similarly, te Beest et al. (te Beest et al., 2013) applied a maximum posterior probability approach to reconstruct transmission pairs and estimated the SI distribution for influenza H1N1 across different social settings, including households, schools, and workplaces.

For household data, a common approach is to define a case-to-case interval (index-case-cases, ICC) based on the assumption that the first reported case in a household is the index case (Cauchemez et al., 2009; Cowling et al., 2009; Leung et al., 2011; Levy et al., 2013; Orellano et al., 2012; Savage et al., 2011). Distinct from the SI, ICC intervals (index-case-cases) refer to the empirically observed time gap between the first case (index case) and the sequentially reported secondary cases in the same household (Vink et al., 2014), regardless of the specific infector-infectee link. By characterizing the link between ICC intervals and SI of potential infector-infectee pairs, Cauchemez et al. analyzed data from 216 households with an index patient during the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic in the US, estimating a mean SI of 2.6 days (2009). Other studies estimated the mean household SI directly from or by fitting distribution to ICC intervals (Cauchemez et al., 2009; Levy et al., 2013; Susvitasari et al., 2023; Vink et al., 2014). Vink et al. (Vink et al., 2014) accounted for various transmission pathways within households, such as two cases sharing the same coprimary source and allowing for negative serial intervals (i.e., the infector has symptom onset after that of the infectee), using a probability model to estimate the SI distribution for influenza. Susvitasari et al. (Susvitasari et al., 2023) extended the ICC interval concept to adjust for partially sampled data in larger social settings beyond households.

When only aggregated data is available, White et al. developed a maximum likelihood method to simultaneously estimate the reproduction number and SI distribution, which they applied to influenza data (Forsberg White & Pagano, 2008; White et al., 2009). Similarly, Archer et al. imputed missing data from the first wave of the influenza A (H1N1)pdm09 pandemic in South Africa and estimated its SI (Archer et al., 2012). Building on these statistical frameworks, Dai et al. (Dai et al., 2023) developed a likelihood-based approach to jointly estimate the instantaneous reproduction number R(t) and SI distribution.

The aim of this study is to carry out a systematic comparison of several widely adopted methodologies for the estimation of SI (Dai et al., 2023; te Beest et al., 2013; Vink et al., 2014) in a controlled setting for which the ground truth is known. Specifically, we developed an individual based model of influenza transmission calibrated that has been validated against empirical epidemiological and seroepidemiological data that keeps track of the entire transmission chain. The model was used to create synthetic patient line lists, including information on patients’ household contacts, which served as input for the four approaches to estimate SI. Performance of the methodologies were assessed against the ground truth coming from the simulation for different levels of data completeness and accuracy, epidemic phases, pathogen transmissibility, risk of developing symptoms upon infection, and transmissibility of asymptomatic individuals relative to symptomatic ones.

Methods

Pathogen transmission model and synthetic dataset

Multilayer network

We used a synthetic population of about 500,000 individuals representing a subsample of the Italian population taken from our previous studies (Liu et al., 2018, 2022). Everyone in the synthetic population is assigned to a household and to a general community. Moreover, according to employment rates by age, individuals are assigned to workplaces, while students are assigned to schools according to school enrollment rates by age. This synthetic population allows us to define a multilayer network of contacts (see Fig. 1A) consisting of the household (H), school (S), workplace (W), and community (C) layers, which represent the main settings for influenza transmission (Ferguson et al., 2005; Merler & Ajelli, 2010). Influenza transmission model

We developed a stochastic agent-based model of influenza transmission on the multiplex network. Influenza transmission is simulated according to an SLIR (susceptible, latent, infectious, removed) scheme (Lee et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022), where infectious individuals were further divided into pre-symptomatic (Ip), symptomatic (Is), and asymptomatic (Ia) individuals (Fig. 1B). If a susceptible individual i is connected on the multilayer network with an infectious (either pre-symptomatic, symptomatic, or asymptomatic) individual j, susceptible individual i will be infected by infectious individual j with a layer-specific probability. The infectiousness of an infected individual follows a temporal trajectory following a gamma function κ(τ) (Ferguson et al., 2005). When symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals are no longer infectious, they acquire immunity that lasts for the entire duration of the simulated outbreak.

The influenza transmission probability between a pair of infectious and susceptible individuals accounts for four factors: age-specific susceptible to infection (Fraser et al., 2009), layer-specific per-contact transmission risk (Liu et al., 2018), the epidemiological status of the infectious individual (Ip, Is, or Ia), and the time elapsed since infection (i.e., temporal infectiousness profile) (Ferguson et al., 2005). The incubation period is assumed to follow a gamma distribution with mean equal to 1.6 days (shape = 2.62, scale = 0.42 and shifted by 0.5) truncated at 10 days (Ferguson et al., 2005). The probability of developing symptom is assumed to be 0.5 (Ferguson et al., 2005; Merler & Ajelli, 2010). We calibrated the weights of the contacts in each layer such that the fraction of infections associated with household, school, workplace, and community contacts is 30%, 18%, 19% and 33%, respectively, as reported in influenza literature (Ferguson et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2018; Merler & Ajelli, 2010). Further, we estimated the age-specific susceptibility to infection by calibrating the model to match the seropositive rates by age at the end of the 2009 H1N1 pandemic in Italy (Merler et al., 2013). Details on model parameters and the calibration procedure are reported in the Supplementary Material.

Generated synthetic line lists

Our model produces a patient line list comprising information on the date of symptom onset, primary infector, list of household contacts for each synthetic agent. Then, we sample n households (n = 100 in the baseline analysis) with at least 2 members. Using these households, we compile a dataset containing all the members of these households with the ID of the synthetic individual, the ID of the household, and the date of symptom onset or NA if that individual was not infected or did not show symptoms.

Evaluation metrics and analyzed scenarios

To systematically compare the methodologies for the estimations of the serial interval, we compared the estimated serial intervals with the ground truth using the following four metrics:1)The difference of mean SI (Dmean) between the SI distribution estimated by the method and the ground truth distribution.2)The maximum distance (DKS) between the cumulative SI distributions estimated by the method and the ground truth distribution, as measured using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.3)The Total Variation Distance (DTVD) between the SI distribution estimated by the method and the ground truth distribution.4)The Jensen-Shannon Divergence (DJSD) between the SI distribution estimated by the method and the ground truth distribution.

According to the definition of the four metrics above, smaller values indicate the better performance of the estimating methods. Details on these metrics are provided in the Supplementary Material.

To explore the performance of the four analyzed SI estimation methods, we considered three phases of an epidemic outbreak: the early phase, peak phase, and decreasing phase (see Fig. 1C). Details are exhibited in the Supplementary Material. For the early phase we sample the first n infected households with at least 2 members and one of them is the symptomatic primary infector; for the peak phase, we sample n infected households during the epidemic peak; and for the decreasing phase, we sample n infected households from the population 2 weeks late after the epidemic peak. In the baseline analysis, we set the basic reproduction number R0=1.5, the probability of individual developing symptom p=0.5, the relative infectiousness of the asymptomatic individual q=1.0, the number of sampled households n=100, and the no-noise in onset time of symptomatic case. In the meanwhile, we explored R0={1.3,1.5,1.7}, p={0.3,0.5,0.7}, q={0.3,0.57,0.7,1.0}, n={50,100,200,400} and the bias onset time of cases (gauss noise with μ=0,σ2=1) as the sensitivity analyses.

Results

In the experiment, we simulated influenza transmission starting with one seed for 1000 simulations. All the following results are averaged or evaluated by using these simulations of epidemic outbreak.

Statistical analysis

The daily effective reproduction number R(t) is defined as the average number of secondary cases generated by an infectious individual at time t (Liu et al., 2018), which is an indicator as important as the serial interval (SI) in understanding and characterizing the evolution of the emerging infectious disease. We firstly reported R(t) and the time evolution of serial interval in Fig. 2A and B, which are directly computed from the micro-simulations by keeping track of the number of secondary infections generated by each individual infected at time t. We found that R(t) increases with time in the early phase of the epidemic, starting from R0index = 1.5 to the peak of about 1.7, which is similar to the pattern as observed in the data-driven model of epidemic in (Liu et al., 2018). While the average of serial interval decreases over the whole time, this differs from the pattern observed in reference (Liu et al., 2018) which reaches the shortest right before the peak time. Evaluation of SI estimating methods

We found that the TM, BY, ML, and MI methods overestimated the serial interval (SI) during the early phase of the epidemic, as shown in Fig. 3A. Among these methods, the MI, ML, and BY approaches estimated the SI mean values closer to the ground truth compared to the TM method, which is further supported by their evaluation across other metrics (i.e., DTVD, DJSD, and DKS), as illustrated in Fig. 3D. Similar to the early phase, all the four methods overestimated the SI mean during the peak phase of the epidemic. While the TM method achieved the most accurate SI mean value in this phase (Fig. 3B), the MI, ML, and BY methods outperformed the TM method in terms of the other three evaluation metrics, as shown in Fig. 3E. In the decreasing phase of the epidemic, the MI, ML, and BY methods provided the most accurate SI estimates, as presented in Fig. 3C and F. Sensitiveness on the number of monitored households

Determining the minimum number of monitored households to achieve an accurate estimation of the serial interval (SI) is crucial for epidemic surveillance. As shown in Fig. 4, the impact of number of households on the SI estimating methods is evaluated in terms of four metrics under three monitoring scenarios. We observed that with the increase of the number of monitored households during the early phase of epidemic, the performance of all these four SI estimating methods is improved, as shown in Fig. 4A, D, 4G, and 4J. To be noted that, the performances of the MI, ML, and BY methods almost stabilized when the number of households reached or exceeded 100. In contrast, the TM method showed gradual improvement as the sample size increased, except for the peak phase (presented in Fig. S5 of the Supplementary Material). Sensitiveness on the parameters of epidemic transmission

In this section, we provided the systematic comparisons and evaluations for the robustness of the SI estimating methods under a variety of sensitivities experiment, by varying the parameters related to influenza transmission and symptom onset timing.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, we found increasing the basic reproduction number (R0) improves the performance of TM method in the early phase but weakens its performance in the peak phase through all the four metrics. In the meanwhile, the performance of MI method is lifted during the early and peak phases of epidemic in terms of the metrics DKS, DTVD and DJSD. Increasing the probability of developing symptoms (p) improves the estimation accuracy consistently for all four SI estimating methods under three monitoring scenarios in terms of four metrics. Conversely, increasing the relative infectiousness of the asymptomatic individual (q) diminishes the effectiveness of SI estimating methods. These results could be explained that the former would increase the number of available infector-infectee pairs for the SI estimation, while the latter would decrease such number. We found incorporating the noise to the time of symptom onset could decrease the effectiveness of all the SI estimating methods. Discussion

Serial interval is an important indicator for characterizing the speed of epidemic spread and providing the guidelines for intervention strategy designing. Although there are many studies have developed the approaches to estimate the serial interval, the effectiveness evaluation of these estimating methods is lack due to the absence of real SI information, the various factors in epidemic transmission, and the differences in household-based surveillance methodologies (Kenah et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2018; Madewell et al., 2023; M. Park et al., 2020; Rai et al., 2021).

In this paper, we developed the data-driven based transmission model to simulate the influenza epidemics and kept the entire micro-transmission chain to enable ground-truth SI calculation for comparison. Through comprehensive literature review, we implemented three established SI estimating methods along with an assumption-free approach to carry out a systematic methodological comparison. Meanwhile, we designed three kinds of monitoring scenarios, listed as the early, peak and decreasing phases of epidemic to collect data and evaluated the SI estimating methods in terms of the mean difference, the maximum distance, the total variation distance and the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the estimated SI distribution and the distribution of ground truth.

Our study presented that SI estimation approach performs better when more detailed epidemic data is available. When only aggregated data is available, the SI estimating method (i.e., TM) assuming the observed number of symptomatic cases at each day follows a Poisson, and thus establishes the relationship between this number with the serial interval distribution and the reproduction number characterized by equation (1) (He et al., 2020). This method would not be able to capture the transmission caused by the pre-symptomatic individuals, and also exhibits the bias arising from the assumption of constant infectiousness of the infected individual (Archer et al., 2012; Dai et al., 2023; Forsberg White & Pagano, 2008; White et al., 2009). These thus lead to the TM method (Dai et al., 2023) underperforming MI method in the simulated epidemic data. In comparison, when individual onset times are available, both SI estimation approaches, whether based on assumptions or not, would perform better. These methods (i.e., MI, ML and BY) are with the similar performance in terms of the estimation for the mean SI. Therein, assumption-based approaches (i.e., ML and BY) outperform method without assumption (i.e., MI). In specific, the BY method (Vink et al., 2014), based on the household follow-up data, regards the first symptomatic case in the household as the only source of infections (Cauchemez et al., 2009; Cowling et al., 2009; Levy et al., 2013; Savage et al., 2011), and develops the probability model accounting for various transmission pathways within households to estimate the SI distribution. This method presents to be effective and robust during various monitoring scenarios. While the method (i.e., ML) utilizes the maximum likelihood to reconstruct the transmission pairs (Du et al., 2020; te Beest et al., 2013), rather than assigning transmission pairs (such as BY and MI). Therefore, ML commonly performs similarly to BY method (with adaptive mixture model about transmission pathways) but better than MI method (without assumption about reasonable transmission pairs). Furthermore, when contact tracing data is available and accurate, estimation approach, according to the entire transmission chain within household, can calculate the “realistic” SI value in the early phase of epidemic.

The sensitivity analysis of the SI estimating methods covers the parameters from the influenza transmission model, the noise in symptom onset timing and the size of monitored households. We found varying the parameters would increase the number of the possible infector-infectee pairs, which could improve the estimation of SI. This also happens to enlarge the size of monitored households. Our results also reveal that monitoring 100 households would be enough to achieve robust SI estimations for influenza transmission, though this threshold remains sensitive to the density of possible infector-infectee pairs in data-sparse environments. Notably, all these results are robust to the noise in the symptom onset timing.

Although this study provides a systematic comparison of methodologies for the estimation of the serial interval. There still exhibits the following limitations: 1) In this study, we evaluated the SI estimating methods based on the simulated epidemic data by simulating the “realistic” spreading of influenza. Our data-driven model was carefully calibrated with empirical surveillance data, while it remains an approximation of actual disease spread. It should be cautious when interpreting them to inform the policy. 2) The effectiveness evaluations of these SI estimating methods are limited to the influenza like disease. Future work should explore how these results generalize to other infectious diseases or varying strains of respiratory pathogens, which may exhibit distinct transmission profiles (Xiong et al., 2024). 3) Although, we classified the SI estimating methods into three categories based on the data used and the potential assumptions made, other approaches, such as those based on machine learning, were not included. 4) Our model assumes a relatively homogeneous contact pattern within main social settings (households, school, workplace and community) and does not account for potential superspreading events or age-specific susceptibility, which could influence the observed variance of the serial interval. In addition, the designed monitoring scenarios for data collection are also worth to be investigated further, such that we can be actively monitor by testing the sample population, instead of symptom-based household monitoring, etc. These aspects can be particularly relevant for real surveillance.

In conclusion, we carried out a systematic comparison of the widely adopted methodologies for the estimation of SI in a controlled setting for which the ground truth is known. Our results presented that the SI estimating methods employing the contact tracing data or household follow up data outperform the methods only using the aggregated data. The additional sensitive results show that the parameters regulating the epidemic transmission increasing the number of available infector-infectee pairs would improve the effectiveness of SI estimating methods. Our findings highlight the importance of including more available information related to the monitoring data for the design of SI estimating methods.
