New Murina (Chiroptera: Vespertilionidae) species and the first Murina annamitica record in China
Authors: Xin Mou, Yishun Qian, Chunping Ke, Lihong Bai, Laniang Li, Song Li, Loren Ammerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article identifies and describes a new species of bat, Murina huanglianensis S. Li & Mou, sp. nov., from Yunnan, China, and reports a new country record of the species Murina annamitica for China, based on morphological and mitochondrial DNA phylogenetic relationships. This research expands understanding of species diversity of the genus Murina in China and provides new information for the biogeographic study of Chiroptera.Keywords: China, morphology, Murina, new species, phylogeny, taxonomy

The genus Murina Gray, 1842 (tube-nosed bats) is currently the second-largest genus in the chiropteran family Vespertilionidae (ASM 2025), characterized by distinctive tubular nostrils, dense pelage, and arboreal habits. Simmons (2005) determined that the genus Murina contained 17 species, and its members have more than doubled over the past 2 decades (Csorba and Bates 2005; Csorba et al. 2007, 2011; Kruskop and Eger 2008; Furey et al. 2009; Kuo et al. 2009; Eger and Lim 2011; Francis and Eger 2012; Ruedi et al. 2012; Soisook et al. 2013a, 2013b, 2017; He et al. 2015; Son et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2017; Zeng et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2020; Mou et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2025a, 2025b, 2025c). Currently, 46 species are recognized globally (ASM 2025), and 23 species are recorded in China (Wei et al. 2025). Based on whether maxillary toothrows are distinctly convergent anteriorly, the relative position of the upper incisors, and the ratio of upper canine and upper posterior premolar, Corbet and Hill (1992) divided Murina into 2 groups, commonly referred to the “suilla-group” and the “cyclotis-group.” However, Son et al. (2015) avoided the term “group,” noting that these morphological groups do not represent separate phylogenetic lineages, and referred to them as the “suilla-type” and “cyclotis-type.” Here, we follow Son et al. (2015) and use the terms “suilla-morphotype” and “cyclotis-morphotype” to more accurately reflect their morphological significance. The dental formula follows the conclusion of Bates and Harrison (1997).

The Hengduan Mountains in southwestern China, a global biodiversity hotspot (Boufford et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2018), provide ideal conditions for speciation due to their varied topography and steep vertical climatic gradients (He and Jiang 2014; He et al. 2015; Zeng et al. 2018). Huanglianshan is located in a transitional zone between the southwestern Chinese mountains and the northern Vietnamese highlands. It constitutes an important part of the southern Ailaoshan, which represents the southern outliers of the Hengduan Mountains, and it features a substantial altitudinal range (320-2,637 m), a distinct seasonal climate with pronounced dry and wet seasons, and a variety of vegetation types spanning from alpine zones to dry-hot valleys. As a key corridor between the Indochina Peninsula and southwest China’s biodiversity hotspot, it is an important location to study species diversity. However, prior biodiversity surveys in this region have been limited, with the most comprehensive documentation dating back to the publication Yunnan Luchun Huanglianshan Nature Reserve in 2003, which contained little data on chiropterans (Xu 2003).

During an examination of specimens in collections, 5 specimens with tubular nostrils were identified at the Kunming Natural History Museum of Zoology, Kunming Institute of Zoology (KIZ), Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS): KIZ20190220 and KIZ20190239 from Mengla, Yunnan, China; and KIZ20231193, KIZ20240188 and KIZ20240189 from Luchun, Yunnan, China. These specimens were morphologically distinct from all known Murina species. Integrative morphological and molecular analyses confirmed that these specimens represent a previously undescribed species within the genus. Here, we provide a formal description of this novel taxon, elucidating its diagnostic characters and phylogenetic position. In addition, the specimen KIZ20190169 from Mengla, Yunnan, China, was different from all known Murina species in China, but similar to M. annamitica distributed in Laos and Vietnam. After morphological and phylogenetic analyses, we documented the first record of M. annamitica in China.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All specimens were obtained following the American Society of Mammalogists guidelines and the laws and regulations of China concerning the protection of wild terrestrial animals (State Council Decree 1992; Sikes et al. 2011). Field collecting protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kunming Institute of Zoology, CAS (no specific permit number).

Sample collection

Five adult specimens (2 males and 3 females) of a putative new species and 1 adult female specimen of a species previously unrecorded in China were examined. These specimens were collected using mist nets between 2019 and 2024 from 2 localities in Yunnan Province, China: Huanglianshan National Nature Reserve, Luchun, Honghe (22.91°N, 102.21°E, 1,883 m; specimens KIZ20231193, KIZ20240188, and KIZ20240189) and Mengla, Xishuangbanna (21.54°N, 101.50°E, 1,024 m, specimens KIZ20190220 and KIZ20190239; and 21.51°N, 101.51°E, 887 m, specimen KIZ20190169). Voucher specimens are deposited in the Kunming Natural History Museum of Zoology, KIZ, CAS under the following field collection numbers: KIZ20190220, KIZ20190239, KIZ20231193, KIZ20240188, KIZ20240189, and KIZ20190169 (the corresponding museum catalogue numbers are KIZ026084, 026085, 026086, 026087, 026088, and 026089, respectively). All novel sequences have been submitted to the NCBI GenBank database with accession numbers PX734038 to PX734042 and PX723521 to PX723523.

Comparative material

Closely related species (M. balaensis, M. eleryi, M. gracilis, and M. recondita) of the putative new species were described and well-documented within the last 2 decades (Furey et al. 2009; Kuo et al. 2009; Soisook et al. 2013a). Because their type specimens are not preserved in mainland China, it is difficult to examine them. Therefore, species comparisons were based on the literature of species in the Murina genus (Furey et al. 2009; Kuo et al. 2009; Soisook et al. 2013a), with type specimens serving as the primary reference.

Measurements

External morphometric data were collected following standard chiropteran measurement protocols. Ten linear parameters were recorded with a precision digital caliper (round to the nearest 0.1 mm): head-body length (HB), defined as the distance from the anterior tip of the snout to the posterior margin of the anus; tail length (TL), spanning from the anus to the terminal vertebra of the tail; ear length (E), measured from the ventral base of the external auditory meatus to the apex of the pinna; hindfoot length (HF), representing the maximal span from the calcaneus to the distal phalanx of the longest digit (excluding claws); tibia length (TIB), taken between the knee joint and the talocrural articulation; forearm length (FA), determined from the elbow joint to the carpal fold with wings adducted; and lengths of the second to fifth metacarpals (MET2 to MET5), quantified from the carpus to the distal end of each respective metacarpal bone. Body mass (WT) was assessed using a calibrated electronic scale (±0.1 g).

Cranial and dental measurements were conducted using a digital caliper (±0.01 mm) under stereomicroscope. All craniodental parameters followed standardized definitions from Velazco and Gardner (2012), Chen et al. (2017), and Mou et al. (2025). Measurements include total length of skull (STOTL), greatest length of skull (GTL), condylobasal length (CBL), condylocanine length (CCL), zygomatic width (ZYW), braincase width (BCW), braincase height (BCH), interorbital width (IOW), width of postorbital constriction (POW), mastoid width (MAW), basioccipital width (BOW), upper canine–molar length (CM3L), maxillary toothrow length (IM3L), upper canine–premolar length (CP4L), upper molar length (M1M3L), anterior palatal width (PWC1C1), posterior palatal width (PWM3M3), upper canine width (C1C1W), upper molar greatest width (M3M3W), greatest length of mandible (MDL), mandible length (ML), coronoid process height (CPH), lower canine–molar length (CM3L), mandibular toothrow length (IM3L), lower canine–premolar length (CP4L), and lower molar length (M1M3L).

Molecular analyses

Total genomic DNA was isolated from muscle tissue using the Trelief Hi-Pure Animal Genomic DNA Kit (Tsingke Biotech, Beijing; TSP202-50), employing the manufacturer’s protocols. Mitochondrial cytochrome b (Cytb) and cytochrome oxidase subunit I (COI) genes were amplified and sequenced. Primers Molcit-F (5′-AATGA-CATGAAAAATCACCGTTGT-3′) and CytB-H (5′-CTTTTCTGGTT-TACAAGACCAG-3′) were utilized for Cytb amplification (Ibáñez et al. 2006; Weyeneth et al. 2008). For COI, custom-designed primers COF (5′-TTCTCAACCAACCACAAAGACATTGG-3′) and COR (5′-TAGACTT-CTGGGTGGCCAAAGAATCA-3′) were optimized and used. PCR reactions were performed in 50 μl volumes containing 45 μl Green-Tsingke TSE101 Gold mix, 2 μl of each primer (10 pM), and 1 μl DNA template. Cycling conditions included 94 °C/2 min, followed by 5 cycles of 94 °C/30 s, 50 °C/40 s, and 72 °C/1 min, then 35 cycles of 94 °C/30 s, 55 °C/40 s, and 72 °C/1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C/10 min, and 4 °C for renaturation. Purified amplicons were sequenced bidirectionally on an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (USA) at Tsingke Biotech (Beijing). Sequence files were checked and assembled using SeqMan (Lasergene v7.1, DNASTAR, Madison, Wisconsin, United States).

The sequences obtained in this study were analyzed alongside 29 Cytb and 35 COI reference sequences of Murina species downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 (Zhang et al. 2020). Sequence alignment was performed in MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021) via the ClustalW algorithm (Thompson et al. 1994) with default parameters, resulting in trimmed lengths of 1,140 bp (Cytb) and 657 bp (COI). Genetic distances were calculated using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) model in MEGA11’s distance module (pairwise deletion for ambiguous sites; 1,000 bootstrap replicates). For model selection, ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) identified the best-fit substitution models under the Bayesian information criterion (BIC): TIM2+F + I+G4 for Cytb and TPM2u+I+G4+F for COI. Maximum-likelihood phylogenies were reconstructed under the best substitution model listed above in IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015) with node support assessed through 5,000 ultrafast bootstraps (Minh et al. 2013). The phylogenetic trees were visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) online platform (Letunic and Bork 2024).

Results

Morphological results

Body mass, external measurements, and craniodental dimensions of all new specimens in this study were recorded (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Data SD1). The specimens exhibited external characteristics typical of the genus Murina including: distinct tubular nostrils that are separated from each other and protrude laterally from the muzzle (consistent with the characteristics of the subfamily Vespertilioninae); smaller size (vs. Harpiocephalus); height of upper inner (I2) and outer incisors (I3) less than half the height of upper canines (C1); height of upper anterior premolars (P2) and upper posterior premolars (P4) less than C1; smaller lower anterior premolars (P2) than lower canines(C1); and a small secondary cingular cusp (distinguished from Harpiola; Figs 1 and 2). The specimens KIZ20190220, KIZ20190239, KIZ20231193, KIZ20240188, and KIZ20240189 exhibited similar external and cranial morphology. Maxillary toothrows of these specimens were distinctly convergent anteriorly—I2 anterolateral to I3—with little overlap, making most of I2 visible in lateral view; and the crown area of P2 is less than half that of P4. These characteristics suggest that these specimens belong to the “suilla-morphotype” of Murina and can be distinguished from all “cyclotis-morphotype” species (Fig. 2). In the “suilla-morphotype,” these specimens can be differentiated from the following species based on their FA (28.25 to 29.63 mm) and STOTL (14.20 to 14.67 mm): M. beelzebub, M. bicolor, M. fanjingshanensis, M. feae, M. florium, M. fusca, M. harpioloides, M. hilgendorfi, M. hkakaboraziensis, M. jinchui, M. kontumensis, M. leucogaster, M. milinensis, M. recondita, M. rubella, M. ryukyuana, M. shuipuensis, M. tenebrosa, M. ussuriensis, M. walstoni, M. yadongensis, M. yuanyang, M. yushuensis, and M. philippinensis (Supplementary Data SD2); the overall brown color of the dorsal fur distinguishes them from M. jaintiana; the golden guard hairs are only found on the head, neck, and dorsal margins, clearly differentiating them from M. aurata and M. chrysochaetes—whose dorsal fur is overall golden-brown—and M. suilla, that lacks golden guard hairs; the underdeveloped mesostyle on the first upper molars (M1) and second upper molars (M2) can distinguish them from M. gracilis; the relative size and height of C1, P2, and P4 differentiate them from M. eleryi and the position of the cingular cusp on C1 further distinguishes them from M. balaensis (Figs 1 to 3). The above combination of external and cranial characteristics can distinguish these specimens from all other currently-recognized Murina taxa. Specimen KIZ20190169 had the following characteristics: FA 32.7 mm; GTL 16.71 mm; dorsal and ventral hair bases are dark; both upper premolars similar in height; mesostyle of M1 and M2 well-developed; as well as the talonids of the first lower molars (M1) and second lower molars (M2) well-developed (Table 2; Figs 4 and 5). These characteristics are consistent with the diagnosis of M. annamitica except that the FA and GTL are slightly larger than those of M. annamitica. There is only 1 female specimen of M. annamitica (with a FA of 32.2 mm and a GTL of 16.4 mm; Table 2), similar to specimen KIZ20190169. Combined with other measurements such as CBL, ZYW, MAW, CM3L, C1C1W, and CM3L, the measurements of specimen KIZ20190169 were closest to those of the female specimen of M. annamitica, but slightly larger than those of the male specimen (Table 2). In addition, specimen KIZ20190169 exhibited external and cranial characteristics consistent with the description of M. annamitica provided by Francis and Eger (2012; Figs 4 and 5).

Molecular results

In the Cytb-based phylogenetic tree, the sequences of putative new species (KIZ20190220, KIZ20190239, KIZ20231193, KIZ20240188, and KIZ20240189), M. eleryi, M. recondita, and M. gracilis formed a monophyletic group with 100% ultrafast bootstrap support. All putative new species sequences formed a clade (100% ultrafast bootstrap support), while M. eleryi sequences from 3 geographic populations formed another clade (98% support, including the paratype HNHM.2007.28.2, GenBank accession KT762294). The tree topology (Fig. 6) and the genetic distance based on Cytb suggest that the putative new species is most closely related to M. eleryi (4.3% to 5.3%; Table 3).

Etymology

The name huanglianensis refers to Huanglianshan and the Huanglianshan National Nature Reserve, where the type locality of the species is located. We propose “Huanglianshan Tube-nosed Bat” and “黄连山管鼻蝠” as the vernacular name.

Measurements

Measurements of M. huanglianensis are shown in Supplementary Data SD1.

Diagnosis

A small-sized tube-nosed bat with FA of 28.3 to 29.6 mm and STOTL of 14.20 to 14.67 mm (Table 1). Dorsal pelage dense, overall brown but unevenly distributed, basal gray-black, medial buffish-brown, distal pale to dark brown (Fig. 1A). Golden guard hairs concentrated on head, nape, and dorsal margins, sparse mid-dorsum (Fig. 1A). Ventral pelage grayish-white, slightly brown-yellow, and sparse (Fig. 1B). Sagittal crest and lambdoid crest markedly degenerated and almost invisible (Figs 2A and C). Narial emargination is wide, with vertical length greater than horizontal length (Fig. 2A). Maxillary toothrows distinctly convergent anteriorly, I2 anterolateral to I3, with little overlap, making most of I2 visible in lateral view (Figs 2B and C). C1 slightly higher than P4, P2 lower than half of P4 (Fig. 2C). Cingular cusp of C1 developed and positioned posteriorly (Fig. 2J). Mesostyle of M1 and M2 underdeveloped (Fig. 2H).

Description

Body

Small-sized Murina species, WT 4.2 to 4.8 g, HB 36.0 to 40.1 mm, and FA 28.3 to 29.6 mm. Tubular nostrils opening sideways. Ears 12.6 to 13.3 mm, with a shallow notch at the base of the anterior margin; the upper-middle portion of the anterior margin to the upper posterior margin is arc-shaped, not pointed; the middle portion of the posterior margin is slightly concave medially, then distinctly convex. The inner surface of the ear bears short, shallow transverse ridges; upper half grayish-brown, lower half paler. Tragus height exceeding half but not reaching two-thirds of ear length; anterior tragus margin nearly straight, posterior margin gradually widening from apex, widening markedly at the distal third, and abruptly constricted at the base to form 3 bulges (1 large and 2 small). MET3 (26.9 to 27.5 mm) slightly longer than MET4 (26.0 to 26.9 mm) and MET5 (26.2 to 27.0 mm), and distinctly longer than MET2 (22.2 to 22.6 mm). HB (36.0 to 40.1 mm) is longer than TL (27.6 to 32.9 mm), tail slightly free at the tip and calcar extending in the uropatagium about half of the distance from the foot to the tip of the tail. The plagiopatagium is attached to the distal portion of the outer toe, near (but distinctly not reaching) the base of the claw (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Fur

Dorsal pelage dense, overall brown but unevenly distributed: darkest (dark brown) on nape, slightly paler along dorsal margins, transitioning to pale brown mid-dorsum. Golden guard hairs concentrated on head, nape and dorsal margins, sparse mid-dorsum. Dorsal hairs tricolored: basal gray-black, medial buffish-brown, distal pale to dark brown. Forearm and digits 1 and 2 are covered with dense golden short hairs, shorter on digit 2. Uropatagium sparsely haired overall, slightly denser near caudal vertebrae. Hindfoot dorsum densely covered with golden hairs. Ventral pelage grayish-white, slightly brown-yellow and sparse; basal dark gray to grayish-brown, distal white to grayish-white; marginal areas faintly brown-yellow. Uropatagium with sparse, short grayish-white hairs arranged in a faint V-shape proximally, becoming indistinct distally. Inner pinna surface with conspicuous pores and short, sparse white hairs (Fig. 1).

Skull

Small-sized (STOTL 14.20 to 14.67 mm; GTL 14.51 to 14.87 mm; CCL 12.62 to 12.90 mm). In dorsal view, vertical length of narial emargination is greater than its horizontal length (Fig. 2A). Rostrum not inflated, with a pronounced midline depression extending to interorbital region. Braincase nearly hemispherical; zygomatic arches slender, widest posteriorly (Fig. 2A). Sagittal and lambdoid crests degraded, nearly invisible (Figs. 2A and C). In lateral view, rostrum relatively short; height and slope gradually increase from snout to frontal end, forming slight concavity; height increases while slope decreases from frontal to parietal, rendering frontal prominence moderately distinct (Fig. 2C). In ventral view, palatal emargination deep, with anterior border terminating at middle of C1; palatal tapering toward C1 (PWC1C1/PWM3M3 = 0.61 to 0.66); teardrop-shaped depressions lateral to basioccipital (Fig. 2B). Mandible (MDL 9.10 to 9.47 mm): Notches between angle and mandibular body, and between condyle and angle, pronounced (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Dentition

Dental formula: I-23123C11P-2-4-2-4M123123=34. Upper dentition: Upper tooth row markedly convergent anteriorly, with a C1C1W/M3M3W ratio of 0.67 to 0.68. CM3L = 4.66 to 4.89 mm, IM3L = 5.24 to 5.69 mm. I2and I3 contact anteromedially, positioning I2 anterolateral to I3, with most of I2 visible in lateral view; I2 bears a posterior secondary cusp lower than the main cusp, while I3 exhibits a lingual accessory cuspule; I2 and I3 are subequal in height, but crown area of I2 is less than half that of I3; distinct diastema between I3 and C1. C1 unicuspid, slender and slightly curved, P2 short and narrow, P4 broader; height of C1 slightly exceeds P4, while P2 less than half of P4; crown area of C1 greater than P2 and approximately two-thirds of P4, whereas P2 roughly half of P4 or smaller; C1 cingulum robust and labial width notably greater than lingual width, while P2 compressed (width exceeding length) and lingual width noticeably greater than its labial width, resulting in a combined shape resembling a parallelogram formed by 2 trapezoids. In M1 and M2, paracrista and metacrista form a distinct W-shaped pattern. Mesostyle is underdeveloped; parastyle, mesostyle, and metastyle are not aligned. Area of antero-external valley is smaller than postero-external valley, antero-external valley of M1 being about half postero-external valley, while in M2 it is about two-thirds. The metacone of M1 is markedly higher than the paracone, whereas in M2, the metacone is only slightly higher. Both M1 and M2 have a prominent protocone but no discernible hypocone. Third upper molar (M3) markedly reduced, lacking postero-external valley, metacone, and metastyle. Lower dentition: ML = 9.02 to 9.35 mm, CM3L = 5.15 to 5.34 mm, IM3L = 5.82 to 5.91 mm. First, second, and third lower incisors (I1, I2, I3) tricuspid and slightly overlap; with I1 and I2 medially contacting I2 and I3 laterally; and I3 medially contacting the cingular cusp on the anteromedial edge of C1. Height gradually increases from I1 to C1, with C1 being markedly taller than P2 and lower posterior premolars (P4), and P4 exceeding P2 in height. Crown area of C1 is markedly larger than that of P4, while P2 has less than half the crown area of P4. In M1 and M2, talonid height is approximately two-thirds that of the trigonid, although their crown areas are subequal; cusps exhibit a height gradient: protoconid > hypoconid > paraconid > metaconid > entoconid; all paracristids, protocristids, oblique cristids and posteristids not smooth curves, but have obvious folds; these molars nyctalodonty type, characterized by a posteristid connecting hypoconid to hypoconulid. Third lower molars (M3) retain a well-developed trigonid but show a reduced talonid (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Comparisons

Murina huanglianensis is clearly distinguishable from all species of the “cyclotis-morphotype” by: distinct anterior convergence of the upper toothrow; I2 positioned anterolaterally relative to I3, visible in lateral view; crown area of C1 exceeding that of P2 and approximating two-thirds of P4; and crown area of P2 less than or equal to half of P4 (Fig. 2). Within the “suilla-morphotype,” M. huanglianensis can be readily differentiated from M. beelzebub, M. bicolor, M. chrysochaetes, M. fanjingshanensis, M. feae, M. florium, M. fusca, M. harpioloides, M. hilgendorfi, M. hkakaboraziensis, M. jaintiana, M. jinchui, M. kontumensis, M. leucogaster, M. milinensis, M. philippinensis, M. ryukyuana, M. recondita, M. suilla, M. tenebrosa, M. tubinaris, M. ussuriensis, M. walstoni, M. yadongensis, M. yuanyang, and M. yushuensis based on pelage coloration and body size (Supplementary Data SD2). For species with overlapping morphological measurements (e.g., FA, GTL, STOTL, CBL, and ML) and similar pelage coloration including M. aurata, M. balaensis, M. eleryi, M. gracilis, and M. recondita (with M. balaensis and M. eleryi showing the closest resemblance to M. huanglianensis), the new species can be distinguished by the diagnostic traits described below.

Comparison with M. eleryi

Cranially, M. eleryi lacks a sagittal crest, and its lambdoid crests, though reduced, remain discernible. In contrast, M. huanglianenesis exhibits severely reduced lambdoid crests that are nearly imperceptible. The narial emargination of M. eleryi is approximately equal in length and width, whereas in M. huanglianensis the emargination is broader. Dentally, P2 of M. eleryi is compressed, appearing oval-shaped with a width greater than its length. In contrast, the P2 of M. huanglianensis is more labiolingually compressed, exhibiting an irregular shape characterized by a narrower labial side and a broader lingual side. The height of P2 in M. eleryi ranges between half and two-thirds that of P4, whereas in M. huanglianensis, the height of P2 is distinctly less than half that of P4. The metacone of M1 and M2 in M. eleryi is slightly higher than the paracone. In M. huanglianensis, the metacone of M1 is markedly higher than the paracone, while in M2, the metacone is only marginally elevated relative to the paracone. Additionally, in M. eleryi, the basal area of C1 is subequal to or slightly larger than that of P4, and the talonids of M1 and M2 distinctly exceed the crown area of their corresponding trigonids, with the entoconid markedly higher than the hypoconid. In contrast, M. huanglianensis exhibits a C1 with a basal area markedly larger than that of P4, the talonids of M1 and M2 are either subequal to or smaller than the trigonids in crown area, and the entoconid is markedly lower than the hypoconid (Figs 2 and 3).

Comparison with M. balaensis

Based on the description and specimen photographs provided by Soisook et al. (2013a) , M. balaensis differs from M. huanglianensis in the following characters: The tragus of M. balaensis measures less than half the ear height, whereas that of M. huanglianensis distinctly exceeds half the ear height. The dorsal pelage of M. balaensis exhibits orange-reddish brown tips with bright shiny silver-golden guard hairs distributed on the head, back, base of the uropatagium, and dorsal side of the foot. In contrast, M. huanglianensis displays a brownish dorsum lacking orange-reddish tones, and the mid-dorsal region lacks golden guard hairs (though such hairs are present on the head, neck, surrounding dorsal areas, hind feet, and proximal part of uropatagium). Ventral fur differs slightly: M. balaensis has grayish-white underparts, while M. huanglianensis displays ventral fur tinged with pale yellowish-brown. The palatal depression of M. balaensis is shallow, whereas M. huanglianensis possesses a deeper palatal depression. The narial emargination differs in depth, being deeper in M. balaensis. The C1C1W/M3M3W ratio ranges from 0.67 to 0.68 in M. huanglianensis, compared to 0.70 and 0.71 in M. balaensis. In M. huanglianensis, C1 is markedly taller than P4, with both C1 and P2 appearing slender in lateral view; P2 height is less than half that of C1 and P4. In M. balaensis, the C1 is only slightly taller than P4, and both C1 and P2 are robust, with P2 height approximately half that of C1 and P4. Although the cingular cusp on the lingual surface of C1 and P4 is well-developed in both species, its protrusion position differs. The mesostyles of M1 and M2 are strongly developed in M. balaensis, forming distinct labial protrusions, whereas those of M. huanglianensis are moderately developed and less prominent. The mandible length of M. huanglianensis (9.10 to 9.47 mm) is slightly shorter than that of M. balaensis (9.63 mm and 10.44 mm). In M. balaensis, the crown area of C1 is subequal to or slightly larger than P4, and the crown area of P2 is approximately two-thirds that of P4. In M. huanglianensis, the crown area of C1 is markedly larger than P4, while that of P2 is less than half of P4. On M1 of M. balaensis, the entoconid height is two-thirds that of the hypoconid, whereas on M2, these cusps are nearly equal in height. In contrast, the entoconid height of M. huanglianensis is distinctly lower than the hypoconid height on both M1 and M2 (Figs 1 to 3).

Comparisons with M. aurata, M. gracilis, and M. recondita

Murina gracilis and M. recondita exhibit highly similar cranial structures. According to the key for Taiwanese Murina species established by Kuo et al. (2009), both M. gracilis and M. recondita possess well-developed mesostyles on M1 and M2, a feature that distinguishes them from M. huanglianensis. Furthermore, M. huanglianensis can be differentiated from M. recondita by the morphology of its P2 and from M. gracilis by its greater upper canine width (C1C1W: 3.51 to 3.66 mm), which exceeds 3.45 mm. Murina aurata is distinguished from M. huanglianensis by the following traits: heights of both upper and lower canines in M. aurata are markedly reduced, being subequal to or even shorter than those of the posterior premolars (in contrast, canine heights in M. huanglianensis markedly exceed those of the posterior premolars). The crown area of C1 in M. aurata is approximately half that of the P4 (whereas in M. huanglianensis, the crown area of C1 is roughly two-thirds that of P4). The crown area of the C1 in M. aurata does not exceed that of the P4 (Furey et al. 2009; Maeda 1980), whereas in M. huanglianensis, the crown area of C1 is substantially larger than that of P4 (Figs 1 and 2).

Distribution and habitat

The holotype and referred specimens (KIZ20231193 and KIZ20240188) of M. huanglianensis were collected in a mountain hollow (22.91°N, 102.21°E, 1,883 m a.s.l.) within the jurisdiction of the Fuha Management Station of Huanglianshan National Nature Reserve in Yunnan. This small, localized depression in the mountainous terrain likely serves as a potential corridor for bats traversing the range, as evidenced by the co-occurrence of other bat species from the genera Murina (M. cyclotis, M. huttoni, M. leucogaster), Pipistrellus, Miniopterus, Myotis, and Rhinolophus captured at the same site. The paratype and KIZ20190239 were collected in Longlin Village, Mengla County, Yunnan Province, China (21.54°N, 101.50°E, 1024 m). Both habitats are characterized by wet monsoon evergreen broad-leaved forest, with a relatively dense canopy cover and high ambient humidity (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study describes a new species M. huanglianensis and the first record of the species Murina annamitica from China based on morphological and phylogenetic analyses. Based on the morphology of the upper ­dentition, M. huanglianensis can be distinguished from all “cyclotis-morphotype” species. Comparisons with “suilla-morphotype” species revealed that M. huanglianensis differs from most members of this morphotype in body size and pelage coloration. Eger et al. (2025) recently described 6 new species of the genus Murina, with 5 of them belonging to the “cyclotis-morphotype.” The species M. philippinensis (FA: 30.8 to 33.4 mm, STOTL: 15.3 to 16.3 mm), which belongs to the “suilla-morphotype,” is markedly larger in body size compared to M. huanglianensis. Finally, detailed comparisons with morphologically similar congeners confirmed that M. huanglianensis exhibits stable diagnostic characters distinguishing it from all named Murina species. Phylogenetically, the Cytb tree indicated that M. huanglianensis and M. eleryi was a recently diverged sister lineage (94% ultrafast bootstrap support), and they formed a monophyletic clade with M. recondita and M. gracilis with 100% ultrafast bootstrap support. In contrast, the COI tree shows that M. huanglianensis is an independently evolving lineage, forming a monophyletic group alongside M. gracilis, M. recondita, M. eleryi, and M. balaensis with high support. The molecular results showed that M. eleryi, M. balaensis, M. gracilis, and M. recondita were closely related, which is consistent with the high morphological similarity of these species. Although the topological support of COI for relationships among M. huanglianensis, M. gracilis, M. recondita, and M. balaensis is moderate (M. huanglianensis and M. gracilis with M. recondita = 83%, these 3 with M. balaensis = 75%; Fig. 7), branch length differences indicate clear divergence. This incongruence may arise from limited sequence data or incomplete lineage sorting. The NCBI database contains a single COI sequence of M. balaensis, whereas M. eleryi and M. aurata have 7 and 14 sequences, respectively. The phylogenetic tree constructed using these sequences revealed that M. aurata and M. eleryi were intermingled and formed 4 distinct subclades (Supplementary Data SD3). Some taxonomists suggest that some specimens previously referred to as M. aurata should be classified as M. eleryi (Eger and Lim 2011; Francis and Eger 2012). We refer to subclades formed by the sequences named M. aurata and M. eleryi as the M. eleryi complex. Therefore, we selected sequences of the paratype HNHM 2007.28.2 (KT762291) of M. eleryi from Bac Kan, Vietnam (Kuo et al. 2015) to construct the phylogenetic tree. Additionally, as the COI sequence on the NCBI’s GenBank named M. aurata does not include the sequence of its type specimen, we could not verify which sequence belonged to the true M. aurata. Consequently, M. aurata was not included in the constructed phylogenetic tree. Recent molecular phylogenetic studies have revealed a higher proportion of cryptic species within Murina than previously acknowledged by traditional morphology-based taxonomy (Eger and Lim 2011; Nguyen et al. 2015; Zeng et al. 2018). Phylogenetic relationships suggest that differentiation may have occurred within the M. eleryi complex (Supplementary Data SD3 to SD6), and further taxonomic studies are needed.

Genetic distances of less than 2% typically indicate intraspecific variation (Avise 2000; Bradley and Baker 2001; Hebert et al. 2003), while genetic distances of greater than 5% often suggest the presence of unrecognized species (Baker and Bradley 2006). The distances between M. huanglianensis and M. eleryi/M. balaensis exceed 2% (∼5% for both Cytb and COI), suggesting that M. huanglianensis is probably a separate species, which is corroborated by morphological evidence.

Combining mitochondrial and nuclear genes would provide a more robust phylogenetic resolution; however, the construction of a nuclear gene phylogeny was inadequate and poorly representative due to the limited availability of comparative nuclear sequence data for the genus Murina. Therefore, we did not analyze nuclear genes, but recommend that nuclear gene markers be explored for further verification of our results based on mtDNA. Although mtDNA barcoding occasionally underperforms in taxonomic identification due to its maternal inheritance and gene introgression, its relatively comprehensive dataset remains a crucial molecular marker for detecting undescribed species in species complexes (Galtier et al. 2009; Dong et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2025). Combining this molecular evidence with morphological studies confirmed the recognition of M. huanglianensis as a new species.
