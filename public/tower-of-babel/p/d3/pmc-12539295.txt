Protocol to pinpoint oxidized and reduced cysteine residues in brown fat using differential alkylation labeling techniques
Authors: Hein Ko Oo, Cynthia M Galicia-Medina, Takumi Nishiuchi, Hiroaki Takayama, Toshinari Takamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCysteine post-translational modifications are important for protein functions and protein-protein interactions. Here, we present a protocol to detect the reversibly oxidized and reduced cysteine residues of proteins using differential alkylation labeling techniques with liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. We describe steps for tissue sample preparation, differential alkylation, trypsin digestion, and LC-MS analysis. We then detail procedures for protein identification and data analysis. This protocol has potential application in pinpointing the modified cysteine residues in organs and cells in various disease models.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Oo et al.1Subject areas: Sequence analysis, Health Sciences, Proteomics

Before you begin

We recently established a differential alkylation labeling method that enables pinpointing oxidized and reduced cysteine residues in brown adipose tissue1 (Figure 1). Traditionally, several studies have utilized a biotin-maleimide to alkylate the oxidized cysteine residues and enrich them using avidin-family proteins for LC-MS analysis.2,3,4 When combined with tandem mass tags, this approach allows for quantification of the proportion of oxidized cysteine residues. In contrast, our protocol offers a direct method for analyzing both oxidized and reduced cysteine residues without the need for avidin-based enrichment prior to LC-MS analysis. This strategy not only facilitates the detection of cysteine residues modified by oxidative stress but also those affected by reductive stress under specific conditions. Since modifications of cysteine residues can significantly influence protein structure, function, and protein-protein interactions, comprehensive identification of both oxidized and reduced forms is essential for understanding their roles in proteins, metabolic pathways, and physiological processes. The success of this technique relies on both the pH of the buffer solutions used during labeling and the protection of samples from light. Therefore, it is critical to carefully adjust the buffer pH and ensure adequate light protection before beginning the experiment. The following protocol outlines the steps for preparing samples for cysteine redoxome analysis in mouse brown adipose tissue. With optimized conditions, this method can also be extended to other tissues (such as liver, and muscle) and to adherent cells in various disease models.

Innovation

This protocol introduces alternative strategies to simultaneously profile oxidized and reduced cysteine residues across diverse mouse tissues and cell lines under different pathological and stress conditions. By enabling comprehensive detection of both oxidized and reduced states, our methodology expands the characterization of cysteine redox modifications and complements existing approaches to define the cysteine proteome in both physiological and disease contexts. Our protocol provides detailed step-by-step instructions, troubleshooting notes, and highlighted critical steps derived from extensive laboratory experience. It also includes comprehensive data analysis, including an in-house Python script to facilitate data processing.

CRITICAL: All these steps require protection from light to prevent degradation of the biotin-peac5-maleimide, n-ethylmaleimide, and to avoid unwanted side reactions.

Note: N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) completes its reaction kinetics within 15 minutes at neutral pH, and the reaction is irreversible.5,6 Furthermore, its rapid reaction rate reduces the need to use large molar excesses.6 This offers a significant advantage over one of the most common alkylating reagents, the haloalkane iodoacetamide (IAM), which requires a basic pH (above pH 8)7 and incubation times of 4 to 6 h with a large molar excess of IAM (up to 1000 times that of protein cysteine).8 Additionally, the reactions with IAM to form thioethers are often incomplete under these conditions and are not irreversible.9,10

Note: Biotin-PEAC5-maleimide (BPM) has a shorter and less bulky spacer arm, which reduces steric hindrance and makes it a more compact label in comparison to Maleimide-PEG2/11-maleimide. This compactness enhances accessibility to buried cysteine residues.

Pause point: At this point, the peptide samples can be stored at 4°C for less than 24 h or at −80°C for less than one month.

Expected outcomes

A successful experiment is expected to yield around 30,000 peptides per tissue sample, including approximately 1,500 peptides containing cysteine residues and about 600 cysteine-containing proteins from a 100 μg input loaded onto the LC-MS system. In brown adipose tissue samples, we typically detect roughly 500 oxidized cysteine residues and about 1,000 reduced cysteine residues. Please note that these numbers may vary by the characteristics of the samples.

Overview of protein identification and data analysis from raw LC-MS results to pinpoint cysteine redoxome

Limitations

Our protocol, which consists of a double alkylation using both NEM and BPM, enables the identification of not only oxidized cysteine residues but also reduced and unlabeled cysteine residues. This direct detection of both oxidized and reduced cysteines allows us to detect not only oxidative stress but also reductive stress cysteine targets under specific stimuli. However, the number of cysteine residues that can be detected may significantly decrease without avidin enrichment. Previous studies,2,3,4 together with the current method, face a common challenge in accurately quantifying reversibly oxidized cysteine residues with S-persulfidation (S-SH). The NEM can react with the thiol group in S-persulfidated cysteines (S-S-NEM), resulting in their misidentification as reduced cysteines (S-NEM) instead of oxidized cysteines. Future cysteine redoxome profiling should employ selective labeling techniques to ensure accurate identification.
