Digital watermarking for virtual physically unclonable function data concealment and authentication
Authors: Raviha Khan, Hani Saleh, Brahim Mefgouda, Omar Alhussein, Sami Muhaidat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSplit-learning-based Virtual Physically Unclonable Functions (VPUFs) in Internet of Things (IoT) networks remain vulnerable to eavesdropping and replay attacks due to insufficient security mechanisms that balance robustness with computational efficiency. This paper proposes a novel digital watermarking approach to improve the security of Split-Learning-based VPUFs. The suggested framework utilizes deep learning-based approaches to generate a watermark to be embedded in the latent representation of the VPUF response to provide additional security against eavesdropping and replay attacks without incurring significant hardware or computational overhead. Watermark embedding is done by simulating Rayleigh fading through Jake’s Model to get the secret channel information, which is input to an autoencoder to create a strong latent representation. The formed latent watermark is embedded into the latent response of the VPUF. Experimental testing demonstrates that fidelity remains high under test conditions, reliability, and unforgability, confirming that the watermarking process does not compromise the VPUF’s performance. Further, the proposal supports dual-factor authentication through simultaneous verification of the extracted watermark and the retrieved latent response. This research not only enhances the strength and security of the baseline VPUF mechanism but also provides a cost-effective, scalable solution specifically designed for resource-constrained IoT networks.Keywords: Watermarking, Internet of things, Physically unclonable functions, Data concealment, AuthenticationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The emerging digital era is driven by rapid advancements in communication technologies and artificial intelligence (AI), signifying the onset of the Fourth Industrial Revolution. This revolution is characterised by the convergence of cyber-physical systems that integrate human and machine intelligence, alongside the Internet of Things (IoT), which enables seamless connectivity across diverse sectors and industries1. At its core, the IoT facilitates continuous connectivity between servers and devices, enabling autonomous data exchange. However, with growing IoT networks and managing progressively larger amounts of data, security attacks such as Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks, replay attacks, and side-channel eavesdropping are becoming ever more serious bottlenecks2

Deep learning-based approaches offer a practical solution to IoT security in the form of attack detection, imposition of safety controls, or identification of emerging threats based on learned patterns. Its wide availability makes it a scalable and practical solution2. Digital watermarking is a technique that inserts a secret message (Watermark) into data. It is a common method for the protection of confidential information in various domains, including remote sensing, medicine, and military3. The use of deep learning for robust watermarking has attracted significant attention in recent years4,5.

Autoencoders are a class of neural networks designed for unsupervised learning, which learns to compress and reconstruct input data into a compact representation6. They consist of two main parts: an encoder that maps the input data to a lower-dimensional latent space to compress it, and a decoder that attempts to recreate the original input from this compressed version. By minimizing the disparity between the input and its reconstruction while training, autoencoders learn to capture the most important features of the data. They are thus usable when employed for tasks such as dimensionality reduction, image denoising, anomaly detection, and generative modeling. Moreover, these characteristics establish a strong base for their use in digital watermarking, which is well documented in various studies7,8.

Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs) draw upon the inherent physical properties of silicon circuits, including path delays and threshold voltages, to create device-unique cryptographic keys using Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs)9–11. Although they provide a hardware realization for authentication like digital fingerprints, PUFs are limited by their cost, aging, and vulnerability to side-channel and modeling attacks12. To facilitate secure and trustworthy authentication in IoT networks, these vulnerabilities must be addressed, and more robust alternatives or refinements to PUF-based schemes should be investigated.

To this end, we previously introduced a Virtual PUF (VPUF) architecture based on a split learning-enabled encoder-decoder model, serving as a software-based substitute for traditional hardware PUFs13. This architecture was trained and validated using the Cross-Architecture and Device PUF Dataset14, ensuring that the VPUF inherits the randomness and uniqueness of real hardware PUFs. It enhances reliability, scalability, and security while reducing dependence on dedicated hardware, making it particularly suitable for resource-constrained IoT environments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the VPUF employs an encoder-decoder framework that transmits only latent representations between client and server. The system assumes that an adversary would require infinite time to exhaustively capture all CRPs, and supports periodic model updates to further strengthen security. Although this design mitigates the risk of eavesdropping and replay attacks, a residual vulnerability window persists and must be systematically addressed.

Representation of the latent space utilizing an encoder–decoder architecture.13.

In this paper, we propose a proof-of-concept autoencoder-based watermarking technique to enhance the security of our VPUF system. Specifically, we embed a digital watermark into the latent representation of the response before transmission. Unlike conventional meaningless watermarks, ours encodes secret Rayleigh fading channel data shared between the node and the server. This approach introduces intentional distortions, rendering the latent response unintelligible to potential eavesdroppers attempting to reconstruct the original VPUF model from intercepted data. As a result, the method significantly strengthens both security and privacy.

The main motivation of this paper is to enhance the security of our VPUF scheme such that it (i) maintains low hardware and maintenance costs; (ii) maintains design and implementation time; (iii) maintains viability and scalability for IoT networks; and (iv) maintains low memory consumption.

Several recent studies have explored watermarking for data protection, ownership verification, and model authentication. However, most existing approaches focus either on image-domain watermarking7,8 or model-level watermarking for DNNs15,16, which do not address the challenges of concealing and authenticating VPUF responses during wireless transmission. Prior VPUF architectures such as13 improve scalability but still expose the latent representation to potential reconstruction attacks. Unlike these methods, our work integrates an autoencoder-generated watermark directly into the VPUF latent space, combining data concealment with dual-factor authentication using secret channel information. This provides stronger protection against eavesdropping, replay attacks, and model approximation compared to traditional PUF-only or watermark-only approaches.

Background and related work

Intrusion detection systems

Sharing data packets over a shared wireless medium exposes IoT networks to various security attacks, including data forgery, packet replay, modification, insertion, and packet drop attacks17,18. For over two decades, Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) have been used to protect networks by monitoring for malicious activities and policy violations, and alerting administrators to security breaches. In IoT networks, IDS can help detect unauthorized access and potential attacks, ensuring data integrity. However, traditional protection techniques like cryptography face challenges in IoT due to resource constraints, unique protocols, and limited computational capabilities19. One major challenge is the processing capabilities and storage limitations of network devices running IDS algorithms. In traditional networks, IDS agents are deployed on intermediary nodes with substantial computing power20. Conversely, network nodes in IoT environments often have limited resources, making it challenging to find nodes capable of supporting IDS agents. A lightweight IDS is essential in IoT networks to ensure data integrity, trustworthiness, and secure provenance21,22. Watermarking techniques, which are lightweight and require minimal computational and storage resources, can meet these requirements23.

Overview of digital watermarking

Watermarking techniques, which are low-overhead and require minimal computational and storage resources, can meet the requirements for a lightweight IDS23. In particular, digital watermarking offers various lightweight solutions for numerous applications, including IoT24,25, and provides confidentiality by hiding ownership data, integrity by detecting tampering, and availability by enabling reliable extraction even under attacks or transformations26. The technology is particularly relevant for IoT, where low-cost and low-power devices connected to the internet face energy and security challenges. Traditional cryptography techniques, such as public and private keys and digital signatures, are unsuitable for resource-constrained devices, leading researchers to explore watermarking systems in IoT27,28. Digital watermarking is a key advancement in Wireless Sensor Network (WSN) security29. Ownership and provenance watermarking

The digital preservation of ownership for the substantial amounts of data generated in IoT networks presents challenges such as copyright infringement and data authentication31–33. To address these issues, digital watermarking, which involves invisible changes to data, has gained significant interest and is established as a method for ownership protection against piracy32. One approach to digitally watermark data is to use data provenance, which tracks the lineage and actions performed on data from the source node to the final destination, as shown in Fig. 3. However, implementing this solution poses several challenges, including the rapid aggregation of provenance data during transmission and limitations on nodes’ bandwidth, energy, and storage capacity34.

Authentication and concealment watermarking

The landscape of cybersecurity is continually evolving to meet the challenges posed by increasing digital threats and the need for secure data transmission. Integrated cryptography and steganography have steered towards being a focus area because they encapsulate both the concepts of concealment and endorsed security35. Steganography and cryptography, when considered individually, are inadequate to ensure holistic information security; thus, a more robust and improved solution can be realized by integrating the two methods36–38. Authors in35,39 review several of these approaches and establish the enhanced security of this hybrid approach. Thus, although conventional watermarking is meant to add information without distorting the original data, it can be modified for data concealment. Aggressively inserting data can make the original data unreadable or invisible to unauthorized parties. Additionally, steganographic encryption can change original data by embedding watermarks in a way that only authorized users can identify the original data.

Digital watermarking for machine learning models

With their ability to extract complex, high-dimensional details from large datasets, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are a crucial pillar in the Fourth Industrial Revolution40. A myriad of Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) applications make notable use of DNNs41–44. As such, several security risks, such as model stealing, tampering, and other threats, impact DNN models45,46. Digital Watermarking has been used traditionally for the protection of digital assets such as photographs, music, and video. On a similar note, DNN models can also be digitally watermarked, with the idea first being introduced in15.

Although several methods for generating watermarks in ML models have been proposed, along with techniques to detect, remove, suppress, or forge these watermarks, the threat landscape surrounding these watermarking schemes has not been clearly defined, and the goals and guarantees of different approaches remain unclear. This lack of clarity makes it difficult for model owners to select the most suitable watermarking scheme for their needs or to compare existing approaches47 effectively.

The process of watermarking neural network models requires careful attention to both usability and security, addressing four key requirements48:

Fidelity: ensures that embedding the watermark does not negatively impact the performance or predictive accuracy of the model, preserving its original functionality.

Unforgeability: prevents attackers from replicating or generating a valid watermark, thereby blocking false and counterfeit watermarks.

Robustness: ensures the watermark remains intact and detectable even after attempts to tamper with the model, which is likely to degrade the model’s accuracy.

There are three primary approaches to watermarking DNN models: white-box watermarking, black-box watermarking, and box-less watermarking.

White-box model watermarking

White-box watermarking involves accessing the model’s parameters or neuron activations to retrieve the embedded watermark. Depending on where the message is embedded, white-box watermarking techniques are categorized into three types: weight-based15,49–52, activation-based53,54, and passport-based16,55.

A white-box model watermarking scheme generally involves two main phases: watermark embedding and watermark verification. In the embedding phase, a secret message (e.g., a bit string) is integrated into the model’s parameters or intermediate activation maps. During the verification phase, the watermark is extracted from a suspect model, with the process designed to retrieve a message of the same length in polynomial time56. The difference in verification using white-box watermarking and black-box watermarking is shown in Fig. 4.

Comparison between white-box watermarking verification and black-box watermarking verification.

Verification in a white-box setting involves accessing the model’s parameters to extract and compare the embedded watermark, offering more reliability than black-box methods that rely on model behavior with specific target inputs49,57. White-box watermarks are generally resistant to various removal attacks, such as modification of parameters and structural changes. However, researchers in49,56 reveal that vulnerabilities exist in these schemes, especially against neural structural obfuscation techniques that can interfere with watermark verification.

Black-box model watermarking

Black-box model watermarking employs poisoned training, where the model classifies clean input normally and poisoned outcomes with watermarks as a predefined class, as shown in Fig. 5. The key challenge lies in constructing these poisoned data samples or trigger sets, which can be grouped into four methods: simple layer overlay, spatial transformation, digital watermarking techniques, and deep steganography. In the simple layer overlay method, a small data sample (e.g., a square) is overlaid onto the original data, with attention to placement and size. Notable examples include BadNets58, Blend59, and SIG60 methods. Spatial transformation methods create poisoned data samples by applying techniques like rotation, translation, and flipping to the original clean data. Examples include WaNet61, which distorts the data, and BATT62, which rotates it by a specific angle. These methods, along with simple layer overlay, are forms of data augmentation, making the process of creating poisoned data relatively simple. The third category, digital watermarking techniques, utilizes conventional digital watermarking methods to create highly inconspicuous poisoned data. A key example is BppAttack63, which embeds the watermark into an input using the least significant bits and dithering techniques. The final category, deep steganography techniques, uses deep steganographic networks to generate poisoned outcomes. Techniques like LIRA64 and ISSBA65 are notable examples.

Workings of black-box watermarking.

The black-box model watermarking methods mentioned either use a simple approach or an extra neural network to create poisoned data. The simple approach significantly alters the original input, making it easier for people to notice, while the extra neural network requires more time and resources for training. Since neural networks are often shared among different users due to the high cost of training, existing watermarking methods struggle to embed unique watermarks for each user, making it hard to identify specific pirates when networks are distributed to multiple users. Additionally, if a skilled attacker gets hold of many poisoned and clean data samples, they could analyze their differences and figure out how the poisoned data points were made. This could allow them to forge poisoned data, leading to disputes over model copyright. This is especially problematic when the same poisoning method is used for all data samples48.

Box-less model watermarking

Box-less watermarking is an alternative to conventional white-box and black-box techniques. It secures deep learning models without requiring access to internal parameters or input-output behavior. During training, box-less watermarking inserts an invisible watermark, typically a pre-established picture or pattern, into the model’s output using a hidden network. The watermark is extracted from the output using an extraction network, and if it matches the original, the model is verified to be watermarked.

This approach, shown in Fig. 6, seeks to withstand several attacks, including pruning, surrogate attacks, and fine-tuning of the model. Current research in box-free watermarking has focused on ensuring watermark quality, resilience to surrogate attacks, adaptability to ordinary data processing, and suitability for different kinds of generative workloads66–68. However, authors in69 suggest that box-less watermarks can still be subject to removal attacks, even in cases where the attacker has restricted access to the model.

Autoencoder-based watermarking

While traditional watermarking techniques are frequently employed, watermarking using deep learning has become a competitive option. Typically, networks are constructed using encoder-decoder architectures, in which a decoder attempts to extract a message from data that an encoder has embedded70,71. Mahapatra et al.6 propose a CNN-based autoencoder watermarking algorithm that balances robustness and invisibility by using a two-network model for embedding and extraction. Another study proposes a digital image watermarking technique using an autoencoder-based CNN, claiming it to be the first of its kind in the domain of watermarking. Their method demonstrates robustness against various attacks such as salt & pepper noise, Gaussian noise, and JPEG compression7. Authors in8 present a robust image watermarking algorithm for copyright protection using variational autoencoder networks, comprising encoder, decoder, and detector subnetworks. Their method embeds and extracts a 1-bit watermark while achieving high visual quality and robustness against geometric and noise attacks compared to traditional transform domain techniques. Alternatively, the researchers in72 propose an invisible watermarking technique using a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) that enables the embedding and extraction of large, full-color watermark images.

Novelty

To the best of our knowledge, limited attention has been given to the use of watermarking for both data concealment and authentication using autoencoders in wireless communication. Exploring this area opens a new research direction that extends beyond traditional watermarking for data ownership protection. In this work, we introduce a scheme that builds upon our previous work in13, while also functioning as a standalone solution or integrating with other security frameworks. Our proposed approach combines watermark generation, embedding, and extraction, leveraging secret channel information and autoencoders to secure data transmission over wireless channels. The watermark serves a dual purpose: first, it distorts the data to shield it from eavesdroppers and attackers attempting to compromise the VPUF-based authentication system in an IoT network. Second, once extracted, it acts as an authentication mechanism, effectively enabling a multifactor authentication system.

Watermarking for virtual PUF (VPUF)

Proposed scheme

In this research, we extend the original split learning architecture, illustrated in Fig. 1, to enhance the security of the VPUF framework. This is done by embedding watermarks within the VPUF’s latent space, as shown in Fig. 7. In this architecture, the encoder resides on the IoT device and the decoder on the authentication server, with both trained jointly using split learning. The encoder processes the input challenge to generate a latent representation, which is then transmitted wirelessly to the server-side decoder. To ensure privacy and data protection during this transmission, the scheme proposed in this paper incorporates watermark embedding directly into the latent space. A dedicated embedding network securely integrates the watermark without compromising the latent representation’s utility, while an extraction network on the server side accurately retrieves the watermark. This approach not only preserves the integrity of the encoded information but also introduces an additional layer of authentication, offering enhanced resilience against potential security threats during communication.

Proposed watermarking scheme.

This method leverages the unique properties of the latent space, providing a more secure and efficient medium for embedding watermarks compared to traditional output-based approaches. By embedding the watermark at this intermediate stage, the scheme not only enhances robustness against tampering or noise during transmission but also allows for efficient authentication processes. Both the latent space and the extracted watermark are subjected to their respective authentication protocols, enabling reliable verification of the transmitted information.

Figure 8 illustrates a detailed overview of the complete watermarking protocol within the broader system, highlighting each stage of watermark embedding, transmission, and extraction. The watermark generator leverages channel data to produce a latent representation, which acts as the watermark. This watermark is concatenated with the latent representation of the response and processed through an autoencoder, compressing the watermarked data into a third latent space. This private information is then transmitted over the wireless channel. On the server side, the data is decoded from the latent domain and separated into the latent response and the watermark, which can subsequently be verified for authentication. This end-to-end framework showcases the potential of latent space watermarking as a robust and flexible approach for secure communication and data integrity in modern transmission systems.

Detailed workings of the proposed watermarking scheme.

The proposed scheme adopts a box-free (also referred to as box-less) watermarking approach. However, instead of embedding the watermark into the output of the VPUF network, the watermark is embedded in the latent space generated by the VPUF encoder and extracted before the latent space is passed onto the VPUF decoder. The watermark is generated using the private channel data shared between the node and the server. The following subsections explain each framework in detail.

In our implementation, the VPUF latent response acts as the host signal for watermark embedding. Given a challenge C, the client-side encoder produces a latent vector , while a separate autoencoder maps the Rayleigh fading channel state h to a compact latent watermark . The vectors and are concatenated and passed through a lightweight autoencoder that embeds the watermark and outputs a watermarked latent representation , which is then transmitted over the wireless channel.

On the server side, the corresponding decoding network reconstructs both the estimated latent response and the estimated watermark . The recovered latent response is fed into the VPUF decoder to obtain the predicted response, while is compared against the expected watermark for authentication.

Because the embedding network is trained using a reconstruction loss on both and , the perturbations introduced by watermarking remain small. This ensures that the unpredictability and uniqueness of the underlying VPUF challenge-response behavior are preserved, while simultaneously concealing the latent response within the watermarked representation .

Watermark generation and verification

Rayleigh fading, a common phenomenon in wireless communication, models the signal degradation caused by multipath propagation, where transmitted signals reach the receiver through multiple paths due to reflection, scattering, and diffraction. This leads to constructive and destructive interference, resulting in rapid fluctuations in signal amplitude and phase. Jake’s Model is frequently used as a statistical technique to produce realistic fading waveforms to replicate this effect. In this study, Rayleigh fading based on Jake’s Model is simulated. Jake’s Model is used to generate a Rayleigh fading envelope by approximating the fading process using a finite number of sinusoids, with user-defined parameters such as the number of samples, maximum Doppler frequency, and the number of sinusoids. It constructs in-phase and quadrature components using trigonometric functions modulated by Doppler shifts and random initial phases, then combines them to compute the fading envelope, which follows a Rayleigh distribution. The simulation parameters, including a Doppler frequency of 5 Hz and 35,250 samples, are chosen to represent a specific communication scenario. The resulting fading envelope is visualized in Fig. 9, showcasing the amplitude variations over time due to multipath effects.

Simulated Rayleigh distribution fading envelope using Jake’s model.

This data is then used to generate the watermark. The Rayleigh fading envelope is stored in a DataFrame and divided into training and testing datasets. The autoencoder consists of an encoder that compresses the input data into a higher-dimensional latent representation (encoding size = 128 neurons) and a decoder that reconstructs the original input from the latent space. The encoder is designed with progressively increasing hidden layer sizes to capture more complex patterns in the input data, while the decoder reflects this structure for accurate reconstruction. The model is trained using the mean squared error (MSE) loss function and the Adam optimizer, with training conducted over 500 epochs using a batch size of 16. After training, the encoder is used to generate a latent representation of the test data, effectively acting as a watermark. The latent watermark is standardized to ensure consistency and alignment with the VPUF latent space’s distribution. This process enables robust and efficient encoding of channel characteristics into a latent space for potential downstream tasks, including watermarking and authentication.

The framework outlines a complete pipeline for integrating, encoding, and decoding watermark data using a deep learning-based autoencoder architecture. We begin by combining the latent response with the watermark, where rows of the latent space are concatenated with a standardized watermark vector to form a combined dataset. To encode and decode the watermarked latent response, this dataset is fed into an autoencoder. The sequential encoder of the autoencoder architecture compresses the input into a lower-dimensional latent form, which is the private information highlighted in Fig. 8, and the decoder uses this compressed representation to recover the original input. The watermark embedding and extraction framework is shown in Fig. 10. The training phase uses the combined dataset, using an MSE loss to optimize reconstruction accuracy over multiple epochs. The model’s performance is periodically monitored to ensure proper convergence. Once trained, the autoencoder can process the combined dataset to reconstruct both the latent response and the watermark.

Proposed watermark embedding and extraction framework.

For post-processing, the code includes a reverse-combination function to separate the reconstructed latent space and watermark from the combined dataset. The latent space and watermark are denormalized using their respective statistical parameters (mean and standard deviation), ensuring they closely resemble the original inputs. The pipeline is designed to evaluate the fidelity of reconstruction through metrics such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and MSE, providing quantitative insights into the accuracy of both the latent space and watermark recovery. This approach highlights the integration of autoencoders for robust watermark embedding and recovery in a latent representation framework.

Experimental results

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we assess four key metrics: the fidelity of the original VPUF model, the reliability of watermark extraction, the robustness of the watermark against noise-based attacks, and its unforgeability under blind reconstruction attempts.

Unless otherwise specified, all evaluations are performed on a MacBook Pro (2023) with an Apple M2 chip, featuring an 8-core CPU, 10-core GPU, and 24 GB of unified memory, running macOS Ventura 13.5.

Fidelity

Fidelity quantifies how the watermark’s embedding affects the ML model’s predicted accuracy. We observe high fidelity, which is a critical metric for assessing the performance of the watermarking scheme. Achieving a high fidelity in our instance shows that the watermarking technique does not impair the VPUF model’s functionality or affect its performance, ensuring that the VPUF maintains its 99% authentication rate even after watermark embedding and extraction, as shown in Fig. 12.

Reliability

Reliability refers to the stability and consistency of the watermark extraction and verification process. Approaching 100% reliability ensures that the embedded watermark remains intact and can be accurately retrieved and verified under all conditions. In other words, it confirms that the watermark can be precisely recovered from the model without degradation. This property is illustrated in Fig. 13, which presents a Kernel Density Estimate (KDE) plot, a smoothed representation of a histogram, used to estimate the probability density functions of both the original and extracted watermarks. The close alignment of these distributions serves as empirical evidence of the system’s high reliability.

Robustness

Robustness gauges how well a watermark withstands attempts to erase or modify it. We use a noise injection attack to test the robustness of the watermark and the authentication rate of the VPUF, simulating the addition of varying amounts of noise to the watermarked latent response. Unforgeability

Unforgeability refers to the difficulty an attacker faces in replicating or forging the watermark without access to the original embedding process or data. In this context, unforgeability is evaluated through a blind reconstruction attack over 10,000 iterations. We consider the watermark to be forged if a similarity of 10% or above is achieved between the fake watermark and the original. Our results indicate that the proposed scheme demonstrates that no successful forgery was observed under random blind reconstruction attempts, with the similarity remaining at 0% across all trials, as illustrated in Fig. 15. This indicates that the watermark cannot be successfully forged or replicated using blind reconstruction. This demonstrates that secret channel information is a reliable approach for watermark generation, ensuring that the watermark remains both unique and secure.

Trade-off analysis: robustness, imperceptibility, and computational overhead

In designing a watermarking scheme suitable for resource-constrained IoT devices, our approach prioritizes low computational overhead and fidelity over extremely high robustness. Because the watermark is embedded entirely within the latent space, the encoder-side operations involve an autoencoder. This design choice maintains the VPUF’s authentication accuracy and minimizes memory usage, which is critical for IoT deployment.

However, this low-overhead design necessarily introduces a trade-off: while the watermark remains reliably extractable under moderate noise levels, its robustness is not ideal to withstand aggressive adversarial attacks. Imperceptibility, in the traditional sense, is also intentionally relaxed because the watermark serves a dual role of both authentication and controlled distortion to protect the latent response from reconstruction attacks.

Lessons learned

This paper focuses on developing a watermarking scheme designed for visible watermarking, which intentionally distorts the original VPUF in such a way that it prevents eavesdropping attackers from easily copying it. We offer valuable insights for both research and practice. The proposed watermarking scheme addresses the limitations of the original VPUF by providing a flexible, scalable, and cost-effective security enhancement that can be integrated into IoT networks without the need for specialized hardware, making it particularly useful in resource-constrained environments where hardware costs and power efficiency are critical. The watermark, acting on data without modifying the original VPUF model, introduces sufficient distortions and complexity in the VPUF output, making it extremely difficult for attackers to replicate the watermark without knowledge of the secret channel information. The channel, which is shared between the node and the server, remains confidential, ensuring that only authorized entities can correctly interpret and verify the watermark. Thus, the watermarking scheme not only ensures security and robustness but also prevents unauthorized replication, further enhancing the security of the overall system.

The use of watermark-embedded latent responses provides additional protection against several classes of advanced attacks that traditionally threaten PUF-based authentication. Because the transmitted representation is a fused vector containing both the VPUF latent response and a channel-derived watermark, the adversary does not have access to clean CRPs, which significantly complicates modeling or ML-based approximation attacks. Replay attacks are challenged by the fact that the watermark encodes dynamically varying Rayleigh fading channel information; thus, a previously captured watermarked response becomes invalid when the watermark cannot be reproduced at verification.

It is noteworthy that while the watermarking scheme shows great promise, these limitations underscore the need for continued research to ensure its robustness and reliability in practical deployments. While no method can guarantee absolute resistance against all adversaries, the proposed embedding strategy reduces the attack surface compared to traditional PUF-only schemes by obscuring the latent structure and binding each authentication sequence to time-varying channel characteristics.

Conclusion and future work

This paper introduces a watermarking framework comprising a watermark generator and an embedding and extraction mechanism. The proposed scheme presents notable advantages in terms of elevated security by data concealment and robust authentication. Compared to prior watermarking and PUF-based authentication schemes, the proposed method introduces a distinct advantage by embedding a channel-dependent watermark in the latent space rather than in the output domain. Traditional digital watermarking methods focus on ownership protection or integrity verification, while prior PUF and VPUF designs rely solely on CRP unpredictability for security. Our approach combines these concepts to provide both data concealment and authentication, thereby improving resilience against eavesdropping, replay, and model reconstruction attacks.

Since the unpredictability and uniqueness of a PUF are reflected directly through its CRP behavior, maintaining the accuracy of the VPUF after watermark embedding is essential. The experimental results show that the authentication rate remains unchanged at 99% before and after watermark processing, indicating that the core characteristics of the VPUF are preserved despite the additional embedding and extraction stages.

Overall, this work presents a proof-of-concept demonstration of latent-space watermarking as a lightweight security enhancement for VPUFs. At the same time, we recognize several limitations: the evaluation is performed under controlled simulation settings, robustness testing is restricted to additive noise, and quantitative uncertainty measures are not reported. Taken together, the findings illustrate that embedding channel-dependent watermarks in the latent space is a promising direction for enhancing VPUF security, and they lay the foundation for subsequent deployment-oriented and hardware-based testing.

Moving forward, the continuation of this research will focus on fortifying the scheme’s security through the implementation of an advanced watermark embedding and extraction mechanism. In addition, this study will perform real-time tests to verify the effectiveness of the proposed scheme in various cyber attack scenarios, thereby demonstrating its feasibility. Due to the constraints of this paper, we presume that an attacker is not able to access both the server and authentic nodes.
