Letter to the editor regarding “Combined uterine injury and high rectal perforation complicating abdominal ectopic pregnancy: A case report”
Authors: Goran Augustin
Published: 2024

Dear Editor-in-Chief,

I read with great interest a case of “Combined uterine injury and high rectal perforation complicating abdominal ectopic pregnancy” by Zaidi et al. [1].

Unfortunately, many flaws in the article, resulted in misleading, starting from the title. First, the title says “uterine injury”. From the case description, there was no uterine injury. In the further text, I will elaborate on why this could be instrumental uterine injury. The second issue in the title is “rectal perforation”. Perforation, by definition, means either underlying pathology (intestinal obstruction, ischemia, or even rectal ectopic pregnancy, which cannot advance until 5th month of pregnancy) that resulted in perforation, or perforation due to penetrating rectal injury, either transabdominally, transanally, transvaginally, or through the uterus, which I will also elaborate on later. Contrary to perforation, rupture is caused by increased pressure either from outside or inside a hollow organ. Why would a woman have a fetus in the abdominal cavity with spontaneous perforations of both mentioned organs? That does not sound pathophysiologically possible. The third issue of the title is that we do not know whether it was a primary or secondary abdominal pregnancy, which is precisely classified. “Abdominal pregnancies can be classified as primary when fertilization occurs outside the uterine adnexa or as secondary (thought to be more common), resulting from undetected rupture of early tubal pregnancy with subsequent implantation onto the peritoneal surfaces. In rare cases of uterine rupture (mostly rupture of a unicornuate or bicornuate uterus), the fetus may be extruded into the peritoneal cavity. At the same time, the placenta remains functional within the uterus, and the gestation continues as a uteroabdominal pregnancy.” [2]. According to this, it was a primary abdominal pregnancy because the authors write,” Adjacent to the fetus, a partially fragmented placenta was discovered, attached to the posterior wall of the uterus” [1]. It is difficult to have an accurate medical history of a psychiatric patient, but hetero-anamnestic data or medical records could help but are lacking. Risk factors for abdominal pregnancy include tubal pregnancies, pelvic inflammatory disease, tubal sterilization, tubal infertility, tubal reconstructive surgery, endometriosis, transfer at the blastocyst stage, a higher number of embryos transferred, decreased endometrial thickness, variation in culture media, fresh embryo transfer, women who conceive despite an intrauterine contraceptive device or progestagen-only contraceptive pills and cocaine use [3].

The next issue in the article is acute intra-abdominal bleeding. If this is a primary abdominal pregnancy, why would a patient present with hemorrhagic shock? It comes from placental infiltration of parenchymatous organs such as the liver or spleen, but hemorrhagic shock is rare [3]. During surgical exploration, in the article by Zaidi et al. [1], no cause was described or hypothetized. The authors write “a 3 mm perforation in the high rectum, which had allowed fecal matter into the peritoneal cavity, suggesting the onset of stercoral peritonitis. Furthermore, a large 5 cm perforation was found in the posterior wall of the uterus”. There is no macroscopic description of these perforations. How do these look like? Were any of these two perforations a cause of bleeding? In conclusion section of the article, the authors give general explanations for ectopic and abdominal pregnancy without trying to enlighten the cause of simultaneous uterine and rectal perforation in their patient with primary abdominal pregnancy. One potential cause is severe blunt trauma in the lower abdomen that sharply increases intra-abdominal pressure, and also a direct blow into the uterus that results in uterine and high rectal rupture because of the proximity of these two organs. There was no mention of abdominal trauma, although it is possible because it is a case of a psychiatric patient. Probably, some cutaneous signs/marks of trauma would be present with such severe uterine and rectal injuries. The second potential cause, not in this case, could be uterine torsion of 180o and Cesarean incision of an unrecognized torsed gravid uterus in the rotated posterior wall instead of anterior wall. In such cases, the potential for other organ injuries is increased. And the third, most probable cause follows. The patient is a psychiatric patient, and it is known that unshielded and unwanted sexual intercourse is present in these populations. After the recognition of the pregnancy, the intention was to terminate the pregnancy, by patient or any other person involved. The procedure that was probably done was transvaginal instrumental termination of pregnancy. In inexperienced hands, especially in more advanced pregnancies, the complication rate is high including uterine perforation [4]. After instrumental uterine perforation, the operator inadvertently pulls the small bowel because the small bowel loops are substituted for the placental cord [5], or, as probably was in this case, the rectosigmoid portion, the closest to the posterior uterine wall, was seized and pulled with an instrument, resulting in perforation [6]. Another element that confirms this mechanism is the lack of an upper extremity of the fetus. The authors did not describe this pathology in detail. One option is that it is the congenital absence of the entire extremity, which is exceptionally rare, and another is that a fetus was forcefully pulled for the hand through the hole of 5 cm in the posterior wall of the uterus. The person doing this procedure was not aware of abdominal pregnancy. The last finding confirming this mechanism is placental fragmentation. Why would intact intra-abdominally placed placenta be fragmented unless it was instrumentally manipulated. The placenta in abdominal pregnancy is firmly attached to surrounding organs, and the division is very difficult and bloody. Still, there is no description of such situation in the article. All these elements explain the acute presentation of a patient with 2 L of hemoperitoneum from transvaginal instrumental criminal abortion.

In conclusion, it is very important to publish case reports of rare pathologies to gain better insight into their incidence, etiology, diagnostic accuracy, adequate therapeutic principles, and outcomes. Another important issue is that the authors should explain these rare occurrences accurately, meaning pathophysiologically correctly, because only then can we understand how to deal with them.
