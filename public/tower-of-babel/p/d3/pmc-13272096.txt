Apathy and resting state functional connectivity across the Alzheimer's disease continuum
Authors: Gladiliz Rivera‐Delpín, Phebe Palmer, Maximilian M. Ramseyer, Claudia R. Aibel, Madeline Yu, Catherine E. Munro, Patrizia Vannini, Jennifer R. Gatchel, Gad A. Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis study examined how apathy symptoms and awareness of those symptoms relate to resting‐state functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fcMRI) across the Alzheimer's disease (AD) continuum using network segregation metrics.METHODSThere were eighty‐three participants, including cognitively unimpaired (CU; n = 46), cognitively impaired (CI; mild cognitive impairment or AD dementia, n = 21), and mild behaviorally impaired (MBI; n = 16) individuals. Apathy was measured using the Apathy Evaluation Scale (AES)‐Self, AES‐Informant, and discrepancy scores (informant minus self). Models tested associations between rs‐fcMRI segregation and apathy. Rs‐fcMRI was computed using pairwise correlations.RESULTSIn CI individuals, dorsal attention network segregation related to greater apathy and reduced awareness of apathy, while in those with MBI, salience network segregation was linked to less apathy.DISCUSSIONNetwork‐level connectivity disruptions differentially relate to apathy and reduced awareness across the AD continuum.

INTRODUCTION

Apathy is one of the most common neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related disorders, across the clinical continuum. 1 Apathy is a multidimensional syndrome characterized by loss of or diminished motivation, reduced goal‐directed behavior, and reduced emotional and cognitive activity 2 , 3 , 4 and it cannot be explained by reduced consciousness, cognitive deficits, or emotional distress. 3 It is prevalent in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), affecting more than one third of this population, 5 , 6 and is associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including cognitive and functional decline, and increased mortality. 4 , 7 , 8 Given its early emergence and prognostic relevance, growing work has sought to characterize the behavioral and neural mechanisms that contribute to apathy before the onset of dementia.

Mild behavioral impairment (MBI) has emerged as a clinical syndrome marked by later‐life onset NPS in pre‐dementia states, and apathy, or reduced motivation, is a core MBI domain. 9 MBI can occur in individuals who are cognitively unimpaired (CU), as well as those with non‐dementia cognitive impairment, including MCI. Moreover, MBI is linked to increased dementia risk and may be subserved by underlying changes in functional brain networks in early AD. 10 , 11 Because MBI may reflect an early neurobehavioral phenotype, it provides a unique opportunity to examine apathy prior to overt cognitive decline.

Recent work has also demonstrated that self‐ and study‐partner ratings of apathy diverge across disease stages, with CU individuals over‐reporting apathy and individuals with MCI under‐reporting it relative to their study partners. 12 , 13 This shift from over‐ to under‐reporting has been interpreted as a marker of declining insight, highlighting the relevance of examining apathy awareness and its neural correlates. These discrepancies demonstrate the need to assess apathy in terms of both symptom severity and awareness, as differences in insight may signal emerging neural alterations.

That said, more research is needed to better understand differences in relationships between brain networks and apathy symptoms in preclinical (i.e., CU) and clinical (i.e., cognitive impairment [CI]) disease states along the cognitive continuum of AD, as well as how these relationships differ in individuals who meet criteria for MBI. Clarifying these stage‐specific patterns requires sensitive tools capable of capturing subtle network alterations.

Resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) provides a non‐invasive method to examine intrinsic connectivity within and between large‐scale brain networks. Key metrics derived from rs‐fMRI include segregation (reflecting functional specialization within a network or the degree to which a network is functionally specialized and internally cohesive), integration (indicating efficiency of cross‐network communication), and weighted degree (a measure of nodal connectivity strength or overall strength of connectivity between nodes within the network). These indices offer a nuanced lens through which to examine the organization of neural systems implicated in motivational processes. Prior research suggests that changes in the structure and function of key brain networks, such as the dorsal attention network (DAN), default mode network (DMN), frontoparietal control network (FPCN), and salience network (SAN), may contribute to motivational and behavioral changes and dysfunction in aging and neurodegenerative diseases. However, much of this work was performed in samples of older adults with CI, at the stage of MCI or AD dementia, and further investigation is needed to determine whether similar relationships are present in CU individuals, including those who meet criteria for MBI, prior to the stage of overt cognitive decline. Thus, examining connectivity across diagnostic groups may clarify whether network alterations associated with apathy emerge before cognitive symptoms.

The present study examined associations between apathy (measured via self‐report, study‐partner report, and discrepancy—defined as informant minus self‐rated scores) and rs‐fMRI–derived connectivity metrics (focused on segregation) in a cohort of older adults in cognitive and behavioral diagnostic groups across the AD continuum: CU, CI consisting of MCI and AD dementia, and MBI. We hypothesized that individuals with MBI, as well as those with CI, would exhibit associations between self‐ and study partner–reported apathy and altered network organization, particularly within the DMN, DAN, FPCN, and SAN.

METHODS

Participants

The sample comprised 83 older adults categorized into three diagnostic groups: CU without MBI (n = 46); CI without MBI (n = 21), which included those with MCI (n = 9) and AD dementia (n = 12); and MBI (n = 16), spanning either diagnostic group (75% CU and 25% CI). Diagnostic classifications followed criteria outlined in the Instrumental Activity of Daily Living (IADL; R01 AG053184, PI: Marshall; R01 AG067021, PI: Marshall) and the Memory Awareness (AWARE; R01 AG061083, PI: Vannini) study protocols and are summarized below.

CU participants were defined as cognitively unimpaired, with Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores between 27 and 30, a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score of 0, and performance above education‐adjusted cutoffs on the Wechsler Memory Scale‐Revised (WMS‐R) Logical Memory (LM) II delayed recall. Participants with MCI met criteria for amnestic MCI (single or multi‐domain), with MMSE scores between 24 and 30, CDR global scores of 0.5 (with memory box scores of 0.5 or 1), WMS‐R LM II scores below education‐adjusted cutoffs, and essentially preserved instrumental activities of daily living, which were determined by a clinician. Participants with AD dementia met National Institutes on Aging–Alzheimer's Association criteria for probable AD dementia, with MMSE scores ranging from 14 to 26, CDR global scores of 0.5 or 1, and WMS‐R LM II scores below education‐adjusted cutoffs. Participants with MCI and AD dementia were combined into a single group (CI) for analyses because of the small size of each group. MBI classification was determined based on a total score of ≥ 7 on the MBI Checklist (MBI‐C), with symptoms present for at least 6 months and reflecting a noticeable change from baseline. This cut‐off point was selected because prior studies suggest that MBI‐C cutoffs vary by cognitive status but generally fall between ≈ 6 and 8.5, with lower thresholds proposed for CU samples and higher thresholds for individuals with subjective cognitive decline or MCI. 14 , 15 Participants with MBI had MMSE scores between 24 and 30 and CDR global scores of 0 or 0.5. To be eligible for the current analyses, participants needed to have both apathy data and rs‐fMRI data. Exclusion criteria for all participants included a history of head trauma, unstable medical illness at recruitment, severe mental illness (e.g., schizoaffective, bipolar disorders), or recent (in the past 1–2 years) substance/alcohol abuse. Participants in any diagnostic group on stable doses of antidepressants that do not negatively affect cognition were allowed in the study. Additionally, participants with CI on stable doses of cholinesterase inhibitors and/or memantine were allowed in the study.

The IADL and AWARE studies were approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. All participants and study partners provided informed consent prior to completing any study procedures.

Neuroimaging acquisition and processing

Rs‐fMRI data were acquired using standardized protocols across participants. Functional connectivity matrices were computed using pairwise correlations between time series of nodes within predefined networks: DMN, DAN, FPCN, SAN, medial visual network (MVN), somatosensory network, and limbic network. These networks were selected based on prior studies evaluating associations between rs‐fMRI and NPS, in particular apathy, across the cognitive continuum. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 From these, three key metrics were derived: (1) segregation, defined as within‐network modularity; (2) integration, defined as the efficiency of cross‐network communication; and (3) weighted degree, reflecting the summed strength of connectivity per node within each network. 23 We chose to focus on the segregation metric for our main analyses to see if any of the networks are functionally specialized for apathy. Segregation provides a focused measure of network specialization, reflecting strong within‐network connectivity relative to between‐network connectivity. 24 Prior work in AD and MCI has shown that apathy has been linked to weaker within‐network organization, particularly in control networks that support goal‐directed behavior. Because our aim was to test a hypothesis about network specialization in apathy, segregation was the most theoretically appropriate metric. Integration and weighted degree were obtained and may be examined in future work.

Apathy measures

Apathy was assessed using the Apathy Evaluation Scale using both self‐report (Apathy Evaluation Scale‐Self [AES‐S]) and study partner‐report (Apathy Evaluation Scale‐Informant [AES‐I]) versions. The AES is an 18‐item questionnaire, with responses scored on a 4‐point Likert scale. 3 , 17 Scores are based on the individual's experiences over the past 4 weeks, with higher scores reflecting less apathy. 25 The total AES score is obtained by summing the individual item scores. 3 , 13 , 25 , 26

Apathy discrepancy was computed as the difference between study partner‐ and self‐ratings (AES‐I minus AES‐S), serving as a proxy for insight or awareness into motivational changes. This discrepancy variable captures the awareness of apathy symptoms and whether participants under‐ or overestimate their own behavioral changes relative to what is reported by a study partner. Positive scores indicate greater awareness of motivational changes relative to study partner (i.e., the participant acknowledges more apathy than the informant reports). Negative discrepancy scores reflect reduced insight or greater unawareness of motivational deficits from the participant's perspective, with study partners reporting more pronounced apathy than the participant. The greater the absolute value of these discrepancy scores, the larger the gap between the study partner and participant reports. In the present analysis, we interpret negative scores as reduced awareness (reduced self‐report relative to study partner report) and positive scores as heightened awareness.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.4.0. Linear regression models were used to examine the association between the segregation network connectivity metric (our predictor of interest) and apathy scores (AES‐I, AES‐S, and discrepancy) as the outcomes across all participants. Models were adjusted for participant age, sex, and education. Models were repeated with interactions between segregation and diagnostic group, as well as separately within individual diagnostic groups. Group‐level differences in network metrics were reported with unstandardized beta estimates, t values, and p values. Significance was determined using a threshold of p < 0.05 without adjustment for multiple comparisons due to the exploratory nature of the analyses. Distributions of AES scores were skewed, with the strongest skew observed in AES‐I data. Discrepancy scores much more closely followed a normal distribution and, as such, had the best general model performance. Assumptions of linear regression models were assessed visually and determined to be met sufficiently relative to the exploratory nature of our findings with the “rempsyc” package in R. One assumption flagged by model checks was collinearity of variables, wherein terms flagged as collinear were the diagnostic group variable and the interaction term. Collinearity between these terms was expected as interaction terms are inherently related to their main effects.

To assess whether associations between connectivity measures and study partner‐dependent apathy scores (AES‐I and AES‐discrepancy) were influenced by study partner characteristics, models were run again with the following two covariates: study partner relationship to the participant (family member, friend, spouse/partner, or other) and whether the study partner lived with the participant (Y/N).

RESULTS

The sample included CU individuals without MBI (n = 46), individuals with CI but without MBI (n = 21; MCI or AD dementia), and individuals with MBI (n = 16, spanning both CU [n = 12] and MCI [n = 4] stages). As shown in Table 1, groups differed modestly in age and sex distribution, while education was high uniformly. Cognitive measures followed expected patterns, with intact performance in CU and MBI and greater impairment in CI across CDR, MMSE, and Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite 5. Apathy discrepancy scores differed by group, with CU individuals slightly over‐reporting apathy (1; −2 to 5), individuals with MBI accurately reporting apathy (0; −9 to 5), and individuals with CI substantially under‐reporting apathy (−6; −14 to −1). Network segregation patterns were broadly consistent with known patterns of network organization in aging and early AD, with the DMN showing the highest segregation (0.96 to 0.97), and limbic (CU [−1.05], MBI [−0.99], CI [−1.00]) and somatosensory networks the lowest (CU [−0.12]; MBI [−0.19]; CI [−0.23]).

WHOLE SAMPLE

Group‐wise linear relationships

To gain a sense of general relationships between apathy and functional connectivity, models were run first without adjusting for diagnostic group. A few noteworthy relationships were observed (see Table 2). High SAN segregation was significantly associated with lower self‐reported apathy (β: 6.3, p = 0.04). High segregation in the FPCN was significantly associated with higher study partner–reported apathy and greater apathy unawareness (AES‐I: β: −8.1, p = 0.047; discrepancy: β: −8.2, p = 0.04). Finally, somatosensory network segregation was marginally associated with lower self‐ and study partner‐reported apathy (AES‐I: β: 5.8, p = 0.06, AES‐S: β: 4.0, p = 0.08). Models including interaction terms for diagnostic group

Models run on the whole sample tested segregation against AES scores, with interaction terms between segregation and diagnostic group (with the CU group serving as a reference; see Figure 1 and Table 3). For individuals with CI, study partner–reported apathy displayed a statistically significant relationship with DAN segregation, with higher DAN segregation corresponding to higher apathy; in contrast, CU individuals (treated as the reference group) were observed to have consistently low apathy across the spectrum of DAN segregation (β: 2.34, p = 0.58); this slope was significantly moderated for those labeled CI (β: −21.32, p = 0.01). This relationship was not mirrored by self‐reported apathy and therefore correlated with higher levels of apathy unawareness (as demonstrated by the discrepancy score) as DAN segregation increased (β: −19.68, p = 0.04). As SAN segregation values increased, MBI participants were reported to have less apathy by study partners (β: 22.09, p = 0.007). For individuals with CI, study self‐reported apathy displayed a marginal association with global segregation, with less global segregation corresponding to higher apathy; in contrast, CU individuals (treated as the reference group) were observed to have consistently low apathy across the spectrum of global segregation (β: −1.8, p = 0.828); this slope was marginally moderated for those labeled CI (β: 37.1, p = 0.077).

MBI group models

Reduced segregation in the DAN was significantly associated with greater apathy unawareness/discrepancy (β: −14.0, p = 0.02) and marginally associated with greater study partner–reported apathy (β: −12.0, p = 0.095; see Table S1 in supporting information). There were no other significant associations.

CI group models

Higher segregation in the SAN was significantly associated with greater study partner–reported apathy (β: 25.0, p = 0.02) (see Table S2 in supporting information). There were no other significant associations.

Adjusting for study partner characteristics

Study partner characteristics included relationship to the participant (family member, friend, spouse/partner, or other) and whether the study partner lived with the participant. Study partners who lived with participants reported a higher level of apathy in the participants. Compared to family members, spouses and friends of participants reported less apathy in the participants. When these covariates were added to the models across the full sample reported in Table 3 corresponding to AES scores dependent on study partner responses, the results were comparable to the prior models, with slightly better model performance. There were still significant associations with the DAN and SAN and apathy, while additional associations were observed with the MVN and somatosensory network in individuals with CI (see Table S3 in supporting information for expanded results).

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we explored the association between network connectivity and self‐reported apathy, study partner–reported apathy, and the discrepancy between the two across the AD continuum. When examining the sample as a whole, we found that higher SAN connectivity was associated with less apathy and that higher FPCN connectivity was associated with greater apathy and reduced awareness of apathy. However, the FPCN effects were not significant by diagnostic group. When accounting for diagnostic group, we found that higher DAN connectivity was associated with greater apathy and reduced awareness of apathy in individuals with CI and that higher SAN connectivity was associated with less apathy in participants with MBI.

These findings extend prior work linking apathy to large‐scale network dysfunction, 4 , 17 , 18 , 27 by showing that segregation differences relate to both symptom severity and awareness of symptoms suggesting that organizational features of large‐scale networks may offer a more sensitive window into how motivational processes decline across the AD continuum. The diagnostic‐specific patterns observed here further show the value of examining network‐level segregation. DAN and SAN segregation showed group‐dependent associations with apathy and apathy awareness that were not evident across the whole sample. This pattern suggests that network‐specific segregation may be more sensitive to subtle motivational changes that differ across clinical stages.

Individuals with apathy frequently show reduced self‐initiated responses to external stimuli. 17 Its presence is associated with higher caregiver burden, 4 and impairment of activities of daily living. 28 These symptoms are believed to stem from disruptions in fronto‐subcortical brain regions responsible for motivation and its translation into action. 4 , 18 , 27 Although structural and metabolic alterations in regions such as the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, temporal lobes, and lateral parietal cortex have been associated with apathy, 18 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 the corresponding changes in functional connectivity are not yet well understood. Recent advances in neuroimaging have pointed toward alterations in large‐scale brain network connectivity as a critical substrate for apathy, implicating disruptions in the communication and specialization of distributed cortical and subcortical systems involved in cognitive and affective regulation. 6

Rs‐fMRI assesses cerebral functional connectivity by measuring time‐varying neuronal activity across spatially distant brain regions. 33 Rs‐fMRI studies have identified associations between apathy in AD dementia and connectivity within the DMN, FPCN, and SAN, though findings have varied across studies. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Our findings from the current study across the AD continuum are mostly in line with these prior studies, implicating the SAN, DAN, and FPCN. In the current study, the associations we observed were driven by participants with CI and those with MBI.

MBI is a novel syndrome that is a behavioral complement to cognitive stages. Symptoms of MBI are divided into five domains, including apathy or decreased motivation, affective dysregulation, impulse dyscontrol, social inappropriateness, and abnormal perception or thought. 16 MBI frequently co‐occurs with MCI and subjective cognitive decline and is associated with a heightened risk of dementia. 11 , 34 Neuroimaging evidence suggests that MBI may reflect early AD changes in the brain, with atrophy in medial temporal structures and reduced connectivity in large‐scale networks such as the DMN, FPCN, and SAN. 16

In a study 21 in individuals with MCI, greater affective symptoms were significantly associated with reduced connectivity in the FPCN. Secondary analyses further revealed that greater apathy was linked to reduced FPCN connectivity, with no significant associations observed in other networks. In another study, 22 reduced connectivity in the medial prefrontal cortex within the anterior DMN was significantly correlated with hyperactivity syndrome, which includes symptoms such as agitation, irritability, aberrant motor behavior, euphoria, and disinhibition. Furthermore, there is increasing evidence that NPS are linked to dysfunction in specific networks, 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 with overlap between proposed networks and conditions. 31

In the present study, study partner–rated apathy scores were more strongly associated with functional connectivity measures than self‐rated scores, highlighting that individuals experiencing behavioral or cognitive difficulties may have limited awareness of their own symptoms. This pattern is consistent with prior literature that reduced insight into NPS is a common feature in aging and neurodegenerative diseases. 5 , 6 , 12 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42

Together, these findings suggest that alterations in network segregation may underlie behavioral manifestations of apathy and that the perspective of study partners may capture clinically relevant patterns of motivational decline that may not be accessible through self‐report alone. By linking segregation to the discrepancy between self‐ and study partner report, this work introduces a previously underexplored network mechanism that helps explain why self‐ and study partner reports diverge in apathy.

Sex also emerged as a significant covariate in study partner models, but not in self‐ or discrepancy models, with males predicted to have a score ≈ 4 points lower than females, indicating greater reported apathy. This finding may reflect subtle sex‐related differences in the expression or recognition of motivational changes, as reported by study partners and has been noted in prior studies. 13

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the current study. The sample size was small, particularly for subgroup analyses, which may have limited statistical power to detect smaller effects. In models including interaction terms, collinearity between the diagnostic group variable and the interaction term was noted as expected. There appeared to be a slightly wider variance along the margins of the AES score ranges, and these extremes of the scales also tended to have less data available. This would likely be improved with a larger sample of data to draw from. Moreover, in the context of the small sample size, the nature of our analyses was exploratory, and we did not correct for multiple comparisons. The cross‐sectional design also makes it unclear whether changes in network organization precede or follow the development of apathy symptoms. Although key covariates were included, other factors such as medication use and affective symptoms could also play a role in lack of motivation and should be explored in future analyses with larger samples. In addition, study partner–reported apathy, while valuable for capturing behavioral changes, may be affected by care partner burden or differences in perception. Expanding this line of work through larger, longitudinal, and multimodal studies is critical to understanding how functional and structural network changes contribute to apathy and to clarifying how these relationships evolve across stages of cognitive decline and behavioral impairment.

In conclusion, our exploratory study showed that network‐level connectivity disruptions primarily affecting frontal and parietal circuits appear to be linked to apathy and reduced awareness of apathy across the AD continuum. Although preliminary, these network patterns may carry meaningful clinical implications. If replicated in larger longitudinal studies, segregation‐based markers of apathy and reduced awareness of apathy could help identify individuals at risk for AD earlier and through less invasive methods than positron emission tomography or lumbar punctures. As anti‐amyloid and other disease‐modifying therapies increasingly target prodromal stages of AD, detecting motivational changes through network features could help clinicians in distinguishing neurodegenerative apathy from psychiatric conditions and support timely referrals for emerging early‐stage AD treatments, which increasingly require recognition of symptoms before significant cognitive decline.
