The Dynamic Management of Working Memory Is Supported by Aperiodic Neural Activity
Authors: Hongchi Zhang, Anyla Konjusha, Shijing Yu, Moritz Mückschel, Bernhard Hommel, Lorenza Colzato, Christian Beste
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT“Metacontrol” refers to the ability to achieve an adaptive balance between more persistent and more flexible cognitive‐control styles. Recent evidence from tasks focusing on the regulation of response conflict and of switching between tasks suggests a consistent relationship between aperiodic EEG activity and task conditions that are likely to elicit a more persistent versus more flexible control style. Here we investigated whether this relationship between metacontrol and aperiodic activity can also be demonstrated for working memory (WM). We analyzed EEG and behavioral outcomes from two independent samples performing the reference‐back task, providing an internal replication of the obtained findings. In both studies we found significant increases in the aperiodic exponent when new information needs to be taken in, showing that the updating of WM is likely associated with a metacontrol bias towards persistence. This observation demonstrates a role of aperiodic activity and/or mechanisms associated with changes in this activity in a memory task, which suggests that the relationship between metacontrol and aperiodic activity extends beyond tasks with particular response‐selection demands. Further, metacontrol adjustments do not seem to create particular states that differ in aperiodic activity, but rather to bias the way selections are carried out, presumably by reducing aperiodic activity whenever the selection is particularly challenging. We advocate that the aperiodic activity observed in EEG signals represents a valid indicator of the neural dynamics underlying metacontrol, portraying the brain's inherent potential to self‐reorganize and alter neural functions to mutable environmental conditions.Keywords: aperiodic exponent, aperiodic neural activity, EEG, metacontrol, working memory

Introduction

Adaptive behavior in the real world often faces a control dilemma (Beste et al. 2018; Goschke 2000; Goschke and Bolte 2014; Hommel and Colzato 2017): While some situations call for a persistent, highly focused cognitive‐control style, such as when dealing with distracting yet irrelevant information, other situations necessitate a more flexible, open, and associative control style, particularly under uncertainty. The ability to balance one's cognitive‐control style between persistence and flexibility is known as “metacontrol” (Hommel 2015; Hommel and Colzato 2017; Hommel et al. 2024). According to the Metacontrol State Model (MSM: Hommel 2015), a bias towards persistence reflects an intense focus on the current goal, strong competition between alternative stimulus or response representations, and the exclusive processing of task‐relevant information, whereas a bias towards flexibility entails a looser focus, weaker competition, and openness to nominally task‐irrelevant information. There is indeed increasing evidence that metacontrol biases vary systematically with the specific demands of the task at hand (Hommel and Colzato 2017; Mekern et al. 2019; Pi et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2022).

Recent electrophysiological research has provided evidence that aperiodic neural activity, which refers to non‐oscillatory EEG activity, is relevant for cognitive control (Gao et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024; Monchy et al. 2024; Pertermann, Bluschke, et al. 2019; Pertermann, Mückschel, et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). Even though the aperiodic component is traditionally considered as either mere noise or an unwanted variable that should be ignored or corrected for (Groppe et al. 2013; Gyurkovics et al. 2021), recent evidence has revealed that aperiodic activity is systematically related to arousal level (Lendner et al. 2020), task performance (He et al. 2010; Podvalny et al. 2015), and cognitive‐control processes like inhibition control (Pertermann, Mückschel, et al. 2019). Notably, the power spectrum is characterized by a distribution that exhibits a decline in spectral power as frequency increases (Donoghue et al. 2020; He 2014; Pritchard 1992). The aperiodic dynamics can be reliably characterized by the aperiodic exponent, which corresponds to the negative 1/f slope of the logarithmic transformation of the power spectrum—that is, the rate or steepness of power decrease across frequencies (Donoghue et al. 2020).

Importantly for our purposes, the aperiodic exponent may serve as an indicator of “neural variability,” that is, the brain's ability to flexibly adjust its neural activity in response to situational demands (Waschke et al. 2021), and of the “E/I ratio” This term is used to express the relationship between excitatory and inhibitory neural processes in the brain, where a relative dominance of excitatory processes implies greater variability and spreading of neural activity, and a relative dominance of inhibitory processes implies less variability and more focus. It is assumed that measures of aperiodic brain activity can indicate the present E/I ratio (Turri et al. 2023), so that a higher exponent (indicating a “steeper” spectrum) signifies a relative dominance of inhibition over excitation, while a lower exponent indicates the reverse (Gao et al. 2017; Lombardi et al. 2017). Indeed, higher levels of excitation have been linked to a reduction in the coordination among rhythmic brain cycles and neural firing (i.e., reduced periodic activity), resulting in increased levels of (not necessarily dysfunctional!) neural noise that might reduce neural communication (Voytek and Knight 2015).

These considerations reveal a strong conceptual overlap between E/I ratio and aperiodic activity on the one hand and metacontrol on the other: A metacontrol bias towards persistence implies comparatively less neural variability and a stronger focus of neural processing (Hommel and Colzato 2017), which in turn implies a dominance of inhibition and, thus, a high aperiodic exponent, whereas a metacontrol bias towards flexibility implies more neural variability and spread of activation, which in turn implies a dominance of excitation and, thus, a low aperiodic exponent. In other words, a high aperiodic exponent is likely to reflect a metacontrol bias towards persistence and a low aperiodic exponent a bias towards flexibility. Consistent with this assumption, the aperiodic exponent was shown to vary systematically with task conditions that can be assumed to induce different metacontrol states (Pi et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023): the exponent increases during persistence‐heavy tasks but decreases during flexibility‐heavy tasks. This supports the idea that the aperiodic exponent may serve as a reflection and, thus, as a proxy of metacontrol biases towards persistence and flexibility. Along similar lines, Zhang et al. (2022) have related neural variability in resting‐state fMRI to conflict monitoring. However, the concept of metacontrol is much broader than just being concerned with the regulation of response conflict and of switching between tasks, which raises the question of whether the systematic relationship between the aperiodic exponent and metacontrol can be demonstrated beyond typical response‐conflict and task‐switching paradigms.

To address this question, the primary objective of the present study was to apply the metacontrol concept and measurement of aperiodic activity to working memory (WM). The control of WM has also been claimed to face a dilemma that can be couched in terms of persistence and flexibility (Nir‐Cohen et al. 2020). Namely, under some circumstances, the content of WM needs to be maintained and shielded from other information that is currently processed (functions that are assumed to be supported by metacontrol persistence), such as when keeping a telephone number in mind. While, under other circumstances, WM needs to be opened to take up new information for maintenance (functions that are assumed to be supported by metacontrol flexibility), such as when facing an unexpected or particularly interesting stimulus. To properly regulate the state of one's WM thus relies on the ability to open or close the gate to WM, depending on task demands (O'Reilly and Frank 2006). Interestingly for our purposes, Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler (2016b) have developed the reference‐back task, which provides separate measures for people's opening and closing performance in handling their WM. In contrast to the standard version of this task (the n‐back task), the reference‐back task entails baseline trials for comparison (Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler 2018), which allows the calculation of gate‐opening and gate‐closing indicators (see the method section for more details). So far, several studies focusing on oscillatory activity showed WM gate opening and closing effects (Konjusha et al. 2023; Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler 2016b, 2016a; Rempel et al. 2021; Yu, Mückschel, et al. 2022; Yu, Rempel, et al. 2022).

To investigate the role of metacontrol as approximated through the aperiodic component of the EEG during WM gating, we analyzed two independent samples (Study 1 and Study 2), which were originally studied for other scientific purposes (Konjusha et al. 2023; Yu, Rempel, et al. 2022). We employed the spectral parameterization approach (Fitting Oscillations and One Over f [FOOOF]) (Donoghue et al. 2020), similar to previous studies using event‐related neurophysiological data (Adelhöfer, Paulus, et al. 2021; Pi et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023) to estimate aperiodic exponents for WM opening and closing performance (see Donoghue et al. 2020). Study 2 served as an independent replication of the findings from Study 1 to assess their robustness. Given that neural processes supporting task performance can also be affected by neural activity prior to the onset of the task‐relevant stimulus (i.e., in the pre‐trial period; Adelhöfer, Bluschke, et al. 2021; Adelhöfer and Beste 2020; Prochnow et al. 2022; Wendiggensen et al. 2022), we also compared the exponents for opening‐ and closing‐related performance in the (theoretically relevant) within‐trial period to the exponents of activity in the (theoretically irrelevant) pre‐trial period. This comparison provided us with two types of information: First, it allowed us to strip the task‐specific levels of the aperiodic exponent from random fluctuations. Given that the stimulus specified whether the gate would need to be opened or closed, only the within‐trial period would contain the information we were interested in. Second, it allowed us to identify whether the expected changes from pre‐trial to within‐trial periods would consist of increases of the exponent in one condition, decreases in another, or both. In that sense, the exponents from the pre‐trial period provided us with a neutral baseline.

Our predictions were motivated by two different scenarios: According to what one may call the state scenario, metacontrol operates by creating neural states that support opening and closing, or other functions that opening and closing may rely on. This scenario would suggest a result pattern in which gate closing is accompanied by an increase of the aperiodic exponent, as compared to pre‐trial exponents (indicating a lower degree of aperiodic activity [Donoghue et al. 2020], implying more persistence, inhibitory dominance, and less neural variability), while gate opening is accompanied by a decrease of the exponent. The reasoning here is that metacontrol would be directly involved in the opening/closing process and, thus, reflect the nature of this process (persistence = closing, flexibility = opening). The state scenario is consistent with previous observations that performing a task that relies on persistence can reduce the crosstalk between different kinds of information in another, overlapping task (Fischer and Hommel 2012). However, metacontrol may also operate according to a process scenario. Considerations regarding the computational characteristics of metacontrol persistence versus flexibility have focused on selection situations, in which either an overt response or some internal representation needs to be selected (Hommel and Colzato 2017; Hommel and Wiers 2017; Hommel et al. 2024). Hence, metacontrol might not operate by creating particular brain states that differ in aperiodic activity per se but, rather, by enabling particular processing modes that come into play when representations need to be selected for further processing. If so, a different result pattern would be predicted. Given that gate closing requires maintenance, but not the selection of new stimuli for memory storage, whereas gate opening does require the selection of a new stimulus, changes in aperiodic exponents might be obtained for the gate‐opening condition only, but not in the gate‐closing condition. Hence, according to this scenario, we should expect an increase of the aperiodic exponent for gate opening, but no decrease of the exponent for gate closing. Note that the reasoning here is that metacontrol would not be involved in the opening/closing process but, rather, serve to deal with the consequences that opening/closing has for the selection process. While closing the gate would not yield consequences for selection, opening increases the competition for selection by bringing alternative candidates into play. Accordingly, opening would benefit from increased persistence, as the persistence‐typical increase of top‐down regulation and mutual inhibition between alternative candidates facilitates and speeds up the selection process.

Study 1

Methods

Participants

Study 1 made use of data that were previously gathered for different scientific purposes from a cohort of 43 participants (the neutral sham condition from Konjusha et al. 2023). In the original study by Konjusha et al. 2023, a total of six participants were excluded for the following reasons: performance was below 50% accuracy, there were issues with EEG signal quality (void electrode channels during preprocessing), the number of EEG trials was insufficient, or there were fewer than 10 valid eye tracking trials detected in at least one task condition during one of the appointments. Therefore, 37 participants were included in the analysis (19 males; mean age 25.09 ± 3.60 years). All participants were right‐handed and had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision and had no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participation was granted only after participants gave informed written consent, and it was ensured that they had never participated in a similar study before. This research was conducted in accordance with the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the TU Dresden and following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Task Design

The Reference Back Task, a modified version of the N‐Back Task, was employed to investigate the gating mechanisms of WM processes (Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler 2016a). In this task, participants were presented with a series of letter stimuli (“X” or “O”) framed in either blue or red. The participants were required to determine whether the currently presented letter stimulus was the “same” (match) or “different” (mismatch) from the previous letter stimulus with a red frame, see the higher panel of Figure 1. This required participants to continuously store the previous red‐framed stimulus in WM until a new red‐framed stimulus appeared for updating. If the letters were identical, participants were instructed to press the right “Ctrl” button; whereas if the letters were distinct, they were instructed to press the left “Ctrl” button. Thus, the Reference Back Task consisted of two types of trials: comparison trials, where the blue‐framed stimulus was only used for comparison with the previous red‐framed stimulus without involving WM updating, reflecting the matching decision process; and reference trials, where red‐framed stimuli were used as both the reference and for comparison with the previous red‐framed stimulus, involving both matching and WM updating processes. Furthermore, trials in which the frame color was the same as the previous trial were considered no‐switch trials, whereas trials in which the frame color was different from the previous trial were considered switch trials. In short, the trials can be divided into 8 types based on different conditions. Gate opening is computed by subtracting the performance on reference trials that are preceded by comparison trials (switch trials) from the performance on reference trials that are preceded by other reference trials (i.e., no‐switch trials). Conversely, gate closing is determined by comparing the performance on comparison trials that follow reference trials (switch trials) with the performance on comparison trials that follow other comparison trials (no‐switch trials), see the low panel of Figure 1.

Each trial began with the presentation of a fixation cross “+” in the center of the screen for 800 ms; this was jittered between 600 and 1200 ms, followed by the presentation of a letter stimulus (“X” or “O”) with a red or blue frame for 1400 ms or until a response was made. Subsequently, the screen turned blank for 1000 ms before the next trial. This process continued until the end of a block cycle. Prior to the formal experiment, the participants received a detailed explanation of the task and practiced in 30 trials to ensure their familiarity with the task. The experiment was divided into 16 blocks, with each block containing 60 trials, resulting in a total of 960 trials. The whole experiment lasted about 1 h, and switch trials accounted for 25% of the trials, while no‐switch trials accounted for 75% of the trials. It should be noted that the first trial of each block was always a reference trial (red‐framed) for which no response was required from the participant. Besides, both the distribution of frame colors and the button press responses were counterbalanced between participants.

EEG Recording and Processing

The EEG data was recorded using QuickAmp and BrainAmp amplifiers (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany) from 60 Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes based on the equidistant M10 layout system. The ground and reference electrode coordinates were located at θ = 58, Φ = 78, and θ = 90, Φ = 90, respectively. All electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. The data was sampled at a rate of 500 Hz using the BrainVision Recorder program (Brain Products) and subsequently downsampled offline to 256 Hz. For the preprocessing of the raw EEG data, we employed the “Automagic” toolbox (Pedroni et al. 2019) and EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig 2004) within MATLAB 2019a (The MathWorks). In the initial steps, we removed any flat channels and then performed re‐referencing of the EEG data to an average reference. Subsequently, we applied both the PREP preprocessing pipeline (Bigdely‐Shamlo et al. 2015) and the clean_rawdata() pipeline in EEGLAB. With PREP, we employed a multitaper algorithm to eliminate line noise at 50 Hz and generated a robust average reference by effectively handling any contamination from bad channels. The clean_rawdata() pipeline employed an FIR highpass filter of 0.5 Hz (order 1286, stop‐band attenuation 80 dB, transition band 0.25–0.75 Hz) for detrending the EEG data. Additionally, it automatically identified and removed any flat‐line, noisy, or outlier channels. Epochs that exhibited abnormally high power (more than 15 standard deviations compared to the calibration data) were reconstructed using a technique called Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR; using a burst criterion of 15, as described by Mullen et al. 2013). Time windows that could not be successfully reconstructed were excluded from further analysis. Additionally, a lowpass filter with a cutoff frequency of 40 Hz (implemented as a sinc finite impulse response filter with an order of 86, according to Widmann et al. 2015) was applied to the data. To further remove artifacts, an independent component analysis (ICA) was conducted. This process helped eliminate artifacts caused by muscle activity, cardiac signals, loose electrodes, and residual eye movements. The identification of components containing artifacts was achieved through automatic classification using two methods: the Multiple Artifact Rejection Algorithm (MARA; described by Winkler et al. 2011, 2014) and ICLabel (developed by Pion‐Tonachini et al. 2019). These identified artifact components were subsequently removed from the data. Finally, any channels that were removed during artifact removal were interpolated using a spherical method. Epochs, time‐locked to the stimulus, were extracted with an interval of 1000 ms preceding and 3000 ms following the stimulus onset. Only epochs with correct responses within a response time window of 1400 ms were included in the analysis. Segments were built for each condition separately, including switch_match_reference, switch_mismatch_reference, no‐switch_match_reference, no‐switch_mismatch_reference, switch_match_comparison, switch_mismatch_comparison, no‐switch_match_comparison, no‐switch_mismatch_comparison.

Parameterization of the Spectral Data

Two time windows of interest were selected: −1000 to 0 ms (pre‐trial period, preceding the stimulus) and 0 to 1000 ms (within‐trial period, following stimulus onset). According to previous work (Welch 1967; 0.25 s Hamming window, 50% overlap), the power spectral density (PSD) for each frequency was calculated using the ‘pwelch’ function in Matlab (version R2018a). The PSDs were estimated separately for each participant, electrode, condition (gate opening/gate closing), and period (pre‐trial/within‐trial). The Python‐based FOOOF toolbox (version 1.0.0; https://github.com/fooof‐tools/fooof) was used to parameterize the power spectra by decomposing and extracting the aperiodic and periodic components of the EEG (Adelhöfer, Paulus, et al. 2021; Donoghue et al. 2020; Pi et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024). Given the presence of ERPs in the post‐event period, we subtracted the ERPs from the single‐trial activity in the time domain. These post‐minus‐ERP difference spectra were then subsequently deconstructed by FOOOF, and their exponent values were stored, like in previous studies (Gyurkovics et al. 2022; Kałamała et al. 2024). The FOOOF algorithm interprets the power spectrum by breaking it down into two parts: aperiodic activity [L(f)] and periodic (oscillatory) activity [Gn(f)]. Each spectrum was then fit across the frequency range [2, 30], with settings{peak_width_limits = [2, 8], max_n_peaks = 8, min_peak_height = 0.05, aperiodic_mode = ‘fixed’}, providing an aperiodic exponent value for each electrode, task condition, participant, and period. Subsequently, we conducted spectral model fitting using Python software. The average R 2 of spectral fits for all 37 participants was above 0.98, indicating good fits.

Even though the aperiodic exponent and the offset are correlated (Zhang et al. 2023), we restricted our analysis to the exponent given that the exponent, compared to the offset, is theoretically more transparent in its interpretation to index metacontrol states. That is, as pointed out by Turri et al. (2023), the aperiodic exponent but not the offset (i.e., higher broadband power) can be mechanistically interpreted in terms of decreased or increased E/I ratio (Gao et al. 2017; Lombardi et al. 2017). Since there were no preconceived assumptions about how the aperiodic neural activity is distributed across the scalp, we employed the “global” exponent for the statistical analysis as suggested by Hill et al. (2022). First, we averaged the exponent values across 60 electrodes for each participant (Hill et al. 2022) to observe the overall trend of variation at different periods between the processes of WM. Second, in order to examine the distribution of the aperiodic components on the scalp, we conducted an extra cluster‐based permutation test, which yielded statistically significant findings at the global level. The non‐parametric cluster‐based permutation test is a method proposed to localize effects in space, frequency, and time while correcting for multiple comparisons in high‐dimensional EEG/MEG data (Maris and Oostenveld 2007). In this study, clusters were formed based on the adjacency of thresholded sample‐level F‐values (α = 0.05), and the sum of F‐values in a cluster was used as the cluster‐level statistics. Significant clusters were determined based on 1000 Monte Carlo random sampling using a significance level of 0.05.

Statistical Analysis

The behavioral data and aperiodic exponent data were analyzed using SPSS software (IBM, version: 26.0). Related‐samples Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were conducted to analyze the behavioral data, including accuracy and reaction times (RTs), and the aperiodic exponent data (only trials with correct responses were used for the further analysis of the aperiodic exponent) to investigate the differences between the gate opening and gate closing processes. JASP software (version 0.17.0) was also used to calculate the Bayes factor (BF10). A BF10 of 1 indicates no evidence supporting the hypothesis; the larger the BF10, the stronger the support for the alternative hypothesis, while the smaller the BF10, the stronger the support for the null hypothesis. For all descriptive statistics, the mean and the standard error of the mean (SEM) are reported.

Results

Behavioral Results

Behavioral data have already been reported by Konjusha et al. (2023). The accuracy and RTs of gate opening and gate closing were compared using the related‐samples Wilcoxon signed‐rank test; see Figure 2. Replicating previous findings (Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler 2016a, 2016b, 2018; Rempel et al. 2021), the analysis revealed that the accuracy in gate closing (−7.8 × 10−3 ± 1.37 × 10−2) was significantly higher than that of gate opening (−5.29 × 10−2 ± 1.32 × 10−2), (Z = −2.27, p = 0.02, r = 0.37, BF 10 = 4.96), while the RTs in gate closing (131 ± 14 ms) were significantly slower than those in gate opening (80 ± 10 ms), (Z = −2.92, p = 0.004, r = 0.48, BF 10 = 41.03), which indicates a trade‐off effect between accuracy and speed.

Moreover, a supplementary Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was conducted to further examine the specific conditions contributing to the difference between gate opening and gate closing in the aperiodic exponent during the specified period. The analysis revealed no significant difference between the no‐switch_match_comparison and no‐switch_match_reference conditions, as well as the no‐switch_mismatch_comparison and no‐switch_mismatch_reference conditions (Z = −0.85, p = 0.39, BF 10 = 0.26; Z = −0.99, p = 0.32, BF 10 = 0.39). Conversely, the aperiodic exponent exhibited higher values for the switch_match_reference and switch_mismatch_reference conditions compared to the switch_match_comparison and switch_mismatch_comparison conditions, respectively (Z = −4.65, p < 0.001, r = 0.76, BF 10 > 1000; Z = −3.48, p = 0.001, r = 0.57, BF 10 = 319.94). These outcomes suggest that the difference between gate opening and gate closing predominantly occurs during switching; see the Supporting Information. To summarize, the analysis of the aperiodic exponent revealed a pattern as predicted by the process scenario described in the introduction, whereas no evidence was obtained for the state scenario.

Cluster‐Based Permutation Test Results

The cluster‐based permutation test on the closing and opening calculations was performed to explore the scalp distribution of aperiodic parameters. The results revealed a significant main effect of “WM gating” on the aperiodic exponent across bilateral frontal, temporal, posterior, and occipital areas (Cluster 1: FP1, AF7, FT7, T7, TP7, P7, O1, FT9, TP9, P9, O9, P11, FC2; Cluster 2: FP2, AF8, FT8, T8, TP8, P8, O2) (F = 9.99, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.12; F = 21.29, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.29). A significant main effect of “time window” on the aperiodic exponent was observed across widespread brain areas (Cluster1: Cz, FCz, FC1; Cluster 2: Fz, F1; Cluster 3: T7, TP7; Cluster 4: TP9, P9; Cluster5: P11, FC2, CP2, CPz, F2, FC4, C4, CP4) (Fs > 13.16, ps < 0.04, all ηp2 > 0.22). Study 2

Methods

Participants

Study 2 provides a replication sample of Study 1. It used another data set previously collected in a cohort of 38 participants while performing the task in the first session (Yu, Rempel, et al. 2022). In the original study by Yu, Rempel, et al. (2022), a total of seven participants were excluded for the following reasons: Six participants (N = 6) were excluded because they did not complete the experiment, and one participant (N = 1) was excluded due to incomplete data resulting from technical issues (pupil data not saved). Additionally, for the current analysis, one participant was excluded for exhibiting a FOOOF exponent that was beyond two standard deviations from the mean. Therefore, 30 participants were included in the analysis (12 males; mean age 25.74 ± 2.53 years). All participants were right‐handed and had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision and had no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participation was granted only after participants gave informed written consent, and it was ensured that they have never participated in a similar study before. This research was conducted in accordance with the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the TU Dresden and following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Task Design

The design was identical to Study 1, except for the adjustment in the duration of stimulus presentation and the number of trials. The duration of the fixation cross “+” and the stimulus were from 800 1400 ms to a range of 600 to 1000 ms and 1500 ms, respectively. The ratio of switch trials to non‐switch trials is 30:70. Besides, the number of trials was reduced from 960 to 900, and blocks are no longer divided, but a short break of 10 s was included every 180 trials to prevent fatigue. All other procedures were as in Study 1.

EEG Recording and Processing

The EEG processing was performed using the BrainVision Analyzer 2 software package (Brain Products Inc.) and included the following steps: First, the EEG signals were downsampled to 256 Hz. Subsequently, infinite impulse response filters with a slope of 48 dB/octave were applied within the frequency range of 0.5 to 40 Hz, accompanied by an additional notch filter at 50 Hz. Defective electrode channels were then discarded, and a new reference was calculated from the remaining channels. Independent component analysis (using the infomax Gradient Restricted algorithm) was employed to remove regular artifacts such as eye movements and pulses, while irregular artifacts, such as technical noises, were manually inspected and removed from the raw data. Following this, previously discarded channels were interpolated using PCA conventional spherical splines from neighboring electrodes (convergence bound: 1e‐07; number of steps: 512). Furthermore, for each trial, an automatic artifact rejection process was implemented to eliminate any remaining artifacts based on the following criteria: a maximum allowed amplitude step of 50 μV/ms, a maximum value difference exceeding 150 μV within a 200 ms interval, minimum amplitude < −100 μV or maximum amplitude > 100 μV, or an activity (max–min) < 0.5 μV within a 200 ms interval.

Parameters

All parameters for the analysis remained the same as in Study 1. The average number of correct and valid trials per condition was over 50. Behavioral Results

The behavioral data were analyzed as in Study 1. The accuracy and RTs of gate opening and gate closing were compared using related‐samples Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. General Discussion

The primary objective of this current study was to test whether the previously observed association between metacontrol and aperiodic activity can be extended from response selection and task switching, which in previous studies was the target of the analyses, to selection in and for working memory. To accomplish this goal, we assessed the aperiodic activity in the EEG power spectrum during the reference‐back task. This task allowed us to calculate indices for gate opening and gate closing separately, both in the pre‐trial period and within trial period. We utilized a cluster‐based permutation test to investigate the distribution of the aperiodic exponent across the scalp. To ensure the reliability of our findings, we conducted an independent replication of Study 1 in Study 2. Several outcomes of our analyses are noteworthy.

First, we found a clear differentiation between the two WM conditions. In both studies, the aperiodic exponents did not differ from pre‐trial to within‐trial periods in the gate‐closing conditions, but they did so in the gate‐opening conditions. More specifically, the aperiodic exponent showed a significant increase when new information needed to be taken in, suggesting that the updating of WM is associated with a metacontrol bias towards persistence, which in turn implies a dominance of inhibition and reduced neural variability. This observation suggests an important link between (changes in) aperiodic activity and cognitive operations in a memory task, which in turn shows that the association between metacontrol and aperiodic activity extends beyond tasks with particular response‐selection or task‐switching demands. Hence, the main question that motivated the present study can be answered affirmatively: metacontrol demands do not need to refer to the selection of an overt response to induce changes in aperiodic activity.

Second, the pattern of our outcomes does not support the state scenario but is fully consistent with the process scenario. Hence, metacontrol adjustments do not seem to create particular states that differ in neural aperiodic activity, so to support particular kinds of cognitive activities. More specifically, our findings do not support the idea that metacontrol may moderate aperiodic activity in order to realize working memory opening or closing directly. This idea would have implied that opening comes with a metacontrol bias towards flexibility, which in turn would imply a decrease in the aperiodic exponent, whereas closing would come with a bias towards persistence and an increase in the exponent. In this study, we found no evidence for such a scenario. Rather, our findings suggest that metacontrol is concerned with the consequences of opening and closing. As explained in the introduction, closing would affect the selection of items for WM, whereas opening can be expected to make the selection much harder by bringing in alternative candidates competing for selection. If so, one would expect no effect regarding the aperiodic exponent with closing, but an increase (indicating increased metacontrol persistence) with opening, which is exactly what we observed. Hence, metacontrol seems to deal with selection issues, but not with the opening and closing of WM.

In previous tasks, selection always applied to competing response representations, like in the Go/Nogo task studied by Zhang et al. (2023), Gao et al. (2024), and Pi et al. (2024). In the present two studies, participants also carried out responses, but the two gating conditions did not differ with respect to pure response‐selection demands. Rather, they differed with respect to whether new visual information needs to be taken in. Only if that was the case, aperiodic activity was affected. This suggests that metacontrol impacts the way selections are carried out, presumably by reducing aperiodic activity (as reflected in an increased aperiodic exponent) whenever the selection is particularly challenging. Given that more internal representations compete for selection in the gate‐opening conditions (namely, the previously held content and the to‐be selected and stored new content) than in the gate‐closing conditions, the increase of the exponent (implying a stronger metacontrol bias towards persistence, more inhibitory dominance of the E/I ration, and less neural variability) in the former but not the latter makes perfect sense. Hence, with respect to the second, more explorative question of our study, we conclude that our present findings support the process scenario, according to which metacontrol mainly operates by enabling particular processing modes that come into play when representations need to be selected for further processing.

The disparity between gate opening and closing conditions, as depicted in Figures 3 and 7, appears predominantly within the alpha frequency band. Studies have demonstrated that higher frequency band activity, such as in the gamma frequency band, contributes significantly to 1/f activity. Importantly, alpha activity likely does not exhibit aperiodic behavior (Ouyang et al. 2020). Consequently, there is no discernible link between 1/f activity and alpha power (Gyurkovics et al. 2021). This implies that analyzing the alpha band in isolation to understand aperiodic effects and to relate such effects to previous findings (Konjusha et al. 2023; Rac‐Lubashevsky and Kessler 2018; Yu et al. 2024) linking alpha frequency band to the paradigm used in the current study is not suitable. Our present findings converge with, and extend previous observations that the aperiodic exponent is sensitive to processing challenges that are calling for increases of persistence or flexibility (Pi et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). Hence, there is increasing evidence that the aperiodic exponent is a valid indicator of metacontrol biases and changes therein. However, it should be noted that other possible approaches to capture the neural underpinnings of metacontrol are available and may be interesting to investigate as well (Van Schependom et al. 2024). In this regard, phase‐based measures may also be of relevance. For individual signals, the degree of regularity versus irregularity can be quantified by the coefficient of phase velocity variation (Espinoso and Andrzejak 2022). Such an intrinsic stability can be captured using the Kuramoto order parameter, which reflects degree of phase‐locking in an ensemble of oscillators at any time. When a network is stable, we expect the Kuramoto order parameter to remain (more or less) constant. However, if other networks start competing, the Kuramoto order parameter may start to increase or decrease. It has generally been considered that synchronization and locking of oscillatory processes in electrophysiological activity are central mechanisms involved in the neural bases of cognition (Beste et al. 2023; Fries 2005; Ward 2003). Yet, locking and synchronization can lead to “maladaptive brains because dynamics becomes locked in a state and shielded from changes in the environment” (Tognoli and Kelso 2014). Since metacontrol is concerned with how individuals can change between opposing states, measures assessing the criticality of neurophysiological networks through phase‐based measures or non‐linear dynamics may be an important new avenue in future studies.

The findings by Gyurkovics et al. (2021) indicate that 1/f activity and alpha power are not positively correlated, supporting an additive model rather than a multiplicative model. This distinction is crucial, as the metacontrol framework (Hommel and Colzato 2017) suggests that 1/f activity plays a role in the dynamic shift between persistence‐heavy and flexibility‐heavy processing. If, as Gyurkovics et al. (2021) propose, dB conversion systematically distorts interaction effects, this could lead to misinterpretations of cognitive control dynamics, including those involved in metacontrol processes. Therefore, since we applied multiple baseline correction strategies to ERPs, following the recommendations of Gyurkovics et al. (2021), we are confident in the validity of our findings.

We acknowledge that this is only a first step towards a more comprehensive selection theory of metacontrol and its association with aperiodic activity. Not only are there other WM tasks in which selection needs to take place, but also other types of memory from which content needs to be selected in order to perform everyday actions. Whether the association between metacontrol and aperiodic activity can also be demonstrated for these other tasks remains to be seen. Nevertheless, the outcome of the present study is encouraging, in the sense that selection of internal representations and the selection of overt actions seem comparable to some degree. A limitation of our study is that FOOOF only allows for the selection of a specific time window (e.g., pre‐trial: 1000 ms before the stimulus; within‐trial: 1000 ms post‐stimulus) to calculate the aperiodic exponent. Future studies might explore the link between working WM and aperiodic activity over time using Spectral Parameterization Resolved in Time (SPRiNT: Wilson et al. 2022). This new method can observe changes in aperiodic parameters over time and may be helpful for further elucidating the dynamics and time course of WM gating and closing.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the pivotal role of aperiodic activity and its associated mechanisms in memory tasks, indicating that the relationship between metacontrol and aperiodic activity transcends tasks with specific response‐selection demands. Notably, our study reveals that metacontrol adjustments do not generate distinct states characterized by different levels of aperiodic activity; rather, they influence the execution of selections by diminishing aperiodic activity in the context of particularly challenging decisions. We propose that the aperiodic activity captured through EEG signals serves as a robust indicator of the underlying neural dynamics of metacontrol, illustrating the brain's remarkable capacity to self‐reorganize and adapt its neural functions to changing environmental conditions. These insights enrich our understanding of cognitive processes and the dynamic nature of neural activity in relation to metacognitive control.
