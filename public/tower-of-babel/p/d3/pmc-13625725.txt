Expanding the Medicinal Chemistry Toolbox for PET Imaging: Aliphatic 11C/18F‑Trifluoromethylation as a Late-Stage Strategy
Authors: Xin Zhou, Yinlong Li, Steven H. Liang
Published: 2026

Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging occupies a unique position at the interface of chemistry, biology, and clinical medicine. Its ability to noninvasively quantify biochemical processes in vivo has made PET indispensable in neuroscience, oncology, and drug development. − In practice, most clinically used PET radiotracers are derived from a small set of radiochemical transformations, dominated by 11C-methylation and nucleophilic 18F-fluorination of simple alkyl or aryl precursors. − Contemporary drug discovery frequently employs fluorinated motifs, especially CF3 groups to optimize pharmacokinetics and binding properties. (Figure A) − CF3 substitution has been exploited to block oxidative metabolism, modulate pK a and conformational preferences, and improve membrane permeability and target residence time. , Yet many CF3-containing drugs and tool compounds remain “PET-inaccessible” because their structural features cannot be readily radiolabeled without extensive scaffold redesign. The lack of general strategies for forming aliphatic C­(sp3)–[11C]/[18F]­CF3 bonds has therefore imposed a structural bias on PET tracer discovery, often favoring radiochemical feasibility over biological relevance. Aliphatic [18F]­CF3 groups are conceptually attractive as metabolically robust surrogates for simpler [18F]­alkyl motifs. ,, Prior approaches including nucleophilic substitution of difluoromethylene precursors or multistep organometallic strategies have suffered from narrow scope, low molar activity, and/or impractical precursor synthesis. − In a recent Science article, Liu, Pike and co-workers reported a general solution to this challenge by merging copper-mediated radical chemistry with practical radiochemistry. The authors introduce two complementary pathways for aliphatic radiotrifluoromethylation: halogen atom abstract of alkyl iodides and photoredox-catalyzed decarboxylative trifluoromethylation of carboxylic acids. (Figure B) Both approaches rely on radiolabeled fluoroform readily accessible in either containing one of the carbon-11 or fluorine-18 atom as a unified CF3 source. It allows the same molecular scaffold to be labeled with either radionuclide, facilitating head-to-head comparison of short-lived 11C-tracers and longer-lived 18F-analogs without extensive synthetic reoptimization. The reported substrate scope spans primary, secondary, and benzylic centers, tolerates heterocycles and unprotected polar functionality, and accommodates complex, pharmacologically relevant molecules.

From a medicinal chemistry standpoint, the most striking feature of this work is its broad substrate scope and functional group tolerance. Figure illustrates the scope and applicability of the developed trifluoromethylation strategy for the radiosynthesis of [11C]­CF3-labeled (Figure A) and [18F]­CF3-labeled (Figure B) compounds from alkyl iodides. In the presence of CuBr, KO t Bu and MesN2BF4, a series of structurally diverse substrates were successfully converted into their corresponding 11C-trifluoromethylated products, including late-stage labeling of pharmaceutically relevant molecules such as indomethacin ([11C]4, 44% yield), tadalafil ([11C]16, 54% yield), tedizolid ([11C]17, 53% yield) and dapagliflozin ([11C]18, 27% yield) derivatives. The reactions proceeded efficiently under mild conditions within 10 min reaction time, delivering the 11C-trifluoromethylated products in moderate to good radiochemical yields with high molar activities. The same protocol could also be extended to fluorine-18 chemistry, enabling the formation of [18F]­CF3-containing analogues from alkyl iodide precursors with comparable efficiency and functional group tolerance, as demonstrated by BAY 59–3074 ([18F]27, 51% yield, outperforming prior organometallic methods) and the NKCC1 inhibitor IAMA-6, which was radiolabeled either as its methyl ester ([18F]28, 75% yield) or free acid ([18F]29, 32% yield). These results demonstrated the generality of this approach and highlighted its utility for rapid late-stage incorporation of PET-relevant trifluoromethyl motifs into complex molecular frameworks. By enabling late-stage installation of CF3 groups at aliphatic positions, this methodology allows PET tracers to more accurately reflect the modern structures of drug candidates and clinical leads.

Figure depicts the application of the photocatalytic trifluoromethylation protocol to both carbon-11 and fluoroine-18 radiochemistry using [11C]­fluoroform or [18F]­fluoroform as the CF3 source, respectively. Under the optimized conditions, involving CuBr, KOtBu, Ru­(bpy)3Cl2·6H2O, and blue LED irradiation at room temperature, a range of alkyl iodide substrates were efficiently converted to the corresponding [11C]­CF3- or [18F]­CF3-labeled products within 10 min. The methodology enabled direct labeling of heterocycle-containing alkyl chains ([11C]38, 59% yield) as well as benzylic ([11C]40, 88% yield; [11C]43, 17% yield) and aliphatic systems ([11C]44, 84% yield), affording 11C-labeled products in good radiochemical yields with high molar activity. Notably, the same reaction manifold was readily translated to fluorine-18 chemistry and provided [18F]­CF3-containing analogues in comparable radiochemical yields and reproducibility. A series of primary ([18F]37, 69%, 73% yield; [18F]39, 65% yield), secondary ([18F]41, 43% yield) and tertiary ([18F]42, 65%) aliphatic acid were successfully converted to [18F]­CF3-containing compounds. These results highlight the versatility of this unified strategy for rapid incorporation of PET-relevant 11C and 18F-trifluoromethyl motifs using short-lived radionuclides.

Concluding Remarks

The work by Liu, Pike and co-workers reflects a growing trend in PET radiochemistry by embracing radical-based activation and late-stage functionalization. Looking forward, the ability to access matched [11C]/[18F]­CF3 tracers from a single precursor opens new experimental paradigms. For example, rapid same day 11C studies could be used for early target engagement assessment, followed by 18F tracers for longitudinal or multicenter imaging. Such flexibility aligns well with contemporary translational research models and demonstrates that aliphatic C­(sp3)–CF3 radiolabeling can be both general and practical. Continued advances in high-activity [18F]­fluoroform production, automation, and microfluidic photochemistry are likely to push this methodology to a higher place. Despite its conceptual achievements, several limitations may affect the broader adoption of this methodology. There is further optimization opportunity to increase molar activity of [18F]­CF3 products derived from fluoroform-based labeling using different generation methods. In addition, the requirement for radiolabeled gaseous fluoroform introduces operational complexity compared with conventional [18F]­fluoride chemistry, which mandates a simplified workflow suitable for routine clinical translation. In summary, general aliphatic radiotrifluoromethylation represents a meaningful expansion of the medicinal chemist’s imaging toolbox. By reducing the structural gap between drugs and tracers, this chemistry brings PET imaging closer to its full potential as a translational bridge between molecular design and clinical insight.
