Median effective dose (ED50) of esketamine combined with different doses of propofol for children to inhibit the response to gastroscope insertion: a prospective dose–response clinical study
Authors: Keqing Niu, Li Li, Xiaole Wu, Na Xia, Jianqiao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether combining esketamine with different doses of propofol could reduce drug requirements and minimize adverse effects in children undergoing gastroscopy.MethodsEighty-eight children scheduled for painless gastroscopy were randomly assigned to four groups receiving propofol at doses of 1, 2, 3, or 4 mg/kg. Esketamine was administered using the Dixon up-and-down sequential method. Failure was defined as coughing, gagging, or airway obstruction during initial gastroscope insertion. The median effective dose (ED50) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the up-and-down method and probit regression analysis.ResultsThe ED50 of esketamine decreased significantly with increasing doses of propofol. The ED50 of esketamine combined with propofol 4 mg/kg was significantly lower than that observed with propofol doses of 1, 2, and 3 mg/kg (P < 0.001), and the ED50 with propofol 3 mg/kg was significantly lower than that with propofol 1 and 2 mg/kg (P < 0.05). Recovery time was significantly longer in the propofol 2, 3, and 4 mg/kg groups compared with the 1 mg/kg group, and the incidence of hypotension was higher in the propofol 4 mg/kg group. During recovery, children receiving propofol 1 mg/kg experienced a higher incidence of delirium and visual disturbances. No significant differences were observed in examination time, physician satisfaction, or respiratory depression among the groups.ConclusionCombining esketamine with propofol reduced the esketamine dose required for pediatric gastroscopy in a propofol dose-dependent manner. Among the regimens studied, esketamine combined with 2 mg/kg propofol appears to provide a more favorable balance between anesthetic efficacy and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03793-0.Keywords: Children, Gastroscopy, Esketamine, Median effective dose, Dixon sequential method

Background

Endoscopy is a widely used and critical diagnostic and therapeutic modality for managing upper gastrointestinal disorders, including gastric ulcers and early gastric cancer. However, these endoscopic procedures are invasive and often lead to physical stress, such as nausea, vomiting, excessive salivation, elevated blood pressure, and tachycardia [1]. In pediatric gastroscopy, sedative and anesthetic regimens—including deep sedation and general anesthesia—have been widely evaluated and applied to improve patient comfort and procedural tolerance in children undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy [2]. It is crucial to assess the potential risks associated with non-intubated general anesthesia or sedation outside the operating room, particularly given the limitations of non-surgical environments in terms of monitoring equipment and anesthesia procedures. The success of non-intubated general anesthesia or sedation relies on the ability to rapidly induce anesthesia, facilitate swift recovery from the procedure, and minimize complications. This necessitates the use of highly effective and safe anesthetic agents and protocols [3].

Currently, propofol is one of the most commonly used intravenous anesthetics for non-intubated general anesthesia outside the operating room. Owing to its rapid onset, stable anesthetic maintenance, absence of autonomic or muscular excitatory movements, and rapid and complete recovery, propofol is widely used for upper gastrointestinal endoscopic examinations [4]. Although propofol offers numerous advantages over other intravenous anesthetics, it also has certain adverse effects that must be considered. The most common side effect is hypotension, primarily resulting from a reduction in systemic vascular resistance and myocardial depression. Additionally, other undesirable effects, such as respiratory disturbances and injection site pain, are significant concerns [5, 6]. Consequently, there is a need to use adjunct drugs alongside propofol to mitigate these side effects. However, no consensus currently exists regarding the optimal drugs to combine with propofol [7].

Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of ketamine, and its pharmacological effects are generally similar to those of racemic ketamine. It can also antagonize N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, exerting sedative and analgesic effects [8]. Esketamine was introduced into clinical practice in China in 2019 and has increasingly been used in anesthesia and procedural sedation. In adult volunteer studies, esketamine has been associated with less impairment of cognitive function and shorter recovery times compared with racemic ketamine [9]. However, corresponding evidence in pediatric populations remains limited and not entirely consistent [9, 10]. Esketamine has also been reported to cause relatively limited respiratory depression at subanesthetic doses and possesses intrinsic analgesic effects, which may help reduce propofol requirements and potentially attenuate propofol-related adverse effects [11, 12]. Wang et al. investigated the dosing and adverse effects of esketamine combined with propofol and demonstrated that subanesthetic doses (< 0.5 mg/kg) were generally well tolerated in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy [13]. Nevertheless, data specifically evaluating dose optimization of esketamine in pediatric patients remain scarce. A recent prospective study by Zheng et al. investigated the ED50 and ED95 of propofol under fixed esketamine doses (0–1 mg/kg) in pediatric upper gastrointestinal endoscopy using a similar up-and-down sequential design [14]. However, in that study, the primary endpoint was the dose requirement of propofol. In contrast, the present study aimed to determine the median effective dose (ED50) and ED95 of esketamine under stratified propofol doses (1–4 mg/kg), thereby exploring the minimal esketamine requirement across clinically relevant propofol regimens. The objective of this study was to determine the median effective dose (ED50) and the 95% effective dose (ED95) of esketamine in combination with varying doses of propofol for pediatric gastroscopy. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the type and frequency of adverse events associated with different propofol-esketamine dose combinations, with the goal of refining sedation protocols for painless gastroscopy in children.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complied with the principles of medical ethics stated in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Xuzhou Children’s Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval number:2022-05-22-H22). The protocol was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry on 29 August 2023(ChiCTR2300075179) and can be accessed at chictr.org.cn (https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html? proj=191385). All participants were informed in detail of the trial and signed consent forms.

Participants

The study included pediatric patients of either sex who underwent elective painless gastroscopy in the operating room of The Affiliated Xuzhou Children’s Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University from August 2023 to April 2024. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: age 6–12 years, ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) classification grade I–II, absence of underlying cardiopulmonary disease, and a planned painless gastroscopy without tracheal intubation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) body mass index (BMI) of 30 or above, (2) severe malnutrition, (3) a history of vomiting within the past week, (4) allergy to any of the drugs used in the study, and (5) participation in other clinical trials within the past three months.

Randomization and blinding

A computer-generated randomization sequence was used to assign participants to one of four propofol dose groups (1, 2, 3, or 4 mg/kg) in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. The allocation sequence was prepared in advance and placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Upon patient enrollment, the anesthesiologist opened the envelope immediately before the procedure and prepared the study medications according to group allocation. Due to the nature of the Dixon up-and-down sequential design, dose adjustment and drug preparation were required in real time; therefore, the anesthesiologist responsible for drug administration was aware of group allocation. However, to minimize bias, the investigators responsible for outcome assessment and data collection, as well as the pediatric patients, their legal guardians, the gastroenterologists, and the nursing staff, were blinded to group allocation. The anesthesiologist administering the drugs did not participate in outcome assessment or data analysis. The allocation sequence remained concealed from all other study personnel until completion of data collection and database lock.

Procedures

All patients followed standard preoperative fasting guidelines. Solid food was withheld for at least 6 h, breast milk for 4 h, and clear fluids for at least 2 h prior to anesthesia induction, in accordance with current pediatric anesthesia guidelines [15]. No preoperative medication was administered. No preoperative anticholinergic agents, including glycopyrrolate (Robinul), were administered, and no additional anticholinergic intervention was required during the procedures. Upon arrival at the operating room, peripheral intravenous cannulation was performed in the preoperative holding area prior to anesthesia induction by experienced anesthesia staff. No topical anesthetic cream or nitrous oxide was routinely administered for intravenous placement. Parental presence was permitted during cannulation to reduce anxiety and facilitate cooperation. Patients were then positioned in the left lateral decubitus position on the operating table. Supplemental oxygen (2–3 L/min) was administered via nasal cannula, and the patient’s peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO₂), non-invasive blood pressure, and electrocardiogram (ECG) were continuously monitored. In cases of oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ <93%), airway repositioning (jaw thrust) was performed and supplemental oxygen flow was adjusted as needed; positive-pressure mask ventilation was available as a rescue measure if required. In cases of hypotension (defined as a ≥ 20% decrease in mean blood pressure (MBP) from baseline), patients were managed conservatively with close observation and adjustment of intravenous fluid administration. No vasoactive agents were required. Transient elevations in blood pressure were self-limited and did not require specific pharmacologic intervention. All anesthesia procedures were performed by the same attending anesthesiologist with more than five years of clinical experience in pediatric anesthesia, who independently conducted the anesthetic management throughout the study. Following confirmation of intravenous access, esketamine (2 mL: 50 mg; 25 mg/mL; Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) was diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 10 mL to achieve a final concentration of 5 mg/mL and then administered at a constant rate over 30–60 s according to a pre-established grouping scheme (Table 1), followed by propofol (10 mg/mL; Sichuan Guorui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China), which was administered without further dilution, over approximately 30 s. Once induction was complete, the patient’s Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score—ranging from 5 (fully alert) to 0 (no response to trapezius squeeze)—decreased to ≤ 1. In addition, absence of motor response to standardized jaw thrust was confirmed. Both criteria were required before gastroscope insertion. At this point, gastroscopy was performed by the same senior pediatric endoscopist with more than five years of clinical experience. All procedures were performed by the same physician throughout the study period. After the examination, the patient was transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for monitoring for at least 30 min. Monitoring continued until the MOAA/S score exceeded 4, the modified Aldrete score was greater than 9, and the patient’s vital signs were stable, at which point the patient was considered ready for discharge. A follow-up was conducted 24 h after the examination to document any complications.

Criteria for successful insertion

Successful insertion was defined as a MOAA/S score ≤ 1 at the time of gastroscope placement, with no significant body movement, coughing, or gagging that interfered with insertion during jaw lift. Failure was defined as failure to meet these criteria.

Remedial measures

Additional doses of esketamine were administered according to the propofol dose grouping (Table 1) until successful sedation was achieved.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

The starting dose and dose interval in each group were determined based on the results of pilot experiments (Table 1). The first patient in each group received the predetermined starting dose. For each subsequent patient, the dose of the study drug was adjusted by the fixed interval according to the outcome (success or failure) of gastroscope insertion assessed by a blinded investigator, following the Dixon up-and-down method. A crossover was defined as a change in the response direction between consecutive patients. In accordance with the recommendations of Dixon and the criteria proposed by Paul and Fisher, the study was terminated after the occurrence of six crossover pairs (i.e., failed insertion followed by successful insertion) in each group, resulting in a sample size of approximately 20–40 patients per group [19–21].

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Comparisons among multiple groups for normally distributed data were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni correction for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to evaluate the effects of group, time, and their interaction on continuous variables.

Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 92 children who met the inclusion criteria were initially recruited for this study. Four participants were excluded (two declined participation and two chose an alternative examination method). The remaining 88 children were randomly assigned into four groups, with 22 patients in each group. No interruptions or losses to follow-up occurred during the examination period, and all enrolled participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The discomfort associated with gastroscopy is primarily caused by intense stimulation during endoscope insertion, which may provoke coughing and vomiting. Controlling these reflex responses during insertion remains a major challenge, particularly in pediatric patients, who often experience greater fear and anxiety than adults and may even develop psychological distress. Consequently, gastroscopy is more challenging in children than in adults. The 2018 guidelines for sedation and anesthesia in gastrointestinal endoscopy recommend the use of appropriate adjunctive agents in combination with conventional sedatives to achieve an optimal balance between procedural comfort and safety [22]. In this context, the present study suggests that combining esketamine with propofol can provide sufficient anesthetic depth for pediatric gastroscopy while maintaining hemodynamic stability and allowing timely recovery.

In this study, we determined the median effective dose (ED50) of esketamine combined with different doses of propofol in children with ASA physical status I–II undergoing gastroscopy. As the propofol dose increased from 1 mg/kg to 4 mg/kg, the ED50 of esketamine decreased accordingly, with a significant reduction observed in the 4 mg/kg propofol group. Notably, a higher incidence of adverse events was observed when relatively high doses of esketamine were required in combination with low-dose propofol (1 mg/kg), as well as when higher doses of propofol (3–4 mg/kg) were administered together with esketamine.

We employed the modified Dixon’s up-and-down method to determine the ED50 of esketamine when combined with different doses of propofol. This method has demonstrated long-term reliability [20, 23] and is frequently used in anesthesia research [24]. The advantage of the sequential method is that it does not require a large number of cases to provide data, but rather efficiently utilizes the collected data to obtain quick and accurate results. The use of the Dixon sequential method is contingent on two conditions: the ease of modifying drug doses and the rapid determination of success or failure. Both propofol and esketamine meet these criteria, making the Dixon sequential method feasible for our study.

Propofol has long been recognized as a fast-acting, safe, and reliable intravenous sedative for procedural sedation [25]. However, the guidelines for sedation and anesthesia in gastroscopy report that transient hypoxemia occurs in 3%-7% of cases sedated with propofol, and transient hypotension occurs in 4%-7% of cases [26]. A 2018 meta-analysis observed a higher incidence of cardiopulmonary complications (24.3%) in the propofol-only group compared to other anesthesia regimens (17.1%) among pediatric patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy [27]. Currently, propofol is often combined with remimazolam, esketamine, and opioids for procedural sedation, improving both safety and patient comfort [28–30]. In patients undergoing painless gastroscopy, a single administration of esketamine at 0.5 mg/kg has been proven to be generally safe and tolerable. In anesthesia, a relatively small dose of esketamine can be used as a substitute for racemic ketamine without considering gender differences [13]. A recent similar study by Hayes and colleagues evaluated the ED50 of propofol combined with ketamine at 0, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 mg/kg for pediatric gastroscopy. Their conclusions align with our findings, indicating that as the ketamine dose increases to 0.5-1 mg/kg, the dose of propofol required for deep sedation and anesthesia decreases significantly. This reduction may lower the incidence and severity of propofol-related hemodynamic changes, while higher doses of ketamine significantly increase the incidence of ketamine-related adverse effects [31]. Therefore, rather than assuming superiority, the present study focused on dose optimization of esketamine when combined with propofol in pediatric gastroscopy. Our findings contribute to understanding the dose–response relationship of esketamine under different propofol regimens, which may assist in balancing anesthetic efficacy and safety in clinical practice.

Our study shares certain methodological similarities with the work by Zheng et al., who examined propofol ED50 under varying esketamine doses using the Dixon method. However, the scientific focus of the two studies differs substantially [14]. Zheng et al. fixed esketamine doses and titrated propofol, whereas in the present study, propofol doses were fixed and esketamine was titrated. This inverse dose–response design allows for evaluation of the minimal effective esketamine requirement under clinically relevant propofol regimens. Furthermore, the age range in the present study (6–12 years) excluded younger preschool children, potentially reducing age-related variability in emergence phenomena. In addition, the observed patterns of adverse events differed between studies, suggesting that propofol-dominant and esketamine-dominant regimens may influence safety profiles differently. In China, esketamine has increasingly been adopted in clinical anesthesia practice as an alternative to racemic ketamine. Its pharmacological profile, including higher NMDA receptor affinity and dose-dependent sedative and analgesic effects, has prompted growing interest in its use for procedural sedation. However, direct comparative evidence between esketamine and racemic ketamine in pediatric populations remains limited, and the relative safety and efficacy profiles of these agents require further investigation. Therefore, rather than duplicating prior findings, our study provides complementary evidence that may assist clinicians in optimizing the propofol–esketamine dosing balance in pediatric gastroscopy.

Our results show that the ED50 values of esketamine combined with propofol at 1, 2, and 3 mg/kg for pediatric painless gastroscopy were 11.6-fold, 8.7-fold, and 2.5-fold higher, respectively, compared with the propofol 4 mg/kg group. Esketamine exerts analgesic, anxiolytic, and anesthetic effects primarily through antagonism of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors [32]. Propofol, by contrast, mainly enhances γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)–mediated inhibition via GABAA receptors [33] and also modulates NMDA-type glutamate receptors [34]. The progressive decrease in the ED50 of esketamine with increasing propofol doses observed in our study is likely attributable to the pharmacological characteristics of these two agents, which produce sedative effects through different receptor pathways. Based on these findings, the observed interaction between propofol and esketamine may reflect complementary sedative mechanisms.

In our study, recovery time was longer in the propofol 2, 3, and 4 mg/kg groups than in the 1 mg/kg group, and recovery time in the propofol 4 mg/kg group was also longer than that in the propofol 2 mg/kg group. The observed decrease in the ED50 of esketamine with increasing propofol doses is consistent with findings reported by Yang et al., who noted that reduced doses of esketamine were associated with prolonged recovery times in elderly patients [35]. Propofol is known to cause dose-dependent hypotension, and our results likewise demonstrated a higher incidence of hypotension in the 3 and 4 mg/kg groups compared with the other groups. The increase in mean blood pressure after induction in the 1 and 2 mg/kg groups may be explained by the lower propofol dose combined with the sympathomimetic effects of ketamine, which increases cardiac output [36]. Furthermore, the higher esketamine dose required in the 1 mg/kg propofol group was associated with a higher incidence of post-procedural adverse effects, including delirium and visual disturbances [37]. Interestingly, previous studies have reported a potential reduction in emergence delirium with esketamine administration. For example, Chen et al. demonstrated that administration of esketamine at the end of anesthesia reduced emergence delirium in preschool children (3–7 years) undergoing tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy [38]. The apparent discrepancy between that study and our findings may be explained by several factors. First, the age range differed, as our study included older children (6–12 years), whereas younger preschool children may have distinct neurodevelopmental characteristics and baseline risks for emergence phenomena. Second, the timing of esketamine administration was different. In the study by Chen et al., esketamine was administered at the end of anesthesia, potentially exerting a stabilizing effect during recovery. In contrast, in our study, esketamine was administered during induction to facilitate gastroscope insertion, and higher doses may have contributed to transient neuropsychiatric manifestations during the recovery phase. Third, differences in surgical stimulus and pain intensity (tonsillectomy versus diagnostic gastroscopy) may also influence postoperative behavioral responses. Therefore, findings from younger pediatric populations or different surgical contexts may not be directly extrapolated to the present study cohort. These findings are consistent with those reported by Wang et al., who observed that combining esketamine with propofol reduced propofol requirements but increased postoperative dizziness and prolonged PACU stay in pediatric patients undergoing gastroduodenoscopy [39].

Coulter et al. conducted a simulation study using pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic models to estimate the optimal ketamine–propofol dosing ratio for procedural sedation and analgesia. They concluded that the optimal bolus ratio of racemic ketamine to propofol for short procedures (5–20 min) is approximately 1:3 [40]. In alignment with this finding, the present findings support the use of an optimal esketamine-to-propofol ratio close to 1:3, specifically esketamine combined with propofol at 2 mg/kg for pediatric gastroscopy. Under this regimen, the ED50 and ED95 of esketamine were 0.253 (0.059–0.412) mg/kg and 0.568 (0.351–2.954) mg/kg, respectively. A decrease in this ratio increases the risk of hypotension, respiratory depression, and delayed recovery during the procedure, whereas an increase in the ratio elevates the risk of postoperative delirium and visual disturbances in pediatric patients.

This study has several limitations. First, due to ethical considerations, higher doses of esketamine alone—which may increase the risk of postoperative delirium, dizziness, and visual disturbances in pediatric patients, thereby causing unnecessary anxiety for families and discomfort for patients—were not used as a control group with propofol at 0 mg/kg. Second, the anesthesiologist responsible for drug preparation and administration was not blinded to group allocation, which may have introduced performance bias. Complete blinding was not feasible in this dose-finding study because drug preparation and dose adjustments were required according to the Dixon up-and-down sequential method. However, to minimize potential bias, the criteria for successful gastroscope insertion were predefined and strictly standardized, outcome assessment was performed by investigators blinded to group allocation, and the primary endpoint was based on objective clinical responses (coughing, gagging, airway obstruction) rather than subjective judgment. Third, the sample size in each group was limited according to the stopping rule of the Dixon up-and-down sequential method. Although this approach is well established for estimating ED50, it is less robust for precise estimation of ED95. Consequently, the relatively wide 95% confidence intervals of ED95 may limit the accuracy of clinical extrapolation. This limitation is inherent to the up-and-down methodology rather than specific to the present study design. Fourth, end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring was not included in the study protocol. Continuous capnography could have enabled earlier detection of hypoventilation and provided additional information regarding respiratory safety. In addition, all procedures were performed by a single experienced pediatric endoscopist in a single tertiary pediatric center. The relatively short examination time may reflect operator experience and the predominantly diagnostic nature of the procedures. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to centers with less experienced operators or different procedural complexity. Finally, this study included only children aged 6–12 years with ASA physical status I–II undergoing diagnostic gastroscopy. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to younger preschool children, adolescents, critically ill patients, or those undergoing more complex therapeutic procedures.

Conclusions

In pediatric patients undergoing gastroscopy, the combination of esketamine with propofol effectively reduced the esketamine dose required to suppress the response to gastroscope insertion in a propofol dose-dependent manner. This study specifically defines the dose–response relationship of esketamine under fixed propofol stratification, which has not previously been reported in pediatric gastroscopy. Compared with a low dose of propofol (1 mg/kg), the use of moderate doses of propofol significantly decreased the ED50 and was associated with a reduction in the estimated ED95 of esketamine, along with a lower incidence of esketamine-related adverse effects during recovery. However, higher propofol doses (3–4 mg/kg) increased the risk of hemodynamic instability and prolonged recovery time.

Considering both anesthetic efficacy and safety, the combination of esketamine with 2 mg/kg propofol may represent a clinically reasonable balance for pediatric gastroscopy, achieving adequate suppression of procedural responses while potentially minimizing drug-related adverse effects. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and multicenter designs are warranted to confirm these findings.
