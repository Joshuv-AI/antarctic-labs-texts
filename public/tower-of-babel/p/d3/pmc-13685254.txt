Impaired thyroid hormone sensitivity is associated with cardiovascular disease risk and mortality in euthyroid subjects
Authors: Yang Jiao, Juan Sun, Yuanmeng Li, Feng Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFluctuations in thyroid function among euthyroid individuals have been linked to cardiovascular disease (CVD). We investigated whether central thyroid hormone sensitivity correlates with CVD risk, CVD-related death, and overall mortality in subjects with normal thyroid function, particularly examining how age modifies these associations.MethodsWe analyzed data from 6,374 euthyroid adults enrolled in NHANES 2007–2012. Central thyroid hormone sensitivity was quantified using three indices: the thyroid feedback quantile-based index (TFQI), the thyrotropin-thyroxine resistance index (TT4RI) and the TSH index (TSHI). The Framingham Risk Score (FRS) estimated each participant’s 10-year probability of developing CVD. CVD mortality and all-cause mortality were ascertained by linkage to the National Death Index through December 31, 2015. Associations were evaluated using logistic regression (10-year CVD risk) and Cox proportional hazards models (mortality), adjusted for sex and race, with analyses stratified by age (< 40 and ≥ 40 years).ResultsOf the 6,374 participants (mean age 48.24 ± 17.59 years; 52.0% male), 18.5% had a 10-year CVD risk > 10%; during follow-up. In sex- and race-adjusted models, all three indices were positively associated with an elevated 10-year CVD risk (> 10%): TFQI (OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.15–1.71), TSHI (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.11–1.45), and TT4RI (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02). Each index was also significantly associated with higher CVD mortality and all-cause mortality, with TFQI showing the strongest associations (CVD mortality: HR 3.30, 95% CI 2.24–4.85; all-cause mortality: HR 2.19, 95% CI 1.83–2.62). These associations remained significant in participants aged ≥ 40 years (p < 0.001) but not in those aged < 40 years.ConclusionImpaired central thyroid hormone sensitivity shows association with elevated CVD risk, CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality in euthyroid individuals aged 40 years and older.Graphical abstract Keywords: Sensitivity to thyroid hormones, Cardiovascular disease, Euthyroid, Mortality, NHANES

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) refers to a group of heart and blood vessel disorders, including angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke [1]. As the leading cause of mortality worldwide, the number of CVD cases and related deaths continues to rise [2]. Between 1990 and 2019, global CVD incidence increased by 77.12%, rising from 31.31 million to 55.45 million cases, while deaths from CVD grew by 53.81%, from 12.07 million to 18.56 million [2]. Understanding the risk factors of CVD is crucial for guiding preventive care. Risk stratification tools allow clinicians to tailor intervention strategies accordingly. The Framingham Risk Score (FRS) is widely used to estimate CVD risk [3, 4].

Thyroid hormones (TH) regulate metabolism across multiple organs including the brain, heart, and liver [5, 6]. The heart is especially sensitive to thyroid hormone, and both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, including their subclinical forms, can adversely affect cardiovascular function. The underlying mechanisms may involve blood pressure fluctuations, dyslipidemia, endothelial dysfunction, and direct actions of TH on myocardial tissue [7, 8]. Abnormal thyroid function, both overt and subclinical, has been consistently linked to CVD [8]. More recently, evidence has emerged that even variation in thyroid function within the normal reference range is associated with a higher risk of CVD [9]. Reflecting this progress, a recent study proposed optimal ranges of thyroid function defined by cardiovascular risk [10], indicating that a deeper understanding of the thyroid–CVD relationship may inform more precise clinical management. This reflects the growing depth of research on thyroid function and its connection to CVD and mortality risk, allowing for more precise clinical management strategies.

The concept of central thyroid hormone sensitivity was introduced early on, based on the negative feedback of hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis. It reflects the degree of pituitary suppression by free thyroxine (FT4) levels, determined through indices based on the quantitative relationship between thyrotropin (TSH) and FT4 [11]. However, previous indices used to assess central thyroid hormone sensitivity, including the TSH index (TSHI) and the thyrotropin thyroxine resistance index (TT4RI), have seen limited clinical application due to their intrinsic limitations [12]. Until 2019, Laclaustra et al. proposed the Thyroid Feedback Quantile-based Index (TFQI) as a new measure of central thyroid hormone sensitivity [11]. TFQI continuously quantifies deviations from the median pituitary response to thyroid hormones, providing a stable and adaptable index [11]. The development of TFQI has revealed that many euthyroid individuals may nonetheless exhibit impaired thyroid hormone sensitivity. Studies have linked impaired thyroid hormone sensitivity to various metabolic conditions such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and hyperuricemia, many of which independently predispose to CVD [11, 13–15]. Given these findings and evidence that thyroid function variation within normal limits affects cardiovascular outcomes, further exploration of central thyroid hormone sensitivity and CVD risk among euthyroid populations is warranted.

Herein, we investigated how central thyroid hormone sensitivity relates to cardiovascular risk among euthyroid individuals, further examining its association with both CVD mortality and all-cause mortality.

Methods

Participants

We analyzed NHANES 2007–2012 data. Our initial sample comprised 30,442 individuals from the 2007–2012 survey cycles. We excluded 12,799 individuals aged < 20 years. Next, participants were excluded if they had missing thyroid profiles, thyroid disease, were taking thyroid hormone-related medication, were pregnant, had missing data for FRS, had coronary heart disease at baseline, or had missing mortality data. This left 7,469 participants with complete data. Finally, after excluding those with abnormal range of FT4 or TSH, 6,374 participants were enrolled in the analytic cohort (Fig. 1).

Flow chat of sample selection from the NHANES 2007–2012

The NHANES study was approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (Protocol #2005-06, #2011-17). Further details are available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html? CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/irba98.htm.

Tests of thyroid function and indices of central thyroid hormone sensitivity

The Access HYPERsensitive hTSH assay, a two-site immunoenzymatic ‘sandwich’ method, was used to measure serum TSH levels. FT4 was measured by two-step enzyme immunoassay, whereas free triiodothyronine (FT3) was measured using a competitive binding immunoenzymatic assay.

Central thyroid hormone sensitivity was assessed using three indices: TFQI, TSHI, and TT4RI. TFQI was calculated as the cumulative distribution function (cdf) FT4 - (1 − cdf TSH) [11]. A TFQI value of 0 indicated normal sensitivity, positive values indicated impaired sensitivity, and negative values reflected increased sensitivity. TT4RI was determined by FT4 (pmol/L) × TSH (mIU/L) and TSHI by Ln TSH (mIU/L) + 0.1345 × FT4 (pmol/L) [16, 17].

Evaluation of ten-year risk for cardiovascular disease and assessment of outcomes

FRS was used to evaluate the ten-year risk for CVD. FRS was calculated by factors including sex, age, systolic blood pressure (SBP), total cholesterol (TC), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), diabetes status and smoking status [4]. According to the score, participants were divided into two groups, group with a 10-year risk ≤ 10% and those with a risk > 10%.

All enrolled participants underwent passive mortality follow-up through December 31, 2015. The primary outcomes included CVD mortality and all-cause mortality. All-cause mortality was ascertained through death certification data provided by the National Death Index, matched using name, sex, date of birth, state of birth, race and ethnicity, social security number, and state of residence.

Definitions

Hypertension was defined as either self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension (indicating a formal diagnosis by a healthcare provider) or measured blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg [18, 19]. Dyslipidemia was defined as diagnosed dyslipidemia or any of the following criteria: TC at or above 5.2 mmol/L, triglycerides (TG) at or above 1.7 mmol/L, or HDL-C levels below 1.0 mmol/L [13]. Arthritis was identified based on a doctor’s diagnosis. The threshold for heavy alcohol consumption was set at four or more standard drinks per occasion for women and five or more for men [20]. Body Mass Index (BMI) was expressed as weight (kg) / height² (m²) [21]. Other variables such as age, sex, race, marital status, arm circumference, waist circumference, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, serum glucose, serum uric acid, glycohemoglobin, Apo B, and C-reactive protein can be accessed on the website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Statistical methods

Data in this study were statistically analyzed following CDC guidelines. NHANES employs a complex, multistage, stratified probability design. As the present study aimed to estimate associations within a defined euthyroid population rather than nationally representative parameters, unweighted multivariable models adjusted for demographic covariates were used, consistent with guidance on analyzing survey data for etiologic research [22]. Mean ± standard deviation was used for continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were represented as percentages. Logistic regression assessed associations between sensitivity indices and 10-year CVD risk (> 10% vs. ≤10%), adjusting for sex and race. Chi-square tests compared groups. Cox proportional hazards regression was utilized to further investigate these thyroid hormone sensitivity indices on CVD and all-cause mortality, adjusting for sex and race. Age-stratified analyses (< 40 years or ≥ 40 years) were conducted to evaluate potential age-specific effects. P < 0.05 indicated significance. All statistical analysis were performed using the R package (http://www.Rproject.org) and Empower Stats (http://www.empowerstats.com).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Among the 6,374 included participants, the mean age was 48.2 years and 52.0% were male; the overall distribution of demographic characteristics, thyroid function parameters, sensitivity indices, metabolic variables, and comorbidities is summarized in Table 1. Based on the 10-year risk for CVD, we divided the 6,374 participants into two groups. Of these, 18.5% had a 10-year CVD risk greater than 10%. Compared to the group with a 10-year risk ≤ 10%, the > 10% group had higher TFQI, TSHI, and TT4RI (p < 0.001). Additionally, FT3 levels were reduced, while TSH levels were elevated in the > 10% group (p < 0.001). Participants with higher risk of CVD demonstrated a greater occurrence of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and arthritis (p < 0.001). Significant differences in lifestyle factors, including marital status and heavy alcohol consumption, were also observed between the two groups (p < 0.001). For participants aged 40 years and older, the frequency of CVD risk > 10% increased significantly (p < 0.001). A similar increase was observed in males and Non-Hispanic White individuals (p < 0.001). Thyroid hormone sensitivity and all-cause mortality

All-cause mortality was significantly positively associated with thyroid hormone sensitivity indices (TFQI: HR = 2.19; TSHI: HR = 1.52; TT4RI: HR = 1.02; p < 0.001) (Table 3). This association remained statistically significant in individuals aged ≥ 40 years after adjusting for age (TFQI: HR = 2.23; TSHI: HR = 1.55; TT4RI: HR = 1.02), as shown in Fig. 4a, c, e and Table 3. In individuals younger than 40 years, however, all-cause mortality was only positively correlated with TFQI, but this association was non-significant (p = 0.77) (Fig. 4b, d, f and Table 3). In the same models, none of FT4, FT3, or TSH was significantly associated with the 10-year CVD risk, CVD mortality, or all-cause mortality (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

Our study finds that elevated TFQI, TSHI, and TT4RI levels are associated with a higher incidence of CVD risk greater than 10%, along with increased CVD and all-cause mortality. These relationships were prominent among individuals aged ≥ 40 years but were not apparent in younger participants.

Research on the correlation between thyroid function and CVD has progressively deepened. It began with early studies examining the relationship between overt hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism and CVD, followed by the recognition that subclinical forms are also linked to CVD, and ultimately led to the discovery that even fluctuations in normal thyroid function are connected with CVD risk [8, 9, 23]. Recently, studies have begun focusing on the link between thyroid hormone sensitivity (central or peripheral) and CVD. The FT3/FT4 ratio, commonly used to assess peripheral sensitivity of thyroid hormone, has been shown to be associated with CVD risk [24–26]. Qin et al. investigated central thyroid hormone sensitivity and CVD risk among individuals with normal thyroid function undergoing coronary angiography, finding that impaired sensitivity was associated CVD events [27]. Li et al. found TFQI was significantly correlated with CVD prevalence [28]. Our study examined central thyroid hormone sensitivity and CVD outcomes. We found that reduced thyroid hormone sensitivity was linked to a higher likelihood of a 10-year CVD risk exceeding 10%, as well as an increased risk of CVD-related death. Notably, these findings were statistically significant in individuals aged 40 years and older but did not apply to those under 40. The age-related differences in this relationship warrant attention. Age plays a significant role in assessing CVD risk and is also closely related to the HPT axis, which changes with advancing age [4, 29, 30]. This may partially explain the inconsistent relationship between CVD risk and thyroid hormone sensitivity across the two age groups. Our findings suggest that age should be considered when exploring the links between thyroid hormone sensitivity, and CVD outcomes. In individuals aged ≥ 40 years, changes in thyroid hormone sensitivity appear more likely to contribute to CVD risk and related mortality.

The mechanism linking impaired thyroid hormone sensitivity to increased CVD risk and mortality remains unclear, but various potential explanations can be considered. Firstly, several studies on euthyroid individuals have demonstrated that impaired central thyroid hormone sensitivity is associated with diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension, all of which are key factors in assessing CVD risk [11, 13, 15]. This association may partially explain their correlation. Additionally, both peripheral and central thyroid hormone sensitivity may directly influence the cardiovascular system. For peripheral FT3/FT4 ratio, a decrease suggests impaired conversion of T4 to T3, often observed in myocardial damage [8, 31]. This reduction in FT3/FT4 may subsequently reduce myocardial contractility and increase vascular resistance, potentially raising the risk of CVD [32, 33]. In euthyroid individuals, impaired central sensitivity reflects a higher TSH set point, whereby more TSH is required to maintain free thyroid hormone within the normal range. This compensatory elevation in TSH, rather than a reduced hormone supply, may accompany an adverse cardiometabolic profile and contribute to the elevated CVD risk in these individuals [34]. Conversely, in individuals with higher CVD risk, related neurohumoral changes may further impact HPT function [35]. This bidirectional interplay suggests a potential feedback loop wherein metabolic dysfunction and thyroid sensitivity impairment mutually reinforce each other.

Our study also found a relationship between thyroid hormone sensitivity and all-cause mortality. Several recent studies have also explored this connection. A longitudinal observational study in a euthyroid population categorized central thyroid hormone sensitivity into six levels, revealing that higher sensitivity contributed to lower all-cause mortality [36]. Other investigators have reported that diminished thyroid hormone sensitivity may predict elevated mortality in euthyroid patients with renal dysfunction [37]. Prior NHANES analysis linked higher TFQI quartiles to increased mortality [38]. Notably, the individual thyroid parameters (FT4, FT3, and TSH) were not independently associated with the 10-year CVD risk, CVD mortality, or all-cause mortality (all P > 0.05), whereas the composite indices were. This accords with the high individuality of thyroid function, whereby a single value within the reference range is not necessarily normal for a given individual, and with the design of the sensitivity indices, which reflect the TSH–FT4 feedback relationship rather than either hormone alone [11, 39]. Our findings indicate that impaired central thyroid hormone sensitivity correlates with higher all-cause mortality, consistently observed across TFQI, TT4RI, and TSHI, though the trend is most pronounced in TFQI. This difference may be due to the intrinsic properties of these three indices [12]. Additionally, age subgroup analysis showed a statistically significant correlation in individuals aged 40 years and older, but not in those under 40 years. The mechanism linking central thyroid hormone sensitivity to all-cause mortality remains unclear. However, alterations in the thyroid hormone set point of hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, as previously mentioned, may offer some insight. The aging process exerts multifaceted effects on endocrine axes, with the HPT system being no exception. Senescent changes in hypothalamic neurotransmitter signaling, reduced deiodinase activity, and altered thyroid hormone receptor expression have all been documented [30]. Accordingly, age-related HPT axis changes may partially explain why this association manifests predominantly in older individuals [30].

Based on a nationally representative U.S. sample, our study’s findings suggest possible applications in clinical practice. First, it demonstrates that central thyroid hormone sensitivity, which can be calculated from thyroid function tests, may help assess CVD risk. If thyroid hormone sensitivity can serve as a CVD risk predictor, it may provide valuable support for advancing disease prevention strategies, as thyroid function is widely accessible and screened. Additionally, our findings suggest that central thyroid hormone sensitivity is associated with both CVD mortality and all-cause mortality. This highlights the need for further research to explore underlying mechanisms and strategies to improve thyroid hormone sensitivity. Ultimately, our findings indicate differences in the aforementioned relationships across various age subgroups, emphasizing that the conclusions are more relevant for individuals aged 40 years and above. This also underscores the need to account for age factors in clinical applications and future studies exploring the link between central thyroid hormone sensitivity and CVD.

This study has a few limitations. First, while we used Cox regression models to analyze the relationship between thyroid hormone sensitivity and mortality outcomes, allowing for time-to-event analysis, the NHANES dataset itself is cross-sectional. This means that we lack repeated measures of thyroid hormone levels and sensitivity indices over time, which would provide a more dynamic view of thyroid function and its potential impact on long-term mortality. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded: we adjusted for sex, race, and age, but not all metabolic and clinical variables could be entered into the same model, as several are components of the FRS or closely correlated with them, and their inclusion would risk multicollinearity and overadjustment; unmeasured factors such as lifestyle, genetic predisposition, comorbidities, and thyroid autoantibodies may also influence both thyroid function and cardiovascular outcomes. Third, the FRS is a continuous measure, and applying a 10% cutoff may entail some loss of information; we adopted this guideline-endorsed threshold for clinical interpretability [40], and the associations were consistent when the FRS was analyzed continuously. Fourth, our age-stratified analysis indicated significant associations in participants aged 40 years and older, but these findings may be partly influenced by the smaller sample size or lower event rates in younger participants, which could reduce statistical power in this subgroup. Finally, because the analysis targeted associations within a selected complete-case euthyroid population rather than national prevalence, survey weights were not applied; results should be interpreted as associations within the included population rather than nationally representative estimates. Future studies using survey-weighted analyses are needed to confirm the population-level generalizability of these findings.

In conclusion, this study found that impaired central thyroid hormone sensitivity is associated with an increased 10-year risk of CVD, CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality in euthyroid individuals, with these associations particularly relevant for those aged 40 years and above. This suggests that central thyroid hormone sensitivity could potentially become a new predictor of CVD risk, and further research into the related mechanisms is needed.
