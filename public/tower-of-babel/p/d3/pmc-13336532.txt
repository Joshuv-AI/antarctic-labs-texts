Multiple Ill-Defined Hypopigmented Scaly Plaques on the Leg in an 8-Year-Old Girl
Authors: Vishal Gaurav, Pavani Gowda, Deepika Yadav, Nita Khurana
Published: 2026

An eight-year-old girl presented with multiple asymptomatic hypopigmented lesions on the lateral aspect of her left lower leg for four months [Figure 1]. There was no involvement of other body sites, and no personal or family history of similar skin conditions or any topical applications. Cutaneous examination revealed multiple ill-defined hypopigmented plaques with slightly scaly surfaces. The plaques were confluent at the center and distributed along the lower leg, extending from just above the ankle to the midshin. The surrounding skin was normal; no erythema, tenderness, or other inflammatory signs were noted. The rest of the examination, including neurological assessment, revealed no sensory loss or nerve thickening. Dermoscopy revealed white structureless areas, orange-yellow peri-eccrine areas, and zones displaying a faint yet preserved reticular pigment pattern, surrounded by a prominent reticular pigment network [Figure 2]. Histopathology showed focal lichenoid lymphohistiocytic infiltrates extending into the upper epidermis [Figure 3a] with vacuolar degeneration of keratinocytes, marked spongiosis accompanied by lymphocytic exocytosis, and overlying focal parakeratosis [Figure 3b]. Mild to moderate lymphohistiocytic perivascular infiltrate was noted in the superficial dermis and periadnexal tuberculoid granulomas in the mid and deep dermis composed of epitheloid histiocytes, forming well-defined granulomas surrounded by dense lymphocytic infiltrate [Figure 3c], along with Langhan’s giant cells in perieccrine locations [Figure 3d]. No necrosis or organisms were identified on special stains (Periodic acid-Schiff, Ziehl–Neelsen).

What is the Diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Granulomatous lichen striatus

Discussion

Lichen striatus (LS) is a self-limited dermatosis that predominantly affects children. It classically presents as a linear eruption of small, erythematous, or hypopigmented papules along Blaschko’s lines. The exact etiology is unknown, although theories suggest a possible autoimmune reaction triggered by environmental factors, such as infections or trauma, in genetically predisposed individuals.[1]

Histologically, LS usually displays a lichenoid inflammatory infiltrate along the dermoepidermal junction. However, the presence of granulomatous inflammation, as seen in this case, is rare. To date, very few cases of granulomatous LS have been reported. In a retrospective case series on lichenoid granulomatous dermatitis (LGD), LS accounted for only 3 (5.4%) cases in a series of 56 LGD cases, highlighting the rarity of this presentation.[2] LGD represents an uncommon histopathologic reaction pattern characterized by a combination of lichenoid inflammation with granuloma formation.[3] LGD is also demonstrated by lupus vulgaris, lichen nitidus, and lichen scrofulosorum, all of which have distinct clinical presentations. In LGD, various histologic patterns can be associated with specific clinical conditions. For example, dermal eosinophilia and psoriasiform epidermal changes were associated with drug hypersensitivity reactions, while perineural and perifollicular inflammation were linked to tattoo reactions and postherpetic dermatitis.[23] The difficulty in correlating clinical features with histological findings in LGD, as outlined in the study, is evident in this case, as granulomatous features in LS are rarely encountered and can complicate the diagnosis. LGD should be differentiated from lichenoid and granulomatous dermatitis based on the spatial relationship of their inflammatory components. In LGD, lichenoid and granulomatous infiltrates are closely interwoven, forming a single composite pattern, as seen in conditions like lichenoid sarcoidosis, secondary syphilis, drug reactions, and LS (rare). In contrast, lichenoid and granulomatous dermatitis features distinct but coexisting patterns, where the lichenoid inflammation remains superficial while granulomas are deeper and periadnexal, commonly observed in LS, tuberculoid leprosy, and certain drug-induced reactions.[3]

The pathogenesis of granuloma formation in LS is unclear. It may represent an exaggerated immune response or a specific reaction to unknown antigens in a predisposed individual. The granulomas seen in this case were non-necrotizing and primarily composed of epithelioid histiocytes, consistent with other reported cases of this rare variant. The simultaneous presence of lichenoid and granulomatous tissue reactions may result from the activation of both cytotoxic CD8 T-cells and histiocytes triggered by self-derived or environmental antigens, including superantigens. Superantigens, such as those from infectious agents, activate a T helper 1 response by binding to major histocompatibility complex class II on antigen-presenting cells and the T-cell receptor on CD4 T-cells. This leads to CD8 T-cell activation, interferon-gamma production, and recruitment of macrophages.[45]

The differential diagnosis of localized hypopigmented scaly plaques in children includes several conditions, each with unique clinical and histological characteristics. Extrafacial pityriasis alba, which manifests as hypopigmented, scaly patches, is commonly associated with a history of atopic dermatitis. Hypopigmented sarcoidosis can present with localized granulomatous lesions, although rare in children. Tuberculoid leprosy should be considered in endemic areas, characterized by hypopigmented anesthetic patches with possible nerve involvement. Lastly, hypopigmented mycosis fungoides, a variant of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, may appear as slowly progressive hypopigmented plaques, necessitating biopsy for diagnosis. Accurate differentiation requires a thorough clinical and histopathological correlation.

LS is a self-limited condition that typically resolves within a year without scarring, though it may leave temporary hypopigmentation or hyperpigmentation. Treatment is generally unnecessary, but for cosmetic reasons or pruritus relief, low- to midpotency topical corticosteroids or topical immunomodulators can be used.[1] Granulomatous LS is a rare variant, and differences in clinical features, treatment, and prognosis compared to classical LS are not well established due to limited reported cases. Further studies are needed to determine any definitive distinctions.

This case underscores the importance of clinicopathological correlation in diagnosing atypical presentations of common dermatoses. Dermatologists should be aware of this rare variant to avoid misdiagnosis, as granulomas are typically associated with other granulomatous conditions. Although rare, the lichenoid granulomatous histological pattern can occur in LS, expanding the spectrum of this disorder.
