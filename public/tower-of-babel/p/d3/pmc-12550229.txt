Incidence and predictors of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Endale Tamiru Burayu, Bekem Dibaba Degefa, Zelalem Banjaw Zegeye, Gemechu Gelan Bekele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Mortality from cervical cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa remains alarmingly high.•Pooled mortality incidence was 32.1% across 57 studies in the region.•Advanced stage at diagnosis was the strongest predictor of mortality.•HIV-positive status, anemia, and older age were significant risk factors.•Targeted early detection and treatment access could reduce deaths.Key words: cervical cancer, mortality, predictors, Sub-Saharan Africa, systematic review, meta-analysis

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

This study aimed to estimate the incidence of mortality and identify predictors among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) to inform targeted interventions and policy.

Key findings

The pooled incidence of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa was 32.06% (95% CI: 29.71–34.41). Key predictors included advanced stage at diagnosis (HR=2.6), age over 65 years (HR=2.0), HIV-positive status (HR=1.52), radiotherapy alone (HR=1.41), and anemia (HR=2.0), highlighting the need for early detection and improved treatment access.

What does this add to what is known?

This meta-analysis quantifies a high mortality burden in SSA, exceeding rates in high-income regions, and identifies region-specific predictors like HIV status and treatment limitations, offering actionable insights for reducing cervical cancer deaths.

Introduction

Cervical cancer continues to pose a major public health issue, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where it ranks as a primary cause of cancer deaths among women.1 Despite improvements in screening and treatment, the rates of incidence and mortality in SSA continue to be disproportionately elevated in comparison to high-income nations.2 The significant burden of cervical cancer in SSA is due to various factors, such as restricted access to healthcare services, late diagnosis, and a notable prevalence of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection.3,4

Worldwide, cervical cancer represents around 604,000 new cases and 342,000 fatalities each year, with over 90% of these cases happening in low- and middle-income nations.5,6 The rate in SSA is concerning elevated because of poor screening initiatives, low vaccination rates, and a deficiency in knowledge regarding preventive actions.7,8 Numerous studies have emphasized the impact of socioeconomic inequalities, inefficiencies within healthcare systems, and comorbidities like HIV/AIDS in worsening cervical cancer results.9,10 Women who are HIV-positive face a notably elevated risk of encountering persistent HPV infections, which results in a greater occurrence of cervical cancer and increased death rates.11,12

Grasping the factors that lead to mortality in cervical cancer patients in SSA is essential for creating impactful interventions and enhancing patient outcomes. Various elements, such as the age at which the diagnosis is made, the stage at which it presents, treatment methods, and existing health conditions, have been recognized as key factors influencing survival in cervical cancer patients.13, 14, 15 A meta-analysis of current literature can offer an extensive insight into the rates and predictors of mortality, thus guiding policies and approaches for cervical cancer management in SSA.16,17

Earlier systematic reviews have mainly concentrated on the worldwide impact of cervical cancer or differences in incidence by region, yet only a limited number have specifically examined mortality predictors in Sub-Saharan Africa.18,19 Due to the elevated mortality rate and scarce research synthesis in this area, a systematic review and meta-analysis are necessary to measure the incidence of death and pinpoint crucial factors affecting survival results.20,21 This research intends to combine current evidence regarding the incidence and factors influencing mortality in cervical cancer patients within SSA, offering guidance for focused interventions and policy suggestions.

Research question

What is the incidence of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa, and what are the key predictors influencing survival outcomes in this population?

Methodology

Our systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered under PROSPERO number CRD420251000700.

Research design

This research conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing studies that detail the incidence and factors predicting mortality in cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [Supplementary File 1] and MOOSE guidelines [Supplementary File 2].

Criteria for eligibility

The review aimed to encompass published studies that provided epidemiological data on cervical cancer mortality and its predictors. The PICOS evidence-based medicine framework guided our inclusion criteria, and nonrandomized studies fulfilled the following PICOS-standardized elements. Criteria for inclusion

Population: Research focused on patients with cervical cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Results: Research documenting incidence rates of cervical cancer and/or factors influencing mortality in cervical cancer patients.

Study design: Observational research (cohort) and clinical experiments.

Language: Research published in the English language.

Period: Research released from January 2000 to 2024.

Context: Research carried out in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Criteria for exclusion

Research concentrating on different cancer types or non–cancer-related ailments, Research lacking extractable data on incidence or mortality predictors, Reviews, editorials, commentaries, and case studies, along with Research performed outside Sub-Saharan Africa.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in the following electronic databases like; PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, African Journals Online (AJOL), CINAHL, and Google Scholar (for grey literature).

The search was used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords related to cervical cancer, mortality, incidence, predictors, and Sub-Saharan Africa. Examples of search terms include; "Cervical cancer," "Mortality," "Incidence," "Predictors," "Sub-Saharan Africa," "Survival," and "Risk factors." Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were used to refine the search. The search strategy was adapted for each database. To ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant studies, the search strategy included both the term "Sub-Saharan Africa" and specific country names (eg, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ethiopia, Ghana, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Senegal, Cameroon, Rwanda, Burundi, Democratic Republic of Congo, Sierra Leone, Gambia, Mali, Niger, and Chad) to capture articles that may not explicitly mention the region but were conducted in SSA countries [Supplementary File 3].

Study selection

All identified records were imported into reference Rayyan software to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers were screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and assessed for inclusion based on the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

A standardized form for data extraction was created and tested. Two reviewers independently extracted the following data from the included studies: Study characteristics (author, year, country, study design, sample size), Population characteristics (age, HIV status, cancer stage), Cervical cancer incidence rates, Mortality predictors (eg, age, diagnosis stage, HIV status, treatment type), and Effect measures (hazard ratios, relative risks, confidence intervals).

Evaluation of quality

The methodological quality of the studies included was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS).22 Two reviewers were individually evaluated the quality of every study, and differences were settled by agreement.

Data integration

Narrative synthesis: A narrative synthesis was performed to consolidate the features of the studies included, incidence rates, and mortality predictors.

Meta-analysis: meta-analysis was conducted utilizing Stata 17 version statistical software. Combined estimates of incidence rates and factors influencing mortality were computed using random-effects models to address variability. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, where values of 25%, 50%, and 75% signify low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Subgroup analyses were performed according to region, publication year, and HIV status. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the strength of the results.

Study search and selection

The search strategy outlined involves multiple databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, African Journals Online (AJOL), CINAHL, and Google Scholar, focusing on cervical cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa with specific outcomes like incidence and mortality predictors. Finally, 57 studies were included in the final review [Figure 1].

Data extraction details

Data extraction was thorough and methodical, concentrating on essential factors to tackle the research question. The subsequent information was gathered from every study as follows;

Study features: Author, year of publication, nation, research design, and number of participants.

Demographic traits: HIV condition and cancer stage upon diagnosis.

Incidence rates: The percentage of cervical cancer patients who succumbed, serving as the foundation for the aggregated estimate.

Mortality predictors: Factors including age, diagnosis stage, HIV status, treatment type (eg, radiotherapy alone versus chemo-radiotherapy), and anemia, along with their relevant effect measures.

Effect measures: Hazard ratios (HR), and relative risks (RR), including 95% CIs, to assess the strength of relationships between predictors and mortality. This thorough extraction facilitated both narrative synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis.

Geographical distribution

The geographic spread of the 57 studies illustrates research endeavors and capabilities throughout Sub-Saharan Africa: South Africa: 16 studies (28%), Kenya: 9 studies (16%), Nigeria: 8 studies (14%), Ghana: 4 studies (7%), Ethiopia: 4 studies (7%), Zambia: 3 studies (5%), Zimbabwe: 3 studies (5%), and Other Countries: 10 studies (18%) from Senegal, Cameroon, Rwanda, Burundi, Democratic Republic of Congo, Sierra Leone, Gambia, Mali, Niger, and Chad (1 study each). This distribution emphasizes a focus of research in nations such as South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria, probably because of their more advanced healthcare systems and cancer registries, whereas other countries are less represented, perhaps because of restricted research infrastructure [Table 1].

Sample size variability

The sample sizes of the studies included exhibited considerable variability, varying from 68 to 59,190 patients, with a median of around 400 patients. This broad spectrum illustrates variations in study design, funding, and access to patient groups. Small studies: Eg, Joseph Daniels et al (2024, Ghana) with 68 patients,23 possibly limited by local resources. Large studies: Eg, Gbenga Olorunfemi et al (2022), South Africa) with 59,190 patients,24 leveraging population-based data over an extended period. Median size: 400 patients, as seen in studies like Anderson et al (2005, South Africa, n=420)25 or Patel et al (2008, Kenya, n=480)26, balancing precision and feasibility.

Outcomes and predictors

The research emphasizes results such as overall survival and disease-specific survival, frequently presenting hazard ratios. Typical predictors of mortality encompass advanced stage at diagnosis (noted in 90% of studies), age above 65 years (80% of studies), HIV-positive status (70% of studies), treatment type (60% of studies), and anemia (50% of studies), emphasizing crucial risk factors.

Time period and quality

Data gathering extends from 2000 to 2024, with recent research indicating enhanced quality. Every study undergone peer review and fulfills inclusion criteria, evaluated with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for nonrandomized studies, ensuring robust analysis.

Quality assessment

Quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The NOS scores studies out of 9 points across 3 domains: selection, comparability, and outcome.

Overall quality distribution: High quality (≥7 points): 37 studies (64.9%), and Moderate quality (5–6 points): 20 studies (35.1%).

Missing data handling

In included studies, complete case analysis was used, excluding participants with incomplete data. For the meta-analysis, studies lacking extractable incidence or predictor data were excluded during screening. No imputation was applied, prioritizing reported data.

Time-to-event data

Time-to-event data (eg, survival curves) from studies like Gbenga Olorunfemi et al (2022) were reported but not pooled due to inconsistent follow-up periods. The meta-analysis focused on incidence proportions and HRs for uniformity, though survival data provide valuable mortality timing insights.

Results of syntheses and reporting bias

Heterogeneity statistics

The meta-analysis revealed high heterogeneity among the included studies, as indicated by an I² value of 95.72%. Additionally, the P-value for heterogeneity was 0.12, providing statistical evidence of significant differences among the study results. A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis to account for the high heterogeneity (I²=95.72%) observed across studies The variations were likely due to; Study Design [Variability between retrospective and prospective cohorts in follow-up and data collection], Population [Differences in age, HIV prevalence, and cancer stage at diagnosis], Healthcare Systems [Disparities in screening, treatment access, and infrastructure across Sub-Saharan Africa], and Geographical or Temporal Factors [Regional cancer burden variations and healthcare improvements over time]. Subgroup analyses partially addressed these, but residual heterogeneity reflects the region’s complexity [Figure 2], [Table 2].

Pooled incidence proportion and pooled effect size of mortality among cervical cancer patients in SSA

The pooled incidence rate of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa stands at 32.06, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [29.71–34.41]. This estimate comes from a meta-analysis that encompassed 57 studies, consolidating data to deliver a strong depiction of mortality in the area. This statistic shows that nearly one-third of cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa succumb to the illness, emphasizing the considerable mortality burden within this group. The confidence interval indicates a precise range in which the actual incidence proportion is likely to fall, considering the variability among the studies included.

Predictors of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa

The studies focus on outcomes like overall and disease-specific survival, often reporting hazard ratios. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, 5 variables like; advanced stage at diagnosis, Age over 65 years, HIV-positive status, Treatment type [specifically radiotherapy alone vs chemo-radiotherapy], and Anemia were significantly associated predictors for cervical cancer patient mortality.

Advanced stage at diagnosis (reported in 90% of studies) had the strongest association with mortality, with a pooled hazard ratio (HR) of 2.6 (95% CI: 2.1–3.2, P<.001). This suggests that patients diagnosed at an advanced stage are 2.6 times more likely to die compared to those diagnosed at an earlier stage. Similarly, age over 65 years (80% of studies) was significantly associated with mortality (HR=2.0, 95% CI: 1.5–2.6, P<.001), indicating that elderly patients face double the risk of death compared to younger patients.

Moreover, HIV-positive status (70% of studies) was linked to an increased mortality risk (HR=1.52, 95% CI: 1.06–2.19, P=.023). This suggests that HIV-positive cervical cancer patients have a 52% higher risk of death compared to HIV-negative patients. In other way, Treatment type (radiotherapy alone vs chemo-radiotherapy) showed that patients who received radiotherapy alone had a significantly higher mortality risk (HR=1.41, 95% CI: 1.22–1.63, P<.001). Finally, Anemia (50% of studies) was associated with a doubled mortality risk (HR=2.0, 95% CI: 1.5–2.5, P<.001) [Table 3].

Comment

Principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 57 studies found that the pooled incidence proportion of mortality among cervical cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is 32.06%, 95% CI: 29.71%–34.41%). This discovery suggests that nearly one-third of cervical cancer patients in SSA die from the illness, highlighting a significant mortality burden in the area. The main factors associated with mortality consist of late-stage diagnosis (HR=2.6, 95% CI: 2.1–3.2), age greater than 65 years (HR=2.0, 95% CI: 1.5–2.6), being HIV-positive (HR=1.52, 95% CI: 1.06–2.19), undergoing radiotherapy alone rather than chemo-radiotherapy (HR=1.41, 95% CI: 1.22–1.63), and having anemia (HR=2.0, 95% CI: 1.5–2.5). These predictors showed statistical significance (P<.05) and emphasize important risk factors affecting survival results. The consequences of these results are significant; indicating the critical necessity for strategies focused on early identification, enhanced treatment availability, and specialized management of high-risk populations to lower mortality rates in SSA.

Comparison with existing literature

The mortality rate of 32.06% corresponds with global data indicating that SSA faces a disproportionately higher burden of cervical cancer compared to high-income areas.27,28 This increased mortality indicates difficulties like restricted healthcare access, late-stage diagnosis, and a high prevalence of HPV, in line with earlier studies.29,30 The identified predictors align with current literature. For example, advanced stage at diagnosis being the strongest predictor (HR=2.6) supports research indicating that late presentation considerably deteriorates prognosis worldwide.31,32 Likewise, the link between being HIV-positive and a heightened risk of mortality (HR=1.52) is anticipated due to the significant HIV prevalence in SSA and its contribution to worsening HPV-related cervical cancer advancement.33,34 The effect of treatment type, where radiotherapy alone is associated with greater mortality than chemo-radiotherapy, highlights the significance of multimodal therapy, a conclusion backed by research on treatment effectiveness.35

These findings highlight the vital importance of early identification via improved screening initiatives, as decreasing late-stage diagnoses could significantly boost survival rates. The increased mortality risk among HIV-positive women and the elderly indicates that specialized interventions for these populations, including combined HIV-cervical cancer treatment and eldercare services, are crucial. Furthermore, guaranteeing access to chemo-radiotherapy in addition to radiotherapy alone may enhance treatment results, emphasizing a crucial requirement for better healthcare infrastructure in SSA. While radiotherapy alone and chemoradiotherapy were the primary treatment modalities reported in the included studies due to the prevalence of advanced-stage diagnoses in SSA, surgical interventions such as hysterectomy or radical trachelectomy are relevant for early-stage cervical cancer. However, limited access to surgical facilities and late-stage presentations in SSA often restrict the use of surgery, highlighting the need for improved early detection to expand treatment options. This systematic review and meta-analysis regarding cervical cancer deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) indicates a combined mortality rate of 32.06%, 95% CI: 29.71%–34.41%), significantly surpassing figures seen in Western nations, developed Asian countries, and Australia, where strong healthcare systems and extensive screening initiatives lead to reduced death rates. In the United States, the relative survival rate for cervical cancer over 5 years is around 66%, suggesting a mortality rate of roughly 34% over the same period; however, this statistic differs according to stage and demographic variables, thanks to successful early detection via Pap smears and HPV testing.36

Likewise, the UK attains a 5-year survival rate of approximately 63%, aided by national screening efforts,37 whereas Australia reports a 5-year survival rate of 74%, among the highest worldwide, thanks to its national screening scheme initiated in 1991 and elevated socio-economic conditions promoting prompt intervention.38 In Asia, nations such as Japan and South Korea, considered developed, have reported 5-year survival rates of around 70% and 78%, respectively, thanks to their advanced healthcare systems and screening methods.39,40 In contrast, less developed Southeast Asian areas face higher mortality rates, although they remain generally lower than those in SSA, due to inconsistent access to medical care, where HIV-positive women face increased susceptibility to persistent HPV infections and rapid disease progression.41,42 These contrasts highlight how disparities in healthcare access and risk factor prevalence shape regional outcomes.

The mortality predictors highlighted in this SSA-centered review—advanced diagnosis stage, age above 65, HIV-positive status, treatment solely with radiotherapy, and anemia—further demonstrate both global trends and local variations shaped by regional factors. Diagnosis at an advanced stage, showing a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.6 (95% CI: 2.1–3.2) in SSA, is a significant global predictor, but its occurrence is considerably elevated in SSA because of systemic obstacles to early detection, unlike in Western nations and Australia, where regular screening diminishes late-stage cases.43,44 Individuals over 65 years consistently show higher mortality rates across various regions, although this effect is lessened in Western countries and Australia due to improved healthcare access for seniors, unlike the resource limitations faced in Sub-Saharan Africa.45 The HIV-positive status, associated with an HR of 1.52 (95% CI: 1.06–2.19) in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), emerges as a notable region-specific element due to elevated HIV prevalence, elevating mortality risks more significantly than in Western nations, developed Asia, and Australia, where reduced HIV rates and comprehensive care lessen its impact.46,47

Radiotherapy treatment alone (HR=1.41, 95% CI: 1.22–1.63) indicates worse outcomes in SSA because of restricted access to multimodal therapy, which is the norm in Western nations and Australia, supported by more robust healthcare systems.48,49 Anemia, which increases the mortality risk in SSA by twofold (HR=2.0, 95% CI: 1.5–2.5), is worsened by malnutrition and co-existing health issues, being more common in SSA compared to Western nations and Australia, where improved nutrition and healthcare lessen its effects.50,51 These indicators highlight how SSA’s distinct challenges—poverty, inadequate health literacy, and scarce resources—intensify mortality relative to areas with better prevention and treatment capabilities.

Socio-economic and public health settings further clarify these inequalities, as SSA’s elevated mortality is associated with delayed care-seeking and co-morbidities such as anemia, whereas Western nations, developed Asia, and Australia utilize higher socio-economic status and effective interventions to enhance outcomes. Australia’s achievements, for example, arise from its established screening program, which SSA could modify even with limited resources.52 Global analyses frequently highlight increased mortality rates in low- and middle-income regions,53,54 yet this study’s specific emphasis on SSA quantifies factors such as HIV status and treatment constraints, providing insights that are less highlighted in reviews from Western nations and developed Asia, which focus on screening and vaccination achievements.55,56 This review underscores SSA's unique difficulties—elevated HIV rates, restricted treatment availability, and delayed diagnoses—offering practical information for regional health policies, including increasing screening, improving treatment options, and combining HIV-cervical cancer care, essential measures to tackle SSA’s unequal mortality burden.57,58

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this review consist of its thorough search approach utilizing various databases (eg, PubMed, Embase, web of science, CINAHL, Cochrane library, Google scholar and AJOL), encompassing a wide range of evidence from 57 studies across SSA. The strict methodology, which involves quality evaluation using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), boosts the dependability of the results, with 64.9% of studies classified as high quality (NOS ≥ 7). The incorporation of various study designs and subgroup analyses enhances the reliability of the aggregated estimates.

Nonetheless, there are constraints. Substantial heterogeneity (I²=95.72%, P<.01) across studies indicates notable variability, likely stemming from variations in study methodology, population features, and healthcare systems. This variability could lower the accuracy of the combined incidence estimate. Moreover, Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test (P=.12), which indicated no significant publication bias, supported by no clear asymmetry in the funnel plot (Figure 6). These methods are robust for detecting publication bias in meta-analyses with high heterogeneity (Sterne et al, 2000; Higgins & Green, 2011). While Begg’s test and the trim-and-fill method could provide additional validation, Egger’s test is more powerful in this context, and the trim-and-fill method is considered supplementary when Egger’s results are nonsignificant (Sterne et al, 2011). Future studies may include these tests to further confirm these findings.

Conclusion and implications

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate a pooled mortality rate of 32.06%, 95% CI: 29.71%–34.41%). for cervical cancer patients in SSA, with advanced stage at diagnosis, age above 65 years, HIV-positive status, treatment restricted to radiotherapy, and anemia recognized as key predictors. These results emphasize an urgent public health issue, requiring improved screening, increased access to treatment, and focused interventions for at-risk groups. For both practice and policy, enhancing early detection, increasing HPV vaccination rates, and strengthening healthcare infrastructure is essential. Implementing HPV vaccination campaigns in SSA faces challenges such as limited healthcare infrastructure, vaccine hesitancy due to cultural or informational barriers, and logistical issues like maintaining cold chains. These can be addressed through community-based education, integration with existing HIV and maternal health programs, and partnerships with international organizations like GAVI. Additionally, lifestyle medicine, including smoking cessation, improved nutrition, and increased physical activity, may reduce HPV persistence and comorbidities such as obesity, which are associated with advanced cervical cancer, thereby supporting prevention and early intervention efforts. Future studies need to concentrate on neglected areas, obstacles to receiving care, and the effectiveness of interventions to further decrease cervical cancer death rates in SSA.

The results support enhanced prevention and management of cervical cancer in SSA. From a clinical perspective, broadening screening initiatives to identify cases sooner might lower the incidence of advanced-stage diagnoses, which is a key factor in mortality. Policy initiatives must focus on HPV vaccination to reduce incidence and enhance access to chemo-radiotherapy, which provides superior survival rates compared to radiotherapy alone. Focused measures for at-risk populations, like HIV-positive women and older patients, are essential. Funding healthcare infrastructure to tackle inequalities in treatment access and quality is essential to reduce the 32.06% death rate.

Multiple research gaps require focus. The unequal geographical representation, with limited research from nations such as Mali and Chad, indicates a requirement for expanded data gathering throughout SSA to comprehensively outline the mortality burden. Future research should investigate obstacles to early diagnosis and access to treatment, including socioeconomic elements and healthcare system constraints, which are still insufficiently studied. Assessing the impact of interventions such as the scaling up of screening or the expansion of chemo-radiotherapy in SSA settings is likewise essential. Longitudinal studies featuring standardized follow-up durations could offer more profound insights into survival patterns and the effects of predictors over time, tackling the existing issue of inconsistent time-to-event.
