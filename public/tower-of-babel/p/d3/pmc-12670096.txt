Coagulation abnormalities in children with chronic liver disease and the association of these abnormalities with disease severity
Authors: Rubaiyat Alam, Mst Arifa Main, Khan Lamia Nahid, Mohammad Rukunuzzaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThis study aimed to evaluate the coagulation profile in children with chronic liver disease (CLD) and to investigate the relationship between coagulation abnormalities and disease severity.MethodsFifty-one consecutive children (mean age 7.4 ± 4.9 years) diagnosed with CLD were prospectively included and stratified by the Child–Pugh class (A, B, C). Laboratory data, including the complete blood count, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen, D-dimer, and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score, were analyzed. Logistic regression identified predictors of the Child–Pugh class.ResultsAutoimmune hepatitis (31.3%) and Wilson's disease (27.4%) were the most common etiologies. Clinical features, such as gastrointestinal bleeding, ascites, and encephalopathy, were more common in Child–Pugh class C (p = 0.002). The INR (p = 0.017), the APTT (p = 0.018), and D-dimer concentrations (p = 0.001) increased with disease severity, while albumin concentrations decreased. The INR (OR: 86.8 for B vs A; OR: 82.4 for C vs A, p = 0.004) and hypoalbuminemia (OR: 0.11 for C vs A, p = 0.004) were the most robust independent predictors. D-dimer concentrations predicted advanced disease but lost significance after adjustment. The ALBI score was correlated with albumin, the INR, prothrombin time, the APTT, and D-dimer, while Child–Pugh score correlations were weaker. Fibrinogen concentrations were reduced in 64.7% of patients but not associated with severity.ConclusionThe INR and albumin are reliable predictors of disease severity in pediatric CLD. The ALBI score provides more reproducible assessment of hepatic reserve than the Child–Pugh score and may be a useful tool for risk stratification in children.Keywords: Albumin-bilirubin score, Children, Chronic liver disease, Child–pugh score, D-dimer, Fibrinolytic activity, Fibrinogen, Hemostasis

Introduction

Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among children in Bangladesh. At present, no epidemiological studies have reported the incidence or prevalence of pediatric CLD in Bangladesh. Therefore, the understanding of pediatric CLD in Bangladesh is largely informed by regional trends and global literature. One of the major complications associated with CLD is coagulopathy. The liver is where all clotting factors and their inhibitors are synthesized, and is thus essential for preserving hemostasis.1 Hemostasis is a dynamic process resulting from the balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors. Therefore, liver damage from CLD can lead to various coagulation abnormalities that disrupt this balance, affecting clotting and fibrinolysis.2 In addition to synthesis, the hepatic reticuloendothelial system also clears activated coagulation factors and enzyme–inhibitor complexes.3 Therefore, coagulation abnormalities are common features of acute liver disease and CLD.4 The range of hemostatic defects includes impaired synthesis of clotting factors, excessive fibrinolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombocytopenia, and platelet dysfunction.3 These abnormalities can lead to complications ranging from minor localized bleeding to massive, life-threatening hemorrhages or thrombus formation. In advanced liver disease, the coagulation system is severely affected, with a reduction in the production of procoagulant and anticoagulant factors.5 Patients with liver cirrhosis often experience severe coagulopathy, leading to bleeding and clotting due to impaired production of coagulation factors. The imbalance in the coagulation system, along with thrombocytopenia, constitute the primary hemostatic abnormalities associated with CLD, and these often contribute to portal hypertension.6 Hemostasis can be rebalanced in CLD.3

Fibrinolysis occurs as a primary process or as a secondary occurrence in certain clinical situations. An elevation in D-dimer concentrations is commonly observed after major surgery, recent trauma, during sepsis, or as a consequence of thromboembolic events.7 In the absence of such triggers, elevated D-dimer concentrations indicate hyperfibrinolysis, particularly in cases involving cirrhosis and ascites.

Liver function tests, including standard laboratory evaluations, are essential for assessing coagulopathy in liver disease. Tests, such as the prothrombin time (PT), the international normalized ratio (INR), the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen concentrations are routinely conducted to monitor the coagulation status in patients with CLD. This assessment becomes even more critical when a patient with a history of CLD requires surgery because it helps evaluate the risk of bleeding.

While various tests assess the coagulation status, there are currently no routine tests for evaluating the fibrinolytic status, although this process can greatly affect the bleeding tendency in these patients. Coagulation followed by fibrinolytic activity causes a decrease in fibrinogen concentrations while increasing fibrin degradation products.8 D-dimer, a stable measure formed by the enzymatic breakdown of cross-linked fibrin, is used in the diagnosis of conditions, such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.9 Based on the fact that CLD is associated with disordered hemostasis, complications could arise not only from coagulation defects but also from altered clot lysis. Therefore, measuring D-dimer concentrations can provide important insights into potential disruptions in the fibrinolytic pathway.

Several scoring systems are used to assess the severity and prognosis of CLD. Based on liver function test results and clinical indicators, the Child–Pugh score rates the severity of the disease and has been demonstrated to predict survival. Using total points, patients are classified into the three categories Child A, Child B, and Child C.10 Studies have shown that plasma D-dimer concentrations, along with PT and INR levels, increase with the severity of liver disease, while fibrinogen concentrations decrease from Child–Pugh classes A to C.9 Besides coagulation defects, increased fibrinolytic activity may also contribute substantially to the bleeding tendency associated with liver disease, and fibrinogen concentrations decline with more severe cirrhosis.11

Coagulation problems in children with CLD are poorly understood and poorly documented. Therefore, this study aimed to examine differences in the coagulation profile in children with CLD and evaluate the association between coagulation abnormalities and the severity of liver disease.

Materials and methods

This was an analytical cross-sectional study. The study followed the STROBE statement for reporting observational studies and was conducted at the Pediatric Gastroenterology & Nutrition Department of a tertiary care national level referral hospital in Bangladesh from July 2023 to June 2024. Fifty-one children were admitted to the hospital and subsequently enrolled in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: children younger than 18 years who had been diagnosed with CLD for the first time, with various etiologies; CLD was based on the clinical presentation, routine laboratory tests, and an ultrasound examination of the abdomen; and no children had received corticosteroid therapy before inclusion. Children were excluded from the study for the following reasons: sepsis or acute gastrointestinal bleeding; they were using vitamin K antagonists or direct oral anticoagulants; they had other hemostatic abnormalities (e.g., aplastic anemia, leukemia, lymphoma, sepsis, and vasculitis); they had a history of taking certain medications (e.g., sulphonamides, penicillin, cephalosporins, thiazide diuretics, and cytotoxic drugs); or they had congenital clotting factor defects (e.g., hemophilia, afibrinogenemia, and deficiencies in factors V and XI). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of all participants before enrollment. The study was approved by the university's institutional review board (reference: BSMMU/2023/5588).

The fifty one patients were categorized into three groups on the basis of disease severity using the Child–Pugh score: Child–Pugh class A (5–6 points), Child–Pugh class B (7–9 points), and Child–Pugh class C (10–15 points). Child–Pugh scoring was performed independently by two pediatric hepatologists, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score was also calculated using the following formula to assess the severity of liver function: ALBI score = [0.66 × log 10 (bilirubin in μmol/L)] – [0.085 × albumin in g/L]. The ALBI was classified into three grades: grade 1, ≤ −2.6; grade 2, > −2.6 and ≤ −1.39; and grade 3, > −1.39.12

In every patient, demographic data and their medical history were recorded. The collected data included age, sex, a history of bleeding tendency, and signs of jaundice, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, or ascites, and any evidence of bleeding. Routine liver function tests were performed for all patients. These tests included measurement of serum bilirubin (total and direct), serum alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and serum albumin concentrations. Additionally, the PT, APTT, INR, D-dimer concentrations, serum fibrinogen concentrations, and complete blood count were measured in all participants. When clinically indicated, the patients underwent special investigations, including tests for HBsAg, anti-HCV, and serum ceruloplasmin, a slit-lamp examination for detection of Kayser–Fleischer rings, urinary copper estimation (before and after a penicillamine challenge), and antinuclear antibody, anti-smooth muscle antibody, and anti-liver–kidney microsomal antibody testing, to identify the etiology.

Abdominal ultrasound was performed in all patients, while computed tomography imaging was obtained in selected cases as required. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was also carried out when clinically indicated.

Plasma fibrinogen and D-dimer concentrations were measured using the SYSmex CS 1600 automated coagulation analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), which applies an enzyme immunoassay-based, latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric principle. The detection range for fibrinogen was 50–1000 mg/dL with an intra-assay coefficient of variation of <5%. The detection range for D-dimer was 0.2–20 μg/mL of fibrinogen equivalent units with an intra-assay coefficient of variation of <6%. Daily quality control was performed using manufacturer-provided control plasma to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

After collecting the data, they were manually checked and analyzed using the computer-based program SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Chicago, IL), version 26. Quantitative data are shown as the mean and standard deviation, and qualitative data are expressed using the frequency and percentage. A univariate analysis for numerical variables was conducted using an independent t-test for two groups and a one-way analysis of variance test for three groups. The relationships between categorical variables was examined using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. We performed multivariate logistic regression to calculate odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for potential confounders, including age and sex. Box plots were created to illustrate the differences in D-dimer concentrations across various Child–Pugh classes (A, B, and C). Correlations between evaluated variables were assessed using Spearman's and Pearson's correlation tests. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

This minor deviation was not expected to affect the study's statistical power.

Results

In this study, 51 consecutive children diagnosed with CLD participated, and they had a mean age of 7.4 ± 4.9 years. Of the participants, there were 27 (52.9%) girls and 24 (47.1%) boys. The distribution of the children across the Child–Pugh classification groups was as follows: 14 (27.5%) in the Child A group, 19 (37.3%) in the Child B group, and 18 (35.3%) in the Child C group. The mean age of children in the Child A group was 8.8 ± 4.1 years, and 71.4% were boys. In the Child B group, the mean age was 6.4 ± 5.1 years, and 42.1% were boys. In the Child C group, the mean age was 7.5 ± 5.2 years, and 33.3% were boys. There were no age or sex differences between the groups (Table 1).

The most common cause of CLD among the participants was autoimmune hepatitis, which affected 16 of 51 (31.3%) children. Half (n = 8, 50%) of these children were in the Child B group. Other etiologies included Wilson's disease (n = 14 27.4%), biliary cirrhosis (n = 13, 25.4%), and cryptogenic causes (n = 8, 15.6%). Clinical features were analyzed to identify differences between the three groups (Table 1). Gastrointestinal bleeding and signs of encephalopathy were more prevalent in the Child C group than in the other groups. Additionally, the presence of ascites was significantly higher in the Child C group than in the other groups (p = 0.002). None of the patients had portal vein thrombosis detected during the study period.

When we compared laboratory parameters, no significant differences in the platelet count (p = 0.23), total bilirubin concentrations (p = 0.186), the prothrombin time (p = 0.176), albumin concentrations (p = 0.176), or fibrinogen concentrations were observed between the groups (Table 1). However, the INR (p = 0.017), the APTT (p = 0.018), and D-dimer concentrations (p = 0.001) were significantly higher in the advanced stages of CLD (Table 1). In our cohort, 31 (60.8%) children showed elevated D-dimer concentrations (>0.5 μg/mL. The mean D-dimer value was lowest in the Child A group (0.5 ± 0.35 μg/mL) and progressively increased in the Child B group (1.1 ± 1.1 μg/mL and the Child C group (3.1 ± 1.9 μg/mL) (Fig. 1).

Multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of the Child–Pugh class. Several coagulation and liver function markers were significant predictors of the Child–Pugh class. Elevated D-dimer concentrations were associated with an increased odds of class C versus A (OR: 7.98, 95% CI: 1.50–42.4, p = 0.015) but not class B versus A. The PT was significantly associated with an increased odds of class B versus A (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.42, p = 0.018) and class C versus A (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.42, p = 0.021). The INR showed a strong association with a higher class for B versus A (OR: 86.8, 95% CI: 4.2–1773.8, p = 0.004) and class C versus A (OR: 82.4, 95% CI: 4.0–1684.7, p = 0.004). A higher APTT was significantly associated with an increased odds of being classified as B versus A (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.24, p = 0.019) and C versus A (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.25, p = 0.013). Hypoalbuminemia was significantly associated with the Child–Pugh class. Lower albumin concentrations strongly predicted being in class C versus A (OR: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.49, p = 0.004) but not being in class B versus A (p = 0.327). Age and male sex male were not significant predictors for the Child–Pugh class, although there was a trend toward lower odds in boys (Table 2).

The correlation analysis showed that the Child–Pugh score was moderately positively correlated with the PT (r = 0.52, p < 0.0001), the INR (r = 0.52, p < 0.0001), the APTT (r = 0.37, p = 0.006), and D-dimer concentrations (r = 0.52, p < 0.0001), and negatively correlated with albumin concentrations (r = −0.508, p < 0.0001). Similarly, the ALBI score showed significant associations with coagulation and liver function markers. The ALBI was positively correlated with the PT (r = 0.38, p = 0.005), the INR (r = 0.414, p = 0.002), the APTT (r = 0.308, p = 0.028), and D-dimer concentrations (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), but strongly negatively correlated with albumin concentrations (r = −0.917, p < 0.0001) (Table 3). These findings indicate that Child–Pugh and ALBI scores are closely associated with coagulation abnormalities and impaired synthetic function in chronic liver disease.

We found low fibrinogen concentrations (<200 mg/dL in 33 children, with a mean value of 165.44 ± 110.9 mg/dL. Among the nine children who experienced bleeding, two had extremely low fibrinogen concentrations (<100 mg/dL). The mean fibrinogen concentration in children with bleeding was 144.5 ± 58.5 mg/dL compared with 169.9 ± 119.3 mg/dL in those without bleeding. No significant relationship was observed between fibrinogen concentrations and Child–Pugh classification (r = 0.04, p = 0.77).

Discussion

This study assessed coagulation and hepatic synthetic function abnormalities in a cohort of 51 Bangladeshi children with CLD and examined the association of these abnormalities with Child–Pugh and ALBI scores. Reliable population-level incidence data of pediatric CLD in Bangladesh are not available, and evidence of these data is derived largely from hospital-based and tertiary-center case series. Studies from Bangladeshi referral centers have shown that inherited/metabolic disease, particularly Wilson's disease, is a major contributor to pediatric CLD, along with viral hepatitis and immune-mediated liver disease.10 On the basis of Child–Pugh classification of CLD, which assesses the severity of the condition, the participants in this study were divided into three groups. Routine coagulation tests often fail to detect bleeding tendencies in patients with liver cirrhosis. Hyperfibrinolysis can lead to major bleeding complications in these patients, with incidence rates ranging from 19% to 95%.9 Nevertheless, what specifically causes hyperfibrinolysis in liver cirrhosis remains unknown. One probable cause of hyperfibrinolysis in liver disease is the reduced clearance of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). Plasminogen is converted to plasmin by tPA, which causes fibrin clots to break down. Normally, plasminogen activator inhibitor binds to tPA in healthy individuals, thereby limiting its activity. In cases of CLD, elevated tPA concentrations may result from increased release by active endothelium, impaired clearance by the damaged liver, or decreased concentrations of anti-plasminogen activators. Additionally, the reduced production of fibrinolytic inhibitors may exacerbate this condition. Ultimately, these factors result in elevated concentrations of D-dimer, which serve as a quantifiable marker for assessing the fibrinolytic system. D-dimer is currently considered a reliable indicator of fibrinolysis.13,14

We observed progressive impairment of hepatic synthetic and coagulation function with advancing disease severity, as reflected in Child–Pugh and ALBI scores. The INR and hypoalbuminemia were the strongest independent predictors of Child–Pugh classification, consistent with their well-established roles as markers of hepatic dysfunction. An elevated PT and APTT were also correlated with disease progression, while D-dimer concentrations, although significantly increased in advanced disease, lost their predictive power after adjustment for the INR and albumin. These findings indicate that the coagulation profile in pediatric CLD is characterized by impaired factor synthesis and enhanced fibrinolysis, with the INR and albumin serving as the most robust discriminators of disease severity. There was also a notable increase in the PT, INR, and APTT when CLD progressed from Child–Pugh class A to C. Our results clearly indicated that patients with CLD experienced rapid fibrinolysis and impaired coagulation. Agarwal et al. reported elevated plasma D-dimer concentrations in 63% of patients with cirrhosis, while other studies similarly documented stepwise increases across Child–Pugh classes.15 Our study showed that plasma D-dimer concentrations rose progressively from Child–Pugh class A to C, which is consistent with findings in previous studies of pediatric and adult cirrhotic populations.16, 17, 18 The mechanistic basis for this finding lies in hyperfibrinolysis. Impaired hepatic clearance of tPA, increased endothelial release, and reduced levels of fibrinolytic inhibitors lead to excessive plasmin generation and fibrin breakdown. Therefore, elevated D-dimer concentrations reflect an activated fibrinolytic system in cirrhosis. Additionally, the inverse association between D-dimer and serum albumin concentrations in our cohort further highlights the association between worsening hepatic synthetic function and systemic hemostatic imbalance.16

Routine coagulation tests, such as the PT and APTT, remain central in assessing hepatic dysfunction, although their predictive value for the risk of bleeding is limited. In our study, the PT and INR were significantly associated with progression of the Child–Pugh, class, reflecting reduced synthesis of vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, and X) and natural anticoagulants, such as protein C.6,19,20 The positive correlations between the ALBI score and coagulation markers (PT, INR, APTT, and D-dimer) further emphasize the interplay between synthetic failure and fibrinolysis in CLD.12

Importantly, our comparison of prognostic indices indicates the superiority of the ALBI over Child–Pugh classification in objectively capturing hepatic reserve. While the Child–Pugh score incorporates subjective variables, such as ascites and encephalopathy, which dilute the weight of biochemical measures, the ALBI score relies solely on albumin and bilirubin. In this study, we found a strong negative correlation between the ALBI and albumin concentrations compared with only a moderate correlation for the Child–Pugh class. The ALBI also consistently changed with coagulation derangements, suggesting that it may serve as a more reproducible and quantitative tool for assessing liver function in pediatric CLD. This finding is in line with previous studies in adults with hepatocellular carcinoma and cirrhosis, and our findings extend this evidence to the pediatric population.21

In our study cohort, we observed lower fibrinogen concentrations in class C, which is consistent with previous research22 However, there was no significant relationship between disease severity and this finding. Rizzo et al. found that patients with moderate cirrhosis had higher fibrinogen concentrations than healthy individuals, while those with severe cirrhosis had lower fibrinogen concentrations.23 Fibrinogen is an acute-phase protein. Therefore, patients in the early stages of liver disease may experience increased liver inflammation, resulting in a decreased clearance rate or an increased release of fibrinogen.24 Previous studies have shown that low fibrinogen concentrations are associated with an increased bleeding risk in cirrhosis, but our findings suggest limited discriminatory value in pediatric staging. However, critically low fibrinogen concentrations may still predispose to hemorrhage.25,26

This study has several limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design precluded longitudinal follow-up, which would have strengthened the evaluation of D-dimer as a predictive marker for liver-related mortality and as a diagnostic indicator of complications, such as liver failure and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. Third, certain hepatic function indicators, such as gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, and cholinesterase, were not routinely available and thus could not be assessed. Fourth, no subgroup analysis was performed by etiology because the limited number of patients within each etiological category precluded meaningful statistical comparisons. Finally, we did not analyze the association between bleeding events and coagulation indicators because only nine children experienced bleeding, which limited the statistical power to detect meaningful relationships. Future larger, multicenter studies incorporating broader biochemical markers, sufficient sample sizes for etiological subgroup analysis, and adequate numbers of bleeding events are required to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of coagulation disturbances in pediatric CLD. Although the INR was a significant independent predictor of higher Child–Pugh classes, the large ORs and wide 95 CIs indicated limited estimate precision, which was likely due to the small sample size and uneven group distribution. Therefore, while the INR appears to reflect hepatic decompensation, its effect size should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger cohorts.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications for managing pediatric CLD. Identification of the INR and albumin concentrations as the most reliable predictors of disease severity highlights the utility of simple, inexpensive, and widely available laboratory tests for routine risk stratification, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced diagnostics may not be feasible. Furthermore, the superior performance of the ALBI score over the Child–Pugh score indicates the potential of the ALBI as an objective and reproducible tool for clinical decision-making. Incorporation of the ALBI into routine assessment could improve the accuracy of prognostication, aid in determining the optimal timing for referral to specialized centers, and support prioritization for liver transplantation. By reducing reliance on subjective clinical variables, such as ascites and encephalopathy, the ALBI may also enhance consistency in disease staging across clinicians and institutions. Overall, these findings support the translation of simple laboratory parameters and ALBI scoring into clinical practice to strengthen the evaluation and management of pediatric CLD.

Conclusions

In this cohort of Bangladeshi children with CLD, coagulation abnormalities and impaired hepatic synthetic function were closely associated with disease severity. Among routine laboratory markers, the INR and serum albumin were the strongest independent predictors of higher Child–Pugh classes, highlighting their value as simple and reliable indicators of hepatic decompensation in pediatric practice. Although D-dimer levels increased with worsening disease, their predictive significance diminished after adjustment, suggesting that hyperfibrinolysis primarily reflects global hepatic dysfunction.

The ALBI score demonstrated stronger and more consistent correlations with biochemical parameters than the Child–Pugh classification, indicating that this objective, laboratory-based index may offer a more reproducible assessment of hepatic reserve in children. While low fibrinogen levels were common, they did not correlate with disease stage.

These findings emphasize the utility of INR, albumin, and ALBI scoring in routine risk stratification of pediatric CLD. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate ALBI in children and to further clarify the role of fibrinolytic activity in predicting clinical outcomes.
