Genome-Wide Identification of the RhoGAP Gene Family and Main Function of OsRhoGAP2 in Seed Germination of Rice by Transcriptome Analysis
Authors: Chaowei Fang, Yuanqing Nie, Yifan Bi, Wenjing An, Dongdong Jia, Weihong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRho GTPase-activating proteins (RhoGAPs) play crucial roles in regulating various biological processes. However, the functions of RhoGAP family genes in rice (Oryza sativa) remain largely unexplored. Here, we identified 19 RhoGAP genes in rice, and preliminarily analyzed the genes information, expression patterns, and evolutionary relationship with AtRhoGAPs in Arabidopsis. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, we generated loss-of-function mutants of OsRhoGAP2 (rhogap2) and found that seed germination was significantly delayed compared to the wild type (WT). Further analysis revealed that α-amylase activity was reduced in rhogap2 germinating seeds. RNA-seq profiling identified 291 upregulated and 130 downregulated genes in the mutant, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) primarily enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and other metabolic pathways. Notably, most phenylpropanoid biosynthesis-related genes exhibited increased expression in rhogap2 germinating seeds. These findings establish a foundational framework for future functional studies of RhoGAP genes in rice and provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms by which RhoGAPs regulate seed germination in plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00843-y.Keywords: RhoGAP, Seed germination, α-Amylase, RNA-seq, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis

Introduction

As a staple food crop, rice plays a vital role in global food security, making yield improvement a key priority for sustaining populations worldwide (Wing et al. 2018). Seed germination marks the initiation of the plant life cycle, playing a critical role in seedling establishment and significantly influencing subsequent crop productivity and grain quality (Chen et al. 2019). Selecting seeds with high germination vigor and uniformity is essential for optimizing seedling emergence rates and field performance (Damaris et al. 2019). Therefore, the identification and cloning key genes governing germination mechanisms, as well as the elucidation of their functional roles, can significantly enhance precision breeding efforts in rice.

The process of seed germination can be divided into three distinct stages: stage I, the dry and mature seeds absorb water and swelling rapidly, causing the transition in membrane phospholipids composition from the gel phase to hydration liquid crystal stage. The membrane system is exacerbated damaged, leading to solute leakage inside the cell while simultaneously accelerating moisture absorption and reducing concentration inhibition, thereby promoting seed germination in this stage (Waterworth et al. 2010). Stage II, the water swelling reaches to dead state, and the products of transcription and translation in the seed are transformed (Howell et al. 2006). The protein abundances of storage or seed maturation-related proteins are decreased, while glycolysis and α-amylase-related proteins are increased in germinated seeds (Yang et al. 2007). Stage III, The endosperm is broken, the radicle emerges through the seed coat as storage materials are degraded, energy is sustained supplied, and the water absorption rate of seed increases steadily, eventually completing the germination process (Bewley 1997). Among them, stage II is the most important for seed germination (Li et al. 2020). Seed germination is influenced not only by the external factors such as illumination (Hirano et al. 2023; Watanabe et al. 2022), temperature (Fukuda et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024; Nie et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2024), water, and salt contents in soil (Fukuda et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2023), but also the internal factor, such as plant hormones, α-amylase, secondary metabolism etc. (Browse and Xin 2001; Evans and Etherington 1990; Seo et al. 2006; Sugimura et al. 2015; Wei et al. 2015; Zhu 2001). Previous study found that the seed germination was affected by GA, ABA, BR, IAA and ethylene (Han and Yang 2015; He et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024a, b; Wei et al. 2015). In seed germination, GA is synthesized in the embryo and transported to the aleurone layer, where it induces the expression of α-amylase genes, leading to synthesis of α-amylase, thereby facilitating the hydrolysis of starch in the endosperm and supplying essential materials and energy for seed germination (Kaneko et al. 2002). The primary energy source for seed germination is sugars produced from the degradation of endosperm starch, a process that requires for the coordinated action of α-amylase, β-amylase, debranching enzyme, and glycosidase enzymes (Pujadas and Palau 2001). The α-amylase worked as the key amylase in starch degradation during seed germination and seedling development (Lee et al. 2014; Sugimoto et al. 1998). The presence of α-amylase is undetectable in desiccated seeds. However, during seed germination, the aleurone layer exhibits differential gene expression for α-amylase, including RAmy3B, RAmy3C, and RAmy3E (Karrer et al. 1991; Sugimoto et al. 1998). These genes encode proteins are responsible for synthesizing and secreting α-amylase into the endosperm, thereby promoting starch hydrolysis and providing energy for seed germination (Karrer et al. 1991).

RopGAPs/RhoGAPs, which function as Rop/Rho GTPase activating proteins, are capable of markedly increasing the GTPase activity of Rop/Rho and transform Rho proteins into the GDP-bound inactive statement (Cherfils and Zeghouf 2013). The Rop/Rho proteins participate in many physiological processes by binding to GTP and GDP, respectively, and undergoing dynamic transitions between active and inactive states (Schaefer et al. 2011). RhoGAPs serve as a critical regulator in the initiation of secondary cell wall patterning in xylem by specifically activating the Rac-type GTPase (Oda and Fukuda 2012). AtREN1 (a new RhoGAP in Arabidopsis) and NtRhoGAP1 interact with AtRop1 and NtRac5, respectively, and mediated polarity growth of pollen tube in Arabidopsis and tobacco (Hwang et al. 2008; Sun et al. 2015). AtRopGAP4 improve the tolerance of oxygen stress by regulating the expression of acetate dehydrogenase gene AtADH through Rop signal transduction (Baxter-Burrell et al. 2002). Besides, AtRopGAP1, AtRopGAP4, and HvMAGAP1 enhance the plant resistance to powdery mildew by inhibiting the activity of Rop GTP in Arabidopsis and barley (Hoefle and Hückelhoven 2014; Huesmann et al. 2011). In rice, OsSPIN6, a RhoGAP, has been reported to be hydrolyzed by the E3 ubiquitin ligase OsSPL11, which mediates programmed cell death (PCD) and defense by regulating the activity of OsRac1 (Liu et al. 2015). The Rho-family GTPase OsRac1 controls rice grain size and yield by regulating cell division (Zhang et al. 2019). Besides, the OsRopGEF3 protein interacts with OsRac3 to regulate root hair elongation and ROS formation (Kim et al. 2021). However, the functions of rice RhoGAPs have not been systematically studied, and there are relatively few studies on RhoGAPs in seed germination of rice.

Here, we updated the number and nomenclature of the rice RhoGAP gene family, and carried out the phylogenetic tree analysis of RhoGAPs in rice and Arabidopsis. We also analyzed the expression patterns of rice RhoGAPs genes. Moreover, we identified and characterized a rice seed germination-related gene, OsRhoGAP2, which encodes a Rop GTPase-activating protein. OsRhoGAP2 displays constitutive expression at root, stem, leaf, sheath, pillow, and developing seeds. The OsRhoGAP2 knockout lines showed delayed germination and significantly reduced α-amylase activity in germinating seeds for 48 h. The OsRhOGAP2 gene may be involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway during seed germination. Our findings indicate that OsRhoGAP2 plays crucial roles in seed dormancy and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of RhoGAPs regulating seed germination in plants.

Results

The Nomenclature, Characterization, and Tissue Expression Analysis of Rice RhoGAP Genes

Accurate identification and a unified nomenclature are essential for future research into the RhoGAP gene family in rice. Here, we identified a total of 19 RhoGAP genes in the rice genome and designated them as OsRhoGAP1 to OsRhoGAP19 according to their chromosomal locations. The 19 OsRhoGAP genes were randomly distributed across 12 chromosomes (Fig. 1A, Table S1). Four genes (21.53%) were distributed on chromosome 3 and 7, which contain the largest number of OsRhoGAPs, while there was no OsRhoGAPs appeared on chromosomes 5, 8 and 10 (Fig. 1A, Tables S1). The length of rice RhoGAP proteins ranged from 129 to 871 amino acids, the molecular weight ranged from 14.37 kDa to 95.95 kDa with the predicted isoelectric point ranging from 4.42 to 9.32. Subcellular localization prediction showed that the 19 OsRhoGAP proteins are mainly localized in nucleus, expect OsRhoGAP7 and OsRhoGAP18 localized in cytoplasm, mitochondrion and chloroplast, respectively (Table S1).

Chromosome numbers are indicated at the top of each chromosome, respectively. The physical location of each OsRhoGAP gene is indicated to the right of chromosomes. Mb, million base pair. To analyze the phylogenetic relationship of RhoGAP gene family, the 19 and 11 RhoGAP protein sequences in rice and Arabidopsis were used to construct the phylogenetic tree by using the maximum likelihood method and the MEGA11 program. All the identified RhoGAPs were classified into three groups (named as group 1 to group 3) based on the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1B). Group 1 could be further classified into two clades (Clades 1–1 and 1–2), which contained 14 RhoGAPs, including 8 in rice, and 6 in Arabidopsis. Group 2 contains 12 RhoGAPs, including 7 in rice and 5 in Arabidopsis, which could be further classified into three clades (Clades 2−1 to 2–3). Group 3 could be classified into one clade (Clades 3−1), contains 4 rice RhoGAPs (Fig. 1B), indicating that these genes appear after the divergency of dicotyledons and monocotyledons during plant evolution. In general, the phylogenetic analysis results provide not only the phylogenetic relationship of the RhoGAP genes, but also giving some helpful information for exploring the functions of unknown RhoGAP genes in rice and Arabidopsis.

To predicted the function of OsRhoGAP genes more accurately, we analyzed the expression of OsRhoGAPs in different tissues of rice leaf, root, and stem at the online website: Rice Genome Annotation Project (https://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml). Based on the expression heat map, all OsRhoGAPs genes could be classified into two clusters (I and II). The cluster I contains two subclusters (I-1 and I-2), those genes were highly expressed in all tissues (Fig. 2A). The cluster II contains two subclusters (II-1 and II-2). Subcluster II-1 includes five genes with low expression at different tissues of rice, five genes in subcluster II-1 were higher expressed in root and stem (Fig. 2A).

To further explore the functions of OsRhoGAPs genes in reproductive organs of inflorescence, anther, pistil, ovary, embryo, endosperm, and seed development, we analyzed the expression data from Rice Genome Annotation Project (https://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml). The results showed that those OsRhoGAP genes were classified into two clusters (I and II). The cluster I including six genes, which were lower expressed in the above tissues (Fig. 2B). The cluster II contains two subclusters (II-1 and II-2). The subcluster II-1 includes four OsRhoGAP genes (OsRhoGAP15, OsRhoGAP8, OsRhoGAP2, and OsRhoGAP18) were high expressed at different stages, however, those genes in subcluster II-2 were lower expressed in one of those tissues (Fig. 2B). Importantly, the 4 genes in subcluster II-1 were higher expression in endosperms than others genes, indicating that those genes might be function in seed development at late stages or seed germination.

OsRhoGAP2 is Expressed in Multi-tissues of Rice and Regulates Seed Dormancy

To test the above assumption, we chose OsRhoGAP2, whose expression showed a highly and progressively increasing trend as the process advanced in subcluster II-1 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we also analyzed the spatiotemporal expression pattern of OsRhoGAP2 by qPCR using total RNA of root, stem, leaf, sheath, pillow, and the developing seeds at seeding and heading stages of rice. The data indicated that the expression level of OsRhoGAP2 was higher in roots at the seeding stage, but it underwent modulation with a notable increase in expression in the sheaths at the heading stage (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the expression of OsRhoGAP2 in developing seeds showed a rising tendency along with the progression of the process, which was seminar with the heatmap (Figs. 2B and 3B). In germinating seeds, OsRhoGAP2 was higher expressed in endosperm (Figure S1A). These results suggested that OsRhoGAP2 possible function in different organizations, especially in seeds.

To investigate the function of OsRhoGAP2, the CRISPR/cas9 vector was constructed to target the first exon of OsRhoGAP2. We produced and selected three homozygous rhogap2 mutants including rhogap2-1 (with 2-bp deletion at + 172 ~ + 173), rhogap2-2 (with 1-bp deletion at + 172), and rhogap2-3 (with 1-bp deletion at + 20) (Fig. 4A, Figure S1B), which caused the premature termination of OsRhoGAP2 protein translation (Figure S2). Compared with WT, the grain length was significantly increased of rhogap2 mutants, but the grain width was no difference and the thickness was decreased significantly, which caused the 1000-grain weight were no difference with WT (Figure S3). Furthermore, all the three rhogap2 mutants had lower seed germination rate in rice panicle under the simulated germination (Fig. 4B). Besides, the seed germination experiment revealed that the seed germination rates of rhogap2 mutants remained significantly lower than that in WT in germinating 60 h (Fig. 4C). Moreover, we evaluated the seed germination in soil using WT, rhogap2-1, rhogap2-2, and rhogap2-3, the result showed that the seed germination was slowly than that in WT (Figure S4), which were consistent with those shown in Fig. 4C. Indicating that the loss function of OsRhoGAP2 delayed seed germination in rice. Moreover, the expressions of seed germination-related genes OsGA2ox3, OsGA2ox6, OsCPS1 (Otomo et al. 2004), OsKS1 (Margis-Pinheiro et al. 2005), and OsKAO (Yang and Chen 2012; Zhang et al. 2020) were significantly decreased (Fig. 4D). Taken together, OsRhoGAP2 influences the grain morphology and negatively regulates seed germination in rice.

The α-Amylase Activity is Decreased in rhogap2 Germinated Seeds

The α-amylase serves as a critical factor in the germination process by hydrolyzing starch, which supplies the necessary energy for seed germination (Damaris et al. 2019). In qualitative detection, the agar plate displays a pronounced purple hue as a result of the reaction between starch and I2/KI. If α-amylase activity is detected in the germinated seeds, a white zone of starch hydrolysis will appear, leading to a distinctly whitened area within the iodine-stained region. The size and intensity of this whitened area serve as an indicator of the level of α-amylase activity. To investigate whether the delayed germination of rhogap2 is attributable to the alterations in seed α-amylase activity, we conducted qualitative and quantitative analyses of α-amylase activity in germinated rice seeds. The results showed that the white zone of starch hydrolysis surrounding rhogap2 seeds was significantly reduced than that in WT under the normal germination conditions (Fig. 5A and B). The content of α-amylase in rhogap2 which was significantly lower than that in WT (Fig. 5C), the relative expression of α-amylase genes of OsRAmy1C, OsRAmy3B, and OsRAmy3E (Hakata et al. 2012) were down regulated in rhogap2s (Fig. 5D), indicating that the activity and content of α-amylase effect the seed germination of rhogap2. In a word, OsRhOGAP2 decreased α-amylase activity by downregulating the gene expression involved in α-amylase biosynthesis, thereby delaying rice seed germination.

OsRhOGAP2 May be Involved in Phenylpropanoid Metabolism During Seed Germination

To investigate the effect of OsRhOGAP2 on seed germination, RNA-seq analysis was conducted using 48-hour germinated seeds from both WT and rhogap2. The principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate the clustering of groups, the result showed that the excellent repeatability among biological replicates and noticeable differences between WT and rhogap2 samples (P < 0.05). The PCA1 accounted for 94.78% of the variance, and PCA2 for 1.88% of the variance (Fig. 6A). Compared with WT, 291 genes were up regulated and 130 genes were down regulated in the germinating seeds of rhogap2 (Fig. 6B and C). The GO annotation analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were classified into 3 ontologies, including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC), the most abundant DEGs of GO annotation terms were classified in the photosynthesis of BP, cell of CC and lipid binding of MF (Fig. 6D). KEGG analysis presented that the DEGs were mainly classified in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and other pathways (Figs. 6E and 7A) (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Furthermore, the expression of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis-related genes were analysis based on RNA-seq data. Those genes were classified into two clusters (I and II) based on their expression level. The cluster I contains two genes LOC_Os06g48010 and LOC_Os07g01410, which encoded peroxidase precursors, were highly expressed in rhogap2 (Fig. 7A and B). The cluster II contained two subclusters (II-1 and II-2), which were lower expressed at the germinating seeds of WT and rhogap2 (Fig. 7A), among them, LOC_Os07g01420, LOC_Os07g44480, LOC_Os07g31610, and LOC_Os07g44590 in subcluster II-2 were highly expressed in rhogap2 (Fig. 7A and B, and Table S3). All of the six genes in cluster I and subcluster II-2 encoded peroxidases (Table S4), they might be involved in the biosynthesis of hydroxy phenyl lignin, guaiacyl lignin, 5-hydroxy guaiacyl lignin, and syringyl lignin during seed germination (Fig. 7A and 7B). So, we assumed that the OsRhOGAP2 might influence seed germination by involving in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway.

Discussion

OsRhOGAP2 Plays Important Roles in Seed Germination by Reducing α-Amylase Activity in Rice

Although the functions of some RhoGAP genes have been reported in Arabidopsis and rice, their regulatory mechanisms remain largely unexplored, with only a few cases investigated. For example, AtROPGAP3, in conjunction with along with AtROPGEF4, mediates local activation of the plant Rho GTPase ROP11, thereby initiating a distinct pattern of secondary cell walls in xylem cells (Nagashima et al. 2018; Oda and Fukuda 2012). In rice, the RhoGAPSPIN6 interacts with OsSPL11 and OsRac1, thereby negatively regulating PCD and innate immunity in rice (Liu et al. 2015). Here, we updated the number of OsRhoGAP family and investigated the functions those genes by gene expression and evolutionary tree (Figs. 1 and 2). Besides, we identified OsRhOGAP2 based on the genome-wide identification of the RhoGAP gene family and CRISPR/cas9 strategy. OsRhOGAP2 gene is highly expressed at 20 d developing seeds and regulates seed germination of rice (Figs. 3B and 4B and D). In rhogap2 mutant, the expression of gibberellin 20-oxidase genes of OsGA20ox1 and OsGA30 × 2, gibberellin biosynthesis enzyme gene OsKO2, and ent-copalyl diphosphate synthase gene OsCPS1, which related to GA biosynthesis and influence seed germination, are significantly down regulated (Fig. 4E). The α-amylase, which is mainly responsible for breaking down starch, provides the energy required for seed germination (Li et al. 2023). The α-amylase gene encodes starch hydrolase and serves as a key downstream effector under the antagonistic regulation between gibberellin (GA) and abscisic acid (ABA) during cereal seed germination (Xi et al. 2010). The α-amylase synthesized de novo in the aleurone layer or scutellum in response to GA hydrolyzes endospermic starch into soluble sugars, thereby providing essential carbon skeletons and energy for the growing embryo (Penfield et al. 2017). This process consumes significant energy (ATP) and carbon precursors, including phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate, which may compete with primary metabolic processes, including α-amylase production and function, for limited resources during early germination (Weitbrecht et al. 2011). Here, by using a well-designed integrative strategy, we demonstrate that the activity of α-amylase is significantly reduced in rhogap2 mutants (Fig. 5). Suggesting that OsRhoGAP2 may have its specific regulatory mechanisms in delaying seed germination, this process might be controlled by OsRhoGAP2 through influencing α-amylase activity or regulating α-amylase-related genes during seed germination.

OsRhOGAP2 Might be Involved in Phenylpropanoid Biosynthesis During Seed Germination

Phenylpropanoid metabolism produces an enormous and diverse array of secondary metabolites (Vogt 2010). A key point of synergy lies in oxidative stress management. Germination inherently increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Phenylpropanoid derivatives, particularly flavonoids and phenolic acids, act as crucial antioxidants, scavenging ROS and protecting cellular integrity (Sasaki et al. 2013). This protection indirectly safeguards α-amylase and other hydrolytic enzymes from oxidative inactivation, ensuring sustained starch breakdown and energy supply (El-Maarouf-Bouteau et al. 2015). Thus, adequate phenylpropanoid-derived antioxidants support the efficient functioning of α-amylase. The mutants that exhibited altered seed coat development or pigmentation showed reduced dormancy in Arabidopsis thaliana under glasshouse conditions (Debeaujon et al. 2000). Increase in phenylpropanoid gene expression led to elevated concentrations of seed coat procyanidins, thereby altering coat permeability to tetrazolium and potentially affecting the seed dormancy rate (MacGregor et al. 2015). Here, the RNA-seq indicated that 291 genes were up regulated and 130 genes were down regulated in the germinating seeds of rhogap2 (Fig. 6B and C). KEGG analysis showed that DEGs were mainly classified in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and other pathways (Figs. 6E and 7A). The expressions of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis genes, which encode peroxidase precursors, were increased in rhogap2 (https://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml) (Fig. 7B and C). In Suaeda glauca black seeds, most genes related to phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis were upregulated in dormant seeds. Among them, peroxidases were the major components involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (Wang et al. 2024a, b). Meanwhile, AtPER7, the orthologues of LOC_Os07g31610 in Arabidopsis thaliana, was down-regulated by AtMYB70 and participated in modulating the ROS metabolic process in the OE-MYB70 plants, besides, the AtMYB70 negatively regulates seed germination in an ABA-dependent manner by physically interacting with AtABI5, which plays crucial roles in modulating seed germination (Wan et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2019). Besides, RhOGAPs function along with transcription factor of MYB family in plants (Lee and Seo 2019). Collectively, OsRhOGAP2 may contribute to seed germination by influence phenylpropanoid biosynthesis indirectly. It implies that OsRhOGAP2 may participate in germination regulation by influencing secondary metabolic pathways, thus opening up a new direction for the study of seed dormancy mechanisms. However, the downstream mechanism of OsRhoGAP2 remains unclear. Therefore, it is essential to establish a robust verification system to investigate the interaction between OsRhoGAP2 and candidate Rho GTPases. Additionally, constructing the OsRhoGAP2 regulatory network through multi-omics integrated analysis should be prioritized in future studies.

Conclusion

This study provides the comprehensive identification and characterization of the RhoGAP gene family in rice, revealing 19 members designated as OsRhoGAP1 to OsRhoGAP19. Expression profiling demonstrated diverse tissue patterns. Functional characterization of OsRhoGAP2 revealed its critical roles: CRISPR/Cas9-generated rhogap2 mutants exhibited significantly delayed seed germination. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis of germinating mutant seeds identified differential expression in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway genes, particularly upregulation of peroxidase precursors. Collectively, our results establish OsRhoGAP2 as a novel negative regulator of seed germination in rice, acting through α-amylase activity and potentially phenylpropanoid metabolism. This work provides a foundation for understanding RhoGAP functions in rice development and suggests OsRhoGAP2 as a potential target for manipulating grain size and pre-harvest sprouting resistance.

Materials and Methods

Genome-Wide Identification of RhoGAP Genes in Rice

The amino acid sequences of Arabidopsis and rice RhoGAPs were accepted on NCBI Arabidopsis genome database TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) and EnsemblPlants (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). To collect information of physical and chemical properties for maize RhoGAP proteins, Expasy (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to determine molecular weight and theoretical pI, and Cell-PLoc-2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) was used to predict subcellular localization. The expression data of RhoGAP genes in rice was obtained from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (https://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml), the unit of expression scale should be TPM. The heat map was built by Tbtools-II.

Phylogenetic Analysis RhoGAP Genes

Multiple alignments of rice and Arabidopsis RhoGAP amino acid sequences were performed by ClustalW of MEGA11. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with MEGA11 using the maximum likelihood method, and bootstrap values were based on 500 replicates.

Plasmid Construction and Rice Transformation

The Oryza sativa L. ssp. Japonica cultivar Xinfeng 2, which was a widely cultivated variety in Henan province of China, served as the genetic background and wild type (WT). For targeted mutagenesis of OsRhoGAP2, the CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid was constructed using the pC1300-UBI: Cas9 vector as described previously (Xiong et al. 2023). For assembly of two gRNAs, the two targets were designed in the first exon (GAP2 target-1: CGCCGTCCCAGATCCGCTT; GAP2 target-2: GCATCAGGCGGCGAGACAC) (Table S2). Then, the two fragments were cloned into the pC1300-UBI: Cas9 vector digested with Eco31I to construct the knockout vector Cas9-OsRhoGAP2. Then, the Cas9-OsRhoGAP2 vector was transformed into rice Xinfeng 2 via Agrobacterium-mediated method. The transformant detection and knockout transformant determination using PCR and DNA sequencing methods. All primers used in this study were listed in Table S2.

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

WT and rhogap2 mutants were grown at the experimental stations of the Henan Normal University in Xinxiang, China.

Agronomic Traits Investigation

To simulate the pre-harvest panicle sprouting of rice seeds under wet or rainy conditions in the field, whole panicles from WT and rhogap2 plants at the full maturity stage were harvested and cultured in a petri dish lined with moist filter paper. The cultures were maintained at 28 ℃ under a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod. The seed germination was observed and photographed, regularly, the standard of seed germination is the radicle breaks through the seed coat. Seed germination was counted after 48 h. The statistical analysis of grain shape was using a Wanshen seed test instrument (Wseen, Hangzhou, China).

Germination on Petri Dishes

Sterilization treatment of newly harvested rice seeds of WT and rhogap2 mutants. The sterilized rice seeds were placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes (20–30 seeds/dish). Dishes were sealed with Parafilm and incubated in a growth chamber at 28 °C under 12 h light/dark cycles. Adequate moisture was maintained by daily water replenishment. Germination (radicle emergence ≥ 2 mm) was recorded daily for 5 days. Three biological replicates (30 seeds each) per genotype (WT/mutant) were used. Germination rates were calculated as:

(Germinated seeds / Total seeds) × 100%.

Embryo and Endosperm Isolation Assay

To isolate embryos, rice seeds were imbibed in deionized water for 0, 12, 24, 48, and 60 h. The embryos were then dissected from the seeds using a surgical blade and fine forceps under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Waltham, MA, USA). For endosperm isolation, intact seeds were imbibed in deionized water for 0, 12, 24, 48, and 60 h, followed by removal of the embryo with sharp forceps to yield intact endosperm. The method refers to previous study of Guan et al. 2024.

RNA Extraction, qPCR, and RNA-Seq Analyses

Total RNA of roots, culms, leaves, collar, development seed at different stages (0–30 d), and the whole germinated seeds, embryos, and endosperms at 0, 12, 24, 48, and 60 h of WT were isolated by using a Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Then, treated with DNase I, and quantified by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop) and integrity-checked (agarose gel). 1 µg total RNA was reverse-transcribed using oligo(dT) primers and reverse transcriptase. The reverse transcription (RT) reaction was operated according to the protocol of the RT system (TransGen, Beijing, China). For quantitative real time-PCR (qPCR), the reactions contained SYBR Green Master Mix, gene-specific primers, and cDNA template. Amplification used: 95 °C (3 min); 40 cycles of 95 °C (10 s), 60 °C (30 s), three technical replicates and three biological replicates were performed on each sample, and data were analyzed by using the 2−△△Ct method as Liu et al. 2022; each data point is the mean from three replicates ± SD. OsActin1 (LOC_Os03g50885) was used as the internal controls. The primers of qPCR analysis are shown in Table S2.

For RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, the germinating seed for 48 h of WT and rhogap2 were carried out by Oebiotech, Shanghai, China. Poly(A) RNA was enriched from DNase-treated total RNA using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA kits. Libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150-bp paired-end; ~30 million reads per sample). Raw reads were quality-checked (FastQC) and trimmed (Trimmomatic). Clean reads were aligned to the Oryza sativa reference genome (IRGSP-1.0) using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (∣log < sub > 2</sub > FoldChange∣ ≥ 1, FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted via clusterProfiler for GO terms and KEGG pathways.

The α-Amylase Determination

The seeds of WT and rhogap2 were disinfected and cultured in a dark incubator at 28 ℃ for 2 days. Those germinative seeds were cross-cut with a sterilized scalpel on a super-clean table. The half-seeds without embryo were placed upright on a solid starch AGAR plate so that the cut surface was in contact with the AGAR. Then, the starch AGAR plate with half seed was cultured in an incubator at 28 ℃ for 48 h, dyed with I2/KI solution, and photographed 5 min later with camera. The diameter of the transparent region formed by the hydrolysis of starch by α-amylase on the plate was measured by ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2021. Data represent mean ± SD from at least three biological replicates. All samples or experimental repeats on statistics have been indicated in figure legends. Statistical significance is represented by * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001, determined by a two-tailed Student’s t-test.
