Lymphocytic oesophagitis: diagnosis and management
Authors: Mohamed Hussein, Miriam Mitchison, Rami Sweis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Lymphocytic oesophagitis is a rare inflammatory condition that was first described in 2006. Although it is being increasingly diagnosed, it remains poorly described and characterised. There is limited research on the natural history, diagnosis and management of this condition. The most common presenting symptoms are dysphagia, chest pain and heartburn. Endoscopic features can mimic eosinophilic oesophagitis. International consensus is needed to secure a histological definition, to agree on an endoscopic severity scoring system and to determine an appropriate management algorithm. This review summarises the main evidence for the diagnosis and management of lymphocytic oesophagitis, thus setting the scene for the future directions needed to improve the management of this condition.

Introduction

Lymphocytic oesophagitis (LO) is a rare inflammatory condition that has emerged over the last decade, yet it remains poorly described and characterised. It is identified in about 0.1% of oesophageal biopsies.1 It was first described in 2006 and it is being increasingly diagnosed over the last 15 years2; however, there are currently no accepted international definitions3, 4 nor for its management.

Histologically, it consists of peri-papillary intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) with or without granulocytes. The most significant symptomatic manifestation includes dysphagia; however, not all patients complain of symptoms, with many cases being identified incidentally. It is important to be able to distinguish it histologically and clinically from other similar conditions such as eosinophilic oesophagitis (EOE). Associated disorders such as Crohn's disease or immune-mediated disorders should be excluded.5

Histopathology

In normal, healthy oesophageal mucosa, IELs are normally present in small quantities with nuclear contours that are either round in shape or irregular. Their distribution is patchy with the peak average number of IELs in the middle and lower oesophagus approximately 10 IELs per high powered field.6

The current diagnostic criteria for lymphocytic oesophagitis were first introduced by Rubio et al and include intraepithelial lymphocytosis, absence of/rare granulocytes and epithelial damage2, 7 (Fig 1). However, there is still controversy around the diagnostic criteria, with no clear consensus with regards to a variety of aspects. Particular areas of controversy are the threshold number of IELs required for a diagnosis and the threshold of how low the number of granulocytes should be, particularly as they can also be seen in gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).5Fig 1Histopathological characteristics of normal oesophageal mucosa (a). Clinical presentation

There are variations in the clinical presentations of patients with LO in the limited published case reports and series.8 It appears to be most common in females in the sixth decade of life.9 The most common presenting symptoms are heartburn, dysphagia and chest discomfort. To a lesser extent patients can also present with food impaction, similar to EOE.8, 9

Cohen et al retrospectively analysed 81 patients with LO. The most frequent symptoms were dysphagia, chest pain and heartburn.9 In a recent case series of 20 patients, LO was most common in elderly women, with the predominant symptoms being dysphagia and refractory reflux.10

A number of immune-mediated conditions have been found to be associated with LO including Crohn's disease, rheumatological diseases, hypothyroidism and lichen planus. This has raised the possibility that LO is an autoimmune condition.7

Endoscopic findings

There is a wide variation in the endoscopic features of LO. Commonly LO can mimic eosinophilic oesophagitis with oesophageal rings, strictures, stenosis, linear furrows and white exudates; however between 23% and 55% of LO have a macroscopically normal oesophagus on endoscopy.8 A systematic review showed that 120 out of 387 patients (31%) with a diagnosis of LO had a normal endoscopic appearance.4

The presence of strictures and the overlap of endoscopic appearances between LO and EOE suggests that in at least a subset of LO, there is an underlying chronic inflammatory process with the propensity to progress to mucosal scarring.7

Any criteria for defining LO endoscopically would need evaluation and validation in a prospective multicentre study, as there would likely be a degree of interobserver variation between endoscopists.

Risk factors and clinical associations

Oesophageal motility disorders

The significance of association between LO and oesophageal motility disorder is not known. Putra et al assessed the association between non-achalasia primary oesophageal motility disorder and LO. 69 patients with a primary oesophageal motility disorder (nutcracker oesophagus, ineffective motility, diffuse spasm) and 70 patients with gastro-oesophageal reflux disease but no dysmotility were assessed. LO was found to have a strong association with primary oesophageal motility disorders, thus suggesting that LO might be an indicator for underlying dysmotility. One theory for this association was that lymphocytic inflammation may be incorporated within the smooth muscle of the oesophagus, thus leading to the dysmotility. Alternatively, the motility disorder itself could be contributing to the lymphocytosis.11 Further, Zaver et al showed that patients with LO were more likely to have achalasia (7.4% vs 2.6%, P=0.01).1

Crohn's disease

Several paediatric studies have shown an association between LO and Crohn's disease. Purdy et al showed a strong association between paediatric Crohn's and LO; out of eight children with LO, three had Crohn's disease (38%).12 Ebach et al retrospectively analysed the oesophageal biopsies of 60 patients with Crohn's disease and 30 patients with ulcerative colitis. LO was identified in 28% of the patients with Crohn's, 7% of patients with ulcerative colitis and in 3 of the non-inflammatory bowel disease control patients.13 Similar results have not been seen in the adult Crohn's population.3 LO may be a potential indicator of Crohn's disease or it may be a manifestation of it in younger patients, but there does not seem to be an association in adults.3

Oesophageal mucosal injury

Moiseff et al assessed the association between CD8 T-cell predominant LO and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease. A retrospective cohort of 161 patients were included with a diagnosis of GORD yet normal oesophageal motility; 25% of patients had endoscopic evidence of oesophagitis. Lymphocytic oesophagitis was identified in 6.8% of patients. CD8 T cells significantly outnumber CD4 T cells in 91% of patients with LO in this cohort. The authors concluded that LO was a major pattern of lymphocytic inflammation in GORD.14

Management

There is no clear evidence or consensus with regards to the treatment algorithm for LO. As presentation is often similar to that of EOE, LO has been managed in a similar manner.20

Patients have reported symptomatic improvement with a proton pump inhibitor. This could be the effect of their response to reflux-associated lymphocytosis or it might reflect a sub-population of LO that are responsive to such therapies.

Topical steroids have been shown to provide clinical benefit in some. Studies have shown benefit of steroids in lymphocytic colitis and EOE, and so similar principles have been applied to the treatment of LO.20 In a retrospective study by Pasricha et al, one patient who was treated with swallowed Fluticasone had symptomatic improvement.21 In a case report by Kasirye et al, a patient with LO was treated with swallowed two puffs of fluticasone (220 μg aerosol) three times a day with resolution of solid food dysphagia at 6 months.22 A patient with LO and oesophageal strictures was treated with endoscopic dilatation followed by one month of swallowed fluticasone with good clinical response.23

A study by Lee et al assessed the response of 62 patients with lymphocyte-predominant oesophagitis to different treatments after excluding GORD and an underlying motility disorder. 24% of patients treated with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) had clinical improvement. On the other hand, all patients with an underlying autoimmune disease exhibited a response to treatment with steroids. The authors proposed that PPI is not an effective treatment strategy in lymphocyte-predominant oesophagitis and that using this as an initial treatment step may cause a delay in effective treatment response.24

In patients with oesophageal strictures, endoscopic intervention such as dilatation might be required in addition to medical therapy (PPI and/or topical steroid treatment).17 A systematic review of 20 studies showed that 12.9% of these patients develop oesophageal strictures.4 A case report showed a patient with lymphocytic oesophagitis and a proximal oesophageal stricture responded well to oesophageal dilatation which was required at 12-month intervals. This was undertaken in combination with PPI therapy treatment.8 However, on follow up oesophageal biopsies, histopathological findings remained unchanged.

The treatment of LO is variable, and is dependent upon the underlying severity of inflammation and strictures. In a case series of three patients who presented with dysphagia, one patient's symptoms resolved spontaneously, another patient had minimal symptomatic improvement with a proton pump inhibitor and topical steroid, whilst the third required a number of dilatations despite being on maximal medical therapy.8

Comparison of lymphocytic oesophagitis and eosinophilic oesophagitis

Lymphocytic oesophagitis has similar endoscopic and clinical features to eosinophilic oesophagitis. EOE is most likely to be seen in young males, whereas LO appears to be more common in females in the 6th decade of life. The histological diagnosis of EOE is eosinophilic infiltration of more than 15 eosinophils per high powered field in the oesophagus. In LO the diagnostic criteria include intraepithelial lymphocytosis, absence/rare granulocytes and epithelial damage. The peak average number of IELs in the middle and lower oesophagus is approximately 10 IELs per high powered field.

Endoscopic features of LO are similar to EOE and include furrows, oesophageal rings, exudates, strictures and narrowing of the oesophageal lumen. Patients with EOE can present with similar symptoms of dysphagia and heartburn.25

Future perspectives

There are a number of areas where research in this area would need to be prioritised in order to improve our understanding of the pathophysiology and the clinical pathways to help with the management of this condition: •The diagnosis of this condition is yet to be secured, particularly with regards to the optimal threshold for the number of IELs in the oesophageal mucosal and the concomitant other histological features that might accompany the disease at its various stages. A standardised definition should be agreed in order to reduce interobserver variability and to clarify which conditions are pathological and which are incidental, clinically irrelevant or are simply just outside of the normal range of lymphocyte concentration.•An understanding with regards to the natural history of those considered to have established LO is required to help decipher who might benefit from therapeutic escalation.•An endoscopic scoring system of macroscopic severity akin to what exists for EOE will help investigators describe pathology using the ‘same language’.•A management algorithm would help determine at what stage patients should initiate medical therapy, which therapy should be used for induction and then as maintenance therapy, what criteria are required before considering endoscopic intervention as adjunctive therapy, as well as when surveillance assessment and biopsies should be taken post therapy.

Conclusions

Lymphocytic oesophagitis is an emerging condition with a yet unestablished diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm. A variety of case reports and cohort studies have described a heterogenous mix of presentations and therapies, but we remain yet quite removed from establishing how to separate true disease from clinically irrelevant findings. There is an even poorer understanding of how to treat this relatively novel condition. Untreated EOE can lead to worsening symptoms culminating in food bolus obstruction. It appears that LO is not dissimilar in its natural history to EOE, but treating LO in the same way as EOE has led to unsatisfactory results in many, thus suggesting a different pathophysiological mechanism. Those with severe symptoms have a marked and progressive impact on their quality of life so there is a clear need for further establishing diagnostic and therapeutic criteria for this disorder.
