Red blood cell membrane proteome as a reporter of disease severity, transfusion impact and genetic background in transfusion‐dependent β‐thalassaemia
Authors: Konstantina Theocharaki, Sophia Delicou, Aikaterini Ntzoura‐Kani, Nikolaos Simantiris, Marianna Politou, Veroniki Komninaka, Martina Samiotaki, Jerome Zoidakis, Marianna H Antonelou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOmics technologies have transformed research in haemoglobinopathies, yet the proteome of RBCs remains largely unexplored in transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia (TDT). In this proteomic analysis, Red blood cell (RBC) membranes from 48 adults with TDT were compared with healthy controls. The resulting profile, corresponding to patient and donor RBCs, captured disease‐linked features and exposure to donor blood. Certain differentially expressed proteins were plasma‐absorbed or remnants of erythroid precursors, recapitulating findings in non‐transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia. Structural proteins were overexpressed en bloc, especially in severe haemoglobin subunit beta (HBB) mutations, though higher transfusion doses attenuate these abnormalities.Other changes suggested impaired volume regulation, increased RBC adhesion and features not included in storage lesions, like downregulation of galectin‐3 and septins (identified in TDT for the first time), which were modulated by genetic‐ and transfusion‐related variables. JAM‐A, an endothelium inflammation marker, was significantly related to heart T2*. Splenectomy correlated with oxidative, coagulative and inflammatory patterns. The moderate and severe genotype groups had similar proteomic and cell morphology profiles compared to the mildest cases. Upregulation of ferroportin, ICAM4, eryptosis and circadian rhythm‐related proteins further characterized severe HBB genotypes. Transfusion dose effects varied across clinical groups. Overall, the RBC membrane emerges as both a target and a sensitive reporter of the complex pathophysiology underlying TDT.Keywords: HBB mutations severity, RBC membrane proteomics, RBC storage lesion, splenectomy, transfusion dose, transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia

INTRODUCTION

Transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia (TDT) is a severe haemoglobinopathy characterized by ineffective erythropoiesis and wide phenotypic heterogeneity. 1 While transfusions correct anaemia and improve survival, they also cause iron overload, further worsened by hepcidin suppression. 2

The severity of mutations in HBB gene varies from mild to moderate and severe, reflecting the escalating degree of deficiency in beta globin production. Accumulation of alpha chains and free haem trigger oxidative stress (OS) through the Fenton and other reactions. 3 The oxidized and denatured Hb species bind with high affinity to RBC membrane, 4 resulting in local damage. Poor protein quality control (PQQ) activity in erythroid precursors and circulating RBCs 5 further worsens OS. Autophagy cooperates with the proteasome for the degradation of excess proteins; 6 however, the need for free alpha‐chain chaperoning in erythroid precursors further promotes ineffective erythropoiesis. 7 Despite several protective stress‐response systems, 8 OS eventually triggers end‐stage maturation arrest/apoptosis of erythroid precursors 9 and extravascular haemolysis. 5

Application of omics has advanced research into thalassaemias by revealing novel regulatory pathways, genetic modifiers of disease severity and candidate therapeutic targets. Matching of plasma proteome with genetic and clinical data uncovered disease signatures (e.g. iron overload), transfusion burden 10 and severity markers. 11 The differential serum proteome in patients on hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) or antioxidant therapies showed the underlying regulatory pathways 12 and markers of treatment response. 13

Substantially fewer studies—and most in HbE/beta‐thalassaemia 14 , 15 —have focused on RBC proteome, which is marked by changes in redox regulators, molecular chaperones and skeletal components. The differential proteome of RBC cytosol in Haemoglobin E (HbE)/beta‐thalassaemia is very like that of sickle cell disease (SCD), revealing common pathophysiological mechanisms. 16 In thalassemic erythroid precursors, 8 increased oxidative metabolism, activation of protein kinases and OS correlate with the extent of erythroid expansion. 17

TDT differs from other thalassaemia entities not only in the severity/fluctuation of ineffective erythropoiesis and haemolysis but also in the effects of chronic transfusions, resulting in a mixed population of donor‐and‐patient circulating RBCs. While individual differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) cannot be definitively assigned to either source, the proteome represents the clinically relevant state of the patient's blood at the end of the transfusion interval. The RBC membrane acts as a signalling, regulatory and metabolic hub, where interactions with intracellular and extracellular proteins regulate RBC shape, deformability, metabolism (including oxygen transport), erythrocrine activity and RBC survival. 18 Therefore, alterations in the RBC membrane proteome can provide mechanistic insights into disease pathophysiology.

Building on our recent studies characterizing the physiological landscape of RBCs and plasma metabolomics in this cohort of patients with TDT, 5 the present study investigates the RBC membrane proteome. A comprehensive picture of how the disease and its treatment interact at the molecular level is relevant to the clinical reality of TDT management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and healthy controls

Peripheral blood samples from 48 adult TDT subjects were obtained before transfusion and examined compared to 13 healthy controls. 5 Demographics and clinical data were collected as previously reported 5 , 19 and are currently used for data interpretation. Liver iron concentration (LIC) was 4.88 ± 8.74 mg/g dw (range 1.10–54.00) and cardiac T2* was 36.2 ± 11.0 ms (range 3.2–61.3) Additional data for patients and methodology are provided in the Supporting Information.

Electron microscopy and proteomics

Pure RBCs were isolated by buffy coat/plasma removal and cellulose column leucoreduction. Membrane fractions were collected following hypotonic lysis of RBCs in the presence of protease inhibitors. For proteomic analysis, the samples were digested using a modified SP3 clean‐up protocol. 20 Peptides were analysed by Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). on a Dionex Ultimate RSLC system coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive/HF‐X Orbitrap (Thermo Scientific). Raw data were processed in DIA‐NN v2.2. RBC preparation for scanning electron microscopy and shape classification were performed as previously described. 5

Statistics and bioinformatics

Intergroup differences were detected by independent t‐test and one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Associations between DEPs and patient/transfusion variables were examined using multivariate analysis Analysis of Covariance (ANOCOVA), adjusting for potential confounders. Spearman's univariate analyses assessed correlations. Computations were conducted using R (https://www.R‐project.org), GraphPad Prism 6 and MATLAB R2021a. Input gene lists were functionally enriched using multiple tools and databases.

From a network‐level perspective, we detected interconnected groups of structural components and autophagy‐related DEPs connected to the small Guanosine Triphosphatase (GTPase) CDC42 (Figure 1B), involved in membrane dynamics at terminal erythroid differentiation. 27 Functional enrichment analysis showed involvement of overexpressed DEPs in cytoskeleton organization, cell adhesion, immune system, apoptosis and haemostasis. Disease gene associations pointed to haemopoietic disease and haemolytic anaemia (Figure S1A).

Conversely, downregulated DEPs were enriched in translation components and septins. Some redox and PQQ components (e.g. heat shock proteins, thioredoxin) had been found overexpressed in the cytosol of SCD and HbE/beta‐thalassaemia RBCs. 16 Downregulation of monocarboxylate transporter Solute Carrier Family 16, Member 1/Monocarboxylate Transporter 1 (SLC16A1/MCT1) implies dysregulated redox/pH homeostasis during glycolysis. Impaired folate‐mediated one‐carbon metabolism is mirrored in the low levels of methylene‐tetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (MTHFD1), and impaired lipid metabolism (supported by both plasma 10 and EV proteomic studies 28 ) in the deficient acyl‐CoA synthetase (ACSL6). Network, functional enrichment (Figure 1C) and reactome analyses (Figure S1B) pointed to translation, GTPase activity and response to amino acid deficiency, among others.

Septins were detected for the first time in TDT RBCs. They were recently found overexpressed in RBC membranes from hereditary spherocytosis patients, 29 characterized by inverse RBC phenotypes (e.g. osmotic fragility, ankyrin deficiency) compared to thalassaemia. Notably, septin‐2 is a component of thrombi isolated from stroke patients. 30

Downregulation of galectin‐3 (LGALS3) was another interesting finding. This protein is a serum biomarker of cardiovascular fibrosis and inflammation 31 that acts through the erythropoiesis‐implicated 32 Toll‐like Receptor 4 (TLR4)/Nuclear Factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) pathway. 33 Its binding partner (LGALS3BP) has been proposed as a plasma marker of thalassaemia severity 10 and response to hydroxyurea treatment. 13

The differential part of TDT proteome is connected to clinical variables

Several DEPs varied according to RBC physiological characteristics (e.g. osmotic resistance, proteasome activity) as well as to total blood count, haemolysis, Haemoglobin F (HbF) and transfusion‐related variables (Figure 2; Figure S2). Hb concentration negatively correlated with septins and galectin‐3, and LIC with the HAGH protein, which uses glutathione to regulate redox and detoxification reactions.

To examine relationships of DEPs with clinical and physiological variables after adjusting for potential confounders, a multivariate ANOCOVA was performed (radial circles in Figure 2; Table S2). According to this, heart T2* and atrial fibrillation (trend) were associated with F11R abundance. This protein (also named Junctional adhesion molecule A [JAM‐A] or CD321) is released in plasma by inflamed vascular endothelium, following cleavage by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase proteins, 34 and thus, it is considered a biomarker of atherosclerosis. 35 Galectin‐3 was related to the duration of transfusion therapy (trend), while flotillin‐2 and glutathione S‐transferase were independently associated with multiple clinical variables. A set of DEPs tracked with iron homeostasis markers apart from ferritin, suggesting that membrane composition reflects current iron flux and erythropoiesis, rather than long‐term or inflammatory iron storage.

Splenectomy‐related variation

Spleen status correlated significantly with 18% of the differential TDT proteome (Figure 3A). Splenectomy positively correlated with globin chains but negatively with structural components and galectin‐3. ANOCOVA revealed association with the expression of acyl‐CoA synthetase ACSL6, involved in membrane phospholipid repairing/turnover via the Lands cycle. This finding probably reflects enhanced lipid remodelling due to peroxidation in splenectomized patients' RBCs, consistent with their increased PS exposure and exovesiculation. 5 Notably, antioxidant supplementation markedly reduces lipid peroxidation in non‐splenectomized, but not in splenectomized, TDT subjects. 36

A comprehensive comparative analysis in splenectomized (S‐RBC), non‐splenectomized (NS‐RBC) and control groups revealed 141 DEPs (Figure S3A; top 50 marked in Figure 3B; distribution across groups in Figure 3C).

Sixty‐eight proteins differed significantly between S‐ and NS‐RBCs. Most of them were expressed at pathological levels (Figure 3C), in contrast to gamma‐globin chains, chemokine C‐C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), fibrinogen, alpha‐synuclein, etc. (Figure 3C,D). For the CCL5 Regulated upon Activation, Normal T‐cell Expressed and Secreted (RANTES), 37 this is still clinically relevant given its role in DARC‐mediated immunomodulation. 38 , 39 The overexpressed immunoglobulins also varied within physiological levels, in contrast to HbE/beta‐thalassaemia. 40

The differential S‐TDT proteome (excess of globin chains, ferrochelatase, etc.; Figure 3B–D) was functionally linked to coagulation, OS and inflammation (Figure 3E, Figure S3B). Increased α‐synuclein 41 is attributable to the presence of older circulating RBCs in the absence of spleen, 23 consistent with the shorter RBC lifespan in knockout animal models. 42 S‐TDT proteome was further characterized by downregulation of galectin 3 and G protein‐coupled receptor kinase 6 (GRK6) kinase, which regulates erythrophagocytosis, 43 and limits platelet activation. 44

Selective overexpression of proteins like Rh‐associated glycoprotein and Kell metallo‐endopeptidase in NS‐RBCs (Figure 3C, Figure S3A) likely reflects reduced vesiculation in the presence of spleen, 5 as these are components of RBC‐derived extracellular vesicles. 45

Mutation‐related variation

Severity of HBB mutations was related to variation in 21% of the differential proteome (Figure 4A). Among others, negative correlations were detected with septins, and positive with structural components (e.g. band 3) and the nucleoside transporter ENT1, which regulates adenosine homeostasis. 46 ANOCOVA supported those significant associations with the expression of structural components.

Comparative analysis of proteomes in escalating severity cases revealed a set of 218 DEPs (Figure S4; top 50 in Figure 4B; pairwise differences across patient subgroups in Figure 4C). Arginase‐1 and the Gardos channel (KCNN4) had similar pathological variation across all severity groups (Figure 4D, Figure S4).

Fifty‐five DEPs (mostly cytoskeleton proteins and organizers) distinguished moderately from mildly affected RBCs (Figure S5). Structural components, transporters and the adhesion molecule intercellular adhesion molecule 4 (ICAM4) (Figure 4D) were overexpressed in moderate cases, in contrast to translation factors and peroxiredoxins, mirroring the impaired translation and PQQ of thalassaemic erythropoiesis. 6 Most followed the same expression trend in severe versus mild comparison. By contrast, the exporter ATP‐binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) was selectively elevated in moderately affected RBCs (Figure 4D), suggesting increased haem export that may be lost in more severe genotypes due to greater membrane damage or vesiculation. 47

Not only additional skeletal proteins but also calpains, fatty acid ligase and ferroportin (SLC40A1, Figure 4D) were upregulated in the severely versus mildly affected RBCs suggesting further membrane remodelling and iron export with mutation severity. Ferroportin protects RBCs against iron toxicity by exporting iron generated from Hb oxidation/denaturation. 48 In contrast, septin 7 (Figure 4D) and several protein synthesis factors decreased (Figure S6).

Overall, severely and moderately affected RBCs showed highly similar profiles (Figure 4B), differing by only 19 DEPs (Figure 4C). This is consistent with the comparable cell morphology of those more severe states that differed from the milder, though equally anaemic, cases (Figure 5). The latter displayed greater heterogeneity and more torocyte‐like forms (with peripheral Hb distribution), indicative of curvature‐driven deformations. Unlike reports in HbE/beta‐thalassaemia showing increased torocytes with worsening anaemia, 49 our findings instead align with milder‐severe differences in red cell distribution width, stress erythropoiesis and mechanical/osmotic haemolysis. 5

Transfusion dose‐related variation

Dose of transfusion correlated with 11% of the differential proteome (Figure 6A). According to ANOCOVA, it is independently related to the expression of marker of self CD47, phospholipid scramblase, biliverdin reductase, membrane transporters involved in cell volume and purine salvage regulation (adenine and hypoxanthine), but also of the endothelium stress marker JAM‐A (F11R; trend; Figure 6B).

Forty‐five DEPs were identified between patients with similar average transfusion intervals receiving one‐versus‐two RBC units per transfusion over the past 4 months (15.6 ± 4.2 vs. 17.2 ± 2.7 days respectively) (Table S3). Given, however, the influence of spleen status and HBB mutations, dose‐related proteome variation was further examined in two equally sized subgroups:

the S‐group of splenectomized patients (n = 22, 10 one unit, 12 two units) exhibiting varying HBB mutations severity (8 mild, 14 moderate/severe), and

the S‐group of splenectomized patients (n = 22, 10 one unit, 12 two units) exhibiting varying HBB mutations severity (8 mild, 14 moderate/severe), and

the M‐group of patients with only moderate and severe mutations (n = 25, 11 one unit, 14 two units), including an equal number of splenectomized and non‐splenectomized individuals.

the M‐group of patients with only moderate and severe mutations (n = 25, 11 one unit, 14 two units), including an equal number of splenectomized and non‐splenectomized individuals.

Notably, more dose‐related DEPs were identified in the S‐group than in the M‐group (Figure 6C,D), likely due to the greater heterogeneity of the S‐cohort encompassing all HBB genotypes. Thus, beyond DEPs and cell morphology, the mild versus moderate/severe dichotomy refers also to potential transfusion dose effects, particularly in splenectomized patients. In the absence of spleen, defective 23 and donor‐derived RBCs 53 are expected to persist longer in circulation. By contrast, transfusion dose seemed to have a weaker impact on the RBC proteome in the M‐group, which lacked mild genotypes and comprised more severely damaged RBCs from both spleen statuses.

Regarding DEP classes in higher transfusion dose, the M‐group showed upregulation of annexins and phospholipid scramblase, and downregulation of proteins involved in haemostasis, platelet activation and production of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (Figure 6D; Figure S8), reflecting transfusion‐related immunomodulation. 54 In the S‐group, cytoskeletal components, redox/glycolytic enzymes, 14‐3‐3 proteins, ferroportin and notably, almost all proteasome subunits were markedly downregulated in two‐unit transfusions. This is notable given the higher membrane‐associated proteasome activity in stored RBCs compared with TDT‐RBCs. 19 Enrichment analysis supported these observations (Figure 6C; Figure S7).

In both groups, higher transfusion doses were associated with increased beta‐thromboglobulin (pro‐platelet basic protein [PPBP] or CXCL7), a platelet‐derived chemokine (Figure 6C,D), suggesting adsorption from transfused units 19 , 55 or enhanced RBC–platelet crosstalk. 56 Other commonly upregulated proteins included molecular chaperones, galectins and septins, whereas biliverdin reductase and several structural, lipid raft and EV components (e.g. stomatin) were downregulated. Overall, higher transfusion volumes—largely independent of spleen status or HBB genotype—appeared to drive long‐term metabolic and redox adaptations related to haemostasis and PQQ, while attenuating thalassaemia‐specific membrane remodelling. These exploratory findings provide valuable hypothesis‐generating directions for future mechanistic and longitudinal studies.

CONCLUSIONS

To summarize, the RBC membrane proteome in TDT captures both disease‐associated and transfusion‐modulated pathways. It is indicative of remodelling, cell adhesion, 57 volume regulation and signalling and exhibits significant associations with patient and transfusion variables, including cardiac iron overload. Sampling was performed immediately before transfusion, thus representing the steady‐state circulating RBC population at the end of transfusion interval, rather than the acute post‐transfusion mixture. Moreover, regular transfusion intervals (~17 days) occur after the initial 10‐ to 12‐day period of accelerated donor RBC clearance in TDT recipients. 58 Nevertheless, patient RBCs have markedly shortened lifespans (often 4–18 days) 59 compared with transfused RBCs, so the pre‐transfusion population likely represents a dynamic mixture shaped by donor RBC survival, thalassaemic erythropoiesis, transfusion interval/dose and spleen status (see Supporting Information).

Despite blurring from donor RBCs, the mixed TDT proteome remains informative. It encompassed several disease markers previously observed in non‐transfusion‐dependent thalassaemia, while plasma‐absorbed DEPs (e.g. prion protein, galectin‐3BP) recapitulated those reported in thalassaemic plasma and extracellular vesicles. Other DEPs (e.g. translation, autophagy) represented remnants of erythroid precursors mirroring ineffective erythropoiesis, previously reported in thalassaemic erythroblasts and their microenvironment.

The differential proteome likely integrates storage lesions, adaptations of donor RBCs and native RBC responses. Despite donor exposure, several DEPs (currently reported for the first time in TDT, e.g. septins, galectin‐3) have not been previously described in the context of RBC storage lesions. The proteome further uncovers potential disease‐treatment interactions making it relevant to the clinical reality of TDT management (e.g. thromboglobulin binding in higher transfusion dose) that set it apart from profiles reported in non‐transfusion‐dependent entities. Finally, the set of proteins associated with multiple clinical variables suggested that oxidative membrane remodelling is a shared molecular feature of disease itself and transfusion response.

DEPs, RBC morphology and transfusion‐dose relations in moderate and severe HBB mutation groups were highly similar and distinct from milder cases. These findings support the binary genotypic classification in β‐thalassaemia, where all but the mildest genotypes are severe in terms of morbidity and mortality. 60 With increasing severity, RBC membranes become denser, crowded and more adhesive, with lower septins and proteostasis capacity. Pathological ferroportin levels, along with DEPs related to eryptosis, and circadian rhythm are reported here for the first time in severe genotypes.

This study has several limitations. While the overall cohort size was adequate for proteomic profiling, subgroup analyses were relatively small, potentially limiting statistical power and generalizability. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference. Furthermore, clinical heterogeneity introduced potential confounders that may have influenced effect estimates, increased model complexity and reduced statistical precision.

However, the findings generate testable hypotheses for further evaluation. Candidates including ferroportin, HAGH, JAM‐A and galectin‐3 warrant validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts to determine their utility in monitoring disease‐ and therapy‐related changes. DEPs related to OS, coagulation, inflammation, transfusion dose and genetic/clinical heterogeneity may also be explored to support more personalized management strategies. In the same context, putative therapeutic targets, such as antioxidants, modulators of membrane deformability and transporters (including ferroportin) and inhibitors of eryptosis, require mechanistic investigation in preclinical studies before clinical translation. Septins may represent one such target if demonstrated to play a functional role in membrane defects.
