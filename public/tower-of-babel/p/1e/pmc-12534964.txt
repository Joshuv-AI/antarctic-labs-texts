Comprehensive evaluation of NAT+/HBsAg– blood donors: confirmation strategies, serial testing outcomes, and S gene mutations associated with occult HBV infection
Authors: Rongsong Du, Wenbo Gao, Zhongping Li, Haojian Liang, Fenfang Liao, Junmou Xie, Feifeng Li, Zhiting Wan, Shijie Li, Ru Xu, Yongshui Fu, Yongmei Nie, Hao Wang, Huaqin Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundS gene mutations may lead to undetectable HBsAg, contributing to occult HBV infection (OBI). Nucleic acid testing (NAT) has been widely implemented to reduce transfusion-transmitted OBI. However, distinguishing between false positives and true OBI among NAT+/HBsAg– donors remains challenging, as these individuals frequently exhibit inconsistent or fluctuating test results. This study aims to improve infection status confirmation and donor management by analyzing detection patterns and investigating S gene mutations.MethodsBlood donors were detected for HBV using individual-NAT and HBsAg enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Routine discriminatory test (dHBV), additional three repeated dHBV (3-repeated dHBV), and serological assays were conducted to confirm the infection status of NAT+/HBsAg– donors. Serial NAT results were analyzed to assess temporal fluctuations. S gene mutations related with OBI were identified through molecular characterization and sequencing analysis.ResultsA total of 336,537 blood donors were detected, and 556 (0.16%) NAT+/HBsAg– cases were identified. Among 297 donors undergoing confirmation, 112 (37.71%) were confirmed by routine dHBV, 152 (51.18%) by 3-repeated dHBV, and 43 (14.48%) by multiple serial NATs. In total, 187 donors (62.96%, 187/297) were confirmed HBV-infected, significantly higher than routine dHBV alone (P < 0.01). Among the 187 confirmed HBV-infected donors, only 59 (31.55%) were positive in all four dHBV tests, while 128 (68.45%) showed missed detections, suggesting extremely low viral loads. In serial NAT testing, 83 (73.45%, 83/113) of donors confirmed by dHBV showed no further NAT-positive results, and an average of 5.6 tests was needed to detect one extra positive case. Molecular analysis revealed that OBI samples had more mutations in both the major hydrophilic region (MHR) and non-MHR compared to HBsAg + donors (P < 0.05), with a significantly higher prevalence of the E2G mutation.ConclusionsOBI is a major contributor to inconsistent NAT results. Comprehensive measures, especially repeated testing, are recommended to improve confirmation rates and reduce the risk of re-entry of potentially infectious donors. Additionally, further molecular research on S gene mutations is essential for elucidating OBI mechanisms and enhancing assay sensitivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11798-2.

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a significant global health concern, with an estimated 296 million people chronically infected in 2019 [1]. HBV can be transmitted through transfusion, vertical transmission, sexual contact, and unsafe injections [2]. Therefore, rigorous blood screening for HBV is essential to prevent transfusion-transmitted infections. Since the 1990s, the use of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) has effectively reduced the risk of transfusion-transmitted HBV in China. However, challenges persist, including the window period (WP) infection and the detection of occult HBV infection (OBI). OBI is defined as the presence of HBV DNA in the liver or blood of individuals who are negative for HBsAg but may test positive for HBV DNA [3]. The mechanisms underlying OBI remain unclear. However, extensive research suggests that mutations in the S gene contribute to the development of OBI and the failure to detect HBsAg [4–6]. Nucleic acid testing (NAT), by directly detecting viral nucleic acids (HBV DNA), can significantly shorten the WP detection time and improved the detection rate of OBI. NAT techniques for blood screening include polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and transcription-mediated amplification (TMA). PCR is used for screening in a mini-pool NAT (MP-NAT) mode, and if HBV-DNA is detected in MP-NAT, individual NAT (ID-NAT) is performed to confirm the infection status of the blood donation. TMA, on the other hand, uses ID-NAT for mixed project screening tests. If the initial screening is positive, HBV-DNA discriminatory testing is then required.

China has an intermediate-to-high HBV prevalence (5–7.99%) [7], prompting the nationwide progressively implementation of NAT alongside dual HBsAg ELISA screening since 2010. While NAT significantly improves blood safety by detecting early infections and OBI, it also introduces new challenges in result interpretation and donor management. First, blood donors who test positive in the initial NAT may show negative results in repeat test or non-discriminatory reactive (NDR), raising concerns about the accuracy of the results [8]. A national study found that 68% of initially positive NAT samples were negative in the discriminatory test, with only 15% yielding positive results on qPCR [9]. Second, NAT+/HBsAg– donors may seek retesting at hospitals, where inconsistent results can cause confusion, anxiety, and disputes with blood banks, ultimately undermining the credibility and harming donor recruitment [10]. Lastly, deferral of unconfirmed NAT+ donors reduces the donor pool, with many seeking the right to continue donating. Distinguishing between false-positive results and true infections remains challenging due to the presence of OBI with extremely low viral loads [3, 11]. A study in Dalian, a northern Chinese city, reported that ​84.5% of NDR donors were false-positive [12]. In contrast, several studies have reported that OBI constitutes approximately 46.72% to 65.82% of NDR cases, accompanied by the presence of multiple high-frequency mutations in the S gene [12–14]. These conflicting findings may reflect regional differences in OBI prevalence, variations in laboratory testing protocols, and methodological discrepancies in confirmatory testing approaches [10]. This underscores the need to further clarify the infection status of NAT+/HBsAg– donors and to enhance the investigation of their detection characteristics and molecular features.

To address these challenges, we supplemented triple HBV discriminatory testing, serological assessment of HBV infection markers, and molecular sequence amplification experiments, thereby enabling a comprehensive understanding of the detection profiles and S gene mutation patterns in NAT+/HBsAg– donors. Additionally, the study analyzed the longitudinal data from these donors, including both historical testing results and follow-up testing data, to further investigate the causes of inconsistent outcomes and to evaluate the feasibility of donor re-entry strategies. By integrating both cross-sectional and longitudinal data, we aim to thoroughly characterize the testing profiles and molecular features of NAT+/HBsAg– donors, ultimately providing evidence-based insights for accurate infection status assessment and optimized donor management.

Methods

Collection of NAT+/HBsAg- blood samples

Blood samples for this study were collected from voluntary donors at the Guangzhou Blood Center, Guangdong Province, China. All donations were tested for HBsAg, as well as serological markers for Hepatitis C virus (HCV), Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and Treponema pallidum (TP), using two different ELISA assays in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions. Additionally, NAT for HBV, HCV, HIV-1 and HIV-2 was performed using the Procleix Ultrio Elite Assay (Grifols, USA), with detection limits of 4.3 IU/mL, 3.0 IU/mL, 18.0 IU/mL, and 10.4 IU/mL, respectively. Donors who tested positive in the multiplex NAT assay but negative in other tests were included. For each eligible donor, both the testing tube (TT) and plasma bag (PB) samples were collected and stored at −30 °C for further analysis.

From September 2019 to October 2020, a total of 556 NAT+/HBsAg − donors were identified during the study period. Among these donors, all blood samples with both TT and PB components available and having sufficient volume for additional NAT and related tests were included in this study. In total, 297 donors (53.42%) met these criteria and were included for further testing and molecular analysis.

Routine discriminatory testing and 3-repeated dHBV testing for NAT+/HBsAg- samples

Routine discriminatory test for HBV (dHBV), HCV (dHCV), and HIV (dHIV) were performed using the Procleix Ultrio Elite discriminatory assay to identify the causative virus. Each test used 500 µL of sample, with detection limits consistent with the initial NAT assay. All these tests were conducted using the TT samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To explore reproducibility and improve detection sensitivity, all NAT+/HBsAg- samples underwent three repeated dHBV tests (3-repeated dHBV). About 5 ml plasma samples were drawn from the PB samples, and 3-repeated dHBV tests were performed concurrently in the same batch. The test procedure was the same as previously demonstrated. According to previous literatures [10, 12, 15], a positive result in either routine dHBV testing or 3-repeated dHBV testing is considered as HBV infection.

Temporal fluctuation analysis of NAT and HBsAg results

To assess the serial testing characteristics of NAT+/HBsAg- donors, longitudinal data—including both historical screening results and follow-up testing data—were retrieved from the Guangzhou Blood Center’s electronic database, covering the period from January 2011 to March 2024. These data included results from NAT, HBsAg testing, and serological markers for HCV, HIV, and TP. NAT assays used in different periods included Procleix Ultrio (2011–2016, HBV LOD: 10.4 IU/mL), Procleix Ultrio Plus (2016–2018, HBV LOD: 3.4 IU/mL), and Procleix Ultrio Elite (2018–2024, HBV LOD: 4.3 IU/mL). Donors who had at least two NAT-positive results at different time points (≥ 3 months apart) were defined as 'multiple serial NATs+'. Those who remained HBsAg-negative throughout follow-up were categorized as OBI, while those who became HBsAg-positive during follow-up were considered as WP infection.

Supplemental serological tests for HBV

Additional serological tests were performed on 3-repeated dHBV positive samples to identify OBI cases. These included HBsAg, hepatitis B surface antibodies (HBsAb), hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), hepatitis B e antibodies (HBeAb), and hepatitis B core antibodies (HBcAb), tested using Electrochemiluminescence Immunoassay (ECLIA) kits on a Cobas e601 analyzer (Roche, USA). Positivity was defined as HBsAg > 0.05 IU/mL, HBsAb > 10 mIU/mL, and S/Co > 1.0 for HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb. Donors with HBcAb positivity were considered OBI cases.

HBV DNA extraction, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis

Total DNA was extracted from 10 mL plasma samples of OBI donors using a self-developed technology (Chinese patent: ZL202111372169.6) [16]. HBV S gene amplification was performed using nested PCR as described previously [17, 18], followed by Sanger sequencing. Sequences were aligned using BioEdit 7.0, and phylogenetic analysis was conducted with MEGA 7.0 software (maximum-likelihood method, 1000 bootstrap replications). Reference sequences were obtained from the NCBI database (accession number: AB116076.1, AB116077.1, AB116081.1, AB205119.1, APO11084.1, D00330.1, DQ448620.1, DQ448625.1, DQ993703.1, DQ993709.1, EU306720.1, M54923.1, AP011098.1, EU306720.1, EU916238.1, X75656.1, AB090270, AB120308.1, AB126581.1, AB205189.1, DQ060822, DQ060826.1, X75658.1, X75663.1, AB086397, AB056513.1, AF160501.1, AP007264.1, AB056513.1, AP007261.1, AY090457.1, AY090460.1, AF241409.1, FJ023672.1, AB486012.1). GenBank accession numbers of the HBV S sequences from 49 OBI and 109 HBsAg + blood donors in this study were PV580089 - PV580246.

Comparison of S gene mutations between OBI and HBsAg+ Groups

Amino acid sequences of the HBV S gene were deduced from the corresponding DNA sequences. Control sequences were retrieved from previous study, including 57 genotype B and 52 genotype C sequences from HBsAg-positive donors [19]. To provide a comprehensive analysis of mutation patterns, this study functionally divided the S gene into three regions for an extensive comparison: the major hydrophilic region (MHR, aa 99–169), the “α” determinant (aa 124–147), and the non-MHR (aa 1–98 and aa 170–226).

Statistical analysis

To describe the result of NAT tests and HBV mutation, median values and ranges were used to represent the distribution of continuous data, while percentages (%) were used for categorical variables. All statistical analyses for comparison between groups were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were less than 5. Continuous variables were evaluated for normality and compared using non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study subjects and confirmation of HBV infection blood donors

From September 15, 2019, to October 16, 2020, a total of 336,537 blood donors were screened, among whom 556 NAT+/HBsAg- donors were identified, accounting for 0.16%. Due to the availability of both TT and PB samples with sufficient volume for additional analyses, 297 donors (53.42%) were ultimately included in this study. Among them, 212 were male and 85 were female, with an average age of 41 years.

Of the 297 NAT+/HBsAg- donors, routine HBV discriminatory testing (dHBV) identified 112 (37.71%) as HBV-infected, while no samples tested positive in dHCV testing or dHIV testing. In the 3-repeated dHBV testing, 152 (51.18%) donors yielded positive result at least once. Further serological analysis showed that 123 (80.92%) of the 152 individuals were HBcAb-positive and were therefore classified as OBI cases based on widely accepted diagnostic criteria [20, 21]. The remaining individuals, who tested negative for HBcAb, could not be conclusively classified but may still represent OBIs, warranting further investigation and confirmation.

In the longitudinal analysis, 195 (65.66%) of the 297 donors underwent at least two rounds of testing (range: 2–26) between 2011 and 2024. The testing items included NAT and serological assays for HBV, HCV, and HIV, as described in the methods section. Among the 195 individuals, 43 (22.05%) donors tested NAT positive on at least two occasions, including 37 donors who were positive twice, 4 donors three times, and 2 donors four times. Inconsistent detection patterns of HBV-infected donors at the same time point

As shown in Fig. 1 and detailed in Table 1, cross-sectional testing (routine and 3-repeated dHBV) frequently failed to consistently detect HBV-infected individuals. Among the 112 routine dHBV+ donors, not all three additional dHBV tests were positive; 53 (47.32%) experienced 1–3 missed detections. Among the 152 3-repeated dHBV+ donors, 93 (61.18%) had 1–3 missed detections across all four cross-sectional tests. Notably, among the three repeat tests performed on the same tube sample within the same batch, 81 (53.29%, 81/152) cases still exhibited inconsistent results. Among the 43 multiple NAT+ cases, 31 (72.09%) showed missed detections, with 13 (30.23%) testing negative in all four cross-sectional tests. Even among the 18 donors confirmed as HBV-infected by all three methods, 6 (33.33%) experienced missed detections.

Overall, of the 187 confirmed cases, only 59 (31.55%) were positive in all four tests, while 128 (68.45%) had varying degrees of missed detections. These inconsistent results suggest that a substantial number of NAT+/HBsAg− cases may be missed due to current detection sensitivity, indicating that most HBV-infected donors carry very low viral loads. This clearly underscores the importance of repeat tests in accurately confirming HBV infection status.

Fluctuations in NAT results for NAT+/HBsAg- donors over time

Only 43 of the 187 donors confirmed HBV-infected donors (22.99%) were identified through serial NAT testing, a rate significantly lower than that of routine dHBV testing (59.89%, 112/187) and 3-repeated dHBV testing (81.28%, 152/187). Among the 174 donors who tested positive by either routine dHBV or 3-repeated dHBV, 113 (64.94%) underwent multiple rounds of serial NAT testing. Of these, 83 (73.45%, 83/113) donors did not yield any additional positive results. Notably, even among the 54 donors who met both the routine dHBV + and 3-repeated dHBV + criteria and underwent serial NAT testing, 36 (66.67%) remained non-reactive despite undergoing a median of 3 tests (range: 2–17, Table 2).

Mutation characteristics of the S gene in OBI sequences

In this study, OBI was identified in 80.92% (123/152) of 3-repeated dHBV+ and 97.67% (42/43) of serial NATs+ donors, confirming its ​​dominant role in NAT+/HBsAg− discrepancies​​. S gene variations affecting detection and OBI development warrant further study. A total of 123 3-repeated dHBV+ OBI samples were amplified and sequenced, yielding 49 sequences, which included 32 genotype B (GTB) and 17 genotype C (GTC) sequences (Supplementary Figure S1). Comparative mutation analysis of the S gene between OBI and HBsAg+ groups was conducted separately for GTB and GTC. Two GTB OBI sequences with unique nucleotide insertions were excluded to mutation analysis (Supplementary Figure S2). The final dataset included 30 GTB OBI, 57 GTB HBsAg+, 17 GTC OBI, and 52 GTC HBsAg+ sequences.

Multiple site mutations were observed in both groups, with distinct mutation patterns between OBI and HBsAg + sequences. In GTB, 9 candidate OBI-related mutations (E2G, Q101R, K122R, M133T, D144E, G145R, V168A, L175S, and I226S) were significantly more frequent in OBI sequences (P < 0.05, Table 3). In GTC, 14 candidate OBI-related mutations were identified, including E2G, F8L, V14A, Y100C, M103I, G112R, F134L, R160K, S167L, V168A, L174N, L175S, N210S, and C211Y. Notably, E2G, V168A, and L175S were observed in both GTB and GTC OBI sequences, while almost absent in the HBsAg+ group. The proportions of E2G and L175S mutations in GTB OBI were 26.67% and 30.00%, respectively, and in GTC OBI, 23.53% and 17.65% (Table 3).

Discussion

The implementation of NAT for blood donor screening has significantly reduced the risk of HBV transmission through transfusion, as blood from NAT+ donors is discarded and these donors are deferred from future donations. However, inconsistent NAT results frequently present challenges for both blood bank and donors. Due to the high sensitivity of NAT, no standard confirmatory method currently exists to reliably distinguish false positive from true infections [10, 11]. Consequently, blood banks must navigate challenges related to communicating results, establishing donor reentry policies, and improving detection capabilities. Addressing these issues requires a re-evaluation of test outcomes and a deeper understanding of the detection and molecular characteristics of the NAT+/HBsAg- population.

In this study, we performed additional 3-repeated dHBV tests and analyzed serial results at different time points to enhance confirmation rates and assess test outcomes. The addition of repeated discriminatory testing significantly enhanced HBV NAT + confirmation rate, increasing from 37.71% (112/297) with routine dHBV testing alone to 58.59% (174/297). This improvement underscores the value of repeated testing in identifying HBV-infected donors who may otherwise be missed with routine methods. These findings align with previous reports, where repeated NAT testing improved confirmation rates [8, 12, 22]. For instance, a study in Zhejiang showed that two-time repeated NAT testing confirmed 25.29% (110/435) of NDR donors [8 WAWA]. In our study, among the 187 confirmed HBV-infected donors, 128 (68.45%) exhibited missed detections in 4 rounds of dHBV tests, suggesting that the viral load in most NAT+ donors may fall below the detection limit of current assays. The Grifols Elite assay, for instance, has a sensitivity of 4.3 IU/mL, whereas many hospital-based HBV tests have sensitivities of only 100 IU/mL, which may explain the discrepancies observed in donor test results.

According to research, when viral load is extremely low, viral particles follow a Poisson distribution, which can lead to occasional miss detection [22–24]. To increase the likelihood of detecting viral nucleic acids, some studies have employed methods that increase the sample extraction volume. For example, the Shenzhen Blood Center uses ultracentrifugation on 2.5 mL plasma samples [12], while the Dalian Blood Center concentrates virions from 12 mL and 6 mL plasma samples using ultracentrifugation (UC) and polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation, respectively [22]. However, these methods require expensive equipment and involve cumbersome procedures—including multiple rounds of nested PCR and qPCR testing—making them unsuitable for routine use in most blood centers. Our results suggest that repeated testing can significantly improve the discriminatory positivity rate, which is consistent with findings from studies conducted in Europe and South Africa [12, 15]. Since repeated testing does not require additional equipment and is straightforward to perform, it is ideally suited for routine evaluation of test outcomes and for enhancing the confirmation rate of NAT+/HBsAg- donors in general blood banks, where resources may be limited. Furthermore, when combined with serial NAT testing, the overall confirmation rate reached 62.96% (187/297), indicating that a multi-layered testing strategy is more effective in accurately confirming HBV infection status. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating repeated testing and serial NATs in detecting low-level HBV infections. This approach not only enhances confidence in the test results but also facilitates more accurate donor management, helping to reduce misunderstandings and potential disputes with donors.

The re-entry strategy for NAT+/HBsAg− donors has drawn growing attention—not only due to ongoing blood shortages, but also to protect donor rights and reduce deferral-related disputes [10]. Our study showed that 37.04% (110/297) of NAT+/HBsAg− donors could not be classified using any of the three methods, suggesting that these individuals may be potential healthy donors. To mitigate the negative impact of false-positive NAT results, some regional blood centers have implemented donor re-entry programs [10, 12, 14, 25]. Typically, NAT+/HBsAg− and/or NDR donors undergo one or two follow-up tests at intervals of 3–6 months, using the same NAT and serological reagents as applied in the initial screening [10, 25]. If all results are negative, donors may be allowed to re-enter the donor pool. Reported re-entry rates vary widely, ranging from 29% to 80% [10, 25], depending on test sensitivity and protocol design across regions.

However, our findings suggest that adopting such a re-entry testing approach has limited sensitivity for detecting true HBV infections. Among the 113 donors confirmed by routine dHBV or 3-repeated dHBV testing who underwent serial NAT testing, 83 (73.45%) donors can’t be identified through serial NAT testing. Furthermore, detecting an additional positive result required an average of 5.6 tests conducted at different time points. Consistent with our results, previous study also reported significant fluctuations in HBV DNA levels among NAT+/HBsAg– donors during follow-up testing [14]. In addition to low viral load, the low confirmation rate may also be attributed to the intermittent replication pattern characteristic of OBI cases [11, 26, 27]. Therefore, relying solely on one or two negative follow-up tests may not be sufficient to rule out OBI.

As HBV DNA levels as low as 3 IU can be infectious by transfusion [28], stricter re-entry criteria should be implemented. Given the marked intermittent detectability of HBV DNA in OBI cases and the low confirmation rate observed with multiple NATs, allowing all NAT+/HBsAg− donors to re-enter, or excluding only those with routine discriminatory positivity, may significantly increase the risk to transmit HBV. While permanently deferring all NAT+/HBsAg− donors may seem the safest approach, it could unfairly exclude a considerable proportion (37.04%, 110/297) of individuals who are potentially healthy. Due to ongoing blood shortages and the importance of protecting donor rights, it is essential to develop stricter yet more refined re-entry strategies. Our findings proposed a practical and effective approach involves improving confirmation rates prior to making re-entry decisions. Specifically, incorporating 3-repeated discriminatory HBV NAT testing into routine workflows and reviewing donors’ prior test history can significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy, enabling more reliable exclusion of truly high-risk individuals. Additionally, enhancing follow-up protocols by increasing the number of testing rounds and including HBcAb testing may further improve sensitivity. Previous studies have shown that HBcAb is a valuable marker for identifying both past and occult HBV infections [10, 29], and its presence may serve as a practical indicator for excluding donors from re-entry. This approach could help to strengthen blood safety and avoid unnecessary permanent deferrals, particularly for regular donors who may have been misclassified based on a single, unconfirmed reactive result. Noteworthily, the proposed strategy is feasible, cost-effective, and does not require additional equipment, making it practical for broad implementation. A well-designed re-entry framework will help balance safety with sustainability, and ensure a fairer and more supportive donation system for donors.

As previously discussed, OBI infection is the primary cause of inconsistent NAT+/HBsAg− blood donation results, posing a serious threat to blood safety. To mitigate the risk of OBI transmission, there is an urgent need to advance the development of novel detection assays to enhance the sensitivity of both HBsAg and HBV DNA detection, as well as to deepen our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of OBI [23]. Molecular studies have shown that specific mutations—such as I126T, G145R, and D144A—can impair the binding affinity of HBsAg to antibodies, leading to false-negative HBsAg results [5, 30]. Additionally, mutations in the HBV S gene can reduce viral particle secretion, thereby affecting the efficiency and sensitivity of nucleic acid testing. These findings highlight that systematic analysis of HBV S gene variability is a key strategy for improving OBI detection and elucidating its molecular basis.

In this study, we amplified the full-length S gene from confirmed OBI cases, obtaining 32 GTB and 17 GTC sequences. Comparative analysis with genotype-matched HBsAg+ controls revealed a significant increase in MHR mutations among OBI donors, consistent with previous studies [30–32]. Importantly, we observed a significantly higher frequency of non-MHR mutations in OBI donors compared to HBsAg+ donors, suggesting that mutations across the entire S gene—rather than just within the MHR—may contribute to OBI development. Emerging evidence indicates that non-MHR mutations can impair HBsAg secretion, thereby promoting OBI occurrence [6, 33]. Furthermore, our research separately analyzed the mutation differences across various functional regions between the OBI group and the HBsAg+ group—a topic that has been rarely explored in the literature. Our findings reveal that in HBsAg+ donors, mutations were more prevalent in the non-MHR regions. In contrast, in OBI donors, MHR mutations were significantly more frequent than non-MHR mutations, highlighting the critical role of amino acid changes within the MHR in OBI development. Additionally, we identified 9 genotype B (GTB) and 14 genotype C (GTC) candidate OBI-related mutations, many of which were consistent with prior studies [18, 19, 31, 34].

Notably, the E2G, V168A, and L175S mutations were markedly more frequent in OBI donors but significantly less frequent in HBsAg+ controls across both GTB and GTC groups, which is consistent with previous studies [19, 34, 35]. The E2G mutation showed a marked increase in prevalence among OBI donors—26.67% in GTB and 23.53% in GTC—both higher than reported in our earlier study (GTB: 11.2%, GTC: 13.3%) [19]. This mutation, located near the N-terminus of the S protein, may cause conformational changes or truncation that impair HBsAg folding and secretion. The resulting intracellular accumulation of viral proteins has been associated with liver inflammation and may contribute to long-term hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis [33, 35–37]. Similarly, V168A and L175S, both situated within or adjacent to the MHR, may disrupt key antigenic epitopes and interfere with antibody recognition, resulting in undetectable antigen levels in standard assays [19, 35]. Given their relatively high frequency and genotype-independent presence, these mutations could be specifically targeted in the development of improved diagnostic assays to reduce the risk of missed detection and support more effective donor management.

This study has a limitation in that our longitudinal data did not include HBcAb testing, thereby failing to assess the capability of HBcAb in excluding HBV infection. However, it is important to note that although the majority of OBIs are HBcAb-positive, a small proportion may be HBcAb-negative, as reported in previous studies [27, 38]. Therefore, relying solely on HBcAb testing for OBI diagnosis may lead to underestimation. While HBcAb testing can be a useful component of donor re-entry strategies, it should complement rather than replace serial NATs. Another limitation of this study is that some NAT+/HBsAg− donors were excluded due to insufficient sample volume, which may introduce selection bias. However, we included all available and sufficient samples (from 297 donors, 53.42% of all eligible cases) without subjective selection. The subjects included in our study remains sizable and representative. Our key findings are also consistent with prior studies [8, 12, 22], supporting the reliability of the results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the characteristics of NAT in NAT+/HBsAg– blood donors, revealing that low viral loads and intermittent replication in OBI are the primary causes of inconsistent test results. These findings underscore the challenges associated with detecting OBI using conventional protocols, which often fail to identify cases and thereby pose risks to blood safety. Considering the intermittent nature of OBI detection, the integration of serial NATs and repeated discriminatory testing into routine workflows is recommended to enhance confirmation rates and support the safe and appropriate management of donor re-entry programs. In addition, establishing stricter re-entry protocols, particularly by increasing the frequency of follow-up tests, is necessary to further mitigate risk. Moreover, strengthening molecular research on OBI is pivotal for minimizing its hazards. Our detailed analysis of mutations in the HBV S gene, with a focus on the MHR and key mutations such as E2G, highlights their significant role in the pathogenesis of OBI. The higher prevalence mutations of S gene among OBI donors reinforces the need for ongoing research into these molecular markers, with the aim of improving detection methods and developing more effective screening strategies.
