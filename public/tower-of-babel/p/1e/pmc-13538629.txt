Deep learning enabled intelligent robotic system for aeroengine blade surface inspection
Authors: Ehtesham Iqbal, Abdelrahman Youssef, Samee Ullah Khan, M A Mohammed Eltoum, Abdallah Mohammad Alkilany, Yusra Abdulrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAeroengine blades (AEBs) are critical components of aircraft engines that require consistent monitoring and inspection to ensure airworthiness and operational safety. The aerospace industry relies heavily on maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) procedures, where surface inspection of AEBs plays a pivotal role. Traditional manual or borescope-based inspection methods are time consuming, labor intensive, and susceptible to human error. In this work, a deep learning enabled intelligent robotic system is proposed for the autonomous inspection of AEBs, addressing key MRO requirements. Two distinct datasets were collected, one for aeroengine blade localization and another for surface defect detection. A robust deep learning model is trained separately on each dataset to perform their respective tasks with high accuracy. The trained models were integrated into an intelligent robotic system to automate the inspection workflow. In real time operation, a container filled with aeroengine blades is placed in front of the intelligent system, which autonomously localizes each blade, picks it up, places it in an image acquisition box, detects any surface defects, and returns the blade to its original position. The system provides real time feedback and accelerates decision making processes. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach significantly reduces inspection time and enhances defect detection accuracy compared to conventional methods. By seamlessly combining vision based deep learning with robotic automation, the system offers a reliable solution for controlled industrial inspection environments for modern aerospace MRO processes, overcoming the limitations associated with manual inspection techniques. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves an mAP of 88.2% and reduces inspection cycle time to approximately 4 seconds per blade, significantly improving efficiency compared to conventional methods.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Aeroengine blades (AEBs) are vital components in the aerospace industry, where their performance and structural integrity are essential for the optimal functioning and maintenance reliability of aircraft engines1. Operating under extreme conditions and complex loads, these blades are susceptible to a range of defects, from fatigue-induced damage to surface imperfections such as scratches, nicks, dents, and corrosion2. Even minor defects can lead to catastrophic failures that jeopardize flight safety, underscoring the need for advanced defect detection methods during both the production and maintenance phases3–6.

To mitigate these risks, the aircraft industry is highly dependent on rigorous maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) practices to ensure airworthiness and operational safety3. Despite the impressive reliability and service life of modern aircraft engines7, failures and malfunctions can still occasionally occur, leading to in-flight shutdowns. This reality has driven a rising demand for more efficient MRO systems and innovative technologies. MRO in the aerospace industry involves a series of processes, beginning with borescope inspection and progressing through complete engine disassembly, followed by rigorous procedures such as cleaning, non-destructive testing (NDT)8,9, and detailed visual inspection10. Borescope inspection is primarily performed on the assembled engine and provides an initial assessment of engine health prior to disassembly. However, borescope inspections11 incur significant costs due to the need for specialized equipment and certified engineers, and the process remains both labor-intensive and time-consuming. Consequently, non-destructive testing (NDT) has emerged as a critical diagnostic tool8,12. NDT techniques allow the identification of defects such as air holes, inclusions, debonding, and fatigue cracks without compromising the structural integrity of the blades, thus minimizing the economic costs associated with engine failures and replacements13. However, as these inspections are primarily conducted by human operators, they are susceptible to human error, which has been recognized as a major contributing factor in aircraft accidents14.

Advanced image processing15,16, whether human-driven or automated, has become a cornerstone of modern MRO decision making. By leveraging cutting-edge detection methods17, the industry aims to improve inspection precision and speed, ensuring timely maintenance that prevents catastrophic events. There is a continuous focus on the development of advanced defect detection methods for aeroengine blades, addressing both structural fatigue and surface defects3,18,19.These cutting-edge techniques are increasingly being adopted in industrial settings to enhance quality control processes, significantly improving the detection of surface coating defects and ensuring robust quality assurance.The integration of innovative techniques into the MRO workflow promises to improve operational safety while also offering substantial economic benefits through reduced engine failures and optimized maintenance schedules.

Recent advancements in machine vision20, computing and artificial intelligence have spurred significant research in surface defect detection using deep learning techniques16,21. For instance, Villinger et al.22 developed a robotic system for sewer inspection by combining vision data, a multilayer laser scanner, and a fast data processing unit. To address challenges like high background noise in aeroengine blades, Li et al.23 introduced YOLOv3 Lite, offering a 50% speed improvement over YOLOv324 with comparable accuracy. Further improvements in detection accuracy were achieved by Xin et al.25 with an enhanced YOLOv7 algorithm for AEB26. Another approach by Li et al. employed a coarse to fine detection framework18, efficiently identifying tiny defects in high resolution blade images. Shang et al.27 proposed a blade damage detection method using a pore probing camera and a shallow texture network, while Shen et al.11 used fully convolutional networks for rapid inspection through borescopes, achieving results consistent with expert markings.

Currently, limited research has been conducted on AEB surface defect detection during the MRO process, and achieving full automation remains a significant challenge. To address this gap, we propose a deep learning based intelligent system that integrates computer vision and robotic automation for the autonomous inspection of AEB surfaces, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The methodology begins with the systematic collection of image datasets. After the aircraft engine is fully disassembled for the MRO procedure, the aeroengine blades are extracted, and two dedicated datasets are developed, the AEB localization dataset (Fig. 4) and the AEB surface defect detection dataset (Fig. 6). Robust deep learning model is trained separately on these datasets to perform blade localization and AEB surface defect detection tasks with high accuracy. To achieve full automation, a robotic arm is integrated with the trained models. As shown in Fig. 1, the intelligent robotic inspection system initializes by setting its home position and alignment. Using the blade localization model, the robotic arm identifies the precise position of each blade. Once localized, the robotic system autonomously picks the blade, places it in the image acquisition box, and captures a high resolution image. This image is then processed by the AEB defect detection model, which identifies and classifies surface defects. The results are displayed in real time through a user interface to support monitoring and decision making. A demo demonstrating the inspection system at technology readiness levels 4 is available demo video. The entire workflow including blade localization, robotic handling, image acquisition, defect detection, and the return of blades to their original position is executed autonomously. This integrated approach streamlines the inspection process, minimizes human error, and significantly enhances the reliability and safety of aeroengine maintenance operations. By leveraging computer vision and robotic automation, the system reduces human error and accelerates defect detection, thereby enhancing overall reliability and operational efficiency in the aerospace industry. Related work

This section provides a comprehensive review of existing methodologies for detecting surface defects in aeroengine blades. It begins with an overview of traditional techniques historically utilized for defect identification, followed by an in-depth analysis of deep learning-based models that have emerged as more advanced solutions. The discussion concludes with a summary highlighting the progression toward autonomous inspection systems, emphasizing their potential to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of surface defect detection in aeroengine blades.

Traditional approaches of surface defect detection

Early methods for surface defect detection in aeroengine blades primarily relied on conventional techniques, including 3D reconstruction and machine learning based approaches. Li et al.28 employed ultrasonic defect point cloud characterization and evaluation technology to construct a 3D representation of defect areas, allowing for precise spatial visualization. Similarly, Jiao et al.29 utilized features of ultrasonic echo signals in combination with a machine learning classifier to categorize various types of defects. Aust et al.30 introduced an image processing technique aimed at the automatic identification of blade damage. Although these traditional methods contributed significantly to defect detection, they are increasingly being replaced by deep learning-based approaches, which offer enhanced efficiency, accuracy, and robustness in defect classification and localization tasks.

Deep learning based approaches for AEB inspection

Recent advancements in deep learning /31 have led to the exploration of segmentation models32 for detecting damage in aeroengine blades. Bian et al.33 proposed a fully convolutional multiscale network trained on a dataset of 128 images, while Wong et al. applied the Mask R-CNN network to blade damage segmentation using a dataset of 89 images34. However, these methods are constrained by the limited size of their datasets, which may not adequately capture the complexities of real-world defects. To address these limitations, Shang et al.27 introduced an instance segmentation network based on Mask R-CNN for blade damage detection. Furthermore, Shang et al.3 later employed transformer-based and graph neural network (GNN) models to improve segmentation accuracy by overcoming the limitations of traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Despite these advancements, achieving high precision in segmentation remains a challenge, particularly in restoring fine details along segmentation edges. This issue arises due to the excessive reliance on high-level semantic features extracted through continuous convolution and pooling operations, which can lead to loss of spatial information and reduced segmentation accuracy.

In addition to segmentation-based approaches, deep learning models have shown considerable promise in defect detection tasks within industrial applications. Zhang et al.35 employed the YOLOv3 algorithm for the detection of damage to aerospace engine blades (AEB), demonstrating promising results. He et al.34 developed an improved cascaded Mask R-CNN with a convolution block attention module, achieving enhanced performance in detecting AEB defects. Li et al.18 introduced a coarse-to-fine detection network specifically designed to identify small defects on the surfaces of the aeroengine blades. Furthermore, Shang et al.11 proposed several innovative methodologies incorporating enhanced Mask R-CNN and transformer-based architectures to improve the intelligence and efficiency of borescope inspections for blade damage detection. Recent studies have also explored deep learning-based localization of aeroengine industrial components using vision-based systems, enabling accurate positioning for robotic manipulation tasks36.

Despite these significant advancements, accurately detecting tiny surface defects on aeroengine blades remains a persistent challenge. These defects are particularly difficult to identify on smaller blades, such as those found in the early stages of turbo compressors37, due to their small size. Standard neural network architectures often struggle with this task, as downsampling operations can inadvertently filter out these fine features. Moreover, the computational demands of deep learning models, characterized by large parameter counts, impose substantial hardware requirements, limiting their practical adaptability for real-world applications. While state-of-the-art object detection models, including the recently introduced YOLOv838, have demonstrated robust performance on widely used datasets such as Pascal VOC and Microsoft COCO , their effectiveness significantly declines when applied to datasets dominated by tiny defects. As a result, improving defect detection accuracy in challenging industrial environments remains an open area of research.

Autonomous AEB inspection system

While significant advancements have been made in defect detection models, much of the existing research focuses solely on improving detection algorithms rather than developing a fully autonomous inspection system. Currently, limited studies have explored surface defect detection in aeroengine blades during the MRO process, and achieving complete automation in this domain remains a considerable challenge39. To address these limitations, Liu et al.21 designed an autonomous defect detection system based on the Faster R-CNN framework. Their approach involved developing a dataset comprising three types of surface defects: scratches, bruises, and pockmarks, and incorporating an improved version of Faster R-CNN to enhance detection accuracy. Similarly, Chuhan Wang et al.40 proposed a defect analysis system utilizing a deep learning-based segmentation model. While their segmentation approach enabled pixel-wise defect identification, it lacked the ability to distinguish between different classes of defects. Despite this limitation, the model demonstrated high precision in segmentation at the pixel level.Detection-based methods17,23,41,42 were preferred over segmentation due to their lower computational overhead and suitability for real-time robotic deployment, where rapid decision-making is prioritized over pixel-level precision. More recently, Kechen Song et al.2 introduced an aeroengine blade defect detection system that uses semantic guidance from the cross layer. Their system used data-augmentation techniques to expand the dataset and focused on improving the detection of minute defects. The detection model was based on YOLOv642, which, while effective, does not achieve the same level of accuracy as the state-of-the-art object detection frameworks.

Given these limitations, the development of a highly efficient and fully automated defect detection system is essential to improve blade surface quality in both manufacturing and maintenance processes. An integrated approach combining advanced deep learning techniques with robotic automation could significantly improve defect detection reliability and streamline MRO operations in the aerospace industry43,44.

Proposed work

In this study, an autonomous intelligent inspection system is developed that integrates a robust deep learning framework with a robotic system to perform surface inspection of AEBs during the MRO process. The proposed system is composed of three main components, as illustrated in Fig. 2, the image acquisition system, the robotic system, and the deep learning based inspection model. The image acquisition system, designed as an optical setup, is responsible for capturing high resolution images of aeroengine blades during both the dataset preparation stage and real time inspection. The robotic system ensures the precise handling, positioning, and manipulation of blades, enabling seamless integration between hardware and vision-based inspection. The deep learning based AI model forms the core of the system, executing blade position localization and detailed surface defect detection to ensure accurate and reliable inspection results.

Deep learning model

The autonomous inspection system’s core component consists of deep learning based models. In the proposed system, two distinct deep learning models are integrated to achieve the desired functionality. The first model assists the robotic system in precisely handling the aeroengine blade by detecting the blade’s geometry and location. The second model is designed to perform visual inspections of the aeroengine blade for defect analysis. For the deep learning framework, a robust model is developed following38 due to its superior performance in object detection and surface inspection tasks. The effectiveness of deep learning model relies on several key steps, dataset preparation for model training, selection of the model architecture, and the training and testing process. Dataset

Two types of datasets, the AEB localization dataset as shown in Fig. 4 and the AEB surface defect dataset as shown in Fig. 6, were collected to enable the autonomous inspection system. The localization dataset focuses on the geometry and positioning of aeroengine blades (AEBs) to assist the robotic arm in accurate detection and handling. It consists of multiple blades captured within a single frame, with nearly 400 frames extracted from video sequences collected under varying blade arrangements and positions.

Architecture

In this study, a deep learning-based detection model as shown in Fig. 7 is adapted following object detection frameworks38 due to their strong performance in real-time industrial inspection tasks. It builds upon advancements in real time image detection algorithms, incorporating features such as cross stage partial (CSP), the spatial pyramid pooling fast (SPPF) module, and the path aggregation network (PAN) with feature pyramid network (FPN) for feature fusion. Rather than introducing a new architectural design, the model configuration is optimized for the specific requirements of aeroengine blade inspection, including high-resolution imaging, reflective metallic surfaces, and real-time deployment constraints. These established components enable efficient multi-scale feature extraction and have been widely adopted in state-of-the-art object detection frameworks.

Two distinct image datasets, the blade localization dataset and the AEB surface defect dataset, used for model training. The figure also illustrates the detailed architecture of the deep learning models and the visual results obtained after training model on its respective dataset.

The backbone architecture retains similarities to its predecessor, YOLOv54,46, while introducing the C2f module as a replacement for the C3 module to further optimize its performance. Additionally, a scaling coefficient approach similar to YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 but differentiates itself by utilizing binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss for classification tasks.

In the neck region,we continues to use the PAN-FPN methodology, which facilitates the integration of multiscale feature representations. This approach improves the ability of the network to perform robust learning by merging the characteristics of the C3 and ELAN modules. Incorporating multiple C2f modules in head design also refines the combination of confidence and regression bounding boxes, contributing to a significant improvement in accuracy.

A key advantage of the adopted model configuration is its flexibility and compatibility with existing YOLO-based implementations, making it suitable for industrial deployment scenarios. This adaptability makes it particularly suitable for detecting defects in turbine and compressor blades37, including irregularities of the edges, dents, and nicks. This model demonstrates improved precision in defect classification, making it an effective tool for quality assurance in industrial applications.

The integration of the C2f module into the backbone network in place of the conventional C3 module and the decoupled head design emphasizes its structural improvements and their role in enhancing performance.

Results and discussion

This section details the experiments performed during the training and testing of both the AEB localization and AEB defect inspection models. After the dataset preparation and architecture selection, the final phase involves the training and testing of the model for localizing the AEB position and for the inspection of the AEB surface defects, respectively. Firstly, the localization dataset, as shown in Fig. 4 and defect inspection dataset, as shown in Fig. 6 are annotated according to the desired outcome, and then the backpropagation method is used for training. Technically, the training process uses the annotated data set to optimize the weights of the model using backpropagation, with the objective of minimizing the loss function by reducing the disparity between the predicted and actual results. The same process is utilized during the training of the proposed model. The model processes batches of images iteratively, adjusting weights based on the error calculated from the ground-truth annotations. This process is carried out over 150 epochs in AEB localization and AEB defect inspection until the model achieves the desired performance respectively.

The model effectively detects defects with high precision, as seen in the clear localization of damaged areas. Fine-tuning includes adjustments to parameters such as batch size and epoch count, along with potential architectural refinements to enhance accuracy.

In addition to defect detection performance, the robotic inspection system was quantitatively evaluated for real-world applicability. The localization accuracy, based on fiducial marker alignment, was within ±1 mm under controlled conditions. This setup reflects realistic MRO conditions, where data availability is restricted.The proposed system can be integrated into the MRO workflow during the post-disassembly inspection stage, where aeroengine blades are evaluated before refurbishment or replacement. By automating blade localization, handling, and defect inspection, the system reduces manual inspection time and improves consistency. The reported cycle time of approximately 4 seconds per blade highlights its potential for high-throughput industrial inspection.

Although the proposed model demonstrates strong overall performance, further analysis using confusion matrix and precision–recall characteristics indicates certain limitations. False positives occasionally occur in regions with surface texture variations that resemble defect patterns. Similarly, false negatives are primarily associated with very small or subtle defects, such as minor dents or weak corrosion signatures. While the precision and recall trends shown in Fig. 10 indicate stable overall performance, these observations highlight the challenges of achieving robust detection in complex industrial environments.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a deep learning enabled intelligent robotic system for the autonomous surface inspection of aeroengine blades, addressing a critical need in the MRO process. The system integrates advanced deep learning models with robotic automation to overcome the limitations of manual inspections, which are often labor intensive and prone to human error. By preparing dedicated vision sensor datasets for both blade localization and surface defect detection, and by training models to recognize defects such as dents, nicks, and corrosion, our approach significantly enhances both detection accuracy and operational efficiency. The rationale for this approach lies in the inherent risks and inefficiencies associated with traditional inspection methods. Manual inspections can miss subtle defects that, over time, can lead to catastrophic engine failures. By automating the process, our system ensures consistent quality control and reduces the likelihood of human error. In addition, the integration of a robotic arm for precise positioning and image acquisition facilitates the capture of high-resolution images, which are critical for accurate defect analysis. This work demonstrates that the fusion of deep learning techniques with robotic automation not only mitigates the limitations of current inspection practices but also sets the stage for further advancements in industrial maintenance technologies. Future research will focus on improving the precision of defect detection, expanding the dataset to cover a wider range of defect types, and further optimizing the robotic system for real world deployment in the aerospace industry. Future work will explore extended datasets and multiple evaluation runs to further assess statistical robustness.

In conclusion, our proposed system offers a promising solution to improve the safety, reliability, and cost effectiveness of aeroengine blade inspections, ultimately contributing to more robust and efficient MRO practices.
