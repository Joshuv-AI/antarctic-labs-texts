sRNAfrag: a pipeline and suite of tools to analyze fragmentation in small RNA sequencing data
Authors: Ken Nakatsu, Mayumi Jijiwa, Vedbar Khadka, Masaki Nasu, Youping Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFragments derived from small RNAs such as small nucleolar RNAs are biologically relevant but remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we developed sRNAfrag, a modular and interoperable tool designed to standardize the quantification and analysis of small RNA fragmentation across various biotypes. The tool outputs a set of tables forming a relational database, allowing for an in-depth exploration of biologically complex events such as multi-mapping and RNA fragment stability across different cell types. In a benchmark test, sRNAfrag was able to identify established loci of mature microRNAs solely based on sequencing data. Furthermore, the 5’ seed sequence could be rediscovered by utilizing a visualization approach primarily applied in multi-sequence-alignments. Utilizing the relational database outputs, we detected 1411 snoRNA fragment conservation events between two out of four eukaryotic species, providing an opportunity to explore motifs through evolutionary time and conserved fragmentation patterns. Additionally, the tool’s interoperability with other bioinformatics tools like ViennaRNA amplifies its utility for customized analyses. We also introduce a novel loci-level variance-score which provides insights into the noise around peaks and demonstrates biological relevance by distinctly separating breast cancer and neuroblastoma cell lines after dimension reduction when applied to small nucleolar RNAs. Overall, sRNAfrag serves as a versatile foundation for advancing our understanding of small RNA fragments and offers a functional foundation to further small RNA research. Availability: https://github.com/kenminsoo/sRNAfrag.Keywords: Fragments, snoRNA, peaks, conservation, sRNA

INTRODUCTION

Evidence of transfer RNA (tRNA) fragmentation has been reported since at least 1969 [1]. However, it was only in the last few decades that the functional roles of these fragments in relation to disease was revealed [2–4]. Fragmentation of other small RNAs (sRNAs) such as small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) have also been reported, majority of which are derived from the 3’ and 5’ ends of the primary transcript [5]. While much of the sRNA fragment literature has been focused on tRNA-derived fragments, there is rising evidence that fragments derived from other biotypes of sRNAs have a mechanistic role in disease and human health by influencing translation and alternative splicing [6–8]. Reanalysis of Photoactivatable-Ribonucleoside-Enhanced Cross linking and Immunoprecipitation (PAR-CLIP) sequencing data sets have revealed that fragments derived from snoRNAs and rRNAs associate with Argonaute proteins, a component of the RNA-induced silencing complex which facilitates RNA interference [9–11]. Furthermore, fragmentation of sRNAs has been found to occur in eukaryotes besides humans, including mice, fruit flies and chickens [12]. Thus, a pipeline that can easily call fragments is needed to understand why fragmentation is conserved, which may provide insights into their function.

One of the shortcomings in non-tRNA sRNA-fragment literature is that it does not have curated annotations such as MINTbase [13]. sRNAfrag seeks to be a step toward generating such annotations. MINTbase was generated using the MINTmap algorithm which creates a lookup table for every possible fragment that can be derived from tRNAs, assigning each to a unique sequence-based license plate allowing IDs to be reproduced easily [14]. One of the downsides of this method, which we sought to address, is that generating lookup tables from longer sRNAs such as rRNAs would result in a file that ranges in the hundreds of gigabytes. Finally, the authors of the MINTmap algorithm recognized the importance of validating potential fragments before claiming their existence by mapping them to other potential sRNAs.

Other tools exist to analyze the fragmentation of sRNAs that are not tRNAs. The FlaiMapper algorithm detects peaks within BAM files and can accurately reconstruct start and end loci for mature micro RNAs (miRNAs) [15]. However, since BAM files are used as an input, users must make decisions while processing raw reads which can lead to different results and introduced biases dependent upon factors such as the assignment of multi-mapping reads or mismatch parameters [16]. sRNAs including rRNAs, small nuclear (snRNAs), snoRNAs and tRNAs also often have many isoforms in eukaryotic genomes that fragments align to [17–20]. Thus, the multi-mapping problem must at least be addressed.

The authors behind SURFr (Short Uncharacterized RNA Finder) present a new alignment algorithm and use continuous wavelet transformation to identify peaks [21]. While it represents a new and interesting way to handle fragments, their web portal could not be accessed as of 16 June 2023.

Notably, sRNA sequencing libraries do not undergo fragmentation (typical in standard RNA-seq) and are instead size selected through gel or bead based protocols. [22]. This is useful to keep in mind when designing computational pipelines and was used in Tang et. al’s. [23] Anchor Alignment-based Small RNA Annotation (AASRA) method to justify the addition of anchors to the 3’ and 5’ ends of sRNA sequencing reads to discriminate between similar sRNAs. With this in mind, we assume throughout this paper that if a portion of a longer sRNA (100–200nt) has supporting reads, we count it as a fragment of that longer sRNA as many others have done [5–10].

With this idea in mind, we sought to combine elements from these different methods and pipelines to create sRNAfrag, a pipeline to analyze sRNA fragmentation not only from sRNA sequencing runs but also between them. This pipeline integrates a vast array of functionalities, including count normalization, distribution analysis, cross-database annotation, motif analysis and relative expression profiling, complemented by a suite of conversion tools, annotation file descriptors and General Transfer Format (GTF) generation and modification utilities. Furthermore, sRNAfrag is designed to be a collaborative and evolving platform: any user can contribute to its development through simple pull requests, thereby enhancing its capabilities and fostering a community-driven approach. This open collaboration model not only expands the tool’s utility but also significantly contributes to improving reproducibility and innovation in the field of sRNA analysis. The pipeline requires only raw reads, an annotation file and a reference genome to run, leaving only the demultiplexing of samples and library preparation to the user.

RESULTS

sRNAfrag effectively calls peaks and has a wide range of potential applications in srna research

Here, we seek to demonstrate that sRNAfrag is able to accurately quantify sRNA fragment counts and identify their potential start and end cleavage sites. We aim to establish that sRNAfrag’s performance is comparable with, if not better than, other existing tools in this field. To accomplish this, we validated the accuracy of our tool’s predicted cleavage loci using mature miRNA start and end positions from MiRbase, treating pre-miRNAs as the progenitors of miRNAs. In terms of obtaining the start and end loci of cleavage, sRNAfrag’s performance was benchmarked against the Flaimapper pipeline. Because the computational task of peak detection is fairly trivial, we deemed one pipeline for comparison to be sufficient. We also compared the sRNA counts obtained from sRNAfrag with those from the AASRA pipeline.

sRNAfrag had 597 pre-miRNAs with supporting fragments after filtering compared with the 642 that FlaiMapper obtained. The discrepancy between the two pipelines exist because sRNAfrag deals with multi-mapping by dividing the number of associated counts by the number of loci it could have originated from. Thus, filtering based on counts is more strict in sRNAfrag. Of these pre-miRNAs, sRNAfrag called 94.7% and 86.5% of start and end loci within 1 bp of their true position (Figure 1A). This is comparable with FlaiMapper which called 92.4% and 85.2% of start and end loci in our dataset with default settings [15]. Notably, offsets are not a result of false peak callings. Rather, count peaks exist at positions that do not exactly match loci in miRbase as can be seen in [Additional Figure 1].

Comparing the counts used to call peaks with that of AASRA shows that sRNAfrag utilizes counts that are comparable with more standard sRNA-seq workflows during peak calling, affirming that, in our pipeline, loci-count relationships are properly preserved and used (Figure 1B). After peaks are called, fragments are clustered together in a manner dependent upon their distance from the nearest start or end peak. After clustering, we find that the fit of the regression improves and that the slope is closer to one, indicating that counts are better resolved (Figure 1C). We infer that the underestimation of slope (0.983) and negative intercept is partially due to the AASRA pipeline’s behavior to assign alignments that have a 1 n.t. mismatch stretching beyond the source transcript due to the anchoring feature of that pipeline. Because of this, starting positions prior to the first loci of the annotation are counted as being apart of that transcript. On the other hand, sRNAfrag does not, as a sequence preceding a transcript is not considered when generating the lookup table. We verify the performance of AASRA by benchmarking the method against a synthetic dataset whose results presented in [Additional Figure 2].

Multi-mapping events reveal potentially important conserved fragments loci

The utility of sRNAfrag expands beyond the basic analyses presented in the summary report depicted in [Additional File 1].In the sRNA space, the multi-mapping problem poses a formidable problem. However, we decided to utilize multi-mapping reads to attempt to elucidate important loci under the hypothesis that important portions of the sRNA fragment would be conserved across evolutionary time and duplication events. We again explore miRNAs as a model. The 5’ seed sequence of miRNAs represent the functionally important portion of the molecule [24]. Furthermore, miRNA families, arising from duplication events, are likely subject to evolutionary pressures to retain the same seed sequence [25]. Utilizing a function packaged with sRNAfrag, we extracted the a) most commonly shared fragment(s) between source transcripts and b) most counted fragment within a cluster.

The mir-30 family had four (three are depicted, with the final being 1 n.t. longer) fragments that were shared among all potential sources (Figure 2A). Each were aligned at their 5’ ends, representing the beginning of the 5’ derived mature miRNA (hsa-miR-30c-5p) despite differing 5’ flanking sequences. With this in mind, we investigated all clusters of fragments with more than one potential source transcript. We found that the 5’ end of the most counted fragment match the most commonly shared fragment 65.9% of the time (Figure 2B). This lays in contrast to the 3’ end which matches only 34.2% of the time, which indicates that cells may be more selective about cleavage sites proximal to the function portion of the fragment.

We attempted to apply the same logic to a fragment derived from snoRD115 and snoRNAs to see if selectivity occurs at either the 3’ or 5’ ends, which may be indicative of functionality. The fragment in question was shared between 12 source transcripts that had slight differences in sequence at their 3’ ends (Figure 2B). While the proposed multiple sequence alignment (MSA) appeared promising, we found that the fragmentation of snoRNAs was inherently different from miRNAs. Exploring the location of these fragmentation events showed that 27.7% of conservation events were at terminal portion of the source transcript compared with just 1.5% for miRNAs (Figure 2D). This may indicate that snoRNA fragment functionality (assuming functionality is derived from the sequence) is either (1) maintained by cleaving the fragment in such a way that the terminal end remains constant or (2) influenced by selective conservation of specific internal sequences, rather than the terminal ends. It is crucial to note that 42.5% and 40.4% of snoRNA fragments have the most counted fragment match, the most commonly shared fragment whose 5’ and 3’ ends are conserved in the middle of the snoRNA, which may indicate that functionality is not specific to any terminal end of the snoRNA. In the future, similar assignments could allow for better understanding of fragment biogenesis in other biotypes of sRNAs. Finally, instances where the most frequently found and counted transcript do not match can be attributed to the inherent complexities of sRNA processing and the nature of multi-mapping events in sRNA sequencing.

Loci level variance score holds potential biological relevance

Each cluster of fragments included in the output from sRNAfrag is associated with a dictionary of loci relative to their potential source transcripts that are associated with counts. Going source by source, the main peak is marked. With this, we calculated a variance-like score for each cluster–source pair by multiplying the square of the difference between the peak loci and fragment loci by the proportion of counts a fragment makes up, providing a descriptive statistic into count spread. This was done for both the start and end loci.

To investigate whether this variance-like score is biologically significant, we applied it to our cell line data with the hypothesis that different types of cells will maintain the stability of their fragments to differing degrees. Interestingly, we found that the score was specific to the biotype of sRNA (Figure 3A-B). After zeroes were removed, a two-tailed students T-test yielded P < 0.001 for all comparisons with snoRNA in the start and end buckets, indicating that each biotype has its own unique level of stability. We also observed that the 3’ ends of sRNAs tended to have a higher score than their 5’ counterparts. Further, 6.0%, 26.2% and 30.1% of cluster–source pairs had an start score of 0 for rRNA, snRNA and snoRNA, respectively, while their end scores were 6.3%, 23.2% and 26.2%. This indicates that, out of the three biotypes we investigated, snoRNAs are the most specific around their cleavage sites.

Loci Level Variance Scores and UMAP Dimension Reduction A-B) Distribution of start and end loci-level variance scores across rRNA, snoRNA and snRNA in human samples. Zero values were omitted from the figure. C-H) UMAP dimension reduction applied to start and end scores for human cell lines with three primary classes of cells: Healthy epithelial, breast cancer and neuroblastoma.

Given that we observed biotype specificity in terms of fragment cleavage site stability, we sought to investigate if the variance-score is sample- or condition-specific in our cell line data. For each cluster, we obtained the sum of of all cluster–source pairs. The score matrix was subjected to uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) to reduce its dimensionality. We observed that replicates derived from separate cultures of the same cell line type typically clustered together, indicating that our loci-variance is reproducible. (Figure 3C-H). The only exception occurred with snRNA start and end scores for the SK-N-SH neuroblastoma cell line. Interestingly, we noticed the strongest clustering on the basis of cancer type in snoRNA start and end loci variance scores (Figure 3E,H). This is noteworthy because this condition-specific level of variance around cleavage sites may impact novel diagnostics such as liquid biopsy as sequence changes may impact primer binding if RT-qPCR is used.

License plating allows conserved fragments to be detected

Given that orthologous snoRNA genes are fairly common, we decided to investigate if fragments of similar sequence could also be conserved across species [26]. Hamming distance calculates the number of differences between two sequences of equal length. Using this method, 1411 conservation events between two species were found with a hamming distance at most of 3, majority of which were fragments shared between M. musculus and H. sapiens (Figure 4A).

Out of the 1411, we chose to investigate one shared fragment (snoRD15A in humans and mouse) derived from the 3’ ends of primary transcripts in three-fourths of our chosen species. RNAfold and RNAplot was used to generate minimum free energy structures of source transcripts [27]. The sRNAfrag workflow associates fragments with a cluster which has associated start and end loci. The cluster start and end are highlighted on the secondary structure, as opposed to the fragment start and end (Figure 4B-D). The fragment derived from the C. elegans snoRNA was the most different out of the three, with a hamming distance of two (Figure 4B). The fragments derived from snoRNAs in H. sapiens and M. musculus shared the same sequence (Figure 4C-D). Fragments were derived from SNORD15A which is annotated for M. musculus and H. sapiens. This is a proof of concept that attempting to explore fragmentation between species may not only be fruitful, but is also made easier with sRNAfrag as its outputs can be joined and used in scripts such as the one used in this section. The MSA of the fragments is also depicted (Figure 4E).

sRNAfrag run time scales linearly for multiple biotypes and species

We tested sRNAfrag with four different species (M. musculus, A. thaliana, C. elegans, H. sapiens) and three biotypes of sRNAs (snoRNA, snRNA, rRNA) with a maximum of one job. The amount of time that sRNAfrag takes exhibits a linear relationship with the number of fragments discovered in a manner that is mostly independent of the number of sequenced reads (Figure 5A). For every 1000 additional unique fragments discovered, users can expect an additional 23 s of run time. Note that parallelization can increase speed of time-intensive modules such as sorting of SAM file and alignment back to reference genome. This is slower than reported times in SURFr, taking an average of 4.5 min to complete jobs [21]. However, our pipeline has two important modules which we find important for varying reasons.

sRNAfrag Run Time and Human Outside Map Composition A) Overall run time in minutes. B–D) The composition of out of space maps for rRNA, snRNA and snoRNA fragments in humans.

Firstly, especially large run times are observed for runs where over 10 000 are processed. This is because when the number of fragments surpasses 8000, sampling of the smallest fragments is conducted, which takes a significant amount of time on top of alignment time. We justify the importance of detecting outside maps by investigating where fragments are mapping to in our cell line dataset. We found that most reads from fragments map to piRNAs (Figure 5B-D). Interestingly, snRNA U1 has been found to be similar to that of tandem repeat tRNA, which may explain why snRNA fragment alignments were annotated as tRNAs [28]. We believe that these results indicate that outside maps should always be assessed as they are always existent.

Secondly, generation of the lookup table takes a significant amount of time as it requires that sequences be sorted and queried. We originally generated a lookup table of all possible fragments. However, this led to memory issues, especially as possible fragments would range in the tens of millions (Table 2). Furthermore, fragments that exist typically represent only a small fraction of possible sequences in nearly every species tested.

Intron-derived fragments that are outside of the 20–22 bp range of miRNAs may be interesting for investigators as fragments of different sizes have been found to be potential sources of biomarkers for disease [29]. Lopes et. al. [30] reported that transcript length peaks at 2065 bp over 92 696 transcripts and genes. Analyzing fragmentation over 20 000 protein coding genes would yield a theoretical number of 1.3 billion possible fragments. Assuming that fragmentation of protein-coding gene follows the trend of snRNAs (0.06% of fragments out of all possible) for a total of 780 000 detected fragments, this would take about 5 h to run. We previously found that lookup table generation occurs at a rate of 11 000 fragments /. Assuming a linear run time, lookup table generation alone would take 32 h and would take up 252 GB of space. Thus, our approach of sorting sequences for lookup table creation allows for wider scalability and application over alternatives like MintMap which require a full lookup table to run. Thus, our pipeline sacrifices run time for generalizability.

DISCUSSION

The development and validation of sRNAfrag presents an opportunity to advance our understanding of sRNA fragmentation by providing a framework to explore multi-mapping events and conserved fragments between species. It packages together tools to generate figures that are relevant to sRNA fragmentation beyond simple analyses. Here, we will discuss why we did not choose to have sRNAfrag generate annotations, how our tool can act as a foundation for future discovery, limitations and next directions for the tool.

Generation of annotations?

Unlike sRNAfrag, Flaimapper generates annotations for users, which is admittedly convenient. Given that sRNAfrag performed similarly in terms of peak calling, it is reasonable to believe that sRNAfrag could assume a similar model of output (Figure 1A). However, we believe this is not an optimal method for advancing the sRNA field for three reasons:

Calling start and end loci solely on peaks is subject to its limitations as can be seen by the similar performance between FlaiMapper, sRNAfrag and other benchmarks [15].

We should collaborate to develop annotations for fragments instead of allowing researchers to develop annotations independently. Currently, the tags ‘small RNA’, ‘snoRNA’, ‘snRNA’, ‘rRNA’ and ‘smRNA’ yield only a total of 13 databases compared with 107 for ‘miRNA’ in the National Genomics Data Center Database Commons [32].

These constraints may seem to paint our pipeline as obsolete. On the surface, the pipeline serves its purpose, to facilitate the analysis of sRNA fragmentation. See [Additional File 2] for how base sRNAfrag outputs can corroborate past findings about sRNAs by investigators such as Taft et. al. and Li. et. al. At its core, however, sRNAfrag seeks to be a framework that engages scientists interested in the Evolutionary, Structural (RNA), Algorithmic, Graphical and Systems components (and more) of bioinformatics to attract a high impact community [33]. We believe such a pursuit possible because of novel discoveries continuing to occur in the non-tRNA fragmentation space with dates ranging from 2009 to 2022 [7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 34, 35]. While ambitious and subjective, this represents the overarching goal of our pipeline.

sRNAfrag as a foundation to build upon

The base function of sRNAfrag outputs a set of tables which can be thought of as a relational database associated with a particular set of sequencing data. The schema of these tables is outlined in the wiki attached to our public repository. With these tables, it becomes possible to acknowledge and explore the biological complexity that is associated with multi-mapping events. These loci can represent sequences with high copy numbers or gene duplication events. By analyzing these, we can gain insight into potentially important sequences such as the 5’ miRNA seed sequence (Figure 2A). By subjecting additional sequencing datasets from different experiments and species to this type of analysis, it may elucidate interesting facts about how these sequences have evolved through time [36].

The relational nature of the outputs of sRNAfrag also allows for the clustering function to provide insight into the relative amount of noise that is associated with particular peaks. It was of particular interest to us that the amount of noise around certain fragments were associated with the type of cell line (Figure 3C–H). We hypothesize that different cell types hold differing levels of RNA stability at the 3’ and 5’ ends of fragments. It may also be possible that the biogenesis of fragments differs with the epitranscriptome serving as one potential mechanism (of many) by which the splicing or stability of RNA fragments differs between tissue type [37]. Interestingly, this hypothesis may hold weight as m6A modifications were found to be abundant in single stranded portions of mouse snoRNAs [38].

Finally, the outputs of sRNAfrag can readily be piped into other tools such as ViennaRNA, as evident by our analysis of the secondary structure of SNORD15A in C. elegans, H. sapiens and M. musculus using scripts attached to the sRNAfrag pipeline (Figure 4B-C). Tutorials for reproducing these results are also included in the wiki attached to our repository. We believe that the ability for the outputs of sRNAfrag to be piped into other tools to be powerful. By utilizing the relational nature of the outputs, one could easily generate local secondary structure predictions that occur proximal to fragments in a manner that can be piped into secondary structure alignment tools such as RNAlign2D [39].

The additional functionalities presented in this paper were built atop of a python script which utilizes the package Fire to allow for command line access to functions. In this respect, sRNAfrag acts as a foundation that allows researchers to add their own functions to serve their own purposes. Furthermore, intermediate tables are kept, which allows users to disregard clustering or peak calling depending upon their use case. Finally, the repository wiki contains documentation for over 60 functions, their use cases, examples, tutorials and more. We also provide a description on how individuals can contribute to the repository to accelerate investigation into sRNA fragmentation.

Limitations

The merging of fragments into clusters makes it difficult to ascertain which standard normalization methods to use. To remedy this, we have integrated three normalization methods, ratio normalization with miRNAs, transcripts per million and counts per million (reads) [40]. This is discussed in further detail in [Additional File 3].

While this offers a method to normalize between samples in one run of sRNAfrag, it does not offer a comprehensive method to compare counts between runs. Combining simply on license plates is possible; however, we were reluctant to implement such an option as fragmentation analysis is likely subject to large technical variations based upon runs [41]. When preparing libraries for Illumina sequencing, size distributions can vary between size selection protocols such as gel electrophoresis, AMPure XP, Caliper Labchip XT or Sage Science Pippin Prep [42]. A library that has a larger composition of shorter reads of lower complexity will lead to lower resolution when clustering fragments. Thus, in this first release of sRNAfrag, we only provide tools to qualitatively compare fragments between runs.

Finally, our peak calling algorithm does not account for secondary structure or motifs that may be indicative of true fragmentation sites. Thus, users are left with very little information as to whether or not their detected fragments are degradation products or not. Including motifs may make our pipeline more effective in detecting true fragments. For example, C/D box snoRNA-derived fragments often contain their respective motifs (C: UGAUGA or TGATGA in the figure), as seen in Figure 2B. The C/D box motif has been reported to direct 5’ capping, thereby increasing the stability of snoRNAs, which may explain why the C/D box is found fragmented at higher rates from the 5’ end of the parent transcript [12, 43].

Future directions

Besides optimization and bug fixes, future development of sRNAfrag will focus on the detection of post-transcriptional modification events. In eukaryotic organisms, RNAs are degraded through 3’ exonuclease activity by the exosome complex (not to be confused with extracellular vesicles) [44]. We hypothesize that protected fragments hold greater biological significance. The 3’ end of hsa-miR-122 is subjected to a non-template addition of an adenine that can increase its stability [45]. We are curious if a similar mechanism exists for sRNA-derived fragments. By pursuing this, we hope to unveil a larger number of potential fragments that may be significant to life and biology.

CONCLUSION

The sRNAfrag pipeline comprehensively and quickly executes to describe sRNA fragmentation in sRNA-seq data. The outputs are easy to understand and to work with. Counts are tabularly summarized and potential fragment sequences are outputted in a fasta format. Because of the variety of outputs, the sRNAfrag pipeline can be utilized beyond standard differential expression measures (which could be accomplished with packages such as DESeq2) [46]. For example, by mining cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) sequencing datasets, one can identify what protein complexes are formed by these fragmented RNAs [47]. One could imagine Figure 4A, with one standard sRNA-seq dataset and three CLIP-seq datasets instead of species to detect which fragments associate certain proteins. Knockdown experiments can also be used to elucidate potential mechanisms of their biogenesis [48].

There are also many tools that can predict function of sRNAs based on their sequence [49]. sRNAfrag outputs can easily be modified with scripts to fit inputs for other methods. For example, a user could recursively loop through two fasta files, one with mRNAs and the other with outputs from this pipeline to generate the proper input for TargetNet, a deep learning target prediction method [50].

We believe that sRNAfrag will be helpful in elucidating not only potential biomarkers for disease, but also for investigating the role of RNA fragmentation in larger contexts. Eventually, we would like to use sRNAfrag to develop a standardized method to curate a database of high-confidence sRNA fragments. Such developments are out of the scope of this paper and will be addressed in future versions of sRNAfrag as we strongly believe that inaccurate annotations are worse than none.

METHODS

Pipeline overview and annotation data sources

sRNAfrag can be broken down into three distinct parts which we have labeled as P1, S1 and P2 (Figure 6). Users specify the location of files, adapter sequences, which modules to run, etc. by modifying a YAML configuration file. YAML files are similar to JSON files. A modified annotation file in GTF, along with a folder of gzipped fastq files, is used as an input.

Pipeline Workflow Summary of the sRNAfrag pipeline. Users first configure the pipeline by modifying an included YAML file. Users may also modify annotation files using included scripts if needed. Three modules, P1, S1 and P2, are used to processed raw gzipped fastq files to a final summary report while also generating figures.

We have created and provided scripts that parse NCBI annotations. This was applied to three common model species: M. musculus, C. elegans and A. thaliana [51]. Human annotations were derived from snoDB (snoRNA), RNAcentral (snRNA) and Integrated Transcript Annotation for sRNA (rRNA) [52–54]. All processing is documented on our public repository (https://github.com/kenminsoo/sRNAfrag). Methods for validating and combining annotations from multiple sources are discussed further in [Additional File 4], which can improve sRNAfrag’s utility. License plate IDs are generated based on sequence, allowing for lookup tables generated from different annotations to share the same IDs, using code from the makers of MINTmap [14]. Then, the lookup table and each sample’s counts are joined on the basis of sequence.

S1 module - multi-mapping correction and basic analyses

An R script parses counts, dividing them by the number of potential fragment sources to account for multi-mapping. A linear regression is applied to the counts before and after the correction to allow users to assess its performance. A line graph depicting count density by length is also generated. ggseqlogo generates logos for each fragment length to detect potential motifs [61]. Transcripts with under 5 adjusted counts are filtered out prior to downstream analysis. Corrected and raw counts are saved to the specified out directory.

P2 module - merging and compiling fragments

Bipartite star graphs are generated to construct count peaks by loci on possible source transcripts with the python package NetworkX. Formally, graphs are built with center nodes from S, the set of possible sources and edges extend to nodes from F, the set of fragments, if its sequence can be found within a particular source node or transcript. Each edge holds count and loci data.

Cluster IDs are assigned to each loci. Fragment IDs (license plates) that associate with each source are assigned to a particular cluster. A clustering graph is initialized with fragment IDs as nodes. Edges are drawn if, on a particular source, a fragment shares the same start and end cluster ID. All fragments are aligned back to a reference genome to detect false positives. Unix commands are used to parse the alignments. FeatureCounts is used to annotate each fragment [62]. If there are over 8000 fragments, fragments are randomly sampled by cluster to allow featureCounts to execute properly.

Fragmentation variance score

To calculate the fragmentation variance score, a function resembling the standard discrete variance was used. This was applied to each merged transcript–source pair where each pair has j associated fragments.

Cell culture

SK-N-SH, IMR32, T47D, HCC1143 and HMEC cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained at 37 in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. SK-N-SH and IMR32 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. T47D and HCC1143 cells were grown in ATCC-formulated RPMI-1640 Medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 0.2 Units/ml of human recombinant insulin (Life Technologies) for T47D. To maintain HMEC, Mammary Epithelial Cell Basal Medium (ATCC) supplemented with Mammary Epithelial Cell Growth Kit (ATCC) was used.

Total RNA isolation from cells

Total RNA was isolated from cells using miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Briefly, cells grown at 80% confluence in a 100 mm cell culture dish were washed with PBS and collected in a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube. Cells suspended in approximately 200ul of PBS were sonicated under RNase-free conditions. Then, the lysate was treated with 700 ul of QIAzol (QIAGEN) and 140 ul of chloroform to isolate nucleic acids in the aqueous phase. RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s instruction and eluted with 30 ul of nucleic acid-free water. Finally, 1 ul of RNase inhibitor was added to the solution. The RNA quality and concentration were measured by nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent).

Library preparation

Preparation of the library was done by the Genomics and Bioinformatics Shared Resource (GBSR) at the University of Hawaii Cancer Center. In accoradnce with manufacturer’s instructions, QIAseq miRNA Library Kits (QIAGEN) and QIAseq miRNA NGS 12 Index IL (QIAGEN) were used for library construction. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a high-sensitivity DNA chip and sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 500 at GBSR.
