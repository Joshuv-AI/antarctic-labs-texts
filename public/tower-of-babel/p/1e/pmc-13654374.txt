Changes in orthopaedic surgical procedures in post-war Afghanistan: A retrospective review of internal fixation procedures at Lashkar-Gah non-governmental organization hospital (2020 − 2023)
Authors: Giacomo Fiorio, Nunan Deirdre, Kushal Ibrahimi, Marcello Zavatta, Massoud Afghan, Luigi Falzetti, Juan Carlos Perdomo-Lizarraga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFollowing 2 decades of conflict in Afghanistan, the withdrawal of international forces and the Taliban's return to power in August 2021 led to reduced war trauma cases. This study aims to analyze the changes in injury patterns and the trends in internal fixation procedures (IFPs) in a non-governmental organization (NGO) hospital in Afghanistan.MethodsRetrospective study including patients treated with IFPs between January 2020 and December 2023 at the Surgical Centre for War Victims-Lashkar-Gah Emergency NGO Hospital. Type of injury, fracture characteristics, orthopaedic implants, temporary fixation, and fracture-related infection (FRI) rate were evaluated. Non-categorical variables are presented as median (Q1, Q3). Group differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test.ResultsA total of 2801 IFPs were analyzed. Non-war injuries (NWI) represented 2500 (89.3%), while war injuries (WI) represented 301 (10.7%). Post-conflict, there was a significant increase in IFPs, and a 275% increase in IFPs for NWI. We found 2085 closed fractures (74.4%), and 716 open fractures (25.6%). Among the NWI cases, 2036 (81.4%) were identified as closed fractures. In contrast, 252 (83.7%) WI cases involved open fracture (p < 0.001). The distal humerus was the most affected site in 736 (26.3%) cases. Kirschner wires were the most frequently used implant in 1561 (55.7%) procedures. The overall fracture-related infection rate was 2.3% (64 cases), significantly lower for NWI 2% (51 of 2500 cases) than WI 4.3% (13 of 301 cases), p = 0.010.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that the end of the conflict brought a significant increase in orthopaedic IFPs facilitated by the reduced severity of trauma in post-conflict Afghanistan. This study highlights the need for tailored training and specific implant availability to meet the evolving healthcare demands of the Afghan population in NGO settings.Keywords: Orthopedic procedures, Fracture internal fixation, Orthopedic fixation devices, War-related injuries, Afghanistan, Orthopedic surgeons

Introduction

Afghanistan suffered from 20 years of uninterrupted war, with a devastating impact on the civilian population. According to the United Nations Assistance Mission in Afghanistan, between 2009 and the first 6 months of 2021, there were 116,076 civilian victims overall: 40,218 killed, and 75,858 wounded.1 However, an imprecise number was affected by the indirect consequences of war. Limb wounds account for 50% − 75% of all injured patients presenting at hospitals during armed conflict. Although not a major cause of mortality, injuries to the soft tissues and bones of the limbs constitute the greatest burden on the surgical workload.2,3

The withdrawal of international forces and the Taliban surge back to power in August 2021 marked the end of the 2001 − 2021 war in Afghanistan. The transition to a period of relative peace and increased stability resulted in a reduction in injuries caused by violent trauma, and the monthly number of security incidents reported by the International non-governmental organization (NGO) Safety Organization has decreased significantly from an average of 2554 incidents in 2020 to a monthly average of 1004 incidents in 2023.4 Despite the end of active conflict, Afghanistan is still a country in a humanitarian crisis.

For over 2 decades, Emergency NGO has provided free, high-quality medical treatment and aid to the people of Afghanistan with Hospitals and First Aid Posts scattered all over the country.5 Since 2004, the Emergency NGO has managed the Surgical Centre for War Victims in Lashkar-Gah, Helmand. Today, the Emergency NGO Hospital is the only free trauma center in Helmand province, serving a population of over 1.9 million people, about half in need of humanitarian support. The activities of the hospital faced significant changes after 2021, since the treatments have been mainly addressed to non-war-related trauma patients, namely, orthopaedic care.

A retrospective study was conducted to analyze changes in injury patterns and trends in internal fixation procedures (IFPs) in an NGO hospital in Afghanistan between January 2020 and December 2023. Variables such as mechanism of injury, fracture type, fracture location, type of orthopaedic implant used for fracture fixation, and fracture-related infection (FRI) rate were evaluated.

While the treatment of war-related and civilian fractures has been extensively documented separately,3,6, 7, 8 however, this study is particularly relevant as it is one of the first to describe fracture care during the transitional period between active conflict and peace.

Methods

Study setting

Surgical Centre for War Victims-Lashkar-Gah is an NGO hospital located in Lashkar-Gah, Afghanistan. The hospital had 93 beds,9 6 intensive care beds without ventilators, 3 operating theaters, and 1 outpatient department that functioned as an emergency department. The orthopaedic trauma facilities included a room for casting/splinting and one for physiotherapy. The 2020 admission criteria were war-related injuries, pediatric trauma, readmission of patients treated with complications, or a life-threatening condition of any cause. Starting from April 2022, admission criteria were officially open to all civil traumas, with progressive steps-narrowing criteria in case of a high bed occupancy rate.

Study design

This was a retrospective study of patients treated at the hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria were patients who underwent orthopaedic procedures involving internal fixation. Patients who were exclusively treated with external fixation, skeletal traction, and simple closed reduction fixed with a Plaster of Paris or slabs, or patients with missing data in the medical chart, were excluded. Demographic information such as sex, age, cause of injury, date of surgery, and information about the surgical procedure was collected by reviewing the surgical reports database and patient files. Pediatric patients were defined as those aged <14 years, procedures were classified based on the anatomical site and type of internal fixation device.

Ethics statement

The data used in this study were obtained from patients’ medical records. Data were collected retrospectively and analyzed pseudo-anonymously. This study was reviewed and approved by the independent institutional review board of Emergency NGO.

Statistical analysis

All the data was collected using Microsoft Excel. Non-categorical variables are descriptively presented as median (Q1, Q3). Statistical significance between the groups was calculated using the Mann-Whitney U test. The Chi-square method was used to examine the differences between categorical variables. Statistical analysis was performed using an online Chi-square test calculator tool. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Between January 2020 and December 2023, the Surgical Centre for War Victims-Lashkar Gah performed 29,034 surgeries in 15,799 patients. Of these, 49.3% (n = 14,326) were war victims and 50.7% presented non-war-related injuries (n = 14,708) (Fig. 1A). A total of 3099 IFPs (10.7% of all procedures) were performed during the 4-year study period (Fig. 1B), involving 2812 patients (17.8% of all patients). Due to the missing data in the medical chart, 298 cases were excluded, leaving a total of 2801 IFPs performed on 2572 patients that were included in the analysis.

There were 81.5% male patients (2096 of 2572) and 18.5% female patients (476 of 2572), the median age at admission was 13 (8, 24) years for the entire cohort, the overall median age of the cohort for the pediatric group (classified as <14 years of age) was 8 (6, 10) years and 25 (18, 37) years for the adult group.

Cause of injuries

Injuries were classified as non-war injuries (NWI), which included all types of civil trauma, and war injuries (WI), which included violent trauma from bullets, knives, mines, and explosions. Violent trauma occurring after the end of the active conflict in Afghanistan in 2021 was still classified as WI. NWI represented 89.8% of the patients (2310 of 2572). The most common cause of NWI was road traffic accidents (RTAs), with 898 patients (34.9%), followed by 803 patients (31.2%) who fell either from a high or ground level. A 275% increase in the number of IFPs for NWI was observed during the study period. A total of 262 patients (10.2%) presented injuries caused by WI. Fracture locations and implants used

Of the 2801 fractures treated with internal fixation (Table 2), 716 (25.6%) cases were classified open fractures, and 2085 (74.5%) cases were classified closed fractures, which increased from 65% in 2020 to 78% in 2023. We determined that 2036 NWI cases (81.4% of all NWI cases) were closed fractures. In contrast, 252 WI cases (83.7% of all WI cases) had open fractures (p < 0.001). The most common anatomical sites (Fig. 2) involved the distal humerus with 736 (26.3%) fractures and the femoral shaft with 675 (24.1%) fractures. The most common internal fixation devices used were Kirschner wires (K-wires) with 1561 (55.7%) procedures that were mostly used to treat distal humerus fractures and intramedullary nails (surgical implant generation network = SIGN®) with 517 (18.5%) procedures that were mostly used for femoral shaft fractures in adult patients.

Temporary stabilization before internal fixation

Of the 2801 IFPs, 463 (16.5%) received temporary stabilization prior to definitive treatment, either through skeletal traction or external fixation, and 81% (375 of 463) were adult patients. Of these 463 cases, 37.1% (172) had sustained open fractures, 13.4% (23 of 172) were treated with external fixation, and 86.6% (149 of 172) had skeletal traction.

WI caused 39% (67 of 172 cases) of the open fractures that received temporary stabilization, with a median time from the application of the temporary stabilization device and the internal fixation procedure of 25 (17, 52) days, while the median time for open fractures caused by NWI was 12 days.5,10 There was a statistically significant difference between the length of temporary stabilization of open fractures due to WI and NWI (p < 0.001).

There were 62.9 % (291 of 463) cases of closed fractures that received temporary stabilization, 2 (0.7%) of them were treated with external fixation, and 289 (99.3%) with skeletal traction. The median time from the application of the temporary stabilization device to the internal fixation procedure was 4.5 days3,8 for WI and 6 days5,8 for NWI (p = 0.130); no significant difference was observed between the length of temporary stabilization of closed fractures due to WI and NWI.

Implants removal rate

Of the 2801 IFPs, 539 (19.2%) implants were removed, in 11.9% of cases (64 of 539) the removal was unplanned because of infection, with an overall FRI rate of 2.3% (64 of 2801). NWI required removal of the implant for FRI in 2.0% of cases (51 of 2500). In contrast, WI needed removal of the implant for FRI in 4.4% of cases (13 of 301) (p = 0.010).

The K-wires comprised 31.3% (20 of 64) of implants removed for FRIs, Elastic Stable Intramedullary Nails (ESIN) with 21.9% (14 of 64), SIGN® nail interlocking screws with 21.9% (14 of 64), SIGN® nails with 17.1% (11 of 64), and screws with 7.8% (5 of 64). The median time from the first internal fixation procedure to the unplanned removal of these infected implants was 110 (49, 184) days.

The rate of removal of implants for non-infected causes comprised 88.1% (475 of 539) of all implants removed. K-wires comprised 32.0% (152 of 475), ESIN with 22.1% (105 of 475), SIGN® nail interlocking screws with 19.8% (94 of 475), SIGN® nails with 17.1% (81 of 475), and screws with 9.1% (43 of 475). The median time for the removal of these implants was 419 (123, 552) days.

Discussion

This study provides an analysis of IFPs performed at the Surgical Centre for War Victims-Lashkar-Gah, Afghanistan, from 2020 to 2023, covering 2 years before and 2 years after the cessation of active conflict. Our findings align with those of previous studies from war-stricken regions.11 revealing a higher prevalence of young male patients. However, following the war's conclusion, the proportion of female patients increased, likely due to the hospital's expanded admission criteria. For the same reason, the proportion of pediatric patients decreased.

There was an evident decrease in war-related injuries from 2020 to 2023 (p < 0.001), whereas the number of civilian traumas related to RTAs increased considerably. Afghanistan, as numerous low- and middle-income countries, suffers from high numbers of RTAs and has fatality rates more than double those in high-income countries.12 The increased mobility caused by improved security conditions after the war, together with unsafe road infrastructure, inadequate law enforcement, and lack of compliance with the United Nations Vehicle Safety Regulations,13 further contribute to increased road traffic injuries and fatalities.

The overall volume of IFPs increased more than 300% from 2020 to 2023, with the most rapid growth occurring after hostilities ceased in August 2021. This surge is attributable to the relatively lower risk of post-surgical infection in fractures caused by civilian trauma, making them more suitable for internal fixation.

War-related fractures often involve significant contamination, soft tissue damage, and necrosis, leading to higher infection rates, reported in the literature at 27% to 40%.7,14 Consequently, internal fixation for such injuries is controversial, with guidelines such as those from the U.S. Army and another published article recommending against its use.15,16 Nonetheless, multiple studies have been conducted on the treatment of war-related fractures through internal fixation devices, which have demonstrated that with adequate soft tissue coverage and infection control, internal fixation represents a feasible treatment option.7,10

The Emergency NGO guidelines, based on more than 20 years of experience in treating WI patients, recommend waiting approximately 4 weeks post-debridement before internal fixation, and proper soft tissue coverage is considered essential, which explains the increased time spent on skeletal traction or external fixation for WI patients before undergoing definitive internal fixation. Infection remains the most significant complication associated with internal fixation.17 Recommendations for FRI include aggressive debridement, implant retention or replacement in cases of non-union, or hardware removal if sufficient callus formation is present.18, 19, 20, 21

FRI removal ranged from 2.5% to 5.6% in a series of patients with open reduction and internal fixation following upper and lower extremity fractures22 comparable to the findings of the present study, we obtained an FRI rate of 4.3% in patients with WI. The higher removal rate of implants in WI patients in comparison with NWI patients is consistent with the higher risk of infection in cases of war-related open wounds.

High resources are not fundamental in war surgery and rely more on life-saving and limb-saving skills, speed, and the presence of standardized hospital pathways,9 while civilian trauma care often requires advanced implants and specialized training, which are frequently lacking in humanitarian settings.8,23 An increasing number of civilian trauma-related fractures were treated with K-wires during the study period. These fixation devices are applicable to nearly every bone in most pediatric fractures and are comparably cheaper, which makes K-wires suitable for off-label use, especially in NGO hospitals with limited resources. The use of nonconventional implants in this study was evident in cases of forearm fractures in adult patients. In many cases, K-wires represent the only alternative to fractures otherwise left untreated, as described in a case series of patients treated in an Emergency hospital with off-label implants in Sierra Leone.24

The lack of specific training also explains the use of the implants. For example, despite the availability of non-locking plates, the use of these implants remains low owing to the lack of specific knowledge and experience of national staff. In response to the need for updated implants, a Dynamic Hip System for hip fractures was introduced at the Surgical Centre for War Victims-Lashkar-Gah at the end of 2023. This introduction followed a comprehensive training program conducted by international surgeons and nurses, allowing local staff to become autonomous in carrying out the procedure. This successful experience indicates the importance of adopting a stepwise approach to introduce new orthopaedic hardware.

The change in volume of orthopedic activities after the end of the active conflict period can be applied to many contexts and should be anticipated properly by NGOs working in those countries and willing to fill this need.

This can be done by identifying key fixation implants that can be introduced in the projects, starting with the cheapest and simplest ones (such as K-wires and ESINs) and progressing towards more complicated fixation systems, with an equal progression and expansion of fractures' complexity in the admission criteria of the hospital. This gradual evolution of the local capacity in terms of availability of implants will allow not only to develop local competencies in a more structured way, but also to analyze the context in order to identify the increase in volume and plan the supply of materials more accurately, otherwise the risk could be of introducing new fixation systems and immediately run out of supplies due to the large number of patients.

The introduction of IFPs will also require strict minimum requirements in terms of infection prevention and control systems, especially in a hospital that does not have previous experience in internal fixation, and these preventive measures will need to consider not only the intraoperative practices but all the preoperative and postoperative phases, as well as the sterilization protocols used in the project.

All of this needs to be accompanied in each step, but a careful training program and monitoring of outcomes, which would provide the necessary technical support for the local staff. This should be organized by the NGOs providing the materials, deploying orthopedic surgeons with previous experience in low- and middle-income countries. The structure of the training program itself should not be taken for granted, since many context and culture-specific aspects need to be considered, and the quality of the training staff should be properly assessed to maximize the efficacy of the program.

Some organizations, such as SIGN® fracture international, produce implants specifically designed for low- and middle-income countries, offering the training as well needed for their use. Local markets should also be investigated for the availability of implants, but accurate quality assessments need to be made, considering the frequent lack of an appropriate system of certification of quality.

Although the end of an active conflict usually entails a relaxation of import restrictions, a great focus should be placed on reusability, which is currently not applicable to modern internal fixation devices but can be considered for many other items involved in the surgical procedure (such as surgical drapes), with an effort to reduce the costs as much as possible. The aim of this is to already include in the project's planning an exit strategy, which will be far more complex if the costs for performing surgical procedures have been inflated by the introduction of new and expensive internal fixation systems, but will be needed if the alternative is to create a system that is dependent on the continuous presence of an NGO.

This study had several limitations. As this is a retrospective study, some data may be missing, our study is prone to selection bias, and the accuracy of some recorded information may be low. The lack of follow-up prevented the authors from providing clear information on the long-term outcomes of the patients included in the study, especially considering that for orthopaedic surgery procedures, the level of postoperative rehabilitation is a crucial factor in evaluating the efficacy of the treatment. One of the main causes of this limitation is that many patients treated at the hospital are from distant provinces, and they do not have the financial means or the capacity to travel to the hospital for follow-up examinations.

In conclusion, the end of the Afghanistan conflict brought significant changes to orthopaedic surgery practices. A notable trend observed was the sharp decline in war-related injuries post-2021, paralleled by a significant increase in civilian trauma cases, particularly RTAs, necessitating increased IFPs. The pragmatic use of off-label implants such as K-wires reflects the current need for cost-effective solutions in resource-limited settings. We obtained an overall FRI rate of 2.3% (64 of 2801) with significant differences according to the type of injury; WI required removal of the implant for FRI in 4.3% (13 of 301); in contrast, NWI had fewer cases of FRI (2.0%, 51 of 2500) (p = 0.010).

While challenges in treating war-related fractures are still present, this study underscores the need for tailored training and specific implant availability to meet the evolving healthcare demands of the Afghan population. Continued adaptation, especially for NGO-managed health facilities, is crucial for sustainable and fair orthopaedic care in post-conflict regions.
