RBFOX3 is associated with neuronal-like phenotypic transition in recurrent glioblastoma
Authors: Xiaomeng Zhang, Jingru Sui, Haoyuan Sun, Ruichao Chai, Zhaoqi Liu, Jiguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging evidence links neuronal-like phenotypic transition to glioblastoma (GB) recurrence and therapy resistance, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. We hypothesized that dysregulated alternative splicing leads to a neuronal-like state and chemoresistance at tumor recurrence.MethodsWe reanalysed longitudinal bulk RNA-seq data from paired primary and recurrent GBs to quantify dynamic alternative splicing during tumor evolution. By integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, we characterized shifts in cellular composition and spatial interactions linked to these splicing changes. We then prioritized splicing regulators and validated their effects on tumor cells using in vitro cell lines and co-culture assays.ResultsA subset of recurrent GBs exhibits neuronal programs and chemoresistance during tumor evolution, accompanied by pronounced differential splicing patterns. Single-cell analyses link these splicing changes to the co-enrichment of neurons and a neuronal-like tumor subpopulation at recurrence. Spatial transcriptomics further indicates close synaptic interactions between this tumor state and adjacent neurons in tumor-infiltrative regions. Through integrative computational ranking and experimental validation, we find that the neuron-specific splicing factor RBFOX3 promotes the neuronal-like GB tumor state by dysregulating splicing. In tumor-neuron co-culture systems, RBFOX3 overexpression is linked to the upregulation of presynaptic markers.ConclusionsDysregulated alternative splicing mediated by RBFOX3 is associated with a neuronal-like tumor state and might contribute to chemoresistance at GB relapse, highlighting the potential role of splicing regulation in GB evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16597-9.

Background

Diffuse glioma is the most prevalent malignant brain tumor in adults, in which isocitrate dehydrogenase wild-type glioblastoma (GB) represents the most aggressive subtype [1]. Although temozolomide (TMZ) has been used as a standard adjuvant chemotherapy for GB and modestly prolongs overall survival, most patients show intrinsic and/or acquired resistance to this treatment and suffer tumor relapse [2]. The promoter methylation status of O6-methylguanine-DNA-methyltransferase (MGMT) is currently the most widely used biomarker for predicting TMZ response [3], but MGMT methylation alone is not sufficient to explain all forms of drug resistance in treatment-refractory patients [4].

To elucidate GB evolution under therapy, multiple studies have generated longitudinal genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic datasets, enabling comprehensive characterization of molecular and phenotypic changes in response to treatment [5–10]. Early work documented frequent transitions among GB molecular subtypes during disease progression, most notably from proneural to a mesenchymal-like state, which was shown to contribute to increased resistance to standard therapies [7, 11]. More recently, an increased prevalence of neural-like subtypes upon GB recurrence has been observed [10], linking neuronal phenotypic transition to tumor chemoresistance [8, 12]. Despite these observations, the mechanisms driving subtype transition in GB remain incompletely defined, and longitudinal analyses specifically investigating the contribution of splicing dynamics to treatment resistance are scarce.

Alternative splicing has emerged as a critical determinant of therapeutic response independent from gene expression, and increasing evidence links drug resistance to aberrant splicing in cancer [13]. For example, in melanoma, expression of splicing isoforms of BRAF (V600E) lacking the RAS-binding domain confers resistance to RAF inhibitors [14]. Similarly, in prostate cancer, expression of the androgen receptor isoform encoded by splice variant 7, which lacks the ligand-binding domain, is associated with resistance to enzalutamide and abiraterone [15]. In GB, however, comprehensive assessments of splicing dysregulation and its relationship to drug resistance remain limited. One recent study compared primary and recurrent GB samples and reported that recurrent-specific spliced isoforms enhance tumor invasiveness [16]. Yet, the primary and recurrent samples used in this study were limited and unmatched, hindering the discovery of patient-specific findings during cancer evolution. The accumulation of primary-recurrent paired transcriptomic datasets [6] provides an opportunity to resolve the role of splicing alterations at a greater scale and higher resolution.

Here, we analyzed transcriptomic data from 96 matched primary-recurrent GB pairs treated with standard therapy and focused on splicing changes under GB evolution. Our analysis revealed a distinct GB subgroup characterized by splicing alterations and a phenotypic shift from mesenchymal to neuronal-like states at recurrence. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed neuron-to-neuron-like GB interactions and prioritized RBFOX3 as a potential driver of GB neuronal transition. In vitro experiments demonstrated that upregulation of RBFOX3 in GB cells enhances overall synaptic connectivity signaling in tumor-neuron co-culture systems. These findings implicate alternative splicing in driving neuronal transformation associated with tumor relapse and TMZ resistance, providing mechanistic insight into phenotypic plasticity and chemoresistance during GB evolution under treatment.

Methods

Bulk RNA-seq processing and differential splicing detection

Paired primary and recurrent GBs’ RNA-seq FASTQ files from CELLO2 were aligned to hg19 using STAR [17] (v2.4.2). BAM files were analysed with rMATS [18] (v4.1.1) to catalog alternative splicing events. We quantified percent spliced in (PSI) values for each tumor by integrating rMATS-identified events with splice junction counts from STAR. For each event, PSI was computed as the number of junction reads supporting the targeted isoform divided by the total junction reads for that event. We collected four event types—skipped exon (SE), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), and alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS)—and retained events with a minimum total junction count > 10 per sample. Differential splicing between paired primary and recurrent GBs was assessed with paired two-sided Wilcoxon tests, using |ΔPSI| > 0.05 and BH-adjusted P-value < 0.05 as thresholds.

C1 GB identification

Significantly differential events (per above) were assembled into a PSI matrix across all bulk-level samples. Hierarchical clustering partitioned the samples into two groups with distinct splicing patterns, Cluster 1 (C1) and Cluster 2 (C2). Patients with C1 recurrences were labelled C1. We further stratified patients by the Neural (NEU) subtype status at recurrence, yielding three groups (C1.NEU+, C2.NEU+, and C2.NEU−) to represent evolutionary changes in splicing and neuronal phenotype.

NEU GB subtype determination

For bulk-level samples, transcriptional subtypes (including NEU subtype) [19] were assigned using the function gsva from the GSVA (v1.50.0) package to compute single-sample gene-set enrichment scores, with the highest score defining the subtype. The snRNA-seq data from Wang et al. [7] were aggregated into pseudo-bulk profiles for each sample to enable subtype calling.

Differential expression analysis and clustering

Differential expression analysis between primary and recurrent tumors was performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0). Genes with an adjusted p-value < 1e-03 and |log2FC| > 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The expression matrix of these significant DEGs was then subjected to hierarchical clustering to delineate the tumor subgroups that contributed most substantially to the expression alterations observed upon recurrence.

Clinical and genomic alteration analysis

Clinical and genomic data for the paired GB samples were also sourced from the CELLO2 dataset [6]. Inter-group comparisons were performed primarily for C1.NEU+ vs. C2.NEU+ and C1.NEU+ vs. C2.NEU− using Fisher’s exact test (categorical variables) and t-tests (continuous variables). For the subset of cases with available sequencing data, CELLO2 identified genomic alterations strongly associated with GB pathogenesis, which were utilized to identify significant differences in the genomic profiles of primary versus recurrent tumors across our three patient groups.

Functional characterization and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Functional enrichment of differentially spliced genes and differential expressed genes during GB evolution was performed with clusterProfiler (v4.17.0) [20] using Gene Ontology (GO) biological process terms. GSEA results were obtained using genes ranked by expression fold change and GO or KEGG pathway collections to assess enrichment.

C1/C2 classification of single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) samples

For samples profiled by snRNA-seq, pseudo-bulk expression profiles were generated for each individual sample by aggregating raw read counts across all cells derived from the same specimen. The significantly upregulated genes identified via differential expression analysis were subsequently applied to distinguish C1 from C2 tumors based on these pseudo-bulk snRNA-seq profiles. In the bulk transcriptomic cohort, single-sample gene set enrichment scores were computed using the above upregulated gene set across all samples. A score threshold of 600 was determined to optimally separate C1 and C2 subgroups. We therefore adopted 600 as the classification cut-off, applied the same enrichment scoring to snRNA-seq-derived pseudo-bulk profiles, and identified 5 samples with scores exceeding this threshold, which were consequently classified as the C1 subtype.

Splicing quantification for snRNA-seq samples

For each snRNA-seq sample, reads from all cells were pooled to generate sample-level FASTQs, which were then processed with the same “STAR + rMATS” pipeline to quantify junction reads and compute PSI for adequately covered events. Samples with sufficient event detection were hierarchically clustered into two PSI-based groups.

snRNA-seq processing and tumor cell identification

snRNA-seq data were processed with Seurat (v5.2.1) [21]. After quality control, 157,803 cells from 58 GB samples were retained for downstream analysis. We integrated cells from each sample using Seurat’s standard workflow with reciprocal PCA, visualized them in a UMAP plot, and classified them into 7 major cell types using unsupervised clustering and marker gene expression. Copy-number variations (CNVs) were inferred using inferCNV (v1.18.1) [22] with myeloid cells, neurons, and oligodendrocytes as references. Tumor cells were identified by characteristic CNV profiles on chromosomes 7 and 10. To annotate subclusters of tumor cells, we first identified cluster-specific upregulated marker genes via differential expression analysis. These markers were then compared with previously defined transcriptional signatures of glioblastoma (GB) tumor cell states reported in published literature [23]. Tumor subclusters recapitulating the expression patterns of known cell states were annotated using the established nomenclature. For subclusters without a matched reference state, we assigned descriptive names based on their representative cell characteristics and biological features.

Cell-type-specific splicing and principal component analysis (PCA) results of splicing programs

To compare splicing features between C1 tumors and individual cell types, we first aggregated the snRNA-seq reads for each cell type to create cell-type-specific pseudo-bulk profiles. For the differentially spliced events identified in bulk RNA-seq, we then calculated PSI values within each pseudo-bulk profile to quantify cell-type-specific splicing patterns. Finally, these PSI values from bulk and pseudo-bulk data were processed together via PCA. We assessed the splicing similarity by comparing the principal component coordinates of bulk C1/C2 samples with those of the pseudo-bulk profiles, thereby identifying the cell type whose splicing pattern most closely resembled that of C1 tumors.

Cell-cell communication inference

CellChat (v2.1.1) [24] analysed snRNA-seq data to infer signalling. Significantly supported pathways (P-value < 0.05) were compared between primary/recurrent samples, visualized as heatmaps.

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) analysis

ST data [25] were used to estimate spot-level cell-type compositions using RCTD [26], with our processed snRNA-seq as the reference.

Spatial co-occurrence was assessed via Pearson correlation of cell-type proportions. For each cell-cell pair, null distributions were generated by simulating 1,000 ST datasets (preserving spot/cell counts and randomizing spatial assignment), and z-tests were used to compare observed vs. simulated correlations.

Master regulator analysis of splicing alterations in C1

To prioritize the master regulators of C1-specific differential splicing, we adapted the core framework of the published MRAS method [27] and extended it by incorporating single-cell regulatory signatures. Cell culture and gene overexpression

U-87 MG human glioma cells (Abiowell) were cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; C04001050 × 10; Biological Industries, Palestine), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin at 37 °C, 5% CO2. RBFOX3 was transiently or stably overexpressed using pHG‑CMV‑RBFOX3‑Flag (HonorGene). Cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection for validation assays.

RNA extraction and PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (15596018; Invitrogen, USA). Concentrations were measured with NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo). cDNA was synthesized using the All‑in‑one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (E047-01 A; Novoprotein, China). Subsequently, real-time quantitative PCR (RT‑qPCR) was performed with SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (E096-01 A; Novoprotein, China). PCR experiments were performed using Premix Taq DNA Polymerase (RR902A; Takara, Japan). Primer sequences of PCR and qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Drug sensitivity assays

TMZ sensitivity was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‑8 (Lablead). Briefly, 10,000 cells per well in 96‑well plates received 10 µL of drug at indicated concentrations in 90 µL medium and were incubated for 48 h. After adding 10 µL CCK‑8 solution per well, plates were incubated for 1–4 h. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured, and viability was calculated relative to untreated controls. Results represent the mean of at least three independent experiments.

Neuron-glioma co-culture and synaptic marker staining

Primary embryonic neural stem cells were isolated from E14.5 C57 mice (SPF Biotechnology). For synaptic puncta assays, neurons were plated on poly‑L‑lysine and laminin (5 µg/mL) coated coverslips at 20,000 cells per well (24‑well plates) and maintained in Neurobasal medium (Gibco) supplemented with B27, FGF‑basic, and EGF (Peprotech). Medium was half‑changed on days in vitro (DIV) 1 and 3. On DIV 5, 10,000 U‑87 MG cells expressing RBFOX3 or a control vector were added. After 72 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min, 4 °C), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‑100 in PBS (5 min), and blocked with 3% normal serum in PBS (1 h, room temperature). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C: rabbit anti‑synapsin‑1 (SYN1, 1:200, Abcam) and chicken anti‑MAP2 (1:500, Abcam). After three PBS washes, secondary antibodies (1:500, Abcam; Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti‑chicken IgG and Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti‑rabbit IgG) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Coverslips were washed, mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories), and imaged.

Results

Differential splicing reveals a post-recurrence GB subgroup with neuron-like transcriptomic reprogramming

To investigate the splicing dynamics during GB evolution, we started with a longitudinal bulk-level RNA-seq dataset from the CELLO2 website [6], including matched primary and recurrent tumors from 96 GB patients treated with TMZ (Fig. 1a). We quantified and compared changes in four types of alternative splicing events between primary and recurrent tumors using the PSI metric, which reflects the relative inclusion level of a spliced isoform based on junction reads (Fig. 1b;; Methods). Differential splicing analysis revealed 154 differentially spliced events (DSEs) upon recurrence (|△PSI| > 0.05 and adjusted. P-value < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1), including events previously linked to tumor invasiveness and proliferation, such as increased inclusion of MAP4K4 exon 17 (Supplementary Fig. 1c-1d) [16] and ITGA6 exon 25 (Supplementary Fig. 1e-1f) [29, 30]. The genes harboring the DSEs were primarily enriched in neuron-associated processes, including neuronal differentiation, synaptic signaling, and cellular morphogenesis (Fig. 1c). Notably, these changes on neuron-related genes confer neuronal-like isoforms in recurrent tumors: for example, increased inclusion of CD47 exons 9–10, corresponding to an isoform predominantly expressed in the nervous system (Fig. 1d and e) [31, 32], and elevated inclusion of MAP2 exon 9, aligning with adult brain-predominant isoforms that stabilize mature neuronal architecture [33, 34] (Supplementary Fig. 1a-1b).

Notably, analysis revealed that these alterations were concentrated in a subset of GB samples (Fig. 1d and e; Supplementary Fig. 1a-1b). Aggregating PSI values across all samples for the above DSEs identified two splicing-defined clusters: C1 and C2 (Methods; silhouette score = 0.50; Supplementary Fig. 1i). The C1 cluster—distinguished by its unique splicing profile from C2—was enriched for recurrent tumors (15/16 samples) (Fig. 1f), showing that major splicing changes after recurrence are not universal but are instead subgroup-specific.

To contextualize these splicing clusters with gene expression profiles, we classified all samples using the established gene expression-based GB subtypes [19] (Methods) and found that all C1 tumors were the Neural (NEU) subtype (Fig. 1f), characterized by elevated expression of neuron markers. The global expression profile of C1 is also distinct from C2 tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1g; Methods) with marked upregulation of neuronal-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1h). This indicates that differential splicing programs in C1 tumors drive a unique expression signature, even when compared with C2 tumors classified as the same NEU subtype.

Given these special C1 patterns emerging after recurrence, we stratified 96 GB patients by both splicing cluster (C1 or C2) and NEU subtype status (NEU+ or NEU−) of their recurrent tumors, yielding three groups: C1.NEU+ (n = 15), C2.NEU+ (n = 11), and C2.NEU− (n = 70). Although both NEU+ groups gained neural-like expression-based phenotypes at recurrence, C1. NEU+ patients consistently underwent subtype switching at recurrence—most notably from Mesenchymal (MES) to NEU subtype—concurrent with splicing reprogramming, whereas C2.NEU+ patients did not (Fig. 1g). The correlation between splicing alterations and the unique subtype transition in C1.NEU+ suggests that splicing reprogramming may exert a distinct influence on GB’s neuronal-like evolution.

C1.NEU+ patients acquire neuronal and TMZ-resistance features after tumor recurrence

In addition to subtype changes, we also compared the clinical features of GB patients across the three groups (C1.NEU+, C2.NEU+, and C2.NEU−) and found no significant differences in data source, tumor stage, age, or other variables (Supplementary Fig. 2a-2b; Methods). We next compared genomic alterations across the three groups (Supplementary Fig. 2c; Methods) and found that PTEN deletion in primary tumors was present in ~ 50% of patients of the two C2 groups but entirely absent in those from C1 group (p-value < 1e-2, Fig. 2a). PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene that negatively regulates the phosphatidylinositol 3-OH kinase pathway by dephosphorylating PIP3, thereby restraining mitogenic signaling [35]. PTEN deletion has been reported to mechanistically associate with tumor proliferation, metastasis, and therapy resistance in brain tumors [36, 37]. Based on our results, primary tumors without PTEN deletion may undergo splicing-related neuronal transition and recur as C1.

We next evaluated pathway-level expression changes between primary and recurrent tumors within each of the three patient groups using gene-set enrichment analysis (Methods). Pathways were significantly up- and downregulated upon recurrence in the C1.NEU+ group was selected and compared with the corresponding enrichment results from the other two groups (Fig. 2b). Besides the activation of neuron-related pathways (e.g., neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction), C1.NEU+ patients showed the strongest suppression of the mesenchymal-related (e.g., epithelial-mesenchymal transition), immune function (e.g., antigen processing and presentation) and cell proliferation (e.g., cell cycle) pathways. We further examined phenotypic evolutionary features by comparing marker-based enrichment scores between primary and recurrent tumors: neural features increased in both NEU+ groups, whereas mesenchymal features decreased exclusively in C1 patients (Fig. 2c), consistent with the previously observed MES-to-NEU subtype transformation in C1 (Fig. 1g).

Among the pathways markedly altered in C1 patients, the deficiency of “mismatch repair” function has been linked to TMZ resistance in GBs (Fig. 2b) [38, 39]. Motivated by this potential connection between splicing reprogramming and TMZ response, we examined the differential expression of TMZ-response marker genes in each of three groups [8, 40] and found that C1 patients displayed significantly increased expression of TMZ-resistant markers (e.g., MGMT) and decreased expression of TMZ-sensitive markers (e.g., MLH1, MSH6) after recurrence (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Fig. 2d). Across three groups, recurrent tumors of C1 also demonstrated the highest level of TMZ-resistant markers (Supplementary Fig. 2e).

While prior studies have emphasized mesenchymal transformation during GB progression [11, 41], our findings suggest that mesenchymal primary GBs can transition to a more neural-like, TMZ-resistant state. This phenotype switch is closely associated with the C1-specific splicing program, suggesting a distinct mechanism of neuronal recurrence and therapeutic resistance potentially driven by alternative splicing.

Single-cell analyses demonstrate that neuron-like splicing programs exist in a substate of GB tumor cells

A limitation of the bulk-level analyses above is that they cannot pinpoint the cell types driving these phenotypic transitions. To elucidate the cellular and molecular basis of splicing changes and subtype shifts in C1 GBs, we next reanalyzed an snRNA-seq dataset from matched primary and recurrent tumors of 29 patients [7]. It is noted that read coverage in this dataset does not support robust quantification of most splicing events; we therefore use gene-expression features to distinguish C1 from C2. Specifically, we derived a classification threshold for the C1 upregulated gene signature from the bulk transcriptomic cohort. We then generated pseudo-bulk expression matrices from snRNA-seq data by summing read counts per sample and applied the same enrichment-scoring pipeline and threshold to assign snRNA-seq tumors to C1 or C2 subgroups (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3a; Methods). We validated these assignments by performing splicing analysis of the snRNA-seq samples and observed that C1 tumors showed splicing patterns distinct from those of C2, consistent with the bulk-level findings (Supplementary Fig. 3b; Methods). By subtype classification, all C1 tumors were identified as NEU subtype, in agreement with bulk-level results (Fig. 3b). Next, to compare patient-level phenotypic changes upon recurrence, snRNA-seq patients were subsequently stratified by recurrent tumor into three groups: C1.NEU+ (n = 5), C2.NEU+ (n = 6) and C2.NEU− (n = 18)—mirroring the classification on the bulk-level patients. And this single-cell analysis consolidated our key findings from bulk-level data: C1 patients underwent a mesenchymal-to-neural transition at recurrence (Fig. 3c), with increased neural and reduced mesenchymal marker expression (Supplementary Fig. 3c), and elevated markers associated with TMZ resistance (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

We next examined the cellular compositions underlying these phenotypic shifts in C1. After cell filtering, integration, unsupervised clustering, and marker-based annotation (Supplementary Fig. 4a-4d; Methods), cells from all primary and recurrent tumors were merged and classified into 6 non-malignant cell types and 1 tumor type (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. 4e). Tumor cell cluster, which was confirmed by mutation status of chromosomes 7 and 10 copy number alterations (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 4d, 5b; Methods), was further stratified into 7 tumor substates based on their expression patterns (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table 2 Supplementary Fig. 5a). Analysis of cellular composition of the three subgroups revealed that C1 group exhibited a consistent increase in neuron cells upon recurrence (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 6a), as well as in neural progenitor cell (NPC)-like GB (Tumor. NPC) cells (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 6b), while the C2 group showed neither of these changes. Tumor. NPC cells, defined by upregulation of NPC markers like SOX4 and SOX11 (Supplementary Fig. 5a) [42], showed strong activation of synaptic signaling (e.g., neurotransmitter secretion) and splicing-regulatory (e.g., alternative splicing via spliceosome) pathways (Fig. 3h). Conversely, mesenchymal-like GB (Tumor. MES) cells declined to the lowest levels in the C1 group compared to two C2 groups (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 6b).

To test whether the two evaluated cellular components, neurons and “Tumor. NPC” cells, account for the C1-specific splicing alterations detected in bulk-level data, we aggregated snRNA-seq reads by non-malignant cell type and tumor subtype to derive cell-type-specific splicing profiles (Methods). PCA was applied to compare C1/C2 splicing profiles from bulk RNA-seq data and cell-type-specific splicing profiles from snRNA-seq data. The sample distribution showed that neurons and Tumor. NPC cells most closely matched the C1 splicing pattern (Fig. 3i). For example, in these two cell populations, inclusion of CD47 exons 9–10 and ITGA6 exon 25 (Fig. 3j; Supplementary Fig. 5c) matched the bulk C1 profiles and differed from other tumor cells.

Together, single-cell analyses suggest that the neuronal transition in C1 GBs is driven by the expansion of neurons in the microenvironment and a subset of neuron-like tumor cells marked by C1-specific splicing features acquired during tumor evolution. By contrast, the neural-like features observed in C2.NEU+ recurrent tumors were largely explained by an increased proportion of oligodendrocytes after recurrence (Fig. 3f).

Spatial transcriptome analyses reveal spatial proximity between neurons and NPC-like tumor cells in GB

Single-cell data revealed the co-enrichment of neurons and Tumor. NPC cells (Fig. 3f and g), as well as the activation of neuronal signaling pathways (e.g., neurotransmitter secretion) in the Tumor. NPC cells (Fig. 3h), suggesting potential synaptic connections generated between Tumor. NPC and adjacent neuron cells in C1 after GB recurrence. To examine how intercellular interactions evolve during progression to the C1 state, we applied CellChat to the snRNA-seq dataset [24] to infer changes in ligand-receptor signalling between matched primary and recurrent tumors from C1 patients. The results indicated that the majority of upregulated signalling pathways upon recurrence were enriched between neurons and Tumor. NPC cells (Fig. 4a; Methods). Notably, synaptic signal transmission via glutamate and GABA neurotransmitters exhibited the most pronounced increase following recurrence (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 7a-7b).

Previous studies have demonstrated that neuron-like glioma cells can form synaptic connections with neurons [43–45], which promotes tumor invasiveness, accelerates cancer progression, and contributes to therapy resistance of GBs [8]. Besides inferring signalling communications from single-cell data, we further examined potential intracellular connections based on spatial proximity using a public spatial transcriptomics (ST) dataset comprising 15 GB samples [25]. Using the annotated snRNA-seq cell signature as a reference, we estimated spot-level cell proportions in ST samples using RCTD [26]. Incorporating the ST dataset’s pathological annotations, we found that both Tumor. NPC cells and neurons preferentially localized in infiltrative tumor regions, showing similar spatial distributions (Fig. 4c-e; Methods).

We then tested the significance of cell-cell spatial co-localization at the spot level using correlation tests on spot-level proportions for each cell type pair. The resulting correlation coefficients were benchmarked against a null distribution derived from thousands of simulated ST samples. Neurons and Tumor. NPC cells exhibited the highest degree of co-localization among all cell-cell pairs across ST samples (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 7c; Methods), indicating the spatial proximity necessary to support synaptic signal transmission. This pattern of neuron-GB cell interaction aligns with prior observations in independent ST datasets [46, 47].

Neuron-specific splicing factors regulate the splicing changes observed in C1.NEU+ GBs

Having delineated the principal cellular components and their interactions in C1 GBs, we next sought to identify regulators of the neuronal splicing program correlated with these evolutionary dynamics. Splicing factors (SFs) are key trans-acting regulators of pre-mRNA splicing [48], and their dysregulated expression drives cancer-associated splicing abnormalities [49, 50]. Therefore, based on the structure of the published method MRAS [27], we finetuned a computational framework to quantify each SF’s association with the C1 splicing signature.

The regulatory targets for each SF were inferred from a correlation test between the PSI values of splicing events and SF expression levels in C1 tumors (Fig. 5a; Methods; Supplementary Table 3). We then quantified each SF’s regulatory strength in three ways: (1) an odds ratio capturing the overlap between its predicted targets and the differentially spliced events at C1 recurrence; (2) a normalized enrichment score assessing enrichment of its targets within a pre-ranked list of all detected splicing events ordered by absolute PSI change upon C1 recurrence (Supplementary Fig. 8a); and (3) expression fold changes of the SF, derived from bulk data (C1 recurrent vs. primary) and single-cell data (Tumor. NPC vs. other tumor subtypes) (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 8b). Finally, SFs were then ranked by a composite score combining the above components. This ranking revealed the top five SFs scoring substantially higher than the rest, and RBFOX3 emerging as the leading candidate (Fig. 5b).

The five highest-scoring SFs were all upregulated in neurons and Tumor. NPC cells (Fig. 5c), consistent with previous findings of the consistent C1-specific splicing program in these two cell types (Fig. 3i). To validate their regulatory roles on differential splicing, we performed both knockdown and overexpression experiments on the top-ranked SF, RBFOX3, in the GB cell line. Splicing isoform abundances were quantified by RT-PCR for selected DSEs that have annotated isoforms with distinct biological functions (Supplementary Fig. 8c-8d, 9a-9c; Methods and Supplementary Methods). Compared with control groups, RBFOX3 perturbation induced some of DSEs in GB, like differential inclusion of CD47 exons 9–10 and ITGA6 exon 25 (Fig. 5e and f; Supplementary Fig. 9c), recapitulating these splicing changes observed in C1 tumors (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Fig. 1f) as well as Tumor. NPC cells (Fig. 3j; Supplementary Fig. 5c).

RBFOX3 overexpression drives the tumor’s neuronal transition and enhances synaptic activity in the GB-neuron co-culture system

By integrating the above results, we proposed a splicing-driven neuronal transition model in GB. During recurrence, neuron-specific SFs, especially RBFOX3, are upregulated in tumor cells, leading to splicing alterations that shift GB cells toward a neuron-like state. These neuron-like GB cells preferentially interact with surrounding neurons, thereby strengthening synaptic cell-cell interactions (Fig. 5d).

To test this model, we focused on the top candidate, RBFOX3, and used an in vitro co-culture system to experimentally assess its effects on neuronal transition in GB cells and synaptogenesis with neighboring neurons. We firstly evaluated the neuronal transition levels between GB cells with/without RBFOX3 overexpression based on the neuron marker (e.g., SNAP25) and Tumor. NPC marker (e.g., SOX11) (Supplementary Fig. 8e) through RT-qPCR, and the results across two tumor groups indicated significant upregulation of these markers following RBFOX3 overexpression (Fig. 5g and h). We then performed enrichment analysis of the corresponding RNA-seq data for these two groups of tumor cells and found higher overall expression levels of Tumor. NPC markers in the overexpression group (Supplementary Fig. 8g; Supplementary Methods). In addition, increasing NPC-like features accompanied by increased resistance to TMZ in drug sensitivity assays, consistent with observations in C1 tumors (Supplementary Fig. 8f).

GB cells with or without RBFOX3 overexpression were then co-cultured with mouse neurons, and synaptic activity in the co-culture system was evaluated by the presynaptic marker synapsin-1 (SYN1; red) (Fig. 6a; Methods). In the fluorescence images, neurons and GB cells were distinguished by MAP2 staining: cells with MAP2 expression (green) surrounding DAPI-stained nuclei (blue) were classified as neurons, whereas MAP2-negative cells were classified as GB tumor cells (Fig. 6b). Compared with NC groups, tumor cells in the RBFOX3-overexpression (RBFOX3-OE) groups were more preferentially clustered around neighboring neurons. Besides, RBFOX3-OE group showed significantly increased presynaptic features, evidenced by increased SYN1 expression (Fig. 6c) and a greater number of SYN1 puncta in tumor-neuron co-cultures (Fig. 6d). Under identical conditions, RBFOX3-OE tumors also exhibited higher total cell counts (Fig. 6e), suggesting a microenvironment that may favor growth of both neurons and GB cells when RBFOX3 is overexpressed. These results implicate RBFOX3 as a key driver of neuronal reprogramming in GB and indicate that its activity promotes synapse formation in the presence of neighboring neurons within the tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

Investigating the mechanism of GB recurrence presents a significant challenge due to the high tumor heterogeneity, frequent diffuse infiltration, and complicated adaptive resistance. One major difficulty lies in deciphering tumor evolution, during which recurrent GBs frequently differ in the molecular and cellular characteristics from primary tumors [5, 51], complicating the development of effective therapies. Recent research is increasingly focusing on longitudinal profiling of GB to uncover the evolutionary dynamics of tumor recurrence. However, investigations into splicing alterations associated with this process remain limited. In this study, we defined a novel GB subtype, C1, based on its neuron-like splicing remodeling after recurrence, distinct from the conventional NEU subtype defined by neuron marker expression. C1 tumors are enriched among recurrent cases undergoing neuronal transition, particularly those arising from mesenchymal-like primaries. While neuronal reprogramming in recurrent GB has been reported in multiple studies [5, 9, 51], its clinical implications have only recently been highlighted by Kim et al., who showed that acquired neuronal features promote TMZ resistance and represent potential therapeutic targets [8]. Our findings support this link, demonstrating upregulation of resistance-associated markers in recurrent tumors with neuron-like splicing profiles. Furthermore, we identify specific SFs that regulate these neuronal splicing programs and overall phenotypic transitions in GBs, nominating them as candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for precision management.

At the cellular level, neuronal transformation during C1 evolution involves the acquisition of neuron-like features by GB cells and an increased presence of neurons in the tumor microenvironment. Neuron-like tumor cells, designated as Tumor. NPC, are relatively rare in most recurrent samples but show a consistent increasing trend in C1 GBs. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal close spatial proximity and synaptic-like interactions between neurons and Tumor. NPC cells. Previous studies have confirmed the existence of neuron-glioma synapses that promote tumor growth [43–45]. Our analyses suggest that such structures are more likely to form in about 16% of recurrent GBs with concurrent increases in both neuron cell and neuron-like tumor cell populations, and this propensity is modulated by neuron-specific SFs. This statement is consistent with the established role of a distinct splicing regulatory network in neural development and function. Within this network, members of the Rbfox SF family—especially RBFOX3, the most strongly associated regulator in our cohort—play central roles in promoting neuronal maturation by controlling neuron-specific splicing programs [52, 53].

Besides establishing synaptic connectivity, the mechanisms by which neuron-like transcriptional changes in C1 GBs drive tumor recurrence and TMZ resistance remain poorly understood. We hypothesize that this phenotypic shift may potentially enable GB cells to evade treatment by mimicking normal brain cells while retaining malignancy, which ultimately results in disease relapse. From a surgical perspective, the spatial overlap between tumor and normal brain cells, especially in the infiltrative region, increases the challenges for complete resection. In terms of chemotherapy, reduced cell cycle activity (Figs. 2b and 3h) in NPC-like GB cells may undermine the efficacy of TMZ, which targets rapidly dividing tumor cells to induce DNA damage and trigger cell death [54].

At this stage, we have not delineated or functionally validated the specific splicing events that causally alter tumor phenotypes. Nonetheless, several differentially spliced events in C1 tumors have prior support for their roles in neuronal state transitions. CD47, a potential therapeutic target in GB [55] and linked to TMZ efficiency [56], shows enrichment in C1 tumors with the isoform containing exons 9 and 10, which is also predominantly expressed in the brain and peripheral nervous system [31]. Although the functional distinctions among CD47 isoforms are incompletely understood, CD47 overexpression—particularly the isoform with two extra exons—promotes neuronal differentiation and induces neurite formation in tumor cells [57]. Similarly, C1-enriched ITGA6 variants with included exon 25 have been associated with enhanced proliferative capacity in colorectal cancer [29, 30] and reduced mesenchymal phenotypes in breast cancer cells [58], supporting their potential relevance to phenotype remodelling in GB.

Conclusion

In summary, our data-driven analysis identifies a subset of neuron-specific splicing factors, most notably RBFOX3, that are upregulated in approximately 16% of recurrent IDH-wildtype GBs. This subset is associated with a distinct neuronal transition, characterized by the acquisition of neuron-like transcriptional and splicing programs, increased synaptic interactions with the microenvironment, and heightened chemotherapy resistance. These findings highlight alternative splicing as a potential promoter of GB phenotypic evolution and suggest that neuron-like splicing signatures and RBFOX3 can serve as diagnostic markers and potential therapeutic targets for the neuronal-transition subtype.

While we have identified a distinct C1 splicing cluster in recurrent GB, this molecular program appears to operate in only a minority of patients. This highlights the substantial heterogeneity of GB recurrence and suggests that most tumors progress through alternative molecular mechanisms that remain to be elucidated. Besides, this study is limited by the absence of an independent bulk dataset to validate the C1 splicing subgroup and the lack of functional characterization of the identified differential splicing events. Future work will address these gaps by validating the C1 phenotype in other independent cohorts. Regarding RBFOX3-mediated splicing regulation, some DSEs did not exhibit obvious splicing changes upon RBFOX3 perturbation (Supplementary Fig. 9c), possibly due to suboptimal detection conditions, involvement of other regulatory factors, or the possibility that RBFOX3 drives the GB phenotype through only a subset of specific events. Another future direction is to establish the causal roles of specific RBFOX3-regulated splicing events (e.g., CD47, ITGA6) through targeted perturbation, thereby defining their functional impact on GB phenotypes and therapeutic potential.
