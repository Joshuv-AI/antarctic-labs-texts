Reshaping static aqueous Zn─Br battery chemistry without liquid Br2 for intrinsic safety and cycle durability
Authors: Junnan Hao, Yilong Zhu, Yan Jiao, Han Wu, Shao-Jian Zhang, Chunsheng Wang, Shi-Zhang Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging static aqueous Zn─Br batteries offer substantial cost and energy density advantages but suffer from toxic Br2 formation and polybromide shuttling. Here, we report a universal electrode engineering for advanced Zn─Br battery via incorporating layered materials into the cathode, reshaping the working mechanism from conversion to intercalation-conversion. Findings confirm that Br− intercalate-converts to Br−0.14 within unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4, effectively avoiding liquid Br2 formation and suppressing its toxicity and diffusion. Consequently, the self-discharge of cell remarkably decreases from 96.5 to 9.5% following 12 hours resting. Through controlling 48.7% Zn depth-of-discharge and industrial-level mass loading 40 mg(KBr) cm−2, the multilevel pouch cell with ampere hour–level capacity exhibits 750 cycles, outperforming aqueous counterparts at high mass loadings. This electrode engineering is applicable to other layered materials, underscoring its practical universality. These findings not only unlock the full potential of aqueous Zn─Br batteries but also enrich the chemistry landscape of aqueous battery family.

INTRODUCTION

Aqueous Zn-based batteries offer a safe, low-cost, and environmentally benign alternative to Li-ion batteries (1, 2). However, state-of-art Zn battery technology is not practical for commercialization due to the lack of reversible Zn anodes and high-performance cathodes (3, 4). For the Zn anodes, while substantial research is reported on boosting Zn stability via electrolyte and electrode modifications, the depth of discharge (DoD) of Zn remains extremely low (<20%), which leads to substantial material wastage and low battery energy density (5–7). For the cathodes, commonly used V-/Mn-based electrodes working with Zn2+/H+ cointercalation mechanism have been reported with good cycling life (8, 9); however, the H+ involvement brings several critical issues, including pH fluctuations, by-product accumulation, and poor reproducibility, particularly in large-format batteries with industry-level mass loading (10).

Recently, cathodes based on a conversion mechanism have received broad attention, e.g., halogen electrodes that offer improved reproducibility by avoiding H+ involvement (11). Among them, I2 cathodes show excellent life span and rate capacity; however, their high cost and sublimation properties challenge practical applications (12). In contrast, Br-based cathodes relying on Br−/Br2 conversion exhibit obvious advantages over I2 cathodes in cost ($0.009 for KBr versus $3.35 for KI per ampere hour, see table S1), discharge plateau (1.75 versus 1.2 V), and energy density (393 Wh kg−1 versus 194 Wh kg−1 Fig. 1A) (13, 14). Unlike I2, the conversion reaction of Br-based cathodes not only produces soluble polybromide (e.g., Br3−) during charging but also generates liquid Br2 at the end of charging (e.g., >1.9 V). The formation of liquid Br2 will raise serious toxicity concerns, while its diffusion will further deteriorate the performance of Zn─Br batteries. To date, only limited electrolyte/electrode strategies have been developed to mitigate polybromide shuttling; however, the liquid Br2 formation remains a unique and critical challenge that has yet to be systematically investigated. Consequently, the practical performance of Zn─Br batteries remains unsatisfactory, especially in terms of self-discharge rates and pouch cell life span under low current densities and high cathode mass loadings (15, 16).

Here, we present a universal electrode engineering strategy to reshape static aqueous Zn─Br batteries by incorporating layer materials into the cathode (e.g., unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4). Findings confirm that Br− insert-converts to Br−0.14 within g-C3N4 by forming Br−0.14-C3N4 through an intercalation-conversion working mechanism (Fig. 1B). Differing from traditional conversion reaction (fig. S1), this mechanism change fundamentally avoids the formation/diffusion of liquid Br2, as evidenced by in situ Raman. A modified ZnSO4 (M-ZS) electrolyte with a dual-functional additive, 0.2 M 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIMBF4), is developed to simultaneously obviate polybromides shuttling and boost Zn reversibility, confirmed by in situ ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy and in situ gas chromatography (GC). The KBr/C3N4 coin cell within M-ZS electrolyte exhibited an initial Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 96.7% at 1 C, much higher than 63.4 and 79.9% for pure KBr within ZnSO4 (ZS) and M-ZS, respectively. After 12 hours of resting, the self-discharge of KBr/C3N4 cell in M-ZS is only 9.5%, much lower than that of pure KBr in ZS (100%) and M-ZS (96.5%). The low self-discharge rate and long cycling stability under both 0.2 and 2 C can also be achieved in large-size pouch cells, setting new benchmarks for future Zn─Br batteries. By controlling a Zn DoD of 48.7% and a high mass loading of 40 mg(KBr) cm−2, we fabricated the multilayer Zn─Br pouch cell with ampere hour–level capacity, which exhibited a long life span for >750 cycles and energy density of 106.2 Wh kg−1 based on the whole cathode and anode. These data surpass previous counterparts based on pure intercalation or conversion mechanism under high cathode mass loadings. This electrode engineering process to alter the cathode working mechanism is apparently universal, as evidenced by other layered materials, including boron nitride (BN) and graphite, which yields a strong foundation for aqueous Zn─Br battery application.

RESULTS

Impact of electrode engineering on coin cells

Based on the electrostatic interaction between the imidazolium cation and halide anion (17), a dual-functional electrolyte additive with imidazolium cation was first developed to mitigate Br3− shuttling and enhance Zn reversibility in Zn─Br batteries (note S1). The electrolyte, 2 M ZS with the optimal concentration of 0.2 M BMIMBF4, is denoted M-ZS (note S2). To reveal the interaction between imidazolium cation and polybromides, M-ZS was added to a prepared solution containing Br3−. The imidazolium cation bound with Br3− to form precipitates, evidencing its capability to suppress shuttling of polybromide (figs. S2 and S3) (18).

The cathode engineering is realized by ball mixing the active material of KBr with layered materials (e.g., unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4; see Materials and Methods for details). The electrochemical performance of various Zn─Br batteries was compared under different conditions (Fig. 1C). The pure KBr cathode in ZS electrolyte exhibited an average charge plateau of 1.81 V based on the Br−/Br2 conversion with a capacity of 152.6 mA h g−1 and an initial CE of ~63.4% at 1 C. This low CE is mainly attributed to the serious Br3− shuttling and liquid Br2 diffusion (19). The KBr cathode in M-ZS exhibited a similar average charge plateau with the boosted capacity of 158.7 mA h g−1 and CE of 79.9%, which was mainly due to the suppressed Br3− shuttling with additive introduction. Nevertheless, the initial CE of 79.9% was far from satisfactory, which was mainly due to Br2 diffusion, highlighting that traditional electrolyte strategies are not operative for Zn─Br batteries (20). To confine the formation/diffusion of Br2, the KBr/C3N4 cathode was prepared by engineering the unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4 (fig. S3B and note S2). With g-C3N4, the initial CE for KBr/C3N4 significantly increased to 96.7% with a high capacity of 164.6 mA h g−1. In the second cycle, the KBr/C3N4 electrode exhibited a CE of 97.3%, surpassing those for KBr cathodes in pure ZS and M-ZS (64.6 and 82.5%, fig. S4). These improvements suggest that adding g-C3N4 is possible to suppress the Br2 formation/diffusion by altering the cathode working mechanism, which was confirmed by differential capacity (dQ/dV) curves. According to fig. S5 and Fig. 1D, the dQ/dV curve of KBr exhibits only a single main peak, indicating one-step Br−/Br2 conversion reaction. While with g-C3N4, two obvious peaks appear, confirming that g-C3N4 may alter the working mechanism of KBr from a one-step to a two-step reaction (21). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and x-ray diffraction (XRD) findings established that synthetic g-C3N4 exhibits a layered structure with a d-space of 3.19 Å (figs. S6 to S8) (22). This d-space is sufficiently large for the intercalation of Br ions (1.96 Å), which provides the possibility for Br intercalation into g-C3N4 layers (23).

The rate performance in Fig. 1E demonstrates that the KBr/C3N4 cathode in M-ZS exhibited a discharge capacity of 134.3 mA h g−1 under a high rate of 20 C, significantly outperforming the KBr cathodes in ZS and M-ZS under the same conditions, which delivered 59.8 and 98.6 mA h g−1, respectively. Notably, the low CE of KBr cathode in ZS under 1 C is mainly caused by the time-consuming shuttle effects of cathode and side reactions of Zn anode (24). It is notable that the KBr/C3N4 cathode in pure ZS exhibited an initial CE of ~80% under 1 C and 101.2 mA h g−1 at 20 C (fig. S9), indicating that polybromide shuttling persists in the absence of the electrolyte additive (note S3) (25).

The cycling stability for cells was evaluated under 1 C (fig. S10). The KBr cathode in ZS exhibited a low average CE of 78.5% and could run only 75 cycles, highlighting the instability of polybromide and liquid Br2. Within M-ZS, the KBr electrode exhibited a greater average CE of 92.5% and a capacity retention of 74.4% after 100 cycles. With g-C3N4, however, the average CE was boosted to 99.1% and capacity retention to 98.9%. This confirms the electrode engineering effectiveness in confining liquid Br2, as further evidenced by optical images of separators after battery cycling (fig. S11). For the KBr cycled in ZS, the separator turned a yellow color, evidencing serious shuttle effects. For the KBr/C3N4 battery in M-ZS, the separator was clean without apparent color change. At 5 C, the KBr/C3N4 exhibited a high-capacity retention of 93.6% after 2000 cycles, surpassing the counterparts (Fig. 1F). This demonstrates good stability of KBr/C3N4 cell, further confirmed by its stable charge/discharge curves (fig. S12). When increasing the current rate to 10 C, KBr/C3N4 cell displayed a superlong life span for over 5000 cycles, superior to previous Zn─Br systems under similar conditions (fig. S13) (24, 25).

Self-discharge is a critical parameter for evaluating the stability of Br cathodes in aqueous systems after battery charging (26, 27). As depicted in Fig. 1G, the KBr cathode in ZS was highly instable during resting. After 2 hour of resting, the discharge capacity retention was 27.9% and decreased to 0 after 24 hour of resting, indicating severe self-discharge behavior. In M-ZS, the KBr cathode exhibited reduced but still considerable self-discharge, with capacity retention of 64.5% after 2 hours and 0.2% after 24 hour of resting, primarily due to Br2 diffusion. In notable contrast, the KBr/C3N4 cathode exhibited low self-discharge, with capacity retention of 94.8 and 87.9% after resting for 2 and 24 hours, respectively. The boosted capacity retention from 0.2 to 87.9% highlights the effectiveness of g-C3N4 in stabilizing Br electrodes, providing a robust foundation for the development of advanced aqueous Zn─Br batteries.

Working mechanism studies

For conventional Zn─Br batteries, the specific cathode conversion reactions during battery charging are2Br−−2e−→Br2(slow)(1)Br−+Br2→Br3−(fast)(2)Br3−+Br2→Br5−(fast)(3)2Br5−−2e−→5Br2(slow)(4)

Based on these reactions, both polybromides and Br2 face diffusion that seriously affects battery performance, including discharge capacity, cycling stability, and self-discharge (28). To reveal the impacts of electrode engineering on the cathode working mechanism, in situ UV-Vis spectroscopy was determined to detect electrolyte evolution using a homemade Zn─Br quartz cell (fig. S14A). UV-Vis spectra for the KBr cathode within ZS electrolyte exhibited a pronounced peak at 287.5 nm during battery charging, indexed to the high-soluble Br3− (a combination of Br− and Br2; Fig. 2A) in water medium (13). This indicates that the intermediates quickly diffuse to the electrolyte, as evidenced in the electrolyte color change. For the KBr cathode in M-ZS electrolyte, a small peak was apparent as the battery was charged to 1.95 V, evidencing suppressed Br3− shuttle (Fig. 2B). However, a slight electrolyte color change may be caused by polybromides or Br2. For the KBr/C3N4 cathode in M-ZS, no apparent peak was observed during the charge, demonstrating that the shuttles of polybromides and Br2 were suppressed because of electrolyte modification and electrode engineering (Fig. 2C). After battery charging, the electrolyte was “clean” without color changes.

Except for the electrolyte analysis, the Br evolution in cathodes was also monitored via in situ Raman spectroscopy (fig. S14B). Upon initial charging, no intermediate was detected in KBr cathode with ZS electrolyte (Fig. 2D). As charging progressed, a peak emerged at ~248 cm−1, which can be attributed to Br5− (29). Notably, no obvious Br3− signal was observed likely because of its quick dissolution into the bulk electrolyte rather than accumulation on the electrode surface. At ~1.92 V, a peak appeared at ~319 cm−1 corresponding to Br2, representing the first in situ evidence of Br2 formation during the charging process. This peak further intensified at 1.95 V, indicating a typical Br−/Br2 conversion. With M-ZS electrolyte, a distinct peak was detected at 154 cm−1, which is attributed to Br3−, suggesting the suppressed Br3− shuttling by the electrolyte additive. As the voltage increased to 1.95 V, the Br3− signal weakened while the Br2 peak became dominant, indicating that the overall conversion mechanism was maintained (Fig. 2E). Upon incorporating g-C3N4, a remarkable enhancement in the Br3− signal was observed at the initial charging stage, implying that g-C3N4 also helps to restrict polybromide diffusion (Fig. 2F). A broad peak at ~295 cm−1 rather than Br2 peak was detected throughout the charging process, suggesting that the conventional Br2-involved conversion mechanism was fundamentally altered through the electrode engineering process (30).

To further elucidate the working mechanism of KBr/C3N4 cathode, ex situ XRD tests were conducted to monitor the d-spacing evolution of g-C3N4 under different state of charges (SOCs) (0, 50, and 100%; fig. S15A). At 0% SOC, the strong peak at 27.57° corresponds to KBr, which completely masks the (002) peak of g-C3N4 (27.91°). At 50% SOC, however, a broader peak appeared at 27.62°, corresponding to g-C3N4 with an increased d-spacing of 3.23 Å. This indicates the intercalation of Br− ions into the g-C3N4 structure. At 100% SOC, the (002) peak was observed at 27.49°, with the d-spacing further increasing to 3.24 Å. This gradual increase in d-spacing from 3.19 Å (pristine g-C3N4) to 3.24 Å at 100% SOC demonstrates the intercalation of Br− ions into the g-C3N4 framework during charging process. Furthermore, the high-resolution TEM analysis was conducted to further verify the structural changes of charged electrodes (fig. S15, B to D). At 0% SOC, the TEM image shows well-defined lattice fringes of g-C3N4. Based on the fast Fourier transform pattern, the d-spacing was calculated to be ~3.19 Å. At 100% SOC, the measured d-spacing increased to ~3.24 Å, which is consistent with the XRD data. These findings demonstrate that the KBr/C3N4 cathode undergoes an intercalation-conversion reaction rather than conversion after electrode engineering.

In g-C3N4, three types of nonequivalent nitrogen exist: pyridinic sp2-hybridized N in C═N─C groups, pyrrolic N, and tertiary N or bridging N-(C)3, denoted N1, N2, and N3, respectively (31). Among these, N1 is an unsaturated group with lone pair electrons, making it more electrochemically active than N2 and N3. Therefore, these red circles mainly consisted of active N1 in Fig. 3A are likely to confine the charge-state Br at initial charging process. Upon full intercalation, the resulting compound should be Br2(C3N4). Ex situ soft x-ray spectroscopy (SXR) of cathodes under different SOCs exhibited three peaks in the C Kα-spectrum at 284.4, 287.6, and 291.8 eV, corresponding to vibrations of structural defects, C-N/C-O, and C-F3, respectively (Fig. 3B). From SOC 0 to 100%, the defect vibration was notably reduced, evidencing that charge-state Br* first occupies defect positions in g-C3N4. In N Kα-spectrum (Fig. 3C), the intensity of unsaturated sp2-N vibration at 398.6 eV reduced during battery charging, while the intensity of saturated sp3-N vibrations increased, indicating that N1 loses the electron by bonding with intercalated Br ion upon charging.

To reveal the coordination evolution of N and Br elements in cathodes under different SOCs, ex situ x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed (fig. S16). The N 1s spectra show three deconvoluted peaks corresponding to N1, N2, and N3 at 398.6, 399.2, and 400.8 eV, respectively (Fig. 3, D to F) (32). Upon battery charging, the overall N 1s peak shifted to higher binding energy, indicating the formation of more saturated N groups. At 0% SOC, the ratio of N1, N2, and N3 are 45.9, 39.3, and 14.8%, respectively, confirming the rich N1 in the prepared g-C3N4. In comparison, the ratios are 17.5, 59.7, and 22.8% at 100% SOC, reflecting a notable decrease in unsaturated N1 by breaking the C═N bond in unsaturated sp2-N to form C─N bond upon Br− intercalation. Figure 3 (G to I) presents the Br 3d spectra at differing SOCs. At 0% SOC, only Br− vibration is observed, with deconvoluted peaks at 68.4 and 69.4 eV, corresponding to Br− 3d5/2 and Br− 3d3/2, respectively. At 50% SOC, a new peak appears because of intercalated Br* that can be deconvoluted into Br* 3d5/2 and Br* 3d3/2 at 69.5 and 70.5 eV, respectively. At 100% SOC, these intercalated Br* peaks dominate, evidencing that Br− undergoes partial oxidation during charging to form intercalated Br*. Therefore, the cathode reaction can be summarized as follows: Initially, Br− intercalates into the g-C3N4 structure, forming Br2(C3N4)x− (x ≤ 1). As charging proceeds, Br2(C3N4)x− undergoes further oxidation, forming a neutral Br2(C3N4) compound. The unsaturated N1 in the g-C3N4 layers will also lose the electron to host Br-ion and stabilize the oxidized Br* species. This mechanism effectively suppresses the formation of liquid Br2, reducing its associated diffusion and toxicity.

Ex situ synchrotron x-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and density functional theory (DFT) were used to determine the valence state of intercalated Br* in the cathode. At 0% SOC, the Br Kα-edge in fig. S17 shows a peak at 13,479 eV, corresponding to KBr, indicating that it is the fully reduced state of Br. Upon charging to 50% SOC, a broad peak near 13,474 eV becomes apparent, attributed to the 1s → 4p transition of intercalated Br* species. This shift suggests that Br undergoes partial oxidation to a lower valence state, indicative of hole formation in the 4p orbitals. However, at 100% SOC, while the further oxidation with the increased peak intensity, the intercalated Br* is not fully oxidized to Br2 but remains partially oxidized (23). This demonstrates that Br* loses partial electron but does not reach a neutral oxidation state. The DFT simulation further clarified the valence state of intercalated Br* in the optimized Br2(C3N4) structure (fig. S18). Simulations revealed that the intercalated Br* remains at an oxidation state of ~−0.14 (Fig. 3, J and K) (33). The diffusion pathway and diffusion energy barrier of Br ion in g-C3N4 were also simulated (fig. S19). Following optimization of the diffusion pathway, the energy profiles for the representative diffusion step were established. The barrier energy for Br diffuse through two circles is 0.57 eV (Fig. 3L), comparable to Li/Na diffusion in layered structures (34).

Zn anode studies

In addition to cathode challenges, the poor reversibility of Zn anode caused by water-induced hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), corrosion, and dendrite growth limits the practical application of Zn─Br batteries, particularly under high mass loading and high currents (35, 36). To reveal the impact of electrolyte additive on water stability, linear sweep voltammetry curves of 2 M Na2SO4 with/without additive at 0.5 mV s−1 were conducted to obviate effects from Zn2+ deposition (37). The Na2SO4 electrolyte exhibited a distinctive current response at −1.13 V versus Ag/AgCl (Fig. 4A), corresponding to HER. After introducing the additive, the current response was delayed by −43 mV, indicating suppressed HER. Generally, Zn─Br batteries require electrolytes with high stability against the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) due to their high operating voltages. As shown in Fig. 4B, OER undergoes at 1.05 V versus Ag/AgCl, slightly above the Br−/Br2 conversion potential (~0.86 V versus Ag/AgCl). This suggests that OER can easily initiate during Br cathode charging, which reduces CE and cycling stability of the battery. With the additive, the OER potential shifts to 1.26 V, boosting the electrolyte stability. In situ GC analysis was conducted to dynamically evaluate the amount of H2 evolution during battery operation. The largest H2 amount generated in aqueous ZS was 48.3 parts per million upon the Zn plating/stripping at 2 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4C). However, the H2 amount is suppressed significantly with the additive, as demonstrated by the contour plots in Fig. 4D.

To establish the effects of the electrolyte additive on Zn reversibility, CE was tested in Zn─Cu half cells using both ZS and M-ZS at a low current and 25°C. In ZS, the CE increased initially but exhibited obvious fluctuations after ~246 hours, indicating battery failure caused by dendrite growth (Fig. 4E) (38). The overall average CE for Zn plating/stripping was just 96.0%. In comparison, the Zn─Cu cell with M-ZS exhibited a much higher initial CE of 89.0% (fig. S20) and maintained excellent reversibility, achieving an overall average CE of 98.8% after 1000 hours of cycling. At 5 mA cm−2, the cell with M-ZS also outperformed the cell with pure ZS (fig. S21). Additionally, the CE for both cells were compared under low temperature of −15°C. The cell with pure ZS failed to operate due to electrolyte freezing (Fig. 4F), as confirmed by the inset figure. The cell with M-ZS, however, exhibited a high average CE of 99.2% for cycling at 500 hours. These results highlight the antifreezing effect of M-ZS, confirming its potential to broaden the battery applicability to extreme conditions.

The morphology of Zn anodes was examined via SEM and small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) after 200 hours of cycling at 25°C. In ZS, the Zn anode exhibited a dendritic and porous morphology (Fig. 4G), remarkably affecting the CE for Zn plating/stripping (39). Conversely, the cycled Zn electrode in M-ZS exhibited an “even” surface with hexagonal Zn deposition rather than dendrite formation and holes (Fig. 4H), evidencing altered Zn plating/stripping behavior in M-ZS. SAXS patterns for the cycled Zn electrode exhibited two distinctive rings under low angles, corresponding to the (002) and (004) peaks for Zn4SO4(OH)6·xH2O by-product (Fig. 4I). This finding evidences serious side reactions at the Zn anode during battery cycling (40). However, the cycled Zn electrode in M-ZS only exhibited clear rings at high angles, corresponding to the three major peaks for Zn (002), (100), and (101), respectively, confirming the suppressed corrosive reactions. Additionally, the Zn anode in M-ZS exhibited a boosted (002) ring intensity, confirming that the modified electrolyte tuned the Zn deposition orientation to promote dendrite-free Zn (002) deposition (41). This preferential Zn deposition was further verified by XRD (fig. S22). After 200 cycles, the intensity ratio of I(002)/I(101) increased from 0.5 to 1.5, evidencing a change in the preferred orientation for dendrite-free Zn deposition facilitated by the electrolyte additive (note S4) (42). Overall, the improved Zn anode performance originates from the preferential accumulation of BMI+ at the Zn surface, where it partially displaces interfacial water molecules and reconstructs the local electric field. This interfacial regulation effectively suppresses water-induced side reactions, including HER and corrosion, while simultaneously promoting homogeneous Zn2+ nucleation and oriented, dendrite-free Zn deposition, thereby obviously enhancing Zn reversibility and cycling stability.

Pouch cell performance

To assess the feasibility of the electrode engineering in scaled-up batteries, small-size single-layer Zn─Br pouch cells were first assembled. The KBr-based cathodes were fabricated using a “dough-rolling” method that minimized water or ethanol usage, reducing active material loss (note S5 and fig. S23). With a mass loading of ~30 mg(KBr) cm−2, higher than that in commercial Li-ion batteries of ~20 mg cm−2, the KBr/C3N4 pouch cell exhibited a high discharge plateau of 1.76 V with a high initial capacity of 176.6 mA h g−1 at a low current of 0.2 C, evidencing excellent consistency following scaling of the battery (Fig. 5A). It is well-known that aqueous Zn batteries, especially those paired with conversion-type cathodes, typically suffer from poor cycling stability at low current densities. KBr/C3N4 pouch cell shows a good stability with capacity retention of 93.8% after 100 cycles under 0.2 C, substantially outperforming KBr cells with/without electrolyte modification (fig. S24). Figure 5B compares the rate capability for different pouch cells from 1 to 5 C. Under 1 C, the KBr/C3N4 cell exhibited high capacity with a high average CE of 97.8%. After increasing rate to 5 C, a high capacity of ~110 mA h g−1 remained with the retention of 71.2% (fig. S25). However, at such high rate, KBr pouch cells with pure ZS and M-ZS electrolytes only exhibited a low capacity of ~5.5 and ~83.1 mA h g−1, respectively. This underscores the effectiveness of g-C3N4 in supporting fast-charging capability for high-loading pouch cells (~10 min of charging).

The performance of single-layer KBr/C3N4 pouch cell under extreme temperatures was further evaluated. At 60°C, the Zn─Br pouch cell exhibited a stable capacity of ~152.1 mA h g−1 at 2 C (Fig. 5C). After 200 cycles, a high-capacity retention of 98.1% remained, confirming good high-temperature stability. Under −15°C, the capacity decreased to 116.2 mA h g−1 but retained a high-capacity retention of 99.3% after 200 cycles (fig. S26), indicating robust all-temperature cycling stability. Self-discharge performance of single-layer Zn─KBr/C3N4 pouch cell was determined to assess because the diffusion of both liquid-state Br2 and polybromide notably reduce shelf life under high mass loading. After charging, the pouch cell retained 90.1% of its capacity after 12 hours of resting (Fig. 5D), similar to coin cells, evidencing its good antidischarging ability even under industry-level mass loading. After charging back to the high cutoff potential, three pouch cells in series powered a computer fan for 3.5 hours, demonstrating the practicality of single-layer pouch cell (movie S1).

Although the single-layer pouch cell exhibits decent electrochemical performance, its total capacity remains limited (<0.4 A·hour), and the testing current is relatively low, making it significantly different from industrial standards. To further assess the practical potential, a multilayer pouch cell with ampere hour–level capacity was assembled, using metallic Zn foil as the anode. Despite the relatively low DoD (<10%) of the Zn anode, this “large-format” pouch cell experienced a fast failure, sustaining only ~200 cycles under 2 C (corresponding to ~2400 mA; fig. S27). After disassembling the cell, the uneven Zn consumption can be found under high-current conditions, particularly near the tab regions. Because the Zn foil functions as both the current collector and active material, excessive current density at the tab interface accelerates local Zn depletion, ultimately leading to electrical disconnection of the battery (fig. S28). These findings highlight the limitations of metallic Zn foil in pouch cell architectures, due to its low DoD and poor cycling stability, underscoring its unsuitability for the multilayer design in industrial-scale production (note S6).

To enhance DoD and life span of Zn in the multilayer pouch cell, a predeposited Zn anode was developed on the Cu current collector with a specific capacity of 1.0 A·hour. With an increased cathode mass loading to ~40 mg(KBr) cm−2, the multilayer Zn─Br pouch cell exhibited a high initial capacity of ~0.95 A·hour, corresponding to a Zn DoD of 48.7%. As shown in Fig. 5E, this battery maintains excellent charge/discharge consistency under different cycles. Impressively, under such high Zn DoD and high current, this multilayer cell exhibited a capacity retention of 98.3% over 750 cycles (Fig. 5F), representing record cycling stability and even surpassing previously reported single-layer Zn─Br pouch cells (13, 18, 43). Upon disassembly, the Cu current collector exhibited good integrity without evidence of holes and corrosion spots, confirming the feasibility of predeposited Zn anode for practical application (Fig. 5G). Additionally, the energy density, calculated on the basis of the active materials, conductive agent, g-C3N4, binder, and unused Zn, was ~106.2 Wh kg−1. At a record mass loading of 40 mg(KBr) cm−2, this high energy density surpasses that of other Zn-based batteries, including Zn─MnO2 (44), Zn─I2 (20). Zn─V2O5 (45), and others (2, 46–48), demonstrating the practical potential of this Zn─Br pouch cell design (Fig. 5H). Nevertheless, the current configuration is not yet ideal for direct commercialization. The use of a heavy Ti mesh current collector (~86.4% mass ratio to active material) and glass fiber separator (>750% electrolyte absorption) limits overall cell-level energy density (detailed discussion in note S7). Replacing these components with lighter, cost-effective alternatives is crucial for further optimization. Based on our estimates, adopting a current collector with a 15% mass ratio to active material and a separator with 200% electrolyte absorption could reduce the electrolyte-to-capacity ratio to 3.85. Under these optimized conditions, with 15 cathode layers, deposited Zn (~50% DoD), and Cu foil as the anode current collector, the projected energy density could reach 103.6 Wh kg−1 on the basis of the whole cell (table S2), highlighting the strong potential of this Zn─Br system for practical grid-scale energy storage.

Universality verification

To confirm the universality of the electrode engineering strategy, other layered materials were introduced in the KBr cathode. With the layered BN introduction, the KBr/BN cathode in M-ZS electrolyte exhibited a high discharge capacity of 156.8 mA h g−1 with an initial CE of 95.9% under 1 C (fig. S29). This CE value is much higher than that for pure KBr electrode (63.4%). In the second cycle, CE increased to 96.9% with a same discharge plateau of 1.73 V. The KBr/BN electrode exhibited good cycling stability. After 500 cycles at 5 C, the battery maintained a high capacity of 118.6 mA h g−1, corresponding to 93.6% capacity retention (fig. S30). The intercalation-conversion mechanism of KBr/BN electrode was assessed via ex situ XRD (fig. S31). The (002) peak for NB shifted to a lower angle during battery charging, confirming Br ion intercalation into the BN layer. The d-space for BN layer increased from 3.33 to 3.37 Å, as presented in fig. S30. The boost in Zn─Br cell performance was confirmed via introducing layered graphite (fig. S32 and S33 and note S8). These findings strongly suggest that the electrode engineering strategy regulates the working mechanism of Br-based cathodes (Fig. 5I), specifically avoiding the formation of toxic Br2 liquid, and represents a universal and effective strategy to enhance Zn─Br battery performance.

DISCUSSION

The design of aqueous Zn─Br battery is “reshaped” through a universal electrode engineering strategy to enhance both safety and electrochemical performance. This design is achieved by incorporating layered materials into the cathode, shifting the cathode mechanism from conventional conversion to an intercalation-conversion pathway. Without liquid Br2 formation, the Zn─KBr/C3N4 coin cell in modified electrolyte exhibits a high initial CE of 96.7% at 1 C and a long life span of >5000 cycles at 10 C, remarkably outperforming counterparts without electrode engineering. Notably, this design also addresses the key challenges of low-rate cycling and self-discharge in static Zn─Br battery. Even in KBr/C3N4 pouch cells, a capacity retention of 93.8% after 100 cycles at a low rate of 0.2 C, along with a low self-discharge of 90.1% after 12 hours of rest, is achieved, establishing high-standard benchmarks for future Zn─Br battery design. Upon scaling to a multilayer pouch cell with a high Zn DoD of 48.7% and a high cathode loading of 40 mg(KBr) cm−2, the ampere hour–level battery exhibits a record cycling stability of >750 cycles and a high energy density of 106.2 Wh kg−1 (based on entire cathode and anode), outperforming previously reported Zn battery technologies. This electrode engineering process demonstrates practical universality with other layered materials. These findings unlock the full potential of aqueous Zn─Br batteries with enhanced reversibility and safety, while also establishing a strong foundation for their commercial potential.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Zn foils (100 μm), Cu-foil, aluminum-plastic film, and Pt electrode were purchased from Shenzhen Kejing Technology. ZnSO4·7H2O (≥99.0%), KBr (≥99.0%), BMIMBF4 (≥98.0%), BN (≥98.0%), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE; 60 m/m%), Br2 (≥99.5%), and urea (≥98.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All other reagents were used directly without purification. Deionized water was used to prepare all aqueous electrolytes.

The unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4 synthesis and electrode engineering process

The unsaturated N-rich g-C3N4 sample was synthesized by stepwise calcination pyrolysis of urea in a porcelain crucible. Dried urea (20 g) was placed in two covered 50-ml porcelain crucibles and heated at 520°C for 4 hours in a muffle furnace (Fisher Scientific). Heating rate was controlled to 4.5°C min−1. To obtain final g-C3N4, the obtained yellow powder was heated further at 540°C for 2 hours with a ramp rate of 2.3°C min−1. For the electrode engineering process, the as-prepared g-C3N4 was mixed with KBr (mass ratio of 3:1, KBr:g-C3N4) and homogenized using a planetary ball mill at 400 rpm for 2 hours to ensure uniform distribution of the layered additive. The resulting composite was used directly as the cathode active material. To demonstrate the generality of this electrode engineering strategy, other layered materials such as BN and graphite were used to replace g-C3N4 using the same mixing protocol.

Electrochemical tests

Electrochemical characterization of Zn─Cu cells and Zn─Zn cells was determined in different electrolytes using 2032-type coin-cells on Neware BTS4000 series, in which the electrolyte additive was optimized with 0.2 M (fig. S3A). Zn plating/stripping in Zn─Cu cells was determined at 0.5 and 5 mA cm−2, respectively. KBr was synthesized using a dough-rolling method using 60 wt % KBr, 30 wt % Ketjen black, and 10 wt % PTFE (6:3:1). For KBr/C3N4 electrodes, different ratios of g-C3N4 were attempted (10 to 30 wt %; note S2 and fig. S3B). The optimized and discussed ratio above of KBr:C3N4:Ketjen black:PTFE was 6:2:1:1. In Zn─Br batteries, the galvanostatic charge/discharge was determined in the voltage range of 0.7 to 1.95 V versus Zn/Zn2+. The loading mass of KBr in coin cells was controlled to ~7.0 to 10.0 mg cm−2, and the loading mass for KBr in pouch cells was ~30 to 40 mg cm−2. Electrolyte addition was controlled in both coin cell and pouch cell to infiltrate the separator. Specific capacities were computed on the basis of the active mass of KBr in cathodes. Self-discharge was measured by charging the cells to 1.95 V, resting for various durations (e.g., 2, 6, 12, and 24 hours), and determining the capacity retention using the ratio of the postrest discharge capacity to that of before resting, as shown in Eq. 5Self−discharge retention(%)=Discharge capacity after restingDischarge capacity before resting×100%(5)

Material characterization

The crystalline structure of g-C3N4, cycled Zn electrodes, KB/C3N4, and KBr/BN was determined by XRD (Bruker D8). The morphology for g-C3N4 and cycled Zn electrodes was observed via SEM (JEOL JSM-7500FA) and the scanning TME (JEOL JEM-200F). XPS measurement electrodes were determined on a VG Multilab 2000 (VG Inc.) photoelectron spectrometer, in which the binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV of the surface adventitious carbon. SXR was determined at the beamline in the Australian Synchrotron, Clayton, Victoria. In situ GC (Agilent 8890B configured with thermal conductivity, and mechanizer/flame ionization, detector) was used to quantify gas product.

Ex situ synchrotron XANES

Synchrotron XANES measurements were determined at the wiggler XAS Beamline (12ID) at the Australian Synchrotron (Melbourne, Australia). The measurements were performed at the Br K-edge, the energy step size through the edge was 0.25 eV, and the exposure time per step was fixed at 1 s. The data reduction followed standard methods using the ATHENA software package. Calibrations and structural refinements were determined using KBr as reference.

In situ spectral measurements

In situ UV-Vis spectra were determined on a UV3600 instrument (Shimadzu, Japan) by attaching cathode and Zn anode on the two sides of quartz cell and, adding the electrolyte between them. The battery charge was performed on the electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Chenhua, Shanghai), and UV-Vis spectra were concurrently determined to in situ monitor electrolyte evolution. In situ Raman spectra were determined using a special Raman cell (Gaoss Union, B002-RM). The battery charge was concurrently determined on an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Chenhua, Shanghai), and Raman spectra were in situ determined through the quartz window of Raman cell. To clearly observe the Br2 evolution, the loading mass of KBr is controlled higher than 10 mg cm−2 in both UV-Vis and Raman cells.

Density functional theory

First-principles calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package code (49). The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional was used for computation of electron exchange-correlation energy (50). Projector-augmented wave potentials were used to represent ionic cores (51). Atomic relaxations were carried out with the quasi-Newton minimization scheme, until the maximum force on any atom was <0.02 eV/Å (52). The Becke-Johnson damping was integrated into the DFT-D3 method (53). Geometry optimizations were determined with a plane-wave cutoff of 400 eV. An irreducible 2 × 2 × 2 Monkhorst Pack k-point grid was applied (54), with the central point shifted to the gamma point. The hexagonal bilayer g-C3N4 structure used in theoretical calculations has a P6¯m2 space group, with unit cell parameters of a = 14.22 Å, b = 14.22 Å, and c = 10 Å. This configuration includes 24 carbon atoms and 32 nitrogen atoms. For the Br-C3N4 compound, the Br atoms were put in the interlayer of g-C3N4 before structure optimization. The climbing-image nudged elastic band was performed for the determinations saddle points and minimum energy pathways for Br atom (55). VESTA software was used to generate schematics depicting the different charge density and diffusion path (56).
