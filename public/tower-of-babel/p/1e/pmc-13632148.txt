Understanding the small print: a 5-year review of readability in phase I trial information leaflets
Authors: Kate Dodd, Tim Rowland, Hassan Burhan, Amin Sammour, Richard J. FitzGerald, Lauren E. Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding trial information is essential for participants to give truly informed consent. Current guidance suggests that health information should be accessible to readers with a reading age of 11 years. Readability describes how easily text can be understood, while reading age represents the minimum age at which a typical reader is likely to be able to understand the material. This study examines the readability and reading age of participant information leaflets (PILs) used in recent phase I clinical trials to assess how well they align with recommended standards.MethodsThis single-centre study analysed phase I participant information leaflets (PILs) produced between 2017 and 2022. Five established readability tools were used to assess readability and reading age: Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog Index, Fry Readability and Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) Index. Readability was compared between academic and industry-developed PILs.ResultsA total of 46 PILs were analysed in this study: 38 (82.6%) industry and 8 (17.4%) academic-led clinical trials. PILs had a mean (± SD) total word count of 8426 (± 2002). The overall mean readability scores, and equivalent reading ages were as follows: Flesch Reading Ease: 53.5 – age 15 + ; Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level: 9.9 – age 14–15; Gunning Fog Index: 11.6 – age 16–17; Fry Readability: 9.9 – age 14–15; SMOG: 13.5 – age 19 + . Academic PILs were more readable than industry PILs, with statistically significant differences for the Flesch Reading Ease (p = 0.046), Gunning Fog Index (p = 0.035), Fry Readability (p = 0.041), and SMOG Index (p = 0.010). No significant difference was observed for the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level.ConclusionsAll PILs were written at a reading age far above recommended levels, raising concerns that many potential participants, particularly those with lower literacy or living in areas of higher deprivation, may struggle to understand essential trial information or may be deterred from taking part altogether. While readability formulas capture only one dimension of comprehensibility, these findings highlight an urgent need to redesign PILs to reduce reading age and to evaluate how improving readability impacts participant understanding and informed consent.

Background

Health literacy has recently been described as “linked to literacy, and entails people's knowledge, motivation and competences to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information in order to make judgments and take decisions in everyday life concerning healthcare, disease prevention and health promotion to maintain or improve quality of life during the life course” [1]. It is reported that up to half of the population have limited health literacy [2]. Although health literacy is more than simply the ability to read and understand words, up to 61% of the working age population in England find health materials difficult to understand due to their complexity and 1 in 6 adults (16.4%) have very low levels of literacy skills. In the UK it is estimated that 7.1 million adults read and write at or below the level of a nine-year-old [3, 4].

Readability, the ease with which a reader can understand a written text, directly affects how well people can access, understand, and use written health information to make informed health decisions. If written materials are too complex (e.g., long sentences, jargon), people with limited literacy may not understand key health messages. This acts as a barrier to their ability to access and apply information when making decisions about their healthcare and contributes to low health literacy. Lower levels of health literacy are more prevalent within economically and socially disadvantaged communities, including people with learning disabilities or those with language barriers, which can widen health inequalities demonstrating the link between literacy and life expectancy [4].

The NHS (UK) recommends that written healthcare materials should be aimed at the readability stage expected of the average 11 year old [5], similarly in the US, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality recommends a 5th or 6th grade level (age 10–12) [6].

Taking part in early phase clinical trials is a complex healthcare decision and requires a thorough understanding of the potential risks and benefits of participation. One of the key principles of ethics in clinical research codified in the Declaration of Helsinki is autonomy [7]. This fundamental ethical principle refers to the right and ability of a participant to make independent, informed decisions and is implemented through the practice of ‘informed consent’. Prior to providing consent to participate in a clinical drug trial, potential participants are provided with written information about the drug in the form of a standardised participant information leaflet (PIL). This written document is the primary source of information that participants will review as part of the decision-making process and in most cases will be an individual’s initial introduction to the trial. Considering the significance of the PIL, it should be clear and accessible, allowing the recipient to understand it fully. These documents should promote comprehension by using simple language and concise sections to explain drug risks and address any questions about medication use. It is essential to develop PILs that deliver clear and accurate information to safeguard an individual’s health and to enable them to have an understanding about the risks of taking part in the specific trial [8, 9].

The NIHR Liverpool Clinical Research Facility (CRF) is a phase I clinical research facility based within Liverpool University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust with a catchment area for clinical trial recruitment across Liverpool City Region (LCR). LCR has some of the lowest literacy levels in England. Walton (Liverpool), Knowsley, West Derby (Liverpool) and Birkenhead are in the top 28 constituencies with the greatest literacy need. This increases the likelihood of potential participants being unable to understand documentation presented to them [10]. Phase I is the critical dose-determining step in the drug development pipeline; the focus is safety and tolerability and is mostly undertaken in healthy volunteers. There is usually very little, if any, human experience data contained within the PILs, and complex information about animal models and in vitro studies needs to be explained. These are particularly challenging scientific concepts, which require clear and understandable explanation if participants are to make informed decisions about participation.

Readability of a document can be assessed using readability formulas, which evaluate the reading age required to understand a document, through the analysis of sentences, paragraphs and word length. They are commonly used in health settings to determine the understandability of information [11]. There are various formulas in use such as Flesch Reading Ease, Gunning Fog Index and SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook) Index. These formulas utilise different calculations and validation methods; therefore, the results of each formula may differ. The use of readability scores to assess the complexity of written information in participant information leaflets may aid in the writing of PILs that use language that is understandable to all participants [12].

Many studies examining readability of clinical trial information have been done for later phase studies of effectiveness, in which there is existing data on safety from human participants. This has also been examined in cancer and surgical trials, in which the trial paradigm is quite distinct [13–15]. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study in adults to examine readability of PILs specifically in phase I clinical trials.

This study aims to explore the readability scores and reading age of PILs for recent phase I clinical trials carried out in the CRF and whether they are written at an appropriate reading age for the target population.

Methods

Setting

The study was a single-centre study undertaken at NIHR Liverpool Clinical Research Facility, within The Royal Liverpool University Hospital, Liverpool, UK. This study formed part of a wider programme of work aimed at improving inclusivity in early phase clinical trials.

Eligible studies

All industry and academic-led phase I clinical trials (n = 46) that had been conducted within the Liverpool Clinical Research Facility (CRF) during a 5-year period (2017–2022) were included in the study. Phase II-IV studies and phase I trials conducted outside of the CRF were excluded.

Regulatory approvals

In line with HRA guidance at the time of writing, ethical approval was not necessary for this study as it comprised a review of existing documentation.

Readability software and data collection

The 5 readability formulas most used to review written health information were used to test the readability of all PILs [12]. These were Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Gunning Fog Index (GFI), Fry Readability and Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) Index Table 1 summarises the scores, advantages and disadvantages of each of the formula. An online tool was used to access these readability formulas (readable.com). The content of each PIL was converted to a text file, with headings, tables and any references or appendices removed before analysis. Results

Eligible studies

There were 46 phase I clinical trials identified during the period of 2017–2022. For each of these clinical trials an electronic copy of the participant information leaflet was acquired. The target population was healthy volunteers (n = 5), patients (n = 27) and hybrid trials involving both (n = 14). There were 38 (82.6%) industry and 8 (17.4%) academic-led clinical trials.

Readability scores

Overall, the eligible PILs had an overall mean (± SD) total word count of 8426 (± 2002). The word counts ranged from 2343 to 12,888 words.

Comparison of the readability of PIL according to industry or academic-led study

Sponsor type was examined because differences in document development processes, institutional templates, legal review, and regulatory interpretation between academic and industry sponsors may influence the readability and accessibility of participant information leaflets. Industry-sponsored studies may involve more extensive legal and risk-related content which may affect readability.

Table 3 shows mean readability scores in all PILs and compares those developed with academic and industry partners respectively. When assessed as a group across all measures, academic-developed PILs demonstrated better readability than industry-developed PILs, with lower mean reading ages in three of the readability formulas and lower mean word counts. All PILs were found to be above the recommended reading age regardless of origin. The SMOG Index, which emphasises polysyllabic word use and is considered a robust measure of health-related readability, was the formula that gave the highest mean reading age across all PILs.

There was a statistically significant difference when four of the five readability scores of academic-developed PILs and industry-developed PILs were compared: Flesch Reading Age (p = 0.046), Gunning Fog Index (p = 0.035), Fry Readability (p = 0.041) and SMOG Index (p = 0.010). This suggests that industry-developed PILs may be harder to read and understand, which is important as the majority of phase I studies are developed by industry sponsors.

Discussion

The Health Education England health literacy guide recommends written material to be aimed at the average 11 year old [5]. We have therefore used a reading age of 11 (equivalent to US grade 5) as the cut off for an acceptable readability score. This equates to a score 5 in all readability scores used in this study apart from FRE, where the equivalent score is 80 + [19, 20]. There is evidence that healthcare documentation, including informed consent forms (ICFs) for clinical trials, is written to a level exceeding the national average reading age [21].

Our data show that all the phase I research studies analysed in this study have PILs with readability scores that exceed the recommended reading age, despite recruiting from a region with lower than the national average reading age. Informed consent for trial participation relies on participants being able to understand the information contained within PILs [14]. The results of this study show that for phase I clinical trials, arguably the most complex and uncertain part of the clinical trial pipeline, this information is not being adequately provided in PILs.

Readability scores are calculated using factors such as the number of syllables, characters and sentence length to produce the estimated grade level of the text for the general population [22, 23]. These scores don’t provide an understanding of comprehensibility, which may involve a range of broader cognitive and contextual skills. For example, some participants in disease specific trials will be experts by experience and may have a greater understanding of medical terminology and treatment information.

PILs are just one aspect of an informed consent process that is often complex. The findings of this study present ethical implications specifically with respect to autonomy; individuals need to make voluntary decisions based on adequate understanding of trial elements including the risks, benefits, alternative treatment options and trial processes they will be exposed to [24]. It is widely understood that there is a link between low health literacy and socio-economic disadvantages; therefore, PILs with high readability scores can systematically exclude disadvantaged communities, contributing to inequality [25].

To ensure truly informed consent, more needs to be done to facilitate the understanding of complex written materials for individuals participating in research. Readability tools are now widespread and integrated into basic word processing software and provide guidance as to what can be changed to improve readability. The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) software, in particular generative AI and large language models (LLMs) could also be a powerful tool for achieving this during the design stage. A recent study examining patient education materials for aortic stenosis demonstrated that two AI-powered LLMs, ChatGPT-3.5 and Bard, were able to successfully convert materials from 10th to 12th grade level down to 6th–7th grade level [26]. However, it has also been shown that AI-generated information leaflets have omitted important information or over-simplified information resulting in a negative effect on comprehension [27].

Although studies have examined the readability of PILs, less is known about the relationship between readability of written material and its effect on comprehension. Demonstrating that improving readability of PILs can improve comprehension, is essential to ensuring that changes are made to the way PILs are written to enable greater accessibility.

Strengths and limitations

To our understanding this is the first time readability of phase I PILs has been conducted across a wide range of specialities, over a period of 5 years, The results support similar findings across other trial literature.

Our work does have some limitations. When calculating the reading age, only one readability website (readable.com) was used to access multiple calculators. To make the findings more robust, the use of an additional calculator may be beneficial. Some research has used two websites; however, the differences in the resulting readability scores between multiple calculators are small [28].

Readability scores calculate reading age only, and do not determine whether a participant has fully understood the written information. Research is currently being conducted at NIHR Liverpool CRF, to establish whether reducing the reading age of a document results in greater comprehension.

Conclusions

It is vital for PILs to be understandable to all potential participants. The phase I PILs included in this study did not meet existing recommendations around readability. There are existing and emerging tools that may help improve readability of PILs, AI and LLM powered software may be a tool to support improved readability, however care needs to be taken to ensure accuracy and accountability. Readability formulas are only one aspect that should be considered when assessing written health information. Other measures to be considered include co-producing with public contributors or patients with lived experience or presenting information in different formats including audio and video formats. Comprehension and application of written health information is ultimately what contributes to health literacy and allows for valid informed participation in early phase trials. Future work may focus on assessing whether reducing the reading age supports an increase in overall comprehension and meaningful informed consent.
