Developmental disorders of the reproductive tract in C57BL/6N-XYPOS mice
Authors: Honoka KAWAI, Toshifumi YOKOYAMA, Chihiro INUZUKA, Sachika KURAMOTO, Yaichi HOSHIKAWA, Youhei MANTANI, Takanori MIKI, Nobuhiko HOSHI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMammalian embryos possess undifferentiated gonads and primordial male and female reproductive tracts. In males, sex-determining region of the Y chromosome (Sry) triggers the differentiation of gonadal supporting cell precursors into Sertoli cells. In C57BL/6N-XYPOS (B6N-XYPOS) mice, a model of disorders of sex development, Sry expression is delayed relative to wild-type B6N mice, and several reproductive tract malformations have been reported. In this study, we investigated the developmental stage and regions at which reproductive tract development fails in B6N-XYPOS. In B6N-XYPOS at 15.5 days post-coitum (dpc), the number of seminiferous tubules showed no difference from B6N, but seminiferous tubules with closed at both ends appeared. In B6N-XYPOS with bilaterally developed ovaries or testes, significant disorders were observed only in the female reproductive tract. B6N-XYPOS ovotestes showed impaired testis differentiation in the cranial and caudal regions, not only in fetuses but also in adults. In female B6N-XYPOS with bilaterally or unilaterally formed ovaries, the Müllerian ducts opened into the urogenital sinus at 15.5 dpc; however, at postnatal day 1, one individual exhibited a uterus with a closed region, and many adult females formed an edematous uterus. These results suggest that impaired cranial testis differentiation may disrupt the connection between the seminiferous tubules and the rete testis. They also suggest that Müllerian duct formation is initially completed even in female B6N-XYPOS with an edematous uterus, but that the duct closes abnormally between 15.5 dpc and birth.Keywords: C57BL/6N-XYPOS, Müllerian duct, ovotestis, reproductive tract formation, sex reversal

INTRODUCTION

The undifferentiated fetal mammalian gonad undergoes morphological changes during sex determination and sexual differentiation. In males, expression of sex-determining region of the Y chromosome (Sry) directly activates the SRY-box transcription factor 9 (Sox9), thereby causing gonadal supporting cells to differentiate into testis-specific Sertoli cells. Sertoli cell differentiation and seminiferous tubule formation throughout the entire testis require sufficient Sry expression between the 12 tail somite (ts) and 15 ts, which correspond to 11.0 to 11.25 days post-coitum (dpc) [5, 13, 24]. Reduced Sry expression levels or delayed onset of Sry expression causes disruption of sexual differentiation, resulting in the formation of ovaries or ovotestes instead of testes from undifferentiated gonads [4, 6, 13]. When Sry is appropriately expressed, supporting-like cells expressing paired box 8 (Pax8) aggregate to form the rete cord [30]. During development, the testis cord differentiates into seminiferous tubules through proliferation, branching, and reorganization, while the rete cord differentiates into the rete testis [28]. In this process, some Sertoli cells constituting the seminiferous tubules differentiate into epithelial cells of the rete testis at the junction between the rete testis and seminiferous tubules [14]. Around 15.5 dpc, the seminiferous tubules and rete testis in the testis connect with the Wolffian duct in the mesonephros, completing the male reproductive tract [20].

The structure of the SRY protein differs between mouse strains, with studies demonstrating that the glutamine repeat sequence of SRY is shorter in the Mus musculus domesticus substrain than in the M. m. musculus substrain [23]. In addition, although Sry was previously considered a single-exon gene, the M. m. musculus substrain is now known to possess a second exon, forming a two-exon type Sry (Sry-T), which functions in sex determination [15]. In the C57BL/6 (B6) strain, which carries Sry derived from the M. m. musculus substrain, replacement of Sry with that from the M. m. domesticus substrain results in XY sex reversal and predisposition to disorders of sex differentiation [1, 3, 26, 32]. In the B6N-YPOS mouse strain generated by introducing the M. m. domesticus‒type Y chromosome from the M. m. poschiavinus strain into the B6N strain, Sry expression was delayed by 2–3 ts [26]. This resulted in the emergence of individuals with testes (T), in addition to ovaries (O) and ovotestes (OT) [26].

In the testes of adult B6N-XYPOS mice, some seminiferous tubules exhibited a thin seminiferous epithelium containing only spermatozoa or tubules containing only degenerated Sertoli cells [26]. SOX9-positive, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)-negative incompletely differentiated Sertoli cells were also observed in the fetal OT gonad of B6N-XYPOS mice [31]. Additionally, edematous uteri were present in individuals with unilateral or bilateral ovaries [26]. These findings suggest that spermatogenesis previously occurred even in seminiferous tubules containing only spermatozoa. However, an incomplete connection between the seminiferous tubules and the rete testis likely prevented sperm transport to the epididymis. This incomplete connection would be expected to impede sperm outflow, leading to accumulation within the seminiferous tubules and subsequent thinning of the seminiferous epithelium. Similarly, secretions were likely retained within the edematous uterus due to the absence of an external opening at its distal end. However, the specific developmental stage at which these disorders arise remains unclear. This study aims to elucidate the developmental stage and specific regions of the reproductive tract where these disruptions occur.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

The B6N-XYPOS strain was derived from the B6J-XYPOS strain provided by The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). It was established by repeatedly backcrossing B6J-XYPOS.Sry129 males (carrying the 129 strain’s Sry gene on a B6J-XYPOS background [11]) with B6N (C57BL/6NCrSlc; Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) XX females for more than 20 generations. Simultaneously, the B6N-XYPOS strain is subsequently maintained by intercrossing B6N-XYPOS mice. Animals were maintained as described elsewhere [19]. Mice with the XYPOS genotype were selected for experiments.

Fetuses at 15.5 dpc were collected immediately after euthanasia under deep isoflurane anesthesia. Neonates were euthanized by decapitation under hypothermia on postnatal day 1 (P1). Adult animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia. Each gonad was macroscopically classified as T, O, or OT as described previously [32]. Individuals were then classified based on their gonadal composition: T (bilateral testes), O (bilateral ovaries), O/T (unilateral ovary and contralateral testis), or OT (at least one ovotestis). The presence of a mating plug was designated as 0.5 dpc. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Permission #29-05-02, #2022-17) and conducted in accordance with the Kobe University Animal Experimental Regulations.

Histological analysis

Gonadal regions from fetuses and neonates were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde containing Zamboni fixative at 4°C for 24 hr. Adult gonadal organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 hr. All specimens were then dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, 5-µm-thick serial horizontal sections, or intermittent continuous horizontal sections at 10 or 25 µm intervals, were cut using a sliding microtome. Sections were mounted on glass slides precoated with 2% 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (Shin-Etsu Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) and stored at −18°C until use.

Immunohistochemical staining was performed as described previously [19]. Primary antibodies included: rabbit polyclonal anti-PAX8 antibody (1:1,600; GeneTex, Arvin, CA, USA), rabbit polyclonal anti-single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) antibody (1:3,200; Cat. No. 18731; Immuno-Biological Laboratories, Fujioka, Japan), rabbit polyclonal anti-SOX9 antibody (1:1,000; Cat. No. sc-20095; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), and goat polyclonal anti-forkhead box L2 (FOXL2) antibody (1:25,000; Cat. No. ab5096; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Secondary antibodies were Dako EnVision+ System-HRP Labeled Polymer Anti-Rabbit (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) or HRP-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (1:200; Cat. No. ab97110; Abcam).

Three-dimensional (3D) analysis

Three-dimensional reconstruction and volume measurement of fetal seminiferous tubules were performed using 5-µm-thick serial sections or intermittent serial sections at 10-µm intervals. For n measured tissue sections, the area of the gonadal region was denoted as S1 to Sn (µm2), and the gonadal volume V (µm3) was calculated using the following formula:

(for 5-µm-thick continuous sections) V=5⋅∑k=1n Sk

(for 10-µm-thick continuous sections) V=10⋅∑k=1n Sk

The stromal ratio was calculated as: (testicular volume ‒ seminiferous tubule volume)/ testicular volume. Fetal seminiferous tubules were 3D reconstructed from these aligned data stacks using the IMOD program (version 5.1; University of Colorado, Boulder, CO, USA; https://bio3d.colorado.edu/imod/).

Analysis of ssDNA-positive cells

Measurements were performed on three sections per individual. Cells exhibiting nuclear ssDNA positivity were designated as ssDNA-positive. Gonads were divided perpendicularly to their long axis into three equal parts. based on their length. Statistical analysis was performed on the number of positive cells in each region, normalized by the total gonadal area across the three sections. ImageJ (1.54; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for area measurement.

Analysis of PAX8-positive cells

Measurements were performed on intermittent serial sections at 25-µm intervals. The dorsoventral spread of PAX8-positive cells was calculated based on the number of sections containing these cells. PAX8-positive cells were defined as those exhibiting PAX8 positivity in either the nucleus or cytoplasm.

Statistical analysis

Normality of distribution was first assessed using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. The Welch t-test or Kruskal‒Wallis test was used as appropriate. For analyses involving ssDNA, a P-value of less than 0.0125 (0.05/4, Kruskal‒Wallis test with Bonferroni correction) was considered statistically significant. For all other analyses (including PAX8), a P-value less than 0.05 (Welch t-test or Kruskal‒Wallis test) was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using BellCurve for Excel (version 4.09; SSRI, Tokyo, Japan).

RESULTS

1) Seminiferous tubules of the B6N-XYPOS (15.5 dpc)

In both wild-type B6N mice and B6N-XYPOS mice exhibiting bilateral testis formation, approximately 11 seminiferous tubules were observed in the superficial gonadal layer, while the central gonadal region lacked seminiferous tubules (Fig. 1A–C). Seminiferous tubules were abundant throughout the entire gonad, not only in the coronal sections, but were distributed primarily within its superficial layer (Fig. 1C). In B6N-XYPOS, only single seminiferous tubule with closed ends, presumed not to open into the rete, was observed in the cranial region of the gonad in a single individual (Fig. 1D).

No significant difference in the total number of ssDNA-positive cells was observed between groups (Fig. 2A). To examine the localization of these cells, gonads were divided craniocaudally into three regions (Fig. 2B). In wild-type B6N, ssDNA-positive cells were largely absent in the cranial region. 3) Rete testis in B6N-XYPOS (15.5 dpc, P1)

At 15.5 dpc, to evaluate the degree of supporting-like cell lineage differentiating into the rete testis, we examined the expression of PAX8, a known marker for this lineage, in wild-type B6N (n=5), B6N-XYPOS (T) (n=7), and B6N-XYPOS (OT) (n=9) individuals. PAX8-positive cells were observed near the testis‒mesonephros border and on the cranial side of the mesonephros in both wild-type B6N (5/5) and B6N-XYPOS (T) (7/7) (Fig. 4A and 4B). Notably, some B6N-XYPOS individuals exhibited a more extensive distribution of positive cells along the dorsoventral axis (Fig. 4C). While the rete testis morphology in many P1 B6N-XYPOS was similar to that of wild-type B6N (Fig. 5A), one B6N-XYPOS individual presented with a dilated lumen (Fig. 5B).

4) Histological analysis of gonads in B6N-XYPOS (15.5 dpc and adult males)

In fetuses, 4 individuals presented with ovotestes, exhibiting both testicular and ovarian regions within the gonads. The ovarian region was located solely in the cranial region (3 individuals), and in both cranial and caudal (1 individual) (Fig. 6A and 6B). In one of the 3 individuals with a cranially located ovarian region, numerous ssDNA-positive cells were detected in both the cranial ovarian region and the caudal testicular region (Fig. 6C). In adults, most individuals developed either testes or ovaries. In ovaries, follicles were sparsely observed (Fig. 6D). Testes contained seminiferous tubules exhibiting spermatogenesis (Fig. 6E). However, 1 adult individual, similar to the fetuses, presented with both testicular and ovarian regions, the latter located cranially (Fig. 6F and 6G).

DISCUSSION

Previous studies suggest that seminiferous tubule formation initiates in the lateral regions of the gonad and is subsequently displaced toward the center by physical pressure [7]. The CD1 strain exhibits 16.8 ± 1.7 seminiferous tubules at 14.5 dpc [7]. In contrast, this study found the number of seminiferous tubules in the B6N strain at 15.5 dpc was approximately 11, and an area without seminiferous tubules was observed in the central region of the testes. These findings suggest two possibilities: First, given the smaller body size of the B6N strain compared to CD1, adult B6N males may naturally possess fewer seminiferous tubules; second, seminiferous tubule differentiation may be delayed in the B6N strain relative to the CD1 strain. If the latter hypothesis holds, potential contributing factors include: 1) delayed Sox9 expression in the B6N strain compared to other strains (e.g., CD1) and 2) higher expression of feminization-related genes in the B6N strain [17]. Notably, Sox9 expression in the B6N-XYPOS strain is delayed by 2‒3 ts compared to the B6N strain [26]. However, the present study found no apparent difference in the number of seminiferous tubules between B6N and B6N-XYPOS strains. This suggests that if differentiation of seminiferous tubules is delayed in the B6N strain, it is more likely associated with the expression levels of feminization-related genes than with Sox9 expression timing. To definitively compare the developmental timing of seminiferous tubule differentiation between the B6N and CD1 strains, a comparison of seminiferous tubule numbers in adult males from both strains is warranted.

Previously, the presence of seminiferous tubules containing only sperm in adult mice suggested incomplete connection between the seminiferous tubules and the rete testis [26]. The identification of seminiferous tubules presumed not connected to the rete testis at E15.5 suggests that this connection was already impaired during the fetal stage. Furthermore, Sry has been reported to induce mesenchymal proliferation and testis cord formation in the gonads [21, 22]. Therefore, delayed Sry expression, potentially leading to reduced mesenchymal proliferation and delayed testis cord formation, could contribute to an impaired connection between the seminiferous tubules and the rete testis. The observed reduction in stromal cell proportion in B6N-XYPOS mice and the difference in the number of ssDNA-positive cells in the cranial region of the gonads of B6N and B6N-XYPOS mice supports this hypothesis. Conversely, while no apparent difference was observed in testicular rete formation between wild-type B6N and B6N-XYPOS, a single P1 B6N-XYPOS individual exhibited a testicular rete with dilated lumens. In the B6N-XYPOS strain, PAX8-positive cells surrounding the fetal rete testis were more widely scattered along the dorsoventral axis compared to the B6N strain. These findings collectively suggest that minor reproductive tract malformations observed during the fetal period may progress to become more severe as development advances.

Rete testis epithelial cells in rodents exhibit endocytic activity and involved in the absorb proteins from the luminal fluid [12, 16]. Furthermore, Sox17-expressing rete testis epithelial cells reportedly support Sertoli cell induction via paracrine signaling, secreting growth factors like RSPO1 and TGFb2 [25]. While no morphological abnormalities were observed in rete testis epithelial cells in the present study, their functional integrity remains to be determined.

Testicular angiogenesis is initiated by Sry expression, and mesenchymal proliferation during this process is reported to drive the segregation of epithelial cells into seminiferous tubule structures [8, 10]. In B6N-XYPOS mice, the stromal proportion was reduced. This reduction is hypothesized to result from delayed Sry expression, leading to insufficient mesenchymal proliferation and subsequent impaired alignment of Sertoli cells compared with the wild type.

Mesonephric endothelial cell migration into the gonads is crucial for the formation of testicular blood vessels and testis cords [9]. However, insufficient endothelial cell migration from the mesonephros to the gonads has been demonstrated in the B6J-XYPOS strain [2]. In the present study, a B6N-XYPOS neonate exhibited red blood cell extravasation into the interstitial region, suggesting similar impairments in endothelial cell migration and/or vascular formation in this strain.

In the B6J-XYPOS strain, ovarian differentiation in the cranial and caudal regions of the gonads has been reported [2, 29]. Additionally, cases of ovarian differentiation confined to the cranial region of the gonads have been reported in humans with gonadal dysgenesis syndrome [27]. In the present study, we also identified areas of ovarian differentiation in the cranial and caudal regions of the gonads in both fetal B6N-XYPOS mice. This finding is consistent with Sry spreading from the central to the cranial and caudal regions of the gonads [5]. Furthermore, adult individuals with gonads possessing distinct testis and ovary regions appeared in the B6N-XYPOS strain.

The Müllerian duct remained open to the urogenital sinus at 15.5 dpc, but 1 individual displayed a closed uterus at P1, suggesting complete Müllerian duct formation with subsequent closure occurring between 15.5 dpc and birth. Degenerated cells observed near the Müllerian duct‒urogenital sinus connection during the fetal period were inferred to be involved; however, the precise mechanism remains elusive. Furthermore, our previous study observed the production of AMH in the fetal OT gonads of 14.5 dpc of B6N-XYPOS mice [31]. Among adult animals, edematous uteri were most frequently observed in O/T individuals. This suggests that regression of the Müllerian duct on the ovarian side, mediated by AMH secreted from Sertoli cells on the contralateral side, was the main factor causing uterine lumen closure. Estradiol reportedly promotes AMH expression in Sertoli cells in prepubertal hermaphroditic individuals [27]. The numerous edematous uteri observed in the O/T individuals of this study suggest that AMH expression promoted by the developed ovaries may have contributed to more pronounced uterine abnormalities. At present, however, no immunohistochemical technique can detect low AMH concentrations in tissues. As the underlying mechanism cannot be investigated directly, this interpretation remains speculative.

Keratinized epithelium, typically found in the vaginal lumen but absent from the uterine lumen, was observed in the caudal uterine lumen. This finding suggests a disorder in the Müllerian duct’s differentiation into the uterus and vagina. Epithelial differentiation in the female reproductive tract is determined by stromal factors [33]. Furthermore, the uterus and vagina have been reported to differentiate based on the expression levels of retinoic acid signaling‒related genes and Hoxa10 in the stroma [18]. Therefore, the edematous uterus likely resulted from the influence of humoral factors within the Müllerian duct during sexual differentiation; however, further investigation is needed to elucidate the precise mechanism.

These results suggest that in B6N-XYPOS individuals with bilateral testes, the connection between the seminiferous tubules and the rete testis was already inadequate during the fetal stage. However, as no other significant abnormalities were observed, the impact of Sry delays on tissue morphology was considered minimal. Furthermore, results from B6N-XYPOS individuals with ovotestes suggest that testicular differentiation is prone to failure at the cranial and caudal poles of the gonad, not only in fetuses but also in adults. Moreover, the present findings indicate that in XYPOS, a model prone to disorders of sex development, Müllerian duct formation is completed not only in individuals with a normal uterus but also in females with an edematous uterus. In these latter individuals, the findings further suggest that duct closure occurred between 15.5 dpc and birth. Many questions remain regarding adult animals exhibiting reproductive tracts discordant with their external phenotype. Although the Wolffian duct is necessary for Müllerian duct formation, further research is needed to understand how these tracts interact during their respective regression or development.
