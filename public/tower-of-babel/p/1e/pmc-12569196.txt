Multiscale drought dataset for the Greater Antilles: a resource for environmental and adaptation studies
Authors: Milica Stojanovic, Rogert Sorí, Albenis Pérez-Alarcón, Raquel Nieto, Luis Gimeno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a multi-drought metric dataset based on the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) for the Greater Antilles, covering Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico and La Española. The SPI was derived from high-resolution (0.1°) Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation (MSWEP) precipitation data for the 1980-2023 period. The MSWEP precipitation and SPI datasets were validated against observational and reanalysis datasets, providing good relationships. Temporal and spatial drought conditions were identified for the SPI temporal scales from 1 to 24 SPI temporal scales, while drought episodes were identified for 1, 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 temporal scales, and characterised according to their duration, severity, affected area, persistence, onset speed, and recovery. A source-sink moisture analysis was also performed to support the attribution of dry conditions. We demonstrate possible uses of the database, and its consistency in representing past drought events that caused severe damage. Therefore, it proves to be a practical tool for understanding the phenomenon of drought in the Caribbean, managing water resources, agricultural development, and many other sectors.Subject terms: Atmospheric science, Natural hazards

Background & Summary

Droughts rank among the most devastating natural disasters, triggering important impacts with a memory effect, from water resources, ecosystems, agriculture, food security, and even human health1–3. In the Caribbean region, the Greater Antilles, comprising Cuba, La Española, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico (Fig. 1), are particularly vulnerable to drought impacts due to their dependence on rainfall for freshwater supply and agriculture1. Socioeconomic activities such as tourism and agriculture, rely heavily on water availability, making the entire region susceptible to climate variability and change4,5. The pronounced seasonal and inter-annual precipitation variability and the occurrence of droughts in the Caribbean, have been largely influenced by large-scale climate drivers such as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO)6,7. Notable drought events that affected individual islands, or the whole Caribbean region, have also been well documented and described in terms of impacts. A major drought event in 1994–1995 greatly diminished the water reservoir levels in Puerto Rico, which induced water rationing and caused approximately US$165 million in agricultural losses8. Drought conditions during 1997 and 1998 also prompted the Jamaican government to order water rationing and assist the agricultural sector with US$100 million to deal with the losses9. The 2009/2010 drought affected a major part of the Caribbean countries10, as well as during the 2015–2017 period11,12, causing serious impacts on agricultural activities and food security.

While drought analyses at national scales have provided crucial knowledge regarding the spatio-temporal variations in drought characteristics, existing global drought datasets13,14 often lack the necessary spatial resolution and temporal extension to effectively resolve island-scale drought variability in the Greater Antilles. Other studies have investigated the occurrence of atmospheric flash drought15, through the Caribbean Evaporative Demand Drought Index datasets, which consider high-resolution evapotranspiration datasets16. However, there exists a lack of drought metrics available for downloading, regarding the characteristics of drought events on individual islands, and a comparison among them. This limitation hinders comprehensive assessments of drought impacts’ mitigation in agricultural, hydrological, environmental, and socioeconomic sectors. Thus, here, we present a reproducible, gridded, multi-metric drought dataset derived from the high-resolution Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation data (MSWEP v2.8)17, covering the Greater Antilles at 0.1° spatial resolution for the period 1980–2023. MSWEP combines gauge observations, satellite data, and reanalysis, ensuring robust performance across tropical regions, including the Greater and Lesser Antilles17,18. Its 0.1° spatial resolution and long temporal coverage make it highly suitable for historical drought reconstruction in small or narrow islands in the Caribbean region.

This dataset includes values of the Standardized Precipitation Indices (SPI) at multiple timescales (from 1 to 24), drought episodes, severity and duration metrics, area affected per island, drought co-occurrence matrices, and hydroclimatic transitions (whiplash events). Additionally, a series of moisture contributions from the principal sources supports the attribution of drought severity and episodes, an approach that has been widely utilised19. The SPI is particularly well-suited for the Greater Antilles due to its robustness, low data requirements, and flexibility across temporal scales20,21. The SPI has been recommended for operational drought monitoring by the World Meteorological Organisation, and consequently, it has been widely adopted for meteorological drought analysis in the Caribbean due to its simplicity and multi-temporal applicability9,22,23. The open availability of the SPI at temporal and high spatial resolution, spanning the entire Greater Antilles, and the associated metrics, provides an opportunity to support new studies for the Caribbean region. Additionally, it could enhance drought predictability, future drought risk assessments, early warning systems, and climate adaptation strategies across agriculture, water resources, and biodiversity, as well as plans for disaster risk management reduction.

Methods

A representation of the workflow used to generate the drought-related datasets from MSWEP precipitation data is shown in Fig. 2. The Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) was computed across spatial and temporal scales (1 to 24 months), yielding two main products: gridded spatial data in NetCDF format and time series data in CSV format. From the temporal SPI time series, drought events were identified, and multiple drought characteristics were derived (e.g., Drought Persistence Index (DPI), Onset Speed Index (OSI), and Drought Episode Recovery (DER)), all provided as CSV files. The source of moisture for each island was identified, and its contribution to precipitation over each island, along with the island’s terrestrial contribution. Their anomalies provide support for understanding the occurrence and severity of drought episodes. All moisture-related outputs were saved in CSV format. This integrated framework enables a comprehensive assessment of drought evolution and its links with moisture source variability across different spatial and temporal contexts.

Input precipitation data

To perform this database, we utilised the MSWEP v2.8 monthly precipitation data18 for the period 1980–2023. This dataset merges gauge-based observations, satellite estimates, and reanalysis data into a global precipitation product at 0.1° (~11 km) spatial resolution and has been shown to perform reliably across the Caribbean region. A study conducted by Centella-Artola et al.24 evaluated the performance of 16 rainfall gridded datasets on a monthly timescale in the Caribbean region, finding that MSWEP achieved the best precipitation representation. Further evaluations highlighted that MSWEP performs well for most statistical metrics and is recommended for most hydro-meteorological research in the Caribbean region18. Based on these findings, MSWEP was selected as the input precipitation dataset for this study.

Standardised precipitation index (SPI) calculation

The SPI20 is used in this study to identify temporarily and spatially dry conditions on time scales from 1 to 24 months. To do it, the first task was calculating the monthly mean precipitation across all grid cells within each island, using a high-resolution mask that matches the spatial resolution of the precipitation dataset. This mask ensured that only grid cells entirely or mostly belonging to each island were included. After spatial averaging, we calculated the SPI time series at the island level using this monthly mean precipitation. The SPI calculation involves fitting long-term precipitation data to a probability distribution, typically the gamma, which is then transformed into a normal distribution. This transformation involves the standardisation of the values, such that the mean SPI value becomes zero with a standard deviation of one, allowing for meaningful comparisons across different regions and time scales.

Based on the multiscalar characteristics of the SPI, we calculated derived drought metrics over particular timescales (1, 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months). These timescales have served as a reference for considering the occurrence of changes in rainfall and their probable impacts on soil moisture content, agriculture, river levels, and groundwater21. Drought event identification and severity metrics

A drought episode is identified when the SPI drops below zero and reaches a minimum threshold of −0.84 or lower. The event is considered to persist as long as the SPI remains negative, and it is deemed to end in the final month before the index returns to non-negative values. A schematic representation of this concept appears in Fig. 3. For each episode, we characterise its duration as the number of consecutive months with negative SPI values, and its severity as the cumulative sum of the absolute SPI values over the entire event. This approach allows for a consistent and quantitative assessment of both the temporal extent and the intensity of drought conditions. The area affected by each episode that occurred on each island was also calculated.

Schematic representation of the onset, duration and end of a drought event.

Drought Episode Recovery (DER). The onset phase is defined as the number of consecutive months during which the SPI decreases from positive values to reach the moderate drought threshold (SPI = –0.84). The peak and recovery phase corresponds to the duration in which the SPI remains negative after crossing the threshold, until it returns to non-negative values. A DER value greater than 1 indicates that the drought persisted longer than it took to develop, whereas a value less than 1 reflects a rapid recovery. This indicator provides insights into the asymmetry of drought episodes’ evolution.

Identification of atmospheric moisture sources for the greater antilles

Global outputs from the Lagrangian particle dispersion model FLEXPARTv9.025, available through the EphysLab group, were utilised to determine the sources of atmospheric moisture for each island, as well as their contribution to precipitation. FLEXPART was run using 6-hour specific humidity and 3D wind data, along with other data utilised for internal model parameterisations26, from 1981 to 2023, based on the ERA-5 reanalysis27 at a horizontal resolution of 0.25° and across 137 vertical levels of the atmosphere28. The model considers the atmosphere to be divided into approximately 30 million particles that are advected by winds, allowing for the determination of net changes in specific humidity at 6-hour intervals (dq/dt) of all particles along the trajectories, assuming them to have an invariant mass (m). where E and P represent the total evaporation and precipitation along the vertical column, over the area A. This approach has been used in several studies to identify sources of moisture and sinks in specific areas or meteorological systems (e.g. Algarra et al.29, Fernández-Alvarez et al.30). A summary of the advantages and disadvantages of the method for this aim, appears in Gimeno et al.31.

A backwards-in-time experiment was conducted to identify the primary moisture sources for the Caribbean region, as shown in Fig. 4. This experiment was based on the mean optimal integration time of Lagrangian precipitation, calculated for the four islands, resulting 8 days for Jamaica, and 7 days for Cuba, La Española and Puerto Rico, respectively. Areas where air masses gain moisture along their trajectories, meaning where evaporation exceeds precipitation, were identified as moisture sources (Fig. 4a). The 99 percentile of mean annual (E-P) > 0 values was used to delimitate the most important source, which is schematically illustrated in Fig. 4b, as the green-blue colour extends across the Caribbean and North Atlantic (NATL). This methodology has been previously applied for similar purposes in several studies (e.g. Gimeno et al.31). Air masses over the NATL and each island individually were tracked forward in time to compute the moisture contribution to precipitation (E-P) < 0 from each region. For the subsequent analyses, we used the absolute value of the moisture balance under negative conditions, |(E–P) < 0|. Although these series do not represent real precipitation, they represent a Lagrangian precipitation32, which explains not only the origins, but also the variability and changes of the total precipitation. Figure 4c highlights the crucial role of oceanic origin precipitation over the islands, which represents more than 80% for all the islands, which is expected for small islands with low evaporation/precipitation recycling, particularly Puerto Rico.

Data Records

This dataset33 includes both gridded and island-aggregated drought products derived from the monthly temporal and spatial SPI. It was calculated using MSWEP precipitation data over the Greater Antilles at 0.1° spatial resolution, for the period 1980–2023, and is available at 10.5281/zenodo.1672603433. SPI was computed for all accumulation periods from 1 to 24 months, covering the period January 1980 to December 2023. The SPI drought index is available for each calendar month for which MSWEP data is available. However, the SPI with accumulation periods larger than 1 month starts with a corresponding delay. E.g., the SPI-12 only starts in January 1981, as the previous 12 months are required for the accumulation.

Technical Validation

Precipitation assessment

To verify the reliability of the input MSWEP precipitation data, it was assessed for each island against the ERA5-Land reanalysis34, Rainfall Estimates from Climate Hazards Center Infrared Precipitation with Stations (CHIRPS v3)35, and high-resolution precipitation data for Cuba (CubaPrec1), obtained from observational data. ERA5-Land is a state-of-the-art high-resolution land-surface reanalysis (with ~9 km grid) known for its improved representation of the hydrological cycle36, and CHIRPS v3 combines satellite-based thermal infrared rainfall estimates with in-situ station observations to produce a 0.05° gridded rainfall over land. Additionally, CubaPrec1 is a database built from 630 stations from the Cuban National Institute of Water Resources. The results show high and statistically significant correlation of the MSWEP precipitation with both, ERA5 Land and CHIRPS for all islands (Fig. 6a,b), with r values predominantly greater than 0.8, confirming a strong temporal relationship. This is consistent with other findings describing these datasets among the top-performing global precipitation products37, particularly for the Caribbean24. Another correlation analysis was conducted between the MSWEP precipitation and CubaPrec138 data for the Island of Cuba, for the 1980–2008 period. The correlation pattern obtained (Fig. 6c), also reveals a great relationship between both datasetss, with the highest values observed in the central and western regions of the country. Although the overall values are slightly lower than those obtained between MSWEP and ERA5 Land and CHIRPS, they remain high and statistically significant across the country. This confirms the validity of the MSWEP precipitation data in reproducing the spatiotemporal variability of precipitation in Cuba and the Greater Antilles.

Cross-validation of SPI across datasets

To evaluate the consistency of the SPI derived from MSWEP precipitation, a correlation analysis comparing it with SPI time series computed from ERA5-Land and CHIRPS v3 precipitation datasets, across multiple accumulation periods (1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months), was conducted (Fig. 7a,b). In addition, we compared the MSWEP-based SPI against the SPI derived from the CubaPrec1 dataset for the 1980–2008 period (Fig. 7c). As shown in Fig. 7a, the SPI derived from MSWEP shows good consistency with ERA5-Land across all islands and time scales, with correlation values generally ranging from 0.71 to 0.88. Correlations between SPI from MSWEP and CHIRPS are very similar and particularly stronger for Puerto Rico, where r values exceed 0.94 at all time scales and reach up to 0.96 at SPI-1. Finally, for Cuba, the correlation values range from r = 0.87 at SPI-1 to r = 0.85 at SPI-24. These results confirm that the temporal behaviour of SPI from MSWEP is consistent with both satellite-based and reanalysis-derived products, as well as with ground-based observations. Overall, this strong agreement across diverse precipitation sources and time scales reinforces the robustness of the SPI calculated from MSWEP for drought assessment in the Caribbean region.

Limitations to consider

The SPI is based solely on precipitation. Therefore, it does not account for evapotranspiration, soil moisture, or other drought drivers. For studies requiring such variables, users are encouraged to compute or complement with indices that involve other parameters.

Usage Notes

This section illustrates potential applications of this dataset. The inclusion of SPI at multiple accumulation timescales (1–24 months) allow users to capture the temporal diversity of drought impacts across different sectors (e.g. water resource management, agricultural planning) in the Caribbean region21. Drought conditions on SPI short-term scales (SPI1 to SPI3) are useful for evaluating rapid vegetation and soil moisture responses, at seasonal scales (SPI6 to SPI12) are relevant for crop planning and water supply strategies, while at long-term time scales (SPI18 to SPI24) are appropriate for reservoir operations, groundwater monitoring, and long-term trend assessments.

The monthly SPI6 and SPI12 time series for Jamaica from 1980 to 2023 shown in Fig. 8, reveal several major drought events, with grey shaded bars marking years identified in the literature as periods of significant drought impacts in this country. Notably, the series capture the severe 1997–1998 drought, described in Campbell39 as an event with significant agricultural losses, amounting to approximately USD 4.7 million. Similarly, the 2014–2015 drought is identified through the SPI-6 and SPI-12. This event caused widespread crop failures and wildfires and was recognised as one of the worst since the 1970s40, causing agricultural losses ranging from USD 7.2 million to USD 9 million. Additionally, both scales identify the prolonged dry conditions of 2019–2020, which were characterised by below-average rainfall, reduced reservoir levels, and restrictions on the use of freshwater41. The complementary statistics of this dataset, DPI, OSI, and DER, can also be used to characterise drought episodes.

Time series of the SPI6 and SPI12 for Jamaica. Identification of moderate/severe/extreme drought conditions and comparison with previous registered drought events.

By visualising these events alongside SPI records, users can better understand how drought severity evolves and aligns with real-world consequences, reinforcing the dataset’s applicability in understanding past hydrometeorological events, as well as supporting climate adaptation strategies for the Greater Antilles.

The panels in Fig. 9 display the temporal evolution of the most severe meteorological drought episodes that affected each island during the study period, and the respective anomalies in the moisture contribution from the NATL source and the local terrestrial extension of each island. The moisture contribution anomalies represent deviations in net moisture export from source regions that contribute to rainfall changes over the islands. Thus, negative anomalies in the moisture contribution from oceanic sources reflect sea surface temperatures and evaporation changes in the source, or altered wind patterns that intensify moisture deficits and should be explored. Additionally, negative terrestrial anomalies in the contribution to precipitation indicate reduced local evapotranspiration, creating a positive feedback that intensifies moisture deficits. It is appreciated as a consistent signal across all islands, with a decline in oceanic but also terrestrial origin moisture contribution to precipitation during drought development, particularly in the months when SPI1 reaches its lowest values. In Cuba (Fig. 9a), a steady decline in the moisture contribution from the NATL anomalies aligns better with a sharp drop in SPI1 from February to May 2004, but also the terrestrial origin anomalies’ moisture contribution, confirming their role as drivers of rainfall decrease. In Jamaica, the anomalies in terrestrial moisture contribution to precipitation follow a pattern of intense negative anomalies that differ from those from oceanic sources, but align with a notable decrease in the SPI1, emphasising the importance of moisture recycling in triggering extreme conditions.

Temporal evolution of the most severe meteorological drought episode for each island according to the SPI1, and the respective anomalies on the moisture contribution to precipitation (E-P < 0) during the most severe meteorological drought episode over each island.

In the episode affecting La Española and Puerto Rico, the anomalies in moisture contribution also followed a temporal evolution very similar to that of the SPI. Overall, the figure highlights the dominant role of reduced North Atlantic moisture supply in the onset and persistence of severe droughts across the Caribbean. Variability in local (terrestrial) contributions further emphasises the spatial heterogeneity in land-surface feedback. Cumulative anomalies from the contribution series on various time scales could be related to the SPI on other time scales. Thus, the moisture contribution series provides insights into the mechanisms driving drought development, and holds significant potential to support modelling efforts and studies on land-atmosphere interactions in the Caribbean.
