Inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap with back-cut for medial canthal reconstruction: A case series
Authors: Sarala Joshi, Anthony Yao, Raman Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The authors present variations of the inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap, augmented with an inferior back-cut, as a versatile option for medial canthal defect reconstruction. A retrospective case series of seven patients with medial canthal basal cell carcinoma, all of whom underwent frozen section-controlled excision followed by immediate reconstruction using this flap technique. Surgical modifications included tailored flap sizing and use of back-cuts to enhance flap mobility. Outcomes included Strasser grading, using post-operative photographs, and complications. All defects were reconstructed successfully, with scars concealed and no major complications. Five tumours were T1a and two were staged T2b, producing defects ranging from 12 × 12 mm to 30 × 18 mm in diameter. These cases demonstrate the flap’s suitability for moderate to large defects that are not amenable to simple techniques such as primary closure. Strasser grading across six patients showed good aesthetic outcomes (scores 0–2) with no cases rated ‘obvious’ or ‘deforming’. This report highlights the versatility of the inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap as a first-line option for medial canthal defects. It provides excellent cosmesis particularly for moderate to large defects in patients with adequate nasal bridge laxity, a natural skin crease inferior to the glabella, and those who wear spectacles.

Introduction

A challenge with reconstruction of the medial canthus following tumour excision is achieving natural cosmesis given the complexity of the medial canthal anatomy and its contours. Lack of skin laxity and the need to avoid webbing often precludes primary closure of defects. Many options have been described, each with their own advantages and drawbacks.

Differences in skin colour between host tissue and skin grafts help explain the preference for local flaps or laissez-faire for medial canthal reconstruction. However, based on differences in skin texture, colour, thickness and histological structure, it is advisable to respect boundaries of aesthetic facial units and limit surgery to within a single unit.1 Popular techniques often stray outside the regional aesthetic facial unit. Glabellar flaps may result in medial displacement of the brow heads and are often poorly matched to the thinner medial canthal skin. Cosmetically obvious loss or bulk in volume in the concavity of the medial canthus, or additional visible periocular flap scars, are notable concerns in medial canthal reconstruction.

The inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap, augmented with an inferior back-cut, is a versatile option for medial canthal defect reconstruction. Despite being described over a decade ago by Sciscio and Joshi,2 it remains underutilised and not widely recognised in routine practice. We present a retrospective case series reporting modifications of this flap highlighting its utility in concealing visible scars behind the nasal bridge of spectacles, and enhancement of flap mobility.

Materials/patients and methods

The authors present a retrospective, single-centre, series of seven cases where variations of an inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap were used to repair the defects following medial canthal basal cell carcinoma (BCC) excision. The surgery was performed by a single surgeon (R.M.). Reconstruction was performed immediately following confirmed clearance using en-face frozen section-controlled excision.3 Data was collected on patient demographics, early and late post-operative complications, and reoperation rate. Standard photographs were taken at every visit. Primary outcome measures included grading of the medial canthal area using the Strasser grading score on standard post-operative photographs. Grading was conducted independently by two of the authors. Secondary outcome measures included any post-operative complications or need for interventions.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local institutional review board. Consent to publish identifiable photographs was obtained and archived.

Surgical technique

The size of the defect was measured with a ruler on the table. Bupivacaine 0.25% and 1 in 200,000 adrenaline (epinephrine) diluted in saline was infiltrated to the surgical site. The flap was hinged at the medial aspect of the defect and marked from the superolateral border. A curvilinear-shaped advancement flap was outlined superiorly across the nasal bridge, ideally following the highest skin crease above the pterion. This placement ensured the flap remained below the thicker skin of the glabellar region and matched the thin skin of the lateral nasal bridge. The flap continued contralaterally, terminating at a horizontal level corresponding to the superolateral defect edge and medial to the contralateral medial canthal tendon. To enhance mobility a back-cut incision was marked extending inferomedially at approximately 60 degrees to the curvilinear flap outline. If needed, this incision could be extended to or beyond the midline during dissection.

A minimum width of 1 cm of the base of the flap pedicle preserved the vascular supply. This theoretically allowed the flap to be up to 4 cm in diameter, based on a maximum 1:4 ratio of flap pedicle to width, and easily mobilised to cover the defect. Thin skin was quilted into the defect to retain the medial canthal concavity.

A No. 15 Bard-Parker blade was used to incise the flap and back-cut in uniform depth into the subcutaneous tissue. Subcutaneous undermining was performed under the base of the flap, beneath the superior edges of the secondary defect and at the primary defect edges to enable tension-free closure. The flap was mobilised and advanced to cover the primary defect and resulted in the entire flap lying over the defect and side of the nasal bridge, as shown in Figure 1. Quilting sutures, using 5–0 poliglecaprone 25 secured the flap to the periosteum of the medial canthal concavity. Skin edges were sutured and the secondary contralateral defect closed vertically with interrupted 6–0 poliglecaprone 25 sutures. Strasser grading

The Strasser grading score is an objective grading system4 to evaluate cosmetic surgical results comprising of five potential flaw categories: malposition, distortion, asymmetry, contour deformity and scar. Each potential flaw is graded: perfect (0 points), noticeable (1 point), obvious (5 points) and obvious and deforming (15 points). Points for each category are totalled and evaluated. A score of 0 has 0 flaws, scores from 1–4 are deemed good, 5–14 are mediocre, and 15 or more are poor.

Tumour staging and defect size

Among the seven patients, five tumours were staged T1a and two were T2b, with defect sizes ranging accordingly. The largest defect measured 30 mm in diameter.

Strasser scores

All patients had scores between 0 and 2 points indicating a good overall aesthetic outcome across all domains (Table 2). None of the patients demonstrated flaws rated as ‘Obvious’ or ‘Obvious and Deforming’ across any category. Follow-up

Patient 4 had a pre-excision web (post-biopsy), which persisted post-operatively and improved after fluorouracil (5-FU) intra-lesional injections.

Discussion

This case series revisits the utility of the inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap,2 augmented with an inferior back-cut, as a versatile and practical approach to reconstructing moderate-sized medial canthal defects. Despite being described over a decade ago, its use remains underrepresented in contemporary textbooks and routine practice. This paper highlights modifications to the technique that minimises visible scars by concealing them behind the nasal bridge of spectacles, making it a valuable first-line option for medial canthal defects not amenable to direct closure or laissez-faire.

Notably, all patients in this series were female. This may influence flap mobility and aesthetic outcome due to differences between male and female skin thickness and laxity. The medial canthal region, being the second most common site for periocular BCCs,5 poses unique reconstructive challenges due to its distinct anatomy and the need to preserve its natural concavity.6 Defects following confirmed tumour clearance are often unpredictable in terms of extent, location and size. In this series, two tumours were staged as T2b and produced defects up to 30 mm in diameter, demonstrating the flap’s reliability in managing moderate to large defect sites. Reconstructive options typically include laissez-faire,7,8 single or multiple local flaps including glabellar, bilobed, rhomboid and pericranial flaps.2,9,10,11 Although several flap techniques can achieve satisfactory outcomes, each carries its own limitations. Glabellar flaps often involve thicker skin which may require secondary debulking to achieve optimal contouring. Laissez-faire is less effective for defects close to the inner canthus.12 The inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap offers distinct advantages by providing thin, pliable skin that closely matches the medial canthal region without necessitating secondary graft sites.

The inclusion of an inferior back-cut significantly enhances the versatility of this flap, enabling the repair of defects up to 2–3 cm while preserving the region’s natural concavity. Additionally, the placement of the transnasal incision along curvilinear creases, high on the nasal bridge and above the pterion, ensures a concealed scar that aligns with the nasal bridge of spectacles, addressing common patient concerns regarding cosmetic outcomes.

Ideal candidates for this technique are patients with medial canthal defects who have sufficient nasal bridge skin and laxity, a natural crease inferior to the glabellar region, and who wear spectacles.

Although a small case series, this study highlights the variations and utility of this underutilised flap technique. The findings demonstrate the flap’s effectiveness not only for smaller tumours but also for moderate to large defects which are not amenable to simple techniques such as primary closure. It achieves excellent functional and aesthetic outcomes, without the need for extensive surgery. Objective assessment using the Strasser scoring system demonstrated that all patients fell within the good outcome range (scoring 0–2 points). Noticeable asymmetry and scarring were observed in some of the cases, whilst all patients had no malposition, contour deformity or distortion.

In conclusion the authors present seven cases where the use of an inferiorly-hinged transnasal advancement flap, augmented with an inferior back-cut allowed repair of medial canthal defects following BCC excision. We advocate for increased awareness and inclusion of this technique in contemporary surgical textbooks to ensure it is considered amongst first-line options for medial canthal defects not amenable to laissez-faire or direct closure in patients with adequate nasal bridge laxity, a suitable skin crease and who wear spectacles.
