Dynamic synchronization and feature region fusion for real time girth weld tracking
Authors: Yi Zhang, Shaojie Wu, Fangjie Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNarrow-gap all-position girth welding of pipelines requires accurate seam tracking to handle spatial complexity and dynamic arc behavior. Since existing methods struggle with signal boundary extraction and real-time control, this study introduces a weld-tracking system that integrates torch oscillation synchronization with current feature-region analysis. Pulse (P) and direction (D) signals were used to dynamically partition the welding current into three regions (left edge, middle, and right edge), which resolved boundary uncertainties. Vertical tracking used a binary linear regression model that correlated the wire feed speed and contact tip-to-work distance (CTWD) with middle-region current. Horizontal tracking used trapezoidal integration to quantify current differences between edge regions. The experimental results showed a steady-state accuracy of 97% and a dynamic response time under 50 ms, achieving a precision of ± 0.10 mm. Cosine-curve displacement control enabled overshoot-free adjustments with steady-state errors below 2.2%. This work enhances automated welding systems by combining dynamic signal synchronization with algorithmic integration, offering a new framework for real-time control in narrow-gap welding applications.Keywords: Automatic welding, Weld horizontal tracking, Weld vertical tracking, Circumferential welding, Narrow gap beveling, Arc sensorsSubject terms: Design, synthesis and processing; Mechanical engineering

Introduction

During the girth welding of long-distance pipelines, seam tracking plays a crucial role in ensuring weld quality due to the complexity of arc control during all-position welding. This process requires precise monitoring and adjustment to maintain alignment between the welding torch and the weld seam. In this context, various sensing and control methods have been developed to improve accuracy. Among them, Park and Moon1developed a voltage-based moving average algorithm for seam tracking that improved torch alignment by smoothing voltage signals to reduce noise. However, its reliance on historical data weighting leads to lagging responses during sudden disturbances and a failure to adapt to nonlinear variations in dynamic arc behavior. To address sidewall fusion defects in narrow-gap gas metal arc welding, Liu et al2. proposed a two-stage tracking algorithm that adjusted the torch swing center using principal component analysis (PCA), which achieved a tracking precision of 0.1 mm. However, its sensitivity to outliers limited its applicability in complex operational conditions. Le J3investigated the input-output mapping of rotating arc stability sensors and revealed the mechanism by which arc stability affected sensing signals, but their model did not address signal partitioning during dynamic oscillation. Lei et al4. developed an arc voltage tracking system based on DC pulse square waves and a two-dimensional fuzzy controller. However, the voltage signal-to-noise ratio degraded during narrow-gap welding due to arc compression effects. Seo et al5. proposed a three-region segmentation method (peak, average, and reference) for welding signals and established a linear relationship between current area differences and lateral deviations. However, static partitioning of feature regions cannot adapt to dynamic torch oscillations. Li et al6. employed a dual-cycle integral difference method to decouple torch height deviation and working angle deviation, but the fixed integration cycles introduced phase errors during variable-speed oscillation. Liu et al7. introduced a narrow-gap pulsed welding tracking method that used current multi-feature fusion with PCA and support vector machines (SVM). The method enhanced the sensitivity at the cost of computational complexity and real-time performance. Hölscher et al8. used contact tip-to-work distance (CTWD) detection during wire arc additive manufacturing, but they did not integrate it with seam tracking control. Kim et al9. optimized arc sensing using current median clustering, but their algorithm exhibited limited adaptability in unstructured environments. Liu et al10. analyzed arc initiation stability during pulsed welding but neglected signal synchronization during dynamic tracking. Lin et al11. developed a variable-gap root-pass tracking method using magnetically-controlled arc sensors, but its reliance on external magnetic fields limited its universality. Gao et al12. designed a gas metal arc welding tracking system for container girders, but did not optimize the algorithms for narrow-gap groove complexity. Chen et al13. modeled rotating arc-based variable-groove narrow-gap weld formation, but the model lacked real-time tracking integration.

The approaches above face two critical limitations, including a dynamic signal boundary partitioning deficiency. Conventional algorithms do not precisely segment current signal feature regions during torch oscillation, leading to cumulative deviation extraction errors. They also face a trade-off between dynamic response and accuracy, as complex feature fusion techniques enhance precision but incur high computational loads incompatible with real-time control requirements. To address these challenges, this study proposes a dynamic synchronization and feature-region fusion system for seam tracking. By integrating pulse (P) and direction (D) signals from the torch stepper motor, welding current is dynamically partitioned into three regions (left edge, middle, and right edge) to resolve the boundary ambiguities of conventional methods. A binary linear regression model was used to link the wire feed speed and CTWD to the middle-region current for vertical tracking, while trapezoidal numerical integration was used to quantify lateral deviations via edge-region current differences for horizontal tracking. By leveraging dynamic signal synchronization and discrete-time system mapping, this framework provides a method for intelligent control during narrow-gap all-position welding.

Method to determine the current characteristic regions

By extracting PD signals from the stepper motor controller, changes in the swing position of the torch within the groove were accurately synchronized with variations in welding current. This synchronization enabled the identification of edge and middle regions during the swing process and monitored the value changes in P and D signals throughout the start-stop-reverse swing cycle of the torch. The corresponding welding current regions were identified based on PD state changes after value modifications. By defining the logical transformation of PD signals “0” and “1”, the condition where P = 0 and D = 1 corresponded to the right-edge region. P = 0 and D = 0 represented the left-edge region. The rest represented the middle region. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the current was divided into three characteristic regions, where the welding current was approximately 200 A, and PD signals were CMOS-level with a 5 V output. These results revealed the relationship between the P signal, D signal, and welding current regions without requiring actual values. The control logic code for the P signal and D signal, as well as the division of intervals for the P signal, D signal, and welding current, are displayed in Fig. 4, wherein the y-axis uses data scaling. The P signal was changed from 5 V to 1 V, the D signal changed from 5 V to 1.5 V, and the original welding current signal data remained unchanged. The scaling ratio was set to 1:100 A to ensure optimal visual representation. Accurate extraction of currents in different regions provided a basis to control vertical and horizontal tracking, supporting further research on tracking algorithms.

Tracking algorithm

Weld vertical tracking algorithm

The weld shape during circumferential welding is significantly influenced by the stability of the welding current, which is directly influenced by the wire feed speed () and contact tip to work distance (CTWD). To precisely control the current, this study combined the characteristic region delineation method (Sect. 3) with an investigation of the effects of the independent and interacting effects of linear velocity and CTWD on the middle-region current. The torch oscillation period during circumferential welding is generally in the range of 2–4 Hz, and the welding process is often accompanied by noise and high-frequency signal interference. To suppress high-frequency noise, we applied a 256-order finite impulse response (FIR) low-pass filter to smooth the main component of the current signal. The middle-region current data were extracted using the characteristic region determination method proposed in Sect. 3.

Using 30 sets of experimental data (Table 3), we established a binary linear regression model to quantify the linear contributions of Vf and CTWD to the middle region current (Imiddle). The experimental conditions included wire feed speeds of 8.0–12.0 m/min and CTWD values of 8.0–13.0 mm. The mean values under each condition were statistically analyzed to optimize the control strategy.

Residual distribution characteristics

Residuals (observed – predicted values) exhibited a uniform dispersion across the entire range of predicted welding currents, with no significant heteroscedasticity (e.g., funnel or fan patterns). This indicated minimal systematic model error, confirming that robust regression mitigated outlier effects. The absence of trend-like deviations verified the structural validity of the current prediction model.

Probability plot characteristics

Blue cross-markers closely aligned with the red reference line (R² ≈ 1), demonstrating residual normality, which is a prerequisite for reliable statistical inference. This demonstrates error sources were random noise (e.g., measurement jitter or environmental disturbances) rather than missing variables or model misspecification. Thus, a statistical foundation was established for subsequent parameter optimization experiments.

Welding current contour characteristics

Parameter coupling: when CTWD < 9 mm, the dense current gradients indicated a high sensitivity (> 20 A current jump per 1.0 m/min wire speed increase). Dominance shift: At CTWD > 10 mm, the extended contact tip-to-work distance significantly suppressed the current and became the dominant factor. Compensation effect: a wire speed > 10 m/min partially counteracted the influence of CTWD. Synergistic optimum: the green zone near CTWD = 10 mm represents the preferred process window with optimal controllability.

To further validate the statistical reliability of the model, normality and homoscedasticity tests were performed on the regression residuals using the Shapiro-Wilk test and the Breusch-Pagan test, respectively. The normality test results indicated a residual sample size of n = 30, a test statistic W of 0.981, and a p-value of 0.851, which is greater than the significance level of 0.05. This supports the acceptance of the null hypothesis that the residuals are normally distributed. Similarly, the homoscedasticity test yielded a χ² value of 2.19 with a p-value of 0.335, also exceeding the 0.05 threshold, thus confirming that the residuals exhibit homoscedasticity. Together, these results provide further evidence for the statistical validity of the model.

In order to further evaluate the performance of the model, an independent verification test was conducted using 10 additional datasets that had not been used in the initial experiments. The accuracy of the model was assessed based on these datasets, and the corresponding results are presented in Table 4.

Weld horizontal tracking algorithms

Horizontal tracking aims to dynamically detect and compensate for lateral deviations in the oscillating torch from the weld centerline. This section details the algorithmic framework, experimental validation, and critical insights derived from analysis of the edge-region current differences. The algorithmic framework is detailed in the following section.

This step ensured the extraction of stable main components from the left-edge and right-edge regions (Sect. 3). The results showed that ± 1.0 A provided an optimal trade-off between FTR (3.2%) and MDR (2.8%) and minimized unnecessary adjustments while ensuring timely correction of significant deviations. Changes in tracking performance mechanisms, including changes in the tracking accuracy at different positions in Fig. 7, were due to physical effects caused by gravity. During horizontal welding, the molten pool drooped, causing asymmetric heat transfer to the side walls, which amplified the random fluctuations in Idiff (standard deviation: −0.32 A and − 0.14 compared with 0.18 A and 0.19 during flat welding). This increased the probability that |Idiff| exceeded ± 1 A under non-offset conditions, thus increasing the likelihood of false triggering. During vertical welding, the flow direction of the molten pool accelerated downward, generating arc fluctuations, which reduced the signal-to-noise ratio of Idiff compared with the flat welding position. This made quantifying deviations during trapezoidal integration more complex, highlighting the importance of threshold selection, which affects the discrimination of tracking execution. Calculating the real-time performance of tracking algorithms

Vertical tracking algorithm

The binary linear regression model (Eq. 1) requires three multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations per sample. At a sampling rate of 12.5 kHz, this resulted in a computational load of 37.5 kFLOPS (12,500 samples/s × 3 FLOPS), which is negligible for modern CPUs.

Horizontal tracking algorithm

Trapezoidal integration (Eq. 3) has O(n) complexity, where n is the number of samples per oscillation cycle. For a typical 50 ms cycle at 12.5 kHz, n = 625 samples. The operation requires two MACs per sample (summing and averaging), totaling 1,250 MACs per cycle (15.625 MFLOPS).

Verification experiment and result analysis

The experimental platform used a high-speed camera mounted perpendicular to the welding cart’s travel direction for vertical tracking tests and parallel for horizontal tracking tests. As the cart moved, the camera continuously monitored the torch’s position, while a data acquisition system recorded relevant signals. For vertical tracking verification, 1 mm-high prefabricated obstacles were placed along the circumferential weld to observe CTWD adjustments. Horizontal tracking was validated using a narrow-gap V-bevel with upper and lower face angles of 5° and 45°, respectively, and a lower opening width of 4.4 ± 0.2 mm. A displacement sensor was installed on the transverse cross-slide to measure swing position signals.

Circumferential welding from 0 to 6 o’clock was divided into twelve 15° zones, each of which was welded using the same parameters but different orientations, i.e., flat, vertical, or overhead (Fig. 10). The sensor measurement uncertainty was calibrated per GB/T 28,854 − 2012, where the welding current sensor (Hall effect, 1:100 scaling) had an uncertainty of ± 0.03 A (k = 2), and the displacement sensor had an uncertainty of ± 0.02 mm (k = 2), where k = 2 corresponds to a 95.45% confidence level.

Experimental verification of vertical torch tracking (Fig. 11a) demonstrated the system’s ability to dynamically respond to obstacles. Upon encountering an obstacle, CTWD shortened rapidly (Fig. 11a-1), and the current decreased and then recovered as CTWD normalized (Fig. 11a-2). When crossing an obstacle, CTWD extended suddenly (Fig. 11a-3), which reduced the current. The torch adjusted downward, increasing the current back to normal, and CTWD was restored (Fig. 11a-4). At one sampling point, the adjustment height was 0.2 mm with an 80 ms oscillation time (Fig. 11b). The torch traveled 1.50 mm per oscillation period (Fig. 11b-4), confirming its real-time performance. The theoretical and actual CTWD trajectories matched closely (Fig. 11c-1), with tracking errors within ± 0.1 mm (Fig. 11c-2), indicating a high accuracy and stability. The system exhibited fast, precise, and overshoot-free vertical tracking, providing reliable control for ring weld quality.

Experiments were conducted using the above process under ambient temperatures ranging from 0 °C to 30 °C. Experiments must be carried out for validation for low-temperature and high-temperature environments. Temperature fluctuations may cause slight drifts in data acquisition and execution. Real-time FIR filtering suppressed high-frequency signals and noise, keeping the signal-to-noise ratio degradation below 2%. Electromagnetic interference from arcs was eliminated through shielded wiring, which ensured the sensor’s stability.

Table 10 shows that, based on referenced data under potentially different test conditions, the proposed dynamic synchronization and feature fusion method has advantages across multiple key metrics. This includes a reduction in its dynamic response time to < 50 ms, a 67% improvement over the multi-feature PCA + SVM method (150 ms, Ref. 7) because dynamic signal partitioning minimizes phase errors from cycle variations in static methods (e.g., Ref. 5). The tracking accuracy reached ± 0.10 mm, outperforming that of Ref. 2 (± 0.15 mm) and Ref. 18 (± 0.20 mm) because trapezoidal integration reduced arc compression noise during narrow-gap welding. The steady-state accuracy reached 97% (vs. 85% in Ref. 1 and 94% in Ref. 17), as supported by a binary linear regression model (R² = 0.99) that captured coupling between linear velocity and CTWD.

The applicability of this method was verified only for narrow-gap V-groove welding and not general grooves (e.g., Ref. 1) or variable gaps (e.g., Ref. 11) due to geometric sensitivity during feature partitioning. The 256th -order FIR filter suppressed high-frequency noise, but it may have also increased hardware resource use in embedded systems, thus requiring a balance between precision and efficiency.

This tracking system has been deployed in multiple long-distance pipeline projects, including the Sichuan Gas East Transmission Line Phase II and the East Africa Crude Oil Export Pipeline, accumulating experience from over 200,000 circumferential welds. After operating routinely for 8 h per day with an average output of approximately 20 welds daily, it has demonstrated long-term stability and a first-time pass rate exceeding 95%. This provides practical evidence of its reliability under long-term working conditions.

Although validated only on X80 steel with a narrow-gap 5° V-groove, the following parameter adjustment guide is provided so that the method can be applied to other materials (e.g., X70/X65) or groove types (U/J-grooves). For different materials, the heat input should be adjusted based on their carbon equivalent (CET), considering compatibility between the pipe material and welding consumables. For alternate groove geometries, the number of weld layers and the oscillation width should be modified according to the groove angle and root radius to ensure compatibility with the tracking algorithm and avoid step loss. Dimensionless scaling based on groove size is recommended for initial parameter estimation, followed by real-time manual corrections. Validation using small-scale specimens, non-destructive testing, and microstructural analysis is advised before full application.

This study synergistically optimized the real-time performance and precision in narrow-gap, all-position welding using dynamic signal synchronization and algorithmic fusion. The innovation was mainly the coupling mechanism between dynamic feature region partitioning and discrete system mapping. Future work should focus on developing lightweight models to reduce computational complexity for industrial edge devices, and extending validation to U/J-groove configurations for method generalizability. Additional work may also include integrating digital twins for closed-loop simulation optimization to enhance the system’s robustness.

Discussion

This study proposed a dynamic synchronization and feature zone fusion weld seam tracking method, specifically developed and validated for X80 pipeline steel, 5° narrow-gap compound V-groove geometry, and all-position girth welding. The core elements of the method exhibit scenario specificity. Dynamic feature zone partitioning relied on the real-time synchronization of specific P/D signals from the welding torch stepper motor and the welding current. The validity of this partitioning logic was strictly established based on the groove geometry and the molten pool behavior of X80 steel. The coefficients of the vertical tracking model binary linear regression model (β0 = 74.61, β1 = 21.39, β2 = −6.234) were fitted using experimental data within the specific material, groove geometry, and process parameter ranges of Vf = 8.0–12.0 m/min and CTWD = 8.0–13.0 mm. Based on this, we quantified the relationship between Vf, CTWD, and the intermediate zone current (Imiddle) under these specific conditions. The horizontal tracking threshold current difference threshold triggering lateral adjustment (|Idiff| > 1 A) was empirically determined by analyzing the characteristics of current difference distributions across different welding positions (flat, vertical, or overhead) and weld layers within the specific X80 steel groove. This was significantly influenced by gravity and groove width variations. The cosine curve displacement control strategy was optimized for the actuation system used in this setup.

The direct applicability of this method is confined to scenarios involving long-distance pipeline all-position girth welding with similar material properties (e.g., X80 pipeline steel or similar grades) and similar narrow-gap V-groove geometries (~ 5° compound V-groove). For other scenarios, the core components of this method (dynamic partitioning logic, model parameters, and empirical thresholds) may require recalibration or fundamental modifications because their effectiveness has not been validated within this study.

Future research should explore the potential of applying this method to other material systems (e.g., different high-strength steels) with similar groove geometries. Efforts should also focus on establishing more generalized physical models to guide parameter adaptation. For applications with substantially different geometries (e.g., U/J-grooves), it may be necessary to develop new feature zone definitions or sensing strategies.

Conclusions

This study presented a dynamic synchronization and feature-region fusion framework to address critical challenges in narrow-gap all-position girth welding. Using dynamic feature-region partitioning, the integration of pulse (P) and direction (D) signals enabled the real-time segmentation of welding current into three characteristic regions (left-edge, middle, and right-edge). This eliminated phase errors caused by static partitioning. This method also enhanced boundary clarity and reduced the signal processing latency by 67% compared with conventional approaches. The precision tracking algorithms during vertical tracking were used to develop a binary linear regression model that established a coupling relationship between wire feed speed, CTWD, and middle-region current (R² = 0.99), which achieved a 97% steady-state accuracy. During horizontal tracking, trapezoidal numerical integration quantified lateral deviations via edge-region current differences, achieving ± 0.10 mm tracking precision. Real-time performance was achieved using a cosine-curve displacement control strategy to ensure overshoot-free adjustments with dynamic response times < 50 ms and steady-state errors < 2.2%. Experimental validation confirmed the system’s robustness across flat, vertical, and overhead welding positions. According to GB/T 31,032 − 202314, this method was applicable to narrow-gap V-grooves during automatic welding for long-distance oil/gas pipelines and a groove angle range of 5–7°. Limitations of this method include material incompatibility with non-conductive materials such as polymers or highly reflective metals such as aluminum, as well as process instability, such as scenarios involving significant dynamic groove width variations.
