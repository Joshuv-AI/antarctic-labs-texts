Challenges in developing academic surgery in a Ukrainian university clinic
Authors: Sergei Zemskov
Published: 2024

Bogomolets National Medical University (BNMU) was first established as a Medical Faculty of Kyiv University of St. Volodymyr of Russian Empire in 1841 and is one of the longest standing medical school facilities in Ukraine. A few years later the University Clinic was built for the needs of medical education. However, in 1920s Bolshevicks changed everything including the medical education system and the medical faculty was united with the School of Dentistry and Women Medical Institute to become Kiev Medical Institute (KMI), later named Oleksandr Bogomolets. Ironically the enlarged Institute left without the Clinic with plenty of its Clinical Departments spread around city hospitals. In 1990s (times of Ukraine Independence) KMI turned into BNMU and only in early January 2021 with departments still spread, BNMU was granted a small clinic (100 beds) by the Ministry of Health of Ukraine. The building was established in 1913, and local renovations are still ongoing. One of the primary general purposes of the clinic was the development of surgical oncology, with an emphasis on minimal invasive approaches. Staffing was as follows: 3 general surgeons, 2 OR nurses for 3 operating tables. Two more ORs and a sterilizing unit were established and surgeons-consultants started practicing and training in ophthalmology, otolaryngology, urology, gynecology, maxilla-facial and abdominal surgery.

However, the Russian invasion in February 2022 brought significant changes to our policy, forcing us to broaden our focus to include the basics of military surgery, trauma care, and reconstructive surgery.

From the very first months of the war, when Kyiv was under threat, the clinic was engaged in the management of wounded combatants, who filled about 90 % of our surgical capacity. Eighty percent of these cases involved extremity trauma, many presenting with massive soft tissue defects due to the high kinetic energy of shells and combustion. A smaller number of wounded had severe complex trauma associated with multidrug or pan-resistant sepsis, requiring constant multidisciplinary management, which incurred high costs and resulted in high mortality rates or severe disabilities. Most patients developed PTSD, leading us to shift our focus towards trauma care and reconstructive surgery. One of the disappointing challenges was the undertraining of our plastic surgeons, who were primarily focused on aesthetic surgery before the war. Consequently, flap plastic techniques became a skill that our general and trauma surgeons began to master.

Later in 2022, the situation on the frontline changed as Russian forces were pushed back from the north of Ukraine. However, our clinic maintained around 60 % of its surgical capacity to continue managing evacuated combatants. First aid, damage control, and triage are conducted at stabilization points close to the frontline. Depending on geographical location, the wounded are then evacuated to large Ukrainian cities such as Kharkiv, Kherson, and Dnipro, where hospitals are filled to capacity. The rest of the patients are moved further to the rear, including to Kyiv. Our clinic receives new battlefield-related admissions at least twice a week.

Currently, the population of the Kyiv agglomeration has returned to pre-war levels (3.5–5 million people) due to the return of locals and the translocation of inhabitants from southern and western regions where hostilities are ongoing. Approximately 40 % of our surgical capacity is now dedicated to civilians, allowing us to gradually proceed with the development of minimal invasive surgical oncology. One of our latest achievements was the organization of a mini-pig class in our veterinary lab. However, we had to split the training between endoscopists and laparoscopic surgeons because of blackout. The endoscopists started on Friday morning and were careful enough with their mucosa resections and hemostasis techniques to keep the animal stable for a few hours until the sudden blackout. Animals were then successfully extubated and supported until Monday afternoon for laparoscopy. The chiefs of Endoscopy and Surgery Departments mentored staff surgeons and residents for TEPP, TAPP, cholecystectomy, gastric resection, intracorporal suturing, etc. The animal was sacrificed afterwards according to bioethics committee protocol.

Reflecting on almost two and a half years of operation at our university clinic, we can say that we have significantly broadened the spectrum of surgical pathology and corresponding skills for our so far small number of students (24) and residents (27). The crisis has presented us with opportunities. Another very important aspect worth mentioning is the humanitarian aid we received from the first days of the war, including surgical instruments, equipment, and disposables, from our partners in Germany, Japan, the US (Allegheny Health Network), and many others. We could not have achieved this without such effective international support.

In summary, before the war, there was a strong social demand for family doctors in Ukraine, which shaped the main goals of medical education prior to postgraduate specialization. However, the existential necessity of the current situation dictates changes to the education plan. We now need to train doctors capable of practicing both family and military medicine. This challenge may not be unique to Ukraine. Given the recent increase in security and defense costs in the European Community, along with the unstable situations in the Middle East and Pacific, the inclusion of military medicine basics in medical school curricula may become a global trend. Future surgical training should encompass knowledge and skills in casualty triage and damage control and reconstructive techniques.
