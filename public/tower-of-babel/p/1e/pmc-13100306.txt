Experiences of Social Support Among Chinese Pregnant Women of Advanced Maternal Age: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Jing‐ping Yang, Lin Zhang, Feng Xue, Qiao‐xian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to explore the experiences of social support among pregnant women of advanced maternal age.DesignQualitative descriptive design with a phenomenological orientation.MethodsData were collected via face‐to‐face, semi‐structured interviews with 21 pregnant women aged ≥ 35 years and analysed using Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis.ResultsThree themes and nine sub‐themes emerged: (1) providers of support. Spouses were the primary supporters, while important others (friends/colleagues) provided substantial aid; however, support from extended family (parents/parents‐in‐law) and medical staff was perceived as limited. (2) Types of support. Emotional and instrumental support were deemed adequate, whereas information support was insufficient. (3) Support outcomes. Social support facilitated the development of positive health behaviours and helped alleviate negative emotions during pregnancy.ConclusionsThis study highlights the critical role of social support in the care of Chinese women of advanced maternal age. Social support should be regarded as a key aspect of perinatal care, and providing greater and more effective support may benefit women in developing positive coping strategies. Additionally, nurses and midwives should offer more personalised support services during the perinatal period.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

Advanced maternal age (AMA) is typically defined as being 35 years or older at the time of delivery (Kim et al. 2022; Zipori et al. 2020). In recent decades, a global trend toward delayed childbearing has emerged. The shift is driven by a confluence of factors, including the pursuit of higher education and demanding careers, financial instability, the availability of effective contraception, advancements in assisted reproductive technology, and changing relationship dynamics (Johnson and Tough 2012; Laopaiboon et al. 2014; Shams et al. 2021). A World Health Organization (WHO) multi‐country survey of 359 health facilities across 29 countries found that the prevalence of advanced maternal age among pregnant women was 12.3% (Laopaiboon et al. 2014). Similarly, in Finland, 19.1% (n = 135,371) of the 707,728 women who gave birth between 2004 and 2015 were aged 35 or older (Roustaei et al. 2022). In China, this trend is also pronounced; the mean maternal age at childbirth increased from 27.1 years in 2010 to 29.7 years in 2017, with the proportion of women aged 35 or older rising from 4.3% to 15.8% between 2010 and 2022 (Cao et al. 2022).

Advanced maternal age is widely recognised as a risk factor associated with elevated risk of obstetric complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes. For expectant mothers aged 35 years or older, potential maternal complications encompass gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational hypertension, premature birth and stillbirth (Kahveci et al. 2018; Lu et al. 2022; Zipori et al. 2020). Similarly, potential adverse fetal outcomes include intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), small for gestational age (SGA), low birth weight, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and perinatal mortality (Elci et al. 2022; Glick et al. 2021). Furthermore, certain studies have suggested that older pregnant women may experience psychological disadvantages related to their age, with findings indicating higher rates of postpartum depressive symptoms and stress compared to younger mothers. Notably, a delayed onset of these symptoms has been observed in older mothers compared to their younger counterparts (Attali and Yogev 2021; McMahon et al. 2015; Mori et al. 2015). However, the existing literature on mental health outcomes in this population remains inconclusive. While McMahon et al. (2011) reported psychological advantages among older pregnant women, including lower levels of depression and anxiety during pregnancy compared to younger counterparts, other studies highlight the potential for increased psychological burden. The mental health implications of advanced maternal age are complex and may be mitigated by factors such as enhanced social support, higher educational attainment, advanced parenting skills, and socioeconomic advantages (Attali and Yogev 2021; Bornstein et al. 2006; Stapleton et al. 2012; Viau et al. 2002).

Social support, which encompasses instrumental, informational, affiliative, and emotional assistance derived from an individual's social network, assumes paramount significance during critical life transitions, particularly during pregnancy, where it exerts a profound influence on maternal and neonatal well‐being (Berkman et al. 2000). Accumulating evidence indicates that insufficient social support during gestation is a notable risk factor for adverse health outcomes affecting pregnant women, fetuses, and infants (Bedaso et al. 2021; Degirmenci and Vefikulucay 2020). Furthermore, numerous studies have underscored the pivotal role of social support as a buffering mechanism against stressors and a catalyst for improved health behaviours, thereby serving as a protective factor for maternal and neonatal health (Mitchell et al. 2022; Orr 2004; Thoits 2011). Notably, a study by Zhong et al. (2018) demonstrated that limited access to social support resources was significantly associated with elevated depression risks during both the initial and follow‐up stages of late pregnancy. This finding is corroborated by a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis, which established a robust association between low social support and heightened mental health risks during pregnancy (Bedaso et al. 2021). Collectively, these insights underscore the imperative to prioritise social support as a cornerstone of comprehensive maternal care during the perinatal period.

Given the elevated health risks associated with advanced maternal age and the pivotal role of social support in maternal well‐being, it is imperative to systematically characterise and deeply explore the status of social support among pregnant women of advanced maternal age. Furthermore, elucidating their individualised experiences with social support can facilitate healthcare professionals in devising targeted interventions to optimise perinatal healthcare services. However, specialised studies focusing on social support for this population remain scant in the existing literature. This study aims to delve into the prenatal social support experiences of pregnant women of advanced maternal age, specifically focusing on the following dimensions: (a) the composition of social support providers; and (b) the types of received support versus unmet needs for support. By elucidating the unique patterns and gaps in social support provision for this vulnerable group, this study seeks to provide empirical evidence to inform the development of tailored support systems, thereby addressing current research deficiencies and guiding clinical practice.

Research Question

How do pregnant women of advanced maternal age perceive social support?

Methods

Design

This study employed a descriptive study informed by a phenomenological orientation to systematically elucidate the experiences of social support among Chinese pregnant women of advanced maternal age. Grounded in naturalistic inquiry (Sandelowski 2000), this approach facilitates direct descriptions of participants' lived experiences while elucidating their subjective perceptions and meaning constructions of social support.

Study Setting

This study was conducted in Guangzhou, a subprovincial city and the provincial capital of Guangdong Province in southeastern China, which has a population of approximately 18 million. Pregnant participants were recruited from a large tertiary teaching hospital affiliated to a leading medical university, with an annual birth volume exceeding 4000 deliveries.

Participants

A purposive sampling approach was employed to recruit participants. Recruitment was conducted by clinical nursing specialists from the participating departments, who were not members of the core research team but had been briefed on the study's inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised pregnant women: (i) with singleton pregnancies, (ii) aged ≥ 35 years, (iii) in the third trimester of pregnancy, and (iv) without health complications at enrollment. Exclusion criteria included women: (i) with a history of mental illness or severe organ dysfunction (e.g., cardiac, pulmonary, or renal impairment), (ii) with a history of psychotropic substance or alcohol abuse, or (iii) admitted to the labor and delivery unit. The sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation, with data collection conducted concurrently with analysis until thematic saturation was reached, indicating no emergence of new concepts and cessation of further recruitment. A total of 34 eligible women were approached; however, seven declined participation, five were unable to engage due to scheduling conflicts, and one withdrew consent for data use following the interview. Ultimately, 21 participants were included in the final analysis. Data Collection Method

Data collection was conducted through in‐person interviews with participants between March and April 2019. The research team comprised three qualified registered nurses trained in qualitative research methods and a Professor of Nursing with extensive experience in qualitative inquiry. All team members were female. Interviews were exclusively conducted by a single trained qualitative researcher. The study involved one‐on‐one individual interviews held in a dedicated space within the nursing staff lounge, which ensured a comfortable, quiet environment for participants to communicate freely. Interview duration was flexible to allow for thorough discussion. The semi‐structured interviews lasted between 40 and 60 min each. To preserve the authenticity and confidentiality of the data, only pregnant participants were included, excluding family members or companions.

The interview guide was developed by an obstetrician, a head obstetric nurse, and a midwife, and pilot‐tested with two pregnant women; the results are presented in Table 1. The researchers had no close relationships with or conflicts of interest regarding the participants and had completed a training course in qualitative research methodology. Interviews were audio‐recorded, anonymised, and transcribed verbatim. Interview techniques such as rhetorical questions, repetition, and follow‐up questions were incorporated as appropriate to obtain more comprehensive information. In addition, participants' nonverbal cues and the interviewers' reflections were recorded as field notes.

Data Analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h of each interview. The data were analysed using Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis method (Braun and Clarke 2006). Step 1: The data were read repeatedly and initial ideas were recorded. Step 2: repetitive research content was coded based on initial ideas. Step 3: themes were identified. Step 4: themes were checked for relevance to the whole dataset. Step 5: clear definitions and names were generated for each theme; and Step 6: an analysis report was produced. After transcribing and analysing the interview data, participants were contacted to verify the reliability of the analysis results. All 21 participants were involved in the verification process; 15 were contacted face‐to‐face and 6 by telephone.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the study hospital. As this study only involved interviews, the hospital's Institutional Review Board (IRB) granted a fast‐track review without assigning a reference number. The study complied with the provisions of the 1995 Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in Edinburgh in 2000). Eligible participants were recruited at the Department of Obstetrics outpatient clinic of the study hospital. After providing written informed consent, each participant completed a self‐developed questionnaire on general demographic information. All interviews were conducted by the same researcher.

Theme 1: Providers of Support

Theoretically, family members (such as spouses, parents or parents‐in‐law), significant others (such as close friends and colleagues), and professional staff (such as healthcare providers) are the main sources of social support for pregnant women, playing crucial roles in the support care system for older pregnant women. In practice, however, the reality of support provision often diverged from theoretical expectations.

Facing a high‐risk pregnancy due to advanced maternal age, the majority of participants reported that they were more likely to feel stressed than the younger pregnant women and that they paid closer attention to their lifestyle to prevent adverse effects on fetal health. Therefore, social support was very important to them.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to deepen our understanding of social support among pregnant Chinese women of advanced maternal age. To this end, we constructed a conceptual framework of social support comprising three components: support providers, types of support, and support outcomes.

Family relationships, particularly marital relationships, are among the most intimate relationships for pregnant women in China. In this study, we found that husbands were the primary providers of support for pregnant women. Support, especially from one's spouse, plays a crucial role in the transition to motherhood (Baheiraei et al. 2012), helping expectant mothers avoid feeling overwhelmed and allowing them more time to care for their babies. In other words, the spouse serves as the main source of emotional and instrumental support for older pregnant women. In China, a pregnant woman's mother or mother‐in‐law typically serves as the primary caregiver during the perinatal period, assisting her in adjusting to motherhood and managing domestic duties (Wan et al. 2009). However, in our study, the parents or parents‐in‐law of older pregnant women were already elderly and unable to provide help; moreover, the pregnant women sometimes had to assist their own parents, resulting in a lack of family support. Consequently, a gap exists in the traditional family support system. This finding suggests that additional social support should be provided to older pregnant women to compensate for this shortfall in family support.

In addition to spousal support, other forms of informal social support, such as that from friends and colleagues, also play an important role during the prenatal period. In our study, nearly all pregnant women considered support from friends and colleagues to be crucial. Pregnant women of advanced maternal age primarily sought emotional support from friends, through whom they could discuss and release negative emotions. Colleagues, meanwhile, provided valuable assistance in the workplace. Previous studies have shown that informal support can, in some cases, benefit obstetric, maternal, and child outcomes (Herbell and Zauszniewski 2019; McLeish and Redshaw 2015; Viaux et al. 2020). Our findings further underscore the important role of informal support—such as support from friends and colleagues—in the social support network for older pregnant women.

Community support has a positive impact on mothers' emotional health, reducing anxiety, unhappiness and stress, while enhancing self‐esteem and self‐efficacy (McLeish and Redshaw 2019). However, community support was not mentioned in this study. One possible reason is that the commercialisation of housing has led to a lack of familiarity—whether geographic, kinship‐based, or work‐related—among residents living in the same community. Moreover, in China, interactions with neighbours from diverse backgrounds and the functionality of community services remain far from adequate in terms of content, procedures, and effectiveness.

Pregnant women tend to rely on health workers for reproductive health advice and information, trusting in the expertise they acquire through training and education. However, this study found that several pregnant women still experienced a lack of health‐related information support, a finding consistent with previous studies (Fonseca and Canavarro 2017; Guy et al. 2014). One possible reason is the high patient volume in China, which leaves medical staff with limited time. Another possible reason is that medical staff may not pay sufficient attention to the needs of pregnant women that fall outside the scope of medical problems. These findings suggest that curricula for public health workers should be evidence‐based with regard to social support.

In addition, the older pregnant women in this study reported that government support was insufficient. In fact, the government provides substantial support to meet the basic health care need of pregnant women. However, according to the findings of this study, pregnant women encountered numerous difficulties and obstacles when attempting to access these benefits. These results suggest that government management departments should clarify the implementation process for delivering such benefits to avoid information gaps and better safeguard the lawful rights and interests of pregnant women of advanced maternal age.

This study found that social support is beneficial for pregnant women in developing positive health behaviours and improving maternal and infant health, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. The more social support pregnant women perceive, the more likely they are to engage in various health behaviours, such as exercising, maintaining a balanced diet, practicing safe sex, and seeking medical care (Cannella 2006). According to a recent study (Tang et al. 2023), social support can influence pregnant women's self‐management behaviours through changes in health literacy. Our study also confirmed the positive effects of social support on the mental health of pregnant women of advanced maternal age. Numerous studies have shown that social support is beneficial for alleviating poor mood during pregnancy. A cross‐sectional study of 246 pregnant women in the third trimester revealed that social support helps to reduce psychological stress in pregnant women (Olabisi et al. 2024). Furthermore, social support is a modifiable risk factor for depression, and a longitudinal study found that the level of social support perceived by pregnant women changes throughout the perinatal period, serving as an important buffer against depression at each stage (Li et al. 2017). Based on our findings, social support systems should be fully mobilised to promote the health of pregnant women.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, while purposive sampling was utilised, the recruitment of participants from a single tertiary hospital in Guangzhou may limit the transferability of the findings to women in other geographical or cultural settings within China. Second, although data saturation was achieved, the reliance on face‐to‐face interviews may have introduced social desirability bias. Participants might have provided responses they believed were socially acceptable rather than expressing their true feelings, which could have influenced the thematic framework developed in this study. Third, it is important to acknowledge the temporal context of the data. The data were collected in 2019, prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic. The pandemic has since significantly altered healthcare delivery and social interaction patterns. Therefore, the current social support experiences of older pregnant women may differ from those described in this study, and the findings should be interpreted within this historical context.

Conclusion

This study aimed to understand the individual experiences of older pregnant women and to identify their social support needs in relation to health care. Our findings indicate that formal social support systems do not play a dominant role for pregnant women of advanced maternal age, while informal social support networks have certain limitations. We recommend the development of additional supportive measures for this population.

Based on these findings, we constructed a conceptual framework of social support to assist pregnant women in developing effective strategies. More importantly, health care professionals need to recognise the significance of social support for pregnant women of advanced maternal age. Accordingly, nurses and midwives should provide support in a professional manner, strive to build trusting relationships with pregnant women, and encourage them to express their views when needed. Finally, medical personnel should offer more personalised support to women during this vulnerable period.
