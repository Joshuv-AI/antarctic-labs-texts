Association Between the Test-and-Treat Strategy and the Severity of Cervical Dysplastic Lesions Among Women Living With HIV in Tanzania (2015-2023)
Authors: Pooja S. Yerram, Chacha Josiah Mwita, Julius Mwaiselage, Batya Elul, Amr S. Soliman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSECervical cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Tanzania where HIV exacerbates the risk of cancer and dysplasia. In 2017, Tanzania adopted the Test-and-Treat program for patients with HIV, which mandates immediate antiretroviral therapy for people living with HIV, regardless of CD4 count. This study examined the impact of this strategy on the severity of cervical dysplasia among women living with HIV (WLWH) at the Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI).METHODSWe used existing data of women who came to the ORCI cervical cancer early detection clinic between 2015 and 2023 for cervical cancer early detection. Of the 3,385 women screened, 1,686 were diagnosed with dysplastic lesions and included in the analysis. This subset consisted of 349 WLWH, 605 HIV-negative women, and 732 women with unknown HIV status. The remaining women either were visual inspection with acetic acid–negative or had suspected cervical cancer and were not included in the final analysis of dysplastic lesions. The year 2017 was chosen as a pivotal point for analysis because it marked the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy at the ORCI. The severity of dysplasia before and after 2017 was compared using trend and logistic regression analyses.RESULTSThe Test-and-Treat strategy was associated with a significant increase in detecting small/moderate lesions (P < .0001). The odds of being diagnosed with small/moderate lesions versus large lesions were approximately four times higher post-2017 (odds ratio, 3.972 [95% CI, 2.462 to 6.409]).CONCLUSIONThe Test-and-Treat strategy has significantly reduced the severity of cervical dysplasia among WLWH at the ORCI, highlighting the importance of integrating HIV treatment into cervical cancer prevention programs. Continuous research focusing on the long-term effects of the Test-and-Treat strategy and expansion of on-site pathology services, including timely histopathologic diagnosis, are essential to further reduce cervical cancer incidence, morbidity, and mortality among WLWH.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer is the leading cancer-related mortality cause among women in Tanzania. The high burden of cervical cancer in Tanzania reflects the broader trends in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where the dual burden of HIV and cervical cancer disproportionately affects women. Women living with HIV (WLWH) have a 4- to 8-fold higher risk of cervical cancer compared with their HIV-negative counterparts.1,2

The Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, is the country's primary referral center for cervical cancer screening and treatment. Among screened women at the ORCI, a substantial proportion is HIV-positive, highlighting the critical intersection of HIV care and cervical cancer prevention. Early detection of dysplastic lesions through visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) and management with cryotherapy or the loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) are key in preventing progression to invasive cancer.2

Beyond HIV, several factors influence the severity of cervical dysplasia in WLWH. Immunosuppression, particularly CD4+ T-cell depletion, is a key determinant of cervical disease progression as lower CD4 counts are associated with an increased likelihood of HPV persistence and integration into the host genome.3 Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has been shown to reduce the prevalence of high-risk HPV and decrease the incidence and progression of high-grade cervical lesions.3 Highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) use has been shown to reduce the incidence and progression of cervical lesions in HIV-positive women, lowering the risk of lesion progression by 45%, with the strongest protective effect observed in those with CD4 counts below 350 cells/mm3.4 In addition, research indicated that HAART-treated women have a lower prevalence of high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia although advanced immunosuppression remains a key risk factor for severe lesions regardless of ART status.5 These findings highlight the importance of early ART initiation and routine cervical screening in WLWH.

Chronic HIV-related inflammation may activate oncogenic pathways, such as PI3K-AKT signaling, which can further promote lesion progression and cervical cancer development, even in patients receiving ART.6 Socioeconomic factors, coinfections, and health care access disparities may also contribute to variations in cervical dysplasia severity in WLWH.7

In 2017, Tanzania adopted the Test-and-Treat strategy, following global guidelines recommending immediate ART initiation for people living with HIV (PLWH), regardless of CD4 count.8 This policy shift aimed to improve immune function and reduce HIV-related morbidity and mortality. Given the impact of HIV on cervical disease progression, it is critical to assess whether early ART initiation has influenced the severity of cervical dysplastic lesions among WLWH. Previous research suggests that ART can significantly decrease the risk of cervical lesion progression,4 but the impact of Tanzania's Test-and-Treat policy on dysplasia severity remains unclear. This study examined trends in cervical dysplasia severity before and after the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy at the ORCI from 2015 to 2023 to determine whether early ART initiation was associated with a shift toward less severe lesions among WLWH.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This is a retrospective cohort study of women who attended the ORCI cervical cancer early detection clinic between 2015 and 2023. The clinic has been operational since 2002, offering screening, diagnosis, and treatment of precancerous and cancerous lesions. Throughout this study period, cervical cancer screening at the ORCI remained consistent despite changes in ART initiation strategies. Before 2017, Tanzania followed the Test and Observe approach, in which ART was initiated based on CD4 count thresholds. After 2017, Tanzania adopted the Test-and-Treat policy, which recommended immediate ART initiation regardless of CD4 count. These policy changes are central to our analysis as we aimed to assess trends in cervical dysplasia severity before and after the adoption of the Test-and-Treat strategy.

ORCI's cervical cancer screening, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment followed a standardized protocol for all patients, regardless of HIV status. Women underwent VIA screening by the same trained clinical staff, followed by colposcopic evaluation when indicated. Data collection procedures remained consistent across all time points, with patient information recorded in hospital logbooks or electronic medical records (EMRs). HIV-positive, HIV-negative, and HIV-unknown women were screened in the same clinical setting, using the same diagnostic criteria, and their data were recorded by the same personnel.

The interval between screening, diagnosis, and treatment was comparable across all groups. For patients who are HIV-positive, immediate ART initiation after an HIV-positive test result was introduced in 2017 under the Test-and-Treat policy, replacing the previous Test and Observe approach. This shift aimed to improve immune function and potentially reduce HPV persistence. However, cervical cancer screening remained independent of ART status, ensuring that screening and diagnostic pathways were not influenced by HIV status.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study included all women who attended the ORCI cervical cancer early detection clinic between January 2015 and December 2023. A total of 3,385 women underwent screening for cervical dysplasia and suspected cervical cancer during this period. Among them, 1,686 women were diagnosed with dysplastic lesions (categorized as small/moderate or large) and were included in the final analysis. VIA-negative and suspected cancers were excluded from the study.

Among the 1,686 women included, 349 were HIV-positive, 605 were HIV-negative, and 732 had an unknown HIV status. To assess the impact of the Test-and-Treat strategy, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by HIV status. The year 2017 was selected as the pivotal time point for comparison, marking the national shift to immediate ART initiation. We categorized the data into three distinct time periods—2015-2017 (pre–Test and Treat), 2018-2020, and 2021-2023 (post–Test and Treat)—to evaluate trends in lesion severity over time. In addition, lesion severity trends among HIV-negative and HIV-unknown women were examined to determine whether broader changes in screening practices influenced our findings.

Data Sources

The study data were obtained from multiple ORCI sources (Fig 1). Initially, patient information was documented in hospital paper logbooks, whereas HIV status was maintained separately in an electronic database. Over time, ORCI transitioned to an EMR system and incorporated standardized logbooks provided by the Tanzanian Ministry of Health, which included both cervical cancer– and HIV-related data.

To ensure comprehensive data collection, records from multiple sources were matched using patient names and examination dates, creating a unified data set with demographic and clinical information for all patients who visited the ORCI cervical cancer early detection clinic during the study period.

Lesion Severity Classification

Cervical dysplastic lesions were classified as either small/moderate lesions or large lesions based on clinical assessment and colposcopic evaluation by medical providers. These classifications were explicitly recorded in patients’ medical records following the standardized protocol used at the ORCI cervical cancer early detection clinic. This approach ensured consistency in lesion size determination across the data set.

Indicators of Immune Competency

While individual CD4 counts and viral load data were not consistently available, HIV status was systematically recorded as positive, negative, or unknown at the time of screening. Given the shift to Test and Treat in 2017, we assumed that HIV-positive women diagnosed post-2017 were more likely to have initiated ART earlier in their disease course than those diagnosed pre-2017. This assumption aligns with evidence linking early ART initiation to improved immune recovery.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

The examination date was simplified to the calendar year 2015-2023, and HIV status was classified as positive, negative, or unknown. Education level was categorized as none, primary, secondary, or tertiary (college/university). Employment was grouped as unemployed, employed, self-employed, or other. VIA-positive test results were classified as small/moderate lesions or large lesions. Treatment was categorized into no treatment needed, not done, antibiotic, cryotherapy, cold coagulation, LEEP, conization, radiotherapy, RT + chemotherapy, not known, and others.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS OnDemand for Academics, version 9.4 TS1M8 (9.4 M8). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics, with categorical variables reported as frequencies and percentages. Fisher's exact test assessed associations between categorical variables, ensuring valid P values. Temporal trends in lesion severity were evaluated using the Cochran-Armitage Trend Test, assessing changes in proportions of small/moderate versus large lesions from 2015 to 2023.

To further assess whether the observed changes in the HIV-positive group were attributable to implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy or reflective of broader trends in clinical practice, additional analyses were conducted in the HIV-negative and HIV-unknown groups. For HIV-negative individuals, a chi-square test and logistic regression analysis were performed to compare proportions of small/moderate versus large lesions before and after 2017. For the HIV-unknown group, because of the absence of pre-2017 observations, Fisher's exact test and chi-square tests were used to examine yearly trends post-2017, assessing shifts in distribution of small/moderate and large lesions over time.

To assess the impact of the Test-and-Treat strategy on lesion distribution, we performed an ordinal logistic regression analysis with lesion size as the dependent variable (small/moderate v large). The primary independent variable was time period (pre-2017 v post-2017), representing the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy.

To control for potential confounders, we adjusted for age and place of residence in the model. Odds ratios and 95% CIs were reported to assess the association between these factors and lesion distribution. Age was included as a continuous variable to account for its potential influence on dysplasia progression. The place of residence addressed regional health care access and screening practices' differences.

Results were reported as odds ratios with 95% CIs, with statistical significance set at P < .05. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Columbia University (New York, NY) and ORCI's Ethics/Human Subjects Committee (reference No. ORCI/ED/2024).

RESULTS

Among 788 WLWH, 29.06% had small/moderate lesions, 15.23% had large lesions, and 55.46% was suspected to have cervical cancer. Among the 1,403 HIV-negative women, 29.86% had small/moderate lesions, 13.26% had large lesions, and 56.59% was suspected to have cervical cancer. Among the 1,194 women with unknown HIV status, 39.45% had small/moderate lesions, 21.86% had large lesions, and 36.01% was suspected to have cervical cancer. The lesion severity differences (proportion of small/moderate v large lesions) and the proportion of women suspected of having cervical cancer across the three HIV status groups were statistically significant (P < .0001). When comparing only HIV-positive and HIV-negative women, the difference in lesion severity was not statistically significant (P = .2460), suggesting that lesion severity patterns between these two groups were similar. Fisher's exact test (P = .2501) confirmed this lack of significance. While WLWH had a slightly higher proportion of large lesions compared with HIV-negative women, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 1).

As expected, treatment type varied by lesion severity, with a significantly higher proportion of women with small/moderate lesions undergoing cryotherapy (59.3%) compared with those with large lesions, who were more likely to receive LEEP (25.4%) or cryotherapy (29.9%; P < .0001). While these differences align with standard clinical management, they also reflect resource availability and treatment accessibility at the ORCI.

Small/moderate lesions increased significantly over time, from 42.86% in 2015-2016 to 82.05% in 2023, whereas large lesions decreased from 57.14% to 17.95% over the same period. The Cochran-Armitage Trend test confirmed a statistically significant shift in lesion severity over time (P = .0029), suggesting a temporal trend in detecting less severe lesions among WLWH post-2017 (Table 3).

Among HIV-negative women, no statistically significant shift in the proportion of small/moderate versus large lesions was observed pre- and post-2017 (P = .0663), suggesting that the observed trend in WLWH was unlikely due to general improvements in screening practices or awareness.

For HIV-unknown women, Fisher's exact test (P = .0049) indicated a statistically significant shift in small/moderate and large lesions post-2017 distribution. However, the number of HIV-unknown patients declined sharply after 2020 (n = 1 in both 2022 and 2023), likely because of improved HIV testing and documentation. Given the small sample sizes in later years, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

The odds ratio (OR, 3.972 [95% CI, 2.462 to 6.409]) indicated that WLWH post-2017 were nearly four times more likely to have small/moderate lesions than large lesions, compared with those pre-2017. The observed shift in lesion size post-2017 is more likely attributable to early ART initiation rather than HIV status alone. Logistic regression analysis in HIV-negative women did not reveal significant differences pre- and post-2017 (OR, 1.793 [95% CI, 0.961 to 3.345]; P = .0663; Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of the Test-and-Treat program for WLWH attending the cervical cancer early detection clinic at the ORCI. Our findings suggest that beyond improving HIV outcomes, early ART initiation may also play a significant role in reducing the severity of cervical dysplasia, a critical precursor to cervical cancer.

First, the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy in 2017 was associated with a significant reduction in severity of cervical dysplasia among WLWH. The observed trend in severity indicates that early ART initiation might have improved immune function and reduced cervical dysplasia progression to more severe forms. Previous studies showed the benefits of early ART initiation in improving overall health outcomes and reducing mortality among PLWH.8,9 The reduction in lesion severity in our study suggests that the Test-and-Treat strategy could have played a crucial role in cervical cancer prevention among WLWH in Tanzania.

Second, among HIV-negative women, we did not observe a statistically significant shift in lesion severity post-2017. Among HIV-unknown women, data were more limited, particularly post-2017. However, the available data suggest that the trends in lesion severity did not mirror those observed in WLWH.

Third, the increase in the detection of small/moderate lesions post-2017 supports the effectiveness of the Test-and-Treat strategy in preventing the progression of cervical dysplasia to more severe forms. This finding aligns with the predictions made by mathematical models and clinical trials, such as the START and TEMPRANO trials, which demonstrated the benefits of widespread ART coverage on HIV-related morbidity and mortality.9 The consistency of our findings with these models and trials highlights the Test-and-Treat implications beyond HIV management, extending to reducing cervical cancer incidence.

Finally, the successful implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy at the ORCI highlights the critical role of early ART initiation in managing not only HIV but also its associated comorbidities, such as cervical dysplasia. Our findings support continuous scaling up of this strategy, particularly in regions with high HIV prevalence, as a means of reducing cervical cancer burden.

These findings reinforce the importance of integrating HIV care into cervical cancer prevention efforts in Tanzania and similar settings. While ART has historically been framed as an HIV treatment, our results suggest that it may also serve as a preventive tool for cervical cancer progression among WLWH.

A key study strength is its comprehensive examination of a large, real-world population over 9 years, allowing for assessing trends and impact of the Test-and-Treat strategy in a resource-limited setting. In addition, the availability of both HIV and cervical early detection clinic data from the same hospital enhances the data robustness by minimizing variability that could arise from using data from multiple institutions. The use of a standardized protocol for measuring the size of cervical lesions also ensured data collection consistency.

A study limitation is the large proportion of individuals classified as HIV-unknown prior to 2017, which declined significantly after implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy. This shift is likely due to increased HIV testing and linkage to care outside of the ORCI, resulting in fewer individuals with unknown HIV status post-2017.

Another limitation is the lack of systematically recorded HIV treatment data, particularly for individuals who might have received ART outside the ORCI. While HIV status was systematically recorded, ART adherence data were also unavailable, making it difficult to determine the extent to which immune recovery influenced lesion severity. Given that the Test-and-Treat strategy aimed to improve immune function through earlier ART initiation, it is possible that lesion severity trends among HIV-positive individuals were partially influenced by ART timing. Future studies should integrate ART adherence metrics, viral load suppression data, and longitudinal CD4 count measurements to better assess the relationship between HIV treatment, immune competency, and dysplasia progression.

Future studies should integrate ART-related variables to better understand their impact on dysplasia progression. Furthermore, while our results demonstrate a statistically significant association between the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy and a reduction in lesion severity among HIV-positive women, causality cannot be definitively established. Other factors, such as improved screening awareness, evolving clinical practices, and potential selection biases, might have also contributed to the observed trends. Incorporating additional clinical and behavioral data, such as ART adherence and duration, can clarify the mechanisms underlying this association and determine the extent to which the Test-and-Treat strategy directly influenced lesion severity reductions.

In conclusion, the implementation of the Test-and-Treat strategy in Tanzania has significantly contributed to downstaging cervical dysplasia among WLWH at the ORCI. This study demonstrated that early and consistent ART initiation, as advocated by the Test-and-Treat strategy, is associated with a marked cervical dysplasia severity reduction. The increase in the detection of small/moderate lesions and the corresponding decrease in large lesions post-2017 suggest the program effectiveness through improved immune function and potentially mitigating progression to invasive cervical cancer.

These findings underscore the importance of integrating HIV treatment with cervical cancer early detection, particularly in regions with high HIV prevalence and the importance of scaling up this strategy across SSA.

Future research should explore the long-term outcomes of the Test-and-Treat strategy, including its impact on cervical cancer incidence and mortality among WLWH. Addressing the challenges associated with ART adherence and access to care in rural areas will be essential for maximizing benefits of this strategy and ensuring that all women, regardless of location, can benefit from timely and complete treatment.
