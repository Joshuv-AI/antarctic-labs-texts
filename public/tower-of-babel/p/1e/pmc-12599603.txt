Test–Retest Reliability of Perception Threshold Estimation in Small Cutaneous Nerve Fibers: A Methodological Investigation
Authors: Helena Brøckner, Sarah Nygaard Jensen, Kristian Hennings, Carsten Dahl Mørch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsNerve excitability testing of small cutaneous nerve fibers has been proposed as a new tool for investigating the underlying mechanisms of small fiber neuropathy. These assessments may be performed using perception threshold tracking with pin‐electrodes that preferentially activate small cutaneous nerve fibers. In this study, the test–retest reliability of two perception threshold (PT) algorithms: the Psi method and the Method of Limits (MoL), was compared.MethodTwenty healthy subjects completed the study. Electrical stimulation was applied using a pin electrode, and the PT to a 1 ms rectangular pulse was estimated six times during two sessions separated by 1 week. The test–retest reliability was evaluated by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and the coefficient of variation (CV). The two PT algorithms were compared by limits of agreement (LoA).ResultsThe ICC indicated better within‐session reliability (Psi: 0.77 and MoL: 0.81) than between‐session reliability (Psi: 0.58 and MoL: 0.50) regardless of the algorithm used. The PTs were significantly lower when measured with the MoL (0.88 mA) compared to the estimates by the Psi (1.12 mA) method.DiscussionThe estimation of PT can be reliably assessed, especially within‐session, with both the MoL and Psi methods. This allows for PT tracking, where PTs to different electrical stimulation provide an estimation of nerve fiber excitability. PT values obtained using different methods are not directly comparable.

Introduction

Pain sensation and sensory loss are common symptoms of small fiber neuropathy and emerge from damage to, and degeneration of, the slow‐conducting unmyelinated C‐ and thinly myelinated Aδ‐fibers, causing impaired nerve function in the affected areas [1]. Standard nerve conduction studies cannot detect abnormalities of small nerve fibers [1, 2, 3, 4]. Since activation of small nerve fibers is challenging, it is important to investigate the validity and reliability of emerging assessment methods.

The perception threshold tracking (PTT) technique has been developed by our group to perform nerve excitability testing (NET) of small cutaneous nerve fibers [5, 6], utilizing pin‐electrodes [7], which preferentially activate small nerve fibers and assess the perception threshold (PT) as an indirect measure of peripheral nerve fiber activation. In previous studies, PTT has demonstrated promising outcomes for the evaluation of both chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy and diabetic peripheral neuropathy [8, 9].

NET provides estimates of nerve fiber properties such as the strength‐duration time‐constant [10] and the ability of the nerve fiber to accommodate to sub‐threshold input [11, 12]. This requires assessing the relation between the PT and different pulse shapes. Small within‐session variation of successive PTs is therefore essential for the accuracy of NET using the PTT technique.

Previous studies using the PTT techniques have often applied the commonly used Method of Limits (MoL), which is a staircase procedure [13, 14]. The step size of the increments and decrements in intensity is a tradeoff between the accuracy of the estimated PT and the number of pulses, and thus the time needed to estimate the individual PT. Alternatively, the Psi method fits the psychometric function to the prior responses and calculates the expected entropy of a range of stimulus intensities. Entropy is a measure of the uncertainty related to the PT and the slope of the psychometric function. The Psi method selects the stimulus intensity with the lowest entropy for the subsequent stimulation [13, 14]. It is expected that the Psi method optimizes the precision of the estimated PT. This study aimed to estimate the test–retest reliability of PTs evoked by small cutaneous nerve fibers and to compare the reliabilities of the MoL and the Psi methods.

Methods

Healthy participants were recruited through posters at the university campus. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Research ethics committee, Northern Jutland, Denmark, approval number: N‐20190055) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. All participants gave written informed consent before participating in the study. Exclusion criteria were: skin diseases, conditions possibly leading to neuropathy, consumption of pain‐relieving medication, alcohol, or nicotine within the previous 24 h, and addiction or prior addiction to cannabis, opioids, or other drugs.

Experimental Design

The study consisted of two experimental sessions performed at the same time of the day, with a 7‐day interval between sessions. The two sessions consisted of two sub‐sessions: one estimating the PT with the Psi method, and one estimating the PT with the MoL method. The order of the sub‐sessions was randomized for each participant (Figure S1).

Experimental Setup

The subjects were seated in an upright position on an inclined hospital bed throughout the sessions, with the left leg resting on a pillow for comfort. Electrical stimulations were applied to the skin 5 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus (S1 dermatome) on the left leg with a cutaneous pin‐electrode (Center for Sensory Motor‐Interaction, Aalborg University) [7]. The pin‐electrode consisted of a concentric stainless‐steel anode with an area of 8.8 mm2 and a cathode consisting of 16 blunt stainless‐steel pins with a diameter of 0.2 mm placed in a circle. To ensure sufficient contact, a stable position, and low impedance, the skin was shaved, wiped with an alcohol tissue, and the pin‐electrode was fixed with adhesive tape to the skin. An electrical stimulator (DS5, Digitimer Ltd., Letchworth Garden City, UK) produced a 1 ms rectangular electrical current and was controlled by a protocol in LabBench (Inventors' Way, Aalborg, Denmark). The subjects pressed a handheld response button to indicate their perception of the electrical stimulations. The temperature of the subject's ankle was monitored by a handheld infrared thermometer and kept at a temperature between 30°C and 34°C by covering the foot with a blanket if the temperature dropped below 31°C.

Estimation of PTs

Two brief series of electrical stimulation were performed prior to the first experimental sub‐session for familiarizing the subjects with the sensation of low amplitude electrical stimulation. All stimuli were administered with an inter‐stimulus interval of 2 s. The MoL included four repetitions of the following staircase procedure: stimuli intensities were increased until the subject perceived three consecutive stimuli. Hereafter, the stimulation intensity was decreased until the subject no longer perceived the stimulation, indicated by three consecutive negative responses. The stimulation was increased or decreased by 20% during the first repetition. After each repetition, the step size was decreased (Figure S2A). The first of the four repetitions was discarded, and the PT was estimated as the weighted average of the three remaining repetitions, with the weight being inversely proportional to the percent‐wise step size from the repetitions. Prior to the first trial using the Psi method, a short series with increasing stimulation intensities was performed to roughly estimate the PT. The Psi method calculates the PT (α) and the slope (β) as parameters of the psychometric function by fitting the obtained responses to an s‐shaped function. Subsequently, the Psi method selects the stimulus intensity of the following stimulation based on the responses to all prior stimulations. For the Psi method, the lapse rate (λ) was set to 0.02, the guess rate (γ) was set to 0.0, and the maximum stimulus intensity was predefined as four times the roughly estimated PT. The lapse rate is the percentage of stimulations that a participant did not indicate as perceived, disregarding the stimulation intensity. Lapses may be due to inattention or inadequate motor responses, among other reasons. The guess rate is the percentage of times the participants respond without having perceived the stimuli. Moreover, 50 stimulations were given with the Psi method to estimate each PT, and the PT was quantified as the intensity at which the electrical stimulation was perceived in 50% of the stimulations (Figure S2B,C). Additional details of the Psi Method have been published [14, 15, 16].

Data Analysis

An estimation of the appropriate sample size was performed based on a previous study by Hennings et al. [6], who assessed the variation of the PT with the pin‐electrode using the MoL. They reported an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.64. Based on that study and a possible increased reliability by using the Psi method, we estimated an ICC of 0.7 and selected a desired confidence interval of 0.3 and the number of replicates to be 12 (six in each session). The sample size estimation was based on the principles presented by Shoukri, Asyali, and Donner [17] and therefore 20 healthy subjects were recruited to ensure sufficient power in case of dropouts. Reproducibility

The relative reliability was assessed by a single‐measurement, two‐way mixed‐effects, absolute agreement ICC model [18, 19]. The absolute reliability was quantified as the coefficient of variation (CV): CV=SDμ·100 where SD represents the within‐subject standard deviation and μ the subject mean of the PTs. The between‐session bias was assessed by a paired t‐test.

Method Comparison

The PTs estimated by the two algorithms were compared by a Bland–Altman analysis. The differences between the PT assessed by the two methods were not normally distributed; thus, the ratio rather than the difference was used in the Bland–Altman analysis. Therefore, 95% limits of agreement (LoA), the bias, including the 95% confidence interval of the bias, and a paired t‐test were calculated.

Habituation

The degree of within‐session habituation to the repeated electrical stimulations was evaluated by calculating the slope between the estimated PTs within each of the two experimental sessions using linear regression [20]. The slope of the linear regression line was used to estimate the habituation, and a repeated measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) was conducted to compare the degree of habituation between the PTT techniques. The within‐subject factors were session (1 or 2) and PT procedure (MoL or Psi). Paired t‐tests and Bonferroni corrections were performed to evaluate interactions between sessions and the PTT techniques.

Numbers of Stimuli

Decreasing the number of stimuli to assess the PT will make the method faster, but more inaccurate. The PT was recalculated using the initial stimuli, from 1 to 50 stimuli, for all assessed PT with the Psi method. Each PT was normalized to the mean of the six PTs obtained by the Psi method. The mean and SD of each person's PT at each session were plotted against the number of pulses used to calculate the PT. The convergence of the PTs was inspected.

All statistical calculations were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and MATLAB version 9.6 (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified, and a p‐value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subjects

Twenty healthy subjects (nine females and 11 males between 21 and 30 years of age, with an average of 24 years). All 20 participants completed both sessions of the study.

Reproducibility

The results showed similar within‐ and between‐session reproducibility of the two techniques for both the absolute and relative reliability. Overall, the within‐session reliability was better than the between‐session reliability. Comparison of the 95% confidence intervals from the reliability measures for the MoL and Psi method showed overlapping intervals, indicative of similar reliability of the methods. Method Comparison

The PTs were significantly lower when measured with the MoL (0.88 mA) compared to the estimates by the Psi (1.12 mA) method (Bias = 0.24 [0.18–0.30] mA; p < 0.001, one‐sample t‐test). The 95% LoA between the ratio of the two PT algorithms was 33% and ranged from 0.39 to 2.28, and the Bland–Altman plot is depicted in Figure 1. This indicated that the difference between the PTs measured by the two methods was less than a factor of 2.5.

Habituation

The PT increased by 0.07 mA/trial on average for each of the six repeated trials. This indicated a habituation of the PT between each assessment trial (Figure 3). When using the Psi method, the PT increased by 0.14 ± 0.14 mA/trial in the first session and 0.09 ± 0.11 mA/trial in the second session. When using the MOL, PT increased by 0.03 ± 0.11 mA/trial in the first session and 0.06 ± 0.07 mA/trial in the second session. The increase was higher when using the Psi method than the MOL method (p = 0.008), but there was no difference between sessions (p = 0.74).

The average perception threshold (PT) increased during both session 1 and 2. The PT were higher when assessed by the Psi method than when assessed by the Method of Limits. Error bars: ±1 standard error of mean.

Discussion

The Psi and MoL methods both showed better within‐session than between‐session reliability for PT assessment to small fiber electrical stimulation. Though the reliability of the two methods was similar, PTs assessed by the two methods cannot be compared directly, as the Psi method estimated a 33% higher PT value on average. Repeated estimation of the PT showed habituation as an increase in PT of 0.07 mA/trial. It is therefore desirable to use as few stimuli as possible for PT estimation. The Psi method may use as few as 37 stimuli to estimate the PT, whereas the MoL used 78 stimuli. The Psi method can therefore estimate the PT with fewer stimuli than the MoL, thus minimizing the time needed to perform the estimation and the risk of habituation. The Psi method thus appeared more suitable for estimating PT to small fiber electrical stimulation.

Reliability of PT Estimation

This study found better ICC values of relative reliability of PT of small nerve fibers assessed by the MoL compared to a study by Hennings et al. [6]. Other implementations of the MoL algorithm for small fibers have resulted in better between‐session reliability or worse reliability [21] compared to the present study. The particular implementation is therefore important for the reliability of the PT estimation.

The within‐session reliability is important for excitability measures using PTT as it relies on the relation between PT estimated to different electrical stimulation pulse forms. Both the strength‐duration time‐constant [5, 6] and the accommodation to slowly increasing stimuli [12, 22, 23] have been shown to differ between small and large sensory nerve fibers. However, the between‐session reliability of the strength‐duration time‐constant has been shown to be poor in healthy participants, although the reliability of the PT was acceptable [21].

The between‐session CV of almost 30% indicated that the assessment of small fiber PT will only be able to discriminate between normal thresholds and small fiber neuropathy if neuropathy causes substantial increased PT. The rheobase is a neurophysiological measure of nerve fiber excitability and is defined as the current needed to activate a nerve fiber using an infinitely long pulse [6, 10]. The rheobase is found by establishing the strength‐duration relation between the duration of a rectangular stimulation pulse and the activation threshold. More pragmatically, the rheobase can be considered proportional to the activation threshold longer than the time constant of the strength‐duration relation. Therefore, the rheobase may be compared to the PT and has been compared between healthy persons and persons with type 1 diabetes [24]. Compared to controls, the rheobase was double in persons without neuropathy, five times higher in persons with pain‐free neuropathy, and seven times higher in persons with painful neuropathy. These findings are based on medians and can therefore not be interpreted as discriminatory ability of the PT. A further study in the same cohort showed good agreement between the rheobase and the corneal nerve fiber length assessed by corneal confocal microscopy [9].

Habituation was observed as an increased PT across the six repeated assessment trials in each session. Habituation has also been observed in using electrical stimulation [20, 25, 26], and previous studies found that small nerve fibers are more prone to habituation than large nerve fibers [20, 25]. Reducing the number of stimuli for each estimation of the PT is therefore important to minimize the effects of habituation on the reproducibility. Similarly, a study by Klit et al. [16] reported that 30 stimulations with the Psi method were sufficient for an acceptable PT estimation in patients with diabetes. Future directions to limit the impact of habituation on the estimated PTs may include a reduction in the number of stimulations; thus, a fast estimation would be preferred in a clinical setting to optimize time but also to reduce habituation. In addition, another approach might be to quantify the degree of habituation and thereby to adjust for habituation in clinical situations for which the within‐session reproducibility is important.

This study found higher PTs than previous studies [5, 6], likely because electrical stimulation was applied proximal to the lateral malleolus, whereas previous studies applied the stimulation to the volar forearm [27, 28]. However, the placement at the distal leg is more appropriate in a clinical setting since length‐dependent neuropathies are the most common [1, 2].

Limitations of the PT Estimation

The estimated PTs using the Psi method were higher than PTs obtained with the MoL. The difference may be explained by the two different principles used for the calculation of the PTs. The MoL method for determining PT is based on the weighted average of the ‘turning point’ and is thus not well‐defined as a stochastic process. The Psi method quantifies the PT as the intensity at which the electrical stimulation was perceived for 50% of the stimulations by mathematically fitting the psychometric function to the responses as the probability of perceiving the stimuli at a given intensity. Furthermore, the MoL is associated with predictability, meaning that the subject might anticipate the intensity of the next stimulus and is therefore prone to make a premature judgment [29]. In contrast, the Psi method does not apply continuous increments and decrements, making it more unpredictable and thereby limiting the subject's expectations. Psychophysics is associated with a degree of uncertainty since the brain processing is easily affected by internal and external stimuli/noise [30]. With the Psi method, such biases can be minimized by adjusting the lapse and guess rates. Inaccurate setting of the lapse rate, guess rate, and the maximal stimulation intensity might affect the convergence and thus accuracy of the estimated PT.

Reliability of Assessment Tools for Diagnosis of Peripheral Neuropathy

Quantitative sensory testing (QST) is widely used for the evaluation of peripheral neuropathy [31] using the protocol recommended by the German Research Network on Neuropathic Pain [31]. The test–retest reliability of this QST protocol has been determined in patients with sensory disturbances to have an overall correlation coefficient of 0.86 [32]. Geber et al. reported reliability of the thermal pain and PTs with correlation coefficients of 0.80 and 0.88 in patients with sensory disturbances of different etiologies [32]. In healthy participants, ICC values between 0.39 and 0.81 have been reported [33, 34, 35]. Relative measures of reliability such as the ICC value tend to be lower in studies of healthy participants, as the ICC value describes the variation between subjects. Therefore, the modest ICC values observed in this study may still be useful in a clinical population in which the variation is expected to be higher.

In conclusion, PT estimation using the Psi method and the MoL showed similar reproducibility and better within‐session reliability than between‐session reliability. The Psi method has some advantages, including being less time‐consuming, and therefore might be preferable to the MoL method in a clinical setting. Assessment of PT in nerve fiber excitability studies using PTT may therefore be applicable in the diagnosis of diabetic‐ or chemotherapy‐induced peripheral small fiber neuropathy. However, further validation of PTT in a clinical setting is needed.
