Quantitative Spatiotemporal Analysis of Ultrasound Images of Fasciculations in ALS
Authors: Ryosuke Sugisawa, Kenji Sekiguchi, Yoshikatsu Noda, Shun Matoba, Hirotomo Suehiro, Jun Kozuki, Tomoko Tanaka, Norio Chihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsFasciculations are a hallmark of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), yet quantitative description of individual events on muscle ultrasound (MUS) is limited. We characterized the spatiotemporal kinematics of individual fasciculations to determine whether they differ between ALS and other neurogenic conditions.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed biceps brachii MUS recordings from 680 examinations (January 2020–June 2025), identifying 74 ALS and 40 non‐ALS neurogenic recordings with fasciculations (167 and 62 segments). After propensity score matching for age and muscle strength, 62 matched pairs were analyzed. The Lucas‐Kanade optical flow algorithm, which estimates frame‐to‐frame displacement vectors from local intensity gradients, was applied at 1‐pixel intervals (57,600 points per 240 × 240 region; ≈60 μm) to quantify twitch durations, peak displacement velocity, and directional anisotropy as a measure of spatial movement coherence.ResultsALS fasciculations showed prolonged total duration (582.8 ± 112.8 ms vs. 489.2 ± 128.7 ms, p < 0.001), reduced directional anisotropy (0.534 ± 0.245 vs. 0.627 ± 0.215, p = 0.028), and lower peak displacement velocity (6.55 ± 6.56 vs. 9.53 ± 9.07 μm/ms, p = 0.039). MANOVA showed significant multivariate differences (Pillai's trace = 0.317 ± 0.030, p < 0.001) with moderate group separation (Mahalanobis distance = 1.10 ± 0.05).DiscussionALS fasciculations showed spatially heterogeneous and temporally prolonged contraction patterns, suggesting motor units in a transitional state of incomplete reinnervation, distinct from the more stable architecture of chronic neurogenic disorders. This framework may complement existing ultrasound assessment and aid the study of motor unit pathology in ALS.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, fasciculation, motion tracking, muscle ultrasound, quantitative imaging

Introduction

Fasciculations are involuntary muscle twitches resulting from spontaneous activation of a single motor unit, or a portion thereof [1]. While observed in various peripheral nerve disorders, peripheral nerve hyperexcitability syndromes, and benign fasciculation syndrome (BFS), they are particularly associated with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), where they constitute a lower motor neuron sign in the modified Awaji criteria [2]. Their mere presence, however, is not specific to ALS, and quantitative characterization of fasciculation potentials (FPs) by needle electromyography (EMG) has therefore been pursued extensively. ALS‐related FPs tend to be more complex, more unstable, and discharge at shorter inter‐event intervals than those in BFS or other chronic neurogenic disorders, although waveforms overlap substantially at the individual level [3, 4, 5]. Among these features, FP stability and firing rate appear to carry most of the discriminative information [4].

Muscle ultrasound (MUS) complements EMG by offering a wider observation field, enabling efficient detection of fasciculations across multiple muscles non‐invasively [6, 7]. Several studies have leveraged this advantage to develop frequency‐ and distribution‐based approaches: high‐grade and proximal fasciculations are characteristic of ALS, whereas low‐grade and distal events predominate in non‐ALS neurogenic disorders [7, 8, 9], and automated event detection from B‐mode video is also feasible [10]. However, these approaches rely on fasciculation frequency and anatomical distribution rather than on characteristics of individual fasciculations. The kinematics of each event—how it contracts and relaxes over time and how coherent its movement is across the activated territory—have received less attention. EMG resolves motor‐unit‐level features such as waveform amplitude, duration, and stability, but comparable kinematic parameters have not yet been established for MUS.

We therefore aimed to characterize how the spatiotemporal kinematics of individual fasciculations differ between ALS and other neurogenic disorders, and whether such differences may reflect ALS‐specific motor unit pathology or general neurogenic remodeling.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study reviewed MUS video recordings of the biceps brachii obtained for diagnostic purposes at Kobe University Hospital between January 2020 and June 2025. Patients were identified by reviewing the electronic medical records and the institutional MUS image database. MUS examinations were performed with subjects in the supine position using a Healthcare LOGIQ e system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a wideband linear probe (L4‐12t, GE Healthcare; operating frequency 10 MHz; footprint 38 mm; depth setting 3 cm; NER‐average preset) placed over the mid‐upper arm, and images were acquired at 39–50 frames per second (fps) by three physicians certified by the Japanese Society of Clinical Neurophysiology (YN, SM, RS) [11]. The variability in frame rate reflected differences in image depth and field‐of‐view settings across examinations; the impact of this variability on temporal measurements is addressed in Section 2.3.

ALS patients were those with “Probable” or higher level according to the modified Awaji criteria [2]. Non‐ALS patients were identified from the same database and comprised individuals with other neurological diagnoses established through comprehensive clinical assessment, electrodiagnostic testing, blood tests, and genetic testing where indicated. We included video recordings that exhibited fasciculations at rest in the biceps brachii from patients with confirmed neurological diagnoses. Recordings were excluded for poor image quality, artifacts (vascular pulsation or probe motion), overlapping fasciculations, or fasciculations located at the imaging field edge. These criteria ensured accurate optical flow tracking.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kobe University Hospital (B250229), which waived the requirement for written informed consent given the retrospective, anonymized nature of the study.

Pre‐Processing of Fasciculation Videos

We adopted a two‐stage tracking strategy. Sparse grid tracking was first applied to full‐length videos to localize each fasciculation in space and time, providing a computationally efficient initial pass. Once the spatiotemporal location was identified, ultra‐dense tracking was applied to a standardized 1‐s window centered on that location, enabling high‐resolution analysis of intramuscular dynamics that would have been computationally prohibitive if applied to the entire video. The two stages are therefore complementary: sparse tracking handles wide‐field localization, while ultra‐dense tracking resolves the fine‐scale kinematics of the localized event.

Preliminary Study by Sparse Tracking for Identifying the Location of Fasciculation

Fasciculations were visually identified from video recordings by a single investigator (RS), following established protocols for distinguishing fasciculations from artifacts [6]. To standardize segment extraction, we developed an automated pipeline using preliminary motion tracking analysis (Figure 1). Sparse grid tracking was applied to full videos, generating velocity‐time graphs for all tracking points. The peak fasciculation moment was identified as the frame showing maximum velocity in the integrated graph, and the fasciculation epicenter was defined as the spatial coordinates showing maximum displacement at this peak. Based on preliminary observations confirming that the entire fasciculation event is captured within this temporal window, 1‐s segments were extracted starting 7 frames before the peak. To avoid within‐patient clustering bias, a maximum of four segments were extracted per individual. Representative examples are shown in Videos S1 (ALS) and S2 (non‐ALS).

Ultra‐Dense Tracking for Detailed Analysis of Fasciculation

Using custom Python scripts, segments (240 × 240 pixels) were automatically cropped and centered on the fasciculation epicenter, with adjustment near frame edges to ensure adequate margins of accurate tracking. Ultra‐dense optical flow tracking was then applied to these standardized segments to capture detailed spatiotemporal dynamics of fasciculations. This automated extraction ensured standardized, objective segmentation across all fasciculations.

Motion Tracking Analysis

Ultra‐Dense Motion Tracking

For each selected segment, tracking points were placed at 1‐pixel intervals across the 240 × 240 pixels field (≈200 mm2), yielding 57,600 points per segment (Figure 2a). We use the term “ultra‐dense” to denote this 1‐pixel‐interval sampling, in contrast to the sparser grid spacing typically employed in prior muscle ultrasound tracking studies [12, 13]. The 240 × 240 field size was chosen based on preliminary observations to ensure adequate coverage of the typical spatial extent of fasciculation activity. With a pixel pitch of approximately 60 μm in the lateral direction (derived from the field‐of‐view width divided by the lateral pixel count, consistent with the lateral resolution achievable at the 10‐MHz transmit frequency used here), this sampling approaches the dimensions of individual muscle fibers and enables detection of spatially restricted, focal contractions originating from single or few motor units [6].

Motion tracking used the pyramidal Lucas‐Kanade optical flow algorithm [14] as implemented in OpenCV (cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK) [15]. The algorithm tracked every pixel from the first frame throughout the 1‐s sequence with sub‐pixel resolution capability [12, 13, 16] (Figure 2b; Video S3). A 50 × 50 pixel search window was used at each pyramid level (maxLevel = 30), and iteration was terminated when the displacement change fell below 0.01 pixels or 30 iterations were reached, whichever occurred first.

The Lucas–Kanade algorithm assumes brightness constancy, small inter‐frame motion, and locally constant flow. Although these assumptions are not strictly satisfied by intramuscular ultrasound speckle, two‐dimensional speckle‐tracking is used in clinical ultrasound for analogous tasks, including speckle‐tracking echocardiography [17] and ultrasound tracking of diaphragm motion [16]. In the present setting, the high frame rate keeps inter‐frame displacements small, and recordings showing probe motion or substantial out‐of‐plane drift were excluded at the screening stage. To reduce residual influence from local violations of these assumptions—particularly at boundaries between contracting and non‐contracting fibers—downstream metrics were derived from spatial aggregates (the five largest displacement points for temporal analysis, and population statistics across the field for spatial analysis) rather than from individual pixel trajectories.

Temporal Analysis of Fasciculation Duration

The peak fasciculation moment was identified as the frame showing maximum displacement velocity across all tracking points. To characterize the temporal dynamics of fasciculations, we selected the five tracking points with the largest displacement magnitudes within each segment and calculated their mean velocity waveform, which exhibited a biphasic pattern reflecting the contraction‐relaxation cycle of the activated muscle fibers (Figure 2c).

Fasciculations produce a characteristic to‐and‐fro tissue displacement on ultrasound images. During the contraction phase, a localized group of muscle fibers move unidirectionally while gradually accelerating, then decelerate and stop at a position different from their starting point. Subsequently, during the relaxation phase, they slowly return to their original position. In the time‐velocity analysis, this bidirectional displacement generates a biphasic velocity curve, where the first peak corresponds to contraction phase and the second peak to relaxation phase. The right panel of Figure 2b illustrates this movement trajectory. On the time‐velocity curve plotted by mean velocity of the five tracking points with largest displacement amplitude, we identified representative temporal landmarks and termed each inflection point as follows: (1) Start, (2) Peak contraction velocity, (3) Reversal point (transition between contraction and relaxation), (4) Peak relaxation velocity, and (5) End (Figure 2b,c). To facilitate accurate identification, we developed an interactive Python interface displaying the corresponding ultrasound image in real‐time as the cursor moved along the velocity curve (Video S4). Three duration metrics were calculated: contraction duration (Start to Reversal point), relaxation duration (Reversal point to End), and total duration (Start to End).

Because all durations were measured in milliseconds, and the difference in inter‐frame interval across the 39–50 fps range (~6 ms; i.e., 20 vs. 26 ms) was two orders of magnitude smaller than the measured durations (typically several hundred milliseconds), the variation in acquisition frame rate did not meaningfully affect the duration measurement; frame rate did not differ between groups or correlate with any temporal metric.

Spatial Analysis of Fasciculation Movement

To evaluate spatial organization of fasciculation‐induced movement, we analyzed displacement vectors at the peak fasciculation frame (Figure 3; Video S5). Each tracking point has a displacement vector representing the movement from its position in the previous frame to its current position. The vector's magnitude indicates the distance traveled, and its direction indicates the orientation of movement in the frame corresponding to the peak contraction velocity (2 in Figure 2). The right panel of Figure 3 shows displacement vectors overlaid on the ultrasound image, visualizing the spatial pattern of fasciculation‐induced tissue deformation. Tracking points with displacement below 15% of maximum were excluded from directional analysis to minimize artifacts from probe micro‐movements; these points are not displayed in the figures.

Directional anisotropy (DA) is a metric that quantifies the coherence of movement directions across the fasciculation area (the upper left panel of Figure 3). When vectors align uniformly in a single direction, their summation yields a long resultant vector and DA approaches 1; when vectors point in random or opposing orientations, they cancel out and DA approaches 0. In the example shown in Figure 3, DA is 0.17, indicating spatially heterogeneous contraction in which activated fibers move in multiple directions rather than as a coherent unit. Additional metrics included: peak displacement velocity (maximum displacement magnitude at peak frame), active area fraction (proportion of points exceeding 15% of maximum displacement), echogenicity (average pixel intensity within 40 × 40 pixels surrounding maximum displacement), and relative echogenicity (echogenicity relative to overall image average). All analyses used custom Python scripts without noise filtering, as temporal smoothing would attenuate the rapid displacement changes at fasciculation onset and offset that define the temporal metrics described above.

Statistical Analysis

Propensity Score Matching

We performed propensity score matching (PSM) using age and biceps brachii strength (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade) to balance baseline characteristics between groups. The Hungarian algorithm—which finds the optimal minimum‐cost assignment between two sets by solving a bipartite matching problem—was used for 1:1 matching. The method and matching results are described in Results Section 3.1.

Univariate Analysis

Group differences for individual features were assessed using Student's t‐test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. We report both uncorrected p values and effect sizes (Cohen's d).

Multivariate Analysis

To assess overall group separation and evaluate the stability of multivariate metrics, we performed multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) and calculated Mahalanobis distance—a measure of separation between two group centroids in multidimensional feature space that accounts for correlations among features—between group centroids using 10 iterations of stratified random splitting (70% training, 30% test) at the patient level with different random seeds. Features were z‐score normalized separately for each iteration, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across iterations. The MANOVA effect size was reported as partial η 2, which equals Pillai's trace in the two‐group case and represents the proportion of multivariate variance attributable to group membership.

Spatial Movement Characteristics

ALS fasciculations exhibited reduced directional anisotropy (0.534 ± 0.245 vs. 0.627 ± 0.215, p = 0.028, d = 0.40), indicating less spatially coherent movement. Figures 3 and 6b demonstrate this difference: the ALS example shows spatially disorganized, multidirectional displacement vectors (DA: 0.17), whereas the non‐ALS example exhibits coherent, unidirectional alignment (DA: 0.80).

Echogenicity was significantly higher in the non‐ALS group than in ALS (100.4 ± 42.4 vs. 85.0 ± 36.3, p = 0.033, d = 0.39). Relative echogenicity and active area fraction showed no significant differences between groups (all p > 0.05).

Subgroup analyses comparing fasciculation characteristics among ALS onset types (upper limb, lower limb, bulbar) revealed no significant differences for any measured parameters (all p > 0.05). Within the ALS group, fasciculation parameters showed no significant correlations with disease duration or ALSFRS‐R scores (all p > 0.05).

Multivariate Statistical Analysis

MANOVA revealed significant multivariate differences between ALS and non‐ALS groups (Pillai's trace = 0.317 ± 0.030, F = 5.29, p < 0.001, partial h 2 = 0.317). The Mahalanobis distance between group centroids was 1.10 ± 0.05, confirming moderate separation in the 8‐dimensional feature space.

Discussion

In this study, fasciculations in ALS showed distinct quantitative spatiotemporal characteristics relative to non‐ALS neurogenic disorders. Specifically, ALS fasciculations showed prolonged contraction and relaxation phases, reduced directional anisotropy of displacement vectors, and lower peak displacement velocity, while echogenicity was higher in the non‐ALS group. These findings complement prior muscle ultrasound studies that have focused on the frequency and anatomical distribution of fasciculations [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] by providing a quantitative description of how each individual fasciculation contracts in space and time.

Principal Findings

The temporal and directional differences observed in ALS persisted after matching for muscle strength.

Beyond these univariate differences, the two groups were also separated in multivariate feature space: MANOVA showed significant multivariate differences, with a Mahalanobis distance between group centroids consistent with moderate separation in the multidimensional feature space. The substantial overlap between groups at the individual level, however, indicates that these features alone are insufficient for patient‐level classification and that they are best interpreted as group‐level signatures of fasciculation kinematics rather than as standalone diagnostic markers.

Interpretation: Motor Unit Reorganization and Fasciculation Dynamics

In ALS, fasciculations occur in muscles undergoing active denervation and reinnervation [18], and understanding the spatial organization of reinnervating motor units may be helpful for interpreting our kinematic findings.

In stable chronic neurogenic conditions, collateral sprouting eventually produces fiber‐type grouping, where motor unit fibers cluster spatially [19, 20, 21]. However, the rapid and successive motor neuron degeneration characteristic of ALS may prevent this maturation, leaving motor units in a transitional state of incomplete reorganization [22, 23, 24, 25]. In such a transitional state, newly captured fibers remain interspersed with fibers from other motor units, showing little fiber‐type grouping except in chronic, slowly progressive cases [26, 27]. When a fasciculation activates such a motor unit, contracting fibers surrounded by non‐contracting fibers from other motor units may produce spatially disorganized tissue deformation (reduced directional anisotropy) and reduced displacement amplitude as contractile forces are distributed across heterogeneous tissue (lower peak displacement velocity).

Prolongation of both contraction and relaxation phases is consistent with this mechanical heterogeneity, and may also reflect the slowed conduction along atrophic muscle fibers in ALS [28], the impaired neuromuscular transmission at small, immature endplates documented in ALS [29, 30], and impaired excitation–contraction coupling [31]; the latter is supported by the recently reported prolongation of fasciculation electromechanical latency in ALS muscle. These electrical abnormalities have, however, largely been documented in comparisons of ALS with healthy controls; whether they are selectively stronger in ALS than in other chronic neurogenic disorders—as would be required to explain the kinematic differences between our ALS and non‐ALS neurogenic groups—has not been directly tested. More fundamentally, these electrical metrics quantify inter‐fiber activation dispersion within a motor unit, whereas the present duration metrics, derived from the top‐five most coherent displacement points within the tracked region, may primarily reflect the local twitch duration of the fastest fibers themselves—which may depend on intrinsic properties such as fiber‐type composition that could plausibly reflect immature reinnervation in ALS muscle. Although the electrical and mechanical contributions to the observed prolongations cannot be fully isolated within the present approach, the differences between our ALS and non‐ALS neurogenic groups may tentatively point to an ALS‐specific factor at the level of individual fibers or fiber‐type composition, consistent with the mechanical heterogeneity within incompletely reorganized motor units proposed above. These temporal and anisotropy differences persisted even after matching for muscle strength, suggesting that they may reflect characteristics not fully accounted for by general denervation.

These interpretations should be viewed cautiously in the absence of histological validation. Future studies directly correlating ultrasound‐derived metrics with quantitative histology (e.g., fiber‐type distribution and motor unit territory mapping), or simultaneous EMG–ultrasound recordings linking electrical and mechanical features of motor unit activity, will be essential to test this hypothesis.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. This study was restricted to biceps brachii in a retrospective, single‐center, cross‐sectional design; generalizability to other muscles and longitudinal changes remain to be established and validation in independent prospective cohorts will be needed before the proposed metrics can be used in clinical practice. Cross‐sectional imaging captures only a two‐dimensional slice of the three‐dimensional muscle contraction, potentially missing contractile components oriented perpendicular or oblique to the imaging plane.

The non‐ALS neurogenic group, although restricted to neurogenic disorders, remained etiologically heterogeneous and may have obscured disease‐specific patterns; healthy and benign fasciculation subjects (n = 5) were too few for separate inferential analysis. Within the ALS group, subgroup analyses by onset type showed no significant differences, and fasciculation parameters did not correlate with disease duration or ALSFRS‐R, likely reflecting limited statistical power—particularly for the bulbar‐onset subgroup (6 patients, 14 segments)—and the fact that global clinical measures do not specifically capture the state of the examined muscle. Propensity score matching was performed at the segment level, which may have introduced residual imbalance in patient‐level characteristics.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Quantitative analysis of fasciculation dynamics may complement existing visual assessment by providing reproducible kinematic descriptors of individual events. These measures may also offer a framework for interrogating disease mechanisms, complementing the interpretation above. Key priorities for future research include: (1) validation in larger, multi‐center cohorts with disease‐specific control groups; (2) longitudinal studies to characterize the temporal evolution of fasciculation properties and their relationship to clinical progression; (3) simultaneous EMG–ultrasound recordings to link electrical and mechanical features of motor unit activity; and (4) extension to additional muscle groups and correlation with histopathology.

Figure S1: Three‐group comparison of fasciculation spatiotemporal characteristics. Distribution of five features compared across the ALS group (n = 167 segments from 74 patients), the non‐ALS neurogenic group (n = 62 segments from 40 patients), and the Healthy/Benign group (n = 13 segments from 5 subjects: 1 healthy volunteer with benign‐pattern fasciculations and 4 patients with benign fasciculation syndrome). Box plots show median, interquartile range, and whiskers extending to 1.5 × IQR. Gray dots represent individual segments. Overall group differences were assessed by the Kruskal‐Wallis test, and significant overall comparisons were followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction (×3). Significance markers indicate adjusted p values: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. Total duration, contraction duration, and directional anisotropy differed significantly between the ALS and non‐ALS neurogenic groups, while echogenicity was significantly higher in the non‐ALS neurogenic group than in the HC group.

Video S4: Real‐time synchronized display of fasciculation waveform and ultrasound video. Left: Biphasic displacement velocity curve. Right: Corresponding video image with tracking point trajectory (inset). This interface enabled accurate temporal landmark identification.
