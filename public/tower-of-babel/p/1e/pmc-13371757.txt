Design and implementation of PharMe: a mobile application to return pharmacogenomic test results to patients
Authors: Tamara Slosarek, Jannis Baum, Benjamin Frost, Nikola Genchev, Leon Hermann, Samuel Kunst, Charlie Meister, Matti Schmidt, Sebastian Wagner, Susan M Mashni, Christoph Lippert, Erwin P Böttinger, Aniwaa Owusu Obeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePharmacogenomics can optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse events. However, the lack of trained healthcare professionals has impeded its widespread adoption. Current patient-facing applications are either institution-specific or vary significantly in their coverage of genetic variants, interpretation guidelines, and data privacy protection. To address these shortcomings, we developed PharMe.Materials and MethodsAll components were developed through co-design; experts created patient-friendly content in English from Clinical Pharmacogenomics Implementation Consortium and Food and Drug Administration guidelines.ResultsPharMe returns pharmacogenomic results for 17 genes and 159 medications, including phenoconversion of CYP2D6 due to inhibitors. PharMe is facilitated by Anni, a web interface enabling consistent, multilingual annotation of guidelines with patient-friendly formulations. Initial evaluations demonstrate that PharMe is usable and stable. PharMe is available for iOS and Android in English.DiscussionPharMe and Anni use transparent, standardized guideline content, are agnostic to data sources, and can flexibly be deployed globally in different settings and institutions, while upholding data privacy. Prospectively, both could support further guidelines and data formats. Validation of clinical utility, patient comprehension, and empowerment is underway in a clinical validation study with diverse patients.ConclusionWe developed a novel, extensible, usable, and stable system to transparently return pharmacogenomic results to patients. These findings support further investigation in larger, more diverse populations and in real-world clinical settings, with the goal of informing scalable, guideline-concordant pharmacogenomics implementation.Keywords: pharmacogenomics, adverse drug reaction, genetic counseling, empowerment, medical informatics application

Introduction

Adverse drug reactions are among the top 10 leading causes of death and disease in developed countries,1,2 and for about 50% of the population, some commonly prescribed medications are ineffective.3 Pharmacogenomics (PGx) has the potential to improve medication efficacy and safety significantly, and can optimize therapeutic outcomes.4–6 However, several barriers impede its widespread adoption into routine practice, such as the lack of trained healthcare professionals.5,6 For example, at the Mount Sinai Health System in New York, with eight hospital campuses and more than 9000 physicians,7 as of January 2025, only a dozen clinical pharmacists are trained to counsel patients following PGx testing.

Expanding the practical use of PGx testing requires reducing the burden of time-intensive, in-person counseling with trained experts, while improving health literacy and patient empowerment. To complement the work of trained experts, we developed PharMe, a mobile application that delivers PGx results directly to patients. Patient-friendly content for PharMe is provided by Anni, a custom-built, novel web interface for experts to annotate PGx guideline texts.

Mobile applications have previously been shown to improve patient outcomes in several health-related fields,8–11 and digital tools have proven effective in communicating genetic results to patients.12,13 While several services exist for returning PGx results, most are designed for healthcare professionals,14 such as the PharmGKB’s DNA-Driven Rx tool.15 Lipschultz et al.16 implemented an administrative dashboard to generate patient summaries, linked to a mobile-optimized, web-based patient portal. This portal displayed summaries along with a traffic-light system (red, yellow, green) and was integrated into an existing clinical decision support system. In evaluating the portal, Truong et al. found that portal access improved patients’ knowledge, understanding, and perceptions of PGx.17 Patients also expressed a strong preference for a well-designed, secure portal that could be accessed via mobile devices and easily shared with their healthcare providers.18 However, it was not clear which clinical guidelines were used, and we assume that integrating into a clinical decision support system cannot be easily replicated at another institution. Anni and PharMe are designed to rely on standardized clinical guidelines and to be compatible with different PGx data sources. Moreover, Anni promotes consistency through reusable phrasing, enabling multilingual support. In its current version, PharMe was configured in the context of a planned clinical validation study at the Mount Sinai Health System, in English language, relying on Clinical Pharmacogenomics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines.

Blagec et al.19 proposed the Medication Safety Code (MSC) system—a QR code representing patients’ PGx profile that can, for example, be printed on a personalized card the size of a credit card and be kept in patients’ wallets. Healthcare providers can then scan a patient’s card and find information personalized for the patient in a web-based portal. As part of the U-PGx project, the PREPARE study utilized cards based on the MSC system to support clinical implementation of PGx across Europe, addressing challenges related to interoperability and data exchange between institutions.20,21 While this system provides an elegant way for patients to transport their PGx information, the information encoded in the QR code is intended for health care professionals. On the other hand, PharMe is designed primarily for patients. Also, several commercial direct-to-consumer (DTC) testing companies offer PGx testing. For example, 23andMe22 began offering FDA-approved PGx reports in 201823 without requiring physician consultation. These reports cover genes such as CYP2C19, DPYD, and SLCO1B1, as well as associated medications like clopidogrel, citalopram, and simvastatin. In contrast, companies like Pharmazam,24 Veritas,25 and Color26 offer direct-to-consumer PGx testing through a hybrid model that involves physician ordering. However, the coverage of genetic variants, the interpretation guidelines used, and data privacy protection can vary significantly.27 With PharMe, we aimed to design a system grounded in data privacy that uses transparent information in line with standardized clinical guidelines.

We hypothesize that PharMe is a usable and stable tool that can be deployed to patients in a larger clinical validation study at the Mount Sinai Health System in New York. In the following, we describe the design and implementation of PharMe prior to the validation study.

Materials and methods

We developed PharMe and Anni through a co-design approach (Supplement 1) based on design thinking methodology.28 A central element is the iteration of observation, ideation, prototyping, and testing. Our process involved layperson and expert interviews based on a PretoVid29 to gather diverse perspectives and insights, a World Café session to further brainstorm and test PharMe, and a remote pilot test to assess usability and robustness using predefined tasks and the System Usability Score (SUS).30 Layperson testing was predominantly conducted with family, colleagues, friends, and acquaintances in Germany and the US. Additionally, a power usability test with an elderly patient at the Mount Sinai Health System in New York, who had undergone PGx testing, to incorporate the perspective of demanding individuals.

In preparation for the validation study, PharMe currently includes clinically actionable information for 17 genes and 159 medications, based on guidelines from the CPIC31 and the US FDA Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations32 (Table 1, Supplement 2).

Results

Figure 1 depicts the software components and data flow between them: the Annotation Interface (Anni), PharMe, a patient health data source, and Scio, a module to assess comprehension (Supplement 3). The source code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/hpi-dhc/PharMe).

Annotation interface (anni)

PGx experts can use Anni to view published PGx guidelines and annotate them with patient-friendly content (Figures 2, S1). Anni is a web-based application built with Next.js36 and MongoDB37 document storage, providing a REST API to query the current annotation version and data. Medications and corresponding guidelines are uploaded using the CPIC API and a custom repository (https://github.com/hpi-dhc/PharMe-Annotation) for additional guidelines, such as the FDA table. For each phenotype of a gene-drug combination, annotations for the clinical implication, PGx-guided recommendation, and warning level (green, yellow, red, none) can be added while viewing the corresponding guideline (Figures 2A, S1d). Anni also supports medication-level annotations with patient-friendly descriptions of drug class, indication, and brand names (Figure S1c). All annotations consist of pre-defined, multilingual text bricks (Figure S1e, f) that can be selected using drag-and-drop (Figure 2B). The text brick approach improves consistency and annotation speed, enables cross-checking across medications and guidelines, and reduces translation effort into other languages. After annotations are added and edited, a review step is introduced: the author or another expert can activate the “review mode” in Anni, which allows annotations to be staged for publishing. In this mode, editing is disabled to avoid accidental changes. After review, staged annotations can be published and made available for PharMe (Figure S1a, b). This three-part process increases the quality and integrity of the annotations. It offers a development environment where 1 can edit the annotations without directly changing what is shown in PharMe. Moreover, Anni can be launched with a backup service, and rule-based validation and update scripts for underlying guidelines are provided.

Annotation creation in Anni; (A) guideline data and annotations for one phenotype; (B) editing annotations with text bricks.

PharMe

PharMe is a mobile application developed with the multi-platform framework Flutter38 that displays PGx results to patients, using patient-friendly clinical PGx guideline content curated in Anni (Figures 3, S2). After successful authentication, the app receives patients’ genetic and medication data from a patient health data source (eg, an EHR system; Figure S2b). PharMe is designed to be agnostic to the data source; data loading can be implemented individually per provider. In addition, PharMe downloads all annotation data from Anni and selects guidelines matching an individual’s genetic data using CPIC lookup keys (Figure S3). Matching is done solely on the phone, and all data is stored in encrypted form to ensure privacy and security. After the initial login, patients can update the list of active medications imported from the patient health data source and review a short tutorial.

PharMe screens: (A) the medication overview screen with search and filter, (B) medication screen containing annotated guideline information, (C) gene results screen, (D) gene screen with implicated drugs.

The first screen is the Medication Overview (Figure 3A). PharMe displays all medications in a color-coded list with the annotated warning level, listing the patient’s active medications first. PharMe displays PGx guidance for both active and future medications implicated by a patient’s genotype, consistent with CPIC and FDA guidelines. Because most actionable PGx recommendations apply at the time of new drug initiation rather than to medications already tolerated without adverse effects, PharMe includes an in-app FAQ and disclaimer to contextualize results as a prospective reference tool. In addition to the color-coding, icons indicate the warning level to increase accessibility. A “none” warning level is used when no recommendation can be made because of missing genetic data or guidelines. In contrast to the “green” warning level, “none” is displayed with a question mark icon. Patients can search for medications by their generic name, brand names, or drug class descriptions, and filter the list by warning levels.

Tapping a medication leads patients to the Medication screen with more details and their personal PGx-guided recommendations (Figure 3B). Patients can mark medications as active, export a PDF copy to their healthcare provider (Supplement 4), and access the underlying guidelines.

On the Gene Report screen (Figure 3C), patients can review their PGx test report. Like the Medication Overview screen, genes associated with the patients’ active medications are displayed at the top. Tapping a gene leads patients to the Gene screen, showing genotype and phenotype information and associated medications (Figure 3D).

To provide further guidance, PharMe includes explanatory tooltips and a Frequently Asked Questions screen (Figure S2c); on the More screen, patients can contact the app team with questions or problems and find additional resources about the app and (pharmaco)genomics (Figure S2d).

PharMe supports phenoconversion of CYP2D6 due to inhibiting concomitant drugs (Figure S4). When an inhibiting medication is selected as an active medication, patients will be presented with a warning dialog explaining that this medication can interact with other medications (Figure S4a). If a strong inhibitor is selected, the phenotype of the impacted gene will be changed throughout the app to “poor metabolizer” (Figure S4b–d). If a moderate inhibitor is selected, the phenotype is not changed; only an interaction warning is displayed.

We intentionally do not show alternative medications or exact dose adjustments, discouraging patients from changing their prescribed medications or doses without consulting a healthcare professional. We also clearly state the app’s limitations through notices displayed throughout the app reminding patients that (1) medications or doses should never be changed without consulting a healthcare professional (Figure 3B); (2) PGx is only 1 important aspect when choosing the right medication and the right dose (Figure S2a, c); and (3) PharMe only includes information about medications and genes that are included in clinical guidelines (Figure 3A, C).

PharMe is currently available for iOS and Android in English. (The authors can grant test access upon request.)

Usability evaluations

First interviews indicated that PharMe is valuable, understandable, and easy to use (Supplement 1). Moreover, 28 diverse testers from Germany and the US, including colleagues, friends, and acquaintances, participated in the remote pilot testing. Testers were younger, between 18 and 45 years old, and highly educated. They finished most tasks successfully; on average, PharMe received 4.1 stars and a SUS score of 82.86, mapping to an “A” on the Sauro–Lewis curved grading scale.39 40 testers across all evaluation steps claimed that the app functions as intended, is well-designed, and mostly runs stably, except for the identified bugs. However, the explanations in PharMe should be clearer and more comprehensible. Additionally, they emphasized that the app should include clear explanations of its capabilities, including that PGx is only one important aspect when choosing the right medication and the right dose, and that PharMe only includes information about medications and genes included in clinical guidelines. The app and its content were adapted accordingly, including further usability features and bug fixes.

Discussion

Haga et al.40 noted that while patients generally view PGx testing positively and consider it valuable, the results must be effectively communicated by providers and made shareable across healthcare institutions; with PharMe, we developed a usable app that transparently returns PGx test results to patients and is agnostic to data sources. Anni supports experts in creating consistent and multilingual patient-friendly annotations based on published clinical guidelines.

Anni and PharMe support the most clinically relevant drug-gene interactions, currently focusing on CPIC and FDA guidelines, including 17 genes and 159 medications.

Present solutions for returning PGx test results are either commercial DTC tests, for which concerns were raised regarding their quality and data privacy,41–43 or are integrated with a specific clinical decision support system, as done by Lipschultz et al.16—PharMe transparently relies on standardized published guidelines and keeps health information safe and in the hands of individuals, and aims to be lab- and institution-agnostic to be deployed in any system.

We iteratively applied different co-design methods to develop PharMe and to make the app valuable, understandable, and easy to use. The remote pilot testing results indicate that we met this objective. Testers reported high satisfaction with the app, as indicated by the star rating and the SUS; however, ratings may be more positive since the remote pilot testing was conducted with peers. Still, the textual feedback aligns with the positive ratings, and most tasks were successfully completed, indicating that the app is also objectively usable. However, most testers were younger and had higher education; therefore, we assume our test group to be more (digitally) literate than the general population. While our power usability test pointed out various design flaws that will affect the elderly, the tester is also digitally literate. More testing with elderly patients, patients with limited health literacy, and patients with different ethnicities is required. Despite these limitations, we improved the app with the feedback collected in all usability discussions and testing sessions, especially by adapting the textual content to lower reading levels. Simple language is essential when designing understandable PGx reports, as Olsen et al. found in a study assessing patient understanding of printed CYP2D6 reports.44 Moreover, they emphasized that PGx reports should include relevant information for the patient personally and actionable information. The administrative dashboard by Lipschultz et al. and Anni follow similar approaches to provide such information. While their approach allows for more flexible and extensive user reports with rich-text content and media, Anni focuses on consistent and concise formulations that can easily be translated into multiple languages. However, extending plain-text bricks to include rich-text and media content in the future may be valuable. Anni could further include features such as providing drug information and brand names to annotators and using ontology-based indications. PharmGKB45,46 could be used as a single guideline source, supporting guidelines that are not yet included, such as those from the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG). Also, additional factors to genetics that are important to be considered as part of PGx guidelines could be defined, such as age, current diagnoses, and ancestry; to support genes currently not included in the CPIC API, additional lookup keys could be defined in Anni. Further, the user interface could be extended to include currently scripted functionalities, such as updating guidelines, rule-based brick usage validation, managing annotation versions, and defining phenoconversion relations. Still, extending and updating the annotations remains a manual task for annotation creators; further, the annotations are not publicly available, so institutions that want to use PharMe need to create their own annotations or request access to Mount Sinai’s. We included the phenoconversion of CYP2D6 due to strong inhibiting concomitant drugs; the implementation of moderate inhibitors has not been added yet, despite showing a warning due to time restrictions, but follows the same logic. Once evidence is robust enough, phenoconversion can be extended to other drug-drug-gene interactions (DDGIs), as we expect the mechanisms to be like those for CYP2D6. However, in complex cases involving multiple genes and medications, the implemented logic may need to be adapted, and it may not be possible to account for all combinations in the future. PharMe could be extended by further features such as: extracting star allele information from VCFs or MSC codes directly, which would expand its applicability to data sources; considering drug-drug interactions (DDIs), which would further increase PharMe’s clinical utility beyond solely communicating genetic information alongside standard DDI reviews; searching medications by scanning the barcode; or including a medication history rather than only current medications. Moreover, current medications are loaded once and never updated automatically. We chose this method to account for potentially inaccurate medication entries in the EHR system. However, manual changes could also introduce errors that can be clinically relevant, especially when considering DDGIs or DDIs; further safeguards, in addition to explanatory warning dialogs, should be investigated in the future. While we focused on PharMe as a mobile application, it could also be deployed as a web service, which is also supported by Flutter, and may be favorable for patients when viewing their results at home. Concerns regarding the color coding were raised in the World Café; further assessments are needed whether green and red warning levels and question mark icons in combination with green warning levels may cause anxiety, medication non-adherence, or misinterpretation of results. Here, Scio could be applied in practice again to monitor patient comprehension in a first step, personalize content in Anni and PharMe in a next step, or even capture other medication-related information, such as adherence or side effects.

PharMe, in its current form, is a research app; while the code is open source and the tools can be used in clinical studies, entering the market may require clinical validation and regulatory oversight, in the U.S., the EU, and elsewhere. The positive first evaluation results verify the hypothesis that PharMe is a usable and stable tool that can be validated with diverse patients on a larger scale in a clinical randomized non-inferiority study at Mount Sinai, comparing results returned by the app versus pharmacist-led counseling over 3 months (Mount Sinai IRB protocol # 22-01404). The study focuses on patient comprehension, in addition to outcomes such as self-efficacy, attitudes toward PGx testing, and actions taken after testing.

As immediate next steps, PharMe should be provided in more languages, using the present localization infrastructure, and additional PGx guidelines should be included. Further, we are currently investigating the use of large language models to create initial annotations for expert review, considerably speeding up the process of adding or updating guidelines, while ensuring clinical safety. Moreover, the interaction between patients, PharMe, and healthcare professionals should be investigated, as we mainly focused on how patients use the app. The current exports for healthcare professionals should be validated and extended, for example, using MSC codes for sharing, which would further strengthen PharMe’s integration in present initiatives like U-PGx.

Conclusion

With PharMe and Anni, we developed a novel, usable, and stable system to deliver PGx results to patients. The system is designed to use transparent, standardized content based on published guidelines and to be agnostic to PGx data sources, which is why it can be flexibly deployed in all clinical settings and institutions. PharMe demonstrates strong usability and patient acceptability as a tool for returning PGx results directly to patients. These findings support further investigation in larger, more diverse populations and in real-world clinical settings, with the goal of informing scalable, guideline-concordant PGx implementation.
