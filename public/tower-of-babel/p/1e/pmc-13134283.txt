Evaluation on Fusarium crown rot resistance and agronomic traits of wheat near-isogenic lines
Authors: Shuying Yang, Baisong Yang, Dou Wang, Xin Ma, Liwei Xing, Han Wang, Huihui Zhang, Shunv Hao, Wanke Yu, Kaidi Lyu, Xiaoneng Wan, Jia Luo, Zhiyu Fang, Jiayi Shao, Junhai Yuan, Guozhong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFusarium crown rot (FCR) caused by species of Fusarium fungus is a global soil-borne disease of wheat. Increasing results indicate frequent controversy between seedling and adult plant resistance to FCR. To understand relationships between FCR resistance and agronomic traits in seedling and adult wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) plants, we developed 193 near-isogenic lines (NILs) and evaluated FCR severity during seedling and adult plant-growth stages. Agronomic traits such as plant height, thousand-kernel weight (TKW), and the composition of vernalization and dwarf genes were analyzed.ResultsAn FCR disease index (DI) for the seedling stage did not correlate with that of the adult. Adult plant DI correlates extremely, significantly, and negatively with plant height and TKW. Adult FCR severity did not correlate with allelic variations of vernalization genes Vrn-A1 and Vrn-D1. The dwarfing gene Rht-D1b significantly reduced plant height and increased FCR severity.ConclusionsThis study shows that the growth and development of wheat can affect its resistance to FCR. The correlation between FCR severity and plant height suggests that it should be feasible to enhance FCR resistance in wheat cultivars through the manipulation of dwarfing genes. Several phenotypically different lines of FCR severity were identified for subsequent gene mining and resistance mechanism analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08610-x.

Introduction

Fusarium crown rot (FCR), a widely distributed soil-borne disease caused by fungal pathogens like Fusarium pseudograminearum, significantly impacts wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) yield and quality, resulting in substantial economic costs [15, 26]. In Montana, USA, FCR was detected in 57% of surveyed fields during the 2008 and 2009 crop seasons [22]. In Australia, from 1998–2008, FCR caused an average annual yield loss of ~ 10.0% and an annual economic loss of ~ 88 million Australian dollars [24]. In China, FCR has gradually spread in Huang–Huai–Hai wheat areas [41]. In 2020, it occurred in more than 800,000 hm2 in Shandong province [40], and in 2021, in Henan province, in more than 20% of the wheat-planting area [36, 37]. Infection causes brown necrosis at the first two or three inter-nodes, blighted white heads, and abortive seeds [29]. In severe cases, mycotoxins such as deoxynivalenol that accumulate in wheat stalks and grains [23] may threaten human and animal health.

FCR isolate variability plays an important role in the host–pathogen interaction, with significant differences in the pathogenicity of different Fusarium species. In China, 11 Fusarium species have been identified from diseased wheat samples in Shandong province, of which F. pseudograminearum was dominant [18]. Additionally, 297 F. pseudograminearum isolates were obtained from diseased FCR wheat samples in Hebei province [47]. Histopathological assessment of F. pseudograminearum colonization in cereal culms indicates that the pathogen initiates colonization through epidermal penetration, mainly via stomatal apertures, and then progresses into the parenchymatous hypoderm [11]. Hyphae spread vertically through the tissues, first via the hypoderm and pith cavity, then through sclerified cells. Both xylem and phloem tissues become colonized, with less extensive colonization in more resistant genotypes.

Wheat disease-resistance breeding involves the use of germplasm collections, hybridization, and integration of complementary genetic sources, as well as extensive screening for target traits across different generations [36, 37].While cultivating disease-resistant varieties is an effective way to control FCR, excellent resistant germplasm is lacking and no genotype that is completely resistant to FCR is known. Inoculation with FCR can occur at seedling and adult plant stages. Wildermuth et al. [39] identified FCR resistance in the adults of 400 wheat germplasms, with only three (Gluyas Early, Mexico234, and Reliance) showing moderate resistance. Zhang et al. [48] identified 82 wheat germplasms at the seedling stage and reported 13 varieties (including Sunco and Purple Straw) to have moderate resistance to FCR. A worldwide collection of Aegilops tauschii accessions was evaluated for FCR resistance at the seedling stage, from which five accessions with disease index (DI) values < 20 showed moderate resistance [16]. These accessions can serve as potential donors for breeding FCR-resistant wheat cultivars. However, while evaluating resistance at the seedling-stage benefits from a short cycle, stable environment, and high throughput, the correlation between seedling- and adult-plant-stage resistance is controversial. Therefore, evaluating adult-plant-stage resistance in the field is important for genetics and breeding of FCR.

Developing wheat varieties with improved resilience to FCR should integrate FCR resistance with other traits such as drought tolerance, heading date, and plant height [1, 10, 19]. The interaction between plant height and FCR resistance in barley and wheat highlights the importance of understanding relationships between FCR resistance and agronomic traits [2, 12, 17]. The dwarf genes Rht-B1b and Rht-D1b are located on the 4BS and 4DS chromosomes, respectively, and explain 15.0%–41.0% of plant height variation [4]. The yield benefits of these gibberellin-insensitive semi-dwarfs rely on optimal growth conditions [3]. However, the severity of FCR often increases under unfavorable growing conditions such as drought and low soil fertility [1, 50].

The genetic basis of FCR resistance in wheat is complex and involves multiple quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and a series of gene expressions [19, 50]. Near-isogenic lines (NILs) have a relatively consistent genetic background and are ideal genetic materials with which to evaluate specific traits or genetic differences in plants. In this study, we identify resistance to FCR in wheat seedling and adult plant stages by artificial inoculation, examine correlations between the two resistance levels using a set of NILs, and investigate relationships between agronomic traits and the resistance level of FCR at the adult plant stage. By screening lines with different resistance to FCR, we also provide several germplasms for subsequent gene mining and molecular mechanism analysis of FCR resistance.

Materials and methods

Materials

A NILs population of 193 lines with different vernalization and dwarfing gene composition (Supplemental Table 1) was used to evaluate FCR resistance and agronomic traits in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). Wheat cultivar AK58 was used as FCR susceptible control; the FCR moderately resistant variety Sunco was provided by Professor Wei Li of Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. A highly aggressive isolate of F. pseudograminearum PY20-20 was provided by Professor Jianzhou Zhang of Henan Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Development of NILs for different vernalization and dwarfing genes

A set of NILs was developed by Xinong 1376 (recurrent parent) and Lumai 23 (donor parent) in a greenhouse at the Hebei Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences between 2010 and 2016. Xinong 1376 is a spring and semi-dwarf variety that carries the genes Vrn-A1b/vrn-B1/Vrn-D1a/Rht-B1a/Rht-D1b. Lumai 23 is a winter, drought-tolerance and tall variety that carries vrn-A1/vrn-B1/vrn-D1/Rht-B1a/Rht-D1a. Xinong 1376 was crossed with Lumai 23 and resulting F1 plants were backcrossed with the recurrent parent Xinong 1376. From the BC₁F₁ to the BC₆F1 generations, individual plants from each backcross generation were systematically screened via PCR amplification using gene-specific markers (Supplemental Table 2) to select those carrying the donor-parent target alleles (vrn-A1, vrn-D1, and Rht-D1a) following Ellis et al. [4], Yan et al. [45], and Fu et al. [6]. In the initial BC₁F₁ generation, a total of 125 plants were genotyped. Selected positive plants were backcrossed to the recurrent parent to produce the next generation. This marker-assisted selection (MAS) protocol was rigorously applied in each subsequent backcross cycle, which ensured the stable introgression of the target genes into the recipient background. BC6F1 plants were continuously selfed to produce BC6F5 generation seeds and progenies. Heterozygous individuals were selected using functional markers for wheat vernalization and dwarf alleles.

Evaluating FCR resistance of NILs in seedlings

Three independent experimental evaluations of FCR resistance at the seedling stage were performed. A fungal inoculum was prepared following Li et al. [13]. The PY20-20 was first cultured on PDA medium for 7 d, then new mass was selected and transferred to CMC liquid medium and incubated on a shaker for 96 h at 25 °C. Spores were harvested and the concentration of macroconidial suspension was adjusted to 1 × 106 spores mL−1. Tween 20 was added to the spore suspension to a final concentration of 0.1% vol/vol prior to use for inoculation. Uniform seeds were sequentially sterilized with 2% NaClO for 15 min, rinsed three times with sterile water, and then 15 seeds were evenly placed in a 22 × 27 cm seedling tray. Seeds from each line were planted three replicates. An appropriate amount of sterile water was added to infiltrate the seeds and incubated at 25 °C for 4 d.

Shoots and roots of seedlings at the GS12 stage [46] were soaked in the spore suspension for 2 min, then transplanted into pots containing sterilized nutrient soil (Pindstrup, Denmark). After culture at 25 °C in darkness for 24 h, seedlings were placed in a chamber with a light/dark 16/8 h, 22/17 °C cycle, and relative humidity of 100%. After two weeks, plants were exposed to moderate drought stress until all the plants of the susceptible AK58 showed obvious necrotic tissue; the FCR severity of each accession was recorded. The procedure to identify FCR resistance of seedlings is detailed in Fig. 1. FCR severity of each line was recorded using a 0–5 scale following Li et al. [13] (Supplemental Table 3). DI was calculated following Jin et al. [9], where DI = (∑nX/5N) × 100, with X = the scale value of each plant, n = the number of plants in the category, and N = the total number of plants assessed for each line. Evaluating FCR resistance of NILs at the adult plant stage

Field trials were performed at Xinxiang (35.00°N, 113.70°E), Henan province, from 2021–2022, and Gaocheng (37.95°N, 114.77°E), Hebei province, in 2023–2024 and 2024–2025. In total, 193 NLs and their parents were planted in one row (2 m long, with 40 seeds) for each line, spaced 25 cm apart. Test plots were managed in accordance with conventional farming practices. The inoculation method was employed according to Yang et al. [43], using millet seeds infected with F. pseudograminearum. At the stem elongation stage (GS30), 50 g of millet seeds infected with PY20-20 were mixed and sown evenly into soil after artificial trenching, the 2–3 cm deep trench was then covered with soil and irrigated to maintain moisture. At the ripening stage (GS91), at least five single plants were randomly selected from each line. The FCR severity in each line at this adult plant stage was recorded using the 0–5 scale of Yang et al. [43] (Supplemental Table 4); DI was calculated as in Sect. 1.2.2. Meteorological data from GS30 to GS91 (March 1st to May 31st) for the Yuanyang and Gaocheng locations were obtained from the weather stations of the Institute of Crop Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, and the Institute of Gaocheng Agricultural Science, respectively (Supplemental Table 5).

Agronomic traits of 193 NILs

In 2024 and 2025, agronomic traits of 193 NILs were investigated at the ripening stage (GS91) in Gaocheng, Hebei province. Plant height in each line was measured for at least five plants; kernels per spike (KPS) on each line were counted for at least 20 spikes. After harvest, the thousand-kernel weight (TKW) of each line was measured at least three times. All traits were measured in accordance with Liao et al. [14].

Data analysis

The DI of each line at seedlings stage or adult plant stage was represented by the mean value of three independent experiments. Statistical analyses of DI, including minimum, maximum, and mean values, standard deviations, and distribution frequencies, were performed in Microsoft EXCEL2019. Pearson correlation analyses and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed using SPSS-AU (https://spssau.com/), with post-hoc comparisons conducted using Tukey's HSD test where appropriate. Statistical significance was defined at α = 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed.

Resistance to FCR at adult plant stage

FCR severity at the adult plant stage was evaluated for the 193 NILs at the three field environments (Supplemental Table 7). DI values of Gaocheng 2024 and Yuanyang 2022, and Gaocheng 2024 and Gaocheng 2025 differed extremely significantly (p < 0.01); there was no significant difference between DI values of Yuanyang 2022 and Gaocheng 2025 (p = 0.492) (Table 1). The severity of FCR in Gaocheng in 2024 was lower than that in any other location, possibly due to the meteorological conditions during the early period of F. pseudograminearum infection. The March daily average of ambient temperature, relative humidity, soil temperature, and rainfall in Gaocheng-2024 was lower than that in Yuanyang-2022 and Gaocheng-2025 (Table 2). Discussion

Relationship between FCR resistance at seedling and adult plant stages

Under field conditions, we demonstrate obvious differences in FCR DI among the three field locations. The incidence of wheat FCR is affected by various factors such as climatic conditions, soil type, soil flora, and tillage management [5, 15]. However, evaluating FCR resistance on seedlings benefits from the plants' shorter life cycle, stable environment, and high throughput, for which reason seedlings have been used in most wheat FCR studies [19]. We demonstrate no correlation to exist between FCR severity in the seedling and adult wheat plant stages for 193 NILs, consistent with results of Yang et al. [43], Poole et al. [25], and Martin et al. [20]. Yang et al. [43] evaluated FCR resistance in 88 wheat varieties at seedling and adult stages, and while all were susceptible at the seedling stage, 10 had moderate resistance to FCR as adults. Poole et al. [25] compared the heritability of FCR resistance in multiple types of disease assays (greenhouse, terrace, and field) and rating systems, and reported no significant correlation between growth room and field FCR severity ratings, and that between the growth room and terrace to be significant but low. Under field conditions, the FCR partial resistance of wheat is affected by complex environmental factors and related to developmental stage [10].

A substantial number of QTLs associated with FCR resistance at the seedling stage have been identified across all 21 chromosomes of wheat [19, 20, 28, 31]. Certain QTLs that confer partial resistance to FCR are exclusive to seedlings, while others may be specific to adult plants [20, 28]. Recent studies have reported several genes associated with FCR resistance. Notably, Fhb7 [38], TaDIR-B1 [44], and TaWAK-5D600 [27] have been shown to enhance the resistance level of wheat seedlings to FCR. However, given the substantial differences in physiological status and environmental conditions between seedling in growth chambers and adult plants in the field, assessing FCR resistance under field conditions is more important for genetic improvement.

FCR resistance at the adult plant stage is negatively related to plant height of wheat

In this study, NILs with shorter plant height at the adult stage to be more severely affected by FCR. The relationships between FCR resistance and various agronomic traits, including plant height and heading date, have been documented in the literature [2, 10, 12, 17]. Notably, several QTLs associated with plant height and heading date are located in proximity to the QTL for FCR resistance [35, 42, 49]. In the seedling stage, shorter wheat plants are often associated with increased cell density, which indirectly reduces the infection rate of FCR [17]. In this study, the tall NIL showed significantly greater resistance to FCR than the dwarf NIL, likely due to its longer stem internodes. Even similar infection lengths, the FCR severity was consistently lower in the tall line. In addition, the resistance to FCR of wheat is related to drought tolerance. A lot of candidate genes conferring resistance to these two types of stresses ([7, 32, 33], and the drought tolerance of dwarf varieties is usually weaker than that of tall varieties [8].

The dwarf allele Rht-D1b can significantly reduce plant height and cause a significant increase in FCR severity in NILs. While the exact mechanism remains to be fully elucidated, it is plausible that the GA-insensitivity conferred by Rht-D1b not only reduces plant height but may also alter plant stem architecture. DELLA proteins can modulate transcription indirectly via interactions with hundreds of transcription factors. The phytohormone gibberellin (GA) triggers DELLA degradation, providing a mechanism by which plants can integrate developmental and environmental signals to regulate gene expression and optimize growth responses [30]. Rht-D1b that encode DELLA proteins resistant to GA-mediated degradation, resulting in constitutive partial suppression of stem growth and more grains per unit area, a higher harvest index, and greater yields [21]. Dwarfing genes diverted more dry matter from developing stems to the spike than tall lines, resulting in limited resources during the stem elongation [34]. Particularly in conditions of heat or drought stress, Rht-D1b may potentially reduce lignin biosynthesis or alter cell wall composition, thereby weakening the physical barrier against pathogen invasion. Furthermore, changes in secondary metabolism or water transport efficiency, often associated with Rht-D1b -mediated development, could influence pathogen colonization. In this study, despite carrying Rht-D1b allele that appears to increase disease severity in the NIL populations, the recurrent parent Xinong 1376 and several NILs with Rht-D1b can maintain relatively high FCR resistance. The results suggest that Xinong 1376 may contain additional resistance loci that mitigate the adverse effects associated with Rht-D1b. Alternatively, the influence of Rht-D1b on FCR susceptibility in NIL populations may be attributed to specific epistatic interactions with other parental alleles. Above hypotheses, however, require direct experimental validation in future studies through comparative analysis of cell wall components (e.g., lignin, cellulose), histological staining, and gene expression profile in NILs differing at Rht-D1 allele.

Conclusion

To understand relationships between FCR resistance and development and agronomic traits of wheat, 193 NILs were developed and used to evaluate the severity of FCR at seedling and adult stages. Agronomic traits such as plant height, TKW, and the composition of vernalization and dwarf genes were analyzed. Most importantly, we identify no significant correlation in FCR DI between seedling stages and adult plant stages. The adult-stage DI was extremely, significantly, and negatively correlated with plant height and TKW. Additionally, FCR severity at the adult stage did not correlate with allelic variations of vernalization genes Vrn-A1 and Vrn-D1. However, Rht-D1b significantly reduced plant height and increased FCR severity. As a consequence of this research, we identify several phenotypic germplasms that are appropriate for mapping the resistance genes of wheat FCR.
