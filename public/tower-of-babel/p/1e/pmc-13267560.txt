Developing a pan cancer therapy based on DISE-inducing short RNAs
Authors: Andrea E. Murmann, Mehrnoosh Ebadi, Monal Patel, Alexander Ewe, Sergio Barajas, Shiyu Xiao, Min Jun Ko, Sanghee Lee, Wenjie Cai, Bidur Paudel, Limin Sun, Elizabeth T. Bartom, Masha Kocherginsky, Yan Liu, Dong-Hyun Kim, Achim Aigner, Marcus E. Peter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA interference (RNAi) regulates gene expression through small RNAs that act via Argonaute-containing RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISCs). We previously found that short RNAs with G-rich 6mer seeds (e.g., GGGGGC and G5C) can kill cells by targeting C-rich 3′ UTR seed matches in essential survival genes (SGs), a mechanism termed death induced by survival gene elimination (DISE). To assess therapeutic potential, we systemically delivered two DISE-inducing sRNAs, sG5C and sCAG (based on CAG trinucleotide repeats), using lipopolyplexes (LPPs) composed of low-molecular-weight polyethyleneimines and lipids. In mouse ovarian and prostate cancer models and a rat hepatocellular carcinoma model, LPP-delivered small RNAs (sRNAs) markedly reduced or eliminated tumors without harming normal tissues. Predicted SG targets were engaged in tumors. Transcriptomic analyses across 10 major human cancers showed that many sG5C-targeted SGs are consistently upregulated in tumors and increase with stage, revealing a therapeutic window. These results support LPP-delivered DISE-inducing sRNAs as a promising pan-cancer therapy.

Introduction

RNA interference (RNAi) is a form of post-transcriptional regulation exerted by 19–25 nt long double-stranded (ds) RNAs that negatively regulate gene expression at the mRNA level. Physiologically, RNAi is primarily mediated by microRNAs (miRNAs), which target mRNAs via complementary base-pairing between the 6 and 8 nt seed regions (positions 2–7/8 of the guide strand) and complementary sequences (seed matches) typically located in the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of mRNAs.1,2 These interactions promote cleavage-independent translational silencing through the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which contains Argonaute (Ago) proteins.3,4 Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), in contrast, target fully complementary sequences within mRNAs, leading to Ago2-dependent degradation of target transcripts.5

We previously found that certain small RNAs (sRNAs) can induce cell death in an RISC-dependent fashion by simultaneously targeting networks of survival genes (SGs), triggering multiple cell death pathways.6,7,8 About 10% of all genes in the genome are critical for cell survival. For cancer cells such SGs have been identified by the Broad Institute’s screening of more than 1,800 different human cancer cell lines (https://depmap.org/portal/download/all). We termed this form of cell death induced by survival gene elimination (DISE). The effect depends on a toxic 6mer seed.9 To define the most toxic seeds, we screened all 4,096 possible 6mer seeds in human and mouse cell lines (6merdb.org).6,7 Universally, the most toxic seeds were G-rich, with the consensus GGGGGC (G5C).7 Such seeds, when present in sRNAs, target C-rich seed matches in SGs’ 3′ UTRs, leading to cell death.6

We showed that the ratio of miRNAs carrying toxic versus nontoxic seeds influences DISE sensitivity in different diseases and biological contexts.10,11,12 Most abundant miRNAs lack G-rich seeds, preventing global cytotoxicity, whereas several tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-34a/b/c-5p and miR-15/16-5p) harbor toxic G-rich seeds and selectively kill cancer cells.6,13 The DISE mechanism was independently validated in prostate cancer.14 We further discovered a second DISE-inducing sRNA class: CAG trinucleotide repeat (TNR)-based sRNAs (e.g., sCAG), also implicated in neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Huntington disease).15 sCAG targets complementary CUG repeat sequences in SGs, triggering cell death.16,17 We hypothesized that both seed-based and repeat-based toxic RNAs constitute an intrinsic anti-cancer mechanism.18,19 When delivered in vivo as templated lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), DISE-inducing sRNAs (Di-sRNAs) reduced tumor growth in human ovarian cancer xenografts without affecting liver cells.16,20

Here, we investigated lipopolyplexes (LPPs) as a delivery platform for Di-sRNAs in ovarian cancer (OC), prostate cancer (PC), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) animal models. Polyethyleneimines (PEIs) are cationic polymers capable of condensing nucleic acids into polyplexes (PPs) for efficient intracellular delivery.21 Low-molecular-weight PEIs modified with tyrosine (e.g., LP10Y) showed high siRNA delivery efficiency, low toxicity, and endosome-independent entry.22,23,24,25 When combined with the lipid DPPC (1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), PEIs form LPPs,25 which have shown strong tumor-targeting ability in vivo.23,26,27,28,29,30,31 We demonstrate that Di-sRNAs (sG5C or sCAG) delivered via LPPs effectively suppress or eradicate tumors in multiple rodent cancer models without harming normal tissues and that predicted SG targets are engaged. Analysis of 10 major human cancers revealed upregulation of SGs with disease progression, particularly those carrying sG5C seed matches, providing a mechanistic basis for cancer selectivity. These data establish LPP-Di-sRNAs as a potential platform for broad-spectrum anti-cancer therapy.

Results

Di-sRNAs delivered as lipopolyplexes selectively kill cancer cells

To deliver Di-sRNAs, we used two PEIs: F10 (branched, 10 kDa) and LP10Y (linear, 10 kDa, tyrosine-modified), which compact sRNAs into PPs subsequently coated with DPPC to form LPPs (Figure 1A). We tested four sRNAs: sG5C, carrying the toxic GGGGGC seed7; sCAG, the CAG-repeat-based toxic sRNA16; and two nontoxic controls, sNT1 and sNT8, containing nontoxic 6mer seeds identified from our 4096-seed screen (6merdb.org). To block passenger-strand activity, the first two nucleotides of each passenger strand were 2′-O-methylated (2′OMet) (Figure 1B). To exclude off-target effects from guide-strand activity in nontargeting controls, we created a fully disabled control (sNT8∗), in which both strands were terminally 2′-O-methylated. Proteomics of HeyA8 and PC3 cells transfected with 10 nM sNT8 or sNT8∗ revealed no downregulation of seed-match-containing genes by sNT8, whereas sG5C strongly suppressed GCCCCC-seed-match-containing SGs, with the degree of downregulation correlating with seed match number (Figure S1). Both sG5C-LPP and sCAG-LPP effectively killed diverse human and mouse OC and PC lines (Figure 1C), and both Di-sRNAs also killed other cancer types across six different species (Figure S2). In contrast, normal human fallopian tube epithelial cells (FTEC) and mouse peritoneal fibroblasts (PF) remained viable at doses lethal to immortalized derivatives (Figures 1D and 1F). A western blot analysis confirmed that FTEC cells expressing PAX832 immortalized with SV40LT lost PAX2 expression, reported to be lost even in early serous ovarian carcinomas,33 upregulated EZH2 as reported,34 and had upregulated p53 and signs of DNA damage35 (Figure 1E). Thus, LPP-delivered Di-sRNAs selectively kill malignant but not normal cells. Conserved survival genes targeted by Di-sRNAs

To identify conserved Di-sRNA targets, we transfected HeyA8 (OC) and PC3 (PC) cells with 10 nM sNT1, sG5C, or sCAG and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses at 24 and 48 h post-transfection. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed enrichment of predicted targets—genes harboring ≥1 GCCCCC 3′UTR site for sG5C or ≥10-nt CUG repeats for sCAG (Figure 2A). Among the 10 most significantly downregulated and most abundant genes (≥2-fold, adjp < 0.05) (Figure 2B), sG5C targets frequently contained multiple seed matches (>1) in their 3′ UTRs, while sCAG targets had extended CUG repeats (>10 nt) in their mRNAs, confirming selective engagement of predicted sites. Among the 10 most abundant genes (by reads) that were significantly downregulated, all but two sG5C target genes across both cell lines contained more than one seed match, despite the fact that 55% of genes carry only one. Similarly, all but two genes of sCAG-targeted genes contained CUG repeats longer than 10 nt against a background of 43.6% of genes only carrying CUG repeats of 10 nt in length. The most strongly downregulated SGs (e.g., HMGA1 and EIF5A for sG5C; RPL14 for sCAG) were selectively silenced by their respective Di-sRNAs (Figure 2C). We previously confirmed that sG5C is killing cells through selective targeting of a set of GCCCCC-seed-match-containing SGs and demonstrated a direct binding between sG5C and seed matches in such genes.7 Similarly, biotinylated sCAG pulled down the top four most abundant downregulated CUG-repeat-containing mRNAs (see Figure 2C) but not control genes (Figure S4A). Targeted knockdown of the top four sCAG-downregulated genes (using siRNA SmartPools targeting outside the repeat region) reduced cell growth of HeyA8 cells, with RPL14, a conserved DepMap SG showing the strongest effect (Figures S4B–S4D). RPL14 was also among the top targets in mouse ID8 DKO and human PC3 cells (Figures S3B and S2C), indicating evolutionary conservation. Cross-species comparison of predicted targets in human, mouse, and rat revealed overlapping sets of SGs enriched for multiple seed matches (sG5C) or long CUG repeats (sCAG) (Figures S3C–S3H). These conserved SGs may serve as biomarkers for treatment monitoring.

Because sG5C acts like an miRNA (translational repression)36 while sCAG resembles an siRNA (mRNA degradation),16 we compared RNA and protein changes upon transfection (Figure S5A). RNA-seq and proteomics showed strong correlation: genes with ≥4 seed matches or long CUG repeats were most significantly downregulated in both datasets. Notably, HMGA1, EIF5A, and RPL14 were consistently suppressed at both transcript and protein levels. Approximately 60%–75% of downregulated genes overlapped between cell lines (Figure S5B), confirming conserved SG targeting. As expected, little overlap was found in the targeted genes between the two Di-sRNAs (Figure S5C). These results show that RNA-based quantification can effectively monitor Di-sRNA target engagement, simplifying clinical translation.

Therapeutic efficacy in prostate cancer xenografts

Prostate cancer (PC) was selected for in vivo testing due to the androgen receptor (AR) harboring the longest CAG TNR in the human genome37,38 averaging 22 repeats in Caucasian males.39 Shorter repeats correlate with increased PC risk, aggressiveness, metastasis, and earlier onset.39,40,41,42,43,44,45 Most significantly, shortening of CAG repeat length is found in in situ PC lesions and its possible precursors,46 suggesting selection for shorter repeats during PC progression. In addition, DISE activity was validated in PC as androgen network DISE (AN-DISE),14 where AR-regulated SGs dominate target networks.

We compared sG5C-LPP and sCAG-LPP with siSurvivin-LPP, previously shown to suppress PC3 xenografts.27,47 Chemically modified sRNAs were designed for in vivo stability and selective guide activity (Figure 3A). Modified sRNAs retained strong cytotoxicity in vitro (Figure S6A). Nude mice bearing established PC3 tumors were treated with LPPs containing sNT1, siSurvivin, sG5C, or sCAG (Figure 3B). While siSurvivin modestly slowed tumor growth, sG5C was more effective, and sCAG was the most potent (Figure 3C). Six of ten mice treated with sCAG-LPP showed no tumor growth, with four showing no detectable tumors at endpoint. Importantly, no toxicity was observed: body weights were stable, liver morphology and enzyme levels normal, and TUNEL assays showed no hepatocyte death (Figures 3D and 3E). qPCR confirmed target engagement in tumors: HMGA1 (sG5C target) was reduced by >60% and LRRC59 (sCAG target) by >75% relative to sNT1-treated controls (Figures 3F–3H). Therapeutic efficacy in an orthotopic model of high-grade serous ovarian cancer

We next tested LPP-delivered Di-sRNAs in an orthotopic, immune-competent ovarian cancer (OC) model. ID8 immortalized murine ovarian epithelial cells gain malignant potential after p53 deletion (mutated in almost all high-grade serous ovarian cancers [HGSOCs]),48 and aggressiveness increases with additional Brca1/2 loss (DNA repair genes are mutated in >50% of HGSOCs).49 ID8 p53−/−Brca1−/− or p53−/−Brca2−/− (DKO) cells form tumors in mice that resemble aggressive human HGSOC.50 Both double mutant cells were more susceptible to sG5C than p53 single knockouts regardless of whether the sRNAs were introduced by LPPs (Figure S7A) or by transfection (Figure S7B).

C57BL/6 mice implanted intraperitoneally (i.p.) with ID8 DKO cells were treated i.p. with LP10Y-based LPPs containing sNT8, sG5C, or sCAG (Figure 4A). Both Di-sRNAs significantly reduced tumor burden without inducing liver damage, serum liver enzyme elevation, or TUNEL positivity (Figures 4B, 4C, and S8), and spleen weight was not affected (Figure 4D). Standard OC chemotherapy causes severe bone marrow (BM) toxicity.51,52 Comprehensive hematopoietic profiling revealed no changes in immune or stem/progenitor cell numbers (Figures 4E and 4F), and BM colony-forming assays showed normal function (Figure 4G). For clinical relevance, we also tested intravenous (i.v.) delivery of sG5C-LPP. While we experienced variable tumor take, tumor growth was reduced (Figure S9A). We did not detect any systemic toxicity. Therapeutic efficacy in orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma in immune competent rats

Given the liver enrichment of LP10Y-LPPs,47 we next tested Di-sRNA-LPPs in an orthotopic rat HCC model using McA-Rh7777 (McA) cells, which recapitulate features of human HCC.54,55,56 Rats were implanted with 2.5 × 106 McA cells into the left liver lobe, confirmed by MRI on day 10, and then treated i.v. with PBS, sNT8∗-LPP, or sG5C-LPP in an escalating dose regimen (Figure 5A). Control tumors grew progressively, whereas most sG5C-LPP-treated tumors stopped growing or regressed (Figures 5B and 5C). No signs of systemic or hepatic toxicity were observed: stable body weight, normal liver histology, minimal TUNEL staining, and no significant liver enzyme elevation (Figures 5D–5G and S10). BM colony-forming capacity was preserved (Figure 5I). We also did not detect signs of DNA damage (used as a surrogate cell death marker in decalcified samples) in endothelial cells lining the tail veins in which Di-sRNA-LPPs were repeatedly injected (Figure S11). Cytokine profiling revealed minimal inflammatory induction; only IP-10/CXCL10 was modestly elevated (Figure S12A). In a separate experiment, we determined that inflamed regenerating liver lobes (after tumor implantation surgery) had a slightly higher TUNEL positivity when compared to liver lobes with no implantation (Figure S12B). It is therefore interesting that even under these conditions normal hepatocytes had remained resistant to DISE. Finally, to test the upper therapeutic limit, we expressed highly toxic sCAG small hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) via an adenoviral vector (AdV-shCAG). High viral titers caused mild liver toxicity, but lower doses achieved complete tumor remission without adverse effects, suggesting a therapeutic window of 1–2 orders of magnitude (Supplementary Results including Figures S16–S22).

Mechanisms of cancer selectivity

We previously proposed multiple protective mechanisms against DISE in normal cells,19 two of which are most relevant here:

Increased SG expression in cancers. Cancer cells exhibit elevated expression of SGs, reflecting their need for high proliferation and stress resistance. Using 1,970 DepMap-defined SGs57 and the Gent2 dataset,57 we analyzed 10 cancers with sufficient staging data (Table S1). In all cancers, SG expression was significantly higher in tumors than in matched normal tissues (Figure 6A). Some cancers (ovarian, skin, and bladder) showed early-stage upregulation, while others (prostate, brain, liver, and breast) displayed stage-dependent increases in SGs, reaching 30%–40% of total gene expression. When SGs were ranked by average expression, enrichment was strongest in bladder and ovarian cancers and lowest in PC (Figures 6B and 6C). For PC the effectiveness of DISE to kill cancer but not normal prostate epithelial cells has been confirmed,14 and it was shown that in PC SGs are dominated by androgen-receptor-induced genes (AGs). We used a list of 448 AGs previously described.61 Consequently, just like classical SGs, AGs were also upregulated (Figure 6D). Loss of miRNA protection in cancer

We showed that Drosha, Dicer, and XPO5 knockout (KO) cells are hypersensitive to any DISE-inducing stimulus,6,9,10,11,12,62 suggesting that without expression of most miRNAs, the KO cells were no longer able to protect the RISC from loading toxic sRNAs. Cancer cells show global miRNA downregulation,63,64,65,66 likely diminishing RISC protection from toxic sRNAs. This loss correlates with reduced expression of miRNA biogenesis components, Drosha, DGCR8, and Dicer through multiple mechanisms.67,68,69 Analysis of OC datasets revealed marked reduction of these genes in advanced stages (Figure S14), suggesting impaired production of nontoxic miRNAs that normally occupy RISC complexes and buffer against toxic RNA loading.

To test the effects of the potent Di-sRNA sCAG on silencing CUG-TNR-containing genes in both tumor and liver cells, we performed an RNA-seq analysis of RNA isolated from either PC3 tumors or liver tissue of the tumor-bearing mice treated with either sNT1-LPP or sCAG-LPP (shown in Figure 3). In this analysis, 301 CUG TNR (10 nt or longer)-containing genes were detected in the human tumor cells and 211 such genes in mouse livers with ≥100 norm reads (Figure S15A). Of these genes, 50 were found in both cell types and species. Interestingly, when a cutoff of 1.5-fold was applied, 1,023 genes (11 of them CUG TNR containing) were significantly (≥0.05 adjp) downregulated in the human tumors versus only four genes (none of them are CUG TNR containing) in mouse livers (Figure S15B). Because treating PC3 tumors using a radioactively labeled siRNA complexed in (L)PPs was previously shown to accumulate in the liver, lung, tumor, kidney, lung, heart, and some accumulation in the brain,27,47 the Di-sRNAs are therefore likely delivered to these organs. The data are consistent with our model that the RISC in normal tissues is protected by large quantities of nontoxic miRNAs.

Collectively, these mechanisms, SG overexpression and miRNA/RISC depletion, explain the tumor selectivity of DISE in the following model. In normal cells, abundant nontoxic miRNAs saturate RISC, preventing toxic RNA entry (Figure 6E, top). In cancer cells, reduced miRNA buffering and elevated SG expression permit toxic Di-sRNAs to load into the RISC and trigger widespread SG suppression (Figure 6E, bottom).

Discussion

Most clinical RNAi approaches rely on siRNAs that target a single mRNA via perfect complementarity, limiting efficacy in cancer treatment due to escape mutations or pathway redundancy.70 By contrast, Di-sRNAs act through partial complementarity, engaging multiple essential transcripts simultaneously.

In human PC3 xenografts, chemically stabilized Di-sRNAs incorporating 2′-OMet modifications to enhance their in vivo stability and guided-strand specificity and to reduce immune activation markedly suppressed tumor growth, outperforming survivin-targeting siRNA controls. Notably, sCAG achieved complete tumor regression in 40% of treated PC3-tumor-bearing mice without detectable systemic toxicity, providing the first in vivo evidence that nanoparticle-delivered Di-sRNAs can eradicate established tumors. The choice of prostate cancer as a first in vivo model was strategic, given the established role of the AR as a CAG-repeat-containing oncogene with reduced CAG repeat lengths in advanced PC.39,41,42,43,44,45

Systemic delivery of Di-sRNAs also reduced tumor burden in immune-competent models of murine HGSOC and rat HCC. Both i.p. and i.v. administration were well tolerated, showing no liver toxicity, hematopoietic suppression, or cytokine storm, important in light of prior challenges with miRNA therapeutics (e.g., MRX34 and its associated IL-6-mediated toxicity).53 Modest elevation in cytokines such as the interferon gamma (IFN-γ)-inducible IP-10/CXCL1071 and CCL11 occurred in animals treated with both sNT8- and sG5C-complexed LPPs, suggesting that this was not caused by the activity of sG5C. A minor increase in IL-18 was detected only in rats treated with sNT8∗-LPP. The IL-18/IFN-γ/IP-10 axis regulates immune responses in conditions of viral infections and chronic inflammation, where it helps recruit immune cells to sites of inflammation.72 However, without induction of IFN-γ, it remains unclear how IP-10 could be induced by RNA-LPP complexes. Given IP-10’s role in recruiting CXCR3+ T and natural killer (NK) cells, these modest responses may even contribute to anti-tumor immunity.73 Importantly, normal tissues remained resistant to DISE across all treatment models, showing no TUNEL positivity or biomarker changes, even under inflammatory stress, defining a broad therapeutic window for Di-sRNA-based therapy.

There are currently six Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved siRNA therapeutics in clinical use.74 While early si- and miRNAs such as MRX34 were unmodified and could trigger innate immune activation—resulting in the production of inflammatory cytokines like IFN-α, TNF-α, and IL-6, primarily via TLR7/8 or RIG-I pathways—modern therapeutics mitigate this using chemical modifications (e.g., 2′-O-methylation) and LNP delivery technologies to enhance safety. While the Di-sRNAs used in this study carried up to 12 2′OMet modifications, which should substantially reduce innate immune activation, there is a potential to prevent immune activation by using different types and numbers of nucleotide modifications at levels that increase stability, reduce toxicity, and allow repeat treatment.

Di-sRNA-targeted SGs are highly expressed in proliferating or transformed cells, consistent with our cross-cancer transcriptomic survey (Figures 6A and 6B). The ability of sG5C and sCAG to exploit this vulnerability underpins their tumor specificity. In contrast, normal cells, with lower SG expression and intact miRNA/RISC buffering, remain largely unaffected.

Our findings strengthen the concept that DISE constitutes an intrinsic tumor-suppressive mechanism, potentially co-opted during evolution to prevent malignant transformation.19 Under physiological conditions, nontoxic miRNAs dominate RISC occupancy, thereby blocking access of toxic small RNAs. In cancers, miRNA loss or RISC depletion removes this safeguard, allowing toxic small RNAs (including Di-sRNAs) to engage and kill transformed cells (Figure 6E).

While both Di-sRNAs induce DISE, their molecular mechanisms differ. sG5C acts primarily through miRNA-like seed targeting of GCCCCC sites within 3′ UTRs, leading to translational repression and mRNA destabilization. sCAG, in contrast, triggers siRNA-like cleavage of complementary CUG repeat regions within coding or untranslated sequences.16,17 Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling revealed distinct but convergent target landscapes: sG5C downregulated SGs enriched in toxic seed matches (e.g., HMGA2 and eIF5A), whereas sCAG preferentially targeted SGs with long CUG tracts (e.g., RPL14). Despite their mechanistic differences, both pathways converge on the suppression of conserved SG networks required for malignant survival. RNA-based profiling mirrored proteomic findings, suggesting that transcript-level biomarkers can serve as practical indicators of DISE engagement in translational settings.

To determine whether normal tissues could be forced into DISE by overwhelming expression, Di-sRNAs were delivered using a high-expression adenoviral (AdV) system encoding a CAG/CUG-repeat-based shRNA (shCAG). Only at supraphysiologic doses (≥2 × 109 PFU) did we observe liver injury and cell death in hepatocytes by shCAG (but not by a control shRNA), indicating that normal cells can tolerate high but finite levels of toxic RNA expression. At clinically relevant doses (25-fold lower), AdV-shCAG induced tumor regression without hepatic toxicity or inflammation. Even at toxic doses, tumor-infiltrating liver regions were preferentially affected, confirming that DISE retains tumor specificity under conditions of extreme Di-sRNA expression. These findings demonstrate a wide therapeutic window in which cancer cells are efficiently targeted while normal tissues remain protected.

Combining DISE-inducing RNAs with standard therapies may further enhance outcomes. For instance, DISE triggers oxidative and genotoxic stress,6,7,8 potentially synergizing with DNA-damaging agents like platinum drugs or PARP inhibitors. Because DISE targets multiple SGs simultaneously, it could also overcome resistance mechanisms to targeted therapies.

In PC, where AR activity drives survival, combining sCAG with androgen deprivation or AR antagonists could provide additive or synergistic killing by concurrently suppressing AR-regulated SGs. Similarly, in ovarian and liver cancers, sG5C could enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy or immune checkpoint blockade by inducing immunogenic cell death.75

Limitations and future direction

Although LPP-mediated delivery achieved strong tumor selectivity, the F10 and LP10Y components show dose-dependent cytotoxicity, and complete tumor eradication was infrequent. Optimization of delivery systems such as ligand-targeted LNPs, liver-sparing formulations, or carrier-free RNA strategies and optimization of the chemical modifications in the sRNA will improve safety and translational feasibility. Future studies should expand efficacy testing to additional tumor models, assess long-term immunogenicity, and develop predictive biomarkers based on SG expression and miRNA/RISC profiles. Combination therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors or stress-inducing agents may further enhance DISE sensitivity. Comprehensive GLP toxicology and dose-escalation studies will be essential for IND-enabling development.

Translational significance

This work explores Di-sRNAs as a first-in-class, mechanism-based therapeutic platform that exploit molecular vulnerabilities unique to the malignant state to induce selective cell death. Unlike conventional cytotoxic agents, Di-sRNAs exert lethality through endogenous RNAi mechanisms that are inherently self-limiting in normal tissues. The distinct targeting modes of sG5C (seed-based) and sCAG (repeat-based) support both broad-spectrum- and tumor-context-specific applications. The ability to track DISE activity using RNA biomarkers offers a clear path toward patient stratification and real-time pharmacodynamic monitoring in early clinical trials. Together, this work provides a foundation for developing Di-sRNA-based therapies as safe, potent, and cancer-selective treatments ready for clinical translation.

Proteomics analysis

Sample preparation

Cell pellets were lysed in a buffer containing 0.5% SDS, 50 mM aqueous ammonium bicarbonate, 50 mM NaCl, and protease inhibitors (Halt, Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA was sheared using probe tip sonication, and the samples were kept on ice to mitigate heating. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min, and 100 μg of protein for each sample, as determined by BCA assay, was used for subsequent label-free proteomics. Overnight protein precipitation in acetone at −20°C was performed to produce a protein pellet, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed with ice-cold acetone, air dried for 30 min in a fume hood, and then resuspended in 100 μL of a denaturing buffer containing 8 M urea and 400 mM aqueous ammonium bicarbonate. Proteins were digested by an in-solution method including disulfide bond reduction (10 mM dithiothreitol, 50°C, 30 min) and alkylation (20 mM iodoacetamide, 25°C, 30 min protected from light). The urea concentration was diluted 4-fold with high purity water. Proteins were digested overnight with 1 μg of trypsin (Promega) at 37°C, followed by acidification with formic acid to halt the digestion. The resulting peptide solution was dried by vacuum centrifugation. Dried peptides were resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% aqueous formic acid and desalted using μ-C18 Ziptips (Millipore Sigma) and dried by vacuum centrifugation. Dried peptides were resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% aqueous formic acid, shaken and sonicated for 10 min each, and centrifuged for 5 min at 10,000 × g.

Data acquisition

Peptide supernatants were transferred to glass auto-sampler vials and then placed in the autosampler of a Thermo Fisher Scientific Vanquish Neo nano liquid chromatography instrument. Two microliters of sample was loaded onto a C18 trap column (Thermo Acclaim PepMap 100, 75 μm × 2 cm) and then transferred to a C18 analytical column (PepMap Neo, 75 × 15 cm). Peptides were separated using an increasing gradient of organic mobile phase (80% acetonitrile and 0.1% aqueous formic acid) and nano-electrosprayed into the heated source of a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. A 120-min data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method was used to acquire raw data files with the following settings: full scan (MS1): 2-s cycle time, 120,000 resolution, 350–1,600 m/z mass range, AGC target set to standard, and auto maximum injection time mode. Settings for ddMS2 (MS/MS) were as follows: charge states 2+ to 5+ inclusive, 30-s dynamic exclusion ±10 ppm, isolation width 1.4 m/z, HCD collision energy 30%, 15,000 resolution, AGC target set to standard, and auto maximum injection time mode.

Data processing and analysis

Raw data were processed using FragPipe v.22.0 with the LFQ-MBR workflow. Searches were performed against the Human SwissProt database (downloaded September 30, 2024). Variable modifications included lysine and N-terminal acetylation (+42.0106 Da) and methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da), allowing up to three variable modifications per peptide. DDA+ mode and MSBooster were enabled, and peptide scoring was conducted via the Koina server (https://koina.wilhelmlab.org:443/v2/models/). The resulting msstats.csv file was analyzed with MSstats Shiny v.1.6.2 using MSstats v.4.12.1. Processing options included log2 transformation of LFQ intensities, removal of runs with over 50% of missing values, imputation of missing values using the accelerated failure time model, and protein summarization with Tukey’s median polish. Statistical testing was performed using a mixed-effects linear model followed by ANOVA-based inference, and potential contaminants were excluded. Data visualization was carried out in R using ggplot2 v.3.5.1.

Real-time PCR

Two hundred nanograms (200 ng) of total RNA was used to make cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems #4368814). The qPCR was then done using the Taqman Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific #4369016) and the following primers (human) from Thermo Fisher Scientific: ARFGEF2 (Hs00197455_m1), BIRC5/Survivin (Hs04194392_s1), HMGA1 (Hs00600784_g1), ICMT (Hs00202655_m1), LRRC59 (Hs00372611_m1), POLE3 (Hs00925187_g1), PRKCSH (Hs00160457_m1), PSMA5 (Hs00160571_m1), RPL14 (Hs03004339_g1), SCARB2 (Hs01072100_m1), SND1 (Hs01032273_m1), and GAPDH (Hs00266705_g1). The relative expression of each target gene was normalized to the level of GAPDH. To confirm direct interaction with target mRNAs, biotinylated sRNAs (Bi-sNT1 or Bi-sCAG, 50 nM) were transfected into HeyA8 cells. After 20 h, streptavidin pulldowns were performed, and RNA was extracted for qPCR analysis of target enrichment compared to input as described before.7 Controls included non-repeat-containing genes with similar expression levels.

Xenografted PC mouse model

Male athymic nude mice (NMRI-Foxn1nu) were obtained from the animal core facility of the University of Leipzig (Medizinisch-Experimentelles Zentrum [MEZ]) and kept in a humidified atmosphere at 23°C and a 12-h cycle of light and dark. The mice were fed with standard rodent chow (ssniff, Soest, Germany) and water ad libitum. For establishing subcutaneous (s.c.) tumor xenografts, 5 × 106 PC3 cells in 150 μL PBS were injected into both flanks of the mice. After tumors reached a volume of approximately 80 mm3 and showed solid tumor growth, mice were randomized in treatment groups of n = 11–18 tumors per group and treated 3× per week by intraperitoneal injection of 20 μg LPP-formulated specific or negative control sRNA, respectively. Tumor volume was monitored over time using caliper measurements (using the formula v = 1/6π × a × b × c), and tumors were harvested at endpoint for qPCR analysis of target genes. Liver toxicity was assessed by histology (H&E and TUNEL) and serum liver enzyme quantification.

Syngeneic orthotopic OC mouse model

5 × 106 Luc-Neo ID8 p53−/−Brca2−/− (DKO) cells were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) into female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, strain code 027). Treatment with LPP-formulated sRNAs (20 μg/mouse, LP10Y-based) was initiated 7–10 days post-implantation and continued three times per week for 5 weeks. Tumor burden was monitored using the Lago in vivo imaging system (Spectral Imaging). The tumor load was quantified by the luminescence of the regions of interest (ROIs) (the same area for each mouse encompassing the entire abdomen) using the Spectral Live Imaging software. To visualize the tumor load in the mice, 200 μL of the sterile Luciferin stock solution (15 mg/mL in Dulbecco’s’ phosphate-buffered saline [DPBS] without Mg2+ and Ca2+) was i.p. injected per mouse (which equals approximately 10 μL of luciferin stock per gram of body weight). Mice were imaged 10 min after injection, at which point the kinetics curve began to plateau for approximately another 10 min. Images were captured during this steady-state period for all treatment groups. Total flux values of the different treatment groups were then compared. All measurements were performed using the same frame size, exposure time, and identical software settings for all animals and time points. Imaging was done one day before treatment initiation, and animals were sorted by tumor size as measured by Lago. The distribution of tumor sizes was evenly similar across all treatment groups. Imaging was performed once per week on the same day to maintain consistent time slots across all sessions.

Rat orthotopic HCC model

McA-RH7777 (McA) cells were prepared before implantation as resuspended in DPBS (Corning, NY, USA), with a concentration of 2.5 × 106 cells per 100 μL. Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 8–10 weeks, 300–400 g) were acquired from Charles River Laboratories (MA, USA), anesthetized with 2%–5% isoflurane in medical oxygen gas (Isothesia, Abbot Laboratories, IL, USA), and then laparotomy was performed to inject a 100 μL bolus containing 2.5 × 106 McA-RH7777 cells into the left lateral liver lobe using a 27-gauge (G) needle. The muscle layer and skin were sutured with 4-0 absorbable Vetacryl sutures (Ethicon). Postoperative analgesia consisted of subcutaneous meloxicam (Loxicom) at 2 mg/kg immediately after laparotomy and extended-release buprenorphine (BUP-ER) at 0.05 mg/kg s.c. upon recovery from anesthesia. Di-sRNA-LPP administration: LLP formulations containing the specified oligonucleotide sequence were administered at a volume of 2.25 mL per dose via either i.v. (tail-vein) or intraperitoneal injection. Dosing was performed on an every-other-day schedule (every 48 h) over a 2-week period, for a total of six injections per animal. Injection was prepared under sterile conditions and administered using standard aseptic technique, and animals were monitored after each dose for any acute adverse effects. Tumor monitoring: tumors were monitored using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; Bruker 7.0 T ClinScan, Bruker Biospin, Ettlingen, Germany) at the Center for Translational Imaging at Northwestern University. Ten days after tumor implantation, tumor size was assessed using T2-weighted turbo spin echo imaging. Scan parameters were as follows: TR/TE = 1,053/30 ms, turbo factor = 12, matrix = 256 × 256, field of view = 130 × 130 mm, 25 slices at 1 mm thickness, and bandwidth = 200 Hz/px. Tumor size was assessed on T2-weighted MR images. For each tumor, the maximal transverse diameter (two orthogonal measurements in the axial plane were taken and averaged to yield a, in mm) and the maximal cranio-caudal length (c, in mm) were measured. The MRI-derived tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × a2 × c, where a is the mean axial diameter, and c is the cranio-caudal length; resulting volumes are reported in mm3. Body weight was recorded for each animal at days 0, 7, and 14 using a calibrated scale. Percent change was calculated as (m(t) − m(0))/m(0) × 100%. Tissue harvest: each rat was humanely euthanized after 2 weeks of LPP treatment and MRI monitoring. HCC tissue, non-HCC tissue from tumor-bearing liver lobe, normal liver tissue from tumor-free liver lobe, tail segment containing the i.v. needle track, and bones for BM analysis were collected. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. At necropsy, blood was collected by cardiac puncture using a 23–25 G needle into VetCutaner blood-collection tubes. For serum, blood was drawn into plain tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 30 min. Then, serum supernatants were carefully transferred to clean, labeled microtubes and stored at −80°C until analysis. All steps were performed under aseptic conditions.

Histology and digital pathology

At indicated time points, animals were euthanized and tissues (liver, intestine, or peritoneum) harvested. Samples were fixed in 10% normal buffered formalin (VWR, Cat. No: 16004-128) for 16 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with H&E, subjected to TUNEL staining using a commercial apoptosis detection kit (Roche) involving 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, stained for γH2AX (using the phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (20E3) rabbit monoclonal antibody, Cell Signaling, Cat:#9718) by the Mouse Histology & Phenotyping Laboratory (MHPL) at the Robert H. Lurie Comprehensive Cancer Center. For decalcification, a 1-cm segment of tail tissue containing the injection site was excised, rinsed with tap water, and incubated in formic-acid-based bone decalcifier (Immunocal, PC-1414) for 24 h. The tissue was then washed in tap water for 5 min and transferred back to formalin before routine paraffin embedding. Slides were scanned using NanoZoomer 2.0-HT (Hamamatsu) by the Lurie Cancer Center Pathology Core. Digital images were viewed with NDP.view2 Next-Generation Viewer software for 20× (0.46 μm/pixel) scanned slides. All images were then transferred and analyzed by using the Visiopharm image analysis platform (Visiopharm, Horsholm, Denmark). For each whole slide image (WSI), first the tissue to be analyzed was identified and outlined as a ROI. Next, a built-in hematoxylin-DAB color deconvolution was used to separate the blue hematoxylin and DAB signals from each other. To identify nuclei and estimate their boundaries, a mixture of built-in polynomial filters was applied to the deconvolved hematoxylin signal. All nuclei in the tissue were identified by applying a threshold to the filtered image. Once nuclei were identified, the boundary of the cells were estimated by expanding the size of the nuclei by a set distance. In the case of closely juxtaposed cells, a limit was set to maintain them as separate objects. Once the cells were identified, deconvolved DAB signal was used to re-classify cells as positive or negative for the DAB signal based on an intensity threshold on the deconvolved DAB signal. To quantify percent positivity for DAB, we divide the count of positive cells by the total number of cells in each image.

Liver enzymes and cytokine quantification

Whole blood was collected from each animal via heart puncture after terminal euthanasia. In brief, mice or rats were sterilized with 70% alcohol spray, and the abdominal skin was opened to expose the diaphragm. Without opening the chest cavity, blood was collected through the intact diaphragm using a 3 mL syringe fitted with a 28 G needle. The negative intrathoracic pressure facilitated blood withdrawal. No anticoagulant was used, allowing the blood to clot naturally for subsequent serum collection. Blood was then allowed to gravity drip into Greiner Bio-One MiniCollect Capillary Blood Collection System Tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No: 22-030-400) that were kept on ice. The collection tubes containing the serum were spun at 3,000 g for 10 min at 15°C. After completion of the spin, two layers formed in the tube. The top layer containing the whole serum was collected, volume measured, transferred to new sterile Eppendorf Snap-Cap Microcentrifuge Safe-Lock Tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No: 05-402-25), frozen, and stored at −20°C and −80°C in two different tubes. The serum samples were analyzed using a liver function profile, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Charles River Research Animal Diagnostic Services, Wilmington, MA). For cytokine analysis, serum samples were diluted 1:2 with PBS and analyzed by Creative Proteomics (Shirley, NY) for 36 (mouse) and 27 (rat) cytokines, respectively.

Data analyses

Genes containing predicted sG5C seed matches (GCCCCC in the 3′ UTR) or CUG repeats (≥10 nt) in the mRNA were used for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). To determine the number of GCCCCC seed matches in the 3′ UTR of all human genes or in the genes containing CUG repeats of 10 to 19 nt in length in the ORF of genes, all ORFs and 3′UTRs were extracted from the Homo sapiens (GRCh38.p7) or Mus musculus (GRCm38.p5) gene dataset of the Ensembl database using the Ensembl Biomart data-mining tool. For each gene, only the longest deposited ORF or 3′ UTR was used, and genes with targeted sequences were identified as previously described.16 GSEA was performed using the GSEA v.4.0.3 software from the Broad Institute (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/). From the RNA-seq data, a ranked list was generated by sorting genes according to the Log10 (fold downregulation), and this list was then used to perform GSEA using the pre-ranked function with 1,000 permutations. A set of survival genes were downloaded from the DepMap site (https://depmap.org/portal/download/all). All Achilles_common_essentials and CRISPR_common_essentials genes were combined, and duplicates were removed, resulting in a list 2,165 essential survival genes (SGs). All significantly downregulated genes (adjp < 0.05, ≥1.5 or 2-fold change) in cells transfected with either sG5C or sCAG (versus cells transfected controls [sNT1 and sNT8] containing at least one putative seed match GCCCCC or a CUG repeat of at least 10 nt in length, respectively, were ranked according to highest expression (base mean). A list of 448 AGs were described previously.61

IC50 values were calculated using AAT Bioquest (aatbio.com). Venn diagrams and full symmetrical matrices were generated using the Molbiotools multiple list comparator (https://molbiotools.com/listcompare.php).

Bone marrow analysis

Flow cytometry

Murine hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells were identified and evaluated by flow cytometry using a single-cell suspension of bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs). BM cells were obtained from tibia, femur, and iliac crest (4–6 bones from each mouse) by flushing cells out of the bone using a syringe and PBS with 2 mM EDTA. Red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience) prior to staining. Hematopoietic stem and progenitor as well mature cells were defined and gated by surface markers: long-term hematopoietic stem cells (LT-HSCs, Lin−Sca1+cKit+CD150+CD48−), short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs, Lin−Sca1+cKit+CD150−CD48−), multipotent progenitors (MPPs, Lin−Sca1+cKit+CD150−CD48+), LSKs (Lin−Sca1+Kit+), megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEP, Lin−Sca1−cKit+CD34−CD16/32−), common myeloid progenitors (CMP, Lin−Sca1−cKit+CD34+CD16/32med), granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMP, Lin−Sca1−cKit+CD34+CD16/32+), myeloid progenitors (Lin−Sca1−Kit+), myeloid cells (Gr1+Mac1+), lymphoid cells (Gr1−Mac1−), B cells (Gr1-Mac1-B220+), and T cells (Gr1-Mac1−CD3+). Experiments were performed on BD LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by using the FlowJo_v10 software (TreeStar).

Colony-forming unit assay

The formation of colonies from mouse and rat BM cells was assessed using methylcellulose medium (MethoCult GF M3434 for mouse cells and MethoCult GF R3774 for rat cells, StemCell Technologies). A total of 20,000 BM cells were plated in each well of a 6-well plate, with three technical replicates and three biological replicates performed for each group. Colonies were counted after 7 days of culture.

Analysis of gene expression in 10 major human cancers

Expression on human cancers with matching normal tissue were downloaded at http://gent2.appex.kr. For each cancer, all U133PLus2 data were used. Only patient data were included in the analysis for which a pathologist had assessed grade or stage and only cancers were included in the analysis with a total of at least 100 patients per cancer (including normal tissue data). The following cancers, cancer subtypes, and stages/grades were included in the analysis (pin addition to matching normal tissue): bladder: stage pTA, pT1, pT2, pT3, and pT4; brain (astrocytoma): grade I, II, III, and IV; breast: each subtype with stage 2 and 3: basal, luminal A, luminal B, HER2; colon: microsatellite stable (MSS) and microsatellite unstable (MSI); liver: stage 0, A, and B + C; lung: non-small cell lung cancer, stage I, II, and III; prostate: Gleason score 4–6, 7, and 8–10; skin (melanoma): stage MIIB, MIIC, and MIV. For ovarian cancer, we selected two different sets of normal and cancer samples: (1) all normal versus stage I, II, III, and IV of all four subtypes (serous, mucinous, endometrial, and clear cell) or (2) only fallopian tube versus stage I, II, III, and IV of HGSOC. To plot the percent of expression of all SGs or selected genes, the norm counts of these genes was added up for each patient (normal and stages/grades) and plotted as percent of all genes in the form of boxplots with medians. Statistical significance of the changes across all normal/stages/grades for each cancer was determined by Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA test.

To generate the plots in Figures 6A and 6B and Figure S13, a total of n = 16,988 genes were available in expression data (n = 21,920) and seed data (n = 18,796). Of these, 1,966 were SGs and 508 contained GCCCCC. Total gene expression was calculated as the sum of expression values across all 16,988 genes (Eall), as well as for the 508 GCCCCC-containing SGs (EGCCCCC). For each sample, the proportion of gene expression among GCCCCC-containing SGs was calculated as p = EGCCCCC/Eall × 100%. Distribution across multiple samples for each disease stage is summarized using boxplots, which display the median (black line inside the box), 25th and 75th percentiles (the box), whiskers extending to the most extreme data points within ±1.5 × IQR (inter-quartile range), and individual points outside of this range. Linear regression models were used to determine the association between the percentage of expression (outcome) and cancer stage (ordinal predictor). The corresponding regression line is shown, and the p value corresponds to the hypothesis test of slope = 0 (no association).

Statistical analyses

IncuCyte experiments were performed at least in triplicates, and the data were expressed as standard error of the mean (SEM). Continuous data were compared using Student’s t tests for two independent groups. Two-way ANOVA was performed using the Stata1C software for evaluating continuous outcomes over time, choosing one component for the treatment condition as primary interest and a second component for time as a categorical variable. All other data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or SEM, as indicated. Comparisons between groups were made using two-tailed Student’s t tests unless otherwise specified. p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. RNA-seq and proteomics data were analyzed with multiple testing correction (Benjamini-Hochberg), and adjusted p values (adjp) < 0.05 were considered significant.
