Inequality in prevalence of unmedicated hypertension or diabetes among older Filipinos: analysis of nationally representative survey data
Authors: B. Supriadi, Aminuddin, Nurfatimah, Kadar Ramadhan
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the article by Kraft et al. on socioeconomic inequality in unmedicated hypertension and diabetes among older Filipinos [1]. Their finding that nearly one third of diagnosed individuals remained unmedicated, particularly among poorer groups, is highly relevant to cardiovascular risk reduction in the Philippines.

Some aspects of the analysis merit further clarification. The authors suggest that persistent inequality despite free medicines mainly reflects non-price barriers, which is plausible but may not fully capture diabetes care. As they note, public provision does not cover all antidiabetic needs, especially beyond limited oral agents, including insulin and related devices. The observed inequality may therefore reflect not only transport barriers, stockouts, awareness, and adherence, but also residual financial barriers from incomplete coverage of essential diabetes treatment. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence showing that diabetes treatment gaps in low- and middle-income countries remain closely linked to limited access to glucose-lowering medicines and diabetes technologies [[2], [3], [4]].

Another issue relates to the use of a composite outcome that combines hypertension and diabetes into a single measure of being unmedicated. Although this approach is understandable from a programmatic perspective, the two conditions differ in treatment pathways, monitoring requirements, and barriers to sustained medication use. This becomes especially relevant in light of the authors’ finding that respondents diagnosed with diabetes only had the highest adjusted risk of being unmedicated, despite representing a relatively small share of the analytic sample. The paper appropriately acknowledges that combining the two conditions may introduce clinically relevant misclassification, yet the main policy interpretation still rests heavily on this composite indicator. Recent care-cascade research increasingly evaluates hypertension and diabetes separately because inequities may emerge at different stages and for different reasons across the two diseases [2,5]. Presenting condition-specific primary analyses would therefore strengthen the clinical and policy interpretability of the findings.

The restriction of the analysis to respondents who had already been diagnosed also calls for cautious interpretation. The article shows that diagnosis itself was more common among individuals who were wealthier, more educated, female, urban, and otherwise socially advantaged. This means that the analytic sample had already been shaped by inequality at an earlier stage of the care continuum. For that reason, inequality in being unmedicated among the diagnosed should not be interpreted too readily as the full extent of inequality in treatment among all older adults living with hypertension or diabetes. Contemporary work on hypertension and diabetes cascades emphasizes that diagnosis and treatment are sequential and socially patterned stages, and disparities in diagnosis can substantially influence the composition of the population subsequently analysed for treatment gaps [2,5,6]. A more explicit framing of the results within a full cascade of care would help readers distinguish inequality in access to diagnosis from inequality in medication use after diagnosis.

Overall, this study highlights persistent treatment gaps among older Filipinos despite publicly supported medicine programs. Its implications would be stronger with clearer distinction between non-price and residual financial barriers, greater attention to disease-specific patterns, and fuller integration into the hypertension and diabetes care cascade.
