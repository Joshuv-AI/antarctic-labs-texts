Piezocatalytic Therapy for Periodontitis: From Biofilm Disruption to Immunomodulatory Tissue Regeneration
Authors: Shiqi Tian, Zihao Wang, Dawei Gao, Anshuo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by an interplay between microbial dysbiosis and dysregulated host immune responses. Traditional therapies, including scaling and root planing combined with localized antimicrobial administration, often struggle with the drug resistance of structured biofilms and persistent inflammation. Piezocatalysis is an emerging on-demand intervention that, in situ, transforms mechanical energy from both endogenous and exogenous sources into therapeutic reactive oxygen species (ROS) and bioelectric signals. Unlike conventional antibiotics, these locally generated ROS can degrade the biofilm matrix to eradicate resistant pathogens, while the concurrent bioelectric cues resolve persistent inflammation, offering a promising solution for periodontitis treatment. This review delineates the evolution of piezoelectric biomaterials and analyzes activation strategies tailored for the oral microenvironment. Key clinical translation bottlenecks, including the protein corona effect and biofilm penetration barriers, are critically addressed. Furthermore, synergistic methods that integrate nanocatalytic therapy and immune engineering strategies are highlighted to modulate the periodontal immune microenvironment. By leveraging stimuli-responsive elements and biomimetic materials, piezocatalytic platforms are evolving from passive bactericidal agents into bioactive modulators capable of simultaneously eradicating pathogens and guiding tissue healing. This shift marks a transition from disease control toward the precise reconstruction of periodontal homeostasis and functional tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a highly prevalent chronic inflammatory disease, and its essential pathology is understood as a polymicrobial disruption of host homeostasis. This condition arises from a breakdown in the dynamic equilibrium between the multispecies microbial community colonizing subgingival plaque and the host’s immune system. In a healthy state, the oral microbial community and the host maintain a symbiotic relationship. In susceptible individuals, however, this balance can shift toward a state of dysbiosis, which is characterized by a reduction in symbiotic bacteria and an enrichment of certain pathobionts, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis. Crucially, the destruction of periodontal tissue stems from the host’s chronic and uncontrolled inflammatory response to this dysbiotic microbiota. This process reveals the underlying pathology of periodontitis: a self-reinforcing vicious cycle between microbial dysbiosis and host inflammation. The dysbiotic microbiota triggers the host’s inflammatory response. In turn, tissue degradation products from the inflammatory process provide a nutritional source for these inflammation-favoring pathobionts, further exacerbating the microbial imbalance. , This positive feedback loop explains the chronic and persistent nature of periodontitis and reveals why conventional strategies that are solely antibacterial or anti-inflammatory often have limited success. An advanced therapeutic strategy aimed at long-term efficacy requires simultaneous intervention at both critical nodes of this cycle by effectively controlling the dysbiotic microbiota and actively modulating the host’s immune response, thus redirecting it from destructive inflammation toward pro-reparative processes.

Periodontitis is driven by a highly structured, multispecies biofilm (dental plaque), which poses a significant obstacle to conventional drug treatments. A biofilm is not a random cluster of bacteria, but an organized microbial community with a complex three-dimensional (3D) structure and functional heterogeneity, embedded in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. The EPS matrix constitutes the first line of defense against external attacks, and its dense network not only physically obstructs the penetration of drugs like antibiotics, but its charged properties can also adsorb and neutralize antimicrobial molecules. More importantly, chemical gradients within the biofilm can lead to metabolic heterogeneity among the internal bacterial community. Due to limited nutrient and oxygen supply, bacteria in the deeper regions enter a low-metabolic or dormant state, forming drug-insensitive persister cells. These persister cells can reactivate after antibiotic treatment concludes, leading to a rapid recurrence of the infection. Therefore, the periodontal biofilm not only harbors drug-resistant bacteria but also forms a pathological biological matrix that impedes drug penetration and bacterial eradication. This characteristic renders traditional treatment models that rely on the passive diffusion of drugs fundamentally ineffective. To overcome this barrier, new therapeutic strategies are needed that can bypass the penetration barrier and kill bacteria at the lesion site.

Piezocatalysis, as an emerging mechanochemical energy conversion technology, offers a new solution. Its core principle is to use piezoelectric materials to convert mechanical energy (whether from endogenous physiological activity or exogenous physical energy) into chemical energy, thereby driving redox reactions on the surface of piezoelectric materials. In the context of periodontal therapy, this process usually refers to the generation of polarized charges on the surface of a piezoelectric material under mechanical stress. These charges react with surrounding water molecules or dissolved oxygen to produce highly oxidative reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anions (•O2 –). These ROS are efficient, broad-spectrum bactericidal agents that can attack bacterial cell membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids. Simultaneously, they can degrade the organic macromolecules of the biofilm’s EPS matrix, achieving a dual impact of physical clearance and chemical sterilization on the biofilm. The prominent advantages of piezocatalysis lie in its on-demand and in situ characteristics. Piezoelectric materials are inert in the absence of mechanical stimulation and become activated only when triggered by a specific energy field. This gives the treatment a high degree of spatiotemporal control, enabling the therapeutic action to be precisely confined to the lesion area and thereby minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. Piezocatalytic strategies can be divided into two categories based on the energy source. The first utilizes low-intensity mechanical stress from daily oral activities, such as chewing and speaking, to achieve long-term, passive prevention or maintenance therapy. The second is triggered by an externally applied physical field (such as clinically common low-frequency ultrasound) to deliver an acute intervention for active lesions. Wang et al. pioneered the application of the piezocatalytic effect by activating BaTiO3 nanoparticles through simulated toothbrush vibrations, achieving nondestructive degradation of organic pigments on the tooth surface. This work provided a proof of concept for the feasibility of this technology in the oral environment.

The potential of piezocatalytic technology extends beyond its antibacterial effects. When piezoelectric materials are mechanically stimulated, in addition to the ROS-mediated chemical clearance, the accumulated charges on their surface also form a microelectric field or alter the surface potential, creating a mode of electrical signal modulation. This electrical signal can mimic the body’s natural bioelectric environment and influence cellular behavior. This characteristic transforms piezoelectric materials from mere bactericidal agents into bioactive platforms capable of modulating the tissue healing process and immune response, aligning with the core concept of modern regenerative medicine. Research has shown that electrical signals from piezoelectric materials can regulate the behavior of immune cells, for instance, by inducing the polarization of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby suppressing inflammation and initiating the tissue remodeling process. This again supports the transition of piezoelectric materials from a passive scaffold to a system that modulates the healing process, providing the physical foundation for realizing temporally controllable therapy.

Given the potential that piezocatalytic technology has demonstrated in addressing pathological challenges of periodontitis, this review aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of this interdisciplinary field (Figure ). This paper will first explain the working principles of piezocatalysis and discuss the current mainstream theoretical models. Subsequently, we will systematically outline the evolution of various piezoelectric material systems for oral applications, focusing on evaluating their performance, biocompatibility, and integration strategies. Following that, we will comprehensively analyze the advantages and disadvantages of endogenous and exogenous energy activation strategies and their respective application scenarios. Furthermore, we will focus on how to overcome practical barriers, such as biointerface interactions and energy delivery, and introduce strategies developed to address these obstacles. Finally, we will look to the future of the field, exploring how to integrate piezocatalytic technology as a core functional module into multimodal synergistic therapeutic platforms that incorporate nanozyme catalysis, immunomodulation, and advanced biomanufacturing. The goal is to realize a shift toward personalized periodontal therapy, moving from disease control to functional tissue regeneration.

Basic Principle of Piezoelectricity: From Mechanical Stress to Catalytic Activity

The piezoelectric effect was discovered in 1880 by Jacques and Pierre Curie. Noncentrosymmetric crystals generate internal electric dipoles under mechanical stress, which produce surface potential differences. The resulting piezopotential drives chemical reactions, a process termed piezocatalysis. The specific mechanism of piezocatalysis remains debated, and several theoretical models have been proposed.

Band Theory

The earliest proposed band theory drew on the principles of photocatalysis. It states that the piezopotential generated in a piezoelectric material under stress forms a built-in electric field. This field not only promotes the separation of internal charge carriers, electrons and holes, but also bends the band structure, enabling it to meet the thermodynamic requirements for redox reactions. , However, this theory has limitations. In theory, band theory for piezocatalysis does not clearly explain the origin of the charge carriers. In fact, band theory cannot account for all piezocatalytic phenomena. For example, it cannot fully explain the piezocatalytic performance of some wide-bandgap insulators, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) polymers. It also fails to clarify why materials like BaTiO3 can still efficiently catalyze ROS production, even when their static band structure does not match the required redox potential. Furthermore, in semiconductors, the piezopotential can be screened by internal free charges, which inhibits band bending. This has led to the development of other models.

Charge Screening Effect

Charge screening effect theory, also called bound charge theory, posits that the reactive participants are not the internal charges within the material, but rather external ions or polar molecules from the surrounding medium that screen polarized charges on the piezoelectric surface. When applied stress changes, these released screening charges become highly active energetic species and take part in the reaction. Through decoupling experiments on a ZnS/In2S3/BaTiO3 heterostructure, Zhou et al. showed that this mechanism may be dominant in certain systems. The catalytic activity demonstrated a strong positive correlation with the piezopotential, far exceeding that of material systems lacking the strongly piezoelectric BaTiO3 component. These results confirm that piezocatalysis does not strictly depend on the semiconductor band structure of the material.

Electrocatalysis-like Mechanism

Additionally, the electrocatalysis-like mechanism, first systematically proposed by Starr and Wang, regards the piezopotential as a localized, self-generated electrochemical bias that directly regulates the electronic interaction between active sites and reactants. Its core logic is consistent with how electrode potential influences reaction kinetics in electrocatalysis.

Displacement Current Theory

Ren et al. explained piezocatalysis using displacement current theory. This theory posits that dynamic stress induces a changing electric dipole moment in a piezoelectric material, which generates a displacement current. The dynamic electric field created by this current drives electrons from the piezoelectric effect to participate in the reaction. This theory is considered more fundamental because it is derived from Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory and provides an effective driving force for other mechanisms. It also explains why piezocatalysis generally requires dynamic stress, because only polarization intensity that varies over time can generate a displacement current, and the resulting dynamic electric field drives charges, whether they are internal carriers or external screening charges, to continuously participate in the reaction.

Although specific mechanisms are still under investigation, it is generally accepted that under mechanical force, active charges are generated on the catalyst’s surface. These charges undergo redox reactions with the surrounding media, such as water molecules and dissolved oxygen, producing highly oxidative ROS (like •OH and •O2 –). These ROS are the key substances for achieving therapeutic effects, particularly antibacterial activity.

Piezocatalytic Technology in the Oral Environment

As an emerging energy conversion technology, piezocatalysis has shown potential in oral medicine, owing to the dynamic energy features of the oral microenvironment. This technology uses piezoelectric materials to convert mechanical stress or thermal fluctuations into controllable bioelectric signals or chemically active substances, thereby achieving therapeutic goals.

In research on oral applications, the piezoelectric effect functions through two modes. The first is oxidative scavenging, which generates ROS to degrade pigments or kill pathogens. The second is electrical signal modulation, which produces a microelectric field that simulates physiological bioelectric signals to regulate cellular behaviors and promote tissue regeneration. , This mechanism provides a theoretical basis for exploring piezoelectric applications in antibacterial treatment, whitening, and tissue regeneration, according to diverse clinical needs. Using endogenous energy from daily mechanical activities such as chewing and oral temperature changes to drive self-powered therapeutic systems is a promising strategy. However, whether this energy is sufficient for certain high-intensity treatments requires further confirmation.

From a physical mechanism perspective, the two functional modes of the piezoelectric effect arise from a single energy conversion process: mechanically induced charge separation. , When a piezoelectric material undergoes mechanical stress, it generates a built-in electric field. This field separates charge carriers and prevents their rapid recombination. These separated charge carriers form the physical basis for all subsequent biological effects. When charges migrate to the material surface and undergo redox reactions with surrounding water molecules or dissolved oxygen, ROS are generated, thus activating the oxidative scavenging mode. This represents one outcome of charge separation. If charges accumulate on the surface, they form a local microelectric field that directly acts on adjacent cells. This field can modulate membrane potentials, govern ion channels, and guide cytoskeleton organization, thereby realizing the electrical signal modulation mode. This core mechanism illustrates the potential of piezoelectric materials in oral medicine, while linking oral applications to wider research in biomedical and environmental sciences, including sonodynamic therapy (SDT) and piezo-photocatalysis. ,

Evolution and System Integration of Piezoelectric Materials

The progress of piezoelectric technology in the biomedical field is closely linked to advances in materials science. To address the diverse pathological challenges in periodontitis, piezoelectric materials explored for oral and periodontal therapies can be explicitly categorized into inorganic ceramics, metal oxides, polymers, and emerging two-dimensional (2D) materials. Their development shows a shift from focusing solely on high piezoelectric performance to balancing biosafety, clinical applicability, and multifunctional integration. Comprehensive Summary of Stimuli-Responsive Materials for Oral Therapy

Early research focused on inorganic ceramics such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT). Its high piezoelectric coefficient (d33 value of 225–590 pC/N) supported the feasibility of piezoelectric applications in biology. However, the inherent biotoxicity of lead and potential risks of in vivo ion dissolution raised biosafety concerns, restricting clinical translation and shifting research toward lead-free alternatives like potassium sodium niobate (KNN) and lithium-doped KNN (LKNN). ,, Although pure KNN exhibits low cytotoxicity and can collect micromechanical stimuli in the oral cavity, such as bite force, to convert into electrical signals for guiding the directional differentiation of dental pulp and periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), it remains in the laboratory stage as an advanced biomimetic bioelectronic material. Furthermore, the release of dopant ions (e.g., Li+) required for performance optimization may cause mild cytotoxicity and compromise long-term stability. , This suggests that lead-free design requires a new balance between piezoelectric performance and biosafety of all components. Consequently, current translational research for periodontal therapies heavily prioritizes the specific material categories detailed below.

Inorganic Ceramics: Hydroxyapatite (HA) and Barium Titanate (BaTiO3)

As the main inorganic component of bone and teeth, the bioactivity of HA has been widely confirmed. Clinically, HA is widely used as a bioactive material with adequate clinical success rates in dentistry. In periodontology and implantology, HA bone substitute grafting is utilized to treat periodontal bone defects and promote alveolar ridge preservation. , In preventive dentistry, biomimetic HA is incorporated into oral care products to mechanically occlude exposed dentinal tubules for reducing dentin hypersensitivity, and to enhance enamel remineralization to counteract early carious lesions. , Beyond these established chemical and structural advantages, the dynamic remodeling of natural bone is deeply dependent on its mechano-electrical microenvironment. Research on the piezoelectric properties of bone tissues dates back to the mid-20th century, when this effect was once attributed solely to the collagen matrix. , Recent discoveries of the intrinsic piezoelectricity of HA have updated this understanding. Although its piezoelectric coefficient is lower than most engineered ceramics, its physiological role in the body is crucial. Modern research confirms that the piezoelectric response of bone arises from the synergistic effect of the organic collagen matrix and HA nanocrystals. This natural mechano-electrical coupling effect supports the self-repair and remodeling of bone tissue under mechanical loads. To emulate and amplify this natural mechanism, combining HA with materials that have stronger piezoelectric properties, such as BaTiO3, allows the design of bioactive scaffolds with enhanced electrical response, with the aim of more effectively guiding the bone regeneration process. ,

Within this family of lead-free biocompatible piezoelectric ceramics, BaTiO3 has drawn attention for its high piezoelectric coefficient, ferroelectric properties, and good biocompatibility. , Although BaTiO3 has yet to enter clinical practice, it exhibits tremendous translational potential in bone tissue engineering, provided that the issues of long-term safety and immune response arising from its in vivo nondegradability are effectively resolved, particularly the risk of released Ba2+ ions displacing Ca2+ in dental tissues and causing heavy metal accumulation. ,, Its dental applications include not only tooth whitening but also antibacterial effects. Studies have confirmed that BaTiO3 nanoparticles can effectively inhibit the growth and biofilm formation of common oral pathogens like Streptococcus mutans. As an efficient piezocatalyst, BaTiO3 is widely used in medical and environmental fields with ultrasound assistance, showing potential as a multifunctional platform. In tissue regeneration, when coated on 3D-printed titanium implants or bone scaffolds and combined with low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) or masticatory mechanical forces, BaTiO3 composite scaffolds can generate a local electrical microenvironment that mimics the natural bioelectric microenvironment of bone tissue, significantly promoting the adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. ,,,, This regeneration strategy guided by electricity imitates the natural piezoelectric properties of bone tissue.

Piezoelectric Metal Oxide: Zinc Oxide (ZnO)

ZnO shows good biocompatibility and antibacterial properties for oral medicine applications. , Clinically, ZnO is a foundational metal oxide in dentistry. Traditionally, macroscopic ZnO has been widely utilized in zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE) cements and periodontal dressings, relying primarily on added agents like eugenol or physical barrier effects for clinical efficacy. Recently, research has shifted toward nanoscale ZnO, which exhibits excellent intrinsic antibacterial activity. Its antibacterial effects rely on multiple pathways, including piezocatalytic ROS generation, Zn2+ ions release, and direct physical contact between nanoparticles and bacterial cell membranes. These processes damage bacterial membranes and cause intracellular content leakage. In dental restorative materials, adding ZnO nanoparticles to resin composites significantly inhibits cariogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans and prevents biofilm formation. ZnO is also applied in periodontal therapy. Studies have shown that gels containing ZnO nanoparticles inhibit the growth of periodontal pathogens through their antibacterial and antibiofilm properties, reducing plaque adhesion. , Recent clinical evidence further validates this in vitro finding. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the repeated adjunctive application of nano-ZnO gels, combined with visible light photocatalysis, significantly reduced subgingival Porphyromonas gingivalis levels and provided sustained improvements in periodontal clinical parameters over three months. However, the specific application of ZnO as a piezocatalyst driven by mechanical force remains largely confined to the in vitro experimental stage. Additionally, in piezoelectric composites, such as ZnO/PVDF systems, ZnO serves not only as a piezoelectric-enhancing phase, but its semiconductor properties also facilitate charge separation during the piezoelectric process, thus improving catalytic efficiency.

Polymers: Poly-l-Lactic Acid (PLLA) and Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF)

Clinically, in periodontal guided tissue regeneration (GTR) and guided bone regeneration (GBR), PLLA-based membranes combined with chitosan act as a physical barrier to prevent fast-growing gingival soft tissues from invading the bone defect area, leaving sufficient space for osteoblasts and guiding periodontal tissue regeneration, while their biodegradable nature avoids the need for a second surgery. , Furthermore, the highly porous, 3D PLLA nanofibrous architecture can load antibiotics such as doxycycline and be introduced into periodontal pockets, achieving a localized, temporally controlled sustained release to combat periodontal infections. , Although basic PLLA/PLGA membranes are commercially mature as clinical physical barriers, complex targeted drug-releasing PLLA/PLGA nanofiber networks are still confined to the preclinical-to-clinical translation stage, primarily hindered by initial burst release and scale-up challenges. To transition from passive barriers to active regenerative platforms, the advanced piezoelectric applications of PLLA are being extensively explored. In biocompatible polymers, PLLA generates piezoelectricity mainly through its shear piezoelectric effect (d14), which differs from the normal piezoelectric effect (d33) observed in materials like PVDF. , Electrospun PLLA nanofiber scaffolds not only mimic the topological structure of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) but can also generate surface charges under mechanical loads, thereby promoting bone regeneration. , Research indicates that the osteogenic effect of PLLA scaffolds depends on piezoelectric stimulation rather than simple mechanical stimulation, and scaffolds with higher piezoelectric charge produce stronger osteoinductive effects. However, their advanced piezoelectric applications in clinical settings remain to be further explored. ,

PVDF and its copolymer, poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-trifluoroethylene) (P­(VDF-TrFE)), are the most studied piezoelectric polymers. Their piezoelectric properties primarily derive from the polar β-phase crystalline form. , Electrospinning is used to prepare PVDF fiber membranes rich in β phase, whose flexible and porous structure makes them suitable as scaffolds for tissue engineering. Combining PVDF with bioactive ceramics such as HA produces bioactive and piezoelectric membranes for GBR. In oral mucosal repair and nerve regeneration, P­(VDF-TrFE) and related electrospun scaffolds support Schwann cell growth and guide the directional extension of nerve axons. , This provides an experimental basis for their application in repairing soft tissue and nerve damage in the oral and maxillofacial region, though these applications generally remain in the preclinical animal testing phase.

Emerging 2D Materials: Black Phosphorus (BP)

As an emerging 2D biomaterial, few-layer BP nanosheets demonstrate remarkable piezoelectric properties derived from their noncentrosymmetric lattice structure, while concurrently possessing exceptional bioactivity. Upon mechanical stimulation, the transient deformation of its highly ordered crystal lattice induces atomic displacement, resulting in piezoelectric polarization and band bending that facilitates robust piezocatalytic capabilities. Beyond its mechano-electrical coupling potential, BP exerts triple synergistic therapeutic effects in physiological microenvironments. First, under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, it exhibits high photothermal conversion efficiency to eradicate local bacteria. Second, its inherent peroxidase-like activity endows it with potent ROS-scavenging capacity, alleviating oxidative stress within the periodontal pocket. Third, in physiological environments, BP progressively degrades into nontoxic phosphate ions (PO4 3–), which serve as natural precursors for biomineralization and promote the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts. Experiments confirm that multilayer BP generates detectable current output under compressive strain, further cementing its role as a dynamic mechano-electrical biomaterial. , However, due to its rapid oxidative degradation in complex physiological environments, the clinical translation of BP currently remains at the preclinical in vivo stage. To prevent premature biodegradation and maximize its piezoelectric efficacy, its successful application relies on structural modifications and integration into stabilized composite systems, such as hydrogels, bioactive glass, or functional scaffolds, rather than utilizing bare nanosheets.

Integrated Multifunctional Systems

The research frontier in this field has shifted from optimizing single materials to designing integrated multifunctional systems. A typical representative is the piezoelectric Janus membrane, which is engineered to resolve conflicting clinical demands in periodontal regeneration. Its gingival side prevents soft-tissue ingrowth and provides antibacterial effects, while the side facing bone defects promotes bone regeneration. Such Janus structures integrate antibacterial and pro-regenerative functions, achieving therapeutic outcomes superior to traditional single-function barrier membranes. In addition to using piezoelectric materials for charge asymmetry, researchers use methods such as lamination, cospinning, and surface modification. These methods create distinct physical structures or chemical functionalities on opposite sides of biodegradable polymer membranes like PLLA, including dense and porous layers, hydrophilic or hydrophobic properties, and drug-loaded or drug-free characteristics. This design achieves multiple goals, including GTR, antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory effects, and immunomodulation.

Another important direction is the fabrication of composite scaffolds. Incorporating piezoelectric ceramic nanoparticles into biomaterial matrices endows the scaffolds with both piezoelectric potential and favorable biomechanical properties.

Furthermore, 3D printing enables the fabrication of piezoelectric composite scaffolds with complex geometries and controlled pore structures, which is crucial for repairing irregular maxillofacial bone defects. Mixing ceramics like BaTiO3 or β-tricalcium phosphate with polymers such as polycaprolactone allows the printing of personalized bone repair scaffolds. These scaffolds combine bioactivity, a piezoelectric response, and suitable mechanical strength. − Supported by precise structural and functional customization via 3D printing, such integrated designs can comprehensively address complex clinical requirements and greatly facilitate the practical translation of related technologies. ,

These system-integration strategies advance piezoelectric biomaterials from functional substances toward intelligent therapeutic devices. Nevertheless, realizing their full potential requires overcoming macro-level translational barriers. Currently, the progression from bench to bedside is heavily impeded by unpredictable therapeutic efficacy within the highly dynamic oral microenvironment. Furthermore, these translational challenges are compounded by a stark lack of supporting clinical data and the complete absence of relevant biological evaluation guidelines tailored for mechano-electrical coupling materials in dental medicine.

Activation Strategies by Energy Fields

After determining the composition and structure of piezoelectric biomaterials, effective functional activation becomes a key scientific issue. The piezoelectric effect relies on input from energy fields. In the oral environment, energy sources can be divided into two main categories: endogenous and exogenous. These two activation strategies differ in energy efficiency, tissue penetration, and spatiotemporal controllability, which in turn determine their respective applications and limitations.

Endogenous Activation

This strategy utilizes the mechanical and thermal energy generated from daily oral physiological activities, aiming to achieve self-powered and passive therapy suitable for scenarios requiring long-term, low-intensity intervention.

Mechanical Energy Utilization

Continuous stress from chewing and occlusion is the main energy source. Related applications include accelerating alveolar bone remodeling in orthodontic treatment by integrating PVDF thin films, achieving antibacterial effects and periodontal tissue regeneration using chewing-driven Janus membranes, and monitoring occlusal force through piezoelectric sensors to expand functions from therapy to precise assessment. ,,

This approach of utilizing endogenous mechanical energy is essentially biomechanical energy harvesting. The substantial force from chewing serves as a continuous power source for low-power piezoelectric therapeutic systems. In orthodontics, in addition to using piezoelectric thin films, research has proposed piezoelectric orthodontic appliances with PVDF actuators. These apply low-frequency (e.g., 30 Hz) microvibrations to actively accelerate alveolar bone remodeling. This method is more controllable than passive reliance on chewing force. In diagnostics, sensors based on flexible piezoelectric materials like PVDF can be integrated into occlusal splints or mouthguards. They enable real-time, noninvasive monitoring of occlusal forces, providing objective data for the diagnosis and treatment evaluation of temporomandibular joint disorders or bruxism.

Thermal Energy Utilization

Temperature fluctuations from food and drink can be captured by pyroelectric materials. Based on this principle, a dental brace loaded with BaTiO3 nanowires can catalyze ROS production under thermal stimulation to achieve passive tooth whitening.

The pyroelectric effect is closely related to the piezoelectric effect, and all pyroelectric materials are also piezoelectric. Within the oral cavity, alternating between hot and cold food and drinks can produce temperature changes of several to even tens of degrees. These rapid temporal temperature variations produce significant surface charge accumulation in pyroelectric materials, thereby driving a catalytic reaction.

Exogenous Activation

Endogenous energy fields often lack sufficient strength or penetration depth for deep tissue lesions, nor can they provide on-demand high-intensity intervention. Researchers have thus adopted exogenous energy fields, particularly low-frequency ultrasound, as a key intervention method.

Ultrasound Activation

Ultrasound provides high-frequency micromechanical pressure and is widely used due to its good tissue penetration. Its core advantage is the ability to focus energy on deep lesions. Ultrasound activates piezoelectric materials in target regions for applications including tooth whitening, antibacterial treatment of peri-implantitis, and therapy for infected bone defects. ,, This strategy has also been applied in SDT and offers a new method for targeted therapy of oral and maxillofacial tumors.

Piezocatalytic therapy activated by ultrasound is an important branch of SDT, which addresses the low efficiency of conventional sonosensitizers. In peri-implantitis treatment, the persistent bacterial biofilms present a major challenge. Studies have shown that piezoelectric coatings, such as BaTiO3–x/LA hybrid coatings on the implant surface, combined with low-intensity ultrasound, efficiently generate ROS, thereby eliminating biofilms in situ with minimal damage to surrounding tissue.

To further enhance antibacterial efficacy, some studies have combined piezoelectric materials with chemodynamic copper-based materials to form a piezo-driven tandem catalytic system. In this system, ultrasound activates the piezoelectric effect to generate ROS and promotes the valence conversion of copper ions, which further produces highly oxidative •OH. Meanwhile, copper overload induces bacterial cuproptosis-like cell death. In oral tumor therapy, ultrasound activates nanoparticles loaded with sonosensitizers such as hematoporphyrin monomethyl ether. This specifically induces pyroptosis in tumor regions. Pyroptosis not only kills tumor cells but also releases tumor-associated antigens and inflammatory factors. These factors activate the body’s antitumor immune response, offering a new immunotherapy for malignant tumors like oral squamous cell carcinoma.

Research Progress of Piezoelectric Materials in Periodontitis Treatment

Core Challenges in Clinical Translation

The Dilemma of Energy Activation Modes: A Trade-Off between Endogenous Stress and Exogenous Acoustic Fields

The core challenge of using physiological activities such as chewing lies in their low-frequency (approximately 1–2 Hz), low-intensity, and nondirectional characteristics. Currently, in vivo data fail to confirm whether this energy reaches the therapeutic threshold deep in the periodontal pocket. Comparison of Endogenous and Exogenous Energy Activation Strategies for Piezocatalytic Therapy

Beyond intermittent chewing, more constant and rhythmic physiological processes are also explored to power implantable medical devices. For instance, energy harvesters based on piezoelectric materials have been designed to convert the mechanical energy from arterial pulsations or respiratory movements into electrical energy, with a theoretical peak power output that can reach the microwatt level. , Biological systems have inherent mechanochemical energy conversion mechanisms, such as the regulation of vascular tension mediated by mechanosensitive ion channels. These mechanisms provide a biological basis for this research direction. Therefore, despite the limitations of chewing force in treating acute periodontitis, using the body’s endogenous energy to provide continuous power for low-power, long-term periodontal maintenance or regeneration devices remains a significant frontier direction.

For deep lesions and clinical needs that require on-demand high-intensity intervention, LIPUS has garnered attention for its tissue penetration and controllable energy. However, its clinical translation faces the challenge of dosimetry. The frequency, power density, duration, and pulse parameters of ultrasound must be systematically optimized to maximize catalytic efficiency while avoiding irreversible thermal damage or cavitation effects to the surrounding healthy periodontal tissues.

In addition to dosimetry, the application of ultrasound to bone-related diseases is also physically limited by acoustic impedance mismatch. When ultrasound waves travel from soft tissue to hard tissue (such as alveolar bone), the significant difference in acoustic impedance causes most of the acoustic energy to be reflected at the interface, thereby limiting effective energy delivery to deep-seated lesions. To address this problem, the magnetoelectric (ME) activation strategy stands out for its unique properties. This strategy typically uses core–shell structured ME nanoparticles, for instance, CoFe2O4 serves as the magnetostrictive core and BaTiO3 acts as the piezoelectric shell. Under an external, low-frequency alternating magnetic field, the core deforms and transfers the strain to the piezoelectric shell, which in turn generates charges on the particle’s surface to achieve piezocatalysis. Magnetic fields can penetrate biological tissues, including bone, with very low attenuation (approximately 1–2 dB/cm). In comparison, ultrasound waves attenuate much more (approximately 10–12 dB/cm). Therefore, the energy transmission efficiency of magnetic fields is much higher, enabling more energy to be delivered to deep periodontal bone defects surrounded by alveolar bone. Furthermore, microwave fields show potential as another noncontact energy source, as their alternating electromagnetic field can cause high-frequency vibration of polar molecules to remotely excite piezoelectric materials, but their biomedical applications are still in exploration. These alternative energy delivery methods, especially ME coupling, offer a new physical and technological basis for treating deep periodontal lesions.

Shielding and Identity Remodeling by the Protein Corona

Similar to other nanomaterials in the gingival crevicular fluid, where charge screening promotes nonspecific interactions, piezoelectric nanoparticles with specific surface potentials can rapidly adsorb proteins to form a protein corona. , This protein layer directly shields the material’s catalytic active sites, thereby markedly inhibiting catalytic efficiency. Moreover, it reshapes the material’s biological identity, which further modulates the material’s recognition and clearance by immune cells, ultimately determining its biodistribution and half-life within tissues. , This means that the excellent catalytic performance observed in an in vitro buffer solution may be significantly diminished in the in vivo environment due to protein corona formation.

To counter this challenge, surface engineering has become a core strategy in the field of nanomedicine. While traditional PEG modification can reduce protein adsorption, the effect is incomplete, and repeated use may trigger an anti-PEG immune response. , A recent research trend is the use of zwitterionic polymers for surface coating, such as polysulfobetaine and poly­(phosphorylcholine) (PPC). These polymers form a dense and stable hydration layer with water molecules, creating a physical and energetic barrier that resists the nonspecific adsorption of biological macromolecules. Compared with coatings like PPC, a polysulfobetaine coating can almost completely inhibit protein corona formation even in a whole serum environment. This antifouling property offers a potential solution for piezoelectric nanomaterials to maintain their intrinsic catalytic activity and avoid rapid immune clearance in complex biological fluids.

The Dual Barrier of the Biofilm

The pathogenic core of periodontitis is a highly structured, multispecies biofilm. The biofilm is encased in a self-secreted EPS matrix, which forms a physical barrier that hinders the penetration of nanocatalysts into its interior. At the same time, the local hypoxia, acidic pH, and chemical gradients within the EPS matrix create a biochemical barrier that can consume or neutralize the ROS generated by piezocatalysis, making it difficult for them to reach and kill the core pathogenic bacteria deep within the biofilm.

The complexity of the EPS matrix, which is primarily composed of extracellular polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA), offers a basis for developing therapeutic strategies. Among these components, eDNA acts as a key structural scaffold for maintaining the biofilm’s macroscopic structure and mechanical stability. Therefore, the active degradation of eDNA has become a strategy for destroying the biofilm’s structural skeleton. To this end, researchers have developed nanodelivery systems loaded with deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) that preserve the enzyme’s activity while targeting its delivery to the biofilm. DNase I catalyzes the hydrolysis of eDNA, which disrupts the structural integrity of the biofilm and increases its permeability. This enzymatic degradation strategy not only weakens the biofilm’s defenses but also creates a pathway for subsequent antimicrobial agents to penetrate the biofilm, thereby facilitating the synergistic therapeutic effect of piezocatalysts.

Regulatory Bottlenecks and Biosafety Evaluation Standards for Long-Term Implantation

The clinical translation of piezoelectric nanomaterials is primarily impeded by the limited predictive validity of animal models, challenges in scalable good manufacturing practice (GMP) fabrication, and a lack of standardization in preclinical testing. Because piezoelectric scaffolds for periodontal regeneration are classified as biodegradable implants with prolonged tissue contact (24 h to 30 days), their safety is strictly governed by standards like ISO 10993–1:2025. This classification mandates rigorous evaluation beyond acute cytocompatibility, requiring thorough assessment of chronic toxicity, local immune activation, and degradation hazards, such as the potentially toxic leaching of metallic ions from piezoceramics like BaTiO3. Furthermore, regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), heavily scrutinize nanoscale modifications. , Incorporating piezoelectric additives into historically safe base materials (e.g., HA) fundamentally alters their geometric and chemical profiles, thereby invalidating prior safety data. To navigate these hurdles and minimize in vivo animal testing, a formal biological evaluation plan (BEP) is essential. This plan must integrate chemical characterization (ISO 10993–18) with toxicological risk assessment (TRA, ISO 10993–17) to quantify chemical leachables. Ultimately, the key bottleneck lies in demonstrating that localized ROS generation, bioelectric signals, and long-term degradation products do not elicit unpredictable systemic toxicity or adverse host immune responses.

Strategies to Address the Complex Oral Microenvironment

To address challenges in the translation from basic research to clinical application, the focus has moved from optimizing the intrinsic physical or catalytic properties of materials to integrating material design with the complex pathological environment. To bridge the gap between in vitro testing and in vivo performance, research frontiers are advancing in two main directions: first, establishing biomimetic evaluation systems that predict materials’ in vivo behavior more accurately, and second, developing smart therapeutic systems that can actively recognize and respond to signals from the pathological microenvironment.

Establishing More Predictive Biomimetic Testing Models

A recognized limitation of current research is that traditional in vitro antibacterial testing models cannot simulate the complex pathological ecology in vivo adequately. For instance, tests based on planktonic bacteria or single-species biofilms fail to reflect the actual conditions within periodontitis lesions, which are composed of hundreds of microbial species and exhibit a high degree of spatiotemporal heterogeneity.

To overcome this limitation, a research trend is to construct biomimetic models that reflect physiological reality more closely. For instance, when evaluating the antibacterial performance of materials, researchers have started using mature multispecies biofilm models cultured on HA surfaces to simulate the tooth surface. These models include various periodontal pathogens, such as P. gingivalis, and can replicate the complexity of real dental plaque in terms of species diversity and spatial structure. As shown in Figure A, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) clearly reveals the mature multispecies biofilm formed on the HA surface, which presents a typical 3D structure. The bacteria are tightly encased by their self-secreted EPS matrix (red portion in the figure), forming a physical and biochemical barrier. High-resolution imaging allows for a quantitative assessment of the comprehensive impact of piezocatalytic therapy on the biofilm’s 3D structure, bacterial viability, and EPS matrix degradation. Furthermore, the academic community increasingly recognizes that a crucial step for predicting in vivo behavior is to preincubate piezoelectric nanomaterials in simulated gingival crevicular fluid or human serum before conducting a biological assessment. This allows for the formation of a stable protein corona on the material’s surface, which reshapes the material’s biological identity, serving as the interface for direct interaction with host cells and bacteria.

Although the static models described above experienced advancements, they still cannot simulate the dynamic interactions between the host and microorganisms within a periodontal lesion. To simulate this dynamic process more realistically, cutting-edge research has shifted to organ-on-a-chip technology, particularly gum-on-a-chip models. These microfluidic devices contain multiple microchannels that mimic anatomical structures like the gingival sulcus, epithelial layer, connective tissue, and blood vessels. Within these channels, researchers can coculture gingival epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells in a 3D matrix, and also introduce circulating immune cells. By introducing periodontal pathogens into the gingival sulcus channel, the system can simulate key pathological processes such as pathogen invasion, immune cell migration, and the release of inflammatory factors in real time. This highly biomimetic and dynamic model provides a high-fidelity in vitro platform for assessing the comprehensive effects of piezotherapy on host-microbe interactions.

Designing Smart Systems Responsive to the Pathological Microenvironment

A more forward-thinking strategy is to use the unique pathological features of the periodontitis lesion as endogenous signals for designing smart material systems that can be specifically activated or enhanced. The microenvironment of a periodontitis lesion has two key characteristics: first, a local acidic environment (pH 5.0–6.5) resulting from the metabolic activity of anaerobic bacteria, and second, high expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) caused by the host’s immune response. , These pathological signals provide the theoretical basis for designing responsive smart systems.

A core strategy is to utilize the acidic microenvironment as a trigger. For instance, Li et al. investigated a system using zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) nanoparticles loaded with dexamethasone, referred to as the dexamethasone-loaded ZIF-8 system. As depicted in Figure B, this system involves encapsulating drug molecules within ZIF-8 nanoparticles. By leveraging the instability of the ZIF-8 framework under acidic conditions, the system achieves the on-demand release of the anti-inflammatory drug dexamethasone and zinc ions, which possess antibacterial and pro-reparative properties.

Another strategy uses the high expression of MMPs as a molecular switch. As illustrated in Figure C, therapeutic nanoparticles can be encapsulated within polymer micelles or liposomes, and PEG molecules are attached to their outer layers via linkers. These linkers serve as specific peptide substrates that are cleavable by MMPs. In inflammatory areas with high MMP expression, MMPs can specifically cleave the peptide chain, causing the PEG layer to detach. This releases the core therapeutic particles in situ, achieving targeted delivery and on-demand drug release.

Beyond responding to host-generated signals, other advanced system designs aim to respond directly to signals produced by the pathogens themselves. Regional high levels of ROS produced by host immune cells are another key pathological feature. This characteristic can be leveraged to design ROS-responsive drug delivery systems, for example, by cross-linking a hydrogel with ROS-sensitive chemical bonds, such as phenylboronic ester bonds. In the high-ROS environment of a periodontitis lesion, these chemical bonds break, causing the hydrogel network to disintegrate and release its loaded drug on demand. A further step is to design systems that respond to enzymes secreted by specific pathogens. The key pathogen P. gingivalis secretes a specific protease called gingipain, and smart hydrogels can be engineered with peptide sequences specifically cleaved by these enzymes. In this design, therapeutic molecules are anchored to the hydrogel scaffold via this sequence. The peptide chain is cleaved only when P. gingivalis is present and secretes gingipains, thus achieving precise release of the antimicrobial drug in the area where the pathogen is active.

These representative strategies reveal a clear progression in responsive design. They start from general physiological triggers such as acidic pH, advance toward specific inflammatory signals including high ROS and abundant MMPs, and ultimately achieve precise recognition of pathogen-specific enzymatic cues. Such advancements reflect the trend toward higher precision and safety.

Actively Engineering the Protein Corona to Regulate Biological Functions

The protein corona is often seen as an obstacle that affects the in vivo behavior of nanomaterials. However, an emerging perspective is to view it as a functional interface that can be actively designed and utilized. By chemically modifying the surface of nanomaterials, it is possible to regulate their ability to selectively adsorb specific proteins from biofluids, thereby engineering their surface’s biological function.

Chen et al. made fundamental progress in this area, with research revealing that the geometry of a protein is key to determining the network structure of the protein corona that forms on a nanomaterial’s surface. As shown in Figure D, the elongated protein fibrinogen tends to form a dense, well-ordered network with uniform pore sizes on the nanoparticle surface. In contrast, the globular human serum albumin accumulates in a more disordered manner, creating a looser protein corona with larger pore sizes.

This discovery provides a new design approach for the regenerative treatment of periodontitis. Regeneration of periodontal tissue relies on interactions between stem cells and fibrous proteins, such as fibronectin within the ECM. Researchers therefore modified the surface of piezoelectric nanoparticles with RGD peptide sequences, which are the primary integrin-binding domain of fibronectin, to establish a nanobio interface. , These sequences provide defined ligand availability to trigger specific integrin engagement on the stem cell membrane. Upon encountering physiological fluids, this engineered surface governs the evolution of the biomolecular corona; accessible RGD motifs specifically bind to transmembrane integrin receptors (such as α5β1 or αvβ3) on the cell surface, initiating and maintaining stable adhesion of PDLSCs. These engineered particle surface interfaces mimic the functions of natural ECM, promoting the adhesion, spreading, and osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. Favorable cellular responses further guide tissue regeneration while particles themselves provide a piezocatalytic antibacterial effect.

Complementary to the strategy of actively capturing functional proteins, another approach is to optimize in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior by creating a functional protein corona. This is achieved by precoating the nanoparticle surface with specific proteins. After nanoparticles enter the bloodstream, their surface is rapidly coated by opsonins, leading to their swift clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system and a shortened circulation half-life. , To avoid these processes, albumin precoating technology has been developed. By incubating nanoparticles with serum albumin before administration, a stable albumin corona is formed, which prevents opsonin adsorption and avoids rapid recognition and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Furthermore, albumin can bind to the neonatal Fc receptor, a physiological mechanism that protects albumin from degradation, extending the nanoparticle’s circulation time in vivo.

The Piezocatalysis-Nanozyme Synergistic Strategy

The synergy between the piezoelectric effect and nanozymes is aimed at building smart catalytic systems. This synergistic strategy is not a simple addition of functions. Instead, it utilizes the potential generated by the piezoelectric field to directly regulate the rate-limiting step of the nanozyme-catalyzed reaction, thereby modulating the therapeutic process. It achieves a synergistic result surpassing the effects of the individual components acting alone.

Piezoelectrically Enhanced Pro-Oxidative Nanozymes

The initial goal of periodontitis treatment is to clear pathogenic microorganisms rapidly. Some nanozymes with peroxidase-like activity, such as iron oxide nanoparticles, can catalyze hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the acidic microenvironment of periodontitis to produce cytotoxic •OH. The key rate-limiting step of this reaction is the reduction of ferric ions (Fe3+) to ferrous ions (Fe2+). To address this bottleneck, Ge et al. designed a piezo-Fenton synergistic system by anchoring iron atoms onto a piezoelectric graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) matrix (Figure A). Under ultrasound excitation, the piezoelectric electrons generated by g-C3N4 drive the Fenton reaction, reducing Fe3+ back to Fe2+ and achieving a burst generation of ROS. The results of their antibacterial experiments showed that, compared with the control and single-intervention groups, bacterial viability dropped sharply in the “g-C3N4–Fe + ultrasound” synergistic treatment group.

Piezoelectrically Regulated Antioxidant Nanozymes

Once the infection is under control, the persistent inflammatory response and excess ROS create an oxidative stress microenvironment that inhibits tissue regeneration. At this stage, the therapeutic goal shifts to promoting tissue repair. The work by Yu et al. provides an example of this. They used cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs), which have enzyme-like activity, as an antioxidant to treat periodontitis (Figure B). CeO2 NPs scavenge ROS through the reversible conversion of the Ce3+/Ce4+ ion pair. In a rat model of periodontitis, microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) analysis showed that alveolar bone resorption was significantly inhibited after local injection of CeO2 NPs compared to the untreated group (Figure C). This result indicates that by scavenging ROS in the lesion with nanozymes, the pathological microenvironment can be shifted from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-regenerative state.

The regulatory mechanism of piezoelectric fields on nanozyme activity is universal, relying on the piezopotential to accelerate electron transfer throughout catalytic cycles. This principle is also applicable to antioxidant nanozymes with superoxide dismutase-like (SOD-like) or catalase-like (CAT-like) activity. In the tissue repair stage, a system integrating piezoelectric materials and nanozymes with dual SOD/CAT activity can accelerate the electron transfer of •O2 – disproportionation and H2O2 decomposition when mechanically stimulated by ultrasound. Such activation greatly improves ROS scavenging efficiency compared with nonactivated groups. This on-demand enhancement of antioxidant capacity allows the therapeutic platform to precisely control ROS scavenging rates via external ultrasound stimulation, matching the requirements of the tissue repair phase. This achieves dynamic and precise regulation of the oxidative stress microenvironment.

Integration of Multimodal Stimulation and Immunomodulation

To adapt to the complex in vivo environment, research is shifting toward platforms that integrate multiple stimulation modes and immunomodulatory functions.

Combining ultrasound-driven piezocatalysis with photothermal modulation is another multimodal stimulation strategy for treating infected tissues. This approach incorporates photothermal conversion agents, such as gold nanoparticles, into piezoelectric materials to trigger synergistic effects, including on-demand wound closure and bacterial elimination. Additionally, 2D materials like MXene show excellent photothermal performance in periodontitis therapy, offering broad potential for constructing multifunctional platforms. When the lesion is irradiated with near-infrared light, the agent converts light into local heat (>45 °C) to physically disrupt bacterial structures. Meanwhile, the sonopiezoelectric capability of the material generates a burst of ROS. This combined effect of thermal ablation with oxidative stress can destroy the resistant biofilms. The generated heat can also act as an additional stimulus, further enhancing the material’s catalytic activity through the pyroelectric effect.

Deep Integration with Immunomodulation

The ultimate goal of periodontal therapy is to reestablish healthy host-microbe homeostasis and tissue function. The microelectric fields and surface charges generated by piezoelectric materials have been shown to influence the behavior of immune cells, particularly macrophage polarization, through the regulation of ion channels on immune cell membranes. , In the initial inflammatory phase, pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype macrophages are crucial for clearing pathogens, whereas in the repair phase, anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype macrophages are needed to suppress inflammation and promote stem cell differentiation. , Therefore, immunomodulation is becoming the core design objective. By precisely designing the parameters of the piezoelectric field, researchers are attempting to guide the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. The immunofluorescence staining results in Figure C visually demonstrate this trend: under piezoelectric stimulation, the number of cells expressing the M1 marker iNOS decreases, while the number of cells expressing the M2 marker CD206 increases, indicating that the macrophages are being guided toward a pro-reparative M2 phenotype.

The scope of immunomodulation extends beyond macrophages. Neutrophils, the earliest inflammatory responders, drive tissue destruction via the overactivation and the excessive formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Emerging research indicates that piezoelectric electrical stimulation directly regulates the activity of neutrophils, including the formation of NETs. For example, by designing piezoelectric scaffolds to generate in situ electrical stimulation, the abnormal activation of neutrophils can be suppressed and the release of NETs reduced, thereby enabling early stage intervention. By modulating these first responders and guiding macrophage polarization, piezoelectric platforms regulate the inflammation and repair process spatiotemporally, reshaping the immune microenvironment of the lesion.

The aim is to create an immune microenvironment that is not only sterile but also conducive to tissue regeneration and vascularization.

While macro-scale 3D printing enables personalized scaffold fabrication, their biological performance depends on the quality of the piezoelectric nanofillers at the microscale. Traditional batch synthesis methods, such as chemical coprecipitation, cannot precisely control nanoparticle size, morphology, and crystallinity, leading to batch-to-batch variations and unstable performance. However, microfluidic synthesis technology addresses this issue. By precisely controlling the mixing of reactant fluids and the reaction time within microscale channels, microfluidic reactors can provide a highly uniform reaction environment. This enables precise regulation of nanoparticle nucleation and growth. Compared to traditional batch synthesis, microfluidics produces piezoelectric nanoparticles with narrower size distribution, higher crystallinity, and more consistent performance.

Conclusion

Piezocatalytic technology provides a revolutionary in situ therapeutic strategy for periodontitis. Its core advantage lies in converting mechanical energy into therapeutic electrochemical signals. , However, the technology’s path to clinical application faces systemic challenges due to the inherent complexity of the periodontal pathological microenvironment. , These are mainly manifested as the difficulty in achieving effective and safe energy activation, and the unpredictability of the protein corona and biofilm, which leads to a vast gap between preclinical findings and clinical translation. ,,−

Therefore, overcoming translational barriers requires more than optimizing individual piezoelectric materials. , It demands a shift toward a systems engineering approach, which involves integrating material design with complex biological environments, developing smart systems responsive to pathological signals in the microenvironment, and building therapeutic platforms combining piezocatalysis, nanozyme catalysis, and immunomodulation. Drawing inspiration from piezoelectrically enhanced nanozyme systems in skin repair, such multimodal strategies promise precise reconstruction of periodontal homeostasis. , Reflecting a shift from a materials-centric to a system-centric approach, recent studies highlight the potential of piezocatalytic technology to evolve into a comprehensive therapeutic modality. Such systems are capable of regulating the complex periodontal microenvironment and guiding tissue regeneration.
