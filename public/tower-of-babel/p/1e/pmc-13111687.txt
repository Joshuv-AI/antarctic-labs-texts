Value of transcranial color-coded duplex sonography-derived middle cerebral artery pulsatility index in intracranial pressure assessment at moderate to high altitudes
Authors: Xiubin Qu, Hao Wang, Chenbeini Du, Bin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To assess transcranial color-coded duplex sonography (TCCD) as a reliable method for predicting intracranial pressure (ICP) in brain injury patients at moderate-to-high-altitude, clinical data were collected from patients admitted to the Intensive Care Unit of a tertiary hospital on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau between April 24 and July 24, 2025. A total of 158 sets of middle cerebral artery pulsatility index (MCA-PI) and ICP data from 54 patients were included. The median PI, ICP, and arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2) were 1.19 (0.99, 1.42), 15.08 (13.61, 17.10) mmHg, and 36.00 (33.00, 40.13) mmHg, respectively. Spearman’s analysis showed a strongly positive PI-ICP correlation (r = 0.769, P < 0.001). The generalized estimating equation (GEE) for non-invasive intracranial pressure (nICP) prediction based on MCA-PI was established as: nICP = 4.46 × PI + 9.62. For PI > 1.21 predicting ICP > 15 mmHg, sensitivity, specificity, and ROC AUC were 0.850, 0.936, and 0.916 (95% CI: 0.868–0.963, P < 0.001), respectively. MCA-PI correlates with ICP and aids in identifying elevated ICP; this non-invasive, low-cost method is suitable for moderate-to-high altitude areas.

Introduction

The cerebral circulatory system is characterized by dense vascular distribution, abundant blood flow, and low vascular resistance. Maintaining stable cerebral blood flow (CBF) is fundamental to the functional activities of neurons and brain cells1. Intracranial pressure (ICP) refers to the pressure exerted by intracranial contents on the cranial wall. ICP stability relies on the dynamic balance between three intracranial components: brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and blood. Disruption of this balance leads to abnormal elevation of ICP2. Elevated ICP is a common pathological change in neurological diseases, which can induce severe complications such as reduced CBF, neurological deterioration, diffuse cerebral edema, and brain herniation, significantly affecting patient prognosis3. ICP is typically measured via invasive methods, such as external ventricular drains (EVDs), which represent the gold standard4. However, as an invasive procedure, it carries risks including hemorrhage and infection, while increasing the complexity of nursing care. The measurement unit is usually mmHg, which can be converted to cmH2O or mmH2O if necessary. In the context of normal CSF circulation and absence of obstruction, lumbar puncture (LP) may serve as an acceptable alternative for ICP assessment5; nevertheless, its application is associated with significant limitations. When CSF dynamics are impaired (e.g., in traumatic brain injury), LP poses a substantial risk of precipitating brain herniation and is therefore generally considered contraindicated6. Furthermore, LP does not allow for continuous ICP monitoring, and repeated procedures increase patient discomfort and healthcare costs. Consequently, LP is more commonly utilized in clinical scenarios with a low risk of brain herniation, such as meningitis and multiple sclerosis. When measuring CSF pressure via LP, a simple gravity-based manometer is typically employed, with values conventionally expressed in cmH2O.

Thus, the development of non-invasive intracranial pressure (nICP) monitoring technologies holds significant clinical value. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) and transcranial color-coded duplex sonography (TCCD) are not only valuable for assessing neurological function after brain injury, cerebral autoregulation, and determining brain death7,8 but also, as studies have shown9,10, can directly monitor hemodynamic parameters of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and calculate the derived pulsatility index (PI), which indirectly reflects dynamic changes in ICP.

The partial pressure of oxygen in ambient air decreases with increasing altitude. Hypoxia induces cerebral vasodilation to enhance oxygen delivery to brain tissue11; however, it simultaneously stimulates hyperventilation, leading to hypocapnia, which counteracts vasodilation and may even provoke cerebral vasoconstriction, thereby reducing CBF and modulating ICP12. In a study involving 24 participants, Wright et al.13 observed that ICP significantly increased as altitude rose from 1,348 m to 3,440 m—a change closely associated with the degree of hypoxia. Notably, as altitude further increased to 4,120 m, ICP gradually returned to baseline levels and remained stable at 5,200 m. This nonlinear ICP response suggests that during sustained high-altitude exposure, compensatory mechanisms—such as enhanced CSF reabsorption and hypoxia-driven hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia—act synergistically to limit further elevation of ICP13. The unique environment of high-altitude areas is characterized by low atmospheric pressure and hypoxia. Through long-term physiological regulation and adaptive remodeling, the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems of long-term residents undergo characteristic adaptations to maintain homeostasis under extreme conditions. Such adaptations include differences in CBF between high-altitude residents and those living at sea level14,15. Wang et al.16 demonstrated that due to the inherent low pressure and hypoxia of high-altitude environments, high altitude is an independent risk factor for poor short-term functional outcomes, in-hospital mortality, and gastrointestinal bleeding in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage. Over 80 million people worldwide live above 2,500 m altitude17, highlighting the urgent need for a non-invasive ICP monitoring method suitable for high-altitude residents. However, the quantitative relationship between MCA-PI and ICP in moderate-to-high altitude environments remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the correlation between MCA-PI and ICP in neurocritically ill patients in moderate-to-high altitude regions, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing treatment strategies and prognosis evaluation in this population.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

Clinical data were collected from patients admitted to the Department of Critical Care Medicine of a provincial capital hospital between April 24, 2025, and July 24, 2025, including general information (age, gender, primary diagnosis, long-term residential altitude, etc.) and measurements of arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2), MCA hemodynamic parameters, and ICP.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Patients admitted to the Department of Critical Care Medicine requiring LP; (2) Patients themselves or their family members provided consent for undergoing TCCD examination; (3) Age ≥ 18 years; (4) Indigenous or long-term residents (≥ 10 years).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Presence of cerebral herniation; (2) Single LP pressure > 24.27 mmHg. Our hospital’s LP kit manometer tube max: 330 mmH2O ≈ 24.27 mmHg (Conversion factor: 1 mmHg = 13.595 mmH2O); (3) Inability to cooperate with ultrasound examination, unclear ultrasound imaging, or inability to undergo LP; (4) Cardiac or vascular diseases potentially affecting hemodynamic measurements by ultrasound (i.e., severe arrhythmia, severe valvular stenosis, moderate to severe cerebral vasospasm).

TCCD examination

TCCD was performed prior to LP. Patients were placed in the supine position. A physician experienced in ultrasound used a 2 MHz pulsed Doppler probe of the SIEMENS ACUSON P500 ultrasound system, held horizontally at the temporal window (or via a craniectomy defect) and directed approximately straight toward the contralateral side. Gentle pressure was applied to the probe, with a sampling volume depth of 45–65 mm. A suitable acoustic window was identified using B-mode ultrasound, and the classic butterfly-shaped hypoechoic midbrain was visualized in the axial mesencephalic plane. Color Doppler flow imaging was then used to visualize the cerebral Willis circle, where forward-flowing spectra indicated the MCA (i.e., the red spots in Fig. 1). Peak systolic velocity (PSV) and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) were recorded. MCA hemodynamic parameters were measured twice consecutively, and the PI value was the mean of the two measurements (PI = (PSV - EDV) / MV; MV = (PSV + 2EDV) / 3; MV, mean flow velocity, Fig. 1).

LP manometry

Within 10 min of the ultrasound examination, an LP was performed. No ICP-lowering or other interventional treatments were administered during this period. Patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position, with the back perpendicular to the examination table, the head flexed toward the chest, and the chin close to the sternum. The lower limbs were flexed at the knees, with the thighs as close to the abdomen as possible to fully expose the intervertebral space. The L4/L5 interspace was selected for puncture, and ICP was recorded after successful puncture.

Data collection

(1) TCCD and LP pressure measurements were performed independently by two physicians, with results recorded in a double-blind manner. Each data collection event was defined as a paired dataset, corresponding to one TCCD examination and a concurrent LP measurement of ICP. For patients who underwent multiple TCCD examinations and LP-based ICP measurements during the study period, each paired dataset was included as an independent data point in subsequent analyses.

(2) Determination of altitude: The altitude corresponding to each patient was determined based on their long-term residential area (≥ 10 years). Geographical location was extracted from the medical record system and confirmed with the patients themselves or their family members. The final altitude was verified and standardized with reference to relevant data from the Qinghai Provincial Bureau of Statistics.

(3) Altitude Classification: Altitudes were categorized as low (500–1,500 m), medium (1,501–2,500 m), high (2,501–4,500 m), ultra-high (4,501–5,500 m), and extremely high (> 5,500 m)18, Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software.

Continuous data were first tested for distribution type via Shapiro-Wilk test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, combined with histograms and Q-Q plots: data following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) [M (Q25, Q75)]. Categorical data were described as frequency (percentage) [n (%)]. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was initially used to explore the association between MCA-PI and ICP. Subsequently, to adjust for the clustering effect of repeated measurements from individual patients, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed to analyze the correlation between PI and ICP and establish a prediction model. The optimal cutoff value of PI for predicting elevated ICP was determined using the maximum Youden’s Index method. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 169 sets of MCA blood flow parameters and corresponding ICP data from 57 patients were collected. Among these, 3 sets of data were excluded due to poor acoustic window in patients, 5 sets were excluded because ICP exceeded 24.27 mmHg, 1 set was excluded due to incomplete medical records, and 2 sets of data were excluded because the LP of the patients failed. Finally, 158 sets of MCA blood flow parameters and corresponding ICP measurements from 54 patients (44 males) were included in the statistical analysis. Each CBF examination was treated as an independent sample for statistical analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics were as follows: 130 male samples, median age 54 (48, 62) years, median long-term residential altitude 2520 (2250, 2848) m. Our hospital is located at an altitude of 2260 m (classified as medium altitude). The median values of PI, ICP, and PaCO₂ were 1.19 (0.99, 1.42), 15.08 (13.61, 17.10) mmHg, and 36.00 (33.00, 40.13) mmHg, respectively. The main clinical diagnoses included intracerebral hemorrhage, cerebral contusion and laceration, and intracranial space-occupying lesions, etc.(Table 2, Additionally, Fig. 2 is provided to illustrate the trend of PI and PSV changes with increasing altitude.)

Correlation between PI and ICP and prediction model in moderate-to-high altitude environments

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between PI and ICP (r = 0.769, P < 0.001). GEE analysis (adjusted for repeated measurements) showed a significant positive correlation between PI and ICP (β = 4.46, SE = 0.3167, 95% CI: 3.840–5.082, Wald χ² = 198.395, P < 0.001). Model parameters were as follows: intercept = 9.62 (95% CI: 8.610-10.626, P < 0.001) and scale parameter = 5.53. The GEE prediction model was: nICP = 4.46 × PI + 9.62 (Table 3).

Qualitative assessment of ICP based on MCA-PI in moderate-to-high altitude environments

Patients were divided into a normal ICP group (5 mmHg ≤ ICP ≤ 15 mmHg) and an elevated ICP group (ICP > 15 mmHg). A PI > 1.21 predicted ICP > 15 mmHg with a sensitivity of 0.850 and specificity of 0.936. The area under the ROC curve was 0.916 (95% CI: 0.868–0.963, P < 0.001, Table 2; Fig. 3).

Correlations of PaCO2 with ICP and PI in moderate-to-high altitude environments

PaCO2 and ICP

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis indicated a weak positive correlation between PaCO2 and ICP (r = 0.211, n = 158, P = 0.008). GEE analysis confirmed a significant positive correlation between PaCO2 and ICP (β = 0.086, SE = 0.0392, 95% CI: 0.009–0.163, Wald χ²= 4.789, P = 0.029) with a scale parameter of 10.140. The small absolute value of the regression coefficient (β = 0.086) indicated a weak practical association, consistent with the “weak correlation” from Spearman’s analysis.

PaCO2 and PI

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed a weak positive correlation between PaCO2 and PI (r = 0.187, n = 158, P = 0.018). GEE analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between PaCO2 and PI (β = 0.017, SE = 0.0072, 95% CI: 0.003–0.031, Wald χ² = 5.664, P = 0.017) with a scale parameter of 0.236. Despite statistical significance, the extremely small absolute regression coefficient ( β = 0.017) reflected a negligible practical impact, consistent with the “weak correlation” from Spearman’s analysis.

Association between PI and ICP after adjusting for PaCO₂ in moderate-to-high altitude environments

Partial correlation analysis, controlling for PaCO2, showed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation between PI and ICP (rp = 0.675, n = 157, P < 0.001). A multivariate GEE model incorporating PaCO2 as a covariate yielded the equation: nICP = 9.27 + 4.42 × PI + 0.01 × PaCO2. Model parameters indicated no statistically significant association between PaCO2 and ICP (β = 0.01, SE = 0.0309, 95% Wald CI: -0.050-0.071, Wald χ² = 0.117, df = 1, P = 0.733). In contrast, PI remained a significant positive predictor of ICP (β = 4.42, SE = 0.3647, 95% Wald CI: 3.708–5.137, Wald χ²= 147.038, df = 1, P < 0.001). These results confirm that PaCO2 has no statistically significant confounding effect on the PI-ICP association, supporting the stable and reliable predictive value of PI for ICP (Table 4). Therefore, it is still recommended to use the formula nICP = 4.46 × PI + 9.62 for the quantitative assessment of ICP.

Discussion

Neurocritical illness, characterized by elevated ICP as a core pathological change, is a life-threatening emergency. The duration and severity of elevated ICP are closely related to disease severity, and abnormally elevated ICP is an independent risk factor for mortality in neurocritical patients19. Thus, real-time and accurate ICP monitoring has become a key component of clinical management20. Despite the emergence of various non-invasive monitoring techniques, no universally accepted “gold standard” for non-invasive ICP monitoring has been established21. In recent years, studies have shown that MCA-PI measured by TCD/TCCD can effectively predict patient prognosis and dynamic changes in ICP, making it a primary non-invasive ICP monitoring technique in critical care and emergency departments22.

Cerebral hemodynamics differs not only across different altitude environments but also within the same high-altitude setting. Residents of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau exhibit faster cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) than plain residents, whereas indigenous inhabitants of the Andean Plateau—another high-altitude region—show slower CBFV than plain residents. This discrepancy may be associated with differences in the high-altitude adaptation patterns of ancient populations in these two regions. The core mechanism might lie in the higher hematocrit (HCT) and sufficient arterial oxygen content (CaO2) in Andean Plateau residents, which eliminates the need to increase CBFV for maintaining cerebral oxygen supply; additionally, high HCT inevitably increases blood viscosity and slows blood flow velocity. In contrast, Qinghai-Tibet Plateau residents have lower HCT, thus requiring increased blood flow volume and velocity to ensure organ oxygenation14,23. Patrician et al.24 proposed that hypoxia-induced increases in CBFV may be related to the regulatory effect of iron metabolism on blood vessels, which provides a direction for subsequent studies on the mechanism underlying the impact of altitude on CBF. Although controversies remain regarding the correlation between altitude and CBF, there is a consensus that the regulatory mechanism of CBF in high-altitude environments differs from that at sea level.

This study demonstrated a strongly positive correlation between PI and ICP in moderate-to-high altitude regions, consistent with findings from numerous plain-region studies where TCD-derived MCA-PI was used to predict ICP25–27. This preliminary finding suggests that TCCD-measured MCA-PI may also be viable for assessing ICP changes in neurocritically ill patients at high altitudes. The underlying mechanism likely involves elevated ICP reducing cerebral perfusion pressure and cerebrovascular compliance, which significantly decreases MCA-EDV while exerting minimal effects on PSV. Impaired transmission of blood flow pulsatility further contributes to increased PI28. Notably, a PI > 1.21 in our cohort predicted elevated ICP (ICP > 15 mmHg). In contrast, Wakerley et al.29 reported a threshold of PI > 1.26 for predicting elevated ICP in a multi-center plain-region study (maximum altitude of the study sites was approximately 500 m) using LP pressure measurements. This subtle discrepancy may stem from the unique effects of high-altitude hypoxia on cerebral hemodynamics. For instance, hypoxia-induced compensatory cerebrovascular dilation at moderate to high altitudes may alter the pulsatile characteristics of CBF.

However, the utility of PI as a clinical ICP monitoring tool remains controversial. Morgalla et al.30 and Brasil et al.31 have challenged the validity of this method. This controversy may be attributed to baseline differences in study populations: as a hemodynamic parameter, PI is regulated by multiple factors including arterial blood pressure, cerebrovascular resistance, blood flow velocity, and vascular compliance. High-altitude hypobaric and hypoxic conditions can modify cerebral autoregulation, erythrocyte rheology, and endothelial function, thereby altering CBF and exacerbating inter-study variability. Support for the uniqueness of PI at high altitudes comes from Stacey et al.32, who found that MCA and posterior cerebral artery PI values were significantly lower in lifelong high-altitude residents compared to sea-level dwellers or those acclimatizing slowly to high altitudes. This may represent an adaptive protective mechanism: reduced vascular pulsatility minimizes damage to neurovascular units, preserving their structural and functional integrity. This finding provides an additional perspective for understanding the PI-ICP relationship in high-altitude environments.

The quantitative PI-ICP relationship established in this study is nICP = 4.46 × PI + 9.62, which differs from previous predictive models developed using plain-region populations. Bellner et al.27(Lund, Sweden; altitude: 42 m) proposed nICP = 10.93 × PI − 1.28, and Budohoski et al.33(Cambridge, UK; altitude: 13 m) derived nICP = 4.47 × PI + 12.68—both confirming a linear PI-ICP correlation. Gura et al.34(Istanbul, Turkey; altitude: 39 m) further identified temporal variations in the dynamic PI-ICP correlation 1–5 days post-craniotomy, establishing time-specific predictive models. Critically, all aforementioned studies were conducted at altitudes < 100 m, markedly lower than the median altitude of our cohort (2,520 m). Hemodynamic alterations induced by high altitude likely explain the divergent predictive models. Hypoxia directly triggers cerebrovascular dilation and increased cerebral blood volume, leading to acute ICP elevation35. In contrast, lifelong high-altitude residents maintain relatively stable ICP through long-term compensatory adaptations. Importantly, elevated ICP in high-altitude patients is not a single-factor event but a synergistic effect of disease and altitude: (1) Chronic high-altitude hypoxia and hypobaria alter vascular homeostasis—hypoxia impairs cerebral microvessels, reduces angiogenesis, diminishes cerebrovascular reserve capacity, and disrupts microcirculation, serving as a “baseline predisposing factor” for ICP elevation36; (2) When illness (e.g., brain injury) compromises compensatory capacity, previously stable ICP fluctuates or rises persistently. Pre-existing vascular dysfunction amplifies disease-induced ICP elevation, activating pathological cascades2, creating a vicious cycle: “disease exacerbates vascular injury, and vascular injury worsens ICP elevation”—a key reason for poorer outcomes in high-altitude brain injury patients compared to their plain-region counterparts. In summary, disease serves as the direct trigger for elevated ICP, while altitude provides a susceptibility context by altering baseline vascular function, together driving ICP elevation in high-altitude populations. This study establishes a TCCD-based rapid ICP assessment model tailored to moderate-to-high altitude patients. Unlike previous models derived from plain-region TCD/TCCD data, this model accounts for the unique vascular characteristics of high-altitude populations, offering significant clinical value. It is also imperative to acknowledge the limitations of the present study. The established model was only validated in patients with mild to moderate intracranial hypertension characterized by an ICP ≤ 24.27 mmHg. However, the most urgent clinical intervention decisions are typically required in the setting of severe intracranial hypertension crises with ICP > 25 mmHg. Given that the relationship between PI and ICP may transition to a nonlinear pattern within this pressure range, the applicability of our model remains unclear. Therefore, extrapolation of the study findings to such clinical emergencies must be strictly avoided.

Previous studies have shown that both ICP and PI are influenced by PaCO237,38. Consequently, current guidelines for the management of neurocritically ill patients recommend maintaining PaCO2 within the range of 35–45 mmHg39. However, this target is often challenging to achieve in clinical practice, as many patients present with respiratory function-impairing conditions such as rib fractures, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cor pulmonale, and pneumonia. To better reflect real-world clinical scenarios, this study did not strictly exclude cases with PaCO2 outside the aforementioned range. We performed partial correlation analysis, which revealed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation between ICP and PI after controlling for PaCO2 (rₚ = 0.675, P < 0.001). Additionally, GEE analysis—adjusted for the clustering effect of repeated measurements—demonstrated that PaCO2 had no statistically significant confounding effect on the PI-ICP association. This finding offers a crucial clinical implication for TCCD-based ICP assessment in neurocritically ill patients: the proposed formula enables rapid ICP estimation even in the absence of arterial blood gas analysis.

A major challenge common to both TCD and TCCD for CBF assessment is the availability of adequate acoustic windows. Previous research has identified age, female sex, and skull thickness as independent factors limiting the acquisition of optimal acoustic windows40, which explains the underrepresentation of female patients in this study (most were excluded due to inadequate windows). Future efforts should focus on enhancing sonographer proficiency through specialized training and optimizing acoustic window acquisition via contrast-enhanced ultrasound41, thereby enabling the inclusion of more female participants and improving the reliability and generalizability of related models. Compared with TCD, TCCD offers distinct advantages: (1) It provides clear visualization of intracranial anatomical structures on two-dimensional imaging; (2) It color-codes the direction and velocity of Doppler shifts generated by moving red blood cells (blood flow toward the transducer is typically encoded red, while flow away is blue; higher velocities correspond to brighter colors and lower velocities to paler shades), facilitating intuitive visualization of intracranial vascular blood flow signals. Furthermore, TCCD incorporates vascular incident angle correction42, which not only simplifies the operational process and lowers the learning threshold but also significantly reduces measurement errors caused by insufficient operator proficiency. These features make TCCD suitable for rapid bedside assessment by novices, as well as in intensive care units, emergency departments, and primary care facilities22.

Strengths and limitations

This method offers several advantages, including being non-invasive, cost-effective, and convenient, enabling repeated monitoring of ICP. It not only simplifies the monitoring process and lowers the threshold for clinical operation, but also effectively alleviates patient suffering and reduces the economic burden. Nevertheless, several limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. First, as this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size and the exclusion of patients with ICP > 24.27 mmHg, caution is warranted when extrapolating these ultrasound-based estimates—particularly to populations with markedly elevated ICP. Second, the study did not include the general population from sea-level areas as controls, making it impossible to comprehensively compare the effect differences across different altitude environments and thereby potentially limiting the full interpretation of the model’s application efficacy in non-plateau populations. Third, age, female sex, and skull thickness are independent factors affecting the acquisition of adequate acoustic windows in transcranial ultrasound. This resulted in the exclusion of a substantial number of female patients due to inadequate acoustic window quality, leading to a relatively low proportion of female participants, which may limit the external generalizability of the model. To address the aforementioned limitations, future research should focus on: first, conducting prospective validation of the developed model, ideally including patients with ICP > 24.27 mmHg where resources permit, to systematically evaluate its predictive accuracy and stability; second, collaborating with multiple medical institutions to carry out multi-center studies, accumulating more robust evidence and laying a more solid foundation for the extensive clinical translation and application of the model; third, strengthening the training of sonographers’ operational skills and applying techniques such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound to optimize the acquisition of acoustic windows, thereby enrolling more female subjects and improving the reliability and generalizability of the model.

Conclusion

Our data demonstrate a moderate correlation between PI and ICP in moderate-to-high altitude regions. A PI > 1.21 indicates elevated ICP (ICP > 15 mmHg), and rapid quantitative ICP assessment can be achieved using the predictive model: nICP = 4.46 × PI + 9.62. Therefore, TCCD-based monitoring of MCA-PI is clinically informative for ICP prediction in moderate-to-high altitude areas. This non-invasive, simple, convenient, and cost-effective method is well-suited for implementation in such regions.
