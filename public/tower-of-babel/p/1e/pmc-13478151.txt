Statistical shape modeling of the proximal femur bone for hip implant design optimization
Authors: Bereket Workie Anteneh, Habtamu Mamo Yimam, Tewodros Belay Alemneh, Abdu Mohammed Seid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe femur is the major load-bearing skeletal component in the human body, and its proximal end is prone to either osteoporosis or high-energy injury-related fractures. When a fracture occurs, prosthetic implants, depending on the type and level of injury, are used to restore the normal biomechanics of the bone. Fracture fixation and the subsequent bone healing process depend on several factors, including the anatomical fitting quality of the prosthetic implant utilized. However, the standard commercially available prosthetic implants are generic, and commonly implants are designed and validated based on a limited set of cadaver bones, which may result in the incompatibility of implants to all patient groups. Utilization of an incompatible implant for patients may cause implant-related complications. In recent years, generic implants have been optimized for a specific population group on the basis of anatomical data obtained from the target population to improve the fitting quality of implants to the intended anatomical region. This study aimed to develop a statistical shape model (SSM) of the proximal femur bone and to carry out morphometric analysis of the femoral neck-shaft angle (NSA). The shape modelling process followed sequential steps, including image segmentation and 3D surface reconstruction, manual annotation of anatomical landmarks, establishment of surface correspondence across datasets, and model building via principal component analysis. Additionally, femoral NSA measurements are implemented. The quality of the SSM was validated through shape quality metrics: compactness, specificity, and generality. The validation result revealed that the first eleven principal components of the SSM represented 92.85% of the total variance in the model. Similarly, the specificity and generalizability of the model were approximately 1.33 mm and 0.83 mm, respectively.Keywords: Statistical shape modeling, Proximal femur, Hip implant design, Morphometric analysis, LandmarksSubject terms: Anatomy, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The femur is the longest and main load-bearing skeletal component in the human body. Its proximal end articulates with the acetabulum of the hipbone to form the hip joint (ball and socket joint), which allows the leg to move at all angles. The proximal femur (PF) region is highly vulnerable to fractures, mainly in the femoral neck and trochanteric regions1. Globally, approximately 1.6 million proximal femur or hip fractures are estimated to occur annually2. Among all fractures in the human body, proximal femur fractures account for 14%, and in terms of cost, it accounts nearly 72% of the total number of fractures3. Although there are limited data concerning the prevalence of proximal femur fractures in Ethiopia, a previous study indicated that out of the 115 patients referred abroad within one year for treatment, 41 (36%) underwent hip joint replacement surgery4. In another study, 50 locally treated total hip replacements (THRs) were reported5. These studies revealed the incidence of proximal femur fractures in Ethiopia.

Proximal femur fractures are typically treated with various prosthetic implants, depending on the level and type of injury. However, the quality of fixation and fracture healing is influenced by several factors, including bone shape variability and the availability of appropriately sized fixation implants that accommodate this variability in the population. Consequently, incorporating shape variability through systematic quantification is vital for developing fixation plates or prosthetic implants that fit the given population6. In recent years, population-specific implants have been introduced in arthroplasty to improve implant alignment accuracy and minimize implant-related complications associated with standard generic implants7. Aldieri et al.8 created three-dimensional statistical models of the proximal femur that combined shape and intensity information to predict the risk of hip fractures better, showing that the combined shape and intensity model was significantly more accurate than standard areal bone mineral density (aBMD) analysis. Bergiers et al.9 used statistical shape modeling (SSM) on retrieved metal-on-metal acetabular cups to identify the location of edge wear in vivo, showing that the parameters of implant positioning, especially the angle of inclination and anteversion, significantly affected the distribution of wear. Dupraz et al.10 developed population-specific statistical shape models (SSMs) to inform the design of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) implants and demonstrated that SSM-derived anatomical parameters were effective in optimizing implant sizing. De Angelis et al.11 employed statistical shape modeling (SSM) to model the pelvic anatomy of patients with severe acetabular damage and demonstrated that the SSM-derived hip center of rotation was accurate compared with surgical planning and the opposite side, thereby validating the preoperative planning and positioning of the implant in complex hip revisions. Schmid et al.12 proposed a statistical shape and intensity modeling (SSIM) method for data augmentation in medical imaging. They reported that the combination of shape and intensity synthesis had a positive effect on deep learning-based segmentation of pathological hip bones compared with traditional data augmentation methods. Betti et al.13 proposed a statistical shape model of the femoral medullary canal on the basis of CT scans, and they found that segment-specific analysis reveals substantial anatomical variation, thus facilitating the design of personalized distal femoral orthopedic implants.

In this context, the statistical shape modeling (SSM) technique has played a significant role in objectively quantifying and identifying anatomical variations in largely scattered data, and in performing population-based evaluations. The basic idea in SSM is to establish the average model and the main pattern of legal variations in shapes and spatial relationships of structures for the given training datasets14. This method is used to parametrize shape variability by providing an appropriate representation of shape and allowing shape constraints to be applied. The SSM enables us to build a flexible shape model from a set of shape instances to ensure that each shape fits the largest number of subjects in the given population of interest15. Despite these advancements, the majority of SSM-based studies on the proximal femur in humans have focused on European, North American, and Asian populations. There is a considerable dearth of morphometric and statistical shape modeling studies on African populations. In Ethiopia, although hip fractures constitute a considerable percentage of orthopedic surgical cases, there is a lack of quantitative studies on the morphology of the proximal femur based on three-dimensional CT images. The absence of a population-specific geometric model may obstruct the design and development of anatomically compatible implants for the population group.

Thus, the current study aims to establish a statistical shape model of the proximal femur in 3D using data from Ethiopian patients who underwent a CT scan. This study also aims to establish a scientific basis for optimizing the design of hip implants and developing orthopedic solutions for the Ethiopian population by understanding the primary modes of anatomical variation and the key geometric parameters of the femur, particularly femoral neck-shaft angle, relevant to the fixation and positioning of hip implants.

Description of the image data

After submitting a letter to Jimma University to access CT image data, permission was granted, and one hundred two bilateral pelvic CT images were retrospectively collected from three different hospitals: Jimma University Medical Center, ALERT Hospital, and Myungsung Christian Medical (MCM) General Hospital. All patients underwent CT scans in the supine position with an imaging interval ranging from the entire pelvis to the proximal femur. CT scans were performed with nominal settings: tube voltage 120kVp, tube current 80–493 mA, and slice thickness 1.25–5 mm. The CT scan images were stored and imported into Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for easier digital transfer and storage. For all the proximal femurs, CT images with signs of trauma such as fractures and joint disease, and demonstrating metallic implants, were excluded. Additionally, images that are too blurred and difficult for optimal segmentation in bone contours were also excluded. The collected bones were classified as healthy if they did not present any abnormal morphological feature or sign of joint disease, such as osteoarthritis. All patients’ proximal femur bones considered functional at the time of scanning by a radiologist are incorporated in the datasets. After defining the inclusion and exclusion criteria as described, 24 right proximal femora and a few left proximal femora were deemed healthy by a radiologist. Finally, 24 right proximal femora (14 males and 10 females) were included in this study, and the left proximal femora were excluded from the datasets due to the following reason. In statistical shape modeling, to increase the sample size, mirroring the left femur to match the right (or vice versa) is a common preprocessing technique. However, it introduces several key limitations, primarily stemming from physiological, functional, and pathological asymmetries between the two sides, and significant side-to-side differences exist between the left and right femur16,17. The main limitations in mirroring include that while the left and right femurs are generally similar, they are not perfectly symmetric, such as leg dominance, which can lead to differences in bone density, structural rigidity, and specific geometric features (e.g., torsion or neck-shaft angle)18. The paper published by Pierre et al. suggests that even if overall right and left femur geometric, densitometric, and structural properties are not significantly different from one another, absolute left–right differences for individual patients can be significant, especially in the case of structural rigidity properties, and should be considered in separate clinical and research studies19. Therefore, in this study, only the right proximal femur is used to build the SSM. The number of right proximal femur CT scan images was limited because of the unavailability of CT scan images of the pelvic bones in scanning centers that were considered. Additionally, since the full-length femur bone was not available for the entire training set from all scanning centers, the right proximal femur bone was the region of interest for this study. The age at the time of scanning varied from 25 to 64 years, and a mean ± standard deviation (STD) age of 42 ± 8.7 years was considered to construct the proximal femur SSM. The results of the segmentation process were evaluated to ensure that they were representative of the objects in the CT images. The segmentation was evaluated via empirical goodness evaluation methods that assess the quality of the segmented images as described in20. After the segmentation was evaluated, the segmented images were converted to 3D surfaces via the surface generation technique of volume rendering and smoothed through the smoothing module in the 3D Slicer software. The 3D surface generation was performed on the default value of vertices in the 3D slicer to carry much shape/form information for the proximal femur. The large number of nodes and triangles that represented the 3D surfaces resulted in large Stereo Lithography (STL) files with no gain in topology and therefore were further processed to reduce sizes and to make the meshes isotropic, i.e., making the mesh triangles uniform across the regions of the meshes. Tests performed by Mutsvangwa et al. revealed that isotropic vertex establishment improved the process of establishing dense correspondence for registration21.

Surface mesh refining and smoothing

After segmentation and 3D surface reconstruction in a 3D slicer, surface meshes might contain holes, non-uniform triangles, and many node points without topological significance. Hence, surface mesh refinement, through hole filling, mesh re-meshing, and further smoothing were performed through the filling of holes via the “Fill Hole Freeform” Module in Materialise, 3-Matic software. As shown in Fig. 5a, the mesh is composed of a rough surface, and in Fig. 5b, the mesh is smoothed and uniform. The mesh refinement process was performed as follows: first, a uniform mesh generation was performed, and second, an isotropic (equilateral triangle) mesh generation was performed. The mesh refinement and smoothing of the meshes reduced the number of points and triangles.

Surface mesh refining and smoothing, (a) shows a mesh before re-meshing and smoothed and (b) shows a mesh after uniform mesh re-meshing and smoothing.

After hole filling, re-meshing was performed to generate a uniform triangle mesh, establish simplification of excess triangle nodes, and further smoothing to remove outliers. The quality of the mesh was evaluated by how well the elements resemble equilateral triangles, in terms of smoothness, and the maintenance of the features of the proximal femur. The quality of elements’ smoothness was evaluated by inspecting in the Materialise 3-Matic software with shape measures related to triangle properties, such as length and angle of triangles. The smoothed and re-meshed surface meshes have isotropic vertices with an average element size (triangle edge size) of 1.0 mm and a sharp edge angle of 60°.

Anatomical landmark identification and annotation

After re-meshing and smoothing of surface meshes, anatomical landmark identification and annotation are performed to establish surface mesh correspondence through registration. Landmarks are points within a dataset upon which correspondence is preserved. These landmark points need to be easily identifiable and reproducible in each session by different annotators at different times without ambiguity. Similarly, the number of landmark points to be annotated shall cover all regions of the proximal femur, i.e., not be concentrated only in some regions of the object. Annotating many landmark points in a Statistical Shape Model (SSM) might provide a high-resolution capture of local morphology, surface curvature, and detailed shape variations, which improves modeling accuracy. However, this often leads to increased computational cost, requires a larger training set to prevent overfitting, and makes finding consistent correspondence across shapes difficult22. However, minimizing the number of landmark points has advantages for manual point placement in terms of making the process simpler and quicker, reducing the time burden required for image processing, and also minimizing overfitting with a small training set22. Considering these facts, nine anatomical landmark points that are easily identifiable and reproducible in each session by different annotators at different times without ambiguity, with optimal coverage of all regions of the proximal femur, are selected in this study. Registrations for correspondence

To establish dense correspondence, a set of registrations was performed. First, rigid alignment is used to collect all the training datasets into a common coordinate system, i.e., normalizing the pose of an object with respect to some reference shape from datasets, followed by iterating the closest point23. Second, a parametric non-rigid registration algorithm was used as a model fitting optimizer, which optimizes the problem via gradient-based optimization. To perform the actual optimization, the workflow starts from building the Gaussian process (GP) model. The method works by fitting a reference shape (selected from the sample datasets, with the prerequisite that it has the representative topology of that species of shapes) to the targets.

Note that we used here not reference the mesh directly, but passed the mesh from which we find the closest points as an argument. Model validation

In SSM, before the model is used for any application, it has to be validated to ensure that the instances generated from the model are representative of the shape of the homologous series. Basically, in the SSM model, validation is assessed through four different tests24. The first goodness test is directly computed on the corresponding points as differences to manually selected anatomical landmarks as described in Sect. “Landmark precision test”. The other three are indirect tests, which are computed using a PCA model based on the correspondence by conducting compactness, specificity, and generality tests through a leave-one-out cross-validation process on the model25.

Femoral neck-shaft angle measurement

In this study, to overcome measurement deviations for the femoral neck-shaft angle (NSA) of 3D proximal femur models in 3-Matic software, a mathematical fitting (least-squares) approach using the software’s analytical tools was employed as a standardized 3-Matic workflow. Therefore, by isolating anatomical regions (marking) of the proximal femur and using “Best Fit” primitives to define axes, a repeatable standard approach is developed as shown in Fig.9 (a) and (b). That means the femoral head, neck, and shaft regions are marked separately using the “Wave Brush” module in Materialise 3-Matic software as described in26. The femoral neck axis is defined by fitting a best-fit sphere to the isolated femoral head surface by considering the sphere’s center (point A in Fig. 10a) as the first anchoring point in the procedure. Then, on the femoral neck an analytical curve was attached with “Create Curve” command in 3-Matic software as shown in Fig. 10a, and a sectioned cylinder (circle) was generated from the fitted curve. Then, the neck center is defined by fitting a best-fit cylinder or an inertia axis to the femoral neck surface, alternatively, creating a cross-section of the neck and setting its centroid (point B in Fig. 10a). Likewise, the line connecting point A and point B was defined as the 3D axis of the femoral neck26,27.

Schematic diagram showing the femoral shaft axis and femoral neck axis plotting.

Schematic diagram showing one of the femoral neck-shaft angles measured (a) measurement of the actual bone and (b) measurement of the plotted lines.

Similarly, the femoral shaft axis is defined by fitting a best-fit cylinder to the proximal diaphysis (shaft) region by marking with the “Rectangular Mark” command in 3-Matic software. Then, analytical section cylinders were formed, and the centers of the two circles were connected with a straight line to create the femoral shaft axis, as shown in Fig. 10a. Finally, the angle is measured using the “Measure Angle” tool to select the two constructed axes. In 3D space, these axes may not intersect; the software will calculate the angle between the two 3D vectors accordingly28.

In this study, to assess the reliability of the measurement method employed, the intra-class correlation (ICC) coefficient was used. After the sample size required in the reliability study is determined using the formula reported by Walter et al.29, four observers made three repeated measurements of proximal femur samples. A repeated measures analysis of variance was applied to avoid a significant difference in the results of the study based on the suggestion proposed by Weir et al.30. Proximal femora samples were subjected to repeated measurements in a fixed order by one senior attending orthopedic specialist with a minimum of a 24-h interval between trials to evaluate the intra-observer reliability. Three other subjects performed the same measurements on the same samples in an independent manner and a fixed order to assess inter-observer reliability.

The validation of the measurements’ consistency and reliability for intra-observer and inter-observer reliability was performed using the “MedCalc” statistical software package for Windows for biomedical research.

Surface mesh registrations

In this work, to establish dense correspondence (model fitting) across the datasets, parametric, non-rigid registration was performed. In this approach, since each target surface mesh is replaced by its corresponding fitted mesh after model fitting through deformable registration, the registration error is computed to evaluate how close the fitted mesh is to the target mesh. The error was measured by computing the point-to-point distances between each original mesh and its approximation through model fitting. The metric used was the mean squared distance between the fitted mesh and its corresponding original surface mesh. The mean squared distance determines how close points in one surface are to the corresponding points in the other surface, and the registration error is shown in Fig. 14.

Model validation

The quality of the statistical shape model developed was validated via shape quality metrics, including compactness, specificity, and generality tests. To evaluate the compactness of the model, model validation using a compactness metric is performed, and the compactness result shows that the first 11 PC components, approximately half of the PC components of the proximal femur model, represented 92.85% of the total variance, as was the case in31,32, as shown in Fig. 16.

Discussions

Landmark precision test

Owing to the ease of identifying the points by operators in a given session and covering all the parts of the object, nine anatomical landmark points were annotated manually via two observers, twice each, as shown in Fig. 6. The operators are experts in anatomical study. These landmark points were used as prior knowledge for initial registration. In addition, even though the landmark points are easily identifiable and reproducible across the training datasets, the occurrence of personal bias in each session of land-marking is natural. Hence, a landmark precision test was conducted to evaluate the annotated landmark points identified by observers with the best precision since the dense correspondence (model fitting) is influenced by the reliability of the annotated landmark points.

The precision test result revealed that more than 94% of the total landmarks were annotated precisely for both the observers and the inter-observer as well. It can be observed for the proximal femur bone that landmarks were annotated with a global error of 0.69 mm, 0.57 mm and 0.66 mm for observer 1, observer 2 and inter-observer, respectively. These results indicate that the annotated anatomical landmarks are easily identifiable and reproducible by observers precisely. Precise annotated landmark guides an effective dense correspondence (model fitting) across the datasets, which leads to efficient model development. From a topological perspective, the precision test results show that the identified and the annotated landmark points are easily identifiable with a small error, which enables optimal correspondence establishment later during model fitting.

Surface mesh registration

Surface registration is required for correspondence establishment across training sets. After all the training sets are transformed into a common coordinate system through rigid alignment, non-rigid registration based on a Gaussian process model fitting is performed, as shown in Fig. 8. Considering this as a model fit, a smooth Gaussian kernel was used as the model’s kernel. Model fitting by itself is trial and error, i.e., the registration result needs to be obtained by changing the tunable parameters that are the span (l) and the degree of deformation (s). After many iterations, model fitting through non-rigid registration is completed with an empirically determined span value of and degree of deformation for the Gaussian kernel. The termination of the model fitting ceases when the target mesh is adequately represented or mapped by the reference mesh, i.e., when it has an equal number of vertices or the same triangulation as the reference mesh. The same triangulation means that all the surface meshes share the same topology across the datasets. In the course of model fitting, since each target surface mesh was replaced by its corresponding fitted mesh after the registration, the degree of deformability of each bone was determined via visual inspection. This was validated by a numerical analysis of registration errors, which were computed to evaluate how close the fitted meshes were to the targets. The errors were measured by computing the point-to-point distances between each original mesh and its approximation or fit after registration through model fitting. The metric used was the mean squared distance between the fitted mesh and its corresponding original surface meshes as shown in Fig. 14.

Model development

The statistical shape model (SSM) was developed from only twenty-four subjects, and the findings of the study are dependent on the training set. Using principal component analysis, the anatomical variations across the twenty-four proximal femur models were quantified by twisting out the mean shape model from a standard deviation (STD) of -3 to +3 with the individual proximal femur models, as shown in Fig. 15. For the first mode, pure size variation in the shaft, neck, and head was observed from -3 STD to +3 STD. In addition, the femoral neck-shaft was more inclined from -3 STD to +3 STD. Similarly, from the -3 STD to the +3 STD, the greater trochanter becomes wider. For the second mode, the femoral neck-shaft was more inclined from -3 STD to +3 STD as shown in Fig. 15. Likewise, the greater trochanter becomes wider from -3 STD to +3 STD as shown in Fig. 15. For the third mode, pure size and shape variation was observed: from -3 STD to +3 STD the femoral neck becomes thicker, the greater trochanter is wider, and femoral neck-shaft inclination decreases slightly, as shown in Fig. 15. In a decreasing order of importance (from mode 1 to mode 3), these modes of variations show the most relevant changes in geometry witnessed in the studied population. In implant design perspective, these modes can be tuned to generate different sizes and geometries specific to the studied population. The above-mentioned anatomical variations are clinically relevant parameters in hip arthroplasty prosthesis. Hence, in the course of implant design optimization, considering these constraints is important to improve implant fitting during hip prosthesis surgery.

Model validation

The validation result revealed that out of the twenty-four training datasets, the first eleven principal components represented for 92.85% of the total variance of the SSM, i.e., the SSM required only half the number of principal components to define 92.85% of the total variance, as was31,32. In SSM, the model needs to be compact, i.e., within a smaller number of principal components; a higher value of cumulative variance is needed. Considering this, our SSM is compact since only half of the number of variables are required for the constructed shape model to define its population variance of approximately 93%. Similarly, the model represented an instance with an average error of specificity and generality, 1.33 mm and 0.82 mm, respectively. This means that the model was able to produce instances that are valid in their resemblance to other shapes in the training dataset with an average specificity error of 1.33 m, i.e., the SSM was able to efficiently represent unseen instances of the proximal femur in the datasets. The model was also able to represent new shape instances belonging to the class object of the training dataset with an average generality error of 0.82 mm. Based on the validation results, the shape of the developed model was sufficiently approximated from the training datasets. Owing to a lack of published evidence in the development and validation of the proximal femur SSM, comparisons with other methods is challenging.

Femoral neck-shaft angle measurement

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) or hip fracture fixation via prosthetic implants or fixation plate is a standard procedure for treating hip joint trauma and patients respond poorly to conventional medical therapy. However, implant-related complications might arise from anatomical variations among different population groups. The femoral neck-shaft angle (NSA) is one of the main design parameters that should be considered when designing hip prosthesis implants. The hip prototype must be created, and thus the hip stem-neck inclination must be optimized by taking into account the patient’s femoral morphology.

Even though the validation test demonstrates an excellent degree of consistency and reliability for intra-observer and inter-observer reliability, a slight NSA value difference is observed between the average SSM models and individually measured NSA values of proximal femurs. In this study, the measured values of the femoral NSA of the Ethiopian population vary from 110.07 to 136.26, and the mean ± standard deviation (STD) of 123.08 7.40. Meanwhile, the femoral NSA obtained from the average SSM model was 120.86. From this result, the mean neck-shaft angle obtained from the individual femur surfaces is slightly varied from that of the average SSM model developed from the datasets. This could be due to the reference bias at the time of mean shape model development, as well as due to personal bias in the course of a hypothetical marking of the femoral head, neck, and shaft with “Rectangular Mark,” since marking and curve sketching were performed by manually selecting the regions of interest.

Due to a lack of published evidence on proximal femur neck-shaft angle measurements in the Ethiopian population, the author was not able to compare the existing results with those of the local population. However, the femoral NSA measurement results of this study were compared with those of other population groups. For instance, the reported average femoral neck-shaft angle measurements of the Kenyans, Brazilians, Americans, and South Indians were 129.21, 130.47, 132.69, and 137.333,36, respectively.

The current study shows that the mean femoral NSA of the Ethiopian femora is smaller than that of the above-mentioned population groups. More specifically, it is much smaller than the American and Indian populations. Locally available THR implants are mainly acquired through donation from developed countries, especially from the United States of America37. Thus, utilizing the standard commercially available hip implants may not be the best fit for Ethiopian patient groups and could be the cause of implants related complications. A recent study on 50 locally treated THR reported that previous partial hip replacement femur neck fractures were converted to THR in 6 patients, one patient lost in the acetabular component and referred later abroad, and one patient loose hip stem37. Consequently, the mean SSM of this study could be used to optimize the available implants, and the femoral neck-shaft angle measurement could also be used to select implants closely related to the Ethiopian population.

Conclusion

In this study, the statistical shape model (SSM) of the proximal femur, including the main modes of variation and the morphometric analysis of femoral neck-shaft angle were conducted based on data from Ethiopian subjects. The quality of the developed model was validated via model quality metric tests (compactness, specificity, and generalization ability), and the validation results revealed that the model developed sufficiently approximated the shape from the training datasets. However, the SSM was developed from a few training datasets and collected from a limited region of the country, so the resulting SSM was expected to be limited in its ability to represent the entire population. Similarly, the mean femoral neck-shaft angle (NSA) and the standard deviation of femoral specimens collected from the Ethiopian population were estimated, and the results of this investigation could serve as a starting point for this population.

Future work and recommendations

In SSM, the number of training datasets needs to be representative of the given population. Additionally, morphometric information on shape variations in the population is vital for implant design and development because these parameters influence implant sizing and stability. In this study, the number of training samples is small due to the unavailability of pelvic CT images. Hence, in the future, a full-length shape model of the femur with a large number of training samples and its morphometric parameters could be developed to further improve the hip rotation and flexion as per the clinical standards and to enhance the reliability of measurement of the femoral head diameter, horizontal offset, and femoral neck-anteversion angle.
