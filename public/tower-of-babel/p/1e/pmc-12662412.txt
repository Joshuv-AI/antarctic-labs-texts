Correlation between plasma cortisol and white matter hyperintensity in middle-aged and elderly individuals with major depressive disorder: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Shipan Zhang, Xueru Zhang, Zhaowei Hou, Di Fei, Chenyang Gao, Man Jin, Peiyuan Lv, Tianjun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To examine the relationship between plasma cortisol levels and the severity of white matter hyperintensities (WMH) in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD). This study included 240 patients with MDD who were hospitalized in the Department of Neuropsychology at Hebei General Hospital from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024. General data and plasma cortisol concentrations were obtained. Based on the total score of the Fazekas scale, patients were divided into 3 groups: no/mild group (Fazekas scale total score 0–2 points, 87 participants), moderate group (Fazekas scale total score 3–4 points, 85 participants), and severe group (Fazekas scale total score 5–6 points, 68 participants). The disparities in general and clinical data among various patient groups were compared, and the risk factors for WMH were investigated. Among 3 groups of patients, significant differences were in terms of age (58.23 ± 8.64 vs 63.59 ± 7.62 vs 67.51 ± 7.78), proportion of hypertension (37.90% vs 42.40% vs 67.60%), total cholesterol (4.85 ± 1.06 vs 4.95 ± 1.12 vs 4.53 ± 1.05), low-density lipoprotein (3.00 ± 0.70 vs 3.10 ± 0.81 vs 2.67 ± 0.79), and plasma cortisol level (13.25 ± 4.31 vs 14.36 ± 4.51 vs 15.86 ± 5.06). Age (odds ratio [OR] = 1.10, P = .00), hypertension (OR = 1.54, P = .03), and cortisol level (OR = 1.09, P = .00) were independent risk variables for the severity of WMH in individuals with MDD, while low-density lipoprotein (OR = 0.30, P = .04) was a protective factor for the severity of white matter hyperintensity in patients with depression. Plasma cortisol is proposed as an independent risk factor influencing the severity of white matter hyperintensity in individuals with MDD, and ameliorating elevated cortisol levels in these patients may enhance their prognosis.

1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is among the most prevalent psychiatric disorders, impacting roughly 300 million individuals globally,[1] and it is anticipated that MDD will be among the most burdensome diseases by 2030.[2] An increasing number of studies have discovered abnormalities, including elevated volumes of white matter hyperintensities (WMH), in the brain of people with MDD.[3] WMH leads to a significant deterioration in cognitive function and daily living skills in individuals with MDD and may be linked to symptoms, such as non-suicidal self-injury, potentially forecasting inadequate drug response and unfavorable prognosis.[4–7] The pathophysiology of WMH is intricate and can be affected by cardiovascular risk factors and hereditary variables.[8] However, the precise etiology of the condition remains ambiguous.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is a crucial neuroendocrine system that regulates the stress response, emotional coordination, and various other physiological functions.[9] Cortisol, a steroid hormone released by the HPA axis, is linked to the stress response and is a frequently utilized biomarker in psychology.[10] Patients with MDD exhibit increased cortisol levels in cerebral fluid and plasma, accompanied by defective negative feedback mechanisms.[9] Prolonged exposure to elevated cortisol levels due to stress or external delivery may adversely affect brain anatomy and function in animals.[11] Cortisol is reported to be linked with the synthesis of WMH.[12] The present study retrospectively examined the correlation between WMH and cortisol levels in individuals with MDD to establish a foundation for diagnosing and treating WMH in this population.

2. Participants and methods

2.1. Research participants

This study retrospectively included 240 patients with MDD who were hospitalized in the Department of Neuropsychology at Hebei General Hospital from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024. Patients aged 45 years or older who underwent cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and serum cortisol assessment during hospitalization were included. Patients were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria: cognitive impairment; any diagnosis of psychosis, bipolar disorder, alcohol or substance use disorder; the presence of mixed intracranial lesions (tumor, infection, history of stroke, neurodegenerative disease, intracranial hemorrhage, and craniocerebral injury); severe hepatic and renal disorders; the administration of exogenous glucocorticoids; major arterial stenosis over 50% in both intracranial and extracranial regions; and suboptimal image quality. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei General Hospital.

2.2. Collection of clinical data

Data related to sex, age, body mass index , and history of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were collected. Blood samples for total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein, fasting blood glucose, and serum cortisol levels were obtained from all patients between 6:00 am and 7:30 am.

Cranial MRI was conducted on all patients within 1 week of admission utilizing a 3.0T MR scanner (Signa EXCITE, GE, Waukesha). The primary scanning sequences comprised T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery. Two seasoned neurologists assessed the severity of WMH based on the cranial MRI results of patients. A consensus was achieved following deliberation with a 3rd physician during the disagreement. The intensity of paraventricular WMH and deep subcortical WMH was assessed using the Fazekas scale (0–3 points) on the T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequence, and the scores were aggregated to derive the overall score. First, Paraventricular WMHs were rated as 0 for absence of lesions, 1 for a cap-like or thin pencil-like lesion, 2 for a smooth halo lesion, and 3 for irregular paraventricular high signal extending into deep white matter; second, deep subcortical WMHs were rated as 0 for absence of lesions, 1 for a punctate lesion, 2 for initial fusion of the lesion, and 3 for extensive fusion of the lesion. Patients were categorized into 3 groups according to their total Fazekas scale score: No/mild group (Fazekas scale score 0–2, n = 87), moderate group (Fazekas scale score 3–4, n = 85), and severe group (Fazekas scale score 5–6, n = 68).

2.3. Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago) was used for the statistical analysis; measurement data are presented as means ± standard deviation. The normal distribution data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA among the 3 groups, while data that did not adhere to normal distribution were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Count data were represented as frequencies (%), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Chi-square or Fisher exact tests. The determinants influencing the severity of WMH were examined by Ordered logistic regression analysis. Statistically significant differences were indicated by P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Comparison of basic characteristics among 3 study groups

Of 240 total patients, 73 (30.42%) were men and 167 (69.58%) were women, with the mean age (standard deviation) of 62.76 ± 8.85 years. The number of male participants in the no/mild, moderate, and severe groups was 19 (21.80%), 28 (32.90%), and 26 (38.2%), respectively. Significant differences were observed among 3 groups in terms of age (P = .00) and the proportion of concomitant hypertension (P = .00). The age of patients in the severe group was highest (P = .00). And the age of patients in the moderate group was significantly higher than that in the no/mild group (P = .00). However, no significant difference was observed for sex composition (P = .07), body mass index (P = .28), smoking (P = .20), drinking (P = .09), and the proportion of combined diabetes (P = .23; Table 1).

3.2. Comparison of laboratory parameters among 3 groups

Cortisol levels differed significantly among the 3 groups (P = .00). Post hoc analyses indicated that cortisol level was significantly higher in the severe group compared with the no/mild (P = .00) and the moderate group (P = .05; Table 2). There were significant differences in TC (P = .05) and LDL (P = .00) among the 3 groups. Patients in the severe group had lower TC than the moderate group (P = .05). Patients in the severe group had lower LDL levels than those in the no/mild group (P = .01) and the moderate group (P = .00), and the difference was statistically significant. Conversely, no significant differences were found in triglycerides (P = .98), high-density lipoprotein (P = .38) and fasting blood glucose (P = .18) levels among the 3 groups (Table 2).

3.3. Ordered logistic regression analysis of determinants affecting the severity of WMH grading

Ordered logistic regression analysis was conducted using variables that exhibited significance in univariate analysis with age, hypertension, TC, LDL, and cortisol level as independent variables and WMH severity as the dependent variable (assigned values: none or mild = 1, moderate = 2, and severe = 3). Age (odds ratio [OR] = 1.10, P = .00), hypertension (OR = 1.54, P = .03), and cortisol level (OR = 1.09, P = .00) were risk factors influencing the severity of WMH in patients with MDD, while LDL (OR = 0.30) is a protective factor for the severity of WMH (Table 3).

4. Discussion

This study classified middle-aged and elderly patients with MDD into 3 categories based on the severity of WMH. Significant differences were observed in age, concomitant hypertension rates, TC, LDL and cortisol levels across the 3 groups. Ordered logistic regression analysis indicated that age, hypertension and cortisol levels were risk variables for the severity of WMH, while LDL is a protective factor for the severity of WMH.

WMH are prevalent in cranial MRI or CT scans among the elderly, those with stroke, and people with dementia.[13] Clinically, paraventricular WMH correlate with cognitive deterioration, whereas deep WMH are linked to depressive symptoms and a variable clinical course.[14–16] The pathophysiology of WMH is intricate and may be linked to the blood-brain barrier failure, neuroinflammation, metabolic irregularities, and aging. Consistent with previous results,[17] age was identified as a risk factor for the severity of WMH in the present study as well. Busby et al discovered a positive correlation between age and the amount of WMH, indicating that WMH may underlie cognitive impairment associated with aging. With advancing age,[18] a greater focus must be directed on WMH in patients with MDD.

Hypertension is associated with increased WMH volume.[19] In addition, antihypertensive drug use, which represents poor blood pressure control, was also associated with increased WMH volume.[20] Consistent with previous findings, hypertension was also a risk factor for increased WMH in our study. Hypertension can exacerbate neurologic, such as microglia activation and astrocyte proliferation, and vascular inflammation, which in turn leads to increased WMH.[21] Thus, controlling hypertension in patients with MDD may be beneficial in improving WMH. In this study, LDL was found to be a protective factor for the exacerbation of WMH, and LDL was negatively correlated with WMH in patients with MDD. The correlation between LDL and WMH is controversial. Some studies have found that LDL is negatively correlated with WMH,[22] while some studies have found no correlation between LDL and WMH.[23] The correlation between LDL and WMH in depression needs further study.

Cortisol is a steroid hormone synthesized during activation of the HPA axis in response to stress. Patients with MDD exhibit elevated plasma cortisol levels relative to healthy controls, particularly among those displaying psychotic symptoms, and their cortisol levels are inversely linked with cognitive performance.[24] The present study identified cortisol as an independent risk factor for the severity of WMH in patients with MDD. The research conducted by Qiu et al[12] on individuals with cerebral small-vessel disease also identified cortisol as a risk factor for WMH. The lipophilic properties of cortisol facilitate its passage across the blood–retinal barrier, and persistently high cortisol adversely impacts brain anatomy and function in both humans and animals.[25] Furthermore, increasing evidence indicates that elevated blood cortisol levels correlate with endothelial dysfunction.[26] Cortisol may induce vascular endothelial dysfunction by decreasing nitric oxide synthase expression and increasing nitric oxide degradation, resulting in a persistent state of hypoperfusion that influences the severity of WMH.[12] Cortisol is a risk factor for the emergence of WMH in patients with MDD and may facilitate cognitive deterioration in these individuals; hence, the manipulation of plasma cortisol levels could serve as a therapeutic target for enhancing cognitive function in patients with MDD.

There are limitations to this study. Firstly, this study was conducted on patients with MDD in a single hospital and the study population was limited. In future studies we should validate the findings in different populations. In addition, the present study was a cross-sectional study and could not confirm the relationship between cortisol and WMH, and in future studies we can conduct cohort studies to confirm the relationship between WMH and cortisol.

In conclusion, plasma cortisol level serves as an independent risk factor for the severity of WMH in patients with MDD, and mitigating elevated cortisol level is crucial for the prognosis of these patients.
