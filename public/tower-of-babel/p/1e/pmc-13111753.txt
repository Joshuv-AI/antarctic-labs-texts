Investigation into the radioactivity of various natural and anthropogenic radionuclides in marine sediments from the Sudanese coastline of the Red Sea
Authors: Elshafeea Abowslama, Mohamed Eltayeb, Khalid Elmamoun Ibrahim, Tarig Elshaarani, Mossap Babikir, Ekrami Osman, Gosai Abdelgader, Nuha Tarig, Nasreen Hassan, Mashaer Khalied, Fatima Elbasheer, Mohammed AbdulRahman, Alfatih Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA total of sixty surface sediment samples were systematically obtained from ten distinct locations along the Sudanese coastline of the Red Sea during the summer season (June 2016) and the winter season (February 2017), subsequently analyzed using high-purity germanium (HPGe) gamma spectrometry. The activity concentrations of natural radionuclides (40K, 226Ra, 232Th) as well as the anthropogenic radionuclide (137Cs) were quantified. During the summer, the concentrations recorded were as follows: 40K = 219 ± 169 Bq/kg, 226Ra = 6.94 ± 3.59 Bq/kg, 232Th = 6.32 ± 3.13 Bq/kg, and 137Cs = 1.89 ± 1.15 Bq/kg. The relatively large standard deviation, particularly for 40K, reflects spatial variability among the different sampling locations. The values recorded during the winter season exhibited a slight reduction. The highest activity levels were detected at Port Sudan Harbor, likely attributed to the influence of industrial activities and the composition of sediments. Correlations among the radionuclides suggest analogous geochemical behaviors. The radiological hazard indices, including Raeq and AEDE, were found to be substantially lower than global safety levels. No indications of recent anthropogenic inputs of 137Cs were identified, supporting prior findings within the region. This study contributes updated baseline data and facilitates a comparative analysis with previous research conducted in both Sudanese and neighboring Red Sea locales.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42807-2.Keywords: Marine radioactivity, Sediment contamination, Environmental radiological hazards, Anthropogenic and natural radionuclides, Red Sea coastal environmentSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences

Introduction

Marine ecosystems play a critical role in biodiversity conservation, food security, and climate regulation. However, these systems are increasingly exposed to contaminants originating from both natural geological processes and human activities. Radioactive contamination is a major concern because radionuclides persist in the environment and can accumulate in sediments and living organisms, causing long-term risks to ecosystems and human health1,2. In marine and coastal environments, the dominant contributors to natural radioactivity are naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM), mainly radionuclides from the Uranium-238 (238U) decay series (commonly quantified through Radon (226Ra), the Thorium-232 (232Th), and Potassium-40 (40K). These radionuclides enter the marine system through weathering, erosion, riverine transport, atmospheric deposition, and sedimentary processes3. The environmental behavior of these radionuclides is strongly influenced by mineralogy, grain size, and depositional dynamics4,5. Consequently, coastal and seabed sediments act as long-term sinks and archives for radionuclides, making them suitable matrices for establishing radiological baselines and evaluating environmental risks6–9. In addition to natural inputs, marine environments may also receive anthropogenic radionuclides, including Cesium-137 (137Cs), Strontium-90 (90Sr), and Plutonium isotopes (239+240Pu), associated with nuclear accidents, past weapons testing fallout, and specific industrial pathways. Although anthropogenic radionuclides are typically detected at low levels in marine sediments, they are valuable tracers of sediment transport, mixing processes, and human influence in coastal systems10,11. Increasing global interest in marine radioactivity highlights the need for regional baseline data to enable long-term comparison and effective environmental management. In environmental the Radiological risk is commonly evaluated using standardized indices aligned with guidelines of United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR) and the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP)1,2. These approaches include the calculation of radium equivalent activity (Raeq), absorbed gamma dose rate (D), annual effective dose, external and internal hazard indices, gamma index, and excess lifetime cancer risk (ELCR). Such indices are widely applied in recent benchmark studies, including assessments of Mediterranean beach sands12, coastal sediments of North Cyprus6,13 Mediterranean black sands enriched in heavy minerals4 and environmentally sensitive and archaeological areas such as El-Dakhla Oasis, Egypt, where these parameters have been explicitly reported to establish reference conditions and evaluate potential health risks8. Therefore, monitoring the of radioactive contamination is essential for assessing the environmental and human health risks, particularly in the regions where rapid industrialization and mining are undergoing to ensure a safe environment and food supplies14.

The Red Sea is a semi-enclosed marine basin of high ecological and economic significance. The limited water exchange, intensive maritime traffic, and expanding coastal and offshore industrial activities increase the potential for retention and accumulation of like radionuclides in coastal sediments. Radiological investigations in the Red Sea region have been conducted since the 1990s and provide valuable baseline information for environmental radioactivity. Along the Sudanese Red Sea coastline, early studies quantified natural and fallout radionuclides in sediments and seawater, establishing the first regional background data15–17. Subsequent investigations extended these assessments to marine organisms and estimated committed effective doses associated with seafood consumption18,19. Comparable studies in neighboring Red Sea sectors, including the Egyptian and Saudi Arabian coastlines, further demonstrated the influence of geological setting, sediment composition, and coastal development on radionuclide distributions in marine environments20–22. Despite these contributions, the Sudanese Red Sea coastline remains underrepresented in regional radiological databases despite increasing anthropogenic pressures. The expanding industrial activities, coastal development, and climate-driven sediment changes necessitate updated baseline data on sediment radioactivity for reliable comparison and radiological risk assessment6,12,23,24. The aim of this study is to investigates the activity concentrations of 40K, 226Ra, 232Th, and 137Cs in surface sediments collected along the Sudanese Red Sea coastline during two contrasting seasons and evaluate associated radiological hazards using commonly applied comparative indices to support baseline environmental monitoring.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area covers the Sudanese Red Sea coastal zone, extending from Port Sudan to Sawakin within the Red Sea State. It includes nearshore marine sediments, coastal harbors, and neighboring onshore zones of the Red Sea Hills geological province. Geologically, the region lies along the eastern mineralized margin of Sudan, where the Red Sea rift system intersects with Precambrian basement rocks containing trace radioactive elements25. The coastal terrain consists of Precambrian crystalline basement overlain by younger sedimentary sequences influenced by Red Sea rifting. Terrigenous input from the weathering of basement rocks contributes to depositional settings that facilitate uranium and thorium enrichment through various geochemical pathways15. Marine sediments show distinct geochemical signatures related to rapid shallow-water deposition at reef and tidal-flat sites, with isotopic disequilibria indicating active scavenging and sedimentation processes16.

Sample collection and measurements

A total of 60 surface sediment samples were collected form the Sudanese Red Sea Coastal zone: 30 during summer (June 2016) and 30 during winter (February 2017), from 10 coastal locations (three replicates per location per season) (Fig. 1). Sediment samples were taken from the top 0–5 cm using a Van Veen grab sampler. Water salinity and pH were measured in situ. The collected samples were air-dried, sieved through a 2 mm mesh and homogenized. 0.5 kg of each sample was sealed in 500 mL Marinelli breakers for 21 days to ensure secular equilibrium between 222Rn and 226Ra.

Gamma-ray spectrometric analysis was performed using a high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector (GCD30185X, Baltic Scientific Instruments, Latvia). Measurements were performed using a coaxial HPGe detector with a relative efficiency of 37% and an energy resolution of 1.95 keV at 1332.52 keV. The HPGe gamma-ray spectrometry system was calibrated for both energy and efficiency prior to sample analysis. Energy calibration was conducted using standard gamma-ray sources emitting well-known energies covering the range of interest (59.5–2204.5 keV). Efficiency calibration was conducted using certified reference standard sources with known activities, prepared in 500 mL Marinelli beaker identical to those used for sediment samples to maintain consistent geometry and minimize systematic uncertainty. The full-energy peak efficiency calibration curve was experimentally established and applied to determine the activity concentrations of radionuclides in all samples. Each sample was counted for 3 h in the led shielded HPGe detector chamber under liquid nitrogen to reduce background interference. The activity concentration of 226Ra radionuclide was then determined from the 609.3 Kev γ beak of 214Bi, while 232Th was estimated using the 911.1 Kev of γ beak of 228Ac. The activity of 40K radionuclide was determined from its own 1460 Kev γ. Background measurements were conducted using an empty Marinelli beaker under identical measurement conditions to account for natural radiation interference26.

Result and discussion

Quality of the data; accuracy, precision, (through analysis of CRM)

To ensure accurate results, triplicate samples were analyzed in addition to efficiency calibration. The relative uncertainty of measurement was 7–18% for all radionuclides. The activity concentrations of radionuclides (137Cs, 226Ra, 232Th, and 40K) measured in coastal sediment samples along the Sudanese Red Sea coast exhibit clear spatial and seasonal variability, with radiological hazard indices consistently below global safety limits. These results are consistent with previous findings in the Red Sea region40,41 but are now reinforced by recent studies that provide broader global context.

A statistically significant seasonal difference in 137Cs activity concentration was observed between winter and summer (P-value = 0.001), with higher levels detected during the summer (0.89–4.63 Bq/kg) compared to winter (0.02–2.36 Bq/kg). The seasonal mean values were 1.89 ± 1.15 Bq/kg in summer and 0.95 ± 0.74 Bq/kg in winter (Fig. 2). This trend contrasts with findings by Al-Hamed et al.32, who reported no significant seasonal influence on 137Cs concentrations in similar marine environments. The increase in activity concentration during summer could be attributed to increased geochemical stability, enhanced salinity, and reduced hydrodynamic disturbance, which collectively favor the adsorption of 137Cs onto fine-grained, clay-rich sediment particles8,42. Elevated temperatures enhance processes such as evaporation and ion exchange, thereby influencing the partitioning of radionuclides between seawater and sediments. Similar seasonal behavior has been reported for Mediterranean beach sands, where higher temperatures and salinity promoted the retention of 226Ra and 232Th on sediment surfaces5. Minor contributions from atmospheric deposition during summer dust events cannot be completely excluded; however, their contribution is expected to be minor comparative to sedimentary retention processes. In contrast, winter conditions characterized by stronger tidal currents and increased rainfall may enhance sediment resuspension, leading to the remobilization of 137Cs into the water column, as also reported by Ha et al.43.

Spatially, the concentration radionuclides were highest at Port Sudan locations (e.g., KHRS, KMRS, SLRS, WRS), and lowest at Dammat (DRS), Kello-8 (8RS) and Sawakin (SRS) (Table 1; Fig. 3). These results are in line with previous findings from same area40. This could be attributed to increased industrial waste discharge, maritime activity, and sediment characteristics enriched in clay minerals and organic matter in the Port-Sudan area. This allows 137Cs to bind to clay particles and be rapidly transferred into sediments as some clay minerals have a high adsorption capacity for cesium44–46. The influence of clay and mineralogical composition on radionuclide retention is further confirmed in the Mediterranean and Caspian regions4,47. However, this argument does not apply in all circumstances, according to45. In contrast, Dammat and Kello-8 are dominated by carbonate- and silica-rich sandy sediments with minimal terrestrial input and high salinity, all of which limit radionuclide binding and deposition48. Similar sediment–radionuclide relationships have been observed along the Saudi Red Sea coast, where sandy marine sediments corresponded with reduced radioactivity levels49. In Kello-8, the very low water levels resulted from maintenance activities over the past few years to preserve beach quality. Dredging can remove contaminated sediments, while the influx of sand may cause dilution46. The present 137Cs activity levels are consistent with previous regional assessments. For example, Ref.50 reported average values of 7.02 ± 1.76 Bq/kg in Port Sudan and 4.51 ± 3.2 Bq/kg in Sawakin. After correcting for radioactive decay, these values closely align with the current measures, suggesting no significant new 137Cs inputs to the region over the past two decades.

Internationally, the 137Cs levels recorded in this study are comparable to those reported in the Arabian Gulf, 137Cs with concentrations ranged from < MDA to 2.16 ± 0.25 Bq/kg51, along the Mediterranean coast ranged from < 0.1 to 3.22 Bq/kg44 and the Red Sea coast of Egypt (1.2 ± 0.3 Bq/kg; Harb, 2008). In contrast, higher levels were reported in the Black Sea (up to 41 Bq/kg) and Gulf of Greece (Up to 64.3 ± 5.6 Bq/kg)52,53. These comparisons suggest that radionuclide inputs in the study area have remained low and stable over time. The spatial and seasonal distribution of 40K activity concentrations in the studied sediments is presented in Fig. 3. The activity concentration levels of 40K ranged from 31.00 to 366.33 Bq/kg in winter and 21.35 to 463.33 Bq/kg in summer with average values of 202.52 ± 141.02 Bq/kg and 219.09 ± 168.90 Bq/kg respectively (Table 1). Similar results were reported along the Red Sea coast of Egypt41, the Gulf of Suez20,21 and Sudanese coast40,50. However, the observed values are lower than those reported for more mineralogically enriched environments, such as the Red Sea coast of Egypt41, the Gulf of Aden54, the Gulf of Aqaba22, and Jeddah, Saudi Arabia55. Overall, the40 K activity concentrations are generally below the world median value of 412 Bq/kg reported by UNSCEAR56, indicating no radiological concern. Within the Red Sea region, 40K activity concentrations in this study exceed those recorded at Farasan Island57 and the Suez Canal58, reflecting regional variability associated with differences in sediment texture, mineralogical composition, and depositional environment. Outside the region, average concentration of 40K was reported by Botwe et al.46 in Tema Harbor, Ghana (320 Bq/kg) and by59 along the Tamil Nadu coast, India (349.6 Bq/kg) exceed the averages observed in this study. Similarly, 40K levels in sediments of the Arabian Gulf ranged from 202 ± 11 to 432 ± 15 Bq/kg51), again slightly higher than the current findings. Studies on Mediterranean and semi-enclosed marine systems support these findings. For example, in North Cyprus and Caspian Sea coastal sediments the concentrations of 40K were varied with sediment texture and mineral composition6,47. In the black sands and coastal zones along the Mediterranean an elevated concentrations of 40K were reported in mineral-rich sediments, especially where heavy minerals accumulate4. In contrast, lower 40K levels were recorded at Dammat (DRS) and Kello-8 (8RS) as the carbonate- and silica-rich sediments have a reduced capacity to adsorb potassium radionuclides60. Furthermore, Kello-8 has undergone beach replenishment with imported sand, likely diluting natural radioactivity. Dammat location is surrounded by salt ponds and exhibits high water salinity with minimal terrestrial input, these factors also constrain radionuclide accumulation.

Seasonally, 40K activity concentrations showed slight increases in summer, observed in 60% of sampled locations. This is likely due to higher evaporation rates and the associated increases in salinity, which promote the precipitation and retention of 40K in sediments51. The increase in water salinity in summer is due to evaporation. Additionally, greater water column stability in summer may enhance sediment–water interactions, allowing more effective sorption of potassium to mineral surfaces. The distribution of activity concentration levels for 226Ra and 232Th across the studied locations during winter and summer is shown in Figs. 4 and 5. In the winter season, the activity concentration levels of 226Ra range from 2.1 to 13.33 Bq/kg, with an average of 7.13 ± 3.86 Bq/kg. For 232Th, the levels range from less than 0.30 to 8.47 Bq/kg, with an average of 4.94 ± 2.97 Bq/kg. In the summer, the 226Ra concentration ranges from 2.42 to 12.23 Bq/kg, averaging 6.94 ± 3.59 Bq/kg. For 232Th in summer, the concentration ranges from less than 0.30 to 10.3 Bq/kg, with an average of 6.32 ± 3.13 Bq/kg. Those levels are comparable to those reported by 31 in the Marsa Alam area along the Red Sea coast of Egypt (Table 1). However, the values are slightly lower than those reported at the Temsah Lake beaches of the Suez Canal58, the coast of Red Sea in Egypt41, and at Al Aqabah Gulf along the coast of Saudi Arabia22. Additionally, these levels are notably lower than those reported by61 on the Gulf of Aden coast, south of Yemen.

When comparing this data with findings from regions outside the Red Sea, the values are comparable to those reported by62 in the Mediterranean Sea near Egypt, which ranged from 3.0 to 13.8 Bq/kg for 226Ra and from 0.6 to 7.8 Bq/kg for 232Th. These values are lower than those found by51 in sediments of the Arabian Gulf, where 226Ra ranged from 18.3 ± 1.6 to 37.6 ± 4.5 Bq/kg and 232Th ranged from 7.8 ± 1.3 to 25.5 ± 3.7 Bq/kg. Additionally, the levels measured in this study are slightly lower than the averages reported by46 at Tema Harbor, Accra, Ghana, which were 14 Bq/kg for 226Ra and 30 Bq/kg for 232Th. Furthermore, these levels are below the world average concentrations for 226Ra and 232Th as reported by30, which are 35 Bq/kg and 30 Bq/kg, respectively. The activity concentrations of 226Ra and 232Th are higher in the Port Sudan area compared to Sawakin, Dammat, and Kello 8. This increase may be attributed to industrial pollutants that enhance the binding of radionuclides to sediments, as noted by45. In contrast, the low concentration levels in Dammat and Kello 8 may be due to the high carbonate content in the sediments, which has an inverse relationship with natural radioactivity60. It reported that sediments with lower carbonate content exhibit higher levels of natural radionuclides.

As shown in Table 2 (winter season) and Table 3 (summer season), the activity concentrations of natural radionuclides (226Ra, 232Th, and 40K) show moderate to strong positive correlations with 137Cs. During winter, the correlation coefficients between 137Cs and 40K, 226Ra, and 232Th are 0.78, 0.76, and 0.80, respectively (Table 2). In summer, these correlations are stronger, with r values of 0.87, 0.88, and 0.87, respectively (Table 3). Overall, the observed correlations (r ≈ 0.7–0.9) indicate clear spatial co-variability between 137Cs and naturally occurring radionuclides in surface sediments. Locations with high levels of 40K also exhibit high levels of 137Cs, and the correlation coefficients remain consistently strong across both summer and winter seasons. This suggests similar sedimentary controls and geochemical behavior of 137Cs and 40K. In addition, the positive correlations observed between 137Cs and both 226Ra and 232Th indicate that areas enriched in 137Cs also tend to have higher concentrations of naturally occurring radionuclides. Although 137Cs is of anthropogenic origin, it is well known to be particle-reactive and preferentially associated with fine-grained terrigenous sediments. Therefore, its spatial distribution may follow that of mineral-bound radionuclides transported and deposited with the same sediment fraction. This may be attributed to industrial pollutants in these locations, which facilitate the binding of radionuclides to sediments45. This evidence suggests that Port Sudan is more polluted than other locations. Additionally, there was a strong correlation between the natural radionuclides (226Ra, 232Th, and 40K) in the winter and summer seasons. The high correlation between 232Th and 226Ra aligns with findings reported by63. These strong correlations indicate that the sources of these nuclides originate from the same mineral fraction, as noted by63. There is no significant difference in the levels of natural radionuclides (226Ra, 232Th, and 40K) between the winter and summer seasons, with t-test results of 0.78, 0.87, and 0.82, respectively. However, the correlation in the summer is higher than in the winter, which may be due to geochemical and environmental conditions. Therefore, future studies should collect sediment samples during the summer for contamination analysis. The 226Ra:226Th ratio in winter is slightly higher than in summer (see Table 4). This may be due to increased salinity in summer, which enhances the desorption of 226Ra from sediment to water64.

Radiological hazard parameters were calculated to assess the radiological risk associated with naturally occurring radionuclides (226Ra, 232Th, and 40K) present in the examined sediments. The derived values of these parameters for both summer and winter seasons are explained in Tables 5 and 6. The radium equivalent activity (Raeq) values are relatively low across all sampling locations, with a peak value observed in the Port Sudan area of less than 60.00 Bq/kg. This figure is markedly below the internationally accepted threshold of Raeq (370 Bq/kg) (UNSCEAR, 2000), indicating negligible radiological concern when compared with internationally reported screening standards. The external hazard index (Hex) remains below the reference value by a factor exceeding five during both seasons which implies the absence of external hazard in studied locations throughout these seasons. The Hex values ranged from 0.011 to 0.168 in summer and from 0.01 to 0.13 in winter, with mean values of 0.081 and 0.08, respectively.

The internal hazard index (Hin) must remain below unity to guarantee screening benchmark of radon and its short-lived progeny (UNSCEAR, 2000). As per the data in Tables 5 and 6, the Hin is below the permissible level by a factor of five in the summer and less than five-fold in the winter. This finding suggests the absence of internal hazards in both seasons. Likewise, the internal hazard index (Hin) values ranged from 0.02 to 0.20 in summer and from 0.02 to 0.17 in winter, indicating that internal exposure resulting from radon and its progeny is also maintained within safe limits. The maximum absorbed dose is 31.06 nGy/h in all locations in summer, while the winter maximum is 24.84 nGy/h. Both values are below the comparison level of 53 nGy/h, as indicated by (UNSCEAR, 2000) for external exposure to terrestrial gamma radiation. This finding suggests no significant absorbed dose associated with natural radionuclides. The maximum annual effective doses measured outdoors were 38.09 µSv/y and 30.46 µSv/y with average doses of 18.4 ± 13.5 µSv/y and 17.7 ± 11.5 µSv/y in summer and winter respectively. These values are lower than the global average annual effective dose equivalent (AEDE) for infants, children, and adults, which are 90, 80, and 70 µSv/y, respectively65. From Tables 5 and 6, the gamma activity index (Iγ) ranged from 0.02 to 0.25 in summer and from 0.02 to 0.20 in winter, with average values of 0.12 ± 0.09 and 0.11 ± 0.07, respectively. All calculated Iγ values are well below the screening criterion of 2.0, corresponding to a dose limit of 0.3 mSv/y, suggesting no radiological concern related to gamma radiation from the studied sediments. This implies no concern regarding sediment activity concentrations in these locations. Natural radionuclides and radiological hazards have been evaluated for deposit samples from Red Sea sediment for an average of 70 years of life. The values of ELCR range from (0.43 to 6.67) × 10− 4 with an average of (3.22 ± 2.37) × 10− 4 and from (0.48 to 5.33) × 10− 4 with an average of (3.10 ± 2.01) × 10− 4 in summer and winter respectively. These values are comparable with 3.7 × 10− 4 reported by66 and world average values of 2.9 × 10− 4 reported by 67 and 30.

The annual gonadal dose equivalent (AGDE) values ranged from 14.18 to 225.41 µSv/y in summer and from 16.22 to 180.19 µSv/y in winter, with corresponding average values of 108.72 ± 80.30 µSv/y and 104.22 ± 68.14 µSv/y. These values are below the recommended safety limit of 300 µSv/y (UNSCEAR, 1988) and are also lower than those reported in comparable studies (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2013), indicating no potential genetic or reproductive radiological risk. Overall, the evaluated radiological hazard indices demonstrate that the investigated sediments from the Sudanese Red Sea coast do not pose any significant radiological risk to the environment or the public. The measured radiological parameters remain well within internationally recommended safety limits, confirming that the study area is radiologically safe for human activities.

Conclusion

This investigation assessed the concentrations and radiological impacts of natural radionuclides (226Ra, 232Th, 40K) and the anthropogenic radionuclide 137Cs in surface sediments along the Sudanese Red Sea coastline during both summer and winter seasons. The recorded activity concentrations were within acceptable safety limits and aligned with global and regional averages as documented by UNSCEAR and other relevant studies. Nevertheless, minor fluctuations were observed, attributable to variations in geochemical properties, sediment composition, and environmental conditions.

Elevated levels of radionuclides, particularly 137Cs, were detected in Port Sudan Harbor, signifying the impact of industrial operations and sediment characteristics that promote radionuclide retention. Seasonal trends revealed marginally heightened concentration during the summer months, potentially due to geochemical processes and diminished sediment resuspension. No signs of recent contamination by 137Cs were discovered, indicating the lack of new anthropogenic inputs over the preceding two decades. The radiological hazard indices remained significantly below established international safety thresholds, signifying no radiological hazard to the environment or public health.
