Structure–Activity Relationships for 1,3-Dipolar Cycloaddition Reactions of Criegee Intermediates with Carbonyls
Authors: Zachary A. Cornwell, Jonas J. Enders, Bridget Ferris, Craig Murray, Aaron W. Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbonyl oxides, or Criegee intermediates (CIs), are key species formed during the ozonolysis of alkenes and are an important atmospheric oxidant. However, direct measurements of CI gas-phase rate constants for many atmospherically relevant reactions are still unknown. Herein, we present various approaches for developing structure–activity relationships (SARs) to predict the reactivity of the simplest CI (formaldehyde oxide; CH2OO) with a range of carbonyls that are significant primary emission components as well as secondary products of hydrocarbon oxidation in the atmosphere. Approaches include the application of Hammett analysis, frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory, and global electrophilicity indices to develop SARs. By combining these parameters with available kinetic data, we establish substituent-based predictive models that extend to carbonyl species and CIs lacking measured rate constants that can enhance the predictive representation of CI chemistry in atmospheric models. Of the different approaches, Hammett constants yield the best correlations overall, however, low-cost quantum chemical calculations of orbital energies offer a practical alternative for systems lacking experimental substituent parameters. Finally, we extend this framework to explore the influence of substitution on the CI and the corresponding cycloaddition kinetics.

Introduction

Although the existence of Criegee intermediates (CIs, carbonyl oxides, R1R2COO) had been postulated since the 1930s, there were no means of directly measuring the gas-phase kinetics of these highly reactive intermediates until 2012, when it was discovered that photolysis of diiodomethane (CH2I2) in the presence of O2 yields a significant amount of the simplest CI, formaldehyde oxide (CH2OO), that can be used for direct kinetic studies. , This result was a major breakthrough, as accurate rate constants are required to characterize the impact of CIs on oxidation processes in the atmosphere and in combustion. These initial experimental measurements have been followed by an extensive body of work characterizing the spectroscopy and reactivity of CH2OO and larger CIs (R1R2COO, where R1 and R2 are alkyl or vinyl groups), − providing an important means of benchmarking theoretical methods needed to model their reactions in the atmosphere.

Ozonolysis reactions are prominent low-temperature oxidation processes and are a major chemical sink of unsaturated hydrocarbons in the atmosphere. , Ozone (O3) reacts with alkenes via a 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition across the double bond to form cyclic 1,2,3-trioxolanes, or primary ozonides (POZs), which decompose to form a carbonyl compound and a CI. As a coproduct of ozonolysis, carbonyls also act as important Criegee scavengers, reacting via an analogous 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition across the carbonyl double bond and leading to the formation of a cyclic 1,2,4-trioxolane species, or secondary ozonide (SOZ). , Reactions of CIs with carbonyls have been the focus of many recent direct experimental kinetics − and computational ,, studies.

1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions occur where an electron-rich 1,3-dipole (in this case, the CI) initiates bond formation via interaction with an electron-deficient dipolarophile (here, the carbonyl). The overall reactivity of this class of reaction has long been known to depend strongly on substituent effects, as argued by Sustmann in his discussion of Diels–Alder reactions. , The impact of different carbonyl substituents on reactivity toward CIs can be understood using concepts from frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory. , Substituent electronic properties (electron-withdrawing vs electron-donating) inductively affect the electron density of the carbonyl, respectively lowering or raising the FMO energies. The result is stabilization or destabilization of the cycloaddition transition state, which consequently increases or decreases the corresponding rate constant. For instance, the room-temperature bimolecular rate constant for the reaction of CH2OO with hexafluoroacetone ((CF3)2CO) is ∼100 times larger than that for the reaction with acetone ((CH3)2CO), , as the strongly electron-withdrawing trifluoromethyl (CF3) groups significantly lower the energies of the FMOs, while the electron-donating methyl (CH3) groups increase them. In lieu of orbital energies, which are typically obtained from ab initio calculations, the electron-withdrawing or donating character of substituents on the carbonyls can be quantified using Hammett substituent constants. , Hansch et al. organized the Hammett parameter coefficients for over 200 substituents that have several different electronic and steric properties.

Here, we provide an analysis of the kinetics of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions CIs with a range of carbonyl species. A sizable experimental data set now exists for reactions involving CH2OO, although there are almost no equivalent measurements for other CIs, where substituent effects are expected to play a similar role. As an alternative, the reactions of larger (substituted) CIs with SO2, which also proceed via a cycloaddition mechanism, will be used as a proxy. Following on from our previous work, ,,− , we propose various structure–activity relationships (SARs) based on Hammett substituent constants and applications of FMO theory to quantify the carbonyl substituent effect on reactivity with CH2OO. The SARs provide a semiempirical means of estimating rate constants for CH2OO + R1R2CO reactions without explicit applications of reaction rate theories that rely on accurate ab initio characterization of transition states geometries and properties. Precise quantification of thermochemical properties from ab initio calculations requires an accurate treatment of the electronic structure along with careful evaluation of the molecular partition functions that go beyond the rigid rotor harmonic oscillator (RRHO) model, by including hindered rotors or improved treatment of anharmonicity, as well as taking into account tunneling contributions. The convenient, low computational cost predictive SARs developed here can be used to estimate rate constants for reactions of CH2OO with carbonyls, as well as the analogous reactions of larger CIs, which have received far less attention experimentally.

Methods

Electronic structure calculations of the unimolecular reaction intermediates and energies involved were performed with the Gaussian and GAMESS packages. , Initial geometry optimizations were carried out with the B3LYP/cc-pVDZ method and thermodynamic quantities were further refined with the CBS-QB3 compound method, which has been shown to provide reliable thermochemistry involving the main group elements at modest computational cost. Frequency analysis confirmed the transition states and minimum structures. In addition, the energies of the frontier molecular orbitals were obtained from the B3LYP/cc-pVDZ calculations. Previous work has shown the orbital energies calculated at a similar level of theory to provide linear correlation with molecular properties such as ionization potential, electron affinity, and excitation energy. This level of theory also can be reasonably scaled to larger systems.

Results

Overview of Reactions and Substituent Effects

Carbonyls are key oxidation products of hydrocarbons in the atmosphere and also contribute to primary emissions, making their atmospheric fate a subject of significant interest. The dominant removal pathways for most carbonyls are reaction with OH radicals and direct photolysis initiated by the presence of the n-π* transition in the carbonyl chromophore. − In addition, wet and dry deposition contribute for some water-soluble species. Aldehydes possess an abstractable hydrogen atom, making OH oxidation the primary atmospheric sink though photolysis is also significant. Aliphatic ketones such as acetone lack this abstractable hydrogen and are instead removed primarily by photolysis. α,β-Dicarbonyls such as biacetyl and pyruvic acid exhibit enhanced near-UV absorption and are dominated by photolysis. , Enals and enones react readily with OH radicals due to addition across the conjugated CC double bond, making this the dominant atmospheric sink for these species. ,

Reaction with CIs represents an additional potential sink for atmospheric carbonyls. Bimolecular rate constants have been measured for the reactions of the simplest CI (CH2OO) with a range of organic carbonyl species (aldehydes, ,,,,− ,, ketones, ,,,,,, hydroxyketones, dicarbonyls, , enones and enals, , and keto acids , ), denoted in general as R1R2CO. Criegee intermediates react with organic carbonyls via 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions to form cyclic 1,2,4-trioxolanes, commonly referred to as secondary ozonides (SOZs). This pathway is illustrated schematically in Figure for reaction of CH2OO with a representative asymmetric carbonyl, acetaldehyde (R1 = H, R2 = CH3). The reactions proceed by formation of a near coplanar entrance-channel van der Waals complex (vdW) that then passes over the cycloaddition transition state (TS) resulting in SOZ formation. The relative energetics are shown in terms of the changes in the zero-point corrected electronic energies (ΔE) at 0 K and the free energies (ΔG) at 298 K. In terms of ΔE, the vdW complex and the TS are both lower in energy than the separated reactants. However, the entropic contribution to the free energy moves ΔG of both the entrance channel complex and the transition state structure above the reactants. In general, the cycloaddition TS is the highest point on the free energy surface and represents the rate determining step in the overall reaction mechanism. SOZ formation is exothermic by approximately 40–50 kcal mol–1, and the energized product can undergo a range of further isomerization and decomposition reactions. It should be noted that for carbonyls with very low barriers to SOZ formation, the rate can be controlled by so-called “outer” complexes and transition states that occur at larger reactant separations. In the present work, our analysis focuses on the shorter range “inner” vdW complexes and TSs.

Geometries of the entrance channel complexes (vdW), transition states (TSs), and SOZ products for the E and Z pathways described in the text are both shown, although energy differences are small.

Before proceeding, we briefly discuss some stereochemical aspects of the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition mechanism. The CIs that will be discussed belong to the Cs point group and the carbonyl moiety of the coreactant is also planar. Ab initio calculations have shown that, in general, the reactions proceed via near coplanar entrance channel complexes (see Figure ), in which the CI and carbonyl species are separated by ∼2.5 Å. Closing the distance to ∼2.2 Å leads to a reactant-like early TS that is typically lower in energy than the reactants. Reaction can occur act at either face (obverse or reverse) of the CO group. For asymmetric carbonyl compounds (i.e., R1 ≠ R2), the CI can adopt two distinct orientations, which we classify as E and Z according to whether the central O atom of the CI is displaced toward the side of the lower or higher priority carbonyl substituent, respectively. These two conformers are shown in Figure . Substituent priority follows organic chemistry conventions (H < CH3 < CH2OH < CH3O etc.). Reaction at either face of the carbonyl leads to stereoisomeric TSs and SOZs that have identical energies. The E and Z reaction pathways proceed via TSs with slightly different energies and lead to different ring puckering conformers of the SOZ products.

Qualitatively, the substituent effects can be understood from consideration of the frontier molecular orbitals, as originally proposed by Sustmann to describe the symmetry-allowed orbital interactions of Diels–Alder cycloaddition reactions. , The substituents are classified as electron-donating groups (EDGs) and electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs). A molecular orbital (MO) diagram for the reaction between CH2OO and formaldehyde (R1 = R2 = H) is shown in Figure . The orbital interactions are labeled based on loose symmetry considerations. The occupied nonbonding (n pC–pO ) orbital on CH2OO and the unoccupied π* orbital of HCHO are loosely symmetric about a center of inversion, and they interact to form the molecular orbitals labeled S and S*. The antisymmetric orbitals are the occupied π orbital on HCHO and the unoccupied π* orbital of CH2OO, which interact to form the molecular orbitals labeled A and A*. The nonbonding orbitals localized on heteroatoms are neglected. In general, the S-interaction involves a smaller orbital energy gap and is likely to be more significant in controlling reactivity (see Section below).

Molecular orbital diagram for the 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reaction between CH2OO (left) and formaldehyde (right). The “symmetric” orbitals interact to form the molecular orbitals (S, S*) (connected by black solid lines), and the “antisymmetric” orbitals form the molecular orbitals (A, A*) (connected by gray dotted lines). The effects on the orbital energies of the CI or the carbonyl of replacing H atoms with electron-donating groups (EDGs) or electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) are indicated with arrows.

Substituent groups raise or lower the FMO energies of the CI and the carbonyl relative to their values in CH2OO and HCHO. The effect on reactivity can be understood qualitatively by considering the dominant (smaller orbital energy gap) S-interaction. Substituents that are more electron-withdrawing than H lower the frontier molecular orbital energies, while substituents that are more electron-donating raise them. As can be seen in Figure , electron-withdrawing substituents on the carbonyl decrease the S-interaction orbital energy gap, stabilizing the transition state, and increasing reactivity. Electron-withdrawing substituents on the CI serve to increase the S-interaction orbital energy gap, which leads to a decrease in reactivity. Electron-donating substituents have the opposite effects. On the CI they are activating, while on the carbonyl they are deactivating.

Thermodynamic Energy Relationships

The reactivity of 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions can be characterized in terms of thermodynamic energy correlations as discussed by Ess and Houk, who explored the reactivity of a range of 1,3-dipoles including oxides, imines, and ylides. , In that work, it was shown that the Marcus-type relationship for electron transfer reactions holds for some 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions but not others. However, in general, lower TS energies are associated with more exothermic reactions.

Passage over the barrier results in SOZ formation, for which the overall free energy changes range from ΔG SOZ ≈ −25 kcal mol–1 (CH2OO + AcAc) to −45 kcal mol–1 (CH2OO + HFA).

Transition State Theory

A canonical transition state theory (CTST) treatment can be applied to the reaction scheme shown in Figure , involving a two-step model for the overall reaction rate which is written in general as CI+R1R2CO↔KeqvdW R1 vdW→kSOZSOZ R2

If it is assumed that equilibrium is established for vdW complex formation (i.e., vdW complex decomposition is faster than conversion to the SOZ), then the overall rate constant for the reaction can be expressed as k=KeqkSOZ 1 where K eq is the equilibrium constant for vdW complex formation and k SOZ is the rate of passage over the cycloaddition TS. Combining the statistical mechanical expressions for K eq and the TST expression for k SOZ leads to the Eyring equation for the overall temperature-dependent rate constant: k=κkB2hp°T2exp(−ΔGTSRT) 2 where κ is the tunneling coefficient (assumed here to be unity); k B is the Boltzmann constant; T is temperature; h is the Planck constant; R is the gas constant; p° is standard pressure (1 bar); and ΔG TS is the standard free energy of the TS relative to the reactants. The factor k B 2/hp° evaluates to 2.88 × 10–12 cm3 s–1 K–2. Application of CTST to calculate thermal rate constants requires only ΔG TS which is available from the ab initio calculations, or from the relationships shown in Figure , if only ΔG SOZ is available.

All the experimental data, except for the AcAc reaction, lie below the CTST line. Most experimental rate constants are at least an order of magnitude smaller than the CTST prediction. The largest discrepancy is found for the HCHO reaction, arguably the simplest to treat computationally, for which the observed rate constant is 140 times smaller than the CTST prediction. The systematic overprediction of rate constants suggests a systematic underestimation of the CBS-QB3 free energies of activation, ΔG TS. Comparison of CBS-QB3 calculated activation free energies (ΔG TS) with those derived from experimental rate constants gives a consistent average offset of +1.75 kcal mol–1. Application of this uniform correction brings the CTST predictions into good agreement with experimental values, as shown in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information.

With the exponential sensitivity of rate constants to the transition state barrier height, even minor errors in the computed transition state free energy can result in substantial discrepancies in predicted kinetics. To enhance the accuracy of theoretical rate constants, refinement of transition state energies is needed. High-level correlation methods such as CCSD­(T)/CBS and CCSDT­(Q)/CBS offer improved electronic structure accuracy, but are very computationally expensive for larger systems and can still introduce errors of approximately 0.4–1.0 kcal mol–1 in 0 K heats of formation. This degree of accuracy in ΔG TS values produces rate constants that vary by factors of 2–6. Furthermore, a more rigorous (and laborious) treatment of the rotational and vibrational partition functions beyond the conventional rigid rotor-harmonic oscillator (RRHO) approximation such as hindered rotor models may be necessary to improve the reliability of room-temperature rate constant calculations. It is, however, not obvious when such effort is required if computation is to be used to predict a rate constant. As noted above, the largest discrepancy between the TST rate constant calculated using CBS-QB3 free energies is for the comparatively simple and computationally most tractable CH2OO + HCHO reaction. Experimental rate constants for reactions involving larger carbonyl species with methyl or larger alkyl substituents, reactions for which hindered rotor treatments may have been anticipated, are much better reproduced by TST using simple RRHO partition functions, likely due to cancellation of errors.

SARs for CH2OO + R1R2CO Reactions

Obtaining accurate TST rate constants is challenging, particularly as molecular size increases, and high-level ab initio calculations of electronic energies and sophisticated treatments to determine molecular partition functions are required. Here, we describe alternative strategies to estimate CI + R1R2CO reactivity through structure–activity relationships (SARs) that are capable of reliably predicting rate constants without requiring high-level ab initio calculations. The SARs presented in this work are based on empirical correlations with experimental rate constants and do not rely on TST predictions for parametrization. The SARs are based on the model for 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions described by Sustmann, , in which the electron-donating or electron-withdrawing characters of the substituents determine reactivity through their influence on the energies of the frontier molecular orbitals, as illustrated in Figure .

The general form of the SARs is presented in eq : log10kSAR=a+bλ 3 where λ is a quantifiable property that characterizes the electron-donating or withdrawing character of the substituents or their effect on the substituted carbonyl. We focus on two alternative approaches: Hammett constants, which have been tabulated for a broad range of substituents, and the frontier molecular orbital energies, which are readily obtained from low-cost DFT calculations.

The SARs presented here focus on room-temperature rate constants which are further assumed to represent high-pressure limit values. Previous experimental studies have demonstrated that the high-pressure limit for 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition reactions of carbonyls with Criegee intermediates is reached at relatively low pressures (∼20–30 Torr of air) indicating negligible pressure dependence under tropospheric conditions. ,, Temperature-dependent measurements for CH2OO reactions with carbonyls have generally shown weak negative temperature dependences with comparable activation energies (−E a/R ≈ 1000 K). ,, Therefore, the room-temperature SARs described below could be extended to other atmospherically relevant temperatures, although with increased uncertainty.

Hammett Substituent Constants

Hammett constants were originally developed to quantify the impact of meta and para substituents on the pK a of meta and para substituted benzoic acids, although they have found applicability in the study of a broad range of organic reactions. The Hammett relationship is defined in eq : log10(KXKH)=ρσX 4 where K X and K H are acid dissociation constants for the substituted and the unsubstituted species (X = H), respectively. , For aromatic systems, the substituent constants depend on the location of substitution, and are identified as σm and σp. The sensitivity of a particular reaction to the substituent effect is captured by the factor ρ in eq , where ρ = 1 for the benzoic acid dissociation equilibrium. Following the discussion in Hansch et al., the substituent electronic effects can be split into a field/inductive component characterized with the σI constant, and a resonance component, characterized with the σR constant.

While the standard meta and para position Hammett constants (σm and σp) work well in many cases, there are known situations in which further parametrization is required. To a first approximation, negative values indicate an EDG (σ < 0; deactivating on the carbonyl) and positive values indicate an EWG (σ > 0; activating on the carbonyl). However, other substituent effects can also influence reactivity. For instance, steric effects can play a large role, as is the case for ortho substituents in the benzoic acid reaction series and Taft parameters have been developed to describe the steric influence of a given substituent.

In comparing gas- and aqueous-phase acidities of benzoic acid, the ΔΔG(gas) is approximately an order of magnitude larger than the corresponding ΔΔG(aq). However, Hammett substituent constants still perform reasonably well even in describing gas-phase reactions, , and the use of tabulated Hammett constants could provide a method to predict rate constants for CH2OO + R1R2CO reactions and insight into the expected reactivity of a given carbonyl. Equation can be modified by replacing the acid dissociation equilibrium constants with the bimolecular reaction rate constants and rearranged to yield eq log10(kX)=log10(kH)+ρσX 5 which has the same form as the general linear SAR eq .

Taft Analysis

We employed Taft analysis to investigate substituent effects on the measured rate constants for the cycloaddition of CH2OO to carbonyl compounds. Unlike the Hammett approach, Taft analysis explicitly accounts for the influence of steric effects in addition to polar (electron-donating or -withdrawing) contributions. It should be noted that Taft substituent constants are derived from a different reference reaction system (acid- or base-catalyzed ester hydrolysis) than the conventional Hammett parameters. In general, Taft relationships are expressed by eq : log10(kk0)=ρ*σ*+δES 7 where σ* and E S represent the polar and steric substituent constants, respectively, and the coefficients ρ* and δ quantify the relative sensitivities of the reaction series to these electronic and steric contributions. In contrast to traditional Hammett parameters, Taft constants are referenced (k 0) to the methyl group, for which both σ* and E S are defined as zero. In this work, available σ* and E S values were compiled from two sources, , however, Taft parameters were not available for all the carbonyls presented in Table . The specific values adopted here are included in the Supporting Information in Table S2.

Using these substituent constants, we performed a two-parameter least-squares fit and obtained values of ρ* = 0.36 and δ = 0.17. These results indicate that the cycloaddition is more sensitive to polar effects than to steric influences. Using these fitted parameters, we constructed a correlation of log10(k exp) versus ρ*σ* + δE S, as shown in Figure . Overall, a good correlation is obtained for the subset of carbonyl species evaluated here, with an R 2 value of 0.85. Therefore, the SAR derived from the Taft analysis above can be used to predict CH2OO + carbonyl rate constants and provides a better R 2 value than the Hammett approaches described above. However, the applicability of this approach is more limited by availability of σ* and E S values and it lacks some of the generality offered by the other SAR approaches presented.

Frontier Molecular Orbital Theory

Figure illustrates the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) that govern the reactivity of CIs with carbonyls. The FMO energies were computed using routine B3LYP/cc-pVDZ calculations, a method that is both reliable and readily scalable to larger molecular systems. The interaction can be defined in terms of the magnitudes of the energy gaps ΔE A and ΔE S, defined as in eqs and : ΔEA=|E(CI,π*)−E(R1R2CO,π)| 8 ΔES=|E(CI,npC−npO)−E(R1R2CO,π*)| 9

For a given CI, the FMO energies are unchanged and the energy gaps are determined by the identity of the R1 and R2 substituents on the carbonyl. Although both A and S orbital interactions contribute to the energy of the cycloaddition transition state, ΔE S < ΔE A for all of the CH2OO + R1R2CO reactions listed in Table , so reactivity is primarily controlled by ΔE S. Semilog plots of k exp versus ΔE A and ΔE S, evaluated at the B3LYP/cc-pVDZ level, are shown in Figure a,b, respectively, along with unweighted linear fits to the general SAR eq . The values of a and b, along with the associated statistical parameters, are collected in Table .

Semilog plots of k exp for CH2OO + R1R2CO reactions vs frontier molecular orbital energy gaps (a) ΔE A and (b) ΔE S, and (c) the global electrophilicity index, GEI. Unweighted linear fits to the data are also shown with shaded areas representing 1σ confidence intervals.

As expected, log10(k exp) correlates positively with ΔE A and negatively with ΔE S, consistent with the stabilizing effect of smaller ΔE S gaps on the transition-state energy. Larger experimental rate constants are observed for carbonyls bearing electron-withdrawing substituents, which lower the LUMO energy of R1R2CO, thereby decreasing ΔE S and increasing ΔE A (see Figure ). The fits to ΔE S display slightly higher correlation coefficients than those for ΔE A, indicating the symmetric interaction exerts the greater control over the observed reactivity.

A similar approach to quantify the electrophilic character of the carbonyls is the global electrophilicity index (GEI). GEI is particularly useful for comparing the reactivity of electrophiles across a series of molecules in reactions where electron transfer is important, such as cycloaddition reactions. , The GEI is related to the chemical potential (μ) and hardness (η) as defined in eq : GEI=μ22η 10

The chemical potential and hardness of a given R1R2CO molecule can be calculated from the energies of the HOMO (the nonbonding n O orbital) and LUMO (the π* antibonding orbital) molecule using eqs and : μ=(EHOMO+ELUMO)2 11 η=(ELUMO−EHOMO)2 12

Figure c shows a semilog plot of k exp versus GEI, evaluated using B3LYP/cc-pVDZ calculations.

The correlation is again linear, indicating that the GEI provides a useful single-parameter descriptor of reactivity for the CH2OO + R1R2CO reactions. The GEI-based SAR performs slightly better than those based on ΔE A or ΔE S, with larger absolute values of Pearson’s r and higher R 2 coefficients. Overall, correlations using Hammett substituent constants still yield somewhat stronger agreement with experimental rate constants. However, SARs constructed from low-cost DFT calculations such as GEI offer advantages in generality as this approach does not depend on tabulated substituent parameters and can be readily extended to a broader range of molecular structures, as will be discussed in more detail below.

Discussion

CH2OO + Aldehydes, Ketones, and Hydroxyketones

The aldehydes (HCHO, MeCHO, EtCHO, nPrCHO, and iPrCHO) deviate from the SAR predictions, generally reacting faster than predicted from all substituent Hammett substituent constant sums, FMO, and GEI. One possible explanation is steric accessibility: the CO in these aldehydes is more easily approached by CH2OO than in ketones bearing two non-hydrogen substituents. However, even within this aldehyde subset, steric effects do not follow the expected trend. For example, iPrCHO reacts faster than MeCHO despite its bulkier isopropyl group. This is likely due to the reactive geometry involving CH2OO approaching in a planar orientation above or below the carbonyl which minimizes steric hindrance. As a result, the steric effects are less important than the electron-withdrawing or donating effect.

For aliphatic ketones (dMK and MEK), the presence of two electron-donating substituents results in smaller rate constants, consistent with Hammett substituent constant SAR predictions. The ethyl group has a slightly less negative σp than methyl, in agreement with experimental measurements showing MEK reacting faster than dMK. However, the experimental rate constants are slightly lower than the Hammett SAR predictions. The carbonyl π* orbital energies for the two ketones are nearly identical, and orbital energy gaps and GEI SARs predict near equivalent rates.

The introduction of a hydroxyl group in the hydroxyketones (AcOH and 4H2B) makes the substituent moderately more electron withdrawing, leading to an increased rate constant. Both compounds are well described by the SAR approaches. Finally, HFA exhibits the largest measured rate constant of any carbonyl studied, consistent with the presence of two strongly electron-withdrawing −CF3 substituents that substantially lower the LUMO energy and enhance the electrophilic character of the carbonyl center. The experimental rate constant is also in good agreement with both Hammett- and FMO-based predictions.

From an atmospheric perspective, aliphatic aldehydes generally react more rapidly with OH radicals through hydrogen abstraction at the aldehydic position. However, trifluoroacetaldehyde (TFA), as discussed further below, exhibits a slower reaction with OH, but has a comparatively large rate constant for reaction with CH2OO. Consequently, reaction with stabilized CIs may constitute a significant atmospheric removal pathway for TFA, although its presence in the atmosphere has not been established. TFA could possibly be produced from the atmospheric oxidation of fluoroolefins or degradation of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), but its abundance and role in atmospheric chemistry is not well documented. Finally, the aliphatic ketones and hydroxyketones generally exhibit smaller rate constants for reaction with CH2OO, and their atmospheric removal is expected to be dominated by reactions with OH radicals and by direct photolysis as detailed below.

CH2OO + α, β-Unsaturated Carbonyls

Conjugation of the carbonyl leads to pronounced substituent effects that deviate more strongly from the SAR predictions. For MACR and MVK, conjugation with the π-electron-donating vinylic group results in a slower reaction than expected, whereas for BiAc, AcPr, and PyrA, conjugation with π-electron-withdrawing groups accelerates the reaction. In particular, the rates for MACR and MVK are substantially slower than predicted by Hammett correlations or FMO theory and fall below the linear fits. It should be noted, however, that these measurements were conducted at relatively low pressure (∼4 Torr, He) and may not represent the high-pressure limit. , Further experiments to characterize the pressure dependence of these reactions would provide insight into the reactivity of MACR and MVK. The α-dicarbonyl compounds exhibit comparatively large rate constants for reaction with CH2OO, with k > 10–11 cm3 s–1. The atmospheric reactions of CH2OO with α-dicarbonyls are discussed further below with a focus on glyoxal and methylglyoxal.

The test set covers aldehydes, ketones, hydroxycarbonyls, α-dicarbonyls, and enals: Specifically: trifluoroacetaldehyde (CF3CHO, TFA), methyl isopropyl ketone (CH3COCH­(CH3)2, MiPrK), diethylketone ((C2H5)2CO, dEK), glyoxal (CHOCHO, Gly) and methylglyoxal (CH3COCHO, MGly), glycolaldehyde (HOCH2CHO, Glyc), and crotonaldehyde (CH3CHCHCHO, Cro). Collectively, these compounds represent important classes of carbonyls found in the atmosphere that span a wide range of electronic environments and substituent effects.

Trifluoroacetaldehyde (or fluoral, CF3CHO, TFA) is an oxidation product of hydrofluoroolefin refrigerants. TFA reacts with OH with a reported rate constant of ∼6 × 10–13 cm3 s–1. Using the empirical Hammett constants (σX), the OH radical rate constant is more than an order of magnitude smaller than the SAR-predicted rate constant for the corresponding CI reaction (Table ), suggesting that CIs may serve as an important atmospheric sink for TFA. Based on estimated ambient concentrations where [OH] exceeds [CH2OO] by approximately 2 orders of magnitude, the ratio k CI[CH2OO]/k OH[OH] ≈ 0.2 indicates that the reaction with CIs can represent a competitive loss pathway. However, due to the slow reaction with OH, direct photolysis and reaction with HO2 have been estimated to be major loss channels of TFA, as well as wet and dry deposition.

We have also provided estimates for the rate constants for the aliphatic ketones MiPrK and dEK. As expected, these compounds are predicted to exhibit relatively small rate constants on the order of ∼10–13 cm3 s–1. Their low reactivity toward CH2OO suggests that reactions with OH radicals and direct photolysis , will dominate their removal under typical atmospheric conditions.

The α-dicarbonyls are particularly significant because of their abundance as secondary oxidation products of isoprene and aromatic hydrocarbons, as well as their central role in aqueous-phase chemistry and secondary organic aerosol formation. Accurate estimation of their gas-phase reactivity is therefore critical for understanding their atmospheric lifetime and partitioning. , In addition, measurements on BiAc, AcPr, and PyrA demonstrate that α,β-dicarbonyls react rapidly with CH2OO. For Gly, the two carbonyls are equivalent and the rate constant for reaction with CH2OO is estimated to range from ∼13–44 × 10–12 cm3 s–1. In comparison, the measured rate constant for reaction of glyoxal with OH radical is ∼9 × 10–12 cm3 s–1. Given that the tropospheric OH radical concentration is estimated to be approximately 2 orders of magnitude larger than that of CIs, reaction with OH is expected to represent the dominant atmospheric sink. However, reaction with CI could make some contribution to glyoxal loss rates especially under conditions of elevated CI concentrations. The reactivity of MGly is expected to be dominated by attack at the aldehyde carbonyl, which is predicted to be faster than reaction at the ketone carbonyl. Based on the SAR approaches, the rate constant for CI addition at the aldehyde site is estimated to fall in the range of 17–35 × 10–12 cm3 s–1 For comparison, the measured rate constant for OH radical reaction with MGly is ∼13 × 10–12 cm3 s–1. These similar magnitudes suggest that, under conditions of elevated CI concentrations such as regions with high biogenic alkene emissions or during nighttime periods when OH concentrations are lower, reaction with stabilized CIs could represent a non-negligible atmospheric sink for methylglyoxal.

Finally, Table also includes estimates for two additional aldehydes, Glyc and Cro. These aldehydes are predicted to have similar rate constants with values of ∼10–12 cm3 s–1. For comparison, atmospheric glycolaldehyde is primarily removed through OH radical reaction and wet deposition due to its high water solubility. In the gas phase, Glyc reacts with OH radical with an estimated rate constant of ∼1 × 10–11 cm3 s–1 which is approximately an order of magnitude larger than its estimated rate constant for reaction with CH2OO. The major atmospheric sink for crotonaldehyde is reaction with OH radical (k ∼ 2 × 10–11 cm3 s–1) with a minor contribution from direct photolysis. Given these relative rate constants, the reaction of CH2OO with either glycolaldehyde or crotonaldehyde is expected to represent only a minor atmospheric loss process.

Reactions of Substituted CIs

While this work focuses primarily on reactions of formaldehyde oxide (CH2OO) with carbonyls, for which the most extensive kinetic data are available, many other important stabilized Criegee intermediates exist in the atmosphere. However, we can provide valuable perspective in the application of these SAR approaches to the available kinetic data for other SCIs. The major atmospheric source of CIs is ozonolysis of isoprene (CH2C­(CH3)­CHCH2), , which is outlined in Scheme . Ozone addition at either of the two CC bonds in isoprene produces formaldehyde oxide (CH2OO) along with two unsaturated CIs: methylvinylketone oxide (MVKO) and methacrolein oxide (MACRO). − Due to the presence of syn and anti conformers for the asymmetric intermediates, isoprene ozonolysis yields five distinct stabilized Criegee intermediates (SCIs): CH2OO, syn-MVKO, anti-MVKO, syn-MACRO, and anti-MACRO. ,, In addition to these conjugated CIs produced from isoprene, we will discuss the aliphatic CIs acetaldehyde oxide (CH3CHOO) and acetone oxide ((CH3)2COO). For asymmetric CIs where R1 ≠ R2, the syn and anti isomers exhibit different reactivity and unimolecular decomposition kinetics. There has been previous work on substituent effects in the unimolecular pathways and for the reaction with H2O and (H2O)2 with substituted CIs. In contrast, the CI + H2O reaction involves nucleophilic addition of water to the carbonyl oxide. Because of these mechanistic differences, the substituent effects that govern reactivity are expected to differ between the two reaction types.

Expected Accuracy of SARs

The uncertainties reported in Table reflect statistical fitting uncertainties and do not account for potential systematic errors in the underlying computational methods or experimental rate constant measurements. As discussed in Section of the Results, CBS-QB3 systematically underestimates activation free energies by approximately 1.75 kcal mol–1. However, this offset is consistent across the data set, and the SARs are based on relative trends rather than absolute barrier heights. Overall, we estimate the SARs for CH2OO predict rate constants to within a factor of 2–3 for saturated aldehydes and ketones. Larger uncertainties should be expected for carbonyls with competitive reaction pathways, such as α,β-unsaturated carbonyls or carboxylic acids. For substituted CIs, there is limited data available to limited experimental data are available for validation. Propagation of statistical uncertainties indicate accuracy on the order of a factor of 2 (Table ), but these predictions should be considered more tentative pending further experimental validation.

Conclusions

Hammett analysis and FMO theory have been used to construct SARs that are based on correlations with experimental rate constants. The reaction between CH2OO and HCHO was used as a baseline case to explain how substituent effects of EWGs and EDGs on the carbonyl can influence the rate of the reaction. An inverse correlation was found between the π* orbital energy of the carbonyls and the bimolecular rate constants for the reaction with CH2OO. The strong linear correlation provides a SAR to predict rate constants for the reactions of CH2OO with carbonyls. While Hammett constants provide the best correlation, low-cost quantum chemical calculations of orbital energies offer a practical alternative for predicting rate constants, especially for molecules lacking experimental or tabulated substituent parameters. Further exploration of steric effects beyond the Taft analysis presented here could enhance the SARs for carbonyls with bulkier substituents.
