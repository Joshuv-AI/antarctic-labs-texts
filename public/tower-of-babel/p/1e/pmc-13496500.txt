Severe Multisystem Toxicity from Combination Ipilimumab and Nivolumab in Metastatic Melanoma: A Case Report
Authors: Bushra Amer, Sheetal Manerkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer therapy by enhancing antitumor immunity. Despite their remarkable efficacy in malignancies, particularly metastatic melanomas, the use of ICIs have been linked to life-threatening immune-related adverse events (irAEs) affecting several organ systems. Combination therapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab increases both efficacy and the risk of severe irAEs. We report a rare case of metastatic melanoma treated with combination ipilimumab and nivolumab, complicated by multisystem irAEs. The patient developed hepatitis, cholangitis, ocular toxicity, and neurologic progression, highlighting the severity and complexity of immune-mediated toxicities following dual checkpoint blockade. This case underscores the potential for severe, multisystem irAEs with combination ICIs and the need for vigilant monitoring, early recognition, and multidisciplinary management. Understanding the mechanisms and predictors of these toxicities is essential to optimize treatment outcomes while minimizing life-threatening complications.Keywords: ICIs, Ipilimumab and nivolumab, irAEs. combination therapy

Introduction

In recent years, cancer therapy has been transformed by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including monoclonal antibodies targeting CTLA4, PD1, and their ligands PDL1/PDL2, which enhance antitumor immunity and improve clinical outcomes across multiple malignancies.1 However, ICIs are associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs) resulting from immune overactivation and loss of tolerance, which can lead to treatment interruption, organ dysfunction, and life-threatening complications.2,3 CTLA4 and PD1 regulate immune tolerance through distinct mechanisms: CTLA4 primarily limits T-cell activation during early immune responses, while PD1 suppresses effector T-cell function in peripheral tissues and the tumor microenvironment 4–14. These differences have enabled both monotherapy and combination strategies. Since the approval of ipilimumab in 2011,9,15 multiple ICIs targeting CTLA4, PD1, and PDL1 have been approved and widely adopted.1 Combination regimens, such as ipilimumab plus nivolumab, demonstrate superior efficacy over monotherapy in several cancers 16–18 but are associated with increased frequency and severity of irAEs. This report describes a rare and severe case of metastatic melanoma treated with combined ipilimumab and nivolumab, complicated by multisystem irAEs, including hepatitis, cholangitis, ocular toxicity, and neurologic involvement, highlighting the complexity and potential fatality of immune-mediated toxicity with dual checkpoint blockade.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old female with past medical history of prediabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, depression, and generalized anxiety disorder, presented with a palpable scalp nodule that she had noticed for several months. On the right crown of her scalp, there was a 5-mm diameter scaly red-to-purple skin lesion. It was non-fluctuant, with no surrounding erythema. A shave biopsy revealed T2 ulcerated melanoma (stage IIA, pT2b) without lymphovascular invasion. Two and a half months later, she had a wide excision with skin graft and a sentinel lymph node biopsy, which revealed no residual melanoma. BRAF mutation testing was not performed this time, as it is not routinely recommended for patients with Stage IIA melanoma according to current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines and was therefore not indicated in this case. Eighteen months after surgery, a follow-up PET-CT scan revealed a solitary FDG-avid lymph node in the left neck, and fine-needle aspiration confirmed melanoma recurrence. A subsequent brain MRI showed no evidence of metastasis. She started combination immunotherapy with ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) and nivolumab (anti-PD-1), with four planned cycles every three weeks, followed by maintenance nivolumab. After the second cycle of therapy, the patient developed grade 1 fatigue and night sweats, grade 2 pruritus, and grade 3 diarrhea according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Infectious workups, including a Gastrointestinal Pathogen (GI) panel and Clostridioides difficile testing, were negative, and the patient had no recent antibiotic use. No radiologic or clinical evidence suggested disease progression involving the gastrointestinal tract. Laboratory evaluation showed a morning serum cortisol of 16 μg/dL (reference range 5–25 μg/dL), Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) of 0.72 μIU/mL (reference range 0.35–4.94 μIU/mL), and normal hemoglobin and white blood cell counts, arguing against adrenal insufficiency, thyroid dysfunction, infection, or hematologic causes of fatigue. The timing of symptom onset following initiation of combination immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, along with the absence of alternative etiologies, supports the diagnosis of immune-related adverse events from immune checkpoint inhibition. Despite these side effects, the neck lymph node demonstrated substantial regression, indicating a favorable treatment response. She was started on prednisone for colitis and rash, and immunotherapy was temporarily held. Two weeks later, she was admitted to the hospital for worsening skin rash characterized as CTCAE Grade 3 erythroderma involving the chest, back, face, abdomen, and upper extremities and a right lower lobe pulmonary embolism (PE). CT imaging confirmed the PE, and the rash worsened following a reduction in prednisone from 60 mg to 40 mg daily. Management included a heparin drip for anticoagulation and Solu-Medrol 40 mg IV twice daily for the rash. Skin biopsy of the rash revealed a drug-induced urticarial hypersensitivity reaction. She was discharged after two days on prednisone 60 mg daily for two weeks, with a planned taper to 40 mg daily thereafter, to be continued for at least two additional weeks. During a follow-up visit to hematology/oncology, the patient demonstrated a dramatic rise in liver enzymes, consistent with autoimmune hepatitis. She was re-admitted and started on IV Solu-Medrol 80 mg for four days followed by Prednisone 100 mg daily, along with mycophenolate 500 mg twice daily. The dose subsequently increased to 1 g twice daily. Five days later, although her liver enzymes improved, her bilirubin continued to rise, prompting initiation of Jakafi (ruxolitinib). On evaluation, an ultrasound revealed gallbladder wall thickening, and a HIDA scan demonstrated poor uptake of radiotracer throughout the liver, consistent with hepatocellular dysfunction (Figs. 1–2).

A liver biopsy confirmed acute cholestatic hepatitis secondary to immune checkpoint inhibitors, showing a mixed hepatocellular and cholestatic pattern with bile duct injury. A repeat liver biopsy was performed to rule out necrosis, revealing extensive damage with ischemic-type changes and cholestasis, confirming grade 4 immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced hepatitis and cholangitis. She was discharged under hematology/oncology follow-up and Prednisone 40 mg daily, but was readmitted 10 days later with generalized weakness, dizziness, and a mechanical fall. During this hospitalization, she developed prerenal acute kidney injury, which improved with IV fluids, and severe conjunctival redness, suggestive of scleritis or episcleritis, potential adverse effects of immunotherapy. On neurological examination, there were no signs of myasthenia gravis, including ptosis, diplopia, or fatigable muscle weakness. There were also no clinical features suggestive of myositis or myocarditis. Echocardiography demonstrated a normal ejection fraction of 55–60% without valvular abnormalities. These findings argue against immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated Triple M syndrome. Prednisone was increased to 60 mg daily, and prednisolone eye drops were initiated three times daily. Ophthalmology evaluation revealed posterior vitritis with debris and multiple floaters, suggesting endogenous endophthalmitis. A differential diagnosis of toxoplasmosis or fungal infection was considered, and empirical treatment with micafungin 100 mg IV daily was initiated. Vitreous sampling for microbiological and pathological evaluation was planned but was canceled due to clinical deterioration. During hospitalization, she developed left upper extremity weakness. CT imaging demonstrated slight hypoattenuation in the right precentral gyrus, concerning early ischemia. Subsequent brain MRI revealed a 1.9 cm solid rim-enhancing lesion in the right posterior frontal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema, highly suggestive of metastasis. Additional smaller rim-enhancing foci were noted in the bilateral posterior temporal lobes, raising further concern for metastatic disease. At this time of disease, BRAF mutation testing was not obtained, as the patient experienced significant treatment-related toxicity and metastasis and was deemed clinically unfit to receive further systemic therapy. Given the deep location of the lesions and her overall prognosis, neurosurgical intervention was deemed unlikely to provide benefit. She was started on dexamethasone for brain edema, and neurosurgery confirmed that no intervention would improve her condition. With the progression of the underlying metastatic melanoma, severe liver toxicity, and ocular complications, her prognosis became poor. She was referred to hospice care for comfort measures, with code status updated to “comfort measures only,” and she peacefully passed away shortly thereafter.

Discussion

The presented case illustrates a broad spectrum of immunotherapy-related adverse effects, both typical and atypical. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) can affect nearly every organ system, with variable timing of onset.19 While several of the patient's immune-related adverse events (irAEs) are individually uncommon, their simultaneous occurrence is particularly rare. Although the precise mechanisms underlying irAEs remain unclear, they are thought to result from nonspecific T-cell overactivation and increased cytokine expression, leading to aberrant autoreactive immune responses or sepsis-like syndromes.20 Ipilimumab and nivolumab are administered as first-line therapy for metastatic melanoma due to their demonstrated improvement in overall and progression-free survival; however, this combination is associated with a higher risk of irAEs, as observed in this case.21 Mild to moderate (grade 1–2) irAEs are typically managed with temporary treatment interruption and corticosteroids, whereas severe or life-threatening (grade 3–4) irAEs often require high-dose systemic corticosteroids and permanent discontinuation of immunotherapy.22 Accurate diagnosis of irAEs, a diagnosis of exclusion, requires comprehensive evaluation and multidisciplinary involvement. 22 Our patient developed multiple grade 4 irAEs after only two cycles of combination therapy, resulting in life-threatening multi-organ failure and eventual transition to hospice care. Reported toxicities included severe dermatologic reactions, fatigue, diarrhea, polyuria, pulmonary embolism, grade 4 hepatitis and cholangitis, ocular toxicity, and neurologic deterioration. Dermatologic toxicity, one of the most common irAEs, was prominent in this case.23 Diarrhea occurs in 27–54% of patients receiving ICIs, with colitis reported in 8–22%.24 Hepatotoxicity has been reported in 3–9% of patients receiving ipilimumab and 1–2% receiving nivolumab.25 Hypothyroidism occurs in approximately 6.6% of patients receiving monotherapy and 13.2% of those receiving combination therapy.26 Serious neurologic irAEs remain rare, with an incidence of approximately 0.93% in patients with melanoma treated with nivolumab alone or in combination.20 Identifying reliable predictors of multisystem irAEs in patients receiving combination checkpoint blockade remains challenging. Limited studies suggest associations with male sex, absolute lymphocyte count, and neutrophil-tolymphocyte ratio.27,28 Further research is needed to improve risk stratification and optimize the safety of ICI therapy.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the potentially the life-threatening nature of widespread immune-related adverse events following combination therapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab in metastatic melanoma. Despite their remarkable antitumor efficacy, dual checkpoint blockade can rapidly trigger severe toxicities involving multiple organ systems. Early recognition, vigilant monitoring, and prompt multidisciplinary management are critical to mitigate risks. Further research is urgently needed to identify predictive biomarkers, optimize treatment strategies, and improve the safety profile of combination immunotherapy while maintaining therapeutic benefit.
