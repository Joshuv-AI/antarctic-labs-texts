Graft Dimensions and Residual Tissue Thickness Impact on Donor Site Healing After FGG Procedure: A 3D Comparative Study
Authors: Ning Wei, Ziyao Han, Wenjie Hu, Yiping Wei, Haoyun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsFree gingival grafting (FGG) is a common procedure in periodontal and implant therapy to augment keratinized tissue and improve esthetic outcomes. However, the effects of graft dimensions and residual tissue thickness (RTT1) on palatal donor site healing remain poorly characterized, lacking standardized 3-dimensional analysis. This study aimed to quantify how these factors influence 3-dimensional tissue recovery over 6 months, with thickness recovery rate as the primary outcome.MethodsThirty-two FGG patients were divided into single-tooth (ST2) and multi-teeth (MT3) groups. Intraoral scans and CBCT quantified donor site thickness and volume changes at 1, 3, and 6 months post-surgery. Statistical analyses (t-tests, ANOVA) assessed graft size and RTT effects.ResultsAt 1 month, the ST group showed significantly faster healing than the MT group, with higher thickness (91.01% vs 86.46%, P = .005) and volume recovery rates (73.19% vs 64.23%, P = .009). No intergroup differences emerged at 3 or 6 months. RTT≥2 mm consistently improved outcomes across all time points (P < .001), while RTT<2 mm required prolonged healing.ConclusionGraft size affects early-phase healing but not long-term outcomes. Preserving ≥2 mm RTT is critical for optimal recovery and re-harvesting potential, guiding graft dimension selection in FGG.Clinical RelevanceUnderstanding the impact of graft size and residual tissue thickness (RTT) on palatal donor site healing can provide valuable guidance for clinical decision-making in free gingival graft surgery. Smaller grafts facilitate quicker initial recovery, while maintaining RTT≥2 mm is essential for optimizing long-term healing. These insights can improve surgical planning, postoperative care, and re-harvesting feasibility, leading to better patient outcomes.

Introduction

Autogenous soft tissue grafting is a commonly performed procedure in periodontal and implant surgery.1,2 Gingival grafts harvested with intact epithelium can be applied directly as free gingival grafts (FGG) or modified into subepithelial connective tissue grafts through de-epithelialization, performed either intraorally or extraorally.1,3, 4, 5 The palatal mucosa remains the preferred donor site due to its accessibility and capacity to provide tissue with favorable healing characteristics.6,7 The donor site in this method undergoes secondary intention healing, which may lead to postoperative bleeding, pain, and discomfort, as well as thinning of the donor site tissue after healing.2 The healing of the palatal donor site after FGG harvesting plays a critical role in determining patient-reported postoperative outcomes and future re-harvesting feasibility.3,6,8

Existing studies suggest that the dimensions of the harvested graft, particularly its size and thickness, may influence donor site healing.9,10 Larger grafts are often associated with greater tissue loss and delayed epithelialization, while smaller grafts may promote faster healing and reduced morbidity.10, 11, 12 However, these observations are primarily based on clinical experience and isolated cases rather than comparative studies. Moreover, there is a lack of controlled investigations to evaluate the impact of graft size on volume and thickness changes during healing.13, 14, 15 Addressing this question through a comparative study is critical for optimizing graft dimensions and improving clinical outcomes.11,16,17

Residual tissue thickness (RTT) is another factor reported to influence donor site healing.16 Studies suggest that maintaining at least 2 mm of RTT could reduce postoperative complications and improve healing outcomes.9,18 Yet, its exact impact relative to graft dimensions remains unclear.16 Additionally, the interaction between RTT and graft dimensions, as well as their combined effects on volume and thickness recovery, has not been systematically studied.11,13 Recent advancements in digital imaging technologies, including intraoral scanning and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), offer crucial tools for accurately assessing these alterations through noninvasive 3-dimensional analysis.19, 20, 21, 22 These methods provide accurate, reproducible data on tissue healing, enabling clinicians to better understand the dynamics of 3-dimensional volume and thickness changes.11,16,23, 24, 25

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of graft dimensions and RTT on the healing dynamics of palatal donor sites, focusing on 3-dimensional volume and thickness changes over a 6-month period. The primary outcome was the thickness recovery rate. The null hypothesis was that graft dimensions and RTT would not significantly influence donor site healing outcomes.

Material and methods

Study design and center

This study was in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration revised in 2013 and the STROBE guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology (Approval Number: PKUSSIRB-202058143; Chinese Clinical Trial Registry Identifier: ChiCTR-2200064979). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before enrollment. Participants were divided into 2 groups based on the pre-surgical design for the required size of the FGG: the ST group (grafts used for a single-tooth area) and the MT group (grafts used for areas involving multiple teeth). No participants were lost to follow-up. A flowchart (Supplementary Figure S1) has been included to illustrate the flow of participants throughout the study.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined based on a power calculation, with a type I error level (α) of 0.05 and a type II error level (β) of 0.10 (90% power). Due to limited prior evidence on the effects of graft dimensions,16 the only available study that reported postoperative thickness difference between groups with RTT<2 mm and RTT≥2 mm. Based on their 1-month data, an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 1.18 was calculated. Given that thickness recovery rate is the primary outcome of this study and RTT was considered 1 of the potential influencing factors, this estimation is deemed appropriate. Accordingly, the minimum sample size required for each group was calculated to be 16 participants, resulting in a total sample size of 32 participants for the study.

Eligibility criteria

All of the participants received oral hygiene instructions, and initial periodontal therapy was performed to establish optimal plaque control and gingival health conditions. Furthermore, the following selection criteria were applied: (1) systemically healthy individuals classified as ASA I or II; (2) good periodontal health with full-mouth plaque scores≤15% and bleeding scores<10%; (3) no active periodontal disease and pocket depths ≤3 mm; (4) candidates requiring a FGG harvested from posterior palatal mucosa; (5) maintenance of good oral hygiene and adherence to regular follow-up visits every 6 months; and (6) adequate palatal mucosa thickness (defined as ≥2 mm before graft harvesting).

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) smoking more than 10 cigarettes per day or a history of quitting smoking within the past 6 months; (2) systemic conditions impairing wound healing, such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (HbA1c>7.0), liver or kidney dysfunction, or chronic steroid use; (3) history of bisphosphonate use or head and neck radiotherapy; (4) pregnant or lactating individuals; (5) known allergies to materials used during the procedure (e.g., suture materials or anesthetics); (6) palatal pathology, including soft tissue lesions or insufficient palatal mucosa for harvesting; (7) wearing removable dentures or recent history of orthodontic treatment influencing palatal anatomy; and (8) incomplete medical or dental records or unwillingness to provide informed consent.

Clinical procedures

All the FGG procedures were performed by an experienced periodontist (W.H.) using a standardized partial-full-partial thickness flap technique, as detailed in previous studies.5,24 Under local anesthesia, the graft was harvested from the palate between the maxillary second premolar and first molar using a #15 scalpel blade. The harvested graft measured 1.0-1.5 mm in thickness and approximately 6-8 mm in width. To better fit the anatomical shape of the recipient area, the graft was designed in a special semilunar shape, with the length tailored to match the dimensions of the recipient site. The harvested graft was then carefully adapted and fixed to the prepared recipient area with interrupted and cross-mattress sutures.

After harvesting, the donor site was compressed to achieve hemostasis, and an intraoral scanner was used to capture immediate post-surgical images of the palatal donor sites. An individualized soft splint is applied to protect the donor site and secure the blood clot. The splint was worn continuously for 1 week and only removed during meals and tooth brushing for 2 weeks until the sutures were removed. Written and oral postoperative instructions were provided to all participants. Postoperative follow-up was scheduled from 1 to 6 months after surgery to assess the healing conditions of the donor sites.

Digital data acquisition

The CBCT scans of the maxilla were taken within 3 months after FGG (New Tom 9000; Aperio Services). All scans were taken at 110 kV, 12-17 mA, with 0.1-0.3 mm slice thickness and a pixel size of 0.125 mm.22 Standard tessellation language (STL) files of the maxilla were obtained using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3; 3Shape A/S). Intraoral scanning was performed at the following time points: preoperatively (baseline), immediately after surgery, and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively.

The STL files captured the dimensions and surface characteristics of the donor sites, as well as the corresponding teeth in 1-half of the maxillary dental arch to facilitate alignment and superimposition. These digital models enable precise assessment of 3-dimensional volume changes, tissue healing, and dimensional stability at the donor site over time. Consistent scanning protocols were applied to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of data across all time points.

Digital imaging assessment and variables of interest

To evaluate the healing dynamics of the donor site, preoperative intraoral scans and CBCT images were first aligned using dental arch features to ensure precise registration. The registration process involved the following steps: (1) STL files from intraoral scans were imported into a software program (Geomagic Wrap 2021; Geomagic Inc). A 2-step alignment process, best-fit alignment and fine alignment, was performed based on adjacent teeth for precise assessment of tissue changes.23,24; (2) aligned STL files and DICOM files from CBCT were imported into another software program (Mimics 21.0; Materialise). (3) anatomical landmarks, such as cusp tips and incisal edges of teeth within 1-half of the maxillary arch, were identified and applied to perform an initial alignment; (4) thresholding was used to obtain the 3D mask models under the applicable thresholds of the crown, followed by fine alignment through local registration to achieve optimal superimposition; and (5) registration accuracy was verified by visual inspection of overlay consistency and quantitative assessment of mean registration errors, ensuring alignment errors did not exceed 0.2 mm. After registration, the immediate postoperative scan was used to define a cross-sectional view through the center of the donor site in a coronal-apical direction. Thickness was measured at all postoperative time points based on the same standardized coronal-apical cross-section, defined through the center of the donor site. This cross-section allowed measurement of the following parameters: initial mucosal thickness (preoperative), RTT immediately post-surgery, FGG graft thickness, and mucosal thickness at the donor site across all postoperative time points. These values were recorded from a consistent central reference point (center of the donor site). The mucosal thickness recovery rate at each postoperative time point was calculated as:thicknessrecoveryrate(%)=postoperativetissuethickness/initialtissuethickness×100.

Subsequently, intraoral scans (preoperative, immediately after surgery, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months) were imported into Geomagic Wrap software for 3-dimensional volume analysis. Using the preoperative intraoral scan as a reference, 3-dimensional volume changes within the donor site were assessed. The software’s "3D compare" function was utilized to calculate the volume reductions and create color-coded 3D maps that visualized differences between preoperative and postoperative scans. These analyses allowed for precise measurement of 3-dimensional volume changes in the region of interest (ROI) and facilitated the calculation of donor site volume recovery rates. The volume recovery rate at each postoperative time point was calculated as:volumerecoveryrate(%)=volumerecovery/volumereductionafterharvesting×100.

These assessments provided detailed quantitative data on soft tissue healing dynamics, including both linear (thickness) and 3-dimensional volume alterations at the donor site, over the 6-month postoperative period.

Measurement reliability and examiner calibration

Each measurement was performed in triplicate by a calibrated examiner (N.W.). Before the analysis, an intra-examiner calibration was conducted to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the measurements using the described methods. Calibration involved repeated measurements of 10 randomly selected donor sites, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate reliability. The ICC values ranged from 0.84 to 1, indicating good reliability of the data collected across all time points.25

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome variable in this study was the thickness recovery rate. Volume recovery rate and palatal tissue thickness were secondary outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using a statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, v22; IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and percentage were used to summarize the data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

For comparisons of tissue thickness and volume recovery rates across different time points, Repeated Measures ANOVA or Friedman tests were applied, depending on the data distribution. Differences between 2 groups were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Two-way ANOVA was used to compare recovery rates among different subgroups of graft size and RTT.

The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. All statistical tests were 2-tailed.

Significant differences were not observed in the distribution of age, gender, ITT, graft thickness and RTT between the ST and MT groups (P > .05). The graft length in the MT group was significantly higher than ST group (18.98 ± 1.05 mm versus 10.88 ± 0.88 mm, P < .05).

In all cases, the residual tissue thickness (RTT) immediately after surgery ranged from 1.73 mm to 3.63 mm (average 2.60 mm). The mean mucosal thickness increased progressively over time across all donor sites. At 1 month postoperatively, the mucosal thickness averaged 3.19 ± 0.47 mm, presenting a recovery rate of 65.01 ± 11.96%. By 3 months, the average mucosal thickness reached 3.47 ± 0.50 mm, with a recovery rate of 83.08 ± 10.17%. At 6 months, the mucosal thickness stabilized at 3.68 ± 0.51 mm, achieving a recovery rate of 92.38 ± 6.14%. The volume recovery rates within the ROI also demonstrated a consistent increase over time. At 1 month postoperatively, the mean volume recovery rate was 64.02 ± 10.22%. By 3 months, the volume recovery had improved to 83.19 ± 8.55%. At 6 months, the donor sites exhibited a nearly complete recovery, with an average volume recovery rate of 92.08 ± 5.12%.

The volume recovery rate of both groups showed significant differences over time (P < .01). Between the 2 groups, there was no significant difference in palatal tissue thickness at any time point (P > .05). The thickness recovery rate was significantly higher in the ST group at 1 month (P < .01), but no significant differences were observed at 3 months or 6 months (P > .05). Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of graft dimensions and RTT on the healing dynamics of palatal donor sites over a 6-month period. Using 3-dimensional digital imaging, we quantitatively assessed 3-dimensional volume and thickness changes, providing insights into the optimal management of donor sites. The findings emphasize the significant roles of both graft size and RTT in influencing healing patterns and offer valuable guidance for surgical planning.

The results revealed that the recovery rates for thickness and volume were significantly higher in the ST group compared with the MT group at 1 month, highlighting the delayed healing process after larger graft harvesting during the early phase of healing. These observations are consistent with previous reports indicating that smaller grafts promote faster epithelialization and better donor site healing during the initial postoperative phase.14 However, no significant differences were observed between both groups at 3 and 6 months, suggesting that graft size mainly affects short-term wound healing, with minimal impact on long-term healing outcomes. This aligns with the notion that donor site healing progresses predominantly through secondary intention, with epithelial cells migrating from the periphery to the central wound area.13 The slower resolution of inflammation in the central region of larger grafts may account for their delayed early recovery rates.4

RTT emerged as a critical factor influencing recovery dynamics, with sites maintaining an RTT≥2 mm exhibiting superior outcomes in thickness and volume recovery at all time points. This finding supports prior suggestions to preserve as much residual tissue thickness as possible during harvesting, while ensuring sufficient graft thickness, to enhance healing efficiency and reduce complications.16 Notably, the effect of RTT was independent of graft size, emphasizing its universal importance regardless of the dimensions of the harvested graft.16 The role of RTT in donor site management is further underscored by its impact on the feasibility of re-harvesting. The results suggest that a recovery period of at least 3 to 6 months is necessary for donor sites to regain sufficient volume for subsequent graft harvesting.4 Sites with RTT<2 mm may require even longer periods, up to 6 months or beyond, to achieve comparable recovery.11,14

The observed healing patterns are consistent with the 3-dimensional volume changes reported in previous studies.12,14,15,17 Palatal donor sites regained most of their presurgical volume by the 6-month mark, with significant recovery occurring within the first 3 months. These findings indicate the dynamic nature of donor site healing and the importance of early postoperative care, particularly for larger wounds.14 Three-dimensional imaging provided a non-invasive and reproducible method for assessing these changes, offering a more feasible and relatively reliable approach for clinical evaluation of soft tissue healing.13,22,24 Additionally, the soft tissue recovery rates between 1 and 3 months in this study were slightly higher than those reported in previous research.11,13,16 This discrepancy could be partly attributed to the adoption of digital measurement methods.13 More importantly, the mean graft thickness harvested in this study was 1.04 mm (ranging from 0.72 to 1.38 mm), slightly thinner than those in earlier studies,16 which might have facilitated a faster healing process. Additionally, the special semilunar shape of the graft and the harvesting technique used in this study resulted in thinner edges and a thicker center, which may have further contributed to the faster postoperative healing.14 Furthermore, the use of an individualized soft splint to protect the palatal wound and personalized postoperative instructions may have also played a role in improving healing outcomes.3,4,12

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. The sample size, while sufficient for subgroup comparisons, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger cohorts are required to validate these results and explore additional variables. Furthermore, the 6-month follow-up period, although adequate for observing major recovery trends, does not capture long-term tissue stability or the full timeline for re-harvesting feasibility. Extended follow-ups are suggested to evaluate tissue maturation and stability beyond 12 months.

Conclusions

This prospective cohort study highlights the significant impact of graft size and RTT on palatal donor site healing dynamics. While larger grafts harvesting impose greater demands on early recovery within the first month, both sizes of grafts harvesting achieve comparable long-term outcomes in 6 months. Preserving RTT≥2 mm is critical for optimizing healing efficiency and supporting re-harvesting feasibility. These findings provide evidence-based suggestions for optimizing graft dimensions and preserving residual tissue on donor sites, emphasizing the importance of meticulous donor site management during FGG procedures to minimize postoperative morbidity and enhance patient outcomes.
