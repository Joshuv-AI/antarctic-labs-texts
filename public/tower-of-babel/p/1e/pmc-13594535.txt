Skin Dose and Toxicity in Patients With Epidermolysis Bullosa Undergoing Radiation Therapy
Authors: Emily MacDuffie, Jenna Rocchetti, Yushi Chang, Erin Davis, Leah Cohen, Karishma Khullar, James Metz
Published: 2026

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) is a rare genetic disorder in which collagen mutations disrupt the dermal-epidermal junction, resulting in cutaneous and gastrointestinal ulcerations, nonhealing wounds following minor trauma, and increased cancer risk.1,2 Because of its rarity, treatment modifications for EB patients requiring cancer treatment are not well established, particularly regarding radiation therapy (RT), where direct skin irradiation raises toxicity concerns.

The EB subtypes simplex (EBS), junctional, and dystrophic (DEB) reflect the mutated epidermal level, with severity described as localized, intermediate, or severe.1, 2, 3 Clinical manifestations vary widely, ranging from minimal symptoms to early lethality in infancy due to infection from skin barrier dysfunction. EBS generally presents with milder disease, while junctional epidermolysis bullosa and DEB typically reflect more symptomatic variants.3

Chronic inflammation and constant wound healing place patients with EB at elevated cancer risk. Although localized or intermediate EBS confers only a slightly increased malignancy risk,2 more severe forms are strongly associated with cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), with an estimated incidence of 90.1% by age 55.1 Across EB subtypes, cSCC typically presents between ages 33 and 36.1

Despite RT’s critical role in cancer care, its use in patients with EB is often underutilized because of the concerns of excessive skin toxicity. Prior small published series reported mixed outcomes, with limited consensus on RT dose thresholds or planning considerations.2 Given the lack of guidance on radiation use and dose, this case series retrospectively identifies patients with EB treated with RT to quantify skin dose delivery, characterize subsequent treatment toxicities, and compare skin dose delivery with different RT modalities.

Case Series

Patients with documented EB treated with RT between 2008 and 2022 were identified through International Classification of Diseases coding at a large institution, and consent for publication was obtained. Clinical and radiation plan information was extracted from the electronic medical record (Table 1). Radiation toxicities were graded using National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.4 Skin dose was calculated in the treatment planning software. Case 1

A 23-year-old patient presented with medulloblastoma and localized EBS with mild blister formation with moderate trauma. He received definitive 3-dimensional (3D) RT to the craniospinal axis (36 Gy in 20 fractions) with a spine boost (45 Gy in 25 fractions) and posterior fossa boost (55.8 Gy in 31 fractions) with concurrent vincristine. Maximum and mean skin doses were 50.4 Gy and 6.6 Gy, respectively, and total volume of skin irradiated was 4696.2 cm3. Skin toxicity included dry dermatitis and erythema at mask points (forehead, chest, and back) requiring application of Domeboro solution (grade 2). Other treatment-related toxicities included dysphagia (grade 2) and nausea (grade 1).

Case 2

A 62-year-old patient presented with metastatic cSCC and intermediate autosomal recessive DEB with active disease consisting of chronic extremity wounds with minimal trauma, esophageal ulcers, and pseudosyndactyly. She received palliative 3D RT to the acetabulum and ribs (8 Gy delivered in 1 fraction). Maximum and mean skin doses were 7.7 Gy and 0.4 Gy, respectively, and total volume of skin irradiated was 4155.9 cm3. No skin toxicity was reported in the RT field prior to or after treatment, despite active disease noted elsewhere on the skin during treatment. No other treatment-related toxicities were reported.

Case 3

A 61-year-old patient presented with pancreatic cancer and intermediate autosomal dominant DEB with intermittent active disease consisting of blister formation with mild trauma and bowel complications, eg, esophageal blisters secondary to nasogastric tube use and recurrent bowel obstruction. Definitive volumetric modulated arc therapy to the abdomen consisted of 52.5 Gy delivered in 25 fractions with concurrent nab-paclitaxel. Maximum and mean skin doses were 16.9 Gy and 1 Gy, respectively, and total volume of skin irradiated was 2331.9 cm3. No skin toxicity was reported in the RT field. Other treatment-related toxicities included nausea (grade 2) and constipation (grade 1).

Case 4

A 46-year-old patient presented with locally recurrent rectal cancer and localized EBS with mild-to-moderate blister formation with moderate trauma. Initial treatment included surgery and chemotherapy alone. Adjuvant intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) to the pelvis consisted of 50.4 Gy delivered in 28 fractions with concurrent cetuximab and capecitabine. Maximum and mean doses were 29.2 Gy and 4 Gy, respectively, and total volume of skin irradiated was 1495.8 cm3. Skin toxicity included dry dermatitis, erythema, and a small skin tear in the gluteal cleft requiring Domeboro solution application (grade 2). Other treatment-related toxicities included nausea (grade 2), abdominal pain (grade 1), and diarrhea (grade 1).

Discussion

EB is a rare cutaneous disorder with no clear guidelines to inform RT planning. Review of 4 patients treated at a large institution revealed heterogeneity in EB subtypes, primary malignancy type, treatment sites, and RT doses. Despite this, none experienced greater than expected cutaneous toxicities. Maximum and mean skin doses approaching 50 Gy and 7 Gy, respectively, appeared tolerable even in mild-to-moderate EB. Dose analysis suggests 3D or IMRT plans may be preferable to proton plans in reducing high-dose skin exposure, despite exposure of more total skin area to the RT field.

There may be hesitation by physicians to use radiation in patients with EB even when RT is included in the standard treatment paradigm given the lack of knowledge regarding toxicities. In case 4, the initial treatment plan omitted RT due to skin toxicity concerns; however, a local recurrence at 16 months necessitated RT, which was ultimately well tolerated. Similar findings of deferring otherwise appropriate RT have been reported.5,6 A 1997 review7 of 11 DEB patients with advanced cSCC receiving 12 to 60 Gy found that those receiving >45 Gy developed moist skin desquamation and delayed wound healing, suggesting a skin tolerance <45 Gy. In contrast, patients in our series tolerated higher skin doses without unexpected toxicity. However, historical treatments used cobalt, limiting the ability to modulate dose. Nevertheless, this account of elevated skin sensitivity in patients with EB receiving RT was the first to suggest a narrower therapeutic index and the need for cautious evaluation of the marginal benefit of RT in this patient population.7

More modern reviews suggest that RT may be safe in EB without unacceptably higher levels of RT-related EB exacerbation.8 A systematic review including 19 patients with cSCC who received RT reported doses ranging from 12 to 72 Gy (median, 50.4 Gy).9 Few of the cases documented toxicities; however, of those reported, 5 patients developed grade 1 skin toxicity, 1 developed grade 2 skin toxicity, and 2 developed grade 3 skin toxicity. Despite limited dosimetric data and inclusion of mostly severe EB cases, the findings support our outcomes that RT is safer for skin in the modern era of volumetric and intensity-modulated treatment planning.

A detailed case report of a patient with severe recessive DEB with metastatic inguinal cSCC described skin-sparing techniques, including using an avoidance structure of 5 mm of the skin surface 3D conformal RT planning and mixed photon energies to deliver 48 Gy in 25 fractions.10 Acute toxicity was mild (grade 1 in-field erythema and alopecia, no desquamation or bullae). He remained clinically disease-free at 41 months.10 This case represents one of the few publications describing dosimetric data comparable with this case series and also suggests that the therapeutic index of RT in EB patients may be wider with modern techniques that spare total skin dose.

Cases with noncutaneous treatment targets also report good tolerability. A 7-year-old boy with primitive neuroectodermal tumor and symptomatic EBS received craniospinal irradiation and focal brain RT (23.4 Gy in 13 fractions and 32.4 Gy in 18 fractions, respectively) with tolerable grade 2 skin toxicity.11 An 81-year-old patient with EB with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma tolerated RT with grade 2 skin toxicity and moderate dysphagia occurring toward the end of the fifth week treatment.12 The authors noted that the patient’s RT course was tolerated similarly well compared with patients without EB when special consideration was given to reducing the cutaneous dose prescribed.12 Only a single noncutaneous case report identified severe toxicity at a low radiation dose in a 42-year-old with advanced oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma and EBS who experienced early treatment termination due to grade 3 mucositis and grade 4 skin toxicity after 30 Gy in 15 fractions via IMRT, although poor baseline performance status likely contributed to his uncharacteristically severe toxicities.13 Overall, consistent with our case series, patients with EB can be safely treated with modern RT techniques without significantly elevated risk of cutaneous side effects.

Conclusion

Given the benefits of appropriate oncologic management and available evidence suggesting relative RT tolerability in patients with EB, RT should not be deferred solely because of the concerns of skin toxicity. To maximize the therapeutic potential of radiation in this population, radiation oncologists should consider: (1) early consultation with dermatologists, wound care specialists, and nutritional support counselors14; (2) minimizing skin trauma from immobilization devices; and (3) designing RT plans that prioritize limiting maximum dose to cutaneous structures over minimizing total treated surface area. These considerations may help limit the risk of EB exacerbation by radiation therapy exposure.
